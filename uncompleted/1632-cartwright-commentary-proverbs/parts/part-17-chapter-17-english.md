CHAPTER XVII.

Verse 1. Better is a dry morsel, with which there is tranquility, than a house full of slaughtered animals of contention.

In ריב (*of contention*) there is an adjectival metonymy, namely, contentious feasting for contentious feasters.

The summary is the same as that which was at 15.17, concerning which we spoke before.

This is the fourth Proverb which instructs us to be content with little, and that what is little, and even mean, when joined with piety and justice, is preferred both to abundance and to delights separated from piety and justice. This is so, however, that of those passages two, chapter 15.16 and 16.8, are referred to the eighth commandment; two others, 15.17 and this one, are referred to the sixth commandment.

Although they pertain to different commandments, since the former refer to the possession of goods and the others to the advantage of sustenance, nevertheless most of the virtues required in either case relate both to establishing an excellent possession and to establishing a wholesome and convenient diet. For these four things are required to make a possession excellent: 1. that the fear of God be present; 2. that it be justly acquired; 3. that a person possess it with the love and goodwill of his neighbors; 4. that he enjoy it quietly and tranquilly. If these are present, although the income is slight and the possession very confined, it is nevertheless preferable to the most ample possessions and vast estates. The same must also be said about the manner of sustenance and diet. For if these four virtues are present as fellow diners, and indeed as the diners' proper dish or table, that table, not only if it is slight and spare, but even if it is unadorned and rustic, is nevertheless to be preferred by far to Dionysian banquets and exquisite feasts.

But if someone objects that the love included in verse 15.17 and the quiet comprehended in this verse are the same, let him understand that love which is begun in this world does not always have quiet and tranquility joined to it. For even among spouses, who are bound to one another by the closest bond of love, there are nevertheless sometimes quarrels and strife.

A singular art is therefore proposed to us by the Holy Spirit: how we may always render our table richly and abundantly furnished, even when we sustain life sparingly and harshly on black bread and stockfish or pickles; and also how we may render an inheritance august, however narrow it may be.

Verse 2. An understanding servant shall rule over a son who causes shame, and among the brothers he shall divide the inheritance.

In נחלה (*inheritance*) there is a synecdoche of the species for every kind of possession.

The summary is that a thrifty servant should be preferred, even in the distribution of a possession, to a spendthrift son, or to one who for another reason brings shame upon his parents; and for that reason wisdom, even in a lowly slave, should be preferred to free birth and liberty.

Nearly all people, carried away by φυσικῇ, so dote upon their own and those sprung from themselves that, although these are utterly unworthy because of a prodigal life, they load them, so far as they can, with every honor and with riches, passing over servants even though they excel in virtue and industry. He lashes this corruption. First, therefore, let it be defined who the son causing shame is. This is most aptly clear from 10.1, where the person here called one who causes shame is called a fool. He calls not only a person who is a simpleton a fool, but a wicked person, as we have seen more often.

Στοργὴ, or the affection by which a person,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 343 | printed columns 660-661]

under nature's guidance, embraces those who are joined to him by blood and affinity, is a lawful thing and is reckoned praiseworthy, just as those who lack this natural affection are, on the contrary, blameworthy. Romans 1.30. But this στοργὴ greatly displeases God unless it is confined within its own barriers and bounds. The mind leaps across and transgresses the appointed limits when those who are sprung from us are dearer to us than those who are born and begotten from God; when we value those who express our own image more highly than those who reflect the express image of God; and, finally, when we embrace more closely those joined to us by the bond of flesh than those joined to us by the bond of the Spirit.

To correct this error, let us consider what the Holy Spirit judges and what his disposition is. First, indeed, he places the thrifty servant above the shameful son in authority when he says that he shall rule over the son; and by this very fact the son will uncover his head to him, rise before him, and step aside from his path. From this it is clear that in appointing magistrates no account should be taken of wealth, nobility, or nearness of blood, although this is generally done. Hence come the ruinous overturnings of commonwealths when magistrates are appointed according to property. If virtue and piety are joined with wealth and nobility, certainly the piety adorned with such supports is to be preferred for appointment to office to piety which lacks these ornaments. What is said in this place was performed by Jacob, Genesis 49.7. He deprived his son Reuben, who by the right of primogeniture had ruled over his brothers to some extent, of his rule, and subjected him to his brother Judah, to whom he would be as a servant, simply because, by mounting his father's bed, he brought shame upon him. He did this even when Reuben had repented of the crime committed with his mother-in-law. Thus Pharaoh, although Joseph was a slave, set him over the whole kingdom and, by this very act, over his own children.

What follows about dividing the inheritance with the brothers is harder. For it often happens that people set a prudent servant over a foolish and senseless son for the benefit of their son, so that he may be governed by the servant's counsel and prudence; yet meanwhile they shrink from transferring any share of their possession to the servant, however prudent he may be. But Solomon sometimes deems a servant worthy to divide the inheritance among the brothers. For it often happens that by the labor of a prudent servant his master's life, sometimes his dignity and reputation, and often even his estate have been preserved. Moreover, reputation and standing, together with income and estate, have been gained and acquired not only by his counsel but also by the servant's industry. But when they leave whatever wealth, revenues, or property there may be to a foolish and shameful son merely so that they may transmit their name to posterity, what else do they do but furnish weapons to a madman, with which he may inflict great harm upon the commonwealth and the Church, especially in that commonwealth where honors are apportioned according to wealth and revenues?

And we see that in former times greater account was taken of faithful servants. For when Abraham had no children, he considered transmitting the inheritance and estate to Eleazar and appointing him as his heir, although Lot and Lot's daughters were not lacking among his kindred. Genesis 15.2–3. Then, when Solomon, the most august prince, had given his daughters in marriage to his servants, 1 Chronicles 2.24–25 and 1 Kings 4.10, 14, what else did he do but what is affirmed in this place, grant a prudent servant a place among the brothers? Thus Pharaoh gave Joseph in marriage Asenath, the daughter of the prince Potiphar. Most merchants imitate this prudence when they give their daughters in marriage to servants brought up by themselves and whose fidelity they have found proven.

Verse 3. The melting vessel tests silver;

[1632 witness | PDF p. 344 | printed columns 662-663]

the crucible tests gold, but the Lord is he who tests hearts.

The summary is that God knows with the utmost exactness whatever is in the mind.

In order that the Holy Spirit may impress this doctrine concerning God's omniscience more deeply and firmly upon our minds—a doctrine of great use to us both for shunning evil and for pursuing good—the Prophet depicts it for us with two images, one of purging silver and the other of removing the dross from gold. The sense is this: just as silver and gold, beaten and mixed together with other metals so that no one can separate them, nevertheless by the goldsmith's art, when melted with fire, at length emerge pure, cleared of dross, and separated from all slag, so God thoroughly examines human minds confused with various thoughts, so that there is nothing in them which escapes him. And just as penetrating fire refines gold and silver, so the fiery and flaming eyes of God discern all the thoughts of human beings. Revelation 1.14. There is, however, a point in which these things are unlike. The melting vessel and the like merely separate and distinguish those metals which have been mixed with the gold when it is applied to the purging; but God foreknows not only present and already formed thoughts, but also those which will one day arise many years afterward. For just as it is said in the Psalm that there is not a single word on the tongue which God does not know perfectly before it is uttered, so there is no thought in the mind or heart which he does not perceive long before it enters the mind.

Furthermore, the testing of hearts differs from that of gold and silver. For metals are cleared of dross and purified to such a point that no sediment and no slag remains attached to them. God, however, does not test to the point at which every evil thing in us is purified and refined away. For then he would take life from us, because, as long as we live, we carry about the sin inhering in our members. Hence in the Prophet God declares that he will test his own in the crucible of affliction, but not as goldsmiths are accustomed to test silver. Isaiah 48.10.

The use of this doctrine was explained above at 15.11. Moreover, since God alone is καρδιογνώστης, just as gold is tested by the crucible alone, care must be taken that people do not claim this for themselves.

Furthermore, since God alone knows hearts, he alone is to be invoked as the one who thoroughly knows the groans which we cannot utter. Therefore the Angels are not to be invoked, though they stand around us, much less the saints, who are so far distant.

Verse 4. An evildoer attends to an evil lip, and a man of falsehood hearkens to a tongue of miseries.

In מקשיב (*one attending*) and מזין (*one hearkening*) there is a synecdoche of a part, namely the ear, for all the faculties of the mind and the organs of the body by which a person brings the evil he desires into effect. So too in לשון (*of the tongue*) and שפת (*of the lip*) there is a metonymy of the subject in און (*of trouble*) and הות (*of miseries*).

The summary is that one devoted and addicted to committing evil seeks abroad every aid by which he may accomplish the evil designed in his mind.

Whoever has enslaved himself to committing crimes is not content with that depravity—whether his turning away from good or the aptitude for committing evil which he has enclosed and fastened within himself—but seeks from elsewhere and abroad all incitements and aids to crime and wickedness. Our nature, alas, makes us exceedingly ingenious and powerful for doing evil; yet like the famished and thirsty we seek elsewhere the means by which we may satisfy our insatiable desire to sin. In this the wicked imitate sufferers from dropsy, who, although they daily have more than enough vicious humors in the body,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 345 | printed columns 664-665]

carry them about, yet are held by indefatigable zeal to enlarge that bilge or pit in their body every day; and instead of draining their own bilge, as they ought, they convey another's into themselves as well. Hence they lend their ears to those who are more practiced in committing crimes, or more ingenious than they themselves. Hence they cast their eyes in every direction, so that if any pernicious examples meet their eyes, they may make them their own through imitation. Finally, hence books full of wickedness, impurity, and evil arts are eagerly sought out for reading.

Many examples of this occur in the Scriptures. There is Ahab, who, when he could discover from himself no means by which to extort Naboth's vineyard, which he desired so greatly, at last obeyed his wife and acquiesced in her frauds. 1 Kings 22. So too Amnon, when he had no power by himself to satisfy his shameful lust for his sister, listened to Jonadab's counsel, by which he obtained his impious love. From this we learn what the duty is of those who apply their minds to virtue and uprightness: they should not rest content with the narrowness of their own powers or the slenderness of their own ability, but should borrow from others aids and supports for acting well. The wicked are not ashamed to beg from elsewhere for the means by which they may bring the wickedness designed in their minds to completion. Woe, therefore, to our rustic shame, by which we blush to borrow from abroad and from others what are, as it were, the supports of virtue and uprightness.

Verse 5. He who mocks a poor man assails his Maker with reproach; he who rejoices at destruction shall not be innocent.

The summary is that he who exults and rejoices over another's evil is to be afflicted with evil.

At chapter 14.31 a Proverb not unlike this one was explained; from it those things which pertain to this place can be sought. It may indeed be asked here why he declares this particularly of the poor man when it ought to be declared universally of mortals of every kind, especially since mockery takes its origin from contempt for the person we mock and for that reason is rather often joined with the term for scoffing, and since it is commanded that no one despise his own flesh. I answer that there are two reasons why this is stated with a certain special emphasis concerning the poor and wretched man. The first is the malice and perversity of human beings, by which they cast their taunts at poor and unfortunate people, but not at the rich and those enjoying prosperous fortune, even though the rich often supply more abundant material and a richer crop for jesting and mockery. For their wealth and abundance of goods are like a cloak with which their blemishes, or rather their tumors, are covered, and like a shield or buckler beneath which they remain untouched by petulance of this sort from the tongue; while in poverty, on the contrary, even the smallest blemishes all lie exposed as though naked, or appear fouler than they are.

The second and principal cause is that, although it is contrary to duty to expose anyone to ridicule with taunts, the person who burdens the poor and afflicted with mockery sins much more gravely. For since they have already been struck by the hand of God, humanity would require us to refresh those afflicted by God and lift up the dejected, not, as is done, to grind beneath heels and feet those lying prostrate on the ground. See Psalm 109, what dreadful things he invokes upon the man who persecutes the afflicted and poor. What of the fact that he casts this up to the Chaldeans, alien to all piety, and reproaches them because they used no mercy toward his people whom he had struck? Isaiah 47.6. Thus it is plainly diabolical to add affliction to one who is afflicted. For we must not suppose that it is lawful for us to strike one whom the Lord

[1632 witness | PDF p. 346 | printed columns 666-667]

has struck, as though he had inflicted a deserved and just blow; instead, we must think that God afflicted him for the very purpose of taking a trial and proof of our charity and compassion toward him.

When he says that this mockery rebounds against God, who both made the poor man and constituted him poor, it ought not to be understood as if no grave injury were done to the poor and wretched man; rather, it is said by comparison that the reproach strikes principally against God. His purpose is to deter such mockers from their mockeries, for they derive their spirit of mockery from the belief that they have to do with a poor and wretched man who has no strength with which to repay an injury. The Prophet therefore teaches that they have to do with God, against whom, if mockery is their heart's desire, they can cast their reproaches and mockeries upon the poor and wretched man. If it seems dreadful to them to deride with taunts the most wealthy God, who is complete in every blessedness, they must take care not to treat the poor and afflicted contemptuously.

The judgment is the same concerning those who mock a person of duller and heavier understanding. Nothing is more unworthy, yet nothing is more common, as though the blow inflicted by God were not already grievous enough when he had, so to speak, harvested that person's spirit. The same judgment must be made concerning simpletons, whom nevertheless nearly everyone treats as a game and a joke. And thus the judgments of God which ought to educate us to humility and trembling lift us up into arrogance and pride. We must think in the same way about those who mock the hunchbacked, the one-eyed, the broad-browed, the lame, and others with defects of nature.

When he says that they shall not be innocent, he threatens them with God's grave vengeance, which is generally of the same kind as that in which we have behaved insolently toward others. Thus Shimei, insolently exulting over David's miseries, later perished when overwhelmed by God's most just vengeance.

Let this admonish those who mock others undergoing punishments, whether civil or ecclesiastical. They do this not because of the sins which those people committed—which is contrary to duty when the latter have been led to penitence—but because they underwent punishments. In that there is no evil, but very much good, if they bear those punishments equably. Galatians 6.1.

Verse 6. The crown of old men is their children's children; and the glory of children is their fathers.

In עטרת (*crown*) there is a metaphor which is explained in the latter half of the verse by the word תפארת (*glory*).

The summary is that children are adorned by God through the long life of their ancestors just as the ancestors are adorned by their children's abundant offspring.

The benefit of posterity is commended by very many testimonies of Scripture and by the ardent vows to God of the pious—Abraham, Isaac, Rachel, Anna, Zacharias, and others—who most earnestly sought this benefit of posterity. And since old age itself is reckoned among good things, how much more when it is heaped up with the benefit of posterity and encircled with a double crown.

But if children are numbered among great benefits, Psalm 128.6, certainly children's children must be something still more divine. Jacob proclaims this benefit at Genesis 48.12: although he had thought within himself that he would never see Joseph, nevertheless God caused him to see Joseph's posterity also in his presence. This, however, must be understood on condition that the posterity is well educated; otherwise they are at once a grief and a shame to their parents. Therefore we must labor in prayer and zeal not only for μακροημερίᾳ and τεκνογονίᾳ, but much more for a pious posterity. But that this benefit is conferred by God, and indeed chiefly upon those who fear God,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 347 | printed columns 668-669]

is clearly taught in Job 5.15, although in chapter 21 Job affirms that this benefit of old age and numerous posterity is sometimes conferred upon the wicked; but for the most part it is conferred upon the godly, Psalm 128.6.

The other part of the Proverb follows: that children and grandchildren are wreathed as with a crown by the gray hair and length of life of their ancestors and parents. Indeed, as to the former part of the Proverb, everyone knows that parents regard it as an honor and adornment to themselves if their children and grandchildren survive during their lifetime; so that there is generally a danger that they may glory excessively in this benefit and rejoice in it more than is right. Yet children and grandchildren, for whom the long life of their ancestors is an equal honor, are often forgetful of the benefit received from their parents, assail the years of their fathers, and place some benefit in their premature death. For if the parents are obscure, they wish them dead so that no traces of their own ignoble birth may remain. But if they are rich and wealthy, they hasten their death in their desire to succeed to their riches. Besides, nearly everyone supposes that the death of their parents will loosen the reins upon their own lusts. A clear example of this matter is presented in Esau, who, once his father was dead, appointed his brother for death.

This Proverb can not inappropriately be transferred to spiritual parents and children, who are an adornment to one another mutually and reciprocally; and all the more because this adornment is not bounded by the limits of this life, as it is in natural generation, but extends even to the day of the Lord, as is evident from 1 Thessalonians 2.19.

Verse 7. A lip of excellence does not befit a fool, much less a lip of falsehood a noble person.

In נאוה (*befitting*) there is a metaphor transferred from comeliness in bodily things to things perceived by the understanding. In שפה (*lip*) there is a metonymy of the cause for the effect and a synecdoche of the part for all instruments of speaking. Likewise, in יתר (*excellence*) and שקר (*falsehood*) there is a metonymy of the adjective.

The sum is that speech which is both holy and sincere is approved by God, while speech which is either corrupt or disguised is disapproved.

In Solomon's customary manner of speaking, both his own and his father's, “fool” is the same as “wicked person.” A difficult question arises here: whether the word נאוה refers to the judgment and disposition which the wicked person bears toward pure and holy speech, or rather to the judgment of God, the Angels, and the godly, to all of whom it is displeasing that a person wicked in life and deeds should nevertheless wish to appear upright in words and speech. Indeed, as far as the signification of the words and the truth itself are concerned, either sense can be admitted. But if we consider how the word נאוה is used in this book, and especially if we weigh the antithesis in this Proverb, the latter sense appears more genuine and more suited to the present purpose. For just as, in all other things—in clothing, buildings, and the proportion of the members of the body—the harmony and agreement of the parts are required to establish comeliness, so this is required much more in the life of a Christian person, since what is unseemly in the eyes of God is more hateful and odious than what displeases human eyes. If, for example, all the parts of a face were beautiful but one nose were monstrous and were to present an exceedingly deformed appearance, the beauty of the remaining parts would make the ugliness in the nose more odious. Or if someone, wearing a silk cap interwoven with gold, had the rest of his bodily attire base and torn, he would gain from it not merely no praise for splendor, but rather

[1632 witness | PDF p. 348 | printed columns 670-671]

would expose himself to everyone's ridicule. So when someone exults in speech but lies low and succumbs in deeds and in the remaining conduct of life, he is altogether hateful and offensive to God and human beings; as when an avaricious person preaches liberality, a prostitute chastity, or a robber delivers a sermon about justice.

Therefore the Prophet sternly assails the person who, though he lives wickedly, nevertheless dares to recount the statutes of the Lord and to take God's covenant upon his lips, Psalm 50.15–19. Hence Christ commanded unclean spirits to be silent when they confessed him, Mark 1.24, 25. Likewise Paul became angry at the unclean spirit possessing the girl because it extolled him and his companions, Acts 16.17, 18. For when holy things are proclaimed by people so impure, praise of this sort engenders in everyone's mind a certain nausea and disgust for those things which by their own nature are most excellent. The godly are thereby deeply grieved, while the wicked thereby gather obstacles for themselves by which they are turned farther away from love of the truth.

Therefore the Apostle's counsel is appropriate here: let all who name Jesus depart from iniquity, 2 Timothy 2.19.

The latter hemistich is a conclusion drawn from comparison with the lesser. For if speaking well is not allowed to a wicked person, much less is it allowed to a noble and excellent man to pour forth false and wicked things. And if it is not right for a wicked person to hide and disguise his wickedness with pure eloquence, much less will it be right for a good man to stain his integrity with impure speech and render it contemptible to everyone. If the former may not disguise ugliness by applying, as it were, eye paint, much less may he deform another person's beauty of countenance and make it hideous by blackening it. The reason is ready to hand: through the pure speech of an impure person some are found among those who are stronger and more confirmed who make progress, whereas no similar progress is made from the impure speech of a godly and good person. From this it can be clearly established how disgraceful it was for David to speak like a fool or madman before the King of the Philistines. Therefore we must take care that the thread of a godly life be woven to its very end in a perpetual course and evenness, since even the smallest disparity in words produces such great deformity.

Verse 8. A gift is a stone of favor in the eyes of its owners; wherever it looks, it will prosper.

In אבן (*stone*) there is a synecdoche of the genus for a gem. In חן there is a metonymy of the adjective. In עיני there is a metaphor transferred from bodily sight to the judgment of the mind. In בעליו there is a metonymy of the subject for the acceptance of a gift or the claiming of it as one's own, as the owner of a possession which he accepts and claims for himself.

The sum is that nothing in a judgment is so holy that it is not violated by the corruption of gifts.

By a stone of favor he understands a gem most pleasing to behold. He compares a corrupting gift to it for this reason: just as the person who carries a gem keeps his eyes fixed upon it so that he may take delight in looking at its form, so corrupt judges take delight in accepting gifts; and the eyes of their minds are fixed upon gifts no less than the bodily eyes upon a most pleasing gem. Hence it also comes about that the gift summons forth whatever sentence the person who offered it desires. For if the judge is wicked, setting gain before his eyes and determining within himself to accommodate his sentence in court to the pleasure of whoever offers a present, it is manifest that the judgment will be prostituted. But if the judge is just and has resolved with himself and in his own mind to pronounce what is right and deliver an incorrupt sentence, nevertheless, if

[1632 witness | PDF p. 349 | printed columns 672-673]

once he has admitted a gift, he is immediately prepared to show partiality. Hence a gift is said to blind the eyes of the clear-sighted and to pervert the words of the wise and just, Exodus 23.8; Deuteronomy 16.19.

The statement that the gift prospers appears to embolden the person who offers a gift to adopt a means by which he may corrupt the judge through the corruption of gifts, especially if he is inwardly confident of the rightness of his own case and persuades himself that he is bringing a good cause into court. But it must be understood that “it will prosper” is not affirmed purely and simply, but only in relation to the thing which the person offering a gift sets before his eyes: that is, he will win his case, although it would have been better to lose the case than to have furnished the occasion for prostituting judgment. For if the person who corrupts the King's money is liable to capital punishment, how much more the person who has attempted to violate God's image in judgment. Therefore litigants must beware in court that they neither assail nor test judges with corrupting gifts. If this is done, judges must take care not to regard gifts as the most pleasing and precious gems, but rather to look upon them as flints and stones of the basest and commonest sort.

Verse 9. The person who covers a transgression pursues love, but the person who repeats the matter separates a leader.

In מכסה (*covering*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things stained with a blemish of ugliness and removed from the sight of the eyes to sins which are not punished by those against whom they are committed. In שוב (*repeating*) there is a synecdoche of the act of repeating in parts for doing it many times. In דבר (*word*) there will be a synecdoche of the genus for an offense. Likewise, אלוף (*leader*) stands for everyone with whom a person is held in a closer bond.

The sum is that the person who duly passes over sins nourishes love; while, on the contrary, the person who rashly rubs them afresh and renews them sows discord and division.

It is commonly understood and plain before one's feet that the nature of love is such that a person wishes the one whom he holds dear to be as good as possible. When he cannot attain this, the next best thing is that he wishes him to be as little evil as possible. By that very fact, he either excuses by a favorable interpretation what can be excused, or also mitigates it, and, to the best of his ability, removes it from the category of sins; or, if that cannot be done, at least counts it only among faults and errors. Further, it is equally well known and widely reported that people who hold another dear seek to have him loved and esteemed not only by themselves but also by others. Hence it also comes about that, with respect to the faults committed by those whom they embrace with love, they are so disposed as to desire their transgressions to be covered as fully and concealed as closely as possible. If, because of the tepidity of our love, we cannot discern the nature of love in loving others, it is easy to recognize its nature in our own φιλαυτίᾳ, for we never show ourselves sharp and severe judges against ourselves, nor readily and rashly publish our own offenses.

See an example of this love in Joseph, Genesis 48.5, 6, 7, 8, and in Paul in the epistle to Philemon, where he affirms that Onesimus fled from him once so that thereafter he might always remain with him, and that he was once unprofitable so that thereafter he might be useful for the whole of his life. But by far the fullest and clearest example of this is in God, who, forgiving us infinite sins, covers them, Matthew 18.27; Psalm 32; and in Christ, who, when our many past sins have been passed over, sets our innocence and integrity before his Father, John 17.6, 7, 8.

Sins are covered when they are not punished; likewise when they are reproved privately and are not divulged publicly; and especially if someone repays good for evil, Romans 12.20, 21.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 350 | printed columns 674-675]

1 Peter 3.13. For in this way love is fostered even among enemies. This must nevertheless be understood so that neither ecclesiastical censures for sins, nor civil punishments in the commonwealth, nor chastisements in the family ought to be omitted or relaxed. Hence David's indulgence toward his sons Absalom and Adonijah, Eli's indulgence toward Hophni and Phinehas, and the connivance of others are justly condemned. For the subject here is private injuries and hidden sins.

What is in the former hemistich is confirmed by its opposite in the latter, just as the latter in turn receives confirmation from the former. For just as people who are estranged are reconciled by connivance, passing over, and remission of offenses, so people who are joined together are separated by rubbing sins afresh and setting them forth again. An example of this occurs in Sheba the son of Bichri. After the Jews and the ten tribes had disputed with one another over which of them had a greater right in David, once the dispute was ended Sheba set the two peoples against each other by rubbing the matter afresh and renewing it, 2 Samuel 19, 20.

This must nevertheless not be understood as though Ministers cannot frequently repeat and rehearse former sins in order to bring more obstinate people to repentance. Those who establish peace between litigants can and generally must rub afresh the injuries inflicted on each side so that, when the matter has been thoroughly examined, peace may be entered into more advantageously and maintained more firmly.

Verse 10. A reproof burns an intelligent person more than if you were to strike a fool a hundred times.

In תחת (*burns*) there is a metaphor in which reproof is compared to fire because it penetrates and strikes. In מאה (*a hundred times*) there is a synecdoche of a definite number for an indefinite one.

The sum is that the godly advance toward repentance with the slightest correction and gentlest chastisement, while the ungodly are not softened by God's most severe threats or even his judgments, but rather become hardened.

For those who inquire why intelligent people profit from a reproof, even if it is but one and gentle, no other cause can be assigned than that God, by the power of his Spirit, has removed their stony hearts and implanted and inserted hearts of flesh in them, Ezekiel 36.26. Hence it comes about that they readily receive an impression of virtue, however slight it may be. Further, they have tender, circumcised, and perforated ears from God; hence a reproof entering the ears of the godly proceeds to the very bottom of the heart, as it were, without any obstacle or impediment. Finally, the external voice of the admonisher does not alone strike the ears of the godly, but is attended by the power of the Holy Spirit; and with him, as it were, for its companion, the doors or gates of the heart and soul are opened.

Examples of this matter occur in Scripture. First is the example of David, who, although he had slumbered for nearly a whole year in the sin of adultery, was awakened from his sleep by a single response from the prophet Nathan, returned to himself, and repented. Another example is in Sarah, who, although she was the mother of all the faithful, nevertheless acquiesced in the rebuke of King Abimelech, Genesis 20.16. But the example most appropriate to the present passage by far is what is related of the two disciples traveling to Emmaus, where it is expressly said that their hearts, as if kindled by a certain fire, burned within them, Luke 24.25, 32.

From this we learn that it belongs to a certain excellent wisdom to maintain a tender and tractable disposition toward reproofs, although most people consider it folly if someone is a little more concerned about a reproof he has received; and, on the contrary, they reckon it a kind of magnanimity and loftiness of spirit to grow callous to reproofs made in words.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 351 | printed columns 676-677]

In the latter hemistich, which says that the wicked do not repent after one hundred blows, the causes must also be investigated, and they are plainly contrary to the causes of the former part of the Proverb. First, they have uncircumcised ears which are heavy for hearing, especially if something sharp and bitter is said. They turn away from every such thing with a certain itching for words, panting after flatteries. Further, they have hearts of stone and rock, and are coated over as though with fat, which is devoid of every sensation. So far are they from advancing toward repentance through words that not even blows and beatings, although repeated and redoubled very often, impel them toward repentance. A memorable example of this matter is found in Pharaoh and the Egyptians. Struck by the most grievous punishments as though by thunderbolts, they nevertheless not only failed to depart from their wickedness but acquired ever greater hardness of heart, until, having experienced the extremity of God's hand, they perished in the Red Sea. Not dissimilar is what is related of the Philistines, who, afflicted with the most exquisite punishments by the Lord because of the captured ark, did not even then repent, although by their own judgments they had been taught that God's hand was striking them.

From this we learn that it is a mark of extraordinary folly to become hardened against God's chastisements, especially those made with blows, however much people generally attribute this very thing to courage and fortitude.

Verse 11. The evil person seeks only rebellion, but a cruel messenger will be sent against him.

In מלאך (*messenger*) there is a synecdoche of the part for every misfortune or any blow which God sends either immediately or mediately through the agency of human beings or wild beasts.

The sum is that a wicked person finally receives the just reward for his rebellion, obstinacy, and refractory spirit, whether against God or against human beings.

It is manifest from countless testimonies of Scripture that a wicked person, one in no respect reborn by the Spirit through faith, is God's enemy and adversary. For it would be foolish to expect anything from a bad tree except rotten and corrupt fruits. Do people gather grapes from thorns or figs from thistles? Matthew 7.16. Therefore, whatever they think, say, or do is all reckoned as sin; and it often happens that the more they exert themselves and their powers to do what is most pleasing to God, the more atrociously they sin against God, so that the entire life of a wicked person, from its first day to the final moment of life, is nothing other than sheer rebellion against God.

But there is a greater question whether a wicked person meditates rebellion only against human beings. For you may see many wicked people who do not rise up against their Prince and Magistrate. It can therefore be asked whether the common saying is universally true: that the person who is faithless to God will not be faithful to human beings. This Proverb does not seem to depart far from the truth, just as it is repeatedly impressed in John that the person who does not love his brother does not love God, 1 John 2.3, 4. Conversely, in 1 John 5 it is said that from this it is known that we love the children of God: if we love God and keep his commandments. Since, therefore, the wicked neither love God nor keep, or can even keep, the least of his commandments, it follows that they do not love the children of God. But if they do not love them, they certainly hate them; and those whom they pursue with hatred they desire to perish. Hence they also bear rebellious dispositions toward them. Therefore David, as both a godly head of a household and a father of the commonwealth, professes that he will not permit a wicked person to remain either in his family or even in his kingdom, Psalm 101.

This will be more clearly established if the cause of this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 352 | printed columns 678-679]

rebellion is investigated. Now it is that impulse of mind which is in the wicked man and does not allow him to be at rest until he has accomplished the thing that he wickedly seeks. Hence the Prophet also likens the mind of the wicked man to a turbulent sea, swollen with waves and always gulping down some portion of mud, Isa. 57.20.

The fact, then, that very many wicked men—such as the Papists and atheists among us, and so forth—conduct themselves calmly and quietly is not because they are well disposed toward a godly Prince, but because they love peace, their children, and their revenues, which they think they could not enjoy if the commonwealth were entangled in foreign or domestic disturbances; or because they fear that they are not sufficiently equipped with strength to accomplish what they desire. Thus David speaks of certain of his subjects when he says that, although they bore minds alien to him, they nevertheless lied to him and feigned the utmost obedience, Ps. 18.43.

Examples of this teaching occur everywhere in the book of Judges. There it is clear that the Canaanites who were preserved alive by the Israelites could not be brought, by any benefits bestowed upon them, to refrain from shaking off the yoke whenever their strength permitted, exercising tyranny in Israel, and becoming thorns in their eyes and goads in their sides. They did not refrain even when they entered into marriages with them and bound themselves to them both by ties of blood and by bonds of superstitious religion.

The punishment owed to wicked men of this kind follows. Although God, the efficient cause, is left unmentioned, it is nevertheless readily apparent that God is the author of the vengeance sent upon them. For there is no evil in a city that God does not do, Amos 3.6. Nor is there any doubt that the instrumental cause of this inflicted punishment is sometimes an angel sent from heaven, whether blessed or reprobate, Ps. 35.5; 1 Sam. 19.9; and sometimes an officer or executioner ordered by the Magistrate to go and exact vengeance. Solomon therefore calls him cruel, not because in severity he goes beyond measure and reason, but because he is inexorable and will remit nothing of the punishment prescribed by the one who sends him. Pre-eminent in this class is pale death, which hell follows and, as it were, treads upon its heels, Rev. 6.8.

This messenger is often sent into this life, but not always, so that from the fact that some are not punished here God may give everyone assurance of the judgment to come. And if punishment is inflicted here, a punishment so much the heavier is exacted insofar as the penalty owed to the sinner and rebel has been postponed and delayed.

Moreover, this messenger is the more terrifying because he comes suddenly and unexpectedly upon all wicked men. Just when they are secure concerning peace and promise themselves every happy and prosperous thing, he rushes upon them like a thief in the night.

V. 12. Let a she-bear bereaved of her cubs meet a man, and not a fool in his folly.

There is an ellipsis in שכול (bereaved); it means, of her cubs.

The sum is that the anger of the fool, or ungodly man, is untamable.

The monstrous savagery of this beast, especially when bereaved of her cubs, is celebrated in the Scriptures. Thus Hushai, setting the savagery of David and his soldiers before Absalom’s eyes, compares them, already exasperated and inflamed with anger, to bereaved she-bears, 2 Sam. 17.8. But the matter is clearer still where God, as the avenger of Israel’s impiety, is depicted by this metaphor, and the effect of a she-bear’s ferocity is set forth: she does not stop tearing and rending the person who meets her until, stripping the heart of all its coverings and wrappings, she can devour it, Hos. 13.8.

Now the fool is compared with the she-bear, and the fool in

[1632 witness | PDF p. 353 | printed columns 680-681]

his folly with a she-bear bereaved of her cubs; and he is so compared that Solomon determines the she-bear bereaved of her cubs to be the lesser evil. These points, therefore, must be examined. The first question is why the Prophet says that a fool in his folly is likened to a bereaved she-bear. Does any time occur when folly does not adhere to the fool, just as there does occur a time when a she-bear is not bereaved? I answer: in this passage the folly of the fool is taken for folly at its ἀκμῇ, when, moved by some passion, he rushes headlong toward that to which his desire leads him, especially when, struck by fury, he grows hot with rage like a bereaved she-bear. For when no occasion for wickedness is offered, a wicked man may conceal and dissemble his wickedness for a time.

The comparison, therefore, is instituted between a bereaved she-bear and a fool, or wicked man, struck by anger. A bereaved she-bear rushes upon a man; so a fool, throwing away the reins of reason, is carried without restraint to satisfy his anger, Dan. 3.19–22. A she-bear attacks whoever comes before her, even though he is not carrying away her cubs; the wicked man does the same in his anger. For he even flies upon the innocent and involves them in the same punishments as the guilty, Acts 12.19; 1 Sam. 22.11,12 and through verse 18.

Thus far we see that the fool in his folly and the bereaved she-bear are equal. There is a respect in which the bereaved she-bear is surpassed by the fool in his folly. For there are devices by which a person may turn away the bear’s fury: if someone throws down one of the cubs, she will seize it and carry it to her den, and will abandon the man upon whom she was charging. But no flatteries, no severity, and no incantation of words, even though devised with the greatest skill, will drive the fool away from the folly of his fury.

To make this clearer still, let us consider a wise—that is, upright and godly—man in his folly, so that from the force of anger, or folly, in a godly man imbued with the Holy Spirit we may understand more surely how ineluctable it is in the wicked. David, provoked by Nabal’s churlish and uncivil reply and struck by great fury of mind—observe, I pray, how far he is led when incited by anger. First he binds himself by an oath to avenge this injury and vindicate it with blood. Then he rushes so precipitately onward that he swore he would kill not only Nabal but his whole innocent household, and indeed even those who had defended Nabal’s cause against his insulting abuse, 1 Sam. 25.

What difference, then, someone asks, is there between the folly of wise David and the folly of a fool? This: wicked men cannot be driven from their undertakings by prudent and modest speech and exhortation; but David, his heat subsiding at Abigail’s wise speech and admonition, abandoned his wicked purpose.

Therefore, however heedlessly we may be caught in the fetters and snares of disordered love, anger, or other passions, yet, unless we wish to be counted and regarded among the wicked, let us come to our senses at holy warnings, reproofs, and lessons.

V. 13. He who returns evil for good—evil shall not depart from his house.

In ביתו (his house) there is metonymy of the subject, or synecdoche of the part: the house, that is, the structure, stands for the whole family which is in the house.

The sum is that God himself avenges from heaven, with destruction and πανωλεθρίᾳ, the savagery of returning evil for good.

God gives his own citizens this law, that they return good for evil, Rom. 12.20,21; he also commands those alien to his polity to return good for good. This rule was cultivated and observed so religiously among the Persians that they were permitted even to

[1632 witness | PDF p. 354 | printed columns 682-683]

summon an ungrateful person, forgetful of a benefit, before the law; and when convicted of an ungrateful spirit they punished him as a criminal. Xenophon, in κυροπαιδ. (*Cyropaedia*).

Therefore, every person devoid not only of piety but also of humanity does something beastly and monstrous if he repays with evil one who has deserved well of him. And if the person who returns evil for evil merits so great a punishment, what will be done to the person who repays evil for good? Although this is a grave crime in everyone, it is far more atrocious in those who profess Christ and the Gospel and in Scripture are called kind and beneficent, Ps. 12.1. Hence, when David had attained the kingdom, he made an accounting of the gratitude to be shown to everyone from whom he had received a benefit. In doing so he did not even pass over those who were strangers and aliens from the polity of God.

Degrees of this crime are also established. For this crime grows and is, as it were, doubled either in proportion to the magnitude of the benefits a person has received, or according to the atrocity of the evils he has employed against his benefactor.

The added punishment which the Prophet announces is to be inflicted proves that this crime, grave and atrocious in itself, is monstrous and savage. For the crime is not expiated in the person of the one who inflicted this signal injury, but diffuses and extends itself to his entire household and posterity. Yet divine vengeance is not satisfied even by this. This vengeance is not bounded by fixed spans of time, but is said never to depart from his house. A conspicuous example of this appears in Pharaoh and the Egyptians. Because they did not remember the benefits bestowed upon that kingdom by Joseph, they were at last destroyed in utter annihilation, Exod. 14.

Here, however, we must take circumspect care lest, while we desire to show ourselves grateful to men, we should be found—as often happens—ungrateful and forgetful toward God. We must take all the more care because well-born people of good character often sin in this respect: they keep their eyes fixed only upon those who have benefited them and pass over God, who is the first and chief agent in this generosity, since he both furnished the means for beneficence and inclined the giver’s mind toward beneficence. It is just as if someone who received a benefit from a master were grateful to the servant whose ministry the master merely used, while neglecting the master to whom the benefit belonged.

V. 14. As one who releases waters is the head of contention; and before it is mingled, cease to contend.

The sum is that the beginnings of contention are to be carefully avoided as full of danger; but once contention has arisen, one must desist immediately, before the parties engage one another.

To illustrate the sin of the person who supplies an occasion for litigation and contention, a comparison of an equal is brought forward. He is likened to someone who releases a pool or river of waters. The waters break through the bank and flow over the opposing embankment; once they have flowed out, they cannot be beaten back and returned at all, nor can they easily be checked and confined so as to prevent them, as they continually gain strength, from sweeping away by their rapidity every obstacle placed before them. In a common and widely used proverb we say that such a man draws ruinous buildings down upon his own head. But this is a most fitting likeness, by which we are taught that not only the seed and beginning but also the fuel and growth of a quarrel are most dangerous.

In a common proverb we say that fire and water are things wholly devoid of mercy because of the impetuous violence and force with which they invade the things upon which they once rush. Therefore, so that the Holy Spirit may deter us from zeal for contention, he likens contention by these

[1632 witness | PDF p. 355 | printed columns 684-685]

two things. For Prov. 26.21 teaches that a man devoted to strife is like coal for embers and wood for kindling a fire; and in Judg. 9.19,20 contention is likened to fire.

Therefore we must beware of furnishing matter and ground for a quarrel. For it often happens that when we wish to withdraw our foot from a quarrel, it bursts out like a whirlpool of waters or a flame of fire, so that we are unable to settle it. See 2 Sam. 2.14,15 and verse 26. Why do I say “often,” when this never fails to happen without ill befalling the person who is the seed and cause? For woe especially to the person through whom the offence comes, Matt. 18. Indeed, even the person who gave the occasion for the quarrel, provided that he is godly, will be flooded with grief and sorrow because he furnished the matter for the quarrel.

What follows applies especially to the person who comes second in a quarrel. Solomon warns him not to seize an offered occasion for contention and involve himself in the quarrel. This is exceedingly appropriate. How few persons will be found who, after suffering an injury from another, do not think they have the right to litigate and contend? Yet in this they are greatly mistaken, since the struggle is begun and engaged in only for the sake of revenge and vengeance. Furthermore, even if it were otherwise lawful to repay evil with evil, one must nevertheless abstain from vengeance for this reason alone: no one is a judge in his own cause; moreover, no one moved by passion will ever preserve equity. But this proverb especially teaches him to desist and turn aside from the quarrel. Bursting waters cannot be blocked after they have acquired strength unless they are immediately beaten back; nor does a flame that seizes houses do otherwise, unless it is extinguished early, than consume and lay waste everything before it.

Nor should the one who is second in the quarrel suppose that his quarrel will turn out well for him, because those who contend against one another are consumed. See the account in 2 Sam. 2 mentioned above. For the young men who fought in sport killed and stabbed one another; and Joab, who was second in this quarrel, there suffered the loss of his dearest brother. Especially worthy of note are Abner’s words in verse 26: “Do you not know that the end and outcome will be bitter?” Memorable also is what Jotham predicts in Judg. 9: that fire would go out from Abimelech and consume the Shechemites, and in turn from Shechem and consume Abimelech. And note that word of the Apostle to the Galatians: take care lest, if you bite one another, you be consumed by one another.

From this it is clear what the duty of bystanders and spectators is when they see people in a quarrel: namely, to labor to settle it. For just as everyone runs to a fire or a flood of waters, so also, and so forth. Yet the contrary often happens, when worthless men pour oil upon the fire in place of water.

V. 15. He who justifies the wicked, and he who condemns the [poor] righteous man—even both are an abomination to Jehovah.

In מצדיק (justifying) and מרשיע (condemning) there is metonymy of effect, namely, for pronouncing someone righteous or wicked; and, first, for punishing, in the case of the one who makes a man righteous who is wicked, or wicked who is righteous. Here, incidentally, is confirmed the meaning of justification by which we are justified by God through faith, which the Papists so greatly abhor and on account of which they bark against the Gospel.

The sum is that in judging we must walk with a straight foot, so that we turn aside neither to the left by condemning the righteous nor to the right by absolving the wicked, since both things are equally hateful to God.

Now this judgment is:

[1632 witness | PDF p. 356 | printed columns 686-687]

              ⎧ private,
either        ⎨
              ⎩ public,

              ⎧ everyone, Matt. 7,     ⎫
which to      ⎨                       ⎬ belongs.
              ⎩ the Magistrate alone  ⎭

Those who exercise this latter judgment ought to reflect that they sustain the person of God, since the functions of judging belong to God, 2 Chron. 19.6,7. Therefore they must devote every care and thought to ensuring that judgment and law are pronounced so purely and unsulliedly that they appear to have proceeded from God himself and not to be unworthy of him. If someone here objects that God justifies the wicked, Rom. 4.5, and that by this very fact the same is permitted to one who pronounces law in the commonwealth, let him understand, first, that God is permitted to do what is unlawful for men; and, second, that when God justifies a wicked person, he makes him righteous out of wickedness, expiates his impiety by the payment of a price, and implants in him a mind different from the one he previously had. Since no mortal does this, it is vain to put forward God’s example and seize it as a defense of oneself.

A person justifies the wicked and condemns the righteous in many ways. First, he does so when, after an unjust sentence has been rendered, he executes it. Next, it is done by delaying the law and by exempting and postponing the day. For as long as judgment is held in suspense, the one who is righteous is often regarded as unrighteous, and conversely the unrighteous as righteous. Thus by this delay and procrastination of law, his justice is taken from the righteous person and justice is added to the unrighteous. Finally, it is done when, after a sentence has been duly and properly rendered, execution of the sentence either does not follow at all or is not committed to timely and prompt execution. Hence Scripture embraces guilt and punishment in one and the same word, declaring by this that the one upon whom no punishment is imposed is regarded as guiltless.

Hence also the third commandment of the first table, when it threatens punishment to the person who violates God’s name, indicates and intimates the same in these words: God will not hold that man innocent. All the more detestable, then, is the blasphemous error of the Papists, by which they impose this injustice upon God: that the faithful are to be absolved in judgment, with punishment reserved.

When it says that both are an abomination to God, it confronts the depraved judgment of men on this point and convicts it of injustice. For men in whom some trace and sign of virtue appears are generally of such a mind that they judge it an unworthy crime to condemn a righteous person; yet they do not equally abhor the acquittal of a wicked one. Captivated by a person’s appearance and rank, or with a view to services in war that he either performed formerly or may perform hereafter, they are inclined to mercy, or they concede something to the requests of others. All of this is an abomination to God, whoever does it, whether godly or ungodly. For although the godly are always dear to God, even when they commit things which are abominations, he nevertheless hates the evils they do no less than those wickedly committed by the ungodly; indeed, he hates them the more because the godly are bound by more obligations to avoid that evil. For when the wicked are absolved: first, those who have been absolved nearly always become worse; second, the upright are partly saddened by this indulgence and partly loosen the reins for themselves toward wickedness; third, the wicked rush headlong into crimes.

But if the person who commits either one of these things is an abomination before God, the one who lapses into both draws God’s indignation upon himself in greater measure.

V. 16. To what end is a price in the hand of a fool? To acquire wisdom? But he has no heart.

In ידו (hand) there is metaphor for possession, because whatever a person has he appears, as it were, to hold in his hand. Likewise, in לב (heart) there is metonymy.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 357 | printed columns 688-689]

of the subject for the endowments of the mind, understanding and will.

The sum is that riches confer no good upon a fool, or wicked man, but rather harm him, because he lacks the knowledge of using them.

In the former part of the Proverb he teaches that the wicked man has no just and lawful use of riches and dignity. He confirms this all the more strongly because he puts it ἐρωτηματικῶς (*interrogatively*), as though challenging everyone to a contest to see whether they can bring forth anything to the contrary by which they might show that the advantages of this life are of use to the wicked. It is as though Solomon said that riches and honor become a fool no more than packsaddles become an ox. For riches and honor have been granted to people, first, so that God may be worshiped and honored by them, Proverbs 3.9; but by the expenditure of his wealth the wicked man, so far as he is able, affects God with dishonor, and diverts his money to obscure God's glory. Next, since they were given to advance his own salvation, the opposite occurs: by their possession the approach to the kingdom of heaven is made inaccessible and closed to them, Mark 10.23. For by trusting in riches, verse 24, they are idolaters, Ephesians 5.5; or they squander them in pursuing pleasures; or, relying upon them, they become proud and despise others in comparison with themselves; or they abuse them in some other way to their own destruction, 1 Timothy 6.17, 18. Finally, they abuse them to oppress others or to corrupt them by luxury and lust, 1 Samuel 25; see the example of Nabal. It may therefore rightly be asked: To what end would a price be put into the hand of a fool? For riches profit him no more than if someone were to put a sword into the hand of an infant—or rather of a madman—who knows either not how to use it at all or how to abuse it to his own and others' destruction.

Someone may object here that the wicked are, as it were, stewards and treasurers who collect money and build houses and so forth for the use of the godly. I answer that this is the benefit of God alone, even against their will.

He may object again that many friends, as the Proverb says, are gathered in this way. But I answer that they are not true friends but flatterers, who love us on account of riches.

In the latter hemistich there is a prolepsis whose anticipated objection is: a fool can procure wisdom for himself by a price. The reply is: but how can he procure wisdom when he is senseless and not endowed with a capacity for receiving wisdom, much less possessed by any zeal for it? In this saying Solomon confirms for us what was said above, that riches were given to us by God for this purpose, that by their aid we might advance toward godliness. This place also confirms that no approach to godliness lies open to us by our own effort or our own powers before God precedes us with his grace—any more than it lies open to a fool or a natural idiot to search out the most profound and most hidden causes of the greatest matters. Hence we are also called brutish by nature by the Holy Spirit, Jeremiah 5.1, 17; Proverbs 30.2, before God has illuminated our minds with the radiance of his Spirit.

It follows also that we ought not to seek riches under any other law or condition unless, together with riches, God has also given the gift of using them rightly—unless perhaps we are so badly idle that we seek our own plague and destruction.

Furthermore, we are taught that neither Princes in the commonwealth nor parents in the family ought to confer their dignities or inheritances upon wicked subjects or children, unless it should please them to do this both to their own disgrace and to the injury of the commonwealth.

Someone may nevertheless object here that God confers riches, honors, and the other advantages of this life indiscriminately upon the good and the bad together, and that therefore they may lawfully do what they know God does. I answer, as has often been said, that it is not safe to set God's example before us for imitation without his command. This is most clearly perceived in the present matter. For if we imitate God's example here, it is fitting that in conferring riches and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 358 | printed columns 690-691]

honors we be more lavish toward the wicked than toward the good, since the wicked abound more than the godly in the advantages of this life, Psalm 17.14. Next, God burdens them with these benefits so as to render them the more inexcusable and, furthermore, to exercise the patience of the godly, Psalm 73—things that it is not lawful for people to do.

Verse 17. A friend loves at every time, and is a brother born in affliction?

In אח (*brother*) there is a metaphor taken from those who are joined to us by blood and transferred to those who are joined to us by will and love. In יולד (*is born*) there is another metaphor. For just as, when someone is brought forth into the light, he is then first seen and beheld by the eyes, so a true friend is then most clearly discerned, because he then hastens to bring aid even though he had previously furnished no token or indication of friendship.

The sum is that a true friend, constant in love, most clearly proves himself when there is the greatest need of him.

It is beautiful in love to imitate God, who commands love. For he persists in love toward his own, all of whom he calls friends, John 15.14, 15. “I am God, who do not change”; nor is any variation or shadow of turning found in him, James 1.17. In this place, however, he appears to indicate that more inward and intimate love which exists between those who are bound together in a singular bond and tie of love, John 15.14.

There are many who feign friendship and an inward love for others when they are not truly friends, such as those who are led to love for the sake of their own advantage, pleasure, or honor; if these things fail, they immediately grow cold in love. There are others who are driven to love another by similarity of condition of life, morals, and disposition, yet their love is unstable. But that friendship which has its beginning and foundation in virtue and godliness is the only true friendship, because, since the godliness which brought us into another's embrace never fails, neither can the friendship which rests upon such a foundation be shaken. These, therefore, are said to be constant in love: they love when they abound in riches and when they are poor; when honored and when dishonored; when they enjoy prosperous or adverse fortune. Let us therefore look somewhat more deeply into these matters. First, although true friendship can scarcely seem capable of being seen or judged in another's prosperity, when we look more closely we shall perceive that no small part of true friendship consists in a person's bearing his friend's prosperity equably and gladly. For there are many who dearly embrace others of the same rank as themselves but envy them a condition better than their own. Saul is an example: at first he loved David, 1 Samuel 16.21, 22, but after he understood that the judgment of the women had preferred David to himself, his love was immediately turned into mortal hatred, 1 Samuel 18.6, 8. Hence it is attributed to Jonathan as the highest praise that he did not cease to love David, though David was to be preferred to himself in the kingdom. But the chief trial of friendship is taken from a friend's afflicted condition. In this matter we see an outstanding example in Abraham, who rescued captive Lot from the power of the enemies, Genesis 14; in Ittai and Chushi, David's friends, 2 Samuel 15.21, 37; and in Jonathan, who exposed himself to danger of life for David. An example of this is also set forth in John, whom Christ deigned to choose as a friend and who did not desert Christ in the flesh even to his final breath, when the other disciples suffered him to be deserted. This is also seen to some extent in the Virgin;

[1632 witness | PDF p. 359 | printed columns 692-693]

likewise in Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea, who, though they professed Christ secretly throughout their life, afterward nevertheless performed it openly and confidently.

To this Proverb also pertains the fact that we ought to love and embrace with charity not only those present but those absent, Galatians 4.18; 1 Thessalonians 2.17; not only the living but also the dead, Ruth 2.19.

Therefore the greatest caution must be employed in choosing friends, so that they are persons whom it is lawful to aid in their greatest peril, because every assistance is bounded, as the Proverb says, “as far as the altars.”

Verse 18. A senseless man clasps his palm; he becomes surety by suretyship before his neighbor—that is, before his neighbor asks this of him.

In תקעכף (*clasps his palm*) there is a metonymy of an adjunct for *he pledges*, or *interposes himself as surety*.

The sum is that people who are improvident and inconsiderate interpose themselves rashly as surety for another's debt, or make another's dangers their own.

Let no one suppose that Solomon here passes beyond the bounds of prophecy and puts his sickle into another's harvest when he hands down household precepts, since the Apostle falls into the same manner of instruction in 1 Timothy 5 when he teaches that the person who does not provide for his own family is worse than a heathen. And so close is the bond of the Church with household and civil government that, when one is shaken, the other must fall and incline toward deterioration. Hence also in chapter 6 of this book he vehemently exhorts a person entangled in suretyship and a pledge to exert strenuous effort to free himself as soon as possible, much less, when freed from those bonds, to put them on rashly again. This, however, must not be taken in the sense that it is not lawful to become surety. For since it is a duty to expose one's life for a friend, certainly one ought not refuse to undergo the risk of loss.

But that rashness and precipitation are condemned, not the thing itself absolutely, is proved by two arguments: first, because the verb placed in the present tense denotes persistence and frequency in becoming surety; second, because he brands with this mark of folly the person who, even when not asked, casts himself into this danger. That it ought to be translated in this way is taught by the passage John 10.8, “All who came before me are thieves,” and so forth; and that passage in turn is illustrated by the phrasing and formula of speech in this Proverb.

Here, therefore, it must be carefully considered who it is for whom we become surety; what merit of his toward the Church or commonwealth exists or may afterward exist; whether he has been reduced to need by luxury and prodigality and has squandered his patrimony; or whether he has been worn down by some inescapable judgment of God. It must not be forgotten that the person who appoints himself as surety ought to reckon with himself and make no other calculation than if the debt were in his own name, nor strive less to discharge it than if it were his own debt. This is neglected by almost everyone who pledges for others more than he himself can discharge, and who makes the stipulation as though certain that payment will be made by the person for whom he stipulates.

Verse 19. He loves defection who loves strife; he who seeks a breach enlarges his door.

In פתחו (*his door*) מגביה (*enlarges*) there is an allegory for propagating defection far and wide; just as a person who widens a door opens a greater way for entry into the house. Further,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 360 | printed columns 694-695]

it appears to be ὕστερον πρότερον (*last before first*) in each part of the Proverb, so that the predicate is placed before the subject of each axiom.

As there is a bond among the virtues, so also among the vices. For the person truly imbued with even one virtue cannot but be led by zeal for the other virtues insofar as they become known to him; likewise, the person who has come to love even one vice will, as opportunity permits, shrink from no crime. Therefore, when it is said here that the person who loves quarrels loves defection, this must be understood κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν (*preeminently*): a person given to quarrels implicates himself in a certain greater manner in most other sins, and not only has other vices potentially within him, but is wholly addicted to them and practices them. Therefore the Prophet, describing people wicked toward God and people wicked toward human beings, calls them “the company of spearmen” and “the people who delight in wars,” Psalm 68.30; for when he called a person zealous and eager for battle, he called him in a certain way covered with every vice. Indeed, in this very book we have learned that a contentious person is proud, Proverbs 3.10. As that vice is hideous and supremely hateful to God, whom it directly assails and who resists it to its face, so it is seen to be surrounded by no small company of other sins. Next, in another place this zeal for contending is said to arise from idleness and sloth, Proverbs 6.12. The idle person is called in the same place a man covered with many crimes, איש בליעל (*a man of Belial*). Hence idleness is not inappropriately called by some the devil's cushion, because the unclean spirit rests and leans upon it with the whole weight of iniquity.

Contentious people, however, are not all of one kind. There are those who, through a certain cowardice and innate timidity, are unwilling to fight themselves, but nevertheless sow contention among others and are, as it were, the firebrands of war and battle, in order to gain advantage from the dissensions and discords of others. Of this kind were, and now are, the Roman Pontiffs, who have wonderfully increased their tyranny by setting Kings and Princes at swordplay among themselves, especially the Germans. Others, relying upon their own strength and courage, are ready to fight and hunt for occasions of contention; they also look not unwillingly upon the discords of others among themselves.

It is said of these that they open a gate for the introduction of many and infinite evils. It was said earlier that the person who provides an occasion for contention lets loose a reservoir which he cannot block and stop up when he wishes, nor can he extinguish the fire or flame of contention; on this account lawsuits are to be vehemently avoided. In this place, however, a greater and graver evil is indicated: namely, that the person who seeks the dissolution of those who are joined to one another opens a door for the admission of many other evils. For contentions concerning a private matter afterward creep toward the most grievous harm of the commonwealth when, as these people litigate privately, many join themselves to one side and the other, and so the commonwealth is torn apart. Next, when those who preside over the commonwealth contend, such is the perversity of our nature that, because of disagreement in private matters, we are also pulled apart in delivering opinions concerning the commonwealth. What is still graver, domestic quarrels often divide and separate us in the very causes of God and religion, so that, if an adversary holds a right opinion concerning religion, we are carried and rush with a certain disordered impulse into the contrary party. Hence the Apostle, in order to retain us in the unity of faith, vehemently exhorts us to concord, 1 Corinthians 1.10.

Verse 20. A man perverse in heart will not find good, and one changeable with his tongue will fall into evil.

In עקש (*perverse*) there is a metaphor from the perversity of material things to the mind, which is devoid of matter.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 361 | printed columns 696-697]

The sum is that those who suppose they can avoid disadvantages and obtain advantages by fraud and dissimulation fall away and are frustrated in their hope.

Who is perverse in heart may easily be gathered from Proverbs 11.20, where he is opposed to an open, ingenuous, and upright person. Therefore, a person perverse in heart is one who, having cast aside the bridle of the fear of God, accommodates himself to that which appears good to him and is, if one may express it in one word, a hypocrite. He is also the one whom the Prophet calls “deep in heart,” Psalm 64.6, whose mind one cannot penetrate or explore by his speech and tongue as by a bucket or a scoop, Isaiah 29.15. Describing him elsewhere, the Prophet attributes to him a double heart, just as if his tongue were impelled by one heart and the thoughts of his mind hidden in the other, Psalm 12.2. By this saying the Holy Spirit likens the hypocrite to a monstrosity and renders him hateful and odious to all. For if it is deformed in the sight of people to be two-headed, because nature assigns one head to each person, how much more will a person have a shameful appearance before God if he has two souls. Hence those who are perverse in heart are also said to be abominable to God, Proverbs 11.20.

One who is changeable with his tongue is the same person who was previously called perverse in mind. For it is an effect of a hypocrite to be double-tongued and many-tongued. These are those who speak flatteringly to gain favor and who accommodate their speech according to the persons with whom they associate or have dealings, whether the question concerns matters pertaining to the future life or the present. Hence it occurs that, just as the tongue disagrees with the heart, so the tongue itself, as though split, is separated from itself, 1 Timothy 3.8. From this another monstrosity of nature is born, as the Apostle also noted somewhere, James 3.10, 11.

But if someone should object that there is no one who does not sometimes speak otherwise than he thinks, and that one cannot find by searching anyone whose speech is consistent with itself through the whole of life, I answer that the Prophet imputes this crime only to those who continually practice this dissimulation as an art and addict themselves to it as slaves. Otherwise, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are found sometimes to have spoken otherwise than they thought.

Now follows the punishment set before these perverse people, and it is described as most grievous. First, they are said not to obtain good; then, to fall into evil. These two things joined together constitute the sum of unhappiness. In this matter the Prophet teaches that they fall short of the hope they fashioned in their own mind. For people of this kind suppose that by their frauds, dissimulation, and transformation, as it were, into every form, they can obtain whatever good they desire and avoid whatever evil they fear. Hence comes what they boast before a certain Prophet, that “with our tongue we shall prevail,” Psalm 12.4. Scripture, on the contrary, teaches that these many-tongued hypocrites are to be destroyed and pierced by their own tongues, Psalm 64.8; 2 Samuel 1.16 and 4.8, 9; Matthew 25. As this often comes to pass in this life, so it will occur much more when all the wicked are brought before Christ's tribunal to receive the final sentence of condemnation.

And God pursues this sin all the more severely because it is not subject to human judgment or chastisements.

Verse 21. One who begets a fool begets him for sorrow to himself; and the father of a fool will not rejoice.

In אבי (*father*) and יולד (*begets*) there is a synecdoche of a part for both parents. Likewise in כסיל (*a fool*) and נבל (*fool*) there is a synecdoche for both sexes.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 362 | printed columns 698-699]

The summary is: in νεκροπαιδείᾳ alone there is sufficient cause for grief.

Since all are by nature children of wrath, Eph. 2. 3., and all who descend from Adam beget children after their own image, just as Adam did, Gen. 5. 3., that is, an image corrupted and depraved, this Proverb may seem to break everyone's desire to beget children. Reply. All in themselves beget children of wrath by nature; nevertheless, by reason of the gracious covenant, godly persons who are sometimes Jews by nature, Galat. 2. 15., or Christians, beget those whom God sanctifies by his freely given Spirit as soon as they are born.

Therefore one must labour not so much for γονῇ as for godly offspring. Accordingly, first, care must be taken that children come into the light from lawful marriage, since bastards, through the hidden curse of God, for the most part turn out wicked and criminal. Next, a godly spouse must be chosen, Math. 2. 15., because apostates are commonly born from unequal marriages, Gen. 6. 2.; Math. 2. 11. Third, we must strive and contend in prayer that God, taking pity on us, may grant us children of such a kind as he himself will deign in turn to receive from us, 1 Sam. 1. 11. Finally, the greatest care must be undertaken that they be instructed in the fear of God, and in the teaching and admonitions of the Lord, Ephes. 6. 4.

At Prov. 10. 1. it is said that a foolish son is a grief to his mother. We know that women are by nature softer and more delicate, and are more readily saddened than men. Further, they do not possess so much prudence that they can drive away the troubles inflicted upon them by foolish children. Therefore in this Proverb Solomon teaches that not even men themselves are endowed with so much prudence or courage that they can resist the grief which foolish children bring upon them, so as not to be compelled, even against their will, to experience to their own hurt the affliction caused by foolish children. Thus those who were to be like arrows with which they might pierce their enemies, Psal. 127. 4., are turned into arrows by which the parents themselves are pierced through.

But we see that Esau, by his skill in hunting, supplied his aged father Isaac with delicacies in which he delighted. Nor is there any doubt that David took very great delight in the incredible beauty of his son. Reply. The Prophet is not speaking of delights which are sometimes felt, but of those which ought to be felt. Besides, they compensated for the momentary and empty delight which they brought their parents with the gravest and most enduring sorrows: Esau, when he took Hittite wives and compelled his brother Jacob to go into exile; and Absalom, by Amnon's slaughter, treachery against his father, intercourse with the concubines, and finally by his dreadful and horrible death.

V. 22. A joyful heart works good medicine: but a sick spirit dries the bone.

In גרם (bone) there is a synecdoche of the member.

The summary is: honest and lawful gladness restores impaired health, while, conversely, needless sorrow of mind shatters good health.

People everywhere agree upon this judgement: that cheerfulness of heart, or of mind, is of the greatest value for preserving health; indeed, that gladness of mind has great weight in recovering lost health. Hence someone said, wittily and yet not altogether foolishly, that three Doctors are required for the restoration of lost health: Dr Rest, Dr Diet, and the playful or witty Doctor—in the common saying, Doctor diet, D. quiet and D. merryman. But although this is agreed among everyone, there is no such agreement as to what that cheerfulness is by which the mind is so greatly refreshed and renewed. For it is utterly denied that every kind of cheerfulness is

[1632 witness | PDF p. 363 | printed columns 700-701]

friendly to health. For we have learned from many experiences that the cheerfulness found in drinking-parties, luxury, and roving lusts is even hostile to health and sometimes deadly.

But Solomon has taught above wherein chiefly consists that gladness by which health is procured: namely in a good conscience, which is like a continual feast, Prov. 15. 15., by which one is cheered night and day. Hence it is that Solomon assigns to the fear of God, which is the inseparable companion of a good conscience, this effect: that it makes one's navel sound and one's bones suffused with fat and marrow, Prov. 3. 7, 8. and 4. 22. From this source likewise flows that promise of sweet and pleasant sleep, Prov. 3. 23, 24., which everyone acknowledges to be an extraordinary aid to health. Hence it is that the staff, or sustaining power, of bread, wine, and other foods retains its power to nourish us, Esa. 3. 1., just as the contrary is plain from Agg. 1. Finally, it comes from no other source that our clothes protect us from the cold, as is likewise most clearly evident from Agg. Brilliant examples of this doctrine are found in Caleb, who remained obedient to God when the others fell away, and who at eighty years of age was as strong and vigorous as when he was forty, Jos. 14. 6. Likewise in Moses, whose eyesight had not grown wholly dim in extreme old age and who at the age of 120 retained the same freshness and strength as ever before. Likewise in David, who says that he was in some manner revived and renewed by a sense of divine mercy, Psal. 103. 5.

These things are not said as though the external supports of health—the wholesomeness of food, bodily exercise, relaxation of the mind from more serious studies, and finally medical knowledge when health has been impaired—ought to be cast aside (for this would be to tempt God), but so that it may be understood that the chief supports of health and of a sound bodily constitution are placed in that gladness which God pours into godly minds from the testimony of a good conscience purged by faith in Christ. Without it, those who seek health and strength act just like people who, wishing to cure a headache, apply plasters outwardly to the stomach, from which the matter supplying the pains arises, while leaving the stomach itself wholly unpurged.

The first half is confirmed by the second from the contrary, just as the second receives reciprocal confirmation from the first. For a proverb commonly used among us says that sorrow or grief is dry. And that dryness, although it is felt chiefly in the mouth, nevertheless penetrates to every part of the body, even the most inward and fullest of moisture. Hence the Prophet complains that his choicest moisture was turned into summer heat, Psal. 32. 4.; and elsewhere he complains that his bones burned like a hearth, his heart withered like grass, and his bone clung to his flesh, Psal. 102. 4, 5, 6. Hence Christ, exhausted by sorrow, was consumed by such thirst, Math. 27. Here, therefore, someone might perhaps ask by what right Ministers of the word labour so greatly to strike sorrow into men's minds. For by this means they seem to take this life from them, or at least to hasten their death, which is inhuman. Reply. Sorrow accepted on account of sins does not harm health, since it is commanded by God; but sorrow arising from the things of the world does very great harm, 2 Cor. 7. 10. For brief sorrow dutifully accepted is compensated by great and enduring joy.

V. 23. A wicked man takes a gift from the bosom to pervert the paths of judgement.

In בחק (in the bosom) there is a metaphor signifying the hidden or secret giving, presentation, or acceptance of a gift; because what is in the

[1632 witness | PDF p. 364 | printed columns 702-703]

bosom is concealed and hidden. Likewise, in ארחות (paths) there is a metaphor for a manifold course.

The summary is: whoever violates justice by giving or receiving a gift is to be reckoned among the wicked.

That justice is sacred to God is shown by many testimonies of Scripture. The more precious this thing is, and the more especially necessary for preserving a holy society, the more criminal is the wickedness of those who attempt to violate, as it were, its virginity and integrity. The breadth and majesty of that justice was to some extent depicted in that most august throne which Solomon caused to be constructed for himself from ivory and the purest gold, from which he administered justice to the people, 1 Reg. 10. 18. The excellence of the throne admonished first the judges, and then both the litigants and those disputing at law, to do nothing unworthy of such majesty. The splendour of this gold must always be kept before our eyes, so that it may instil reverence into us lest by some wickedness of ours we defile this throne. But this should move us most of all: that God sits on the throne of judgement, observing from every side all things done or said in a trial, so as to render the due reward.

Now justice is violated in many ways: namely, by ignorance of law, envy, hatred, fickleness, and a tumultuous handling of law; and among other things judgement is violated by the corruption of gifts. This happens in more than one way. For judgement is corrupted not only when the person who sits as judge upon the tribunal receives a gift, but also when the plaintiff or defendant disputing at law offers a gift to the judge. Each receives the gift: the disputant, so that he may give it; the judge, so that he may enjoy it. Nor is justice violated only when a gift is given or received in an evil cause, but even when, so that we may obtain a good cause, we introduce a gift, Acts 24. 26. Nor only when we ourselves do this, but when we arrange for it to be done through others; indeed, if, so far as lies in us, we do not oppose that corruption. This Proverb confirms that general doctrine: you shall not do evil so that good may come from it, Rom. 3. 8.

What is said, that a wicked man receives a gift from the bosom, seems to be a prolepsis whose anticipated objection, passed over in silence, is this: a man is rightly to be held wicked if, with his face uncovered and his forehead hardened, he gives or receives a gift; but he is not likewise to be held wicked if he does this secretly or in concealment. The reply is that even the man who gives or receives it secretly must be judged wicked. For doing this openly and at midday declares a man to be of desperate and ruined hope. Receiving from the bosom seems to be opposed to someone who either gives or receives a gift taken from his money-box or purse. Here we are taught that those who commit sins so covertly and secretly as to escape men's notice are not for that reason absolved. Yet men are commonly so disposed that they are not troubled by God's knowledge provided they hide it from men; they are not concerned about God's tribunal provided they are not summoned to law before men; they do not revere the glorious eyes of God provided they are removed from the eyes of mortals. When they have drunk to the point of drunkenness, provided they can secretly and without others' knowledge retreat to their rooms, where they may sleep off their wine, even if they contract disease from their drunkenness, nevertheless, like senseless men, they do not once tremble. And it is to be feared that, if they do not soon return to their senses, God will at last, by a just judgement, hand them over to a reprobate mind, so that they do not shrink from doing evil in the sunlight and at midday. But it is astonishing that men who profess faith in Christ do not fear the sight of God even when, present in the ecclesiastical assembly, they are brought nearer to God's presence, so that they are not ashamed to play, smirk, and sleep provided they escape men's eyes. Is this to walk continually before God, and to

[1632 witness | PDF p. 365 | printed columns 704-705]

show oneself upright? Yet this is required of all the faithful as sons of Abraham.

When he says that this corruption by gifts perverts the paths of justice, he teaches thereby not only that justice is perverted by the giving or receiving of gifts, but moreover that there is nothing so small or slight in judgement and the administration of justice that it may lawfully be bought with a price—not even that one be heard equitably and patiently or that a just sentence be hastened. This appears to be signified by the word ארחות (paths).

V. 24. Wisdom is present before the face of an intelligent man: but the eyes of a fool are at the end of the earth.

In פני (face) there is a synecdoche for the whole composed state and bearing of the body; but it is said especially of the face, κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν, that wisdom resides there. There is the same synecdoche in עיני (eyes). In קצה ארץ (the end of the earth) there is likewise a synecdoche, and it is hyperbolic.

The summary is: a wise man may often be distinguished from a fool by the composed or disordered carriage of the body.

We saw above, at 6. 13. and 10. 20., that signs of wickedness are discerned from the bearing and gesticulation of the body. That very thing is taught more fully here and illustrated by its contrary: namely, that the signification of godliness and wisdom lies in the face itself and in the other parts of the body when they are governed in an orderly and composed manner.

But before we proceed further, a knot must be untied which a passage of the Prophet seems to bind for us and, as it were, to lay a hand upon us, lest we judge anyone from the appearance of his face. For it is affirmed of Christ the Lord that he would pronounce sentence neither by the sight of his eyes nor by the hearing of his ears, Esa. 11. 3. To this is added that Samuel was rebuked by God because from outward appearance he would judge concerning a fitting choice of king, 1 Sam. 16. 6, 7. Reply. Christ, κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν (*pre-eminently*), has no need of the aid of the senses for investigating anything; and for that reason what is pronounced concerning Christ must not be twisted against us. Further, although it is not right to judge anyone's worth and virtue from a single glance, as Samuel did, nevertheless at least a probable judgement may be formed from prolonged observation of his face and gestures.

What Solomon says in this place is therefore confirmed elsewhere, where, admiring the advantages of wisdom, he declares that wisdom lights up and renders illustrious the face of a man, and that by it the firmness of his face is doubled, Eccl. 8. 1. A brilliant example of this is found in Job, who bore such gravity in his face that even when he laughed, those who were accustomed to be with him did not think that he had laughed or jested, Job 29. 24.

What is said about the face of the intelligent man ought to be applied to the other members of the body. For when account is taken of the manner and composition of the face, and it is to be brought into conformity with the rule of wisdom and godliness, what is so slight and contemptible in the whole course of human life that it is exempted from the government of that same wisdom? It should therefore not seem strange if the hair is brought under this rule. For although human laws do not concern themselves with trifles, nevertheless nothing is so minute and light that it will not be arraigned before God's tribunal.

This is further confirmed from the Opposite, when he says that the eyes of the fool run to the end of the earth. By this expression he seems first to censure the fool's inconstancy, while he wanders uncertainly to and fro in his mind and has nothing certain to follow: now he directs his mind to this business, then, affected by weariness of it, he flees to another, and like the Euripus fluctuates in all his counsels. The fool persuades himself that in his undertakings alone he will be of the greatest benefit to himself; yet wherever he goes, his own folly follows hard upon his heels. He does not change his mind when he has changed the sky.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 366 | printed columns 706-707]

Then by this form of speech he seems to expose the fool's ignorance in this: that, scorning the things which are before his feet and within his power, he turns himself toward things that exceed his capacity and strength. For the sight of the eyes is altogether unable to penetrate to the furthest parts of the earth. This is what we say in the Proverb: he builds citadels in the air. He buildeth Castles in the ayr.

V. 25. A foolish son is a vexation to his father, and bitterness to his mother.

In ממר (bitterness) there is a metaphor transferred from bitter foods to grievous torments of the mind.

As was said at 16. 25., one and the same Proverb is repeated in precisely the same words so that the excellence and extraordinary use of the Proverb may be indicated; the same judgement must also be made concerning the repetition of this Proverb. To this is added that the Holy Spirit, foreseeing that men's failure in obedience to parents would be frequent, and that parents would show foolish indulgence in the education of children, judged that by this reiteration of the Proverb this evil must be opposed.

Yet there seems to be an amplification in this Proverb which is not found in the preceding Proverbs. For there is more in the words כעס (vexation) and ממר (bitterness) than in the earlier terms by which he signified pain, grief, or the want and deprivation of joy.

Those who lack children are hereby admonished to bear more equably either sterility or the loss of the children whom they begot. But those whom God affects with this benefit are admonished to devote themselves with great care and thought to the godly education of their children, by which means alone they may turn aside and escape this evil.

Moreover, when bitterness is attributed to the mother—a word by which a greater severity of pain is designated than by the word vexation, which is assigned to the father—the most just judgement of God shines forth, whereby she who more greatly corrupts her children by her indulgence is made to bleed more.

Finally, the chief reason for this repetition, as for that Proverb at 16. 25., seems to be that the Proverb immediately following may illustrate it by its connection and comparison, as will be perceived in the explanation of that Proverb.

V. 26. Even to fine a righteous man is not good, nor to strike the noble contrary to what is right.

The summary is: it is a thing supremely displeasing to God to punish the innocent as though guilty.

It is established in the common sense of all that it is a thing exceedingly hateful to God and men when children, ungrateful toward their parents, not only fail to repay the duties of piety and fail to follow up with reciprocal offices of goodwill the benefits their parents have bestowed upon them, but even repay their parents' bounty toward them with evil deeds and crime. Although all men whose disposition is not brutish and beastly willingly acknowledge this, yet the parents themselves who have suffered this injury are touched by a certain more inward and tenderer perception of it. Surely it is no less hateful and hostile to God for parents—whether those placed over their own children at home, or those ruling over citizens in the Republic, toward whom they ought to put on the mind of a parent—to afflict the innocent with penalty and punishment.

The Apostle teaches that those who are established in authority are not a terror to those who do well, but to those who do evil, and that there is one way by which men may free themselves from fear of the Magistrate: if they do well, Ro. 13. 3. But when the godly and righteous are punished, God's purpose in establishing government among men is utterly shaken and overthrown. Furthermore, for you (he says)

[1632 witness | PDF p. 367 | printed columns 708-709]

to you who do well, the magistrate has been ordained for your good. Therefore the man who is afflicted with evil on account of justice is turned into a plague, though he ought to procure the welfare of the people. Hence it is that Solomon counts it vanity that he had seen what belonged to the upright befall him to whom belonged what was due to the wicked, and conversely, Eccles. 8 14. It is a grievous thing not to receive the reward of justice and of deeds rightly done, whether that reward be praise or some other recompense owed to virtue and piety. But to be punished instead of rewarded is much more grievous still and abhorrent to all justice. For so it will come to pass that zeal for justice, blunted by this injustice, will languish; indeed, it will often turn aside into wickedness. Nor, indeed, will the harm of this matter come only to that man, or remain in his person, but it will spread itself to very many. For both the hands of the wicked will be strengthened to perpetrate crimes, and the zeal of the just in pursuing innocence will waver.

It is an injustice, and it incites others to commit injustice, when the just reward of virtue is not rendered in due time and promptly; much more when punishment is received as wages in place of reward, Eccl. 8. 11.

Who is so made of iron that he would add spurs to a horse running of its own accord, and that at greatest speed, or add a whip; or would beat with a stick the keenest-scented dogs that serve the hunt exactly as desired? If this should indeed seem absurd, it is much more discordant and alien to punish one who devotes his labor to justice.

Yet here the thought of the reward someday to be rendered by God ought to hold us in our duty and in zeal for justice, however unjustly we may have been treated by men.

V. 27. He who restrains his words knows knowledge (is thoroughly skilled), and a man cold in spirit is a man of understanding.

In קר רוח (*cold in spirit*) there is a metaphor from cold waters, which in comparison with boiling waters are placid and quiet.

The sum is: moderation of the tongue flowing from a settled and placid spirit supplies a splendid token of wisdom.

Sparing use of the tongue is enjoined universally. For what is said in Eccles. 5 is enjoined in the worship of God, that [ words be few, because just as dreams arise from the multitude of dealings, so from many words comes foolish speech; ] but it also extends more widely, and reaches even those conversations which are held back and forth with men. And the command in James 1 appears by its compass and embrace to comprehend every kind of speech.

Hence also arose the proverb common in ordinary speech, that empty vessels, or jars, resound most, but that those filled with wine or wholesome liquor make no noise. Now, as this dutiful silence is praiseworthy in all circumstances, so it shines forth especially when someone, an occasion having been furnished by an injury or an insult offered to him, passes it over in silence, or at least does not pursue it with that sharpness of words.

And so that the Holy Spirit might spur us on to this duty, he extols it with the highest praises and attributes it to a certain excellent and singular knowledge and prudence. For when he says that such a man knows knowledge and is a man of understanding, he establishes this moderation of the tongue in a very lofty degree of wisdom. All strive that they may become celebrated and renowned for a reputation of wisdom. Why, then, should we not labor, when provoked and incited by injury, to restrain the tongue from bitterness? But if we wish to commit ourselves to discipline in this matter, behold David, who opens a school for instruction in it, Psalm 39. There the first lesson is that, following his example, we ought to settle this with our own souls. For thus he speaks: *I said*, that is, I resolved it wholly in my mind. For this is the beginning of doing well. See Acts 11. There are many who are ashamed to be regarded as placid and gentle. Indeed, they would rather be distinguished as hired killers

[1632 witness | PDF p. 368 | printed columns 710-711]

The second lesson is that he resolved to do this throughout the entire course of his life. The third is that he decreed to do it and was unwilling to interrupt his patience, even if he should be harassed a thousand times by injuries from the wicked. The fourth is that he would apply a halter to his mouth and tongue, by which the tongue's wantonness would be constrained and its ferocity tamed; that is, although it would be grievous and troublesome to repress his tongue, he nevertheless would not desist from the silence he had purposed for himself. Next he teaches what he accomplished, namely, that he restrained his tongue even when he could have composed an apology with the most just defense. See examples of this in Psalm 38. 13. 14. 15, where he professes what was done by him repeatedly. But a more special and most lucid example is found in 2 Sam. 16. 5. 6. 7.

He teaches, moreover, that the source and wellspring of so great a wisdom is a cold spirit, that is, one which does not easily grow hot with anger. For when we are inflamed by anger, we feel within our entrails a heat and warmth that are unusual and in no way natural. Hence also the Prophet in Psalm 39 teaches that, after he had grown hot and the fire within had begun, as it were, to burn, he departed a little from his proposed patience and silence and burst out in rash and ill-considered words, for which he begs pardon. Therefore, whoever is not easily provoked to anger, being of a placid and composed spirit, is the man who reaches the summit of this excellent virtue. In this class, apart from Christ, who like a lamb silent before its shearers endured infinite injuries, we find that Moses, who is described as the meekest and gentlest of all men, belonged. Num. 12. For when the mind has once been thus inflamed, insults at once break out, as Christ teaches in Matt. 5. Anger, indeed, is a heat of the soul and an evil master of speech. For just as in a flood of waters everything is carried headlong, so in anger words are hurled out rashly and confusedly, as though by a violent impulse. Hence elsewhere those who cannot restrain their Spirit are compared to a city without a wall. Prov. 25. last verse.

But here someone may object that certain people are so constituted by nature that they endure injuries more placidly and do not so quickly blaze up, whereas he himself is so evil and so constituted that he cannot bridle and restrain his own movements. I do not deny, indeed, that some by nature and bodily constitution are more inclined and susceptible to anger. But since this nature is vicious, it must be restrained and subdued by us; and we must strive all the more to resist this refractory nature, nor will God's grace fail us as we exert ourselves and contend, James 2. 4. 5. 6.

Then, since Solomon speaks here of knowledge and understanding sent down from heaven, there are very many men who are excluded from this grace. For those who are choleric are just as much participants in it as those who are phlegmatic. This will appear more clearly if we consider from David the cause of a placid and cold spirit. For David in Psalm 38. 16, giving the cause of his patience and placidity—why he was deaf and dumb when the wicked afflicted him with harm—establishes it as this: that he waited and looked for God to hear him. It must therefore be attributed to unbelief that men so prone to irritation leap immediately to vengeance and insults. For a man's heart purified by faith would quench those rages and would certainly cool greatly the soul's seething motions.

Here we see what the Holy Spirit requires of his own: that those affected by injury abstain not only from evil deeds but also from insults and reproaches.

V. 28. Even a fool who keeps silence will be reputed wise; he who closes his lips, prudent.

This is a confirmation of the preceding Proverb by a comparison from the lesser, whether generally and ἁπλῶς, or εἰδικῶς, concerning which see Job 15. 5. For he says, *even a fool is taken for a wise man*, and so forth. In this Proverb there appears to be a prolepsis whose tacit anticipation is arranged from an adjunct in this way: But if, after receiving an injury, I repay it neither with words nor with blows, I shall be held to be a brute, a drone, a fungus, and a dolt. The reply is from a comparison with the greater: not only will you by no means be held a fool, but further, even a fool—or a wicked man—who does not avenge himself will be reputed wise and understanding. For after Tamar had been violated by Amnon, who would not have thought Absalom a good man so long as he concealed the injury inflicted upon him in the person of his sister? 2 Sam. 13. Whatever judgment, therefore, the wicked pass concerning connivance at an injury received, and however they charge it to folly or madness, this nevertheless is the judgment of God, the Angels, and godly men: that he is among the wise who pursues injuries done to him neither in deed nor in words. Come, therefore, the choice between these two is offered: whether you wish to be regarded as wise by God, the holy Angels, and the choir of the godly, or by wicked men who are hateful to God and the Angels.

But at this point another difficulty arises which makes us slower in this obedience to God; it is tacitly indicated by Peter and resolved by the same man. For it enters our minds that by this meekness we are exposed and given over to the injuries of men, who will set no end or measure to inflicting injury so long as they are permitted to do it with impunity. And *who* (he says) *will afflict you, if you are imitators of what is good?* 1 Pet. 3. 13. And he splendidly confirms this saying from the Psalm. There he teaches that the greatest expedient for quietness of life is to restrain your tongue, seek peace, and pursue it when it flees. Psalm 34. 13. A splendid example of this exists in the Syrians, who, having been treated humanely by the King of Israel, were so melted by that kindness that thereafter they ceased for a long time from making war. 2 Kings 6.

Nothing, however, prevents these last two Proverbs of this chapter from being referred to the just and due silence required in every matter, and to coldness of mind in every duty. For we are taught by this proverb that there is a settled state of souls which restrains all sudden motions and disturbances of the mind: anger just as much as love, joy just as much as grief, fear, and so forth. At the same time we are forbidden, when driven by these motions of the mind, to blurt out anything rashly, whether in praise or in blame, whether it is to testify to our joy or to disclose our sorrow, and so forth. But the silence of a fool is most of all praiseworthy when, conscious of his weakness and ignorance, he keeps silent so that he may be instructed by others who excel in wisdom. But if he is silent with another purpose, so that by silence he may not betray his foolishness, then it is not to be attributed to wisdom.
