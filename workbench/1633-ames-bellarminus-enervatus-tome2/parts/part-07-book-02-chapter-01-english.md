BOOK II.

ON THE CHURCH.

CHAPTER I. On the definition of the Church.

Bellarmine 1. *The first question concerns the name and definition of the
Church militant.*

Protestants. Two grave and fundamental faults occur here at the outset,
whether from negligence or from fraud, con—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n254 | printed p. 37]

—trary to the laws of sound disputation. I. Bellarmine undertakes to treat a
part of the Church, namely, the militant Church, and yet teaches absolutely
nothing about that whole Church whose part he calls militant. He certainly
gains this by this device: inexperienced readers fail to notice when he
ascribes to the Church militant either things that belong to the whole and not
to this part, or even things that are altogether foreign to the common nature
of the Church and therefore to the militant Church. These, however, are like
the chief sources from which Bellarmine draws both his opinions and his
arguments. But that whole Church, of which the Church militant is a part,
*comprises all the faithful from Abel to the consummation of the world,* says
Cano in Bellarmine, chapter 3. It *is the mystical body of Christ, consisting
of all the faithful from the beginning of the world to its end,* according to
the definition of Thomas Aquinas, part 3, question 8, article 3. The second
fault committed here by Bellarmine is that he omits a proper division of that
Church whose parts he wishes to explain. According to the Scriptures, the
Church has two parts, militant and triumphant. The Pontificians, however, have
sewn on a third part, teaching that the Church is threefold: *triumphant,
sleeping, and militant.* Thus Cardinal Cusanus, *On Catholic Concordance*, book
1, chapter 4; or, as Báñez has it in 2a2ae, question 1, article 10,
*triumphant, militant, and purgative.* Bellarmine treats those three parts in
their order, that is, the Church on earth, in Purgatory, and in heaven; but he
thought it most prudent to pass over that three-membered division in silence,
although both the preceding definition and some division of this sort are
plainly necessary for understanding the questions about the Church distinctly
and with certainty.

2. Bellarmine. *The first opinion of the heretics is that the Church is the
congregation of the predestined. Another is that the Church is the
congregation of the saints who truly believe and obey God. The third is that
the Church consists only of the predestined righteous.*

Protestants. I. These are not three opinions, but one and the same opinion
explained with some variety of words. II. This one opinion cannot be heretical
unless, in addition to Scripture, not only the ancient Fathers but also Thomas
Aquinas and Pope Nicholas himself are called heretics; for in the chapter
*Ecclesia*, *On Consecration*, distinction 1, Nicholas defined the Church as
*the collection of the faithful*.

3. Bellarmine. *The Confessionists and Calvin teach that there are two
Churches: the true Church, which is the congregation of the holy faithful, and
the external Church, which is the Church in name only, in which the good and
the evil are found; but they teach that the evil are in the Church, not of the
Church.*

Protestants. It is a manifest slander that the fiction of two militant
Churches is imputed to them. They do not divide the Church militant into two
species, but distinguish and set forth two respects or considerations of one
and the same Church—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n255 | printed p. 38]

—one with respect to its inward essence and the other with respect to its
outward mode of existence. This is exactly what Bellarmine himself reports
from Augustine: *In the Church there is an inward soul, namely, the gifts of
grace, and a body, or external profession. Hence some belong both to the soul
and to the body of the Church, while others belong to the body but not to the
soul.* Alexander of Hales explains the same thing in another way, part 3,
member 3, article 3: *The evil indeed belong to the unity of the Church, but
not to the unity of the Church’s body. They do not belong to the body of the
Church according to the proper nature of a body, or with respect to the union
of the mystical body.* Finally, this is the same thing that the Apostle John
teaches in 1 John 2:19: *They went out from us, but they were not of us.* It is
exactly the Apostle Paul’s distinction in Romans 2:28–29 between *the Jew
outwardly and the Jew in secret; the Jew in the letter and the Jew in the
Spirit; the one whose praise is from men and the one whose praise is from
God.* Bellarmine himself also says in chapter 9 that in Augustine’s time the
Donatists slandered the orthodox in the same way as the Pontificians slander
us, and that they received the same reply from the orthodox: *When the
Donatists slanderously charged the Catholics with making two Churches, one
which contained only the good and another which contained the good together
with the evil, the Catholics replied that they had never dreamed of two
Churches, but only distinguished parts or times of the Church, and so forth.
Augustine says that the good are in the house of God, which is the Church, in
such a way that they themselves are the house of God constructed from living
stones; the evil are in the house in such a way that they themselves are
nevertheless not the house.*

4. Bellarmine. *Our opinion is that the Church is a body of men bound together
by profession of the same Christian faith and communion in the same
sacraments, under the government of legitimate pastors and especially of the
one Vicar of Christ on earth, the Roman Pontiff.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine himself immediately admits in express words that
this definition does not explain the essence of the Church. *Our definition*,
he says, *comprises only this last mode of existing in the Church.* What that
mode of existing in the Church is like, he declares in these words: *like
hairs, or nails, or diseased humors in the human body.* Bellarmine therefore
acts in defining the Church just as if someone were to define a human being
solely by the things found in hair, nails, or diseased humors. II. That final
element, subjection to the Roman Pontiff, cannot by any reasoning be proved
necessary, not even for the Church’s hairs, nails, and diseased humors;
although we readily admit that the Pope’s government pertains to such a
Church.

5. Bellarmine. *The difference between our opinion and the others is that all
the others require inward virtues to constitute someone in the Church,
whereas we do not think any inward virtue is required for someone to be able
in some manner to be called a part of the true Church of which Scripture
speaks, and so forth. For the Church is a body of men as visible and palpable
as the body of the Roman people, the Kingdom of France, or the Republic of
Venice.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n256 | printed p. 39]

Protestants. I. This distinction is established sophistically. For in the
first part he relates what others require *to constitute someone in the
Church*, but in the second he teaches what he and his followers require *for
someone to be able to be called in some manner a part of the true Church*.
The distinction therefore does not refer to one and the same thing. II. It is
false that we require inward virtues for someone to be *in the Church* with
respect to its visible state. III. Inward virtues are necessarily required
for someone truly and properly to be called a part of that Church which in
Ephesians 4:16 is said to *possess a power acting inwardly*. IV. If the Roman
Church Bellarmine defined is so palpable, then it is not that Church which we
believe by faith, which concerns *things that are not seen*, Hebrews 11:1, and
which the Apostle calls *a great mystery*, Ephesians 5:32.

6. Bellarmine. *The unbaptized, heretics, apostates, schismatics, and the
excommunicated are not of the Church.*

Protestants. I. These things do not properly pertain to the question. II. As
to heretics and apostates in the proper and full sense, we agree. As for the
infant children of the faithful and believers not yet baptized, what is
affirmed is so manifestly false that if such people were not to be regarded as
members of the Church, they ought not to be baptized. For baptism by its
nature is the seal of an engrafting already accomplished into Christ, and
therefore into the Church, Acts 10:47–48. Schismatics can belong to that Church
from which they have not separated themselves. Those who have been
excommunicated from a particular church can be and remain true members of the
Church militant.

7. Bellarmine. *The reprobate can belong to the Church.*

Protestants. This cannot be denied if it is understood according to the
outward aspect of the Church militant, *as hairs, nails, or diseased humors
belong to the human body*, which is the explanation Bellarmine gave us earlier
in chapter 2; or *as the Roman city is said to be filled with many who are not
citizens*, which is Bellarmine’s illustration in this very chapter 7. Thus far,
therefore, we have had no question established.

8. Bellarmine. *Great and manifest sinners are in the Church.*

Protestants. But we also concede this in the same sense as the foregoing
statement; that is, in the way Bellarmine admits in this very chapter 9 that
John of Torquemada, Alexander of Hales, Hugh, Thomas, Peter de Soto, Cano, and
others explain it: namely, that sinners of this kind *are not true members,
nor members of the Church’s body simply, but only in a qualified and equivocal
sense.* Or again in the way Bellarmine interprets himself: *They are not
living members of Christ’s body. They pertain to the Church’s outward man, but
not to its inward man. They are not in the Church in the manner in which the
good—*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n257 | printed p. 40]

*—are. They are dead and withered members which adhere to the rest only by an
external conjunction. They belong to the kingdom of God in such a way that
they belong at the same time to the kingdom of the Devil.*

9. Bellarmine. *Secret unbelievers belong to the Church.*

Protestants. Nor do we dissent from Bellarmine about them, when he explains
his opinion in this chapter 10 as follows: *They are outward parts, withered
and dead members. They are not in the Church in the manner in which they seem
to be. They are not united except by an external conjunction. They are not of
the Church except according to appearance and putatively, not truly.* Thus
this whole disputation, completed with great effort over ten chapters, has no
certain question at all, or any controversy proper to it around which it
turns.

10. The question of which Bellarmine presented us a kind of shadow, wrapped as
it were in a dense cloud of various sophisms, is whether those magnificent
things said in the Scriptures about the Church of Christ—that it is *redeemed
by Christ, the body of Christ, the bride of Christ, given life, increased, and
guided by the Holy Spirit, a participant in all spiritual blessings, so that
the gates of hell will not prevail against it*—the question, I say, is whether
these and similar things properly and fully belong to the whole multitude
professing the Christian faith and subject to the Roman Pontiff, or only to
true believers; or whether they belong to the Church militant according to
its essential nature, which is proper to true believers, or according to that
outward mode of existence in which Bellarmine admits *the reprobate, the
ungodly, and the wicked* are included. The truth of this question is so clear
that Bellarmine could not treat it with any semblance of plausibility.
