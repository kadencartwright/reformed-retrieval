ARTICLE FOUR

ON THE MANNER OF CONVERSION

### Chapter I

#### The state of the question is elicited.

The fourth thesis reads as follows:

THAT this grace of God is the beginning, progress, and perfection of every
good, and indeed to such an extent that even the regenerate man, without this
prevenient or assisting, awakening, consequent, and cooperating grace, can
neither think, will, or do anything good, nor resist any evil temptation; and
indeed to such an extent that all good works which we can conceive must be
attributed to God’s grace in Christ. But as for the manner in which that grace
cooperates, it is not irresistible, for many are said to have resisted the
Holy Spirit, Acts 7 and in many other passages.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 187 | printed p. 177]

Although the former part of this thesis contains certain things which cannot
be reconciled with the Remonstrants’ doctrines, as will become clear from the
discussion that follows, I shall not raise any dispute about it at present.
We do have a controversy framed by them concerning the latter part, in these
words: “Does the grace by which the beginning, progress, and perfection of
all good actions—such as faith, conversion, and so forth—is produced in a
person operate irresistibly in him, or otherwise, that is, resistibly?”

We cannot understand this issue clearly, however, unless the nature of
resistance is first explained. For the Remonstrants wish to locate the very
heart of the whole controversy in this term, which our men customarily reject
as foreign to this mystery.

Resistance is a struggle, or the recipient’s contrary action against the
agent, by which he hinders or repels the agent’s action to the extent of his
ability. Philosophers commonly call it reaction, counteraction, or the
reciprocation of action. In every reaction, the forces exerted by the agents
are either equal or unequal. If they are equal, nothing is accomplished
beyond a futile struggle. If they are unequal, then either the agent prevails
and conquers, encountering some resistance, but an unequal and incomplete
one, so that what was opposing him finally gives way; or the recipient
prevails in acting in return and not only delays but wholly obstructs and
frustrates the agent’s endeavor. This latter resistance is prevailing and
complete. These statements made in general terms perhaps have some obscurity,
but when applied to the present issue they will receive light and also yield
a return not to be regretted.

God’s grace acts upon a person for his conversion. The person, pleased with
himself in his present condition, resists and tends in the opposite direction
from the inclination of the flesh. Now, if this opposition is altogether of
equal strength with the exerted power of grace, then conversion is neither
accomplished nor can it be expected to be accomplished, for an effect always
comes from the unequal force which prevails. But if the power of grace
exceeds it, then although some incomplete resistance is made by the flesh,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 188 | printed p. 178]

it is nevertheless conquered by grace, because grace acts more strongly. But
if the opposition is greater and the operation of grace is less, or acts less
powerfully, then the flesh overcomes it or renders it ineffectual. This is
prevailing and complete resistance; and this is the resistance to which the
Remonstrants maintain that God’s grace is liable when they contend that the
operation of grace is resistible. Our men readily acknowledge the former
opposition, which I called incomplete (pp. 217, 238). But the Remonstrants
expressly declare on p. 274 that this sense is foreign to the thesis. They
must therefore understand the latter, since they say that a person can oppose
grace in such a way that he “hinders and turns it aside” (p. 274). But to
hinder it in this way is plainly to overcome it. Unless the matter is being
conducted solely by persuasive reasons, the Remonstrants acknowledge that
this is nothing other than “one person’s force defeating another’s with a
greater force” (p. 276). Yet here they are unwilling to admit that, on their
view, God produces faith solely by persuasion; instead, they say that those
who present their position so unfairly are doing them an injury (p. 309).
Nevertheless, if God were persuading a person by reasons alone to will to
convert himself, the opposing obstacle in his resistance which turned aside
and overcame that persuasion would have to be greater and superior, at least
in the same kind. This needs no proof by reasoning, for the Remonstrants
themselves assert it (p. 310): “It cannot happen,” they say, “when two parties
contend to attract a person to themselves, that one does not act more
strongly than the other; otherwise neither would draw him to himself.”

However the matter stands regarding grace’s production or persuasion, if
grace is the proper and direct cause of conversion with respect to its
beginning, progress, and perfection, as this thesis appears to represent it,
then it brings its own force to the production of conversion and tends toward
the effect with causal force or power. Grace itself cannot properly be
resisted except by some opposing force which acts more strongly in the
opposite direction than grace acts to convert. The axiom, therefore, which
stands diametrically opposed to the Remonstrants’ thesis

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 189 | printed p. 179]

is this: “The operation of grace has an efficacy so powerful in the
conversion of human beings that its action cannot be overcome or wholly
hindered by a person.” The Remonstrants themselves acknowledge this in their
reply to argument 5, p. 312.

To make still clearer what resistance or resistibility the Remonstrants
defend, something must be said both about the actions which they attribute to
God in this matter and about the power of resisting which they leave to a
person in conversion. The name “efficient cause” designates in general that
from which some motion has its beginning. But because actions, or certain
motions, in some measure have their beginning from an occasion presented and
an object proposed, together with its advantageousness—although no effect
exists without a cause more real and immediate than these—there has arisen a
useful distinction which calls one cause *moral*. It bears the name “cause”
only improperly and metaphorically, because it directly and immediately
brings no force or power to producing what it wills and intends to
accomplish. Rather, it proposes arguments which another can use to advance
his own action, and it also awaits his will and determination so that by his
power the desired effect may follow. The action of this moral cause is
exercised only by proposing, persuading, counseling, and commanding. The
other cause, which alone properly deserves to be so called, is the principle
which produces its effect through its own true and real power of causing. In
the schools this is commonly called *physical*, *quasi-physical*, or
*effectual of itself*. By the same reasoning grace itself can and commonly is
distinguished into moral grace, which persuades us to do something good, and
physical grace, or grace effectual of itself, which itself brings about that
good work in us. Now it is entirely certain that the Remonstrants—whatever
they sometimes represent to the contrary—do not admit that real efficacy

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 190 | printed p. 180]

or efficiency of grace by which we believe that it acts in a real way upon
the will for the work of conversion. Instead, they locate in moral persuasion
everything which they say grace does as a principle for eliciting the will’s
free consent. Thus Nicolaas Grevinckhoven writes (p. 214): “Effectual aid does
not precede the will’s efficiency, not even in the order of causality, nor
does it act or exert influence upon the will before the will itself exerts
influence upon its own determination. God acts nothing upon the will on the
way to that consent, or as its cause.” And on p. 297: “Nothing prevents moral
grace alone from making natural people spiritual.”

He openly brings forward and professes what the Remonstrants thought it wiser
and safer to veil somewhat in the Colloquy. Yet they too saw fit to give some
indication of what they had decided among themselves by these forms of
speech, on p. 311: “It might also be disputed—as some do—whether the mode of
acting in a person which occurs by persuasion is the noblest here; whether it
would be fitting if the properties of human nature were preserved; and
therefore whether precisely the same action would be sufficient in execution
if it were like Satan’s. There would be no absurdity if God’s operation were
called effectual for this reason: that, in accordance with God’s infinite
knowledge, it is administered around that time to people of this sort in such
a condition.” And on p. 122: “Those who say nothing other than that drawing
is an operation by which our resisting will is so changed in fact by God that
he makes us willing from unwilling—do they think they have established
anything more than enticing grace? We do not think so, for even the unwilling
can be made willing by enticement alone. Unless, perhaps, those who are
converted remain unwilling and stubborn.”

These words indicate clearly enough that the Remonstrants attribute nothing
to grace with respect to conversion beyond moral persuasion, and therefore
that God’s grace is not the proper efficient cause of conversion. For they
themselves distinguish an efficient cause from a moral cause on p. 314. “The
efficacy

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 191 | printed p. 181]

of God’s power,” they say, “can be regarded either as an efficient cause of
our faith, which we reject here, or as the foundation upon which faith rests,”
that is, as they interpret it a little later, “as arousing faith in the manner
of an object.” They rightly note that an efficient cause must be distinguished
here from moral persuasion operating in the manner of an object.

But the Remonstrants do not concede even the highest degree of the moral
efficacy of the grace of calling—not the degree which Suárez himself, a
Jesuit and supreme enemy of irresistible grace, admits must be conceded in
*On the Aids of Grace*, book 3, chapter 20, section 11. There he teaches that
all who are called according to God’s purpose receive greater grace than the
rest: with respect to the character of the moral benefit and the free divine
good; with respect to the singular benevolence from which it proceeds; and
with respect to the infallible efficacy which it possesses, at least from the
suitability of its administration. The Remonstrants reject this infallible
calling no less than irresistible calling itself in the *Colloquy*, p. 277.
Indeed, they have given us just cause to doubt whether they acknowledge that
any grace is necessary for a person’s conversion beyond the bare external
presentation of the word; and one may safely assert this at least about some
of them. For Episcopius, as I noted above, vigorously contends in his
*Disputation on the Perspicuity of Scripture*, theses 1 and 3, that anyone,
provided he possesses the use of reason, can most easily understand and
perceive, without any peculiar immediate or inward illumination of light,
every meaning of Scripture necessary for salvation, whether to be known,
believed, hoped for, or done. But when those meanings are perceived in the
mind, they are most easily shown to the will; and the will, according to their
position, has by nature the free and inseparable faculty of choosing any
object shown by the intellect (Nicolaas Grevinckhoven, p. 299; *Colloquy*, p.
298). They therefore think that nothing is necessary for a person’s
conversion beyond the external presentation of the word. That this is
Episcopius’s genuine position

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 192 | printed p. 182]

is perfectly clear from his private theses, where he attaches this corollary
to disputation 46: “Is any immediate action of the Spirit upon the will or
the mind necessary, or promised in Scripture, in order that someone may be
able to believe the word outwardly presented? We shall defend the negative.”
Any credit for this corollary, however—if it has any—belongs not so much to
Episcopius as to Faustus Socinus. In his *Catechism*, chapter 6, p. 212, the
same position is dictated in nearly the same words: “Is an inward gift of the
Holy Spirit not necessary for believing the gospel? By no means, for we do
not read in Holy Scripture that this gift is conferred on anyone except one
who believes.”

Now if this is all that they understand by the grace of conversion, no one
should wonder that they deny that it ought to be called irresistible. It will
nevertheless be worthwhile to hear how, and to what extent, they teach that
the operation of grace is resistible. They place the principal, if not the
only, act of prevenient grace in the illumination of the mind or intellect;
but here they will allow resistibility no place, for they teach in the
*Colloquy*, p. 275, that knowledge is engendered in people by a plainly
irresistible power. They teach that the second stage of calling appears in
the affections, which they say are affected by a certain sense of the divine
knocking, and are even healed and renewed; indeed, that new affections are
inspired and kindled to desire supernatural goods. Whatever this may be, they
concede that it too is accomplished irresistibly (*ibid.*; Nicolaas
Grevinckhoven, pp. 292, 300; Johannes Arnoldi against Bogerman, pp. 255, 277).
I said “whatever this may be” because I can scarcely understand what they
mean by these affections healed, reformed, and kindled. I had thought that
there were no affections which could be restored in this way either before
or without the will. If they include the will itself, then they acknowledge
that the person’s whole conversion is irresistible. To untie this knot,
Johannes Arnoldi, against Bogerman, pp. 229, 255, and 277, wishes to locate
these affections

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 193 | printed p. 183]

in the will itself, introducing a distinction within the same will between
its mode of affection and its mode of freedom. For my part, I do not
understand what he means by this. By its nature the will is nothing other
than affection, or rational or intellectual appetite; and insofar as it is
such an affection, it is also free. Therefore, one who distinguishes between
the mode of affection and the mode of freedom in the will seems to me to act
just as if he made two modes out of an affection and its mode. I am not
unaware that there are certain sudden motions of the will which philosophers
call “first-first,” because they precede every use of reason, and
“second-first,” because they precede due consideration. But these are not the
affections which the Remonstrants mean, for they teach that a proper
illumination of the mind precedes these affections and produces them in an
orderly way. Nor are those transitory motions subjects capable of the
renewal and healing which they attribute to these affections.

The same Johannes Arnoldi seems to propose some explanation of this
difficulty on pp. 258, 271, and 277, where he teaches that the irresistible
action of grace concerns only the mind’s passive potency, not its active
potency. But this does not agree well enough with what he set down before. If
grace irresistibly produces intellection in the mind and affections in the
will, it also causes acts to be elicited in this way. For anyone who says that
intellection, desire, and awakened motions are mere passions can be refuted
more than sufficiently by the common sense of all human beings. I therefore
confess both that I cannot grasp what they mean by these affections awakened,
renewed, healed, and kindled toward spiritual good without any consent of the
will, and that I do not believe they themselves know. But whatever it may be,
they say that the whole of it is accomplished by irresistible power.

There remains, therefore, the will’s bare consent alone, in which the
resistibility of grace can have a place.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 194 | printed p. 184]

But 1. this actual consent is not required beforehand for a person’s
regeneration, if the things they concede are true and not mere words. For
they relate that through the prior acts, which they acknowledge to be
irresistible, the intellect and affections are brought to life, while the
will is also imbued with new affections and endowed with new powers
(*Colloquy*, p. 300; Johannes Arnoldi against Bogerman, p. 257). Just as human
offspring is begotten when it first becomes a participant in life, even
though it cannot yet exercise its acts, so the person who has been spiritually
brought to life in mind and affections and has received new powers in the
will together with new affections must be said to have been regenerated and
made a son of God, even though he has not yet exercised this required act.
Indeed, he deserves the name “regenerate” much more than the other deserves
the name “begotten,” because the latter cannot yet perform human operations,
whereas the former possesses a present capacity to perform Christian ones.
What is more, the very essence of regeneration consists in the reformation
of evil affections and the inspiration of good ones, through which his mind
is borne toward God with love, desire, and reverence, though previously the
whole formation of his heart was only toward evil (Deut. 30:6; Jer. 32:39–40).
Consequently, if this reformation and inspiration occurs before that actual
consent of the will, and does so by irresistible force, then regeneration
itself, with respect to its inmost essence, must also occur irresistibly
before that consent.

2. If the will’s consent is required for this effect, yet it follows
infallibly and certainly, if not irresistibly, upon that illumination of the
intellect by which a judgment of particular approval is elicited concerning
the good proposed, then its freedom of indifference appears already to have
been removed by the antecedent acts.

3. Although the things which grace has already accomplished in the mind and
affections contribute in the greatest degree toward arousing this consent of
the will, grace does not properly cause the consent itself, I say, according
to the Remonstrants’ doctrines, but

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 195 | printed p. 185]

awaits it from the person’s choice. Grace does entice and invite him to
consent, but leaves the consent itself within his free and indifferent power.
Thus Johannes Arnoldi writes against Bogerman on p. 263: “Once all the
operations which God uses to effect conversion in us have been posited,
conversion itself nevertheless remains so within our power that we can fail
to be converted.” Here it must be carefully observed that even Arnold himself
does not number the accompanying act of grace, by which it concurs with the
will in the work, among those operations God uses to effect conversion. For
once this concurrence has actually been posited, it is not within a person’s
power to fail to be converted, since conversion itself is presupposed as a
present act. Arnold therefore deceives and trifles on p. 274 when he pretends
that God causes consent in the will through this concurrence. The others also
deceive when they affirm the same thing. For concurrence toward the will’s
effect does not possess the character of an efficient cause or principle
with respect to the will or to the will’s influence upon that same effect.
Therefore, when this consent, or the will’s own endeavor in consenting, is
present, it is not a proper effect of grace; and when the same consent is
prevented, it likewise cannot properly be said that grace is resisted or that
its operation is resistible in this failure. For resistance properly belongs
to the recipient while he strives against the agent, and hence is also called
reaction, counteraction, and reciprocation of action by learned men.

Johannes Arnoldi meets these points in his work against Bogerman, p. 274, and
observes that resistance is offered not only to one who acts, but also to one
who intends; that one who is prevented from acting is not the only one
hindered, but also one who is opposed in such a way that no effect follows
his action; and that grace is called resistible on this account, because when
a person has received from grace the powers to convert himself, he can abuse
those powers by failing to convert himself. But 1. it has already been shown
that resistance is properly offered only to one acting, not to one intending;
and this is clear from the fact that effects are intended for a long time

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 196 | printed p. 186]

before anything is done to produce them. Throughout that interval they are
not said to be hindered, but only when at last something is done which
something else obstructs. 2. On the Remonstrants’ position, God does not
absolutely intend a person’s conversion unless the concurrence of that
person’s will has been posited; and this concurrence is required beforehand
in order that God should absolutely will the effect of conversion. Nicolaas
Grevinckhoven, p. 177. A conditional intention, however, is something held in
suspense upon the awaited outcome, so that meanwhile it does not act and thus
cannot properly suffer resistance. 3. Consent is not the proper and direct
effect of the antecedent action of grace, if what they say is true. For they
teach that, once grace’s own proper production is complete, consent lies in
the power of another, so that it follows or is halted according to his choice.

Thus, even if the account of converting which the Remonstrants defend is
conceded, the proper seat of this resistance appears nowhere in any part of
conversion. Yet at last we have a sufficient explanation of what they mean by
this improper phrase: “Once all the operations of grace which God uses to
effect conversion in us have been posited, conversion itself nevertheless
remains so freely within our power that we can fail to be converted”—that is,
we can either convert ourselves or not convert ourselves. These are the words
of Johannes Arnoldi against Bogerman, pp. 263, 274. From these words, framed
precisely, emphatically, and deliberately to explain the resistibility of
grace, it follows irresistibly—as I intimated before—that grace is not the
proper and immediate cause of this conversion except with respect to its bare
possibility. For logic and the law of nature dictate that once all the
operations of a proper and immediate cause, whatever it may be, have been
posited, the effect always follows—or, rather, exists at the same time.

The questions which emerge from the preceding inquiry

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 197 | printed p. 187]

are therefore these. 1. Is the grace of calling, or the entire operation of
God which he uses to convert a person, only moral—persuading, proposing,
inviting, and so forth—or is it also real and possessed of proper efficiency?
2. Is there no action of the Spirit acting immediately upon the mind and will
by which he causes us to believe, but is the presentation of the things to be
believed alone sufficient to engender or elicit faith? 3. Is the mode of
operation, with respect to the efficacy of grace, not the same in bringing
the intellect, affections, and will to life; rather, are the intellect and
affections brought to life irresistibly, but the will in a resistible manner?
4. Is the will ever renewed with respect to its mode of affection in such a
way that it remains wholly unregenerate with respect to its mode of freedom?
5. Are no powers given to the will for its conversion beyond the illumination
of the mind and the awakening of the affections? 6. Beyond the illumination
of the mind, the awakening of the affections, and the invitation of the will,
does grace do nothing as a principle or antecedently to conversion, but only
accompany the will when it is already consenting? 7. Once all the operations
which God uses to effect conversion in human beings have been posited, does
conversion ever, or often, fail to follow? 8. When conversion follows, does
it follow from God’s intention and action neither by any necessity of the
cause nor by the infallibility of the outcome, but only contingently and
uncertainly, according as the indifferent human will pleases to incline
itself? What the Remonstrants maintain on these questions has been
sufficiently declared above.
