QUESTION XXIV.

ON THE RIGHT OF CALLING.

Does the right of electing and calling pastors belong to bishops alone, or to
the whole church? We deny the former and affirm the latter against the
Romanists.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

I. In order to grasp correctly the state of the question concerning the right
of calling pastors, which the Romanists raise against us here, observe first
that the question does not concern the primary Author of the calling, to whom
the right belongs properly and sovereignly and from whom it is derived to
others. For everyone acknowledges that this belongs to God alone, who calls
people to the ministry and, as Lord and Master of the household, sends laborers
into his harvest. Rather, the question concerns the instruments whom he wills
to use in this work and upon whom he confers this power. Are they only the
bishops and rulers of the church, to the exclusion of the people, as the
Romanists contend? Or is the instrument the whole church, that is, the body and
multitude of the faithful, as we maintain?

II. Second, the question is not whether pastors and bishops possess the right
of election and calling and may exercise it, which we do not deny. It is
whether they possess it from themselves primarily and originally, or instead
secondarily and derivatively from the church; and whether they exercise it in
their own name or in the name of the church which granted them this authority.
We assert the latter.

III. Three things must be considered especially in a calling: (1) the right of
calling; (2) the act or exercise of the calling itself; and (3) the rites and
ceremonies customarily observed in a calling with respect to ordination and the
laying on of hands. The present question properly concerns neither the act nor
the rites of calling, for no one denies that these two must be performed by the
presbytery and the rulers of the church. It concerns the right and power and in
whom these reside: in the bishops and the pope, or in the whole church?

IV. The Romanists, who maintain that the bishop is distinct from the presbyter
and that by divine right, strip not only the church, or assembly of believers,
of every right to call pastors, but the presbyters themselves as well.
Consequently, they transfer that right to bishops alone who descend in an
uninterrupted succession, and especially to the Roman pontiff as the ecumenical
bishop of the whole church. Thus the Council of Trent decrees in chapter 4 on
the sacrament of orders: “The holy Synod teaches that in the ordination of
bishops, priests, and the other orders, neither the consent nor the calling and
authority of the people or of any secular power and magistrate is required in
such a way that the ordination is void without it. On the contrary, it decrees
that all who rise to these ministries when called and appointed only by the
people or by a secular power and magistrate are to be regarded not as ministers
of the church but as thieves and robbers.” And canon 7 says, “If anyone says
that orders conferred by bishops without the consent or calling of the people
or secular power are void, let him be anathema.” Bellarmine likewise writes in
book 1 *On the Clergy*, chapters 2 and 3: “All Catholics teach with the highest
agreement that the right to ordain bishops and call them to the people can in
no way belong to the people. The right of electing did at one time and in some
manner reside with the people, but by the concession and sufferance of the
pontiffs, not by divine law.”

V. The source of the Romanists’ error is the false idea of calling which they
fashion for themselves when they conceive it as a certain sacrament which they
call the sacrament of orders. From this arises at once the opinion that the
people cannot confer a sacrament. Next they maintain that this sacrament
imprints an indelible character which is

[1847 vol. III printed p. 191 | IA leaf 225 | PDF p. 225]

a physical quality inhering in the pastor’s mind. Third, they contend that
Christ and the apostles deposited this sacrament in the hands of the bishops so
that it might be dispensed by none but them. Once this foundation has been
demolished, the other things built upon it easily collapse.

VI. Our position, however, is that the right of calling belongs originally and
radically to the church, with which Christ deposited it, but that it is
exercised by the rulers in the church’s name, just as royal officers appoint
judges and magistrates not in their own name but in the king’s. In a
commonwealth, the power of creating a magistrate resides in the body of the
community which constitutes the commonwealth, and the inauguration is
performed in the community’s name by the person delegated for that purpose.
Furthermore, just as there are generally three orders in the church—the
political, ecclesiastical, and household orders, or the presbytery, magistrate,
and people—and the church consists of them all as of members, no estate of the
church is to be wholly excluded from this work. Each must be left its own part
and duties. No one denies that this belongs to presbyters. Yet because they are
not the whole church but only a part of it, they ought not to seize the calling
for themselves alone and exclude the church’s other members. They must also
leave the magistrate, if he is a Christian, his own part, not only by reason of
his person, because he is a principal member of the church, but also by reason
of his office, because he is the guardian of the divine law and a nursing
father of the church (Isa. 49:23). The whole people in general, whose own
affair is at issue here, must likewise have its part. Although the form of
calling and election varies in its particular form according to places,
persons, and the manner of both political and ecclesiastical government, it can
nevertheless be said in general that the presbytery has the right formally,
with respect to examination, election, and ordination; the magistrate has it
eminently, with respect to approval, confirmation, and appointment to the
office; and the people have it virtually, with respect to consent and voluntary
admission. Thus the church’s body always retains the right of calling as its
own.

[Marginalia: The right of calling is proved to belong to the church. 1. From
the delivery of the keys to it.]

VII. There are various reasons why we so hold. The first is drawn from the
delivery of the keys, because the power of the keys, which includes the right
of calling, was given to the churches. This is clear from Matthew 16:19, where
the keys of the kingdom of heaven are promised to Peter and, in his person, to
the whole church; and from Matthew 18:27, where Christ gives the church the
power of binding and loosing. I acknowledge that the church exercises this
right through its rulers. Yet in doing so the pastors exercise the right which
belongs to the body, whose representatives they are. Thus this right always
belongs radically to the body and is its own, while it belongs to the pastors
with respect to its use and exercise, which must be carried out in the body’s
name. Bellarmine says in vain in book 1 *On the Clergy*, chapter 7, that “Peter
received the keys in the person of the church because he received them for the
advantage and benefit of the whole church, and because he was not to use them
alone but would transmit them to successors and share them with bishops and
presbyters.” For Peter confessed Christ in the person of the church not only
because that confession redounded to the advantage of the whole church, but
also because, when Peter confessed, the church itself also confessed. In the
same way, the keys of the kingdom of heaven were given to Peter not only for
the advantage and benefit of the church, but also because the church received
them in Peter’s person in order to exercise them, both in the other parts of
the power signified by the name of the keys and in electing and calling
suitable pastors. Hence Ferus says on Matthew 16: “The church possesses the
keys as mistress and bride; Peter possesses them not as lord but as minister.”

VIII. Tostatus treats this point admirably in question 48 on Numbers 15, where
he proves by various arguments that the keys were given to the whole church and
therefore that the right of exercising them belongs to it primarily and
radically, but to others only

[1847 vol. III printed p. 192 | IA leaf 226 | PDF p. 226]

secondarily and by participation. He says: “Since a community is not properly
one thing but is one accidentally, like a heap of stones, it cannot exercise
any acts. Indeed, it is impossible for jurisdiction to exist actually in a
community. Yet jurisdiction exists in the community according to its origin
and power, because all persons who receive jurisdiction receive it by the power
of the community, since they can judge individually but the community cannot.
The same seems true of the church’s keys, because they were given by Christ to
the whole church. Yet because the whole church could not dispense them, since
it was not a single person, he handed them to Peter in the church’s name. If,
however, they were understood to have been handed to Peter in particular, not
only would the familiar difficulty follow which is raised, namely, that the
other apostles would have had no authority of the keys—which is false, because
the power to forgive sins was given to them (John 20), and this is the very
power of the keys, and so forth—but a still greater difficulty would follow:
after Peter died, the keys would not have remained, as must have been the case
if they had been given to Peter alone. And not only if they had been given to
him alone, but even if they had been given to all the apostles as determinate
persons, the keys would not have remained in the church when they died, because
they had no power to give the keys to others by making them their successors,
and so forth. Yet all the successors of blessed Peter and the other apostles
possess the keys, just as Peter and the other apostles did. Therefore, the keys
were not given to them as determinate persons but as ministers of the church.
In this way the keys can never cease to exist in the church, since the church,
which possesses them radically, never dies, and so forth.” And in question 49
on the same chapter he says: “The church received the keys from Christ, and the
apostles received them as ministers of the church. Now the church possesses
them and the prelates possess them as well, but the church possesses them in a
different way than the prelates. The church possesses them according to origin
and power, while the prelates possess them according to their use. It is said
to possess them by origin because the prelate’s power does not originate from
himself but from the church’s power through election, for the church which
elects him bestows that jurisdiction upon him. But the church, once it has
received the keys from Christ, receives them from no one. It therefore
possesses them by origin and power, and when it confers them upon a prelate, it
does not confer them in the manner in which it possesses them—namely, with
respect to origin and power—but with respect to use, and so forth.”

[Marginalia: 2. From the right of the ministry.]

IX. Second, the same thing is proved from the right of the ministry which
belongs to the church. The right of calling belongs to that body to which the
right of the ministry belongs, for whose sake it was instituted by God, which
must give its consent, and which is obliged to possess the ministry for the
preservation of faith and piety. Such a body is not the pastors and bishops
alone, but the whole church. For since it is an assembly which God gathered
through the ministry of the apostles and which he gathers and preserves daily
through the preaching of the Word and the use of the sacraments, God, who does
not fail to provide necessary things, must by the very act of forming it have
given it a full and sufficient right to employ all the means necessary for its
preservation, chief among which is undoubtedly the ministry. Just as
Providence, which gathers people into civil society and in doing so commands
them to preserve that society in lawful order, thereby gives them the right to
call and set over themselves magistrates by whom it may be governed and who
make laws and see that they are observed for the good of the whole community,
so the church is a holy society of people whom God himself has gathered by his
Word so that they may live together not in confusion but in good order, and
which he wills to be preserved in the world until the end of the age. He must
therefore by that very fact bestow upon it the right to have and call pastors
by whom it may be instructed and governed. This is further confirmed by the
fact that God, who gave the church faith and piety, also

[1847 vol. III printed p. 193 | IA leaf 227 | PDF p. 227]

thereby bound it indispensably to these four duties: (1) to persevere in the
exercise of those virtues to the end; (2) to defend them against the errors and
deceptions of Satan; (3) to confirm and increase them more and more; and (4) to
propagate them to the best of its ability not only to its children but also to
outsiders. It necessarily follows that God gave the church a full and
sufficient right to the ministry, which is the lawful and most suitable means
to this end. No right can be more lawful than one grounded upon indispensable
duties, because then it is not merely a right which makes the matter just, but
also an obligation which imposes the necessity of carrying it out.

X. From this it is clear that the church does not exist for the ministry, but
the ministry for the church, and that the church does not depend upon the
ministry, but the ministry upon the church, just as in civil society the
magistrate depends upon the society, not the society upon the magistrate. For
in a society we conceive first that God made human beings, second that he
gathered them into one, and finally that the magistracy takes its origin from
that union, which cannot be preserved without order. So also in the church,
grace first makes believers and then unites them to one another by the bond of
mutual communion. Because communion cannot exist without order and government,
the necessity of the ministry arises from it. Thus the ministry is subsequent
to the church. It does not make the church true, for the church has this from
the truth of faith; rather, the true church makes the ministry lawful, because
the legitimacy of the ministry depends upon the truth of the church. I
acknowledge that the church was first produced by the extraordinary ministry
of the apostles, and that the same ministry preserves it daily and will
preserve it to the end. Faith is what makes and will always make the church,
and the apostles’ ministry makes and will always make faith: the same Word
which they preached endures even today and gathers people into one. But the
case of the ordinary ministry is not the same, because ordinary pastors were
not instituted until after the church had already been produced, for its
preservation and propagation. Thus in the order of nature the church precedes
the ministry and produces it; the ministry does not produce the church.

[Marginalia: 3. From the right of superiority.]

XI. From this flows the third argument, from the right of superiority, because
authority and the right of acting belong to the superior, not the inferior. The
church is superior to its pastors, not the pastors to the church. The church
does not belong to the pastors, but the pastors to the church. “All things are
yours,” Paul says in 1 Corinthians 3:22, “whether Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas.”
There he rebukes those who gloried in men as though they were heads and for
whose sake they stirred up disputes and factions among the Corinthians,
showing that they acted wrongly because the church is greater and superior to
them all. Hence pastors are called servants and ministers of the church: “We
are your servants for Christ’s sake” (2 Cor. 4:5). If they are ministers of
the church, they must therefore be elected and called by it. The church is
Christ’s bride, while the ministers are attendants and servants who must
prepare her for her Bridegroom. Although pastors are ministers of the church
not by obeying it but by governing and presiding over it, it does not follow
that the church lacks the right of calling. These things are not opposed to one
another: a pastor’s lawful calling by the church, and the reverence and
obedience duly shown to a lawfully called minister. A magistrate who commands
the people may nevertheless be elected by the people. The office of governing
the church does not take away the church’s right to call its pastors.

[Marginalia: 4. From the examination of teachers.]

XII. Fourth, this is proved from the examination of teachers. The right of
calling belongs to the one whose task it is to distinguish teachers from
deceivers, examine sound doctrine, distinguish the voice of Christ the Chief
Shepherd from the voice of false apostles, refuse to follow a stranger, and
anathematize those who preach another gospel. All this belongs to the whole
church

[1847 vol. III printed p. 194 | IA leaf 228 | PDF p. 228]

by the force of the divine command: “Beware of false prophets” (Matt. 7:15);
“My sheep hear my voice and do not follow a stranger” (John 10:27); “Test the
spirits to see whether they are from God” (1 John 4:1); and “If anyone preaches
to you a gospel other than what was preached to you, let him be anathema” (Gal.
1:8). Bellarmine answers in vain that the people must distinguish a true
prophet from a false one by the agreement of the doctrine he preaches with the
doctrine of their predecessors and of the apostolic see. For it follows on this
account that judgment concerning doctrine belongs to the people. The standard
of judgment must not be the doctrine of predecessors or ordinary pastors,
because they can stray from the faith, as has happened all too often, and the
truth of doctrine is not bound to a succession of persons. Otherwise, the
apostles ought not to have followed Christ’s doctrine, which differed from the
doctrine of the priests who were the ordinary pastors. This standard must be
sought nowhere outside God’s Word, according to Isaiah 8:20; 1 John 5:39; 2
Peter 1:19; and Galatians 1:8. Therefore, if pastors are to be heard, they must
not be heard absolutely and without qualification, whatever they may say, but
conditionally: if they themselves hear Christ, speak the utterances of God (1
Pet. 4:11), and do not depart from the pattern of sound words. Otherwise,
Christ commands his disciples to beware of the leaven of the Pharisees, and
Paul wills anyone who teaches a gospel different from Christ’s to be anathema
to us. Nor should it be said that the uneducated people cannot judge the truth
of doctrine in any way other than by comparing the doctrine delivered to them
with that of their predecessors and ordinary pastors. If the people’s lack of
education does not prevent them from comparing the doctrine delivered to them
with that of their predecessors, why would it prevent them from comparing
their pastors’ doctrine with Christ’s? The Bereans successfully did just this
when they compared Paul’s discourses with the Scriptures every day (Acts
17:11). Although the people can judge a pastor’s doctrine for themselves, it
does not follow that they have no need of preachers. It is one thing to
investigate the truth of the doctrine delivered and another to teach publicly.
The former belongs to private persons; the latter to pastors alone. The sheep
ought not to be pastors, but neither are they brute beasts. They are rational
sheep, able to distinguish true from false. They not only ought not to follow a
pastor who errs, but must flee him, withdraw from him, seek another who teaches
the truth, and call that teacher in place of the one who errs.

[Marginalia: 5. From apostolic practice.]

XIII. Fifth, it is proved from apostolic practice. The primitive apostolic
church observed this order of calling, so that it did not take place without
the church’s consent. In Acts 1:23 the whole church chooses two to be put
forward for the lot of the apostleship, and when the lot falls upon Matthias he
is added to the number of the apostles by common consent. In Acts 6:3, 5–6 the
whole multitude of the godly chooses seven deacons, who are confirmed by the
apostles through the laying on of hands. In Acts 14:23 the apostles appoint
presbyters town by town through the people’s *cheirotonia*, or by their free
votes. The word is derived from the Greek custom of voting with hands stretched
forth and extended. Hence, when transferred to every kind of election, sacred
as well as political, it means to create by votes. Although the verb
*cheirotonein* is sometimes taken more broadly for any appointment and
election, as in Hesychius, where *cheirotonein* is *psēphizein* (“to vote”), it
cannot be used in that sense here. It must properly be understood as an
appointment by votes. First, *cheirotonia* must be distinguished from
*cheirothesia*, or ordination, which is performed by the laying on of hands.
The former is done by the people, but the latter by the pastors. Second,
ordination is treated in the words which follow, when it is added that “after
praying they commended them to God,” which was not done without the laying on
of hands (Acts 6:6; 13:3). Nor can the votes of Paul and Barnabas alone be
meant, because the circumstances of the text indicate that the body of the
church participated in this calling, and similar examples prove the same
thing. Acts 15:22 says, “It pleased the apostles and elders, with the whole
church, to choose men from among them and send them”; and verse 25 says, “It
pleased us, having assembled together, to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 195 | IA leaf 229 | PDF p. 229]

send them to you.” In 1 Corinthians 16:3 Paul says, “When I am present, I will
send those whom you approve by letters.” In 2 Corinthians 8:19 Luke is said to
have been *cheirotonētheis hypo tōn ekklēsiōn* (“chosen by the churches”) to be
the companion of Paul’s travels; Chrysostom on this passage, Theophylact,
Oecumenius, and Estius understand an election. If the church’s vote was
employed in choosing those who were to carry extraordinary commissions to the
churches, how much more in choosing those who were to discharge the ordinary
office of teaching? And if it was employed for the *diakonia* (“ministry”) of
the collection, how much more for the *diakonia* of the Word? Cotonus therefore
wrongly censures our French version, which interprets *cheirotonia* as an
appointment by votes, or election. The force of the word conveys this, as
Salmero observes on Acts 14, treatise 41, and Estius on 2 Corinthians 8:19. The
fact that *cheirotonia* and *cheirothesia* are sometimes confused by the
ancients and in the canons, and used interchangeably by synecdoche for the
election or ordination of pastors because both contribute to their appointment
and calling, does not mean that they have the same meaning in Scripture or
retain it everywhere in the fathers. Zonaras observes this on Apostolic Canon
1: “At the present time *cheirotonia* is called the office of consecration,
because the archbishop extends his hand in blessing the person promoted; but
formerly the vote itself, *hē autē psēphos*, was called *cheirotonia*. For when
the people were admitted to the election of archbishops, they assembled and
gave their votes by extending their hands.” This is confirmed by canon 5 of the
Council of Laodicea, which commands that *cheirotoniai* not be performed before
the hearers, among whom were catechumens. This cannot be said of the laying on
of hands, which was performed before the whole assembly.

[Marginalia: 6. From the primitive church.]

XIV. Sixth, it is proved from the primitive church. The decrees of ancient
councils vindicate the church’s right to call pastors, especially the decree of
the Council of Nicaea in its synodical letter found in Theodoret, book 1,
chapter 9: “If the ruler of a church should happen to die, let those received a
short time before have the right to succeed to the place of the deceased,
provided they appear worthy and the people elect them, and so forth.” Canon 27
of the Third Council of Carthage and canon 12 of the Fourth say, “Let no bishop
ordain clerics without the assent and testimony of the people.” This canon is
cited in chapter *Epicopus*, distinction 24. The councils of Nantes, Toledo
XII, canon 6, and Sardica belong here; Gratian relates the last council’s decree
in distinction 65, chapter *Si forte*. Here also belong the various rescripts
of the pontiffs Nicholas, Leo, Gelasius, Stephen, Pelagius, and others which
Gratian carefully inserted into his distinctions, and the numerous and
illustrious testimonies of the fathers which could be adduced from Tertullian,
Origen, Cyprian, Ambrose, Chrysostom, Isidore, Gregory the Great, and others.
They are cited by Gratian in the *Decree*, distinctions 23, 24, 62, 63, and the
following distinctions. Thus antiquity judged with virtually one voice that
all elections made *aneu laou syneseōs*—that is, as Cyprian renders it,
“without the people’s knowledge and assent”—were void.

XV. We give a few of many passages from the fathers. Cyprian treats this at
length in Epistle 68, *To the Clergy and People in Spain*, and so forth: “The
people themselves especially possess the power either to elect worthy priests
or reject the unworthy. This also appears to descend from divine authority: the
priest is chosen in the presence of the people and before everyone’s eyes, and
is approved as worthy and fit by public judgment and testimony.” He establishes
this by various arguments in what follows and adds: “In the ordination of our
colleague Sabinianus, it was done in this way: the episcopate was conferred upon
him by the vote of the whole brotherhood and by the judgment of the bishops who
had assembled in their presence and had written letters about him to them.” In
Epistle 52 he confirms this by the example of Cornelius, bishop of Rome. He
says: “He was made a bishop by the judgment of God and his Christ, the
testimony of nearly all the clergy, the vote of the people who were then
present, and the college of ancient priests and good men.” Pamelius

[1847 vol. III printed p. 196 | IA leaf 230 | PDF p. 230]

himself cannot conceal this in his note on Cyprian’s Epistle 68: “We do not
deny the ancient rite for the election of bishops, who were customarily elected
with the people present, and indeed by public votes.” Origen says in Homily 6
on Leviticus 8, cited in chapter *Licet*, question 1: “The presence of the
people is therefore also required in ordaining a priest, so that everyone may
know and be certain that the person who is more excellent than all the people,
and so forth, is elected to the priesthood, and so forth.” Leo I says in
Epistle 89: “Let there be the subscription of the clergy, the testimony of the
honored men, and the consent of both the order and the people.” And in Epistle
96: “The one who is to preside over all should be elected by all.” Ambrose says
in book 10, Epistle 82: “He deservedly became so great a man whom the whole
church elected; he is deservedly believed to have been elected by divine
judgment whom everyone had requested, and so forth.” Chrysostom says in book 3
*On the Priesthood*: “All elections and callings which are made without the
knowledge and assent of the people are void.” Isidore says in book 2 *On
Ecclesiastical Offices*, chapter 5: “If the archpresbyter in a congregation
should die, and so forth, let the archdeacon go there as quickly as possible
and conduct an election with the clergy and people of that congregation, so
that a worthy shepherd may be appointed for the Lord God.” Gregory of Nazianzus
relates in *In Praise of Athanasius* that he was elected *psēphou tou laou
pantos*, “by the vote of the whole people.” Theodoret asserts the same in book
4 of his *History*, chapter 10, as does Nicephorus in book 11 of his
*Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 20, concerning Peter, Athanasius’s successor.
Socrates likewise says in book 4 of his *History*, chapter 25, concerning
Ambrose, that “by the common vote of all, and by the emperor’s own will and
consent, he was elected bishop of Milan in the place of Auxentius.” Augustine,
Epistle 110, similarly relates that Eradius was designated his successor by the
consent, votes, acclamations, and subscriptions of the church at Hippo. Finally,
Antonio de Dominis proves at length from the ancients in book 3, chapter 3 of
*On the Ecclesiastical Commonwealth* that the election of bishops always
belonged to the clergy and the people.

XVI. The opponents catch in vain at various evasions to escape the force of the
argument drawn from the fathers. These evasions easily vanish when the passages
are examined. Will they say, “The subject is custom, not divine right”?
Cyprian, however, expressly notes that this descends from divine authority and
that God establishes by precept, as well as showing by example, that priestly
ordinations ought to be performed only with the knowledge of the people in
attendance. He confirms this from Acts. For what the apostles observed by the
impulse of the Holy Spirit as a perpetual practice in a matter of such
difficulty rightly takes the place of a general rule and is judged to descend
from divine right. Will they answer, “The people’s testimony and consent are
required, but not their votes”? The passages teach the contrary, since Cyprian
calls an ordination “just and lawful when it has been confirmed by everyone’s
vote and judgment.” Add that consent and testimony have the function of a
vote. Will they add, “It is a vote of petition and testimony, not of power and
authority”? But who does not see that petitioning is one thing and voting
another, and that a vote cannot be cast in an election except from power and
authority? Finally, will they say this occurred “by the concession of the
pontiffs, not by divine right,” as Bellarmine speaks in book 1 *On the Clergy*,
chapter 7? But where is any mention made of papal concession or sufferance, as
he also calls it? Before the Roman bishops arrogated this authority to
themselves, was not the people’s consent and vote required as necessary for a
lawful calling? Further, how could such a concession have had any place while
the see was vacant?

XVII. Nor should we omit what is read in the old pontifical, where the bishop
being ordained says: “It was not instituted by the fathers without reason that
the people also be consulted concerning the election of those who are to be
employed in governing the altar, because each person will more readily show
obedience to the one ordained when he has given his assent to his ordination.”
This is so plain upon the present subject that Passius

[1847 vol. III printed p. 197 | IA leaf 231 | PDF p. 231]

Valentinus, a canon who attended the Council of Trent, advised the council to
correct it and permit the passages which mention this matter to be expunged.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVIII. Although Christ sent the apostles and disciples without any consent or
vote of the multitude, it does not follow that the same right belongs to the
bishops apart from the church. Christ’s power, which is absolute and sovereign,
differs greatly from the limited and dependent power of human beings. Nor can
this be established from John 20:21, where Christ is said to send the apostles
just as the Father sent him. The particle “just as” is not to be taken without
limit or extended to a complete equality of power, but only to a certain
likeness: the office’s divine authority, the promise of success in the
ministry, protection amid dangers, and other things of this kind.

XIX. Brute and irrational sheep cannot elect a shepherd because they lack
discerning judgment. But the case of spiritual and rational sheep is not the
same. Just as they distinguish the shepherd’s voice from the voice of the
hired hand and wolf, so they themselves know how to elect suitable pastors.
Indeed, they are commanded to distinguish true pastors from false, to follow
the former, and to flee the latter. Otherwise, on the opponents’ hypothesis,
the pope could not be elected by the cardinals, who are themselves also his
sheep. Nor in a polity could subjects, who are called sheep, occasionally elect
their kings and magistrates. Although they do not formally possess the power of
the office which they confer upon pastors, they nevertheless possess it
originally and with respect to its power. It is one thing to possess the right
of calling pastors and another to possess the right of exercising the
ministry. The former is said of the church, but not the latter.

XX. It is one thing to call a pastor to constitute a church and convert
unbelieving peoples, and another to call one to a church already constituted
and a people already converted. In the former respect pastors could be sent by
the apostles without at least the prior consent of the people. But in the
latter respect, which is now in question, we say that the apostles did not omit
the votes of the church in calling pastors, as is clear from what has been
said. The office of evangelists, which was extraordinary and temporary, is one
thing; that of pastors, which is ordinary and perpetual, is another. The former
could be conferred by the apostles alone without the church’s consent, although
even here the apostles did not neglect the vote of the church and the
presbytery, as was seen in Timothy’s ordination, when hands are said to have
been laid upon him by the presbytery (1 Tim. 4:14). The latter office
necessarily requires the church’s consent.

XXI. Titus is commanded to appoint presbyters town by town (Titus 1:5), but in
accordance with the custom of the apostolic church and with everything fitting
observed, as the added clause “as I commanded you” sufficiently teaches. In
addition, the subject is a church being constituted rather than one already
constituted, since he was required *ta leiponta epidiorthōsai*, “to reform and
correct the things which remained to be corrected.”

XXII. The apostolic and synodical canons which forbid the multitude and people
the election of pastors—such as the first Apostolic Canon, that a bishop be
ordained by three other bishops, which was afterward sanctioned at the Council
of Nicaea; canon 13 of the Synod of Laodicea; and canon 12 of Constantinople
IV—possess human, not divine, authority. Second, we oppose to them the councils
mentioned above, which require the church’s consent and vote. Third, those
canons do not simply exclude the people’s consent from election. They only take
care that the people not abuse their right, seize for themselves alone the
power of election while excluding the presbytery, insolently prescribe the
presbytery’s manner of proceeding, or proceed turbulently and confusedly in
this holy business. To this end the canons also decree that the leading men of
the church who are honorable and prudent should, in the name of the rest,
carefully

complete this business.

XXIII. Although we vindicate the people’s right in the calling of pastors, we
do not wish with the fanatics to assign them this right to the exclusion of the
magistrate and presbytery. Rather, we wish everything to be done in order and
decently, so that each order of the church is assigned its own parts according
to the circumstances of the places and the manner of government, as was said
before.

XXIV. Although the body of the church has committed the exercise of the right
of calling pastors to the presbytery in order to avoid confusion, it has not
therefore stripped itself of that right absolutely and without qualification,
so as to be said to lack it and be unable to use it any longer in any case. For
it has so committed the exercise of that right to rulers, who administer it in
its name, that it has nevertheless originally reserved it as its own proper and
peculiar right. Nor is the example of civil society relevant here, where the
people so yield their right to the prince whom they elect that they divest
themselves of it absolutely and without qualification. Political and sacred
society differ greatly in this respect. In the former, the people can yield
their right absolutely to the prince by subjecting themselves to him as lord.
But the church does not transfer its right to pastors with respect to its
ownership, as though to lords, but only with respect to its use and exercise,
as to ministers who administer it not in their own name but in the church’s.
The reason for the difference is that in civil society, where only temporal
goods are at issue, nothing prevents the people from yielding their right
absolutely; indeed, at times it is expedient for avoiding confusion and
anarchy. But in the church, where salvation is at issue, believers cannot
without guilt divest themselves absolutely of the right which they possess in
the means given to them for promoting their salvation, such as the ministry.
For although their faith and piety do not absolutely depend upon pastors, the
exercise of a pure and uncorrupted ministry is a great help to piety; by
contrast, preserving the faith is most difficult under a corrupt ministry.

XXV. If it is further asked whether the church is always bound to confer its
callings through the involvement of pastors, and whether in certain cases when
pastors are lacking it is not lawful for the church to confer callings
immediately by itself, we answer that calling must be distinguished as it is
considered in itself with respect to its essential parts, or with respect to
the manner in which it is conferred, namely, the ceremonies and rites usually
employed in it. The essence of calling consists in the threefold consent of
God, the church, and the person called: of God, because the person called must
speak in his name; of the church, because it must be taught and governed; and
of the person called, because he must fulfill the duties of his office. The
manner of calling consists in certain preceding or accompanying acts, without
which the calling would be confused and disorderly, such as an examination of
faith and morals, testimony to an upright life, blessing, and the laying on of
hands. With respect to the former, because the essential element of calling can
exist in an assembly where pastors are lacking, it is certain that the faithful
people can perform a calling in a case of supreme necessity. I acknowledge
that where pastors are present in a constituted and duly ordered church, the
people ought to join with the presbytery in instituting the calling if this can
be done without confusion. If not, they must ratify and approve by their
consent the calling performed by the presbytery, because it was performed in
their name. But where they have no pastors and cannot obtain them without the
danger of dispersion, it is certain that the people alone can confer the
calling. A calling does not proceed from a society insofar as it has pastors,
but insofar as it is an assembly—that is, a body of believers joined to one
another by the bond of faith, piety, and righteousness. Therefore, just as it
establishes its own order, it is also master of its own consent. Thus a calling
does not cease to be full and sufficient in its essentials without pastors.
The rites and ceremonies of calling, which do not belong to its essence, ought
to be employed in a constituted church, but cannot always be observed in a
church being constituted and reformed. See what was said in the preceding
question.

XXVI. Because it is again asked here how laypeople who are not ministers can
make ministers, since no one can give what he does not possess, the answer is
easy: no one can give what he does not possess at all. Yet although the church,
or body of believers, does not possess the ministry with respect to its
exercise—that is, so that it may fulfill the ministry’s duties by preaching and
administering the sacraments—it nevertheless possesses it with respect to the
right and power of conferring it. For by Christ’s institution, the church’s
authority and consent make the functions of the ministry valid, since Christ
willed to endow this religious society with that right. Thus the ministry
belongs to the church, because it was instituted only for the church’s sake and
benefit, and calling consists in entrusting this public right to the person
called so that he may use it in the name of the whole society. The people
cannot be denied the lawful power to entrust this deposit to certain persons,
for no calling is more just than one founded not only upon a lawful right but
also upon an indispensable duty and obligation, such as the power which we have
said belongs to the church’s body. Nor does it follow that this power cannot be
assigned to the body of believers because no private person considered
separately can claim it for himself. Nothing is more absurd than to imagine
that the body of a community cannot possess and entrust to others what does not
belong to the individual private persons of whom it is composed. Otherwise,
the people could not create a king and magistrate, because no private person
among the people possesses this right.
