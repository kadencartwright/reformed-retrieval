Fourth. This is also clear in the *eighth* precept, which presupposes a
division of ownership. But this division of ownership does not flow from the
nature of God. Therefore neither does that precept which necessarily
presupposes it.

The adversary replies on pages 37–38: “On the contrary, where two people hold
goods in common, truth and justice require both to wish to hold the goods in
common, and one not to wish to take more than the other; otherwise it will be
theft. Where ownership has been distributed, it is true and just not to envy,
seize, or covet. It is an eternal truth that it is unjust to covet another’s
property. And that truth precedes the precept. It cannot please God that one
person should covet what he has given to another; see axiom 6 above. Dr. Ames
likewise pronounces in accordance with this in *Cases of Conscience*, book 5,
chapter 41.” Reply: 1. Where two people hold goods in common there is
property, and each one has ownership of at least half of the whole or of the
increase arising from the whole. But we are not speaking about such common
ownership between only two people. We are speaking about universal community,
which, once posited, removes theft, because *mine and yours*—that is, property
and ownership—are removed. Let us suppose that God had established this
community of goods, just as the earth is by nature common to all brute
animals, and the sea to fish. Then this precept against stealing, or about
practicing justice toward one another in these external things, would
certainly have no place. Therefore these commands do not flow necessarily
from the divine nature, because without any contradiction they could have
been left ungiven, since distinct ownership could have been left
unestablished. 2. So it is an eternal truth that it is unjust to covet
another’s property. Nevertheless, where nothing belongs to another, that
unjust covetousness has no place. Therefore what follows only from a
contingent antecedent cannot be said to flow from the divine nature. 3.
Although that truth precedes the precept, it does not precede God’s free good
pleasure. 4. It is true that it cannot please God that one person should covet
what he has given to another, because it is prohibited. 5. We subjected what
he had said in axiom 6 above to examination. 6. Even if we conceded that the
division of ownership rests not only upon human right but also upon natural
and divine right, it still does not follow that it rests upon a right natural
to God himself. For what rests upon this right cannot fail to be prescribed.
That is, it does not follow that the division of ownership necessarily flows
from the nature and attributes of God in such a way that God could not have
left it unenjoined unless he wished to do violence to his own nature and
attributes.

Fifth. The same thing can be shown in the *fifth* precept, for although it is a
moral precept that subjects should render obedience to magistrates, it is
nevertheless certain that this precept does not flow from God’s nature, since
apart from political magistracy all people could have been subject to God
immediately.

The adversary responds on page 38: “As though no certain and immutable justice
were given by hypothesis, although we saw above that all the natural justice
of the second table proceeds from a hypothesis. Therefore neither the
veneration of God nor of parents will be natural, but the greatest precept of
the first and second tables will be a mutable precept, because it is by God’s
choice that a human being and creature exists and is born from parents. As
though a person were not God’s possession because created by God, nor the
parents’ because begotten through them.” Reply: We never said that no certain
and immutable justice is given by the hypothesis of a decree, namely, the
divine decree. Let us also concede that certain and immutable justice is given
by the hypothesis of creation. What will he establish from this? Can it be
proved from this that even those things which are just, not only by the
hypothesis of creation but also by one or another added hypothesis,
necessarily flow from the nature and attributes of God? It therefore does not
follow that the veneration

of God and parents is not natural, as he pretends. For the question is not
what necessarily follows once a human being is posited, nor whether that which
necessarily follows once a human being is posited should be called natural;
no one denies this. The only question is whether that which presupposes not
only a human being’s creation and dependence upon God, but also other things
which could have occurred or not occurred or occurred otherwise without
altering human nature, should be said to flow from the divine nature, although,
as is clear, it should not be said to flow from human nature. It is therefore
sufficiently clear that the precept by which subjects are bound to obey
magistrates and servants their masters does not flow from the divine nature
but from his free good pleasure, although it is moral. There are accordingly
moral precepts which are not natural but positive for God, although they are
natural for us.

“It is therefore moral,” he says, “that magistrates ought to be superiors. It
is morally good to be subject to them and evil to deny them subjection. But
what form of government is established—whether through parents or other
magistrates, and whether these are kings or leading men, and so forth—was a
matter of free constitution and is not moral but free. Therefore, if it had
pleased God to govern us without magistrates, they would not have had to be
honored. Nevertheless, those who would have been our superiors in their place
would always have had to be honored—at least our parents, to whom we are
always naturally subject.” Reply: It is moral that magistrates ought to be
superiors, just as it is moral that the same thing ought to be the same thing,
for magistrates are superiors. 2. It is indeed morally good to be subject to
them, yet this precept by which we are commanded to be subject to them does
not necessarily flow from the divine essence or nature. This is all the more
evident because a free human constitution intervenes. 3. He cannot assert
confidently enough that those who would be our superiors would always have had
to be honored, since he adds, “at least our parents, to whom we are always
naturally subject.” But we are not speaking of parents. We are speaking of
magistrates, masters, and the like, to whom obedience and honor are to be
shown in this fifth precept.

“And this is properly the moral content of the fifth precept: to honor
superiors,” he says. Reply: We are not asking what the properly moral content
of that precept is, nor do we deny that this is its moral content. Rather, we
are speaking of a particular duty commanded to people in this precept, which
they concede to be moral and which we have proved does not flow from the
divine nature.

Finally he asks what this contributes to the hypothesis concerning the
Sabbath. We will show that in its proper place. His colleague answered as
follows in the *Vindiciae*, page 38: “If someone objects, ‘But by the
hypothesis that God ordained political magistracy, he could not fail to bind
subjects to obedience,’ I will likewise say that by the hypothesis that God
ordained the weekly Sabbath, he could not fail to bind people to its
observance.” To this the adversary responds: “Correct; but did the nature of
God or of man require the ordination of the Sabbath, as it required the
ordination of superiors?” Reply: What? Did the nature of God or man require
the ordination of political magistrates? For we are speaking about these now,
not about superiors in general. Since he cannot say this, he must confess that
this instance contributes greatly to our purpose.
