### Assertion IX

In a lawful calling to discharge the pastoral office, the people’s election
is not sufficient. In addition, ordination and separation are necessarily
required for performing this office whenever they can be had—for I am not
disputing about an extraordinary case. By ordination here I do not mean the
rite of laying on of hands, because this rite or complement can sometimes be
absent without destroying ordination. Rather, I mean that authoritative
mission by which someone is constituted a pastor, sent with authority to
discharge that office, and set apart. Because Velthuysen denies this, it must
be proved.

1. This order is observed everywhere in constituted commonwealths, so that no
one seizes some public office for himself before he is duly called and
appointed. Who, without being called and sent, dares assume the office of a
higher or lower magistrate, or even the office of public herald, deputy,
legate, general, admiral, and so forth? Will this order obtain to a lesser
degree in the church?

2. Christ himself was sent by the Father, John 20:22–23; anointed, Acts 10:38;
and solemnly installed, Matthew 4:16–17. See also Hebrews 5:5; John 8:54; and
the *Synopsis of Purer Theology*, disputation 41, thesis 7.

3. The apostles themselves were set apart and sent by Christ, Matthew 10:1,
5: “he sent them.” Likewise Luke 9:1–2; Mark 3:13–15: “and he made twelve,”
and “he appointed twelve.” So afterward they were solemnly set apart and sent,
Matthew 28:18–19; John 20:21.

4. Just as Christ appointed the apostles, so the apostles appointed other
ministers, 2 Timothy 1:6.

[Volume II; printed p. 132; ONB canvas 140]

Thus in Acts 14:23 they created presbyters by votes in every church. Thus
Matthias was solemnly set apart, Acts 1:23–24, 26. At the Spirit’s command,
Paul and Barnabas were set apart, Acts 13:1–3, and Timothy, 1 Timothy 4:14.

5. Titus had been commanded by the apostle to appoint and ordain presbyters in
every church. Titus 1:5: “I left you in Crete, that you should appoint
presbyters city by city,” and so forth. That word is customarily used for the
ordination and appointment of ministers. See Scapula. It is so understood in
Matthew 24:45, 47; 25:21, 23; Luke 12:14, 42, 44; Acts 6:3; 7:10, 27, 35;
Hebrews 5:1; 7:28; 2:7. Nor can it be understood otherwise here, as the very
circumstances of the passage prove. For no one doubts that the discussion here
concerns ecclesiastical ministers.

6. In 1 Timothy 5:22 Paul enjoins Timothy “not to lay hands hastily on anyone.”
There he understands ordination by “the laying on of hands,” for the sign,
according to Calvin on the passage, is taken for the thing itself. It is
certain that the apostle speaks here about ecclesiastical ministers, as is
clear from verses 17–19. This rite was indeed used by superiors in blessings,
Genesis 48:14; Matthew 19:15; in setting sacrifices apart, Numbers 8:12; in
miraculously curing illnesses, Mark 5:23; 6:5; Luke 4:40; Acts 8:19; 28:8; and
also in giving the Holy Spirit, Acts 8:17–18; 19:6; 9:17. But it cannot be
understood so in this passage, as should be evident to anyone, for in those
cases there was no need for such a caution. It must therefore be understood as
ordination, as it is elsewhere, namely 1 Timothy 4:14; 2 Timothy 1:6; Acts 6:6;
13:3. Indeed, the attached reason confirms this: “do not share in the sins of
others.” This, says Calvin on the passage, “means that the person who consents
to an unlawful promotion is implicated in the same guilt and becomes one of
its chief authors.” And as Aretius says on the passage, “lest you become guilty
of a corrupt ministry.”

[Volume II; printed p. 133; ONB canvas 141]

7. Paul similarly commands in 2 Timothy 2:2: “What you have heard from me,
commit these things to faithful men.” *Paratithēmi* sometimes means to commit
a deposit to someone’s trust. See Scapula’s lexicon. It is indeed so understood
in Luke 12:48; 2 Timothy 1:18. “The meaning,” says Aretius on the passage, “is
that you must take care that suitable ministers be substituted in the church,
through whose care sound doctrine may be propagated to others.” Calvin
similarly says: “See to it that after my death there remains a sure testimony
to my doctrine. This will happen if you not only faithfully teach what you
have learned from me yourself, but take care that it is published more widely
through others. Therefore, entrust this treasure to the fidelity of whomever
you see to be fit for the task.”

8. This is evident even from the calling of the deacons in Acts 6. They were
not only elected but also ordained: “whom,” say the apostles, “we shall
appoint.” *Kathistēmi* means “to appoint and set over,” and very often means to
appoint someone to an office he did not previously discharge, or to set him
over a work with authority. See Matthew 24:45, 47; Luke 12:14, 42, 44; Matthew
25:21; Acts 7:10.

9. Romans 10:15: “How will they preach unless they have been sent?” This
interrogative expression is emphatic, and the necessity of this clause is the
same as that of the preceding ones. “For just as,” says Hoornbeeck in the
place cited above, page 207, “it is impossible to call upon him in whom you do
not believe, so that faith must precede calling upon him; and it is impossible
to believe in one of whom you have not heard, or to hear without someone
preaching; so also it is impossible for preaching to occur without a preceding
mission. Therefore, for the preaching of the gospel it is altogether required
that the one who is to preach be sent to do this. This is established by the
threefold argument in the apostle’s discourse. 1. The impossibility of the
matter, namely, with respect to its lawful order, permission, and right: ‘How
will they preach unless they”

[Volume II; printed p. 134; ONB canvas 142]

“have been sent?’ This is the same as if he had said: it is truly impossible,
and cannot rightfully happen, that someone should preach and announce the
gospel apart from being sent. 2. Scripture also confirms this same thing: ‘as
it is written.’ 3. By an equal reason and necessity he confirms the higher
things in relation to one another: hearing in relation to preaching, faith to
hearing, and invocation to faith. Just as these precede—faith precedes due
invocation, hearing precedes faith, and preaching precedes hearing—so mission
also precedes preaching.” Thus far he, who also amply refutes there what the
Socinians reply.

10. Under the Old Testament not only the high priest but all the others,
including the Levites, were solemnly inaugurated and consecrated, Leviticus 8;
Numbers 8. Those who wished to intrude themselves into this office suffered
punishment, as may be seen in Korah, Dathan, and Abiram, and likewise in Saul,
Jeroboam, and Uzziah, as stated above. No one should say, “These things have no
bearing on us under the New Testament,” for “whatever things were written
before were written for our instruction,” Romans 15:4. It must also be observed
that God says in Isaiah 66:21, “And I will also take some of them as priests
and Levites.” There is a prophecy there about the times of the New Testament.
When God speaks of New Testament ministers, he adorns them with the titles of
Old Testament ministers and specifically indicates that such a function,
distinct from others, will also exist in the New Testament: “For I will take
some of them as priests,” says God, and so forth. He intimates that these
ministers are to be solemnly set apart and consecrated, although without the
ceremonies that were in use under the Old Testament.

11. Hence the apostle lays down the general axiom in Hebrews 5:4: “No one takes
this honor to himself except the one who is called by God, as Aaron was.” He
confirms it by the example of the ancient priests, especially Aaron, and
finally Christ. “This passage,” says Hoornbeeck in the place cited above, page
209, “does not merely furnish an argument for our posi-”

[Volume II; printed p. 135; ONB canvas 143]

“tion, but also gives it not one confirmation but two, both of the greatest
weight. The axiom itself lays its own foundation in calling it an honor—namely,
a divine honor—which therefore must be conferred on someone individually by
God. For no one is permitted to rush into an honor, much less a singular and
eminent honor such as this, even if perhaps endowed with some qualifications
for occupying that place. Something more is always required: namely, a lawful
calling to occupy that station and dignity, John 3:27.” See how he vindicates
this argument from the Socinians’ exceptions.

12. This is also proved by those rules and canons by which we are directed in
ordaining pastors in Paul’s epistles to Timothy and Titus. They were not
prescribed only for that age, as is clear from 1 Timothy 6:18; 5:7, 21.

13. In Hebrews 6:1–2, the laying on of hands is numbered among the heads of
catechetical and necessary doctrine. By it the interpreters understand the
ordination and sending of ministers, whose ordinary rite is the laying on of
hands, or the ministry itself.

14. By this ordination, not by the people’s bare calling, someone who was not
previously a pastor is made a pastor. For church officers were appointed and
set over others by this ordination, not by a bare calling, as is clear from
Acts 6:3; Titus 1:5.

15. By ordination, not by calling alone, a pastor is sent with authority to
announce the gospel and administer the sacraments. But a people cannot properly
send anyone to themselves.

16. Hence this act of ordaining was performed only by apostles, evangelists,
prophets, and presbyters, at Christ’s command. It was therefore something
necessary to the very being of a lawful calling and was performed

[Volume II; printed p. 136; ONB canvas 144]

with great solemnity—namely, with prayers, fasting, and the laying on of hands.

17. No one is permitted to administer the sacraments unless called in this
manner, as we have proved. “The argument,” says Hoornbeeck in the place cited
above, page 216, “has three parts. It first shows from the nature of the thing
that no one is permitted to dispense the sacraments except ministers appointed
to do so by God and the church. For just as in a commonwealth no one except the
city’s secretary, called to this task, may handle the city seal and affix it to
things at the magistrate’s pleasure, so the sacred seals that are the
sacraments must be delivered by no one except appointed ministers of sacred
things.” See what follows.

18. Nowhere in the New Testament do we read of a pastor constituted by the
people’s calling alone. Rather, other ministers who constitute him are always
mentioned as well. The practice of the apostles and the apostolic church
obliges us, as will be proved.

19. If the people’s calling were sufficient to constitute a pastor, that
constituted pastor would be pastor only of that particular church. For he
would have all ecclesiastical power only from the election or calling of that
particular church. The Independents indeed assert this, but it will appear
incongruous to all the orthodox, since they are pastors of the visible church,
1 Corinthians 12:28; Ephesians 4:11–12, which is one. Consider what the
celebrated Voetius has in *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 2, book 2, treatise 3,
chapter 3, question 1, pages 431–433.

20. It would also follow from this that when a pastor was removed from the
church that had previously called or elected him, he would by that very fact
be deprived and divested of the ministry. Who does not see how absurd this is?

21. It would then also follow that the conversion of the Indians could not be
accomplished by the work and labor of constituted pastors, because no one is a
pastor before he is elected by some church. But he cannot be called or elected
by a church of Indians before they are converted and a church is consti-

[Volume II; printed p. 137; ONB canvas 145]

tuted. Indeed, according to this opinion it is impossible for any other parish
to be converted by a pastor as such. This is contrary to right reason,
Scripture, and the end of the ministry.

22. If this position stands, it is also necessary for the church to be prior
to the minister both in time and in nature, because the minister has his whole
*being* and all ministerial power, at least instrumentally, from the church.
That church, however, will consist either of unbaptized persons or of those who
were baptized by someone other than a minister, yet it will be a church that
can create a pastor.

I acknowledge that there are some who place the foundation of the ministry or
ecclesiastical power in election. But if they are rightly understood, they do
not contradict us. By *election* they do not mean the people’s bare act by
which they choose this man over another by votes, but the whole thing that
constitutes calling, except only the external rite of laying on of hands. For
they explain themselves in this way: “Coronation does not make a king, nor
inauguration a prince, but either succession by hereditary right or election,
without which a kingdom cannot lawfully be obtained or possessed. It can,
however, be possessed without coronation, as political writers discuss. For
there are many kingdoms in which there is no coronation or anointing, such as
the kingdoms of Castile and Portugal.” Again: “It is proved that consecration
is not the foundation or cause by which the ministry is conferred, because it
is a manifestation, declaration, promulgation, signification, and solemn
testimony before the church that the ministry has been conferred upon this or
that person.” Again: “Consecration or investiture can be absent in some case,
but election never can—because consecration, which political writers call
inauguration, is nothing other than a commission outwardly exhibited by words
and certain signs. That is, it is a calling, mission, or commission with a
certain manner, and that manner is not of the essence of calling.” Hence it is
clear that they distinguish this manner

[Volume II; printed p. 138; ONB canvas 146]

from mission itself. They do not wish the ministry to be founded upon the
manner—that is, upon the rite—and so they make no point against us. This can
also be amply demonstrated from their other statements. There is no need to
bring them all here; a few will suffice. They say that *calling, election, and
appointment* are the same. Indeed, in a certain way they distinguish
*election* from *nomination and presentation*, which they say tend toward
election. They say that *ordination, appointment or induction, and solemn
declaration* depend upon election, and they hold *ordination* to be the same
as *solemnization, investiture, or confirmation*. They speak of a separable
adjunct or consequent that cannot be had in times of persecution, when neither
the people nor the presbyters can meet together so that the minister may be
confirmed before the face of the whole church. Elsewhere they say there is no
difference between *mission and calling*, Romans 1:1; Luke 6:13; Acts 15:25.
They say that *to call, send, appoint, give, elect, and elect by votes* are
synonyms, from Luke 6:13; John 6:70; Romans 1:1, 5 with Galatians 1:15; Hebrews
5 with Romans 10:14; John 20; Ephesians 4; Acts 14:22; 1 Timothy 1:12; Titus
1:5. Indeed, they say that *to sanctify, consecrate, separate, set apart, and
lay on hands* are synonyms with *to call and send*, Romans 1:1, 5 with
Galatians 1:15; John 10:36; Acts 13:2–4. And they grant that the one “who is not
called by God through a lawful ecclesiastical calling—that is, through persons
endowed with the power to ordain—is not called.” They hold *mission* to be the
same as *commission*, “by which some office or care is entrusted to someone in
full, or at least he is taken into a share of it by the one who has the care.
This,” they say, “is nothing other than to call and appoint. For as mission
denotes the action or passion, or a certain relation of the sender to the one
sent and of the one sent to the sender, so also do calling and ordination.” So
they say: “Ordination is to thrust out into the harvest, send into the
vineyard, entrust the feeding of the sheep, commit the gospel, set someone
over”

[Volume II; printed p. 139; ONB canvas 147]

“the church, send him to preach, and appoint him over the nations.”

Before we undertake to resolve some principal objections—for there is no need
to touch upon them all—it will be worthwhile to see how the contrary position
accords with the Socinians’ doctrine.

Smalcius, *On Ecclesiastical Order*, page 377: “We do not deny that from that
custom of the primitive apostolic church it follows that what was done formerly
can also rightly be done today. If the church had always remained uncorrupted,
we do not doubt that it would have been very right for that manner of ordaining
ministers to be observed, just as even now, where a Christian commonwealth has
been constituted, we think it very fitting for it to be observed.” I do not
know whether Velthuysen will concede as much to us as this Socinian does. “But
this is not chiefly what is asked. The question is whether this kind of
appointment is absolutely necessary to constitute a minister of God’s word.”
He means the necessity of precept, which we defend. Thus he says in the same
place: “Is this kind of appointment absolutely necessary to constitute a
minister of God’s word? We deny this. Nothing of the sort—which is the chief
point—is found in the description of the things required for appointing a
bishop, though all the things without which that office cannot subsist must be
enumerated there.” Likewise: “No account is to be taken of the fact that those
who teach others have neither been called nor sent by others, provided they are
fit to discharge that office according to the apostolic canon.” Likewise page
386: “We are certainly persuaded, and have also taught above, that all the
things done concerning priests before Christ have no bearing on Christians.
The things done in the time of Christ and the apostles can indeed be done and
therefore are also observed by us, but there is nothing that compels us to
hold that they must necessarily be done.” And page 374: “We do not deny that
Paul appointed presbyters. We admit that a definite manner of appointing them
was observed in succeeding centuries. But”

[Volume II; printed p. 140; ONB canvas 148]

“Frantzius has not yet touched the matter at its sharp point or reached its
core. Let it be taught that this must be done with all who announce God’s will,
and let it be taught either by the testimony of Holy Scripture or by a reason
drawn from it, and the matter will be settled.”

The *Racovian Catechism*, chapter 2, page 344: “Do those who teach in the
church and watch over the preservation and maintenance of order need to be
sent in some special manner? Response: Not at all.” And question 15: “Hence the
apostle, when expressly describing everything that pertains to appointing
persons of this kind, makes no mention of mission.” And elsewhere, *On
Christ’s Church*: “When persons of this kind are appointed according to the
prescript of apostolic doctrine and excel in these two things—innocence of life
and aptitude to teach—they ought, on account of such an appointment, deservedly
to find just authority among everyone.” Theophilus Nicolaides, chapter 10,
pages 86–89: “Let him prove that no one can be a minister of God’s word unless
sent by someone. We believe that it can happen without a mediated mission,
that someone is permitted to teach others provided he has an aptitude for
teaching them. The office of teaching is not so much an honor as a labor. It
is always permitted to undertake a labor, but not honors. Ministers do not
therefore take the office of teaching for themselves. A person takes something
for himself when he arrogates what is not owed to him. But the office of
teaching is owed to those who are fit to teach and blameless in life. If a
calling is necessary, it is owed to one who is fit to teach and blameless in
life by those in whose power that election lies, if indeed it lies in anyone’s
power. If it is not offered to him, he can lawfully discharge the office of
teaching others—indeed, he ought to discharge it.” And page 98: “I had also
indicated in this answer of mine that this passage does not bear on the point,
because the office of teaching is not so much an honor as a labor. It is
always permitted to undertake a labor, but”

[Volume II; printed p. 141; ONB canvas 149]

“not honors. Bercovius omitted this. I now add that even if the discussion were
ultimately about offices or ministries in general that pertain to God, this
whole passage must nevertheless be referred only to the Old Covenant. For in
it God’s commands concerning that ordination stood expressly, but in the New
there is not even the slightest trace of this ordination as commanded and
ordinary.” And page 101: “But I say that this inference is correct: if it is
permitted to desire the office of teaching, then it is also permitted to
undertake it. For ‘to desire’ in that passage is nothing other than to
undertake the office of teaching. Who could doubt that a person who desired
the episcopate in his mind, apart from discharging it, had a good thing in
mind, so that it was necessary for the apostle to say this? But because of its
great dignity, it could be doubted whether someone might spontaneously take
that office for himself and undertake it without a special mission, calling,
or election. Paul therefore teaches that this is rightly done and that anyone
can rightfully undertake it, provided he is fit for what he thinks of or
desires to undertake. Paul immediately describes this fitness at length, even
if in the end the man is called or sent by no one.” Similarly page 97: “Whether
it is permitted to teach without a mission—that is, whether one who is fit to
teach others and blameless in life is permitted to teach others Christ’s
gospel without a preceding human calling and permission given by others, which
he calls a mediated mission. But this must chiefly be noted here: no mission
that proceeds from human beings, or mediated mission, exists. Mission belongs
to God alone and to Jesus Christ; and thus even the apostles themselves could
not, properly speaking, have sent anyone, nor do we read that they did.” And
page 120: “The one who does not reveal new things neither needs nor can have a
mission. The apostles needed a mission, not the ministers who now exist.”
Hence it is clear that the position we reject is Socinian.

Let us now proceed to resolve the objections.

Objection I. Pastor and flock are correlatives and exist together by nature,
and once foundation and terminus are posited, they exist in the subject. But
the church’s election and *cheirotonia* is the sole foundation of the ministry,
and the particular church is the terminus in which the correlative exists.

Response. A pastor has a twofold relation: one to the catholic church and the
other to that particular church. We grant that the foundation of this latter
relation is the people’s election or calling, because it follows from it that
this pastor is pastor of that flock and that flock is the flock of this
pastor. In this respect they certainly exist together by nature. But insofar as
he is a pastor, in first actuality pastor of the whole catholic church—that is,
able to discharge the pastoral office in any part of the church, at least as
regards the power of order—the foundation of this relation is different, just
as its terminus is different. It is ordination, by which he is made a pastor,
set apart, sanctified, separated, and consecrated to God to exercise this
office. The people’s election does not make a pastor but, properly speaking,
presupposes that the person they call is a pastor. For they call a pastor to
discharge the pastoral office among them. Similarly, when I choose this
physician, this teacher, or this craftsman, I do not make him a physician,
teacher, or craftsman, but apply him or his work to myself. Thus by this
calling only an application to this particular church occurs. Therefore, in
the order of nature someone becomes a pastor before he becomes pastor of this
or that church, although election and ordination sometimes occur at the same
time. This avoids absolute and general ordinations without assignment to a
definite flock, which were condemned even by the Council of Chalcedon.
Concerning these unattached individuals, see Didoclavius in *The Altar of
Damascus*, pages 208–209, 232, 570–572.

Objection 2. The foundation of marriage and the marital union

[Volume II; printed p. 143; ONB canvas 151]

is mutual consent. Thus pastoral sheep can choose a pastor for themselves just
as a bride chooses a bridegroom for herself.

Response. 1. An analogy illustrates; it does not prove. It serves for display,
not for battle. 2. Mutual consent between the flock and pastor indeed makes
the pastor pastor of that flock, as stated, but it does not constitute him a
pastor. 3. The analogy limps because: (1.) Through mutual consent a union is
formed between bridegroom and bride that is dissoluble only by death, adultery,
or obstinate and persistent desertion. But the union between pastor and church
is not so firm. (2.) A wife is bound to cleave to her husband no less than the
husband is bound to cleave to his wife. Yet individual private persons from
the flock can leave the pastor whenever they wish and live elsewhere to
conduct their private affairs. (3.) A husband becomes the head of his wife,
but a pastor is not the head of the church. (4.) In some case a pastor can
discharge his office among a people without this consent preceding, but a
bridegroom cannot take his bride against her will. (5.) A bridegroom or husband
cannot exercise marital power elsewhere or over another wife, but a pastor can
discharge his office anywhere in the world. (6.) A wife ought not subject
herself to another husband, but a people can—and indeed sometimes ought—to
hear another pastor and obey him when he commands in the Lord.

Objection 3. The election of a magistrate makes a magistrate. Therefore the
election of a pastor also makes a pastor.

Response. The inference is null. (1.) The power of electing a magistrate
belongs to the people by nature, apart from any positive institution of God,
and nature teaches everyone that a people is free to create a magistrate and
set him over themselves. But the power of electing a pastor depends upon God’s
positive institution and is God’s free gift. (2.) The people are said to
create a magistrate in 2 Chronicles 29:22; 1 Chronicles 12:28; 1 Samuel 11:15.
Yet we nowhere read of the people creating a pastor; we always see a pastor
created by pastors,

[Volume II; printed p. 144; ONB canvas 152]

1 Timothy 4:14; 2 Timothy 2:2; Titus 1:5. (3.) A people can limit the prince
they elect, but the people cannot limit a pastor in that way. (4.) A people can
determine how long that magistrate will rule the commonwealth—one year, two
years, or three years—but a people cannot limit a pastor in this way with
respect to time. (5.) A people can change its form of government from
democratic to monarchical and thus create new ministers and replace the
former. But this is not permitted to the church: she cannot change ministers
or create new ones. (6.) A magistrate created by this city cannot exercise a
magistracy in any other city, nor does he by that fact become a magistrate with
respect to the whole world. But a pastor is a pastor everywhere and can
discharge his office. Therefore he has something other than what a magistrate
has from the people, and the rationale is far different.

Objection 4. Ordination is nothing other than solemnization, inauguration, or
a public declaration of the calling. It therefore does not make a pastor,
just as coronation does not make a king or inauguration a prince, but
succession by hereditary right or election does.

Response. 1. If ordination is understood as the rite of laying on of hands,
the argument can be granted, because nothing follows from it against us. But
if it is understood as it ought and commonly is—as the act of ministers who
ministerially send the ordained person into the Lord’s vineyard and separate
and consecrate him to discharge this office, or as an authoritative mission,
as it is called—it is false that ordination is nothing other than
solemnization or that it no more makes a pastor than coronation makes a king.
2. Symbolic theology is not argumentative. An analogy proves nothing. 3. In
the answer to the preceding objection, we showed that although the people’s
election makes a king, it does not follow that the church’s election makes a
pastor. The ordina-

[Volume II; printed p. 145; ONB canvas 153]

tion of a pastor therefore differs greatly from the inauguration of a prince.
4. Once the common election that generally precedes ordination has occurred,
the elected person may nevertheless be neither a pastor nor their pastor, for
the presbytery must examine him. What if he is found unfit? Must they ordain
him? If not, he will be neither a pastor nor their pastor. Therefore election
does not make a pastor, for after election has occurred the elected person may
nevertheless not become a pastor. Ordination is therefore something other than
the coronation of a king or inauguration of a prince, because when ordination
has not occurred he is not made a pastor, and when it occurs a pastor is
created. But a man can be—and often is—a king without coronation.

Objection 5. A case may arise in which this ordination cannot be had: if a
church is gathered on some inaccessible or unknown island where no ministry
has yet been constituted, or if all pastors are consumed by the savagery of
war, a flood, an epidemic, or something similar. But the faithful would be
perpetually deprived of all external means of salvation because there would
be no one to ordain others.

Response. Our assertion was limited to ordinary cases in which there is no
such insuperable difficulty. We readily grant that in a case of necessity,
which does not observe law but rather makes law, many things are permitted
that cannot rightfully be done outside that case. In the proposed or a similar
case, we do not deny that someone endowed with gifts can act as pastor even
though other pastors are lacking to impose the office upon him ministerially
and instrumentally, and that he can be a lawful pastor though not ordained.
Yet if an opportunity afterward presents itself, I judge that he ought not to
hold Christ’s institution in contempt. Nor in that case does he need to await
some calling immediately revealed from heaven, because God amply testifies to
a lawful calling by the gifts of the Holy Spirit with which he is enriched,
by divine providence arranging matters in this way, by pressing necessity, and
by the circumstances requiring it.

[Volume II; printed p. 146; ONB canvas 154]

But we are speaking about the ordinary, established manner, which cannot be
overturned or weakened by some singular and extraordinary case. I acknowledge
that in some case a pastor can be sent to an unwilling people—for example, if
the people are heretical and obstinately refractory, and peremptorily declare
that they wish to have no pastor who is not a heretic. Yet this extraordinary
case must not prejudice the ordinary manner. Nor can it be concluded from this
that the people’s calling or election is not required. See what Polyander says
in the *Synopsis*, disputation 42, theses 42 and 44.

Objection 6. By this rationale we will defend that papal succession for which
our adversaries sharply contend.

Response. 1. We do not say with the papists that a *local* succession of the
catholic church must be believed, as though that succession were necessarily
bound to a particular nation, city, or church. 2. Nor do we say that a
*personal* succession of the church—that is, an uninterrupted succession of
pastors from head to head—must be believed with firm faith. 3. Nor do we say
that this uninterrupted succession of persons must be demonstrably proved in
order to receive the true faith. 4. With the celebrated Voetius, *Desperate
Cause of the Papacy*, book 3, section 1, chapter 1, we deny that every
succession, as such, formally entails continuity. For not every interruption
abolishes succession. 5. Our writers deny that papal succession—that is, an
uninterrupted local succession of the church or personal succession from head
to head, manifestly known and demonstrated among pastors and teachers—is a
mark of the church. Yet they grant a real succession, that is, a succession in
the truth and apostolic doctrine, which was necessarily propagated through
human beings, although they by no means judge it necessary for all those who
successively propagated that doctrine to be described and demon-

[Volume II; printed p. 147; ONB canvas 155]

strated. Therefore we can grant that a personal succession joined with the
real one exists, although we cannot demonstrate it and do not judge knowledge
of it necessary, as the papists do. See how the most distinguished Voetius
proves in the previously cited chapter 2 “that a demonstration of this
uninterrupted hand-to-hand succession is neither necessary nor principal”; in
chapter 5, “that a true succession of the church can exist even if the
demonstration and knowledge of personal succession should fail somewhere”;
and in chapter 6, “that a demonstration of this personal succession from head
to head is not in itself and absolutely necessary to faith.” In chapter 7 he
proves that “true succession does not exist without truth of doctrine.”

Objection 7. If this ordination is so necessary, then it must be said that all
present-day ministers are by no means ministers because they have not been
lawfully ordained. Or it must be said that Antichristian ministers truly were
ministers and consequently that the Roman church truly was the church. The
reason is that the first Reformers—Luther, Zwingli, and others—had their
ordination from the Roman church. They ordained others, and so on in succession
to our times.

Response. 1. This objection does nothing against our assertion. We are
speaking about the ordinary manner of constituting pastors and except
extraordinary cases. We may suppose the condition of our first Reformers to
have been such a case, for at that time they could not without sin submit to
or embrace Roman ordination. Since no one is bound to violate God’s law, that
case can be called extraordinary and an unavoidable necessity. Therefore,
even if we granted that those Reformers had not been ordained, our assertion
would nevertheless remain firm, as shown above. The most distinguished
Voetius, in *Desperate Cause of the Papacy*, book 2, section 1, chapter 6,
denies that because the Reformed ministers had a calling in and from the papal

[Volume II; printed p. 148; ONB canvas 156]

church, it follows that the papacy was the true church, free from corruption.
He confirms the denial in this way: “There was a calling among heretics, as
Jerome proves in the *Dialogue Against the Luciferians*, and yet the true
church did not exist among them, much less was it there alone. It is the same
as if we argued: the papists acknowledge that true baptism exists among us;
therefore, they acknowledge that the true church exists among us. The calling
and ordination of the Novatians were preserved among the catholics, and those
who passed from them to the catholics were received without a new ordination.
Yet it does not follow that the Novatians constituted the catholic church. In
times of schisms in the church, very many had ordination only from schismatic
churches, bishops, and popes. Yet they would not wish it to be concluded from
this that the schismatic faction constituted the lawful and sole Roman church.
Among the Greeks, Armenians, Ethiopians, and Abyssinians, something of
Christianity, ministry, calling, or ordination still exists today, as the
papists acknowledge, yet they will not grant that these therefore constitute
the true and sole catholic church. In a dying body torn by many ulcers or
wounds, life still exists, and the principle of life—the soul—is united to
that body. Therefore, that ulcer and wound is not a wound; indeed, that ulcer
alone is the soul and life, the principle of life, in which all the activity
of life resides? How absurd the consequence! Under a usurping or abusive
tyrant, there is some true polity of the people, some administration of the
kingdom’s officers, and some administration of justice and judgment.
Therefore, the tyrant and the tyrannical faction alone is the lawful polity,
and tyranny is justice? If the major premise is framed in this way: they had
their calling in and from that church; therefore, some true church and some
ministry, though contaminated, still existed there—the consequence is good.
The early Reformers granted it, and according to our position there is nothing
absurd in it. When Israel and Ju-”

[Volume II; printed p. 149; ONB canvas 157]

“dah were contaminated by idolatry, some form of the church, ministry, and
sacraments still existed. Thus, although on account of their abominations God
called them children of the Ethiopians, the people of Gomorrah, and so forth,
yet on account of the covenant and the covenanters who were still among them,
he called them his people, their children, and his sons, Ezekiel 16:21. For
just as an adulterous wife is a wife as long as her husband has not repudiated
her, so also there is still in some respect a Christian church, though
contaminated by errors and idolatry, as long as God in his mercy does not send
a bill of divorce—as he did to the Muslims—by removing the tablets and seals
of the covenant, that is, his word and sacraments, and thus all the first
principles and fundamental articles of the faith.” See more in the same place
and section 2, chapter 8.

3. Therefore, our writers do not say that the Roman church was then in no way
a church, for in treating this subject they employ a great many distinctions:
1. between the Roman curia and the deceived people; 2. between the church
considered materially and formally; 3. between the church in the concrete and
in the abstract; 4. between a church physically and metaphysically true and a
church ethically and morally true; 5. between the assembly that calls and the
assembly that is called; 6. between the teaching church and the church that
uses; 7. between the church in the process of becoming, or according to
pastoral or ecclesiastical being, and the church in accomplished being, or
according to Christian being; 8. between the church considered speculatively
and practically; 9. between the church considered in a certain respect and
simply; 10. between the church that professes the rejection of the foundation
and the one that professes the foundation; 11. between the church’s name in
the process of becoming and in accomplished being; 12. between a pastoral act
ratified by formal consent and the same act finally and ecclesiastically
ratified; 13. they also say it is one thing for the Roman church to be saved
and another for salvation to exist in the Roman church. See how our Rutherford
applies these distinctions in *Examination of Arminianism*, pages 65–66 and
following. Therefore, if the Roman church was in some respect a church, it also
had in some respect a ministry.

4. If this argument is firm in any way, it can no less prove that our baptism
is now null, because our first Reformers were bap-

[Volume II; printed p. 150; ONB canvas 158]

tized in the Roman church, and that our churches are now null because they
consist of members marked by no baptism other than the one that descended to
us through that corrupted church. Indeed, if they say that nothing pure could
be derived to us through that Antichristian church, God’s own word and the
truths propagated to us hand to hand, even in that church, will have to be
rejected. But we must distinguish between truth and the errors attached to it.
Truth is always truth, by whomever it is received. Although that synagogue of
Satan professes fundamental truths, far be it from us to reject them. Although
it corrupted them by attaching many errors, far be it from us to reject the
gold itself with the dross. So Christ’s ordinations and institutions are
always Christ’s institutions and must be held and acknowledged as such,
although they are corrupted through human fault. Baptism is baptism and God’s
institution, although that Antichristian assembly vitiated and corrupted it by
its additions. So ordination, though from that corrupted church, remains
Christ’s true institution.

5. Therefore, with the celebrated Voetius, *Desperate Cause of the Papacy*,
book 2, section 2, chapter 8, we must distinguish between the defect of calling
and function and the calling and function themselves. “Distinguish,” he says,
“the true and essential acts of ecclesiastical function from the superadded
human inventions, which are nothing other than the deformity, corruption, and
abuse of those acts. Just as in a warty and scabby body, the parts of the body
must be distinguished from the warts or scabs. Whatever belonged to calling
and function was derived to our people from Christ through the church as such,
if indeed under the papacy there was still some church—which nevertheless was
not the papacy, any more than the body is the scab or disease. Whatever defect
adhered to the calling was affixed by the papacy, papal function, the Roman
curia, or the corruption then spreading and prevailing in the church. Our
people separated and rejected the defect but retained the power and calling
themselves.” We must therefore carefully distin-

[Volume II; printed p. 151; ONB canvas 159]

guish between what flowed to our people from the corrupted and diseased body
and what flowed from the corruption or disease itself. Although the ordination
of the Reformed theologians flowed from a corrupted body, a corrupted church,
it did not flow from the corruption itself, for it is Christ’s institution.
Jansen therefore erred when he argued in this way: if the Roman church, and
thus its power of calling, was not sound and whole but thoroughly corrupted,
then either the Reformers’ power was not derived from it or it was derived
from anathema, abominations, and corruptions. For as Voetius says excellently
in the place cited above, the consequence of the major premise is utterly
false. “It is the same,” he says, “as if I concluded: a sinful act comes from
the intellect and will, but they are corrupted. Therefore, all the vitality of
that act comes from the fault or corruption of the will. A limping gait is
produced by a lame horse. Therefore, that whole gait or animal motion comes
from the defect of the foot, or no gait exists there. Vital functions are
derived from a diseased heart. Therefore, no vitality exists in those
functions; or it must be said that it is derived from the disease or bad
condition of the heart; or the heart is falsely said to be afflicted with a
deadly evil. The jurisdiction and power of inferior magistrates and judges
depends upon or is derived from a tyrannical government, or from the political
power to which tyranny and a tyrannical faction adhere. Therefore, it is
derived from tyranny, and consequently either that power is null, or there is
no tyranny in that commonwealth and it is falsely said to be such.
Ecclesiastical commissions and functions once depended in the Jewish church
upon the idolatrous and apostate high priest Uriah, 2 Kings 16:11, 16.
Therefore, they depended upon idolatry and apostasy.” Thus, as he says there,
page 162: “From that papality as such we derive nothing of ministry, calling,
doctrine, succession, or salvation—neither from it as a first or principal
cause, an instrumental cause, nor a cause without which it could not exist.
But we derive these from the Western church insofar as it still participated
in the nature of the church,”

[Volume II; printed p. 152; ONB canvas 160]

“as an instrumental cause, in whose communion the Reformers were born,
educated, and ordained.”

It would also be futile for anyone to say with Jansen that the authority of
the ecclesiastical ministry and of sending workers into Christ’s vineyard was
granted only to the true church, not to heretics, schismatics, and idolaters.
Therefore, either the Roman church had no authority if it was idolatrous, or it
was not idolatrous if it had that authority. For “this consequence is null,”
as Voetius says there. “What was not granted to heretics, idolaters,
Antichrist, and so forth in no way exists or remains in an idolatrous church,
a church infected with heresy, or one subjected to Antichrist’s tyranny.” It
is indeed true that this power was by no means granted by Christ to the pope as
such or to papal defects and abuses as such. Yet it does not follow that no
power or pastoral function existed in that church which was infected with
heresy and ruled by Antichrist.

6. We must distinguish between a calling and ordination that is evil and
corrupt from the intention of the actor and ordainer, and an ordination and
calling corrupted from the intention of the work and of the ordination itself.
The latter is indeed null and must in no way be acknowledged as a true and
lawful ordination or calling. But the former must not be wholly rejected, for
Christ’s true and excellent institution can be made evil and corrupted by the
actor’s intention, while Christ’s institution itself always remains good.
Therefore, although the Reformers’ ordination was evil with respect to the evil
intention of those ordaining and was corrupted to that extent, it was
nevertheless good in itself and from the intention of the work.

7. We must also distinguish between God’s ordinance or institution itself and
the faults or sins of those who are ordained or appointed. Those who, when
ordained, solemnly promise to defend errors—such as the papacy or papal
heresy—sin grievously, yet the ordination itself remains God’s true

[Volume II; printed p. 153; ONB canvas 161]

institution. When the ordained person, convicted of sin, repents and rejects
and refutes the errors he had promised to defend, his ordination is not
annihilated, nor does he need to be ordained again. Similarly, one who is
baptized in the Roman church and at least implicitly promises to defend that
church, corrupt though it is, but afterward, when better instructed, rejects
it and regards it as utterly corrupt, does not need baptism to be repeated.
For by baptism, from the nature of the thing, he was received into the bosom of
Christ’s true church as such. So by ordination, from the nature of the thing,
the pastor had power granted to him to announce the gospel, administer the
sacraments, and so forth.

8. We must distinguish between an ordination in which the essentials are
absent and one in which many things required for duly performing ordination
are absent. The former is null; the latter can be valid. For every error in the
manner does not destroy the essence of the thing. A person who acquired this
dignity for himself through simony, or who was ordained not by the presbytery
but by a prelate, can be regarded as an ordained pastor. Caiaphas was high
priest even though, contrary to the law, he discharged this office for only
one year. Therefore, although there were many corruptions in the papacy with
respect to ordination, both on the part of those ordaining and those ordained,
ordination remains Christ’s institution and is valid.

9. It is one thing to receive *ordination lawfully* and another to receive *a
lawful ordination*. The truth of this distinction is clear from what precedes.
Although the Reformers did not lawfully receive ordination from those papal
prelates, they nevertheless had a lawful ordination. Those who are baptized in
the Roman church sin and do not lawfully receive baptism, yet they have lawful
baptism.

10. We must distinguish between God’s forum and the human forum. In God’s
forum someone can be no true pastor and rightfully and deservedly fall from
the power

he had, so that rightfully and in good conscience he cannot exercise that
power or discharge that office any longer—for example, when he falls into
heresy, schism, or idolatry. Yet he does not immediately fall from that power
with respect to us. A tyrant falls from his right before God, or in God’s
forum, but all his authority with respect to us does not immediately perish,
so long as he is tolerated by those whose concern it is. Nor are all his
public or royal actions null or invalid. Hence it is clear that although in
God’s forum those papal prelates and other members of that hierarchy were not
pastors, all their ministerial acts were not therefore immediately annihilated
with respect to us.

11. We must also distinguish between a sound and whole ministry and a ministry
corrupted in many respects. Although the papal ministry was not healthy,
sound, and whole, something of ministry was nevertheless there, or a diseased
and corrupted ministry. A diseased body can exercise actions that are truly
vital, although not altogether healthy and whole.
