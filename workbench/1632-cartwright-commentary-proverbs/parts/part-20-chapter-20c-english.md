Verse 9. Who can say, I have cleansed my heart? I am pure from my sin?

The purpose is to render everyone liable to God and to declare them unfit to expiate their own guilt.

By this interrogation—“Who,” and so forth—it is denied more forcefully that there is anyone who is pure or can cleanse himself than if he had done this by an affirmative proposition.

There have been no lack of people to answer the one who poses this question, nor are such people still lacking: I am pure; I have cleansed my heart. In the times of Christ and the Apostle there were Pharisees and other justiciaries who did not hesitate to profess that they were just and justified by their own proper merit. Today there are also Anabaptists, and among the papists there are those who assert that the Virgin was without sin and who claim for themselves the praise of purity and cleanness. But the Prophet’s sense is that the question is not who says it, but who says it truly and in such a way that he can offer a solid account of his statement. Hence it must also be noted in passing that we ought to say a thing, provided that what we say is true.

The truth of this Proverb is confirmed by the testimony of David at Psalm 143: [If you should preserve iniquities, O Lord, etc.] Likewise at 1 John 1: [If anyone says that he has no sin, etc.] Likewise by Eliphaz at Job 9: [You cannot answer him one thing out of a thousand.] It is further argued from a comparison with the greater at chapter 4.18–19: [that he placed no firmness in his servants], or truth, [nor set glory and light even in the Angels; much less in human beings, who dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is dust, and who are crushed more quickly than a moth.] So also chapter 15, verses 14–16.

The clearest indication of this matter—or rather its μαρτύριον (*testimony*)—is that all the holiest persons among human beings are astounded not only at the voice and vision of God, but also at an Angel’s, and fall down as though dead, as is plainly evident in John the Apostle and Daniel. Yet if we were free from sin, we would speak fearlessly with God himself, as Adam did before he fell into sin.

An example of this doctrine is evident in Paul, who, though lifted up to the third heaven, nevertheless professes that he did what he did not approve, Rom. 7.15; and that in those things in which he was conscious of no wickedness in himself, he was nevertheless not justified, 1 Cor. 4.4. As for the statement at 1 John that the person who is from God does not sin, this is understood strictly of sin against the Holy Spirit, which the godly cannot commit.

Now, as for us who are smeared with mire, who is there to take us out of this mire, since we are blind, so that we cannot see the remedy or bath; lame, so that even after it is known we cannot approach it; maimed or disabled in our hands, so that we cannot grasp it though it is present and before our eyes; and finally dead in sins, so that we cannot move either foot or finger?

Nor does anything proceed from us who are regenerate which is not defiled at least in some part. Thus the cleansing which comes from us is not unlike the cleansing of those concerning whom the Proverb says, ὄνος ὄνον κνάπτει, “an ass scratches an ass.” It is true, indeed, that David says that he cleansed himself.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 435 | printed columns 844-845]

At Psalm 73, and likewise James 3, he exhorts sinners to cleanse their hands and clean their heart. But neither is that cleansing perfect, nor is the cleansing which occurs accomplished by any powers of our own, but by the Spirit of God.

There remains, therefore, the sole mercy of God in Christ, which, being apprehended through faith, cleanses us in the blood of the Son of God and renders us pure.

Verse 10. Stone and stone; measure and measure: both alike are an abomination to the Lord.

In אבן (*lapis*, stone) there is a synecdoche of the part for every material from which weights are made. So also in איפה (*mensura*, measure) there is a synecdoche of the species for every kind of measure, whether of dry or liquid things. There is also a metonymy of the efficient cause, namely, for the fraud and injustice which is practised by those weights. For God is not angry with stones, and so forth, but and so forth. Finally, in תועבת (*abominatio*, abomination) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for an abominable thing.

The purpose is to stir human minds to hatred of that fraud which is committed against commutative justice.

Proverbs of this kind have been proposed previously at 16.11, but especially at 11.1, so that the difference between this one and that is very slight. Yet these things are not repeated without fruit and benefit. First, in this Proverb the measure or bushel is expressed, which is not numbered in the preceding Proverbs, so that it may meet those who often cling so tenaciously to the words themselves that they suppose everything which is not plainly and expressly forbidden to remain free and unimpaired for them. Hence the insipid questions: Where are *cyclades*, where are dances, and so forth, forbidden? At chapter 11.1, therefore, he makes mention of scale-pans and weights; at 16.11, of the beam; but in this place, of the measure and bushel, so that he may condemn every kind of fraud and circumvention in exchange.

Next, at chapter 11 he noted one kind of fraud, when, namely, the weight is lighter or less than justice requires. In this place, however, he indicates another fraud: when a thing is bought with one weight or measure and the same thing is sold with another; or when people use just weights with those who are more cautious and more experienced in the knowledge of weights, but deceitful ones with the inexperienced and unwary.

Since this matter is an abomination to God, it ought altogether to be hateful also to the Magistrate, who sustains the person of God.

Nor is it without emphasis that he says that the stone and measure are an abomination to God. For by this formula of speaking the prophet testifies to a greater detestation than if he had said that God abominates fraud. For we see that God sometimes brands the mark of his wrath for the sake of greater detestation upon the very instruments that lack life or reason: upon the serpent, and upon the gold from which the calf was cast; likewise upon the ground where human blood was poured out; and upon the goring ox which kills a human being. For the remaining things which pertain to this place, see above at 11.1 and 16.11.

This may not inappropriately be transferred to the equity of the judgment which is pronounced before the tribunal and to ἀπροσωποληψίαν (*impartiality*), both in civil judgment and in the Church.

Verse 11. Even by his actions a boy makes himself known, whether his work is pure and upright.

The purpose is to stir parents to devote their care to the upright education of their children.

“Boy” is taken in this place for one who has passed beyond infancy, such as one who has completed his fourth or fifth year. It is customary and usual for parents to care anxiously and diligently for this age, so that the child may be well fed, clothed, and so forth. Yet no age is

[1632 witness | PDF p. 436 | printed columns 846-847]

more neglected with respect to pious and honourable education. For if anything is done wrongly by children of this age, parents pass over it on the grounds that it is childish, that it is done without judgment, and that the child himself will correct and undo it when he has become a little older. Their error—or rather their foolish indulgence—is so much the crasser and grosser because, if anything of a good disposition appears, if even a spark of virtue and piety has merely flashed forth, they receive it as a good omen of virtue and piety which will one day be established. Although by this judgment of their own they are amply convicted of their former error, nevertheless, observe, I pray, how shamefully they stumble in this very matter. For by that judgment they are not led to devote their effort to the pious education of their children. Rather, they ascribe to a certain goodness of nature and disposition that which will establish itself as age increases. Hence it happens that neglected boys and girls afterward fall headlong into every excess and wantonness.

There are others still more perverse who not only pass over the errors of boys and girls with impunity, but take pleasure in their wickedness and petulance, as though a boy’s deviation from the right way were a foretoken of future virtue. Hence that impious proverb has arisen among us: “From a shaggy colt comes a good horse.” By contrast, Solomon in this most holy proverb refutes that proverb when he teaches that childhood itself furnishes evidence of a later condition which will be holy and well-mannered, if the childhood is holy and well-mannered, or wicked, if it is otherwise. Another of our Proverbs supports it: “That which will become a dog-rose pricks early”—“Soone it pricks that thorn will be.”

This is excellently confirmed at Psalm 144, where one end of peace acquired and established is proposed: that sons and daughters may be uprightly and piously educated in their childhood. It is also confirmed by a comparison with like things, namely, tender plants, which must be cared for especially while they are still fresh, and must be nourished by those who desire a fruitful garden. This is further illustrated by the example at Tim. 2.3 of one who was nourished in the knowledge of Scripture from his very cradle. See the example of Joseph.

Therefore attention must be given to everything that boys do in play, words, deeds, and gait. Nothing in boys should be so slight that it is not worthy of inspection, so that from it we may draw out not only the art and occupation for which the child is suited, but also the virtues or vices toward which he is inclined in mind, in order that the latter may be met by setting an obstacle in their way and the former may be confirmed as they arise. For sometimes in a very small matter an indication either of virtue or of wickedness is discovered. Hence writers say that a boy who pierced out the eyes of small birds in play was condemned to the punishment of death. And a judgment is formed about them the better, the further they are removed from hypocrisy than those who have become older.

For this purpose παιδείᾳ (*training*) and instruction must be employed, and likewise νουθεσίᾳ (*admonition*), with rewards and punishments proposed which suit a child’s age, so that by the former they may be brought toward the pursuit of virtue and by the latter restrained from vices.

Verse 12. The hearing ear and the seeing eye—the Lord has made both alike.

There is a synecdoche in אזן (*aurem*, ear) and עין (*oculum*, eye). For by the natural and inborn faculty of these parts he embraces the natural faculties of all the members.

The purpose is that, having recognized God’s providence and special administration in those things which seem less to fall under it, we should depend upon him and recur to him in all things.

It is beyond controversy that God created the structure of the whole body, and that among the other parts of the body not only does God’s marvellous workmanship shine forth in these two members, but their exceptional and excellent

[1632 witness | PDF p. 437 | printed columns 848-849]

use beyond the other parts exists for attaining the knowledge of God the Creator and of all good letters. Therefore care must be taken that we do not abuse them in pursuing vain things. But a more recondite and hidden sense seems to be present in these words. For he appears to institute a discourse neither about these members nor about their unusual faculty, but about the action itself of seeing and hearing.

Since, therefore, almost everyone is persuaded that they are participants in sight and hearing and are able, whenever they wish, to see and hear whatever they wish by the inborn faculty of seeing and hearing, the Prophet refutes this error by this Proverb. He teaches that by his special providence God causes us, whenever he wishes, though seeing, in a certain way not to see, and though hearing, not to hear those things which it is important for us to see and hear. Consequently we must flee to God in prayers, so that he may direct our members and bestow strength from day to day and hour to hour, and that we may return to him with thanksgiving for things rightly done.

The proofs of this matter, so far as vision is concerned, are at Gen. 31.19, where Hagar did not see the spring of water which was before her feet, though it was of the greatest importance to her, until, after the darkness was dispelled, God showed her the spring. The same judgment applies to the blindness with which both the Sodomites at Gen. 19 and the Syrians at 2 Kings 6 were struck. Hence, finally, it happened that the two disciples who set out for Emmaus did not recognize Christ speaking with them, Luke 23; and likewise Paul’s companions, Acts 9.7. As for hearing, an example exists at Acts 22.9. What is confirmed in this place concerning the seeing of the eyes and the hearing of the ears is elsewhere affirmed of the offices of the other members, Psalms 144.1 and 18.29, 33–34. We ourselves experience this in ourselves, who are very often astounded that we do not see the things which are before our feet.

Therefore we are admonished of our weakness even in those matters in which we are most confident, as though those faculties rested with us and belonged to our choice; much less are they ours in those matters which are not within our powers, and toward which there exists not merely no propensity by nature, but the greatest aversion and alienation of mind and body.
