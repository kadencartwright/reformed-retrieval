## Chapter V

The arguments of Suárez, Fonseca, and the Arminians concerning God's new
knowledge, which they call conditional or middle, are subjected to
examination.

As I hasten to other matters, middle knowledge confronts me. It is
distinguished from the knowledge of simple intelligence and the knowledge of
vision, and is partly natural, as they say, because they maintain that such
knowledge cannot fail to be in God; and partly free—namely, from its object,
because God is free to create or not create knowable things. By this
knowledge, says Fonseca, God from the height of his knowledge foreknows all
contingent effects antecedently to the act of his decree, as Luis de Molina
holds, if causes are placed in these or those circumstances. But against this,
1. I argue as follows. That knowledge is empty whose object is unknowable,
untrue, and a non-

[Marginalia: Molina, *On Concord*, question 14, article 13, disputation 52.]

[1651 logical printed p. 188 | IA leaf 206 | PDF p. 206]

being. But since the object of this knowledge is a being that has existence
and truth antecedently to its cause—namely, God's decree—it is unknowable and
untrue. Therefore, etc. Proof of the minor. Hypothetically future contingents
have no real truth in themselves from eternity, for this is proper to God
alone. Nor can any reality belong to them by reason of an efficient cause,
namely God's decree. Suárez replies: “The object of this knowledge is not
altogether independent of God's decree, since God's decree is concomitantly
included in the object in the manner of a possible thing, although no decree
is antecedent and, according to our mode of knowing and speaking,
foreknowledge is prior to the decree.” Reply. 1. If God's decree is the cause
of the object of middle knowledge, it does not accompany but precedes the
object. 2. Those who teach that necessity from the decree is consequent, no
less than necessity from God's knowledge, are hardly very sincere when they
wish the decree to be concomitantly included in the object. 3. Observe how
timidly and fraudulently the sophist speaks: “The object is not altogether
independent of the decree.” But if it is dependent in some manner, he ought to
say whether it is causal dependence; instead, the snake wishes to hide in
thickets and brambles. 4. The decree is included in the object in the manner
of a possible thing. But if the object is determinately future, as he admits,
is the decree merely possible? Then the decree can fail to exist; yet the
future contingent is determinately future. Will the sophist therefore
demonstrate to us how the object

[Marginalia: Suárez, prologue 1, part 1, *On Divine Grace*, question 4.]

[1651 logical printed p. 189 | IA leaf 207 | PDF p. 207]

of this knowledge is not altogether independent of the decree when it exists
without any decree? 5. If no decree is antecedent, does the earlier depend on
the later? And that which possesses existence antecedently to the decree in no
way depends upon the decree in its existence and therefore in the truth of its
existing. Speak freely and do not rub your brow, I pray. 6. “According to our
mode of speaking and knowing,” you say, “foreknowledge is prior to the decree.”
But tell us from your dreams: is it prior only according to our mode of
knowing, or is it not also prior by nature and prior according to God's mode
of knowing? Indeed it is, if we believe you. 7. For the object of this
knowledge to be a being and knowable, it must have a cause. But if the decree
is later than it, the decree cannot be its cause. Give us the cause, then.
Behold where the concomitant decree has now vanished.

2. Argument 2. That knowledge is to be rejected whose object is such that,
once it is posited, the effect is reduced to created choice as the first
determinant; for God alone is the first determinant. And if the decree is
later than the object of middle knowledge, this object will ultimately be
resolved into created choice, for it cannot be resolved into God's decree as
its first determinant, since the decree is later than it. Suárez replies in
the same place, folio 7: “This is not contrary to God's omnipotence or absolute
dominion, because such an effect will not be future without the influence of
God's omnipotence, by which he wills to allow the created will to operate
freely and to concur with it if it wills. He could, if he wills, prevent that
act by conducting himself differently with the will; but if he wills to leave
it indifferent, what it has willed must be performed by it.” Reply. If

[1651 logical printed p. 190 | IA leaf 208 | PDF p. 208]

this is not contrary to God's dominion, but contingent effects are within the
power of created choice as the first determinant, in whose power it lies that
they should exist or not, James argues badly: “If the Lord wills and permits,
we will do this or that.” For if the human will is the first determinant, it
is from the person, not from God, that out of many possible journeys we take
this rather than that one. 2. His statement that God can prevent free acts is
far too loosely and irrationally stated. For if I will this rather than that
antecedently to God's decree and even to middle knowledge, as the Jesuit
admits, God cannot prevent free acts. For what already is, and what God
infallibly foreknows will be future, will infallibly be future. But let the
Jesuit say whether divine omnipotence has any place to prevent an identical
proposition from being true. 3. “The contingent effect,” he says, “will not be
future without God's will.” But what sort of will do you mean, sophist? A
consequent rather than antecedent will, and a power not actually applying
itself unless human choice wishes to act together? Thus dominion is taken from
God and transferred to the created will. 4. The Jesuit means a will of general
physical concurrence, of the kind by which he concurs with pigs eating and
sparrows flying. A gift and a prevailing grace truly worthy of the enemies of
grace! 5. At last the conquered Jesuit surrenders: if God wills to leave the
will indifferent, what it has willed must be performed. God must therefore
adjust his will and concurrence to the nod of created freedom; and God,

[Marginalia: James 4:13–15.]

[1651 logical printed p. 191 | IA leaf 209 | PDF p. 209]

conquered by fate, cannot fail to concur and will whatever the created will,
as first determinant, has willed. What sort of object of this knowledge is
this? 6. God can prevent the will, he says. But how? Either by not creating
the will, annihilating it, or necessitating it. But thus he departs from the
question, which concerns not God's dominion over the will simply, but God's
dominion over the will insofar as it is a free agent. For we say that by this
knowledge contingent acts are placed beyond and against the Creator's
dominion. I doubt that the Jesuits can untie this knot.

3. Argument 3. The object of every infallible knowledge is necessary. But
future contingents antecedently to God's decree are not necessary. Therefore
no object of this knowledge is given. The major is certain, because every
infallibility of knowledge is founded upon objective necessity. And all
metaphysicians proclaim with one voice that certainty of mind, or subjective
certainty, cannot be given apart from all certainty of being, or objective
certainty. Proof of the minor. Antecedently to God's decree and from
themselves, the Tyrians upon seeing signs are just as able not to believe and
repent as to believe and repent; from themselves, the Keilites were just as
able not to hand David, if he remained, into Saul's hands as to hand him over.
For this is the nature of their freedom: when all requisites for acting are
posited, they can act or not act.

[Marginalia: Suárez, volume *On Divine Grace*, book 2, chapter 2, section 1,
folio 6.]

[1651 logical printed p. 192 | IA leaf 210 | PDF p. 210]

4. The adversaries leave no stone unturned to remove this argument, but in
vain. For 1. Suárez says that there is a twofold determination in future
contingents: one in its cause, or in first act; the other in itself, or in
second act. He wishes God to see the latter just as if it already existed, and
to have it presented to divine knowledge, by whose efficacy he beholds not
only what the human will can do but also what it does. Reply. He begs the
question, for we believe that the determination of the contingent cause is
presented to God's infinite knowledge. Yet the question remains: in what
objective ground does God see this determination? Either he sees it present
in itself; but this presence is objective, not real (for things did not really
coexist with God from eternity), and this presence is not real in itself nor
real in its cause, namely God's decree, for they wish this object to precede
the decree, while they cannot give any cause other than God's decree. 2.
Lessius replies that God foreknows these things in the determinate existence
which they will have if this or that is posited. But the question still
remains: how does God know such an existence, if not in the inclination of the
contingent cause? For Aquinas, Valencia, Suárez, Durandus, and Vasquez say this
knowledge is conjectural and cannot

[Marginalia: Lessius, *On Efficacious Grace*, chapter 4.]

[1651 logical printed p. 193 | IA leaf 211 | PDF p. 211]

be attributed to God. 3. Valencia replies that God sees these things not in
themselves but in a contingent cause insofar as the contingent cause is
determined by itself to acting. Reply. The argument will return infinitely:
what is the objective ground in which God foreknows that free determination?
Either in another free cause, and thus the argument revives; or in another
necessary cause, and this is none other than God's decree. 4. Jackson says
that God sees all these things in his own essence, others say in the ideas,
and others in the height of infinite knowledge. But reply. 1. Things in God's
essence are possible, not future, except through God's free decree. If they
are necessary in God's essence, then antecedent necessity and contingency
agree perfectly well. Yet, as Scotus rightly observed, both God's essence and
the ideas precede every act of the divine will, and naturally represent things
under possible being. Therefore they do not represent them in the essence as
determinately future; indeed, we know possible contingents under possible
being. 2. The infinity of God's essence and knowledge is the ground on the
knower's side why he knows all things; but it is not the objective ground on
the side of the known object. Thus this departs from the state of the
question, which is whether there is an infallible objective ground of the
futurition of contingents antecedently to God's decree. We utterly deny it.

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Essence of God*, book 2, section 2, chapter 8.]

[Marginalia: Scotus, on book 1 of the *Sentences*, distinction 39, sole
question, chapter 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 194 | IA leaf 212 | PDF p. 212]

5. Argument. God reasons invincibly in 1 Samuel 23: If David remains, the
Keilites will hand him over to Saul; and if the Tyrians had seen the signs,
they would have repented. These are syllogistic conditions and are known to
God; and a good consequence is always good, so this will always be necessary:
the Tyrians see signs, therefore they repent. But if there is truth in these
things antecedently to God's decree, that decree not yet posited, this
consequence is bad, impossible, and false: the Tyrians see signs, therefore
they repent. Indeed, this consequence is equally firm: the Tyrians see signs,
therefore they do not repent. For there is no necessary connection, but one
plainly contingent and indifferent, between seeing the signs and the Tyrians'
repentance. Fonseca, Suárez, and the declaimer Grevinchovius—who owes the
Jesuits everything—reply: “God does not know the consequence together with
the antecedent, but knows these things in the manner of simple coexistence and
of an affirmation of one concerning another under such a condition; that is,
not in an inferential connection but objectively from the concurrence of the
objects of both parts of the proposition, either at the same time or in the
certainty of the divine promise or prediction.” Reply. 1. This is miserable
logic. For this reasoning belongs to God, who devised logic: “The Keilites
will deliver you into Saul's hands; therefore flee.” Indeed, they say, the
consequence is false, good God; therefore there is no reason for you to flee.
For suppose David remains: the Keilites here are free agents and therefore
are just as able not to deliver him as to deliver him. 2. They thrust upon us
connected propositions which cannot be resolved into arguments, which is

[Marginalia: Fonseca, on book 6 of the *Metaphysics*, chapter 2, question 4,
section 8.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, little work 1, book 2, *On Conditional Knowledge*,
chapter 5.]

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 401.]

[1651 logical printed p. 195 | IA leaf 213 | PDF p. 213]

to concede the whole while denying its parts and to deny that the whole
consists of its parts. On this we appeal as judges to everyone skilled in
logic—Aristotle, Ramus, and who not? Either it is day or it is night. Before
this time it always contained a syllogistic connection: it is not day;
therefore it is night. So here they give us new connected propositions along
with their new knowledge. 3. This simple coexistence is wholly invented and
false, for the sight of the signs and the Tyrians' repentance cohere with each
other just as contingently as Socrates' walking at the tenth hour and the
sun's shining and flashing. Indeed, antecedently to God's decree, they cohere
either necessarily or contingently. For the necessary and the contingent
divide the whole breadth of being and are contradictorily opposed, says
Arminius. But these new logicians deny that every being is either necessary or
contingent. They give a middle between contradictory opposites, although all
candidates in metaphysics confess that these principles have stood unshaken.
If the two cohere contingently, they are not knowable; if necessarily, we
demand a cause of the necessity other than God's decree. 4. God sees these
things in simple coexistence. But this begs the question. That coexistence is
contingent and therefore is not visible even to infinite knowledge unless it
is necessitated in some manner. 5. All God's promises in Scripture are
argumentative for us; otherwise God would reason sophistically in this
scriptural consequence: I have promised; therefore this contingent effect
will irresistibly

[1651 logical printed p. 196 | IA leaf 214 | PDF p. 214]

occur. Jeremiah 32:40; Ezekiel 36:27; Deuteronomy 30:6; Genesis 17:8;
Zechariah 12:10; Joel 2:28; Malachi 1:11. 5. If their coexistence is resolved
into God's promising will, then this promising will is either subsequent or
antecedent to the promised thing. If subsequent, God promises in Ezekiel
36:27 that he will cause us to walk in his ways later by nature than he causes
us to walk in his ways. Behold ridiculous promissory propositions! If the
will is antecedent in relation to the promised thing and God's promise gives
being to promised things, just as a cause really gives being to an effect,
then an antecedent efficacious will and a contingent effect (for God often
promises contingent effects) agree perfectly well. 6. For them this
coexistence depends upon the certainty of divine prediction. Come then: upon
what, I ask, does the coexistence of Peter's denial and the temptation with
which he was assailed in the governor's hall depend? On the Savior's
prediction, plainly. Did Christ, then, predict that Peter would deny him if he
were tempted, and was the prediction certain because the matter was first
certain in itself, not conversely? Peter therefore denied the Savior because
the Savior predicted that he would deny him, as everyone knows. Do not these
nails disturb everything in the church—theology, logic, and even the common
notions of nature? 6. They say that God foreknows this coexistence upon a
hypothesis. I wish to learn the hypothesis. He sees that the Tyrians would
repent if they had seen signs, but this hypothesis is a mere cipher, for after
seeing the signs they can fail to repent. What does Grevinchovius say here?
God, he says, sees truth here,

[1651 logical printed p. 197 | IA leaf 215 | PDF p. 215]

not necessity. But is the man altogether sane? God sees the truth infallibly.
Therefore necessity is in that truth; otherwise it is the same as if he said
that God infallibly knows what is fallible—as if he said that a person sees
what is not visible, hears what is not audible, or God knows what is not
knowable.

6. Argument 6. If the object of this knowledge precedes God's free decree,
then all contingents precede God's freedom, and he will be unable to have mercy
upon whom he wills, reveal Christ to whom he wills, or arrange contingents
otherwise than they arranged themselves antecedently to God's decree; and God
will be compelled by fatal necessity to will all things. For if contingents
precede middle knowledge and foreknowledge precedes the decree, much more do
all contingents, as to their real futurition, precede God's decree. But this is
absurd. Therefore the object of this knowledge is absurd. Suárez replies:
“Although that knowledge does not presuppose any free decree as though it
preexisted in God, it includes within the hypothesis itself a decree to be
posited in reality, so that the effect can have being from it.” Reply. 1. If
this knowledge does not presuppose a decree as preexisting in God, it knows
the effect before it is a being, true, or knowable. For nothing has being or
truth of existing unless it is presupposed from its cause. 2. It includes
within the hypothesis itself, he says, a decree to be posited—and not posited?
Once the effect is posited, is the cause merely to be posited and not already
posited? What kind of logic is this? 3. If the decree is to be posited once
middle knowledge, and thus the futurition of the contingent, is posited, this
decree is to be posited either freely or not freely. If freely, the decree can
fail to be posited,

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Divine Grace*, book 2, section 1, folio 7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 198 | IA leaf 216 | PDF p. 216]

and thus all future contingents can be given and, by the same reasoning, can
actually exist apart from every decree of God. But if God's decree is to be
posited not freely, God is not free in positing his decrees, which is utterly
irrational. Thus by fate and necessity God cannot decree what already is. 4.
This decree is merely an approval of a thing already future antecedently to
God's volition, not a decree. For whatever someone decrees has being through
the decree; the decree makes and gives being to the decreed thing, just as
knowledge presupposes its object. But approval concerns a thing already
preexisting, and therefore is not the thing's cause but presupposes it as
already existing in its being. 5. The decree is included in the hypothesis as
something to be posited so that the effect may have being from it: everything
here is absurd, incongruous, and inept. If middle foreknowledge already exists,
its object already exists—indeed, the object is prior by nature to the
knowledge. But if, when the object exists, the decree is to be posited so that
the object can exist from it, then what already is can be, and thus that which
is is not yet—as though a person who is actually a person were potentially
able to be a person, and a futurition which already exists actually were
potentially able to exist actually. Who could endure a great and renowned
Jesuit, who published sixteen volumes of sacred theology, speaking so
incoherently? 6. The object of middle knowledge is prior to the knowledge
itself; and accordingly, since this knowledge is prior to God's free decree,
it follows that future contingents are truly future antecedently to acts of
omnipotence. For omnipotence acts nothing in God except as determined through
acts of the free will. Thus all future contingents are future before every act
of divine omnipotence, intellect, or will, and therefore before every act of
providence. O

[1651 logical printed p. 199 | IA leaf 217 | PDF p. 217]

Jesuit-Arminian providence! My soul, do not enter into their secrets.

7. Peter Fonseca answers this argument differently: “It does not follow,” he
says, “that knowledge of this sort has taken anything of God's freedom to will
or permit the opposite of what the created will does. Prove that, Jesuit. For
he also certainly knew that, if it were placed on certain other occasions, it
would do the opposite; so that after he understood through conditional
knowledge what it would do when placed in these or those circumstances, and
willed by an absolute will that this should occur or be permitted, it would
now be contrary to the hypothesis to will or permit the opposite to occur;
otherwise a change would be admitted in God's will.” Reply. 1. The Jesuit's
reason is worth nothing. For if antecedently to God's knowledge and decree he
knew that the will was to be placed in these rather than other circumstances
and would will this, then he could neither know, will, nor through omnipotence
bring about that in any manner it should will something other than it willed.

[1651 logical printed p. 200 | IA leaf 218 | PDF p. 218]

I do not deny that the will could have willed something else, but that is not
what I contend in the argument. If, however, antecedently to his knowledge and
will God sees that the created will wills this rather than that, he could not
bring about that it did not will otherwise. But, the Jesuit says, he could
have placed the will in other circumstances in which the created will would
have willed the contrary. I reply that the will was freely placed by God in
these circumstances; and according to the Jesuit's teaching in the same place,
reply to argument 4, God foreknows his own free acts by middle knowledge.
Therefore, antecedently to his will, that free placing of the created will in
these rather than other circumstances existed. Consequently he could not
place the will otherwise than middle knowledge foreknew that he would place
it. 2. I demand that the Jesuit prove that the created will, in circumstances
other than those under which it wills what it wills, would have done the
opposite of what it now does. Indeed, under all possible circumstances it
could do the very same thing it now does. For under all circumstances it is
indifferent to acting or not acting; those circumstances would not determine
the will to do the opposite. He therefore pronounces this after his custom but
does not prove it, and proving it is no small matter for strengthening this
argument. 3. By middle foreknowledge God foreknew that the created will would
will this and not that, and actually the opposite. But according to the Jesuit,
the futurition of this volition rather than its opposite preceded every act of
both God's knowledge and free will. Consequently, God could not fail
absolutely to will these free acts which occur rather than others. Knowledge
of this sort therefore removes all freedom from God. Is Hilary not far safer?
“The things God decreed to do, he knows in his will.”

[Marginalia: Fonseca, on book 6 of the *Metaphysics*, chapter 2, question 4,
section 5.]

[Marginalia: Hilary.]

[1651 logical printed p. 201 | IA leaf 219 | PDF p. 219]

8. The things the adversaries bring forward for this knowledge have no weight.
For 1. God knew by free knowledge in his predetermining decree that the
Keilites would hand David into Saul's hands if he remained, that the Tyrians
would repent if they had seen the signs, and things of this kind. By that
decree he absolutely predefined the chain of all possible beings. This surely
belongs to God's marvelous providence, whose will is undetermined and
suspended toward no beings, whether actual or possible. Thus the sayings of
the Fathers and Scholastics are to be explained which Francis Suárez piles up
at excessive length for this knowledge. The Jesuit could easily have relieved
himself freely of this burden of writing.

9. Objection 2. They object: a middle is given between the absolutely future
and the possibly future—namely, the conditionally future. Therefore a middle
knowledge is given between natural knowledge, by which God knows possibilities,
and free knowledge, by which he knows things absolutely future and existing.
Reply. God knows conditional futures by free knowledge, and so this middle
knowledge is empty, because by God's will alone the Tyrians' repentance and
the sight of signs are necessarily connected with one another. But when
antecedent and consequent are essentially connected—as, if Peter is a human
being, therefore he is an animal—God naturally knows such things apart from
every decree, since they possess being antecedently to God's decree.

[1651 logical printed p. 202 | IA leaf 220 | PDF p. 220]

10. Objection 3. Before every decree these two are contradictory: if Peter is
placed in such an order, he will sin; if he is placed in such an order, he
will not sin. One of contradictories is true and the other false. Therefore
God determinately knows the truth of one, but not by free knowledge, which
presupposes the decree, for this is knowledge of things antecedent to the
decree. Nor does he know these naturally as mere possibilities, for here
something is conditionally future. Reply. 1. If one of contradictories in
contingent matter is true, the other will necessarily be false. Yet before
God's decree this proposition is not true: “If Peter is placed in such an
order of things, he will sin.” Nor must one proposition be determinately true
antecedently to the cause connecting the terms with one another—that is,
before God's will permits it to occur. Peter cannot sin even if he were so
constituted. 2. Concerning a truly future being, neither side before God's
decree is properly true or false, because the terms of the proposition—namely,
Peter, his constitution in such an order, and the sin—are nonbeings and thus
not true or knowable. Consequently their connection is a nonbeing and untrue,
but indifferent to truth and falsity. For it is not necessary, upon the
positing of any hypothesis, that one of contradictories be true and the other
false, since a condition can be posited by which they cease to be
contradictories. Nor are just any hypotheses available to us, and it is not
necessary that in every instant of reason one of contradictories be true, as
the most learned William Ames, who if anyone deserved well of God's church,
observed after Diego Álvarez in his reply to Grevinchovius, section 4. Suárez
himself acknowledges that there are no beings unless a decree is presupposed,
at least in the manner of a possible thing. This argument therefore strikes
those adversaries. For truth and falsity are affections of a true and real
being, but there is no true and real being antecedently to its cause.

[1651 logical printed p. 203 | IA leaf 221 | PDF p. 221]

11. Objection 4. No wise person absolutely decrees anything unless he
deliberately foreknows what will follow from this or that. Therefore it is
foreign to God's wisdom for him to decree future contingents before he
foreknows them; otherwise he decrees rashly. So Fonseca, Suárez, and Jackson,
who drew such things from them lest his meditations should be lean and
emaciated. Reply. The argument assumes that God's wisdom excels in this: that
he foreknows the things to be done and decreed. But even every fool and idiot
possesses this much—that some knowledge of things to be done and determined
precedes his deeds and decrees. 2. We say that the knowledge of simple
intelligence, by which God knows what is possible, what is expedient to do,
and what is not, precedes all God's decrees; and that the knowledge of vision,
or free knowledge, follows the decrees. Thus

[Marginalia: Fonseca, book 6 of the *Metaphysics*, question 4, chapter 2,
section 4.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Conditional Knowledge*, book 1, chapter 5.]

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Divine Essence*, book 2, p. 133.]

[1651 logical printed p. 204 | IA leaf 222 | PDF p. 222]

God decrees from his infinite wisdom and knowledge and by no means rashly—as
though rashness in decreeing would fall upon God unless before his decree he
saw all possibilities as necessarily future, which implies a contradiction.
3. Then let middle knowledge preceding all God's decrees be given, and we
shall see what sort of wisdom they attribute to God. “Before I decree that
Peter should believe,” God says, “I foreknow by middle knowledge that Peter
will most certainly believe; therefore I decree that he should believe. I
foreknow antecedently to the permissive decree that Judas will betray the
Savior, Pilate will condemn him, and the soldiers will crucify him; therefore
I decree to permit these things.” Is this the Jesuitical wisdom they ascribe
to God? He freely decrees that this will exist after it already exists. What
sort of providence would this be? A shipmaster most certainly foreknows that
the ship will undergo shipwreck and then decrees the shipwreck. Peter foresaw
and foreknew his own denial of the Lord in Christ's prediction and willed it.
Therefore Peter decreed his own denial of the Lord. 4. Wise people would wish
to decree from middle knowledge lest perhaps they err by decreeing means
unsuited to the end. But the Jesuits have no reason to fear that means willed
by God are unfit for the end he intends. Because God willed such means, they
are suitable; but matters do not stand thus among people. Shall we therefore
perhaps give people a certain middle knowledge lest they err? Shall we also
give it to God because of the danger of error? Away with trifles.
