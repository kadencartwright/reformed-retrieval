### Chapter III. What transubstantiation is

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. Transubstantiation is the total conversion of the substance of
bread and wine into the body and blood of the Lord. The theologians agree on
this because of the authority of the councils and the Church.

Protestant reply. 1. It is well done that Bellarmine himself admits that faith
in this dogma depends on the authority of the councils and the Church, not on
the authority of Scripture. By “the Church,” however, he understands not the
apostolic and pure Church but the Roman apostasy, and by “the councils,”
certain recent conventicles of the same faction. This is the candid confession
of Scotus, on Book 4, distinction 11, question 3: “They appear to be moved
chiefly by the fact that what the holy Roman Church holds must be held
concerning the sacraments. The Church declared this interpretation under
Innocent in the Lateran Council. The words of Scripture could be preserved
according to an easy interpretation, and one truer according to appearance.”
2. No example can be given, either ordinary or extraordinary by miracle, of
the total conversion of one individual substance into another pre-existing
and persisting individual substance. This transubstantiation, therefore—which
according to the Romanists is a matter of ordinary administration—is such a
thing that God has never effected anything like it through man or angel: that
is, it is an invention without example. 3. Bellarmine's assertion that the
theologians, that is, the scholastics, agree about this total conversion is
utterly false. Some, such as Durandus, maintained that the matter of the bread
remains while its form passes away. Others, on the contrary, maintained that
the matter passes away and the form remains. Alexander of Hales, part 4,
question 10, reports both opinions and attempts to refute them. 4. This total
conversion cannot be explained in any respect so as to avoid the
contradictions which Scotus, in his *Reportatio*, on Book 4, distinction 10,
question 1,

[1632 witness | printed p. 108]

briefly sets out but which no one resolves: a quantity that will come to be together with a
quantity; the greater with the lesser; quantity without a quantitative mode;
part outside part, and yet the whole in every part; something pre-existing
that will come to be in a new “where” without change.

2. Bellarmine. In a conversion it is required that what is converted cease to
be what it was before. If it is a composite, the composite itself must cease
to be, although something of it remains, just as water is converted into air
while the same matter remains. But if what is converted is not only itself a
composite but its parts are also composite, then the parts too must simply
cease to be; and this occurs in the Eucharist.

Protestant reply. 1. There is not a word or syllable in Scripture which
indicates that the bread ceases to be. Nor does the nature of a sacrament
permit the element to be destroyed before its sacramental use. 2. Bellarmine's
reason for proving that the whole bread according to all its parts must cease
to be in its conversion—namely, because its parts are composite—is plainly
deranged. Bellarmine admits that when Lot's wife was converted into a pillar
of salt, the same matter remained; yet her parts were no less composite than
the parts of the bread are.

3. Bellarmine. It is also required in a conversion that something succeed
into the place of what ceases to be.

Protestant reply. 1. Christ's body, which existed before and remains what it
was, can in no way succeed the bread as the terminus of conversion. For then,
through that conversion, it would begin to be in the same manner in which the
bread is imagined to cease to be, and this contains a manifest contradiction.
2. Among the Romanists there are many who maintain that by the power of this
conversion Christ's body is not placed under the species of bread or in the
place of the bread. “God,” they say, “can convert the bread into Christ's body
so that it has its existence in heaven, because there is no greater
contradiction in this than in the conversion which is now posited.” Thus John
de Rada the Aragonese, *Theological Controversies*, part 4, controversy 6,
article 3, where he proves this assertion with five arguments. Among them this
one is worthy of notice: “What is converted into something pre-existing is
drawn to the conditions of the pre-existing thing, and not the reverse.
Therefore the bread is where Christ's body is, rather than conversely.” 3.
Succession is not sufficient for this conversion unless something remains
which was bread and is Christ's body. Otherwise, as Cajetan says on part 3,
question 75, article 4, it would not be truly said: “What was bread is now
Christ's body.”

Bellarmine. This speculation of Cajetan was empty and superfluous. For this
can be truly said even if absolutely nothing remains which was previously
bread, because, according to the common manner of speaking, such statements
signify only that what is converted does not cease into nothing but into
something which succeeds it.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself testifies against this in chapter 22: “In
no

[1632 witness | printed p. 109]

way could the Lord's statement, ‘This is my body,’ be true if the whole were
so changed into the whole that nothing common remained in the two termini.”

4. Bellarmine. Third, a certain connection and dependence is required between
the cessation of the one thing and the succession of the other, so that the
one ceases in order that the other may succeed and the succession occurs by
the power of the cessation.

Protestant reply. Christ's body does not come to be by the power of the
bread's cessation, because the bread can be reduced either into the elements
or into nothing without any succession of Christ's body. Nor is there even
the slightest indication of God's will that Christ's body should depend on
the bread's cessation.

5. Bellarmine. The fourth condition of a perfect conversion is that both the
terminus from which and the terminus to which are truly positive, so that no
privation intervenes between them.

Protestant reply. This very argument is also effective against the
transubstantiation of the bread. For since all the accidents of bread are in
the Eucharist, and since according to the order of nature bread ought to be
present together with those accidents and was present before, it necessarily
follows either that the bread is present there or that, by the bread's
privation, a place is left for Christ's body.

6. Bellarmine. Transubstantiation is the most perfect conversion and is
distinguished from all other mutations. Yet it is more similar to a
supernatural conversion, from which it is distinguished only in two respects:
1. that there the same matter remains, but not here; 2. that here the same
accidents remain, but not there.

Protestant reply. This is just as if Bellarmine had said: transubstantiation is
the most perfect conversion, but it is distinguished from the most perfect
conversion, 1. in that it is not a conversion, and 2. in that it implies a
manifest contradiction. For the imagined succession of Christ's body into
the place of the bread, with which it has no substance in common, is no more a
conversion of the latter into the former than the succession of an elected
king or an heir into the place of a deposed or deceased king is, especially
if the successor is clothed in the same garments. And for a substance to be
changed without a change of accidents is as if a man were changed into a
brute while human properties remained.

7. Bellarmine. The conversion of the bread into the Lord's body is not
productive, such that by its power the Lord's body is produced anew. Nor is it
conservative, such that the body previously existing in the same place with
the bread is naturally conserved through this conversion. Rather, it is
adductive, such that Christ's body, previously existing but not in the place
where the bread was, begins by the power of this conversion to be in that
place under the species of bread.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine distinguishes not real things but the most
foolish inventions of certain idle scholastics. For no example of either a
conservative or an adductive conversion, such as Bellarmine describes to us,
has ever existed since the world

[1632 witness | printed p. 110]

was created, whether natural or supernatural, nor can it exist. For the
terminus of a conversion receives its being through that conversion; that is,
it is produced. The succession of the latter terminus must also have a
proportion to the recession of the former. But that recession is, as they
say, destructive; therefore this succession must be productive.

Bellarmine. The conversion of food into a man through nutrition is almost of
this kind, so far as the introduction of the soul is concerned.

Protestant reply. By a comparison that is “almost similar,” anything whatever
can be called similar. For who has ever heard of food being converted into a
soul? Who has even dreamed that the whole soul is brought through nutrition
into a new, commensurate place? Finally, who can imagine that the soul's
succession follows from the cessation of the nourishment?

Protestant reply 2. If Christ's body is not produced through this conversion
but only becomes present, then the terminus to which is not a substance or a
body, but an accident or the body's presence. Consequently, with respect to
the terminus to which, it is not transubstantiation but rather
transaccidentation.

Bellarmine. The terminus to which is a substance. For presence does not come
to be; rather, it comes to pass that Christ's body succeeds the bread.

Protestant reply. This distinction is merely verbal. For the most acute of the
scholastics maintain, as John de Rada, the Aragonese bishop of Patti, has it
in *Theological Controversies*, part 4, controversy 7, article 2, that “the
formal terminus to which of this conversion is the presence of the body at
the species; the material terminus is Christ's body; and the total terminus is
Christ's body present. The formal terminus from which is the bread's not-being
here; the material terminus is the bread; and the total terminus is the
bread's not-being present here.” The sum of all this trifling is that Christ's
body acquires nothing through this fictitious adductive conversion except a
new presence, and therefore a new accident is the proper terminus of this
action.

8. Bellarmine. Conversion, insofar as it destroys the bread, is not so much an
action as the negation of action or conservation. The placing of the body
under the species, however, occurs from that conversion not as it is in the
bread, but as it is received in Christ's body.

Protestant reply. 1. Transubstantiation therefore occurs through negation and
adduction, in neither of which there is any substantiation. 2. The blessing of
the bread effects this—if this is true—that God denies his conservation to
that very bread which has been blessed. Thus destruction is the proper effect
of blessing, which is plainly absurd. 3. In this way transubstantiation will
not be one action, but the negation of action and an Action.

Bellarmine. Transubstantiation is one action, which is a certain

[1632 witness | printed p. 111]

will or volition of God by which he does not will to conserve the bread, in
order that the Lord's body may be placed in its place.

Protestant reply. The will to remove one body so that another may be placed in
its place is not one and the same action as the placing of the other body in
that place. There is certainly no reason in this whole explanation of
transubstantion, so that it appears to have introduced transubstantiation
along with itself.

#### Bellarmine's arguments

9. Bellarmine. “This is my body.” These words necessarily imply either a true
mutation of the bread, as the Catholics maintain, or a metaphorical mutation,
as the Calvinists maintain.

Protestant reply. 1. We concede the conclusion; therefore they do not admit
the opinion of the Lutherans. But transubstantiation does not immediately
follow from the denial of consubstantiation. 2. The mutation from common use
to sacred use which we maintain is not opposed to a true mutation but to a
substantial mutation. 3. The genuine sense of the Lord's words has been
explained above. Yet from an abundance of evidence we add here that
transubstantiation does not follow from these words even if they indicated a
real and substantial presence, provided Bellarmine's proposition in chapter 5
is true: that penetration of bodies can occur, so that Christ's body is
present together with the bread in the same commensurate place.

Bellarmine 1. When a contained thing is pointed out, only one thing is pointed
out.

Protestant reply. 1. The reason is that two bodies cannot be contained at the
same time in the same commensurate place. But if they could, then two
contained things would be pointed out at the same time by the pronoun “this.”
2. Scotus replies on Book 4, distinction 11: “Granted that the substance of
the bread remained, the substance of the bread would not be pointed out, but
the thing contained under the bread, just as the accidents are not now
pointed out, since then the proposition would be false. Rather, the sense is:
‘This entity contained under this sensible sign is my body.’”

Bellarmine 2. The accidents alone are the containing thing.

Protestant reply. On the hypothesis of that penetration of dimensions, Scotus
rightly maintains that the bread would be the containing thing. Bellarmine has
nothing else from Scripture by which to confirm transubstantiation except
these words, “This is my body.” Yet not only do these words sound nothing of
the kind, but the Scotists maintain that they can have another sense, and this
is even proved from Bellarmine's own concessions. The poverty of this cause is
therefore certainly so wretched that its authors ought to be ashamed of their
invention.

Those stale arguments from congruity which Bellarmine adduces to establish
transubstantiation are opposed only to the Lutherans and

[1632 witness | printed p. 112]

presuppose their consubstantiation. The Lutherans, therefore, may see to them.

#### Our arguments

We shall not pursue everything here, lest by repeating with Bellarmine what
was said before we should do what has already been done. We shall therefore
dwell only on those things which seem to cast still fuller light upon the
darkness even of Roman transubstantiation.

10. Protestant argument. If the substance of bread is absent, the nature of
the sacrament is taken away.

Bellarmine. The substance of bread in no way pertains to the nature of the
sacrament of the Eucharist.

Protestant reply. 1. What Christ blessed in the institution of this sacrament
pertains to the nature of the sacrament; but he blessed the substance of
bread. 2. The earthly thing, or the element to which the word is added,
pertains to the nature of the sacrament; but substantial bread is that element
in the Eucharist.

Bellarmine 2. The earthly thing in the Eucharist is the sensible species. The
matter from which the sacrament is made is true wheaten bread, which remains
insofar as there is need of it.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine absurdly distinguishes here between the
earthly thing and the matter of the sacrament, although he himself previously,
in *On the Sacraments*, book 1, chapter 18, has this proposition: “In all the
sacraments of the new law are found things as the matter and words as the
form.” There also he says expressly in words that “the bread is the thing of
the Eucharist.” 2. Christ did not bless the species but the bread. 3. If bread
is the matter from which the sacrament is made and constituted, then it must
necessarily remain in the sacrament. For matter is an internal cause which
enters into the effect and remains in it, so that when the matter is taken
away, the effect itself ceases. 4. There must be an analogy in the sacrament
between the earthly and heavenly thing, and in the Eucharist the analogy
requires that the bread nourish and consist of many grains. Therefore it must
be substantial.

Bellarmine 3. The analogy does not require that the bread truly nourish and
consist of many grains. It is sufficient if it naturally represents the thing
which truly nourishes and consists of many grains. The appearance of the dove
and the bronze serpent had their analogy, and yet they were not a true dove
and a true serpent.

Protestant reply. 1. In all the other sacraments, the analogy is such that the
thing itself has its natural operation. Why not in the Eucharist? 2.
Sacramental analogy consists not only in the thing but also in the use and
operation of the thing. But the accidents or species of bread do not have that
use and operation. Third, nothing can be said less analogically than that the
bread is removed from the midst so that afterward it is represented

[1632 witness | printed p. 113]

through its species and those species, insofar as they represent the bread,
are the sacramental sign. 4. Thomas, together with many others, affirms that a
true dove was present at Christ's baptism. If it was not a dove, it was
nevertheless a similar substance, not an empty species. Yet it was not a
sacrament and did not have the use of a living dove. 5. By the very analogy
which it had to the thing signified, the bronze serpent ought not to have been
a living and venomous serpent.

11. Protestant argument. The doctrine of transubstantiation opens a window to
those heretics who said that Christ's body was phantasmal. For if it flies
invisibly through innumerable places and begins to be now here, now there, who
would not suspect that it is a phantom?

Bellarmine. Phantasmal bodies appear to be true bodies and yet are not, while
Christ's body in the Eucharist does not appear and yet is.

Protestant reply. 1. From this description it follows that the bread in the
Eucharist is a phantasmal body. Yet it is such a sign of Christ's body that
many of the fathers proved from its nature that Christ's body is not
phantasmal. 2. If Christ's body is present under the species of bread, it
appears to be that body which it is not, and in that respect it is phantasmal.
2. Just as something can exist and not be seen, so also it can be seen and not
exist; and by this reasoning the heretics are confirmed in their heresy.

12. Protestant argument. In the confession of Berengar, *On Consecration*,
distinction 2, it is stated that after consecration the bread and wine are not
only the sacrament but also the true body and blood of the Lord, and are
sensibly and in truth handled and broken by the priest's hands and ground by
the teeth of the faithful. These things cannot be said of Christ's body.

Bellarmine 1. It is true that the bread after consecration is the sacrament.

Protestant reply. It is therefore true that the bread after consecration is
the sacrament, for “sacrament” signifies no privation of substance.

Bellarmine 2. Bread substantially changed is Christ's natural body.

Protestant reply. 1. Therefore the bread remains, because nothing is properly
said to be something positive which in no way exists.

Protestant reply 2. Therefore the same substance remains. Otherwise it could
hardly be said that even the name of bread remains: the bread that existed
would not exist, what now exists would not have existed, and thus no bread
would remain.

Bellarmine 3. Christ's body is seen, touched, broken, and ground through the
species of bread, which alone are formally seen, touched, broken, and ground.

[1632 witness | printed p. 114]

Protestant reply. In Bellarmine's opinion, therefore, Christ's body in the
Eucharist is seen, touched, broken, and ground with respect to its substance
in the same manner as common bread at an ordinary supper. For there too the
species are formally seen, and so forth, while the bread is seen through its
species.

Bellarmine 4. “Sensibly” there signifies the same as “in the truth of the
matter.”

Protestant reply. Those for whom all truth is sensible are much too sensual.

13. Protestant argument. If bread is not present in the Eucharist, our senses
are deceived there and something false is present in the sacrament of truth.

Bellarmine 1. The senses are often deceived.

Protestant reply. 1. No example can be given in which the senses of all men,
with the organ, medium, and object well disposed and every means of
investigating the truth employed, are deceived about a sensible object. But
this occurs in the Eucharist if bread is not present there. 2. God has not
instituted any sacrament in such a way that its foundation lies in a delusion
of the senses.

Bellarmine. There is nothing false in this sacrament, because those accidents
naturally signify that the substance of bread lies hidden under that species.
In the Eucharist, however, it occurs not naturally but above the order of
nature that the substance of bread is absent from under those accidents.

Protestant reply. 1. This is just as if Bellarmine had said: the species of
bread does not deceive us, for it performs its office in its natural manner;
but God deceives us, since he supernaturally causes the thing which the
species signifies to be present not to be present. 2. Bellarmine said above,
in chapter 23, that the analogy of the sacrament consists in this: “that which
truly nourishes and consists of many grains”—that is, bread—“is represented.”
On this reasoning, therefore, the sacramental representation would be false.
3. In this way every external sign appears to be taken away. For the bread is
absent; the species of bread does not truly signify that bread is present; it
cannot immediately signify anything other than that of which it is the
species; and it was not sanctified to signify anything else. Therefore it
appears to signify nothing.

14. Protestant argument. What is under the species of bread and wine in the
Eucharist receives on any occasion various changes which are appropriate to
bread but not to Christ's body. It is altered, corrupted, devoured by mice;
worms are generated from it; it can be burned and turned into ashes.

Bellarmine 1. If the change is alteration alone, such as heating,
condensation, and so forth, then matter or substance is not required, for all
the accidents have quantity as their subject.

Protestant reply. We shall see later, concerning quantity, whether it is
separable from matter. Meanwhile this is evident in itself. 1. Qualities are

[1632 witness | printed p. 115]

never altered without their subject being altered at the same time and
admitting a new disposition, but quantity is often not changed through the
alteration of qualities. The quantity of a hot or cold host is disposed in
exactly the same way. Who has ever heard of a hot or cold quantity? 2. Natural
qualities also have that subject from which they flow and in which their
principles are contained. But they do not flow from quantity as from their
principle. They are secondary features of nature and flow from primary
nature, and they cannot exist without it. 3. The alteration of qualities by
its nature tends and can extend so far as to have corruption for its terminus,
and this can occur while the same quantity remains. Is some matter or subject
other than quantity not required here?

Bellarmine 2. If the species are corrupted, as in human nutrition, burning,
and so forth, then matter is indeed required, but it is supplied by God in the
very instant in which those species cease to be and something else is
generated; and this occurs without a miracle. For when the species of bread
are brought through alteration to the point that the dispositions required
for the introduction of some form are present, God, as the order of things
requires, supplies matter, and in that very instant the substantial form is
introduced by the natural agent through the dispositions previously
provided.

Protestant reply. Here we have the appearance of an answer without any
underlying substance. For 1. Bellarmine says that matter is required in the
corruption of the host, but he does not wish that matter to be present except
at the very instant of generation. Thus that matter has nothing in common
with any corrupting transmutation; it is not a substance which is corrupted
but only one which is generated. 2. Without reason he connects these two
things: the corruption of accidents and the generation of a substance. 3. He
says that matter is supplied, but does not indicate whether this is the
re-substantiated matter of the bread, primary matter, or some other secondary
matter. 4. He imagines that God supplies the matter, but does not affirm
whether he does so productively or adductively, by creating it or by
transferring it. 5. First he has the accidents disposed without any production
of matter, then has the matter supplied by God in an instant and the form
introduced by a natural agent. Thus a body first acts upon an incorporeal
object; accidents are disposed toward a form without matter, although that
form has no matter prepared for it; and finally God supplies matter to be
informed, after the material dispositions have been completed, into which a
natural agent introduces the form in an instant. All these are monstrous
inventions formed in the image of transubstantiation and for its sake. 6. But
this surpasses a miracle: that such things should occur without a miracle, as
the order

[1632 witness | printed p. 116]

of things so requires, although there is no order of nature established
either between a natural agent and an immaterial object, between separated
accidents and new matter to be supplied, or finally between form and
unprepared matter. These things certainly seemed so astonishing to Scotus, a
man who was not stupid, that he did not hesitate to affirm that there was “an
occasion to turn all philosophers—indeed, nearly all who follow natural
reason—away from the faith (understand the Roman faith), and that a
philosopher, or anyone who follows natural reason, would hold the things
posited by denying the substance of bread to be a greater incongruity than all
the articles we hold concerning the incarnation; and that because of these
things the faith lies open to the contempt of all who follow reason,” on Book
4, distinction 11, question 3.

15. Protestant argument. The accidents or sensible species of bread and wine
cannot exist without their subject, because they do not subsist in themselves.

Bellarmine 1. Inherence in a subject does not pertain to the essence of an
accident.

Protestant reply. 1. It is enough if it pertains to the accident's existence.
2. Although inherence itself is not the essence of an accident, the principles
of proper accidents are nevertheless of their essence, and those principles
cannot be separated from substance.

Bellarmine, second. An accident is prior to its subject. For example, whiteness
is not such because it makes a wall white; on the contrary, it makes a wall
white because it is whiteness in itself.

Protestant reply. This is mere sophistry and plainly childish. For everyone
knows that a subject is prior by nature to its adjunct, which flows from and
depends upon it. But because the same thing which is the adjunct secondarily
stands in relation to the subject in which it inheres as an accidentally
informing form, the form in that relation is therefore prior to the thing
formed. Yet it is not prior to its subject either simply or under the
description of an adjunct.

Bellarmine 3. Accidents are in themselves but do not subsist, because they are
in themselves negatively, not positively. They are in another but do not
inhere: that is, they are in something which conserves them, not in a subject.

Protestant reply. 1. They are no more in themselves negatively than they are
in those substances of which they are truly denied. 2. They are in another as
in something which conserves them even when they are in their own proper
subject. There is therefore nothing in this answer except ridiculous verbal
trickery, by which it is maintained that the capacity for laughter can exist
without any subject capable of laughter.
