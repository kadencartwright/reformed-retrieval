So much for the external causes. The internal causes follow, namely, the
matter and the form.

The matter of the Church’s ministerial government is an office, not a
benefice; a ministry, not a dignity; business, not leisure—or, rather, the
laws of this government handed down by Christ and the apostles.

The form of ministerial government is aristocratic, not democratic or
monarchical. Concerning its form a twofold question must be examined.

### QUESTION 1: Whether the Government of the Church Is Ministerial or Democratic

That it is not democratic is proved by these arguments.

Argument 1. Scripture teaches that the government of the Church was entrusted
to bishops and presbyters, as was proved above, but nowhere says that this
power was granted to the people. Therefore.

Argument 2. In popular government the magistrate is appointed by the common
people and receives authority from them; appeal is made from the magistrate to
the people; the magistrate can be deprived of office and punished by the
people; and the people enact laws and decree wars. But these things have no
place in the government of the Church, because the people cannot create
bishops,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 49 | printed col. 97 | bsb10175568]

depose or excommunicate them. Nor was an appeal ever made from the bishops to
the people, nor did the people ever enact ecclesiastical laws; only Christ,
the apostles, and councils did so. Therefore.

Argument 3. If the people had authority to govern the Church, they would have
it either from themselves or from another, but neither can be said. Not from
themselves, because this power belongs not to natural law or the law of
nations, but to divine or supernatural law; not from another, because then it
would be from God. But it is not from God, because this is read nowhere;
indeed, everywhere the people are called both the people and the flock which
must be fed. Therefore.

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1, from Matthew 18:17. In controversies we are referred to the
Church, for it says, “Tell the Church.” Therefore.

Response. This is a fallacy from the homonymy of “Church,” because there it
does not signify the assembly of the people, but the ecclesiastical senate,
as was said elsewhere.

Objection 2, from Acts 1 and 6. The whole Church is said to have elected
Matthias and those seven deacons. Therefore.

Response. The passage in Acts 1 is irrelevant, because the form of that
calling was extraordinary and it was to an extraordinary office. 2. In Acts
6 the apostles conceded something to the people in order to quell the
disturbance, and because the issue concerned only the distribution of money.

3. The people did not ordain ministers even in these passages. They did indeed
take part in Acts 1, and in Acts 6 they only named and designated those who
were to be ordained, whom the apostles afterward ordained, Acts 6:6. It does
not follow that because this was done at the beginning it ought to be done
now, any more than it follows that because the first kings were designated by
the people, they should be so even today.

### QUESTION 2: Whether the Church’s Ministerial Government Is Monarchical

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 49 | printed col. 98 | bsb10175568]

We and the Papists agree that monarchical government, considered simply and
absolutely, is the best. Yet the properties which Bellarmine, in books 1 and 2
*On the Roman Pontiff*, chapter 2, attributes to monarchy also belong to other
forms of government: namely, order; the union of citizens with one another;
peace; strength and power; stability; duration; and ease of government. We
further agree that in the Church there is a government distinct from political
government, and finally that Christ is its absolute king and monarch, and that
in this respect the government of the Church is monarchical.

We differ from the Papists, however, because they contend that ministerial
government is monarchical. And because other questions depend upon this
question, we reduce the whole ensuing dispute to six questions:

1. Whether Peter was instituted by Christ as head and monarch in this
   government.
2. Whether the Church’s ministerial government was instituted as monarchical
   by Christ.
3. Whether Peter was at Rome.
4. Whether the pope is Peter’s successor.
5. Whether the pope was designated by Christ as head of the Church.
6. Whether the pope is that great Antichrist.

#### QUESTION 1: Whether the Church’s Ministerial Government Was Instituted as Monarchical by Christ

Bellarmine, in book 1 *On the Roman Pontiff*, affirms that ecclesiastical
government is chiefly monarchical. We maintain the contrary, relying on these
arguments.

Argument 1. The Church’s ministerial government was instituted as aristocratic
by Christ, Matthew 18:17; Acts 20:28; Ephesians 4:11; and elsewhere, as was
proved above.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 50 | printed col. 99 | bsb10175568]

Argument 2. If Christ forbids every exercise of dominion such as that of the
kings of the nations, then there ought not to be a monarchy through one
ecumenical pontiff. But the former is true, Matthew 20:25; Mark 10:40; Luke
22:25, where by the words “not so among you” he forbids not only the manner of
exercising dominion but dominion itself. Elsewhere too such words deny the
thing itself, not only its manner, as in 1 Corinthians 14; Matthew 19:8; and
elsewhere. Therefore.

Argument 3. Scripture does not teach that there is another monarch in the
Church besides Christ; indeed, it teaches that Christ alone is the Church’s
head and monarch, 1 Corinthians 12:27; Ephesians 1:22; 4:15; 5:23; Colossians
1:18; 2:10. Therefore.

Argument 4. If there were several monarchs and heads of the Church, then the
Church would be a monstrous body. But the latter is absurd. Pope Gregory,
therefore, rightly beat back the arrogance of John, bishop of Constantinople,
who claimed for himself the title of head of the universal Church, Letters 36
and 38, book 4.

Argument 5. If another monarch besides Christ were needed in the Church, it
would be because of Christ’s bodily absence and in order to preserve the
Church’s unity. But Christ is always present to the Church in the manner
needed, and in him the Church’s unity is preserved most perfectly, John
15:16–17; Romans 12:5; 1 Corinthians 12:12; Galatians 3:28; Philippians 1:27.

Argument 6. If the apostle, when describing the Church’s hierarchy, makes no
mention of monarchical government or of one supreme ministerial monarch, then
there ought to be none in the Church, since no one will deny that the
apostle’s description is complete. But the former is true, Romans 12:7 and
following; 1 Corinthians 12:28; Ephesians 4:11. Bellarmine therefore rightly
concedes that supreme ecclesiastical power was given not only to Peter but to
the other

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 50 | printed col. 100 | bsb10175568]

apostles. Yet without any foundation in Scripture he says that it was given
to Peter as one who would have a successor, but to the others as those who
would not.

Argument 7. If no bishop ought to exercise dominion over the clergy, but all
ought to care for the flock under the one head, Christ, then no universal
monarch has been constituted under Christ and hence there is no monarchical
government. But the former is true, 1 Peter 5:2–4. Therefore.

Argument 8. If no human being can be equal to the task of governing the
Church, then its government is not monarchical in the person of one human
being. But the former is true, because not even the world can be governed
civilly by one person. And even if this could be done, it would not follow
that the Church ought to be governed so, because such government has not been
instituted in the Church. Furthermore, in civil government viceroys and others
are the king’s vicars, but bishops are not the vicars of bishops, as will be
said later. Therefore.

Argument 9. If there were one monarch in the Church, then all bishops would
have to be appointed by him, as viceroys and others are by a king. But the
latter is false, as the practice of the primitive and subsequent Church has
shown. Therefore.

Argument 10. If the Church’s monarchical government rested with one person,
then he would have to be such that the Church could be certain he would never
fall away from the faith. But after the apostles there is no such human being.
Pope Gregory uses this argument against the bishop of Constantinople in book
4, Letters 36 and 32. Indeed, Bellarmine in the preface to his treatise *On the
Supreme Pontiff* concedes that several pontiffs have been far from upright,
and in book 4 *On the Supreme Pontiff*, chapter 2, acknowledges that many
Papists think that infallibility of judgment in matters of faith rests with
general councils, not the pope.

Argument 11. If there were one supreme pontiff in whose hands this

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 51 | printed col. 101 | bsb10175568]

monarchy rested, then the other bishops could not be called supreme pontiffs.
But individual bishops are called supreme pontiffs by the Fathers and
councils. Thus the Council of Strasbourg, chapter 5, calls bishops “pontiffs
constituted in the supreme priesthood.” Ambrose also rightly says in his
letter to Felix, bishop of Como, “You announced everywhere that the day on
which you took up the helm of the supreme priesthood was celebrated among
us.”

Argument 12. If besides Christ there were another who ruled the Church
monarchically, he could rightly be called the Church’s head, bridegroom, king,
lord, and foundation, because the relation between body and head, and so
forth, always remains. But these things can be said of Christ alone, Ephesians
1:22; 1 Corinthians 3; and elsewhere. Therefore.

Argument 13. Cyprian, in his letter to Cornelius, bishop of Rome, and Letter
53, says, “To each of the pastors a portion of the flock has been assigned,
which each one is to rule and govern, being about to render an account of his
conduct to the Lord.” Likewise: “The episcopate is one, spread through the
concordant multitude of many bishops.”

Jerome, in his letter to Evangelus, says, “Wherever a bishop may be, whether
at Rome or Eugubium or elsewhere, he has the same merit and the same
priesthood. The power of riches and the humility of poverty do not make a
bishop either superior or inferior.” Gregory, bishop of Rome, in Letter 32,
attacks John, bishop of Constantinople, most sharply for calling himself the
universal bishop: “Priests who ought to weep have taken names of vanity for
themselves and boast in new and profane titles.” He says again, “With new
presumption and pride, this brother of mine calls himself universal bishop.”

### OBJECTIONS FROM CHAPTER 9, BOOK 1, *ON THE ROMAN PONTIFF*

Objection 1. If monarchy is the best government, and if it is certain that
the Church is governed in the best way by the wisest prince,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 51 | printed col. 102 | bsb10175568]

Christ, then its government ought to be monarchical. But the former is true.
Therefore.

Response. This concludes only that Christ governs the Church monarchically,
which we concede. Furthermore, the antecedent of the proposition must be
distinguished. Monarchy is the best government considered absolutely: this is
true. But in view of the circumstances it is false, because in the government
of the Church, besides the circumstance of the person—namely, that one human
being cannot govern all churches—the circumstance of Christ’s institution is
required. For the question is not absolutely about the excellence of this or
that government, but about the government instituted by Christ. And it is
certain that the government instituted by Christ is most useful to the
Church, although it is not monarchical. Nor does this follow: monarchy is a
more excellent government in the civil polity; therefore it is so in the
Church—unless Christ’s institution is added to establish it in the Church.

Objection 2. The Church is bodily and visible. Therefore, besides Christ who
governs it invisibly, it needs one supreme visible judge by whom disputes over
religion may be settled.

Response. The consequence is denied, because although it is necessary to have
a visible judge, it is not necessary that the judge be one particular human
being. Furthermore, Christ instituted his Word and general and lesser
councils, which ought to judge controversies of faith according to the Word.
Finally, the Church insofar as it is bodily is not the Church, but insofar as
it is spiritual. Therefore, insofar as it is the Church, it has no need of a
bodily head.

Objection 3. From the likeness which the Church of mortals has to that of the
angels: the Church of the angels is the exemplar and idea of the Church
Militant, Hebrews 8:1 and following; Revelation 21:2. But among the angels,
besides God the supreme king, there is one who presides over the others. At
first this was the one who is now the devil, who in Job 40:14 is “the beginning
of the ways of the Lord,” in Isaiah 14:12 is Lucifer, and in Ezekiel 28:13 is
the one

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 52 | printed col. 103 | bsb10175568]

whom the nine orders of angels surrounded, signified by the nine stones. But
after the devil’s fall Michael is prince, Revelation 12:7. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because it has no foundation in
Scripture. Hebrews 8 is only a comparison of the figures of the Old Testament
with their truth in Christ. Nor does this follow: there is a likeness between
the Church of the angels and the militant Church; therefore they have entirely
the same form of government.

2. The assumption is denied, nor do the cited passages prove it. Job 40 speaks
of the elephant; Isaiah 14 of the destruction of Nebuchadnezzar, whose glory
is compared with Lucifer; Ezekiel 28 of the king of Tyre.

3. What he asserts about Michael’s succession into the devil’s place is false,
because that Michael in Revelation 12 is Christ, while his angels are all the
faithful, as very many interpreters, including Pontifical interpreters,
interpret it.

Objection 4. The Old Testament was a figure of the New, 1 Corinthians 10:11.
But in the Old Testament there was always one who presided monarchically,
before and after Moses. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because in 1 Corinthians 10:11 “type” is
not taken for an analogy of signification, but for the moral application of
examples and similar punishments. 2. This is a fallacy from what is said in a
qualified sense. Those things which the apostle enumerates happened as
figures. Therefore, the Church of the Old Testament itself was a figure of the
New: this does not follow. Nor does it follow that it was a figure in respect
of having a supreme priest who was merely human. Finally, that figure was
fulfilled in Christ; see Hebrews 9:12.

Objection 5. The Church is compared to an ordered army, Song of Songs 6; a
beautiful woman, Song of Songs 7; a kingdom, Daniel 2; a sheepfold, John 10; a
house, 1 Timothy 3; and a ship or Noah’s ark, 1 Peter 3. But in an army there
is one

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 52 | printed col. 104 | bsb10175568]

commander; a bride has one bridegroom; in a kingdom there is one king; in a
sheepfold, one shepherd; in a house, one lord and steward; in a ship, one
pilot. Therefore.

Response. The whole argument is conceded, because all these things belong to
Christ and represent the Church’s spiritual government under Christ its head.
Furthermore, although these likenesses describe the Church, they need not
agree in every respect. 3. In Song of Songs 6 the army is considered not as
having one commander, but as marshaled under banners and ready for battle. Nor
does this follow: the Church is compared to a beautiful woman; therefore it
has a head besides Christ. Likewise, Daniel 2 speaks of Christ; and in 1
Timothy 3, where it speaks of a house, it is not necessary that there be only
one steward. Indeed, 1 Corinthians 4 testifies that there are several in the
Church. Finally, the Church is nowhere in Scripture compared to a ship; 1
Peter 3 compares baptism to the ark.

Objection 6. If the Church gathered by Christ began at first to have visible
and external monarchical government, then it must also be governed
monarchically thereafter. But the former is true, because Christ himself
governed it as supreme shepherd. Therefore.

Response. The Church’s government is twofold: 1. Christ’s internal
monarchical government, which still endures; 2. the external government which
Christ exercised for a time over the particular Church in Judea, concerning
which see Romans 15:8. Thus, if he speaks of this government, the proposition
must be denied, because it is one thing to speak of the presence of Christ as
the God-man for a time and of his government, and another to speak of
government through another human being. Furthermore, it is one thing to speak
of the Church being constituted and another to speak of the Church already
constituted. Nor does this follow: after Christ’s ascension the form of
government was changed; therefore the Church itself was changed. For Moses
for a time

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 53 | printed col. 105 | bsb10175568]

discharged both kingship and priesthood, but afterward the priesthood was
handed over to Aaron and the other priests, and yet that Church of the Jews
was not changed.

Objection 7. Bishops who preside over other ministers are rightly appointed in
individual places, metropolitans who preside over bishops, and patriarchs who
preside over all. Therefore, it is right that there be one person who presides
over them and the Church. The consequence is proved, because the causes are
the same. For they are appointed only because the other ministers cannot be
governed except by one person, kept in unity, summoned to a synod, compelled
to perform their office, and so forth. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because there can be several bishops in
one church, as in Acts 20:28; Philippians 1:5. And Jerome, on this passage,
teaches that this prefecture was not assigned to a bishop over the other
ministers by the first and divine institution, but in order to remove schisms.
2. The consequence is denied, because by the same reasoning, for the sake of
good order, one person would have to be set over all kings in civil affairs.
3. The reasons by which he proves the consequence are not necessary, because
all those things can be done through councils, and in antiquity archbishops
and patriarchs were subject to the council of their bishops. Finally, the
unity of the Church depends on unity of doctrine and faith.

Objection 8. The Church ought to grow until the gospel has been preached in
the whole world, Matthew 24:14. But this cannot happen unless there is one
prelate of the Church upon whom this concern rests, because no one can preach
unless he is sent, while no bishop can send into other provinces because he
has his own boundaries. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because it is evident that God propagates
the Church in various ordinary and extraordinary ways, and nowhere is this
said to have rested upon one person. Indeed, history teaches that not only

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 53 | printed col. 106 | bsb10175568]

bishops but also kings and emperors attended to this matter. 2. If the
assumption of the prosyllogism is understood of provinces assigned to bishops
in which the gospel has been preached, it is irrelevant, because the matter
at issue is the propagation of the Church; but if it is understood of
provinces in which the gospel has not yet been preached, it is false, as is
clear from ecclesiastical history. Furthermore, this can be done more
conveniently through councils. Finally, the things Bellarmine alleges about
the conversion of many nations through those sent by the Roman pontiff are
false. For he asserts that the Germans were converted through Boniface, sent
by Pope Gregory II, but Irenaeus testifies that in his time the Germans had
established churches; he lived six hundred years before Gregory. He further
asserts that the English were converted through Augustine the monk, sent by
Gregory I, but Tertullian, in his book *Against the Jews*, testifies that in
his time the Britons had received the gospel—yet he lived around the year 400,
after Gregory’s times.

Objection 9, from the unity of faith. All the faithful must agree entirely in
matters of faith, Ephesians 4:5. But there cannot be one faith unless there is
one supreme judge in whose judgment all are bound to acquiesce, because where
there are many equals, each prefers his own judgments. Therefore.

Response. There is one supreme judge whom all are bound to hear, namely, the
Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture. Furthermore, the assumption is false if it
is understood of one human judge, because in the apostles’ time, when the
Church was most excellently governed and ordered, controversies arose, but
there was never recourse then to one person—not even to Peter—but to the
council in Acts 15. So also after the apostles’ times, questions which touched
the whole Church were debated in councils.
