I know that this does not please our adversary, for on pages 22 and 23 he
strives at length to assail the claim that a precept removes the indifference
of an act. There he argues as follows: “When he says that a precept removes
indifference, he thereby admits that some human acts are indifferent by their
own nature and of themselves. Therefore moral and indifferent acts are
distinct in nature before the addition of a precept. For what makes them
differ so much in nature and character before a precept?” Reply: We do indeed
admit that some human acts, considered according to their species, are
indifferent, because they are neither commanded nor forbidden by the law of
nature. Yet when a positive law is added, they lose this indifference; and
insofar as they are commanded or forbidden by this positive law, they take on
moral character and become morally good or evil—not naturally so. It is from
the law of nature that these acts differ so much in nature and character
before a positive precept. What he next adds does not assail this but confirms
it, when he says:

“Nothing of the kind can be said about truly moral acts that can be said about
indifferent ones: namely, that before the precept they were indifferent—that
is, that people were free to do or omit them. For nature dictates to all
people that God must be loved and parents honored, and this is of the class of
things spoken of in Romans 2:14–15. Hence if a precept were given to hate God
and one’s neighbor, nature would abhor it. Therefore these two kinds of acts
differ not only by precept but also by nature.” Reply: We concede that a
difference between human acts arises by reason of natural law, but we regard
natural law as a divine precept. Thus, when these two kinds of acts differ by
nature, they also differ by precept. What God commands or forbids by a
positive precept can be called morally good or evil, just as what he commands
or forbids by natural law is so called. Thus it will always be necessary to
recur to law; for even things commanded or forbidden by natural law, if they
are considered purely in the physical order and not in the moral order as
subject to a divine precept, are neither morally good nor morally evil.

He then speaks as follows: “Even if a precept makes an indifferent thing
necessary, that necessity does not make it depart from the nature of an
indifferent thing, any more than, when a thing is free, nonfreedom inheres in
it. And in the very obedience by which someone judges that a thing
indifferent in itself must necessarily be done because of a precept, it is
altogether requisite—lest the obedience be corrupted—that the person obeying
judge what he does to be indifferent in itself. Thus he indeed loves the lord
and the authority of the one who commands, but he does not love the
indifferent act as an act which commends us to God in itself.” Reply: If a
precept makes an indifferent thing necessary, that necessity must make the act
depart from the nature of an indifferent thing insofar as the precept reaches
it. That is, if the precept reaches the act with respect to its object, or as
considered according to its species, then the act considered according to its
species also departs from the

[1674 logical printed p. 185 | IA leaf 203 | PDF p. 203]

nature of an indifferent thing. Consequently it is not permissible to do or
omit the act according to the demand of varying circumstances. But if the
precept reaches the act only as considered in its individual instance, then
the indifference of the individual act is indeed removed, and it is not
permissible here and now to do or omit that act considered in its individual
instance. Rather, it must necessarily be done or omitted according to what
the circumstances require. Although this act, because of the precept, is
necessary and not indifferent in its individual instance, yet as considered
in its species it does not depart from the nature of indifferent things. Just
as whatever is, when it is, is necessary, although in respect of its essence
it may remain free, so too an act in respect of moral character, although in
its individual instance it must necessarily be good or evil, can nevertheless
remain indifferent in its species. This is because the precept reaches that
act only in its individual instance and not also in its species; otherwise it
would depart from the nature of an indifferent thing in its species as well.
Therefore one who obeys a precept enjoining an act in its individual instance
or as attended by particular circumstances must judge that act, considered in
its species, to be indifferent. Although it must be done here and now, it may,
and indeed must, be omitted at another time because other circumstances
require its omission. But if the precept reaches an act considered in its
species and commands or forbids it, people would judge wrongly if they thought
that act indifferent in its species while the precept remains in force.

I explain it as follows. As long as the precept concerning circumcision
remained in force, that act could not be judged indifferent even when
considered in its species. After Christ’s resurrection, however, but before
that order had been abolished and the temple destroyed, it took on a different
indifference and could be done or not done as circumstances required. Thus
Paul circumcised Timothy, although on another occasion he was unwilling to
yield even for a moment; Galatians 2:4–5. Now, finally, when the precept has
been wholly abolished, religious circumcision is indifferent neither in its
species nor in its individual instance, but is altogether unlawful.

He next adduces this argument: “Even after the precept has been abolished, we
are bound by love of our neighbor to abstain from meat and to deprive
ourselves, for his sake, of the use of many indifferent things, while
preserving our liberty”; Romans 14:21. “Why not in the same way, while
preserving the same liberty, when the command remains in force?” Reply: What?
Will love of our neighbor teach us to violate God’s precepts while they remain
in force? When the command remains in force, do we enjoy the same liberty in
actions commanded by God that we enjoy after the precept is abolished? What
then is the force of a divine precept? Once the precept had indeed been
abolished in respect of its strict force, people were formerly bound by love
of neighbor to abstain from meat, because the act previously made necessary
by reason of the precept in force had become indifferent once the command was
relaxed and those ceremonies had died. So too we are bound to deprive
ourselves of the use of many indifferent things in a case of scandal, although
outside a case of scandal we may freely enjoy them. The reason is that no
divine command removes the indifference of those acts in their species; only
here and now, in their individual instance, did the consideration of scandal
have a place at that time. But will he say that before Christ’s coming the
consideration of scandal had any place in those ceremonies? Or will he say
that we are now bound to circumcise our infants in order to avoid scandalizing
the Jews? I do not think so. It is therefore evident that these things are
irrelevant.

Finally, he says that the indifference “of the action” must be distinguished
from that “of the judgment.” We too concede this. Yet we add that insofar as a
precept removes the indifference of an act, we must judge the act necessary to
that extent. That is, if

[1674 logical printed p. 186 | IA leaf 204 | PDF p. 204]

a precept removes the indifference of an action with respect to its species,
the action in its species must be judged not indifferent but morally good. If
the precept removes the action’s indifference only here and now in its
individual instance, then although we are bound to judge that action in its
individual instance not indifferent but either morally good or morally evil,
we must not, I admit, judge that act considered according to its species to be
necessary or morally good, but rather to remain indifferent. Thus it is true
that freedom of judgment is entirely consistent with necessity in practice,
as the adversary says and confirms from Romans 14:6. Consequently the things
he piles up here accomplish nothing against us. We agree with him that the
Apostle’s judgment was true and conformed to the facts when he judged that at
that time nothing was unclean in itself. We do not say that because he judged
nothing unclean in itself, therefore nothing was unclean in itself. Instead,
because it was true that nothing was unclean, his judgment conforming to the
fact was true. We do not say that the πεποίθησιν, or persuasion, which he had
through faith in Christ changed anything in the thing, but rather that it
confirmed him in his judgment. This notwithstanding, there was a time when
these things were unclean, not only to the person who judged them unclean but,
by reason of the precept, even to a person who falsely judged them clean.

I do not, however, assent to the following statements. “God,” he says, “had
given a precept not to touch unclean things; that is, he had made certain
things unclean, which he wanted to be regarded as such and avoided. This was
not as if God’s intention were that they should judge those things unclean in
themselves, but that they should abstain from them as unclean because of the
decrees.” Reply: If God made certain things unclean and wanted them to be
regarded as such and avoided, he certainly did not want them to be regarded
as clean or the Israelites not to avoid them as unclean. He therefore wanted
them to judge that those things, with respect to the command and in respect of
use, were unclean, and consequently that touching them was unlawful or
morally evil.

“For although,” he goes on to say on page 24, “the Jews had to sacrifice,
circumcise, wash, and so forth because of the precept, they did not have to do
these things as moral works. On the contrary, they had to know and expressly
consider that the thing was commanded as indifferent and nonmoral, since it
depended upon God’s will alone. Otherwise they would have sinned. Thus
abstaining from pork was an act of obedience and worship when the distinction
of foods was commanded by God, yet it was never a virtue. Indeed, if someone
abstaining from pork judges that abstinence to be a virtue, or himself to be
righteous because of it, or that it commends him to God, he will sin
grievously. So far is it from the case that a precept concerning an
indifferent thing made it moral.” Reply: 1. If the Jews had to sacrifice and
so forth because of the precept, they had to do it as a work morally good and
pleasing to God, because it was commanded. 2. Although they had to know and
consider that the thing was not commanded by the law of nature, and therefore
to regard it as indifferent with respect to that law and as something
dependent upon God’s will alone, yet with respect to that positive precept
they had to know and consider that the action was necessary, not to be
omitted, and therefore morally good. 3. What else is virtue in worship than to
comply with God in all his ordinances? And since abstaining from pork was a
part of worship for as long as the distinction of foods was commanded by God,
was that abstinence a good action and therefore not indifferent, since an
indifferent action, as such, is neither good nor evil? 4. If he intends virtue
to be nothing other than what makes us righteous before God and commends us
to God, he will exclude from virtue not only the things enjoined through
“decrees,” as he calls them, but also the things which he himself

[1674 logical printed p. 187 | IA leaf 205 | PDF p. 205 | ends before Novi adversarium nostrum]

acknowledges to be moral, unless he attempts to restore justification through
works of law and thereby overturn the entire Gospel. I do not believe that he
will.

I do not, however, wish to be understood as though these things which I affirm
can be called morally good by reason of a divine command were of the same
consideration and nature as the things properly called moral, and which some
people—not wrongly—call moral *par excellence* (κατ’ ἐξοχήν).
