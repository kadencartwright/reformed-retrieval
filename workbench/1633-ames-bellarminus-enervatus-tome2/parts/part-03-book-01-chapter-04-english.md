CHAPTER IV. On the president of a council.

The fourth question is whether it is the Pope’s proper office to preside over
a general council, either in person or through his legates, and to direct
everything as supreme judge. The Pontificians affirm this; we deny it.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. *We say that it belongs to the Pope to preside, either in person or
through his legates. For the Pope himself was always absent from the Eastern
councils by deliberate policy: 1. because it did not seem fitting for the head
to follow the members; 2. because the emperor or his legate always presided
over those councils, at least as regards the physical place, and they would
also have wished to do so if the Pope himself had been present. Therefore, so
that either a secular prince should not sit before the Pope—which is in no way
fitting or tolerable—or a disturbance should not arise, the Pope did not go to
those councils, but sent only his legates.*

Protestants. There is no reason in those fictitious explanations which
Bellarmine has devised for the Pope’s absence from all the ancient councils;
rather, more than enough of Antichristian pride is apparent. For I. If the Pope
convoked those councils, as Bellarmine maintained earlier, then all who
assembled there were following him, and if he had gone there, he would have
followed his own counsel, not other men. II. The Pope shows himself to be an
excellent head when he does not deign to attend the assembly of the members,
the most solemn assembly of all. The good Shepherd seeks even one lost sheep
in the wilderness, Luke 15:4. III. If at that time emperors presided in
councils as regards the physical place, then they were displaced by the Pope
with the greatest injury from their physical place, if not also from their
formal place. Bellarmine obscurely admits this here when he says, *at least as
regards the physical place.* IV. How intolerable this is in Peter’s successor:
that he cannot tolerate having the emperor himself seated above him. *Woe to
you, Pharisees, who love the first seat in assemblies,* Luke 11:43. V. By this
pride of the Pope it came about that those most celebrated councils—on the
Pontificians’ position—did not possess in framing decrees that sure guidance
which the Pope’s presence is said to have imparted to certain provincial
synods. *For the power of defining infallibly cannot be subdelegated or
entrusted to legates.*

Bellarmine’s arguments.

2. Bellarmine. *The Pope is the shepherd and father of the universal Church;
all others are his sheep.*

Protestants. This is to beg the question, not to prove it.

3. Bellarmine. *Peter presides at the apostolic council, Acts 15.*

Protestants. I. If Peter presided at that time, it does not follow that he
always presided. II. If Peter always presided when he was present at
assemblies, it does not follow that this honor was also bestowed upon the
legates of an absent Peter. III. If this had been granted in fact both to Peter
and to his legates, how could a necessity of law have been concluded from it?
IV. If this right had belonged to Peter, nevertheless no documents are
produced by which it is proved that it descended to the Pope and his legates
as the universal heirs of his estate. V. The very thing affirmed about Peter
appears to be false. For the text implies rather that James was president, as
Dionysius the Carthusian observes.

4. Bellarmine. *It is proved from the acts of the councils.*

Protestants. I. Divine right is not proved from human acts and deeds. II. We do
not possess the acts of the councils described so precisely that we can found
a perpetual right upon their circumstances. III. Those councils which
assigned the presidency to the Roman bishop took account not of any divine
right, but of an order introduced by custom, by which they used to assign the
first place to the Roman see in many matters, without any difference of power.
IV. There was no single president at that time such as the Pope now wishes to
be acknowledged—namely, armed with both swords—much less such as there was at
Trent, where everything was conducted according to his direction. For the
manner of presiding is twofold: political and ecclesiastical. Politically, the
supreme magistrate presided. Ecclesiastically, the one person, or those
persons, to whom that office was conferred by common consent presided—in
respect of order, not of power. Thus at the Council of Nicaea, Constantine was
president as regards political power; but in the ecclesiastical order it was
either Hosius of Cordoba, or Macarius of Jerusalem and Eustathius of Antioch,
not the Roman bishop or his legates. The arguments Bellarmine advances to the
contrary are of no weight. The same is also sufficiently evident concerning
the other ancient synods: Sardica, the First Council of Constantinople,
Ephesus, and so forth. When Bellarmine contends that the Roman bishop’s legates
presided at these, he exposes his effrontery to everyone. Bellarmine himself
dares to affirm that neither the Roman bishop nor his legates attended the
Second Council of Constantinople, and yet from the truncated and distorted
words of Zonaras he childishly concludes that this presidency was due to him.
From this one example it is sufficiently proved that a legitimate ecumenical
council can be celebrated without any presidency of the Roman bishop.
