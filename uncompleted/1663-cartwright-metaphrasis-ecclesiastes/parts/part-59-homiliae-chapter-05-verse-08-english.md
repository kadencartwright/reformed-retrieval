VERSE 8. If you see the oppression of the poor and the overturning of right
and justice in the province itself, do not marvel at that purpose; for one on
high watches over another on high, and indeed the Most High is over them.

This is a prolepsis, in which a tacit objection—one drawn from contrary things,
as it appears to the flesh—is indicated plainly enough. For this seems
altogether to conflict with the true happiness which is assigned to the lawful
worship of God:

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA213 | printed pp. 213–214 | begins with complete quod after catchword quod on p. 212; ends after catchword Deum at foot of p. 214]

the godly are crushed and trampled underfoot in judgment. He aggravates this
oppression from its location as subject: it is not contained within the
boundaries of one town or city, but rages throughout the whole province and
kingdom, so that an appeal from one court to another supplies no remedy for
their evils.

This obstacle to happiness is also increased by the place that is its subject:
the poor—such as those who lawfully worship God generally are, Matthew 11, 1
Corinthians 1, Psalm 10—who are already afflicted by their straitened condition,
are oppressed. He further amplifies it by another place as subject: this
oppression occurs on the throne of justice and judgment, as in chapter 3:16.
This temptation has severely tried very many godly people, including those
most advanced in godliness. Psalm 73; Jeremiah 15. Indeed, poverty considered
by itself is usually an obstacle keeping many people from joining themselves
to the lawful worship of God. How much more is that so if it is heaped up with
the additional calamity of oppression?

Here you should observe that the corruption and oppression of courts not only
undermine household property and what are called the goods of fortune, but
also create danger to this life and even to eternal salvation. The atrocity of
corruption in judgment must therefore be assessed chiefly from this fact:
corrupt judges thereby become not only thieves, but murderers, and not only
murderers of bodies, but murderers of souls.

Next comes the warding off of this dart, which consists in an accompanying
prohibition against wonder. For who would wonder that wicked men act wickedly,
that the more powerful crush the weak and feeble, and especially that the
ungodly, like serpents, lie in wait for the Woman's Seed by striking at him?
Or to whom, finally, will it seem strange or novel if a wolf pursues a sheep?
Therefore let us follow the counsel of 1 Peter 4:12.

Yet he does not so much dissuade us from wonder as warn us not to be stupefied
by this circumstance and left without counsel, and especially not to fall away
from faith and the pursuit of truth. This is what the Prophet expressly marks
in Psalm 37:8. After he had once and again forbidden us to grow heated because
of the prosperity of the wicked, he finally commands us not to become so
inflamed that, burning with this heat, we do something evil and depart from a
commendable manner of life. For many people join themselves to the profession
of truth and the Gospel for the sake of advantage or honor. When they do not
obtain the things which follow, they cast aside the pursuit of truth and blame
God's most wise government in heaven—especially if they not only fall short of
their hope but are then struck by the contrary things, loss and contempt.

Against either this stupefaction or the falling away from truth caused by it,
he strengthens the godly by the accompanying inspection and observation of
God. This is amplified by God's accompanying height, which is illustrated by
something lower than it, namely, the height of the greatest princes. From this
confusion and disorder of affairs, people are often assailed by the temptation
that the things done on earth are no concern of God's. Therefore, to break
that thought, the Prophet teaches that God keeps his eyes fixed upon these
things, so that he may record them in his notebooks, and turns his ears—ears
held ready—so that he may hear both corrupt judgments and the cries of the
poor that arise from them. He then strengthens them with the fact that the God
who observes these things and keeps them for vengeance is higher than all
corrupt judges and oppressive princes. They therefore must not doubt that it
will be easy for him to bring all these things into order.

His additional statement that “those on high are over them” presents a rather
more difficult question: whether it is spoken of God in the plural number, to
indicate the distinction of the Persons, or should be referred to the elect
angels. Although it matters little to the sum of the doctrine which of these
senses we follow, for my part I incline to this opinion: just as above he
opposed

God and God's loftiness to the king, or supreme governor of the commonwealth,
and to his pomp, so here he opposes the blessed angels, subordinated by God and
sent for the service of the godly, to the subordinate judges and magistrates
sent by the king. Thus, just as the tyranny of kings and their ministers
strikes us down, so the thought of God and his servants may lift us up and
console us.

For although the one God is abundantly sufficient for salvation of every kind,
he nevertheless takes account of our unbelief and weakness, since we can
scarcely, if at all, hope for salvation except through means. He therefore
appoints the holy angels, through whose work we may both be safe and protected
from threatening evils and obtain the good things we desire. Thus Elisha
strengthened himself and his servant against the army of the Syrians. Thus in
Psalm 34 the Prophet strengthens the godly with the fact that the Angel of the
Lord encamps around them. And in Matthew 18 Christ terrifies everyone away
from offending the least and most abject, because their angels continually
stand in God's presence, ready to exact punishment from those who offend the
least in the faith of Christ.

But this whole argument concerning the apparent obstacle which affliction
poses to the happiness of the godly is treated throughout the whole of Romans
8, where affliction is taught not only not to impede that happiness but even to
advance and promote it. For there are two things which appear to assail the
happiness of the faithful in this life: one is the sin which adheres within
those who are justified, in their very innermost chambers; the other is
affliction brought upon them from elsewhere. The former is overthrown in
Romans 6:7, and the latter in chapter 8.
