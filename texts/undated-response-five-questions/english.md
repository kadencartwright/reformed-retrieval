# Response to Five Questions

*An undated French minute signed by Calvin as Charles d'Espeville; a fresh translation from CO X/1 255–258.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 255]

On the first article, where it is asked how far the advice to leave a husband or wife in order to flee persecution or idolatry extends: I will always exhort married people not to leave their spouses until they have tried every means possible to win them and draw them along, for such a union well deserves that every duty be performed. It is certain that, besides the scandals and bad consequences that may arise, anyone who lightly departs does not keep the faith owed to his or her spouse.

First, then, if the husband or wife who opposes the gospel permits the other spouse to serve God purely, in that case I do not see that it is lawful for the man to abandon his wife or the wife her husband. If there is opposition, let the believing spouse remain steadfast so as not to turn aside, yet try through gentleness to soften the spouse's disposition. Thus let no one depart without being constrained by imminent peril—for example, if the wife threatens the husband or the husband the wife and one sees that the threat is in earnest, or if persecution comes from elsewhere. There must always be necessity, and separations must not be made by choice.

It is nevertheless not said that men or women who have left their spouses ought or are permitted to remarry. Such a withdrawal does not break the marriage bond but serves to flee the fury of the gospel's enemies. Indeed,

### [CO X/1, p. 256]

the man or woman who has withdrawn in this way must take care to entreat the spouse until an evident cause of divorce is found. Those who are thus separated must live as widows or widowers.

As for a man or woman whose spouse is adulterous, unless the crime can be verified, they must exercise patience, for a divorce cannot take place in such a case without judicial inquiry. Whatever injustice there may be among the judges, if the crime is notorious the offended spouse will still be able to have evidence taken in order to verify it. And if, in what is called an adversarial proceeding—that is, after the accused has been heard—the adultery is proved, even though no sentence has been given, a Christian church may receive in marriage the man or woman who produces the record of that proceeding, but not otherwise.

On the second article, where mourning clothes are asked about: I find no great scruple in them. First, the thing is not in itself so unlawful that it cannot be tolerated like other weaknesses of a worldly polity. And because abstaining from such things is not strictly required when scandal, murmuring, or offense may result, excessive rigor and austerity in this matter does not seem good. Nevertheless, let everyone be warned and exhorted to cut off all pomp, superfluities, and much petty frippery, so that everyone can see and discern that those who have profited in God's school take no pleasure in such a thing but use it only as a duty in order to conform to custom.

On the third article, namely, whether one may claim the confiscated property of those who are persecuted, in their favor or that of their families: it is certain that this cannot be done without offense. For the legal letters obtained in such a case will contain

### [CO X/1, p. 257]

manifest blasphemies against the honor of God, who will be blasphemed under headings of errors and crimes of treason against the divine majesty. The person who wishes to enjoy their benefit will have to produce them in court, which is a kind of acknowledgment. There is inexcusable evil in that. I therefore do not see that it is lawful for a man well instructed in the gospel to involve himself in such disguises.

Nevertheless, if there were someone among those of influence who favor the gospel and wished to do a kindness, I do not say that one could not take advantage of such an opportunity, provided that the person who desires to save his property or part of it neither procures such letters to the reproach of God's name nor even consents to their being ratified, but recovers what he can without participating in the evil.

On the fourth, namely, whether it is permissible by counterfeit keys, money, or other schemes to cause those held prisoner for religion to escape: for my part, I would not dare give or approve such advice. But when such undertakings occur, I will pray that their outcome may be good and will rejoice if someone escapes without disturbance or scandal. I see that when there was a prisoner among the disciples, the church contended through prayers rather than otherwise. Even Saint Paul was unwilling to be redeemed with money. In order not to go wrong, therefore, it would be best to abstain from such schemes. But when someone has escaped by such a means, although I do not dare justify the deed, I will praise God for the deliverance and will even bear with those who use it rather than condemn them.

On the last question, namely, how one ought to act toward those who return to idolatries after communicating at the Supper: it is certainly necessary to maintain an order and discipline that serves as a bridle, but in this as in everything it is expedient to keep due measure. On the one hand, the Supper must not be permitted to be profaned without any correction; on the other, one must guard against excessive rigor that discourages the weak.

For example, it seems sufficient for the Supper to be denied once to someone who has turned aside to superstitions, provided that he has not persisted or committed an extraordinary act that would intensify or aggravate the idolatry. But before everything else, careful attention must be paid to whether there is repentance. For those who relapse a second or third time, the severity ought to be greater for several reasons: both because being too lenient with them would nourish their evil and make us the cause of their destruction, and because it would expose to mockery God's name and the

### [CO X/1, p. 258]

promises that would be made, besides giving everyone license. In the end there would be nothing but contempt in place of reverence.

Charles d'Espeville.

## Translator's notes

1. Charles d'Espeville was a name Calvin used; CO says the minute is signed by Calvin. The spelling *Charles despeville* is retained diplomatically in `french.txt` and expanded only in the English display.
2. The first answer distinguishes necessary flight from a dissolution of marriage and requires separated spouses to remain unmarried unless an independently provable cause of divorce emerges. This is a historical ecclesiastical judgment, not present-day family-law advice.
3. “Adversarial proceeding” translates *iugement contradictoire*, which the text immediately glosses as a proceeding in which the accused has been heard. The proposed church action depends on production of that judicial record even before a formal sentence.
4. The third question's *demander la confiscation* is clarified by the answer: the persecuted person or family seeks to recover property under confiscation through official legal letters that stigmatize the condemned religion. “Claim the confiscated property” reflects that context; it does not mean asking the state to confiscate someone else's property.
5. *Mesioyray* is an old elided form meaning “I will rejoice.” The source's allusions to a prisoner among the disciples and to Paul refusing monetary redemption are left as allusions; no biblical locator is supplied in the witness.
6. At the p. 256/257 turn CO divides *mani-festes*; the marker precedes complete *manifestes* and English “manifest.” At 257/258 it divides *pro-messes*; the marker similarly precedes complete *promesses* and “promises.”
7. The questions about clandestine prison escape and marital separation concern legally and physically consequential acts. This edition documents a historical source and does not offer contemporary legal, safety, or operational advice.
