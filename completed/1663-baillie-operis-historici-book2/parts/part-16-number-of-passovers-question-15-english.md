## QUESTION XV

### On the number of Passovers from Christ’s baptism to his death

This question is famous among all chronologists, ancient and modern, and also among most interpreters of the Gospel. I say “most,” for I acknowledge that some possess such prudence that they skip over, untouched and unnamed, the knots they cannot untie. The question is also of great importance, for the certain determination of Christ’s years on earth depends upon it. Indeed, as the question is resolved, the entire body of sacred chronology in this part is either fitted together correctly or badly dislocated by the necessary addition of one, two, or more years, or by their no less necessary subtraction. The body of chronology is of such a kind that all its limbs cohere so closely through astronomical and chronological cycles that if there is error or variation in days, much less years, the entire face of the heavens and the whole structure of the times is altered.

There are five principal opinions here. The first is that of Irenaeus in *Against Heresies*, book 2, chapter 39. He assigns Christ forty-six or fifty years, while nevertheless placing his baptism in his thirtieth year, as the evangelist has it. Since he thus assigns the Lord at least sixteen or twenty years after his baptism, he must have thought that the same number of Passovers should be counted after it. Augustine and others who refute this opinion appear to attribute it to more people than Irenaeus, yet I read of no one by name except Irenaeus who defended it. It certainly conflicts with manifest Scriptures, for the Lord was crucified under Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius, in whose fifteenth year the Lord had been baptized. But if he had not died before his forty-sixth or fiftieth year, he must have suffered long after Pilate was recalled from Judaea and Tiberius had died. For Pilate presided over Judaea—

[Marginalia: Irenaeus’s first opinion is rejected.]

—no longer than twelve years, and Tiberius reigned no longer than twenty-three.

The arguments Irenaeus brings in his support are wholly invalid. First, because the Jews said at John 8:57, “You are not yet fifty years old,” it follows that Christ was then close to fifty years of age. Reply: from this supposition of the crowd, that Christ had not yet attained his fiftieth year, nothing follows about his proximity either to fifty or to forty. It follows only that he was not a man of advanced age, so that his assertion that he was before Abraham would appear all the more absurd.

Irenaeus’s second argument: Jesus sanctified every age—infancy, childhood, youth, and old age. Therefore he reached his fiftieth year, from which old age begins. Reply: Christ sanctified many things that never happened to him in his own person. He sanctified for his people all diseases—fevers, leprosies, childbirths, consumptions, pleurisies, dropsies, and innumerable others. Through Christ every kind of life is sanctified for the godly: trade, military service, and all liberal and mechanical arts, which Christ nevertheless never practiced in his own person.

The third argument: Christ was rightly and deservedly called Rabbi and Master by everyone. Therefore he had the age of a master, and the age of a master was forty or fifty years. Reply: the law required no more than thirty years for carrying out the office of an ordinary Levitical teacher and priest. No fixed time was appointed for a prophet called outside the ordinary order to the office of teacher and master.

Fourth: apostolic men who associated with John and the other apostles reported that Christ reached so great an age. Reply: we acknowledge that in his youth Irenaeus associated for some time with Polycarp, and Polycarp with John the apostle. But it must not be conceded that either John or Polycarp, or any other apostolic man, was the source of that tradition, unless perhaps Papias or some similarly simpler-minded man is intended, one more inclined to mix fables with true histories.

This opinion of Irenaeus is certainly so displeasing to Baronius that he affirms it was inserted into Irenaeus’s writings by ancient heretics. His principal argument is that in the same place Irenaeus affirms that Christ was baptized in his thirtieth year and crucified at the third Passover thereafter, statements incompatible with either his forty-sixth or his fiftieth year. I would gladly admit this censure of Baronius, but Feuardentius, Maldonatus, Jansenius, and all other learned writers, papist as well as Protestant, impute this error not to forgers but to Irenaeus himself. Moreover, this addition of the heretics, as Baronius would have it, is not merely appended at the end of chapter 39, but is defended at length from Holy Scripture throughout almost all of chapter 40.

When the monk Feuardentius discusses this matter in his preface to Irenaeus, he vomits out upon the entire Protestant nation gallons full of bile in the most abusive words, as though they were despisers, mockers, and tramplers upon the Fathers. I certainly know no learned man in our communion who does not rise before the Fathers with as much reverence as any papist. Yet whenever they find blemishes in the Fathers, in correcting them they follow no rule other than that which the Jesuits themselves approve and prescribe. These, indeed, are Petavius’s words on this very question in his treatment of Epiphanius’s heresy 51, the Alogi, page 205: “Many things are sometimes written by the most holy Fathers with insufficient truth and care. To wish to defend them is perhaps the part of a religious but insufficiently prudent man; to assail them too violently is the part of neither a religious nor a prudent man. With the moderation that is fitting, we do not so much search into the errors and slips of divine men—yet men—as disclose those that freely present themselves and confront us even against our will, so that they may harm no one. We should no more preserve and defend their faults, if there were any, than imitate them.”

The first opinion erred by excess; the second errs by defect. Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Lactantius, Tatian, Julius Africanus as cited by Jerome on Daniel 9, Augustine, and many other Fathers count only one year between Christ’s baptism and death. This opinion conflicts with express passages in John the evangelist, where at least three Passovers from the baptism to the cross are mentioned in clear words. Henricus Philippi’s distinction does not conceal this error: namely, that these authors take the number thirty indefinitely as a round number and do not deny that in exact calculation it can be extended by some additional portions of years. The argument they bring from Isaiah 61:2 shows clearly enough that this was not their meaning and that they acknowledged only one year without any extension from the baptism to the death. For from it they conclude that Christ preached the acceptable year of the Lord, which Isaiah expresses in the singular and thus makes only one. Moreover, those authors hold that the baptism and the cross occurred under the same consuls of the same year, “[the two Lords].” Thus this entire distinction is a fig-leaf, and the excuse for a manifest error is fruitless.

The third opinion counts three Passovers, or two years, between the baptism and the cross. Its author is Irenaeus and, after him, Epiphanius, whom very many follow: Lyranus, Abulensis, Pererius, Maldonatus, and Petavius; and among our writers Calvin, Musculus, and—

[Marginalia: The second opinion, Tertullian’s, which assigns Christ only one Passover after his baptism, is rejected.]

—others. They follow Epiphanius in the number of Passovers but not in his reasons for that number. From Isaiah 61:1 he concludes that Christ preached for two years: an acceptable one, in which Christ’s doctrine was welcome and pleasing to all; and a year of contradiction, in which the Jews contradicted Christ. Petavius shows in the passage cited above how frivolous this argument of Epiphanius is. There he also rightly corrects Epiphanius for wrongly wishing to gather two Passovers from John 7:10, 37. Only one feast is at issue there, not two, and it was not Passover but the Feast of Tabernacles, σκηνοπηγία. Irenaeus far more correctly builds three Passovers upon three other passages of the evangelist, which are so express that they cannot be contradicted: namely, 2:13, 6:4, and 13:1. Had Irenaeus stopped here and not added through an apocryphal tradition many other years after the baptism about which the Gospel says nothing, he would have kept himself free of a great blemish. In this third opinion we find fault only with its assignment to Christ of fewer years and Passovers than are clearly and necessarily elicited from the Scriptures.

Thus the fourth opinion, against the preceding, affirms four Passovers, not merely three. It is followed by Chemnitz, Beza, Junius, Rollock, Grotius, and the Dutch annotators. The English annotators are ambiguous here, for they merely present this opinion together with the preceding and determine nothing themselves. It is also followed by Baronius, Jansenius, a Lapide, Henricus Philippi, Emmanuel Sa, Toletus, Ferus, and many others on John 5:1. The primary foundation of this opinion is that, besides the three Passovers expressed in the preceding passages of John, a fourth is also established by a sound inference from chapter 5, verse 1. Irenaeus indeed asserts this, but, as Petavius correctly notes, he does not therefore establish more than three Passovers, because he confuses the feast in chapter 5 with the Passover in chapter 6. Irenaeus certainly errs in this, for no one doubts that these are two different feasts. The entire difficulty, however, is to demonstrate that the feast in chapter 5 was Passover and not another. The authors of the preceding opinion hold that it was Pentecost because it immediately follows the Passover in chapter 2. But this is plainly false. For after the Passover in 2:13, Christ states specifically in 4:35 that four months still remained until the harvest. The final wheat harvest was almost concluded at Pentecost, when loaves of fine flour were offered to God fifty days after the sheaf of firstfruits was offered at Passover. Yet from Passover to the first harvest there was not one month—indeed, scarcely one day—for on the second day after Passover the sheaf of barley was offered, with which the first, barley harvest began. From Passover to the second, wheat harvest at Pentecost there were not two months, much less four, for the fifty days of Pentecost lack ten days of two months. It is therefore impossible that this feast was Pentecost after the first Passover. If we say, as the defenders of this opinion do not wish, that it was Pentecost after the second Passover, this will overthrow the third and establish the fourth opinion no less than if the feast were expressly conceded to have been Passover.

I do not entirely grasp Grotius’s meaning here. He holds that the feast in John 5:1 should be understood as Passover, but also holds that the four months mentioned in John 4:35 should be counted from the first of Nisan. Certainly, a feast occurring four months after the first of Nisan can be neither Passover nor Pentecost.

Cartwright and certain papists, as Maldonatus also notes, hold that the feast named in John 5:1 is the Feast of Tabernacles, σκηνοπηγίαν. But this too is impossible, for the Feast of Tabernacles was celebrated in September, as we have in Leviticus 23:31. September preceded the first, barley harvest by at least seven months and the second, wheat harvest by nine. Thus the first feast that immediately followed the point four months before harvest among the Jews could in no way have been the Feast of Tabernacles, for two other feasts, Passover and Pentecost, intervened between it and the period four months before harvest.

Some affirm that it is impossible to know what that feast in John 5:1 was. Cajetan and Gualter take this view in their comments on the passage; they should be referred to the third class, against which we are now arguing for the fourth opinion.

The other argument for the fourth opinion is Daniel 9:27, where it is asserted that the Messiah’s preaching of the Gospel before his death will last for half a week, that is, three and a half years. Unless you count four Passovers up to the Passion, so much time will not be given to the Messiah’s ministry. Three Passovers make only two years, but four make three, together with the months that passed from the baptism to the next Passover. Those months were at least three according to the common calculation that refers the baptism to January 6 and the following Passover to March 25 or April 3. Those three years and three months fulfilled half the prophetic week of years, though not mathematically and precisely to the month, day, hour, and moment, yet in a civil and political reckoning, where the calculation is looser according to the common manner of speech.

Thus far the fourth opinion appears true, but the inquiry proceeds further: whether it is necessary to acknowledge more than four Passovers. The authors of the fifth opinion—Mercator, Scaliger, Calvisius, Casaubon, Deckerius, and others—affirm that it is. Suarez acknowledges that this opinion cannot be convicted of falsity from Holy Scripture, and Henricus Philippi finds no adequate reason for its rejection other than its difference from the common judgment of the learned. But these considerations will satisfy no sensible person if any true foundations for this opinion can be brought from Scripture, such as those that follow are customarily brought by its supporters. —the second-first Sabbath, σάββατον δευτερόπρωτον, at Luke 6:1 is the first Sabbath after the second day of Passover, or the first day of Unleavened Bread, as was abundantly confirmed above by both reasons and authorities. Therefore this Sabbath presupposes a Passover immediately preceding. That Passover was neither the first at John 2:13 nor the second at John 5:1; it was therefore a third interposed between John 5:1 and John 6:4.

That it was not the first is evident because John the Baptist was still free from prison long after the first Passover following the baptism, as may be seen in John 3:24. Yet before the Sabbath on which the disciples plucked the ears of grain, John was in prison, as is clear to one comparing Matthew 11:2 with 12:1. Nor was that Sabbath the one which immediately followed John’s second Passover, for on that Sabbath Christ remained in Jerusalem, as we have in John 5:2, 5, and healed the sick man at the pool of Bethesda. He did not walk through the grainfields with his disciples that day. Nor could the Lord have left the city at Passover before that Sabbath which always intervened between Passover and the seventh day of Unleavened Bread, or the eighth of Passover, because those who came to the feast did not leave it until the feast was concluded and all seven days of Unleavened Bread were completed. It is therefore most likely that the Sabbath on which Jesus walked through the grainfields immediately after Passover but before the feast had ended occurred in Galilee, when Jesus did not ascend to Jerusalem for fear of the Jews. This is the primary foundation upon which Scaliger and his followers build the fifth Passover.

Most papists angrily reject this invention of Scaliger, or rather Mercator, but to restrain their indignation about the fifth Passover they should remember that very many of their most learned writers seem compelled by necessity to acknowledge this number, though from a different principle. For if one Passover must be placed between the baptism and the first miracle at Cana, then at least five Passovers must be acknowledged, at least by all the authors of the fourth opinion, for they all count four Passovers after the wedding at Cana. We prove as follows that a Passover intervened between that wedding and the baptism.

In every complete year there is always one Passover. But according to a great company of the most learned papists, there was a complete year from the baptism to the wedding at Cana. They not only affirm this, but receive it as the public faith of the church, because in her liturgies the church celebrates Christ’s baptism and the miracle at the wedding on the same day, namely January 6, as though the latter had occurred on the exact anniversary of Christ’s baptism. This is the view of Rupert, Alcuin, Bernard, Aquinas, Durandus, Cajetan, Suarez, Barradius, Salianus, Mariana, and many others whom Henricus Philippi cites in his questions on the birth and Passion of Christ, page 66. I acknowledge that Baronius and others hold a different opinion. Yet through all their inventions they cannot entirely preserve the Roman Church’s authority, which proposes for the people’s belief that Christ’s first miracle was performed on the same anniversary as his baptism, namely January 6. But if nothing of the kind occurred on that day, the Roman Church will in this way teach something manifestly false.

The fifth Passover is also established in another way. Deckerius, a most learned Jesuit, gives Christ thirty-five years on earth, while Kepler, a chronologist of the highest learning, gives him thirty-six. On their foundation the fifth Passover will be necessary. Indeed, the Jesuits have no ground to argue here against Scaliger until they have thoroughly overthrown the foundations of their brothers and colleagues.

Our learned Nisbet brings five arguments against the fifth Passover on page 260 of his book. The first, which he calls infallible, is taken from the annual turns of the high priests Annas and Caiaphas. Annas was high priest at the time of the baptism, Luke 3:2; Caiaphas was high priest at the time of the Passion, John 11:49 and 18:13; but in the following year Annas returned to the high priesthood, Acts 4:6. From this it appears that Annas and Caiaphas administered the high priesthood by turns. Thus Annas was high priest in the year of the baptism, Caiaphas in the second year after the baptism, Annas again in the third, Caiaphas in the fourth, and Annas again in the fifth. Therefore from the baptism, when Annas was high priest, to the cross, when Caiaphas was high priest, there is an interval of three or four years, not more, and thus no more Passovers. Reply: even if we conceded that Annas and Caiaphas administered the high priesthood in annual turns—which most papists as well as Protestants reject as absurd—the argument would still not be infallible. First, those who assign only one year to Christ’s ministry could confirm their opinion through this argument, for in the Gospel we do not read an express mention of Annas’s and Caiaphas’s high priesthood except in the year of the baptism and the year of the cross. Therefore there was an interval of only one year between the baptism and the cross. Second, those who assign five or six years to the Lord’s ministry could equally resort to this argument. Nisbet counts Caiaphas’s high priesthood at the cross as the fourth turn after Annas’s at the baptism; why could Kepler not call it the sixth? Kepler certainly regards his arguments proving that six years passed between the baptism and the cross, and thus that four turns of the high priests were omitted in the Gospel, as no weaker than—

—Nisbet regards his own, by which he proves that four years passed and two turns were omitted. Third, I do not know whether any other learned man affirms what Nisbet holds from Acts 3:6: that Annas was high priest because the high priesthood had then returned to him from Caiaphas. The Jewish year certainly began with the month of Nisan, and with the new year began the high priests’ new turns. Thus if it was Caiaphas’s turn at Passover, in the middle of the month of Nisan, the high priesthood could not yet have returned from Caiaphas to Annas immediately after Pentecost of the same year. Fourth, there will be difficulty in the manner of counting the turns, for if you count exclusively, the interval will be only two years, but if inclusively, you will have four.

Leaving these points aside, however, let us attack the foundation of the argument itself. I find that the explanation of how Annas and Caiaphas are called high priests together in the same passages of Scripture is a very difficult question much exercised by the learned. We do not wish to digress at length into the matter itself on the occasion of the present argument. I acknowledge that Beza, Diodati after him, and the Dutch annotators before Nisbet make Annas and Caiaphas administrators of the high priesthood in annual turns. Yet in this matter Calvin’s judgment seems much more solid. It is followed by Scaliger, Casaubon, Calvisius, Jansenius, and most others: namely, that there were never two high priests at one time. Beza relies upon the testimony of Eusebius, *History*, book 1, chapter 10, but Calvin upon that of Josephus, *Antiquities*, book 18, chapter 3 and those following. Eusebius is indeed the sole authority for two men administering the high priesthood in annual alternation and thus for the three or four years between Christ’s baptism and death deduced from the turns of Annas and Caiaphas. Yet Scaliger correctly refutes Eusebius in *On the Correction of Time*, book 6, page 505, and in his preface to Eusebius’s *Chronicle*; Casaubon likewise in his thirteenth exercise against Baronius, paragraph 4; Petavius in *On Times*, book 10, chapter 56; and others. For Eusebius professes to take everything he has here from Josephus alone, but how different—indeed, contrary to Eusebius—the things Josephus presents are will readily appear to anyone comparing their passages. In *Antiquities* 18, chapters 3, 6, and following, Josephus relates that Annas was appointed high priest by Quirinius at the end of Augustus’s days; that he was removed by the Roman governor Valerius Gratus shortly after the beginning of the emperor Tiberius’s reign; and that in four years the same Gratus changed the high priest five times. The last of these, Joseph Caiaphas, remained in office not only until the eleventh year of the governor Gratus, but throughout Pilate’s entire prefecture, until in the twenty-second year of Tiberius Pilate was sent to Rome by Vitellius, prefect of Syria, to plead his case. Caiaphas too was then removed from the high priesthood by Vitellius, and a successor was given to him. From these statements of Josephus, Eusebius first infers very badly that the time of Christ’s preaching extended from the high priesthood of Annas to that of Caiaphas, between whom were four others, each high priest for one year. What carelessness this is! Does Josephus say a single word suggesting that Christ’s ministry began in the high priesthood of Annas and ended in Caiaphas’s? Moreover, if this were true, would there not be six, or at least five, years of Christ’s preaching, since it continued through the terms of six annual high priests whom Eusebius names from Josephus? Second, does Josephus say anything about the conjunction of the high priesthoods of Annas and Caiaphas? On the contrary, he expressly inserts between them the names of four other high priests. Third, does Josephus say a single word about an annual administration of the high priests? It is true that he assigns four years to the four high priests interposed between Caiaphas and Annas. But he gives several years to Annas, who was appointed by Quirinius under Augustus and not removed by Gratus until he was the fourth or fifth governor after Quirinius; likewise to Caiaphas, who was appointed by Gratus, continued throughout Pilate’s prefecture, and was removed from office by Vitellius. Indeed, he openly teaches that after Herod’s time until the temple’s final desolation, the high priests were appointed for years, months, and days at the mere whim of tyrants, without any rule except that prescribed by ambition and greed.

Eusebius wrongly claims to deduce those points from Josephus. But what Beza and others after him add concerning the administration of those annual high priests by turns has no word in Josephus or even Eusebius himself. On the contrary, Eusebius does not suggest that any one of all the high priests he names returned by any turns to the office after he had once ceased to hold it.

Petavius censures Beza for this invention in the place cited above, and yet in the same place he falls into that very error in an even worse form. For the solution in which he rests after rejecting all others to the grave difficulty, “How were Annas and Caiaphas high priests at the same time?” is that they administered the high priesthood by turns. Yet so that he may differ from Beza, he holds that the turns were not annual, but either daily or monthly, as Pilate prescribed. This invention of Petavius is far more empty and contradicts what we have already brought from both Josephus and Eusebius. Throughout Pilate’s whole prefecture Caiaphas alone administered the high priesthood without any colleague, whether daily, monthly, or annual. But we have pressed Nisbet’s first argument too far.

The second is this. The Passover lamb, Exodus 12:3, 15, was set apart on the tenth of Nisan to be slain on the fourteenth, and thus had three days of preparation for death. Therefore Christ had three years of preaching preparatory to the cross. The author affirms that this argument—

—is not merely valid but necessarily valid. Reply: if we conceded that this argument had any force, not three but four or five years would be concluded from it, for from the tenth day to the second evening of the fourteenth there are five days. Nisbet wishes the days to be counted from the preceding evening; therefore the tenth day will be one, the eleventh another, the twelfth the third, the thirteenth the fourth, and the fourteenth the fifth, especially since the lamb was slain between the two evenings, that is, at the end of the fourteenth day and the beginning of the fifteenth, as we proved above. Nor do I remember any interpreter who does not name at least four days of the lamb’s separation. But if one prefers to count only three with Nisbet, what will become of the half day and half year for which the argument is being made here?

I answer the matter itself. No years are deduced from these days by any authority or reason. The Passover lamb was indeed a most true and clear type of Christ, and, as far as we are concerned, the lamb’s setting apart for sacrifice may signify Christ’s setting apart for the cross. But before Nisbet no one affirms that the three days of the lamb’s separation prefigure three years of Christ’s preaching. Among our writers I see Ainsworth, and among the Jesuits Toletus, teach that the tenth day on which the lamb was set apart prefigured Christ’s entrance into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday, which was the fifth day before his death. But to conclude three years of Christ’s preaching, or four Passovers from the baptism to the cross, from three days of the lamb’s separation seems more inept than if from the lamb’s age, which could not exceed one year, we concluded with the Fathers that Christ’s ministry could not have lasted longer. Such an application of types is gratuitous, not to say highly rash and worthy of censure.

The third argument is from Daniel 9:27, where the Messiah’s public ministry and the confirmation of the covenant are defined as half a week, that is, three and a half years. Reply: although Nisbet, following Beroaldus, wrongly explains this passage as if Christ died not in the middle but at the end of the last of the seventy weeks, as we demonstrated in its place, nevertheless from it is elicited a difficult argument to solve: that from Christ’s baptism to his death there was half a week, or three and a half years. Yet Nisbet himself opens a sufficiently easy source for a solution by distinguishing between Christ’s private and public ministry. He begins the former at the baptism and the latter at the Passover in John 2, assigning half a year to the former and three years to the latter. We indeed acknowledge no private ministry of Christ, for Christ’s teaching and miracles were no less public in Galilee than in Jerusalem. Nevertheless, if it is permissible to distinguish here, as appears necessarily to be done from Matthew 4:17, where it is asserted that Christ did not begin to preach and set apart the twelve apostles for preaching until after John was cast into prison, it will appear that more than half a year passed from the baptism to that preaching and more than three years from that preaching to the cross. For most learned writers think John remained in prison for two full years. But I do not wish now to descend into this difficult controversy.

The fourth argument Nisbet promises does not appear. The fifth is the question itself: namely, that there were four Passovers after the baptism, not more. This has now been answered sufficiently.

Let us conclude the question. The first opinion is altogether absurd. The second, third, and fourth are all true and certain in their positive part, but false in their negative and exclusive part. For it is true that Christ preached one year, likewise two and three, but false that he preached only one, only two, or only three. I would not wish to contend for the fifth opinion against so many and such great authors; nevertheless, it appears very probable.
