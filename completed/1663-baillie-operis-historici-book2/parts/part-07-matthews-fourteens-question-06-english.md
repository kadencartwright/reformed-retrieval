## QUESTION VI

### On Matthew’s three groups of fourteen

Two things are asked here: do they exist, and why do they exist? Concerning the first there is a very serious difficulty. If all the generations, or all the persons begotten whom Matthew names from Abraham to Christ, are counted, the total is not found; at least one of the forty-two will always be lacking. The first fourteen is complete, as may be seen in the calculation set out here: Abraham first, Isaac second, Jacob third, Judah fourth, Perez fifth, Hezron sixth, Aram seventh, Amminadab eighth, Nahshon ninth, Salmon tenth, Boaz eleventh, Obed twelfth, Jesse thirteenth, and David fourteenth.

The second also is complete, as this calculation demonstrates: Solomon first, Rehoboam second, Abijah third, Asa fourth, Jehoshaphat fifth, Joram sixth, Uzziah seventh, Jotham eighth, Ahaz ninth, Hezekiah tenth, Manasseh eleventh, Amon twelfth, Josiah thirteenth, and Jeconiah fourteenth.

The third, however, numbers no more than thirteen in this way: Shealtiel first, Zerubbabel second, Abihud third, Eliakim fourth, Azor fifth, Zadok sixth, Achim seventh, Eliud eighth, Eleazar ninth, Matthan tenth, Jacob eleventh, Joseph twelfth, and Jesus Christ, blessed forever, thirteenth. The fourteenth is lacking here.

Different people give different answers here. The later as well as the middle English annotators follow the wrong road after the Jesuit Salmerón, though they do not name him. They complete the third group of fourteen by counting Jeconiah first and Christ last; the second by counting David first and Josiah last; and the first by making Abraham first and David also last. The annotators ought not to have followed the Jesuit’s solution, for it is manifestly unsound. It counts David twice and makes two generations from his individual and singular person, which cannot be tolerated. Salmerón’s pretext is vain: that David bore two characters, first a private one and then a public one when he reigned. If this reasoning held, it would double all the kings after David, and two generations would have to be made from each of them, for they all lived for some time as private men before entering upon the kingdom. Indeed, to forestall this pretext for error, the evangelist, naming David twice in verse 6, twice adds to him the epithet “king.”

The second solution, which Dr. Hammond appears to approve, is no better: namely, that the name Abner, wrongly absent from the Greek, should be added to the third group of fourteen from the Hebrew text of Matthew. It is excessive audacity to correct the evangelist’s authentic text from an apocryphal and corrupt one. The learned dispute whether Matthew ever wrote in Hebrew, but before recent papists no Christian ever doubted the authority of the Greek Gospel. Nor will anyone who reads the Hebrew Matthew deny that it is corrupt and very often departs from the original purity. In any event, there is no mention of the spurious Abner in Münster’s Hebrew edition; what Mercerus’s edition, which I have not yet seen, contains I do not know.

Third, although Grotius offers and proves the true answer, which we shall discuss shortly, he adds other things here that are entirely displeasing. He thinks it necessary to acknowledge not only no deficiency in the third group of fourteen but even posits a great excess and excessive abundance. From Chronicles he inserts Assir between Jeconiah and Shealtiel, indeed in the middle of his catalogue from Matthew. In the third group of fourteen he inserts ten or more anonymous men, as many, that is, as the number by which Luke exceeds Matthew between Jeconiah and Christ. Certainly most interpreters take *Assir* as a common noun, not a proper one—namely, as the attribute “captive.” This suited Jeconiah, who either begot Shealtiel in Babylon or had him as his successor. As for Grotius’s added anonymous men, they lack all authority as well as reason. Neither reason nor experience suggests that in two different lines—whether taken legally, naturally, or in any other way—the persons in each must be equal in number.

The fourth answer is Antonius Thysius’s, which Louis de Dieu embraces with such applause in his appendix on Matthew. It appears no better than those before it. Thysius wished to supply the deficiency of one person in the third group of fourteen by counting the Virgin Mary. Tostado rightly hissed down this invention long ago. Besides the fact that the Jews did not number women in their genealogies, although the Virgin Mary’s miraculous conception by the Holy Spirit gave her a singular privilege, adding her would nevertheless not solve the knot. Her addition would exclude her husband Joseph, for in no lineage can two collateral degrees be accepted as different generations; they count—

—only as one. If one excludes Joseph, adding Mary produces no increase in the number.

Two other solutions remain, each of which has many defenders, both ancient and recent, in both the papist and Protestant churches. We think they should be combined into one, for each comes down to one and the same thing. Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine, Epiphanius, Chrysostom, and other fathers; Calvin, Chemnitz, Beza, Piscator, Gomarus, Spanhemius, Grotius, Gualter, Bucer, and others of our writers; and the *Glossa ordinaria*, Nicholas of Lyra, Tostado, Jansenius, Maldonatus, Barradas, and other papists hold that two persons are to be understood under the single name Jeconiah: namely, the father Jehoiakim and the son Jehoiachin. Although Jerome rightly observes a difference of two letters in their final syllable, the father being written with K and M and the son with CH and N, nevertheless in translating from Hebrew into Greek and Latin a much greater difference is ordinarily swallowed up in proper names.

Many others after Epiphanius thought that, to clarify this obscurity, the father Jehoiakim should be expressly added. Robert Estienne did add him from an ancient Greek manuscript, as did Beza in the earlier editions of his notes, Piscator, the older English Bibles, the French Bibles, and others—with the goodwill of Maldonatus and other papists. Calvin, however, as well as Beza in his final edition, the new English Bible, Junius, and most others did not consider it advantageous to alter anything in the text, although they placed the aforesaid reading, “Jehoiakim begot Jeconiah,” in the margin. The recent London Greek polyglots—the small, I say, not the great Polyglot—vary here in no respect from our common text, whether in the Vatican Greek (the Alexandrian exemplar lacks many chapters at the beginning of Matthew), the Syriac, Arabic, Persian, or Ethiopic. Nevertheless, all the former interpreters take the first Jeconiah in verse 11 as the father and the later one in verse 13 as the son, so that under one name the father closes the second group of fourteen and the son begins the third. In this way they make all three groups complete. In any event, with Bucer I would rather let anyone regard these solutions as discretionary, or even as one and the same solution, than divide them with Tostado into two contrary ones and ascribe the former to Augustine and the latter to Jerome as their first authors.

We shall touch briefly upon the latter difficulty: why did the evangelist arrange Christ’s ancestors into three groups of fourteen rather than other numbers? First, no anxious investigation is needed here, nor is any answer necessary beyond the fact that this pleased the Holy Spirit. The reasons for his will are generally inscrutable when he does not reveal them elsewhere. This is perhaps why Piscator, Cartwright, and others think that no question should be raised here.

Second, as Chrysostom notes, and after him Calvin and many other Protestant and papist commentators on the passage, there were three notable changes in the Jewish commonwealth from Abraham to Christ. The people lived from Abraham to David under the extraordinary government of judges; from David to the Babylonian captivity under the ordinary government of kings; and from then to Christ under the weak rule of governors, high priests, and others. Under the judges, as Grotius rightly notes, at first glance fourteen generations appeared from Abraham to David when both endpoints were included; under the third regime the same number also appeared from Jeconiah to Christ. Under the second, though there were more, the second group was conformed to the first and third to ease the memory.

Third, after the custom of the age, Origen and other fathers searched for allegorical mysteries in these numbers. The *Glossa ordinaria* and Nicholas of Lyra added some of their own, and Tostado some of his. Salmerón repeats these and adds new ones to the old from his own store, then authoritatively pronounces all those fantastical who refuse to accept these fantasies as solid truths. Maldonatus adds to them all political crises from his medical stockroom. If we wished to delay over examining nonsense of this sort, we would badly waste good hours of leisure.
