### The Württemberg Doctors

Ezekiel 18: “As I live, I do not will the death of the sinner, but that he turn and live.”

### Theodore Beza’s Antithesis

To Ezekiel 18 we reply that the statement is indefinite, which differs from a universal statement in many respects. Its restriction concerning conversion shows that it must be understood only of those to whom the grace of conversion is given, which is certainly proper to the elect. Those who scorn God’s warnings and exhortations made through the preached Word are nevertheless rendered inexcusable. Those whom the Lord does not inwardly convert customarily do this stubbornly. Hence: “Convert me, and I shall be converted.”

### Andreae’s Observations

1. The matter itself teaches that the statement is universal. THE LORD says: “I do not will the death of the one who dies,” whoever he may be. It is therefore manifest impiety and abominable doctrine to make particular what is universal contrary to the express letter and promise.

2. Election is not defined by God’s absolute decree but in Christ, who calls all human beings to repentance. Therefore let no one exclude himself from the number of the elect, but let us say with Augustine: “If you are not predestined, make yourself predestined.”

3. How would they be rendered inexcusable if what you affirm were true: that they were created and destined for eternal destruction by God’s hidden and absolute decree, and because God DOES NOT WILL them to be converted, they therefore cannot be converted? These things are dreadful and abominable to hear in Christ’s church.

4. This prayer does not confirm the error concerning God’s hidden and absolute decree in condemning human beings, but teaches that conversion to God cannot occur through human powers. It therefore implores God’s grace, offered to ALL human beings and thus the whole world.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Observations

[Marginal note: To the replies.]

Let Andreae now reproach us for what he calls our glosses in comparison with his appendix, “whoever he may be,” than which nothing more contrary could be said to what Ezekiel adds: “but that he turn from his ways and live.” From this it is understood that this divine will pertains only to those who are converted—that is, drawn—if it is understood of that secret purpose concerning which Augustine writes in book 2—

—chapter 9 to Boniface: “Not all who are called are called according to purpose, since many”—he does not say all—“are called but few elect. Those called according to purpose are those elected before the world’s foundation.”

But if Andreae thinks that whoever hears the gospel preached is also given the faculty or ability to embrace it—and he maintains that the gospel is offered to every individual without exception, a fiction I have so often refuted, although it is so false as to need no refutation—I first ask at what boundary he terminates this ability. Does he stop it at understanding or extend it farther, to assent, from assent to personal application and the Holy Spirit’s testimony to our spirit, and finally to faith’s fruits and the gift of perseverance? As for understanding, if Andreae thinks that the ability is divinely offered to every individual through preaching the Word, then even if I granted that the gospel is offered to every individual—which can only ridiculously be affirmed—the falsehood of his statement appears from the matter itself. How few among the great number of hearers become, even through repeated sermons, not merely better but more learned! “Because he does not will it,” our adversary will say. I do not excuse the hearers’ negligence or stubbornness. But what if he cannot even will it? As all human beings are by nature after the fall, no one, the apostle testifies, can even think—much less learn, either by himself or from another—what belongs to God except through God’s Spirit.

But if universal grace gives everyone the ability to will, why do not all who can will actually will? Is it not because another grace is needed so that they actually will to learn and understand what they have only been given ability to learn by understanding? It lies beyond controversy, not among Pelagians but all Christians, that no first grace can be efficacious unless a second accompanies it. If this second grace, by which the offered power [δύναμις] and capacity [ἱκανότης] exert their energy, is held to be common to every individual, it necessarily follows—most absurdly and falsely—that no person fails actually to understand the mystery of our salvation. But if it is not common to all—as certainly it is given to very few above the rest, and indeed to some for their own destruction—a distinction is now established among those whom God addresses. Since all are deaf and blind by nature, the cause certainly will not be found in them—not only of deafness and blindness, but of why he heals this deafness and blindness in some and not in others. It lies entirely, without any fault, in the will of him who has mercy upon whom he wills and hardens whom he wills, as seemed good to him from eternity. This statement of Augustine also is most true: “It is certain that we will when we will, but he causes us to will” (Philippians 2:12); “and it is certain that we act when we act, but he causes us to act” (Ezekiel 36:26). Thus, even if I conceded to our adversary that fictitious universality of first grace, he would nevertheless be compelled to arrive at the inequality of second grace.
