20. Objection 11. *The evil of guilt is unfitting to GOD’s intellect and
repugnant to right reason in GOD. Therefore God can in no way will it.*

[Marginal summary: How sin agrees with GOD’s reason, and how it does not.]

I reply. Sin *in the class of moral beings* is repugnant to GOD’s reason, and
to that extent God can in no way will—that is, approve—sin. But sin *in the
class of real beings* having material, demeritorious, and occasional causality
is not repugnant to divine reason; rather, to infinite wisdom it is a means
most suited to the manifestation of GOD’s glory. Yet from this it is by no
means established, with sophistical license, that *GOD in no way wills sin*.
The instance would be this: *Remove sin,* they say, *and the good of justice is
not a good. Therefore the universe would possess a good without sin.*

I reply. Remove sin, and the good of justice would not be an existing good:
true. Yet it would in itself be a good uniquely conducive to the order of the
universe and the expression of the Creator’s attributes. Thus precisely the
contrary consequence follows. Remove sin, and forever the good of justice to
be experimentally manifested, and of many other attributes of GOD—such as
mercy, grace, and power—would remain hidden from human beings and angels.

21. Objection 12. *Sin is essentially evil. Therefore it is impossible that it
be desired in any manner by GOD’s holy will.*

I reply, 1. Sin is essentially evil to a creature subject to law: true. But it
is not evil to GOD himself. Therefore it is rightly concluded that in another,
holy manner it can be desired by God, although a sinner could not de-*

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 512; IA PDF page 569; BSB canvas 564]

*—sire it. 2. What is essentially and in every respect evil, as the
supreme evil is, can in no way be desired by God. But sin is not evil in this
way.

Objection 13. *GOD cannot will what disturbs the order of the universe. Sin
disturbs the order of the universe against GOD’s original institution, since
it turns the rational creature away from the ultimate end, the supreme good.
Therefore GOD can in no way will sin.*

[Marginal summary: Whether sin disturbs the order of the universe.]

I reply. It is true that God cannot desire *what absolutely disturbs the order
of the universe in relation to the universal order and GOD’s ultimate end*.
But the minor premise understood in this way is false. For sin does not disturb
the order in its relation and ordering to the universal order; it conduces to
the ultimate end—the manifestation of glory, power, grace, patience, mercy,
justice, and goodness. Thus it can in some manner be desired by God. If what is
meant is *what disturbs the order of the universe only in a qualified respect,
with respect only to the manifestation of GOD’s legislative and imperial
glory*, God can in no way will it. Understood this way the major premise is
false, the minor true, and there will be no conclusion.

Objection 14. *If GOD willed sin in any manner, it would be lawful for us to
will sin and to conform our will to GOD’s just will. But it is not lawful for
us to will sin in any manner. Therefore GOD in no way wills sin. The reason for
the major is that whoever wills what GOD wills has a just will.* Thus Gregorio
de Valencia, Corvinus, and others.

[Marginal references: Valencia; Corvinus.]

I reply. The major premise, understood universally, is plainly false. God
justly permitted Peter to deny the Savior. Yet Peter was bound to strive
against and resist such a permitting will of GOD, and to pray that God would
not lead him into temptation, even after that permitting will of GOD had been
revealed through CHRIST. The decreeing will is not the rule regulating our
actions; only GOD’s approving will is that to which it is right that our wills
be conformed. Deuteronomy 29:29; Deuteronomy 5:29; Psalm 40:8; Isaiah 8:20;
Romans 11:2; 1 Thessalonians 4:3; 1 Peter 4:2. (2.) This objection is insinuated
in other words in the person of carnal people, Romans 9:19: *Why, then, does GOD
find fault? If he hardens whom he wills, we then sin by willing what GOD wills
with his will, which no one can resist. Our will is thus just, since it is
conformed to GOD’s just but irresistible will.* Paul rejects this carnal and
blasphemous objection in the same place: *Who are you, and so forth?* 3. It is
commonly repeated among theo-*

[Marginal summary: The conformity of our will to GOD’s will of good pleasure
does not free us from sin.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 513; IA PDF page 570; BSB canvas 565]

*—logians, with Augustine, that a wicked son with an evil will wills
his father’s death; that GOD with a good will wills and decrees that the father
die of that same disease; that a pious son prays to God for his dying father’s
life; and that this pious prayer is contrary to GOD’s decree by which God
resolved to remove the father from this life. Hence it necessarily follows that
the wicked man’s will is not just merely because he wills what God wills, and
the pious man’s will is not unjust merely because he wills what God did not
will—indeed, what God decreed the contrary of. Duval says: *It is true that GOD
sometimes with a good will wills what we will with an evil will, and
conversely. But it is false that we with an evil will will what GOD wills us
not to will, for these are contradictory both in GOD and in us.* Corvinus gives
Molina the same reply.

[Marginal references: André Duval, on 2 Thomas, treatise *On Sin*, question 10,
article 3; Corvinus against Molina, *On the Will of GOD*, chapter 3, § 4, p.
49.]

I reply, 1. We are not now disputing whether God with his permitting will wills
us to sin. But for refuting this objection we have guilty parties confessing
that the sinner’s will is not just merely because he wills what God wills. 2.
The son’s volition by which he wishes his pious father to die so that he himself
may obtain the inheritance is impious; but God also wills the father to die so
that the inheritance devolves upon the impious son. Yet in this way God wills
evil, as Arminius says, and *a volition is evil,* he says, *which is directed
toward an object that is not due. And a volition by which someone wills evil is
evil.* For the object is evil and undue for the son. Therefore, if we believe
Arminius, God cannot will it either. Thus either God, because he is above the
law, justly wills what a human being cannot will without sin—which is what we
teach—or this objection is empty.

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 701.]

22. Objection 15. *No wise person wills that at which he himself is incensed
and angry. But GOD is incensed and angry at sin. Therefore GOD by no means
wills sin.*

I reply, 1. The major premise is not universally true even among human beings.
Suppose a judge foreknows that robbers are inclined to theft and does not close
the doors, but allows them to enter and plunder with the plan and purpose that,
once more severely punished, they may be examples to the whole commonwealth
and spectacles of just vengeance to deter others from theft. This judge wills
the theft at which he is justly angry. Next, the major is not true of one in
whom anger is not a passion or weakness, but a manifestation of his most just
will. God permissively, not approvingly, wills that at which he himself is
justly incensed, in order to bear witness to his ho-*

[Marginal summary: In what respect God wills sin and at the same time hates
it.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 514; IA PDF page 571; BSB canvas 566]

*—liness and purity. 2. The argument supposes that GOD’s being angry at
sin means GOD’s not willing, not decreeing, and not willing the existence of
sin—which is *the point at issue*. If this were true, as the opponents foolishly
presume, God could in no way will that at which he is incensed; that is, he
could in no way will what he in no way decrees and in no way wills. But GOD’s
being angry at sin is GOD’s disapproving, hating, and punishing sin. With this
it is entirely consistent that God decreed sin to occur, with another
committing it and himself permitting it, so that GOD’s holy wrath, just
punishment, and the marvelous power of the most just Judge might be revealed.

23. Objection 16. Duval says: *If a human being sins because he is subject to
law, while GOD does not sin because he is not subject to law, as the Calvinists
teach, it follows that the name of sin is denied of GOD, but its rationale and
malice are ascribed to him. If the object is evil, the volition cannot fail to
be evil, whatever the case concerning subjection. It would then follow that
GOD absolutely could lie and hate himself without sin, because when he lies and
hates himself he has no law above himself. Moreover, since God can do through
himself whatever he can do through creatures, if he works sin through angels
and human beings, he can work sin through himself.*

[Marginal reference: Duval, *On Sin*, on 2 Thomas, question 10, article 1.]

I reply, 1. Indeed, if God is not subject to law, it follows that neither the
name, essence, nor formal rationale of sin can be attributed to him, because
sin is transgression of the law. 2. Nor does it follow that God can lie or hate
himself, because he would thus be subject to law and would deny himself and his
holy nature. Nor do our theologians propose this distinction so loosely. They
teach that GOD’s holy nature is the cause why God can neither sin nor cause sin;
but since God is above the law and a human being is subject to law, therefore
the same act that in a human being is culpable and sin is not sin in God, but a
just action. Such is the removal of Job’s goods, done by God and caused by the
Chaldeans and Sabeans. 3. Nor can God do through himself whatever he can do
through creatures. Through creatures he can perform the vital acts of eating,
drinking, and laughing, yet God himself cannot eat, drink, or laugh through
himself. It is false and blasphemous that God sins or works or causes sin
through rational creatures, because he only uses them as they sin, as a
craftsman moves a saw. Nor, because a just judge affixes a murderer to a cross
through an executioner who puts him to death with a plainly malicious and
bloodthirsty zeal for vengeance, does the judge therefore affix the guilty man
to the cross with a bloodthirsty-*

[Marginal summary: How God is not the cause of sin while we are guilty: because
God is above the law, whereas we most certainly are not.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 515; IA PDF page 572; BSB canvas 567]

*—mind.

24. Arminius says that God prevents sin in four ways: *1. By deprivation of
life. 2. By removal of the powers of acting. 3. By opposition of equal powers.
4. By removal of the object.* Bellarmine says that *GOD can be said to act upon
the will in four ways. 1. By physically driving and moving the will
immediately, or also morally—that is, by truly and properly commanding it. In
Bellarmine’s judgment this mode is impious. 2. GOD is said to provoke to evil,
or even to command evil, because he permits people to act badly, but this is by
a trope, as someone sets a dog upon a hare merely by loosening the rope and
reins for the dog. Thus GOD stirred up the Chaldeans and the devil against Job;
there was no need for GOD to incite the devil, who was sufficiently inclined to
evil. 3. GOD stirs up to evil or uses evil wills as instruments, not only by
permitting, but also by abandoning those who are afflicted. Thus he permitted
the evil spirit to deceive Ahab, not only permitting the devil but also taking
the light away from Ahab: “Lead us not,” we pray, “into temptation.” 4. He
presides over the evil wills themselves, rules, governs, twists, and bends them,
working invisibly in them while they are evil by their own fault, so that by
divine providence they are ordered toward one evil rather than another, not
positively but permissively. 1 Chronicles 21: Satan rose up against Israel and
incited David to number the people.*

[Marginal summary: How God prevents sin according to Arminius, and how he wills
it according to Bellarmine.]

I reply, 1. By the unanimous consensus of the doctors, every action of GOD is
either GOD’s positive volition or permission. In the former manner God commands
and orders. Therefore, unless God deprives someone of life, opposes equal
powers, takes the powers away, or removes the object, God through his
omnipotence can prevent evil only in the manner in which human beings and
angels prevent sin, for they can prevent sin in these four ways. 2. According
to Arminius’s learning, Adam, long since dead, is today said to be prevented
from eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; and Alexander the
Great, long since dead, is said to be prevented from devastating Britain, for
each long since yielded to fate. But I believe nothing can be said to be
prevented from acting unless it has an inclination to act, while a nonbeing has
no inclinations. 3. It is marvelous that Arminius acknowledges no prevention
of sin by the withdrawal and denial of divine concurrence. But perhaps in his
conscience Arminius had doubts about the procurement of the act of sin by the
grant of concurrence. And unless my surmise is false, all Arminians would wish
this concurrence removed with Durandus and-*

[Marginal summary: Arminius attributes to God no prevention of sin other than
that which belongs to creatures.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 516; IA PDF page 573; BSB canvas 568]

*—Louis de Dole. 4. To these four modes he adds only two other modes of preventing sin, namely
by legislation and persuasion. Since anyone can resist both GOD’s law and his
persuasion, on Arminius’s account it is impossible for God efficaciously to
prevent sin unless he puts the person to death or removes the object. But in
that way God does not prevent sin in such a manner that the person who does not
sin obeys GOD by not sinning. This is to remove all GOD’s providence concerning
sin and obedience. To Bellarmine I reply: 1. Elsewhere Bellarmine considers it
probable that God immediately and physically moves the will. But the opinion
which is probable elsewhere, when the dispute is with Soto and other Roman
Catholic doctors, is impious when the dispute is with Calvin and Beza. 2. A
physical and a moral motion of the will differ as widely as heaven from earth;
therefore they should not have been comprehended in one mode. 3. All the more
famous Jesuits except some more recent ones, and all the Dominicans, stand for
GOD’s physical predetermination of the will toward its acts, but in Bellarmine’s
judgment all of these are impious in this matter. 4. How God acts upon the will
in the second manner—commanding with an efficacious command and efficaciously
inflicting punishment upon sinners—is, according to the tenets of Bellarmine
and Arminius, *ἀκατανόητον θεῷ*, incomprehensible to God. 5. One who loosens the
rope for a dog flying at a hare does not run along with the running dog—no
Jesuit creature is so swift—but only idly permits the dog to run. Yet God
causatively concurs toward the act of sin. Therefore, in the second manner God
does not merely release the will. 6. But Bellarmine wrongly denies that the
second manner is incitement. Since the saints are inclined by the habit of
grace toward acting well and loving God, that is not a sufficient reason for us
to deny that it is necessary for God to incite Peter to believe and love God,
or to say that God only improperly and tropically stirs the saints, who are
inclined by the habit of grace toward supernatural acts. On that account God
would permit supernatural acts rather than effect them. 7. The third mode
coincides with the second and adds nothing to it. In either case they must have
people say, *Lead us not into temptation*. 8. The fourth mode is ridiculous. 1.
Either the twisting and bending of the will toward this sin rather than that,
as the Jesuit has it from Hugh wrongly understood, is something other than bare
permission and involves a positive action of GOD inclining and driving it, or it-*

[Marginal reference: Bellarmine, *On Grace and Free Choice*, book 4, chapter
10.]

[Marginal summary: According to Bellarmine, because God twists a person toward
this rather than that sin, God is the cause of the graver rather than the less
grave sin.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 517; IA PDF page 574; BSB canvas 569]

*—is only bare permission, or finally it is a direction of already
existing sin toward the manifestation of GOD’s glory. But all these things are
said inconsistently and falsely. If the first, the Jesuit loses his case, for
one who positively moves the will toward one sin rather than another
positively moves it toward sin, because the positive follows from the
comparative. Sometimes God positively drives toward the graver sin—for example,
toward one weighing eight rather than four. And God will be the author of the
graver sin; therefore he will be the author of sin. God, therefore, drives the
Pharisees and Pilate to kill CHRIST rather than merely to curse him. This will
hardly agree with Bellarmine’s tenets. If this bending is only bare permission,
then first the Jesuit deceives us with an empty heap of words. For when
Bellarmine says that God not only permits the will but also twists and bends it
toward evil, he would be saying, *GOD not only permits sin but also permits
sin*, since the Jesuit means his fourth mode to add something to the other
three. 2. In bare permission the will is left indifferent to every object, but
if God *twists and bends the will toward this evil rather than that, the will
is not left indifferent*. Nor can the third option stand, because twisting the
will toward this evil rather than that supposes that the sin does not yet
exist, but says that God drives the will toward the sin. One who only draws an
effect from a bird’s flight toward the south rather than the north, on the
supposition that the flight already exists, is in no way said to direct, bend,
and drive the bird toward the south rather than the north. Finally, concerning
what GOD’s action upon Adam’s will was in the first sin, these four modes do not
contain a syllable.

24. Objection 17. Arminius says: *The one who lays down a law and does not give
the aid necessary for fulfilling it is the cause of the transgression of his
law. But you say that the human will intervened in this abandonment, because a
person is not abandoned unless he wills to be abandoned. I reply: if that is
so, then the person merited abandonment. But I ask whether the person could
have willed not to be abandoned. If you say he could, he did not sin
necessarily but freely. But if you say he could not, the blame redounds upon
GOD no less than before, because GOD is the cause of that volition by which the
person wills to be abandoned, since he did not give the necessary grace by
which the person could have willed not to be abandoned.*

[Marginal reference: *Antiperkins*, p. 704.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part C; printed page 518; IA PDF page 575; BSB canvas 570]

I reply, 1. Arminius accuses God under many headings. Did God give the devils
powers of grace adequate for fulfilling the law of nature? Can the devils, if
only they will, obey God in all things? Can they, through GOD’s grace, avoid
transgressing the law? Yet the law is already imposed upon the devils, because
they now sin, John 8:44; 1 John 3:8. Do people who are now by nature enslaved to
sin and unequal to fulfilling GOD’s law, Romans 3:9; Proverbs 20:9; 1 John 1:8,
possess the grace by which they can perfectly fulfill the law and *continue in
all things written in the Book of the Law*, Galatians 3:13? And insofar as the
regenerate sin against GOD’s law, to that extent God will be the cause of such
transgressions of the law. Yet neither is one who imposes a law upon murderers,
drunkards, fornicators, and idolaters the cause of transgressions of such a law
because he does not give all *subjects the aid necessary for fulfilling the
law*. Who would accuse such a human judge? If God gives the creature the
natural freedom to act or not to act, he is not bound to give further aid for
fulfilling the law. 2. Before Adam’s first fall God took from Adam nothing of
his image or of first-act grace, for Adam possessed the ability not to sin. But
predetermining grace is no part of GOD’s image; it is only its second act. God
denied Adam predetermining grace, not to one formally and expressly denying and
refusing it, but to one materially and interpretatively willing, in effect and
consent, at the same instant of nature, the lack of that predetermining grace.
Therefore, to Adam interpretatively unwilling to have the will to obey, God
denied the will to obey, not by Adam’s formal but by his virtual demerit. But
Arminius fails in proving the consequence by which he infers that God is the
cause of sin. 3. Nor does it follow that Adam merited to be abandoned, or that
God is the cause of the fall, because God did not deny grace in the class of a
potential and material cause. But in the class of an actual cause he denied him
the necessary grace by which, had it been given, he would not have sinned,
which nevertheless GOD was not obligated to give. 4. And since Adam was able
not to sin, he was able to abstain from that virtual volition of abandonment,
and indeed freely. Therefore, Arminius infers, he sinned from no necessity of
the decree. But we say that he sinned with a necessity flowing from the
positing of the decree, which amicably conspired with the character of freedom.
Neither the necessity flowing from the decree nor its flow in any way disturbed
freedom in its connatural mode of acting.
