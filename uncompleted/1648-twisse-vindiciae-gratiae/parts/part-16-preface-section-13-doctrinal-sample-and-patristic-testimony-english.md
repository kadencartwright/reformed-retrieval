SECTION THIRTEEN

PERKINS

Now I offer you a specimen of this doctrine composed from these principles, and a model for imitation. I also publish it so that, as far as in me lies, I may free students of theology who are at a standstill in this difficult locus concerning predestination; so that I may dispel the charges cast against the truth, that is, against the doctrine of Calvin (as they call it); and so that I myself may soothe and soften the minds of certain brethren, exasperated against it more than was right. For I both freely acknowledge and teach universal redemption and grace, insofar as this is possible through the Word. My intention is to pursue peace as it flees, and I would have everyone interpret my action in this way.

Everywhere I bring forward testimonies of the ancients, not because even one evident and perspicuous statement of sacred Scripture concerning any settled locus of doctrine and faith is not more precious and firm than all the testimonies of Doctors and Schoolmen, but because I deem it necessary that there be proof of consensus and agreement in that doctrine which is set forth in the sacred books and has been propagated to all posterity. And indeed I hope to persuade an impartial judge that these doctrines which we transmit in our churches and schools were not lately born in our own home, but were drawn and sought by us from the Fathers themselves as well.

ARMINIUS

Upon these principles you partly superstructure the doctrine of predestination, which is indeed very difficult, because human beings do not fear to add to Scripture whatever has seemed right to them, and are accustomed to attribute as much as possible to their own conceptions, which they call natural notions. By all means, everyone must bring light to the truth as far as he is able; calumnies and charges with which the truth has been assailed and attacked must be dispelled; and minds exasperated against it must not merely be soothed and softened, but also brought back into reconciliation with it.

2. Nor can it be turned into a fault against you that you bring forward the opinions of ancient theologians, especially when the caution you lay down is observed; otherwise we would indeed enter upon an endless course. For the Fathers themselves are liable to differing interpretations, and indeed so much more than the divine and θεοπνεύστους—“God-inspired”—writers, inasmuch as they were endowed with a smaller and less perspicuous knowledge of the truth and were able to express the thoughts of their minds less accurately and suitably. When I consider this, I do not know whether those who in this age have thought that they should employ the opinions of the Fathers in the business of religion for the sake of authority have served the Church’s interests. But the die is cast; we must advance farther wherever the present fortunes of the Church will.

3. Furthermore, when you say that you bring forward the testimonies of certain Doctors and Schoolmen for the sake of demonstrating consensus in this part of doctrine, I do not see what this amounts to. For I am thoroughly persuaded that nothing more apt could be devised for confusing that entire doctrine of predestination and the grace of God and burying it in darkness than for someone to endeavor to produce all the opinions of the Fathers and Schoolmen concerning it and set them against one another.

4. I would not, however, have you immediately pronounce that assessor or judge unjust who dares to assert that the doctrines you deliver in this book are found neither in the Scriptures nor in the Fathers. For if you prove this after the reasons advanced by that assessor, arbiter, or judge, you will find him resisting not with an unjust and prejudiced mind,

[Praefatio; printed p. 33; BSB canvas 41]

but prepared, as is fitting, to yield with equanimity to the truth demonstrated.

5. Nor will you easily persuade me that the doctrines you treat here are proposed and delivered in precisely the same manner and sense in all the Reformed churches; do not suppose that you can burden one who disagrees with you by the prejudgment of those churches.

RESPONSE 1

Does it seem very difficult to you on this account alone? I do not believe so. But does it seem so chiefly on this account? I do not think that either. Certainly, when the Apostle restrains the objector in Romans 9 in this manner, “O man, who are you who answers back to God? Will the thing formed say to its maker, ‘Why have you made me thus?’”; and likewise, when in Romans 11 he exclaims, “How inscrutable are his judgments and unsearchable his ways?”, he indicates the depth of this mystery and professes not so much difficulty as impossibility in searching it out. He also signifies that its origin lies elsewhere than in human preconceptions. Moreover, I do not understand at all how it should appear very difficult on this account. Tell me, for whom would it be very difficult? For those who set these common notions before themselves? But they undoubtedly think otherwise and trust that from these notions they can open an easier road for themselves to treating and explaining these mysteries. As for others who do not admit these notions as principles and presume that they can easily refute them, I cannot divine how they could be obstructed by any difficulties arising from this source. Even if it were so, I do not grasp why Arminius should plead this as a reason in the present case. For what? Does he resist these principles? Of the nine theses proposed by Perkins, the adversary himself admits every one except the eighth; and in that same thesis we showed that by far the greater part even of the eighth ought to be admitted by Arminius himself and is in fact admitted. Meanwhile I am confident that I will construct no difficulty for Arminius on this account. I ask him to concede nothing which we have not demonstrated either by an express testimony of Scripture, by conclusions drawn from Scripture itself, or by a sound consequence deduced from the best-known principles. I keep this statement of Augustine before my eyes: “If an argument is given against the authority of the divine Scriptures, however acute it may be, it deceives by an appearance of truth, for it cannot be true. Again, if the authority of the sacred Scriptures is set as though against the most manifest and certain reasoning, the one who does this does not understand. He sets against the truth not the meaning of those Scriptures, which he could not penetrate, but rather his own meaning; and he opposes not what he finds in them, but what he finds in himself as though it were in them.” Finally, the prudent reader will easily notice while reading which of us are accustomed to put more trust in the judgments of natural human reason.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *To Marcellinus*, epistle 7.]

2. If the caution proposed by Perkins pleased you, what need was there—as though that caution had been excluded—to detain your reader with such utterly unnecessary interpretations? For who among either the Roman Catholics or the Evangelicals does not acknowledge that the Fathers are liable to differing interpretations? Yet none of them, so far as I know, are accustomed on this account to shrink from citing the Fathers for their own opinion. But are not the Scriptures themselves also open to differing interpretations? You admit that they are, but you say that they are not equally open. Yet what sort of claim is this? 1. First, the matter can be doubted. Indeed, see whether it is not manifestly false. For who has ever found as many interpretations of any statement in Augustine or any other ecclesiastical Doctor as anyone who reads the commentaries will easily discover in almost any chapter of the Bible? The stated reasons have no weight. For a perspicuous knowledge of the truth does not block the road to differing interpretations; rather, a perspicuous explanation of that truth does. Nor does the inability to express the thoughts of one’s mind accurately and suitably close off anything from varying interpretations; the actual expression itself does. In the Apostles and Prophets that expression was indeed such as God wished it to be, not such as we imagine it ought not to have been. Finally, the very depth of the matters, which human beings cannot easily grasp, can provide an occasion for varying interpretations, and do so more in those who first state simply the things that belong to faith than in those who labor to explain that profound material and make it familiar to us by comparing various passages and observing various circumstances. 2. But let your claim stand. What then follows? This, to be sure: that they are not to be cited on an equal footing with the θεοπνεύστους—“God-inspired”—writers. Perkins could easily grant this and indeed has granted it; it does not therefore follow that they are to be passed over altogether.

Indeed, what fair assessor of affairs does not gratefully embrace the writings of the Fathers and revere the solicitous care and providence of the heavenly Father, as though keeping watch for the safety of his Church? He first raised up such great lights of his Church, so that they might preserve intact the Gospel truth assailed and attacked by various enemies of heretical depravity and transmit it uncorrupted to posterity. Thereafter he propagated their writings down to our own times with no unhappy success, given the Church’s condition today and the almost identical crop of heretics which, cut down long ago by pious labors, now gathers strength afresh and sprouts again. Among those writings perhaps preeminent are the didactic works of Cyril of Alexandria on the person of Christ and Augustine’s works on Christian grace—the former against Nestorius, the latter against Pelagius. How appropriately and timely, therefore, are the Fathers cited, especially in controversies by which they were exercised long ago just as we are today?

3. If “for the sake of authority” is said with respect to creating belief in some article, I think we agree among ourselves that those who judge that the opinions of the Fathers should be employed for this purpose have by no means served the Church’s interests, but have prostituted her and had no regard for her safety. Perkins undoubtedly was not among their number. This opinion of Luther on the matter is indeed of the greatest weight among us all: “Faith is owed to the canonical books alone; judgment is owed to all others.” But if it is said with respect to confirming a faith already created from God’s Word, I do not see why you censure this policy. For which of our theologians has ever recoiled from it? The Roman Catholics cry out that we despise the authority of the Fathers; Arminius complains that we have not sufficiently neglected and abandoned it. We hope, however, that the more our road is a middle one, the safer it also is, and that we have both walked it thus far and by God’s grace will walk it to the end. Thus we neither rely upon the authority of human beings, however holy and however learned, for creating the faith of religion, nor neglect or fail to value highly the cultivation of communion in that same faith with the ancient Fathers and also, insofar as it will be lawful, with the primitive and succeeding Church.

As for me, I lay down this rule: the judgment of no learned ecclesiastical writer in the business of religion is to be despised. Indeed, I would not willingly depart even from the opinion of Bellarmine or any Roman pontiff if he had written something, unless they were first found to have departed in some respect from truth itself and from either common or Christian reason. So far am I from wishing to retaliate in kind against either Bellarmine or Maldonado, both Jesuits, who sometimes—with characteristic modesty—do not blush to profess that they dislike some opinion even on the ground that they have discovered it once pleased Calvin.

But it is more certain than certainty itself that the Pelagian heresy was long ago rejected by the Fathers—among whom Augustine is preeminent above the rest in this business—conquered with remarkable success, and condemned by the Church. See whether this stung Arminius and whether for this reason he preferred the testimonies of the Fathers to be buried in silence rather than produced in public and employed. Yet, to confess the fact candidly, I am not going to be especially troublesome to our adversary on this score, for Arminius is reputed to be a powerful disputant, and I am pleased to contend with him in this very arena.

3. Perkins professes that he brings forward testimonies of the ancients, but not of them all; perhaps he will ordinarily be content with two or three witnesses. But if he produces the testimonies of more, he will undoubtedly produce testimonies not of those who disagree, but of those who agree with one another. Thus your fear that the truth will be confused or buried is altogether empty, and you will have no just cause for complaint, since it will also remain open to you to observe, from such a great variety of opinions, those which

[Praefatio; printed p. 34; BSB canvas 42]

favor your side and to transfer them to your own use—unless perhaps you would prefer us to have learned absolutely nothing of what was written in antiquity about the Pelagian heresy. Nor do we labor greatly to be superior on the strength of human authority; provided Arminius prevails by Scripture and reason, he will carry the day. Meanwhile, however, the weight of the universal Church rejecting the Pelagian heresies will weigh upon the man. For when he notices at every turn his opinions pierced by Augustine and his arguments dispelled by the same author, he will not easily be able either to feel no pain or entirely to conceal the pain he does feel. Yet this is wholly what we seek: unless we prevail by Scripture and reason—but reason illuminated by faith—we have no desire at all that our cause should be regarded as superior.

But what is that so very manifold opinion of the ancient Fathers concerning these matters? For this is what I wish to know. It is reported that there were only two opinions of the Fathers concerning predestination: one was that of the ancient Fathers before Augustine; the other was Augustine’s own and that of those who either followed him or defended the same opinion with him. Only two, I say, either because there were only two altogether or at least because only two were celebrated; and what need is there to pursue others? Thus, concerning happiness, Aristotle professes that he will enumerate only those opinions which were maximè ἐπιπολάζουσαι—“most prevalent.” But perhaps the opinions of the Schoolmen are more numerous. Yet Perkins says that everywhere he brings forward testimonies of the ancients; he does not say of the Schoolmen. In your usual manner, you mix them together so that you may have somewhere to range. But even among the opinions of the Schoolmen, those which enjoy some celebrity are not numerous. Indeed, the Jesuits and Dominicans who engage one another most fiercely on opposite fronts today are, I say, nearly agreed concerning predestination. They contend chiefly over grace, yet even concerning grace there are only two celebrated opinions: one which the Dominicans defend, and the other for which the Jesuits fight as for their altars and hearths, lest they seem to favor us, whom they call heretics. Perhaps more discrepancies are found in the details and minutiae of individual controversies, as commonly happens in almost every controversy framed among all parties; but those are not to be reckoned disagreements concerning the substance of the controversy.

[Marginalia: Aristotle, *Ethics*, book 1.]

4. There is a respect in which one may rightly pronounce this assessor unjust, and there is a respect in which one may not pronounce him so at all, or may do so only with injury—namely, according as he conducts himself either in skillfully interpreting the Scriptures and Fathers or in shamefully evading them by a sophistical invention. Let the prudent reader judge from what follows which of these you have habitually done. Let him profess himself ready, with a mind by no means prejudiced, to yield with equanimity, as is fitting, to the truth demonstrated, if he wishes; that matters less. May God grant that he perform it rather than profess it.

5. Nor does Perkins say that these doctrines are proposed in the same manner and sense in all the Reformed churches. He says only that the doctrines he proposes are delivered in our churches and schools. We do not so much fail to know as grieve that the Lutheran churches dissent from the rest of the Reformed on these points. As for the others which they call Calvinian, I acknowledge that even among them there are theologians who do not think alike concerning the object of predestination—whether it is a mass not yet created, only created, or also corrupt—but I have not yet learned that churches dissent from churches on this point. Indeed, no schism has ever been made on this account thus far, and I am confident that none ever will be.

Such was the Preface. The treatise follows, which we divide into three books. The first explains and confirms the orthodox opinion. The second dispels the charges. The third refutes the opposing opinion. Again, the first book can be divided into three parts, of which the first is on predestination, the second on election, and the third on reprobation. Each of these is completed in definite sections, which we have nevertheless fitted not so much to Perkins’s book as to Arminius’s examination, lest the discussion in each should grow beyond what is proper. We have therefore cut Perkins’s sections into smaller parts.
