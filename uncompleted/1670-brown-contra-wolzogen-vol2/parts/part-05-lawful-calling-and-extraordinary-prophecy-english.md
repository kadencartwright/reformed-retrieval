Although these points could be sufficient to confirm this assertion, yet
because Velthuysen saw fit to undertake the rejection and refutation of this
truth in his book, I will add several further arguments before I come to
examine what he has to say.

I. If our assertion were not true, many absurdities would follow, namely:

1. That institution of the ministry would be rendered entirely empty, for then
people not lawfully invested with that office could nevertheless discharge it
by right and perform the works pertaining to it. For announcing the gospel is
the chief work of this office, 1 Corinthians 1:17. Pareus says on the passage,
“The office of preaching is prior, more important, more difficult, and more
necessary.” Therefore, if anyone not lawfully called were permitted to seize
the office of preaching for himself, that very office instituted by Christ
would be entirely abolished and rendered empty. The ministry would be a mere
invention and a name without a reality.

2. Gifts and office would in this way be confounded, and anyone furnished with
gifts could discharge the office itself. This is regarded as absurd in every
society and every constituted commonwealth. For not everyone furnished with
gifts can by right exercise or discharge a magistracy or any public office.
May anyone who judges himself qualified and sufficiently equipped assume and
discharge the office of admiral, general, or commander?

3. Gifts and calling would in this way be confounded, for no calling beyond
gifts would be needed to discharge any public office.

[Volume II; printed p. 75; ONB canvas 83]

4. Then anyone furnished with gifts would be permitted to discharge this
office, even women, contrary to 1 Timothy 2:12 and 1 Corinthians 14:34.

5. Indeed, everyone who says and judges that he possesses sufficient gifts
will be permitted to perform these things. For once calling is removed, there
will be no one to examine the gifts.

6. In this way the widest possible door will be opened to introduce, spread,
and publish every damnable error. For any heretic will seize this opportunity,
spit out his poison, and corrupt the whole church. How horrible this would be!

7. The church of Christ would then be a monster, not an organic body. For the
whole would be an eye, ear, or head, since those who discharge the ministry are
regarded as principal members of Christ’s organic body—as eyes or the like.
Paul demonstrates in 1 Corinthians 12:14–16 and so forth that this would be
utterly absurd.

8. In this way every order instituted by God, the author of order in his
church, would be abolished. For there is the greatest confusion in a
commonwealth where anyone is permitted to discharge any public office, and all
order is abolished. But our God is not a God of confusion, 1 Corinthians
14:33, and he commands that all things be done decently and in order, 1
Corinthians 14, the last verse. To confound everything is Satan’s sole work and
endeavor. How wicked, therefore, it will be to promote this diabolical work and
endeavor!

9. It would follow that this ministerial office was not difficult and
burdensome if anyone were permitted to discharge it and seize it for himself,
contrary to 2 Corinthians 2:16. Paul himself asks, “Who is sufficient for these
things?” See also Job 33:23. Those who judge the matter rightly will regard
this office as a burden formidable even to angelic shoulders. If this office
were not indeed weighty, grave, laborious, and burdensome, God would never
require so many qualifications in those lawfully called to discharge it as
Scripture testifies are required in 1 Timothy 3:1, 2 and so forth; Titus 1:5–7;
and very many other passages of Scripture.

[Volume II; printed p. 76; ONB canvas 84]

10. It would then be commanded in vain that each person adorn the role he has
obtained and remain in the calling in which he was called, contrary to 1
Corinthians 7:20. Paul’s command in 1 Thessalonians 4:10, 11 will also be empty:
“We beseech you—to strive eagerly to be quiet and attend to your own affairs.”

11. In this way the pastor would be confounded with the flock, for everyone
would be a pastor, contrary to Ephesians 4:11.

12. It would follow that double honor is owed to them—that is, every dutiful
service and support. See 1 Timothy 5:17, 18; Matthew 10:10; Galatians 6:6; and 1
Corinthians 9:13, 14.

II. The contrary assertion cannot be proved and rests on no true foundation,
as will appear from the solution of everything Velthuysen adduces. But since,
as we suppose, it cannot be proved that anyone not yet lawfully called may
discharge this office, doing so will be unlawful, because whatever is not from
faith is sin, Hebrews 11:6. The adversaries have so far not demonstrated by any
command or lawful example that it can lawfully be done. For the commands they
adduce enjoin a private and limited exercise of gifts, as also do the promises
the adversaries mention, such as Joel 2:28; Isaiah 54:13; Jeremiah 31:33, 34;
and John 6:45. The passages Hebrews 5:11, 12; 1 Peter 4:10, 11 and 2:9; Hebrews
3:13 and 10:24; 1 Corinthians 12:7; and John 4:28 prove nothing else. The
examples either do not speak about this spiritual office, such as Numbers
11:25 and so forth; 2 Chronicles 17:7–9; 19:6–10; and 29:4, 5; Luke 2:42, 46;
and Acts 7; or speak only about an extraordinary action, such as 1 Samuel 10:5,
6 and 19:20, 21. Or they speak about people who were extraordinarily called,
such as Christ, Matthew 29:35; Luke 4:16, 17; or about those called by Christ,
such as the apostles, Matthew 10:1, 5–7, whose commission was afterward made
more extensive in Matthew 28:19, and the seventy disciples, Luke 10:1 and so
forth. Or they speak about those appointed to an extraordinary office, such as
Philip, Acts 8 and 21:8, and others, Acts 8:4. So too Acts 13:

[Volume II; printed p. 77; ONB canvas 85]

14, 15; Acts 18:24, 25, compared with 1 Corinthians 3:5, 6, 22; 1:1, 12; 4:6;
16:12; and 14:9 compared with verse 6. And finally, those mentioned in 1
Corinthians 14:1–31.

III. The contrary assertion would overthrow the order Christ instituted for
the celebration of instituted worship. For instituted divine worship would be
celebrated by people other than those to whom Christ entrusted it. Christ
commanded this part of worship to be administered by those lawfully called and
ordained, not by others. This is clear both from the express commands in Titus
1:5, 7–9; 1 Timothy 5:22; and Acts 13:3, and from the examples already
mentioned, which are more numerous than can be surveyed here.

IV. No one not lawfully called was permitted even to discharge the office of a
deacon, Acts 6:2, 5, 6. Much less, therefore, will anyone not lawfully called be
permitted to arrogate the pastoral office to himself.

V. The Photinians and Schwenckfeldians formerly fought for this license, just
as the Arminians, Socinians, Quakers, and Anabaptists still fight for it, so
that they may publish their errors and spread their poison more freely and
easily and infect others with it. This opinion, therefore, ought deservedly to
be suspected.

VI. If our assertion were false, Paul would have said in vain in 2 Timothy
2:2, “The things you have heard from me among many witnesses, entrust these to
faithful men who will be competent to teach others also.” The reason is that it
is clearly apparent from this, first, that Christ’s church requires people who
teach others; next, that those who teach others be qualified and faithful; and
finally, that they have this office entrusted and assigned to them by others
possessing that authority. Paul says *parathou*, which signifies something
other than simply presenting a matter—namely, entrusting something to
another’s care, custody, and faithfulness, as in Luke 12:48; 1 Timothy 1:18; and
1 Peter 4:19. For Timothy was bound to announce the gospel not only to faithful
men and those equipped to teach others, but also to others.

VII. Among other arguments, Maccovius proves this in his *Commonplaces*, page
847. I. “All things in the church ought to be done decently and in order, 1
Corinthians 14:40.” He proves the consequence as follows: “For if mission and
calling will not be necessary for those who ought to discharge the office of
teaching in the church, then anyone can seize the office of teaching for
himself. How, then, can confusion be avoided there?” II. “The Holy Spirit does
not command a person to thrust himself into the episcopate, but to seek it, 1
Timothy 3:1, 2. It is plain”—he says—“both that he does not will just anyone to
be admitted, nor admitted in just any way, but that the person first be tested:
the former from 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1:7–9, and the latter from 1 Timothy 3.
Hence it now necessarily follows that no one can thrust himself into teaching
without mission and calling.” He proposes a twofold objection in these words:
“Someone says, first, that the assertion from 1 Timothy 3:1 that the Holy
Spirit commands this to be sought is not found there, but only that it is good
to desire the episcopate. Second, as for the statement in the same chapter that
someone cannot be admitted in just any way, but must be tested: that pertains
not to bishops but to deacons.” He replies: “To the first: plainly, either to
desire and to seek are identical in meaning here, or the latter certainly
follows from the former. For if someone could spontaneously seize the
episcopate he desires, then there would certainly be no need to desire or seek
it, since he could thrust himself into the office whenever he wished. To the
second: I reply that the particle *also* plainly shows that this very testing
also pertains to those who wish to be bishops.”

VIII. Gerhard defends this same assertion in *On the Ecclesiastical Ministry*,
section 54 and following. Among other arguments, he confirms it from Jeremiah
27:9, 14, 15, drawing the following inference from the passage: “1. Whoever we
are forbidden to hear is not a true minister of God or teacher of the church.
But we are forbidden to hear the person who has not been sent by God.
Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is proved: Whoever has no hearers is
not a true teacher, by virtue of the mutual relation.

[Volume II; printed p. 79; ONB canvas 87]

But the person whom we are forbidden to hear by divine right and command has
no hearers. Therefore the person whom we are forbidden to hear is not a true
teacher.” To this he refers certain New Testament passages in which the
characteristic of pseudoprophets and false teachers is established: that they
come in their own name, neither sent nor called by God. Such passages are
Matthew 7:15; 24:24; John 5:43; 10:8; Acts 20:30; and 2 John 10. He adduces
another argument from John 3:27: “A man can receive nothing unless it has been
given to him from heaven.” He says: “The Baptist plainly means this: My calling
is entirely distinct from Christ’s calling. I am the bridegroom’s friend, but
he is the bridegroom. I was sent before him to prepare his way, but he is the
angel of the covenant. It would therefore be sacrilege if I wished to arrogate
the Messiah’s office to myself without a calling. I am accordingly content
with and rejoice in my calling, which I received from God, because I am the
bridegroom’s friend and see him increase, and so forth, since a man cannot
receive anything unless it has been given to him from heaven, that is, from
God, James 1:17. From this a general rule is rightly established: No one ought
to undertake the duties of any office or arrogate any honor to himself unless
a divine calling and mission attends it, and so forth.” Furthermore, on page
91 in my copy he proves the same point from the fact that no one in either the
Old or New Testament rightly administered the public office of teaching in the
church unless divinely called to it. He confirms this with examples:

1. The patriarchs, Genesis 14:18; 20:7; 12:7; 13:18; and 21:31.

2. The Levitical priests, Exodus 28:1, and the other Levites, Numbers 1:49.

3. The prophets, Isaiah 6:8, 9; Jeremiah 1:4, 5; Hosea 1:1; Luke 1:70; Hebrews
1:1.

4. John the Baptist, John 1:6; Luke 3:2.

5. Christ himself, Matthew 10:40; 15:24; John 3:17; 5:36; Isaiah 48:16; 61:1,
2; Luke 4:18; Hebrews 5.

[Volume II; printed p. 80; ONB canvas 88]

6. The apostles, Matthew 10:1, 5; John 20:21; Matthew 28:19; Mark 16:15; Romans
1:1; 1 Corinthians 1:1; 2 Corinthians 1:1; Galatians 1:1; 1 Peter 1:1.

7. Those who succeeded the apostles in the office of teaching, Acts 14:23; 1
Timothy 4:14; 2 Timothy 1:6; 1 Corinthians 4:17; 1 Thessalonians 3:2.

8. Those to whom this office was entrusted by the apostles’ successors, 1
Timothy 5:22; 2 Timothy 2:2; Titus 1:5.

He then says that “Christ, placed at the heavenly Father’s right hand, not only
formerly gave his church apostles, prophets, and evangelists, but even today
continues to give it pastors and teachers, Ephesians 4:11—not any longer
immediately, but through a lawful mediate calling, which ought to be recognized
as divine no less than the immediate calling.” He says that “this will be
proved afterward.” In the following section he vindicates this argument from
the Socinians’ exceptions. Finally, in section 63 he adduces three reasons
drawn from Scripture. The first is from the weight of the causes: “Whatever
rests upon the gravest causes concerning God’s honor, the church’s salvation,
and tranquility of conscience cannot and ought not be omitted in the church.
But the calling of ministers rests upon causes of this kind. Therefore, and so
forth. The assumption is proved because the causes on account of which public
teachers in the church ought to possess a lawful calling are very weighty.
They are such that some concern God, some the hearers, and some the ministers
of the word themselves. The cause relating to God is that his institution,
will, and ordinance be satisfied, Romans 10:15; Hebrews 5:4. He does not will
to deal with us immediately or to send teachers immediately, but to set
teachers over his church through lawful and ordinary calling. Whoever,
therefore, despises it resists the divine ordinance. The cause relating to the
hearers is that they may be assured of the lawful calling of the minis-

[Volume II; printed p. 81; ONB canvas 89]

ters: that God wills to employ these very lawfully called persons in the work
of converting and saving people; likewise, that in the ministry conducted in
his name he wills to be present with them by his grace and the efficacy of the
Holy Spirit, to furnish them with necessary gifts, to bless their labors, and
to protect them against adversaries, and so forth. This consideration arouses
reverence and obedience toward the ministry in the hearers’ minds. It is not
only useful but also necessary for the hearers to know what the future
minister’s doctrine is and from whom he learned it. Examination, ordination,
and testimonials are consequently also necessary. The cause relating to the
minister himself is that from his calling he may be certain that God wills to
use his labor in the ministry, and from this conceive a firm confidence that
God wills to be effectual through his ministry for the conversion and
salvation of the hearers. Without this confidence he will be able neither to
pray for the ministry’s success and the hearers’ salvation nor to raise
himself up by firm consolation amid such great hatreds and dangers of the
world. Hence calling and protection in the ministry are joined in Isaiah
49:1, 2; Jeremiah 1:7, 8; Isaiah 51:16; 59:21; 2 Corinthians 3:2, 5; and 2:12.
By all means see Chemnitz in the commonplace *On the Church*, chapter 4,
member 1, page 316, where he reviews at greater length the reasons why it is so
important for a minister to possess a lawful calling.”

The second reason is drawn from comparisons. He says: “No ambassador
undertakes the duties of that office without a calling and particular
commission. But the ministers of the church are God’s ambassadors, 2
Corinthians 5:20. Therefore no minister of the church should undertake the
duties of that office without a calling. No one claims for himself the
government of a household and the administration of its economy, nor does the
family receive and hear anyone except the person whom the *oikodespotēs*, or
householder himself, has placed over his house and commended to his servants
to be heard. But the church is God’s house, 1 Timothy 3:15; Hebrews 3:6. The
minis-

[Volume II; printed p. 82; ONB canvas 90]

ters of the church are stewards in this house, 1 Corinthians 4:1. Therefore
they should not undertake the administration of this house, nor should
Christ’s family hear them, unless through calling they have been placed by God
himself over this economy. No one is to be regarded as a soldier unless he has
been enlisted by the commander among the number of the soldiers and bound by
the military oath. But ministers of the church are called soldiers in a
particular way, because beyond the rest of the godly—who themselves fight under
Christ’s standards and are called soldiers in a general way—they fight against
Satan and his squadrons by preaching the word, most earnest prayers, refutation
of heresies, and so forth, 2 Timothy 2:3, 4. Therefore ministers of the church
should not enter that particular military service unless they are enrolled in
it by Christ, the heavenly commander. No one ought to administer the office of
a magistrate without a calling. For whoever invades it on his own initiative
becomes guilty of tyranny or certainly of sedition. Therefore no one ought to
claim the ecclesiastical ministry for himself without a calling.”

This is his second reason. The third and final one, drawn from the harmful
consequences, follows. He says: “Whoever arrogates to himself the public office
of teaching in the church without a calling invades another’s possession. He
is not God’s ambassador and minister but a thief and robber, since he does not
enter through the door. He is not the church’s minister, since he was not
called by it. He disturbs the order instituted by God and introduces
*akatastasian*, disorder, of which God is not the author, 1 Corinthians 14:33.
He opposes the example of Christ, the prophets, and the apostles, all of whom
preached only when sent and called. He causes the most august dignity of this
office to become contemptible, deprives himself of necessary consolation, and
usually brings unhappy outcomes upon himself, and so forth. Sacred Scripture
and ecclesiastical histories testify that all these things, individually and
together, customarily arise from a defective calling.” Thus far he.

IX. The words of the most distinguished Apollonius in *Consideration of
Certain Controversies*, chapter 5, pages 95–97, should be noted. He says: “We
reject the antithesis of those who maintain that this is a perpetual and
ordinary institution in the church: that by divine right a private Christian
endowed with gifts may publicly preach God’s word with all authority in an
ecclesiastical assembly, either ordinarily or as occasion offers, when he is
judged qualified by those to whom this judgment belongs, even though he holds
no other ecclesiastical office. But we are certain that even under the Old
Testament not everyone in the Israelite church was admitted to speak publicly
in the synagogues. Rather, this was the ordinary office of those called
*scribes* and *lawyers*. The Levites were also distributed among many places
for this reason, whence they are said to have sat upon Moses’ seat. If anyone
was endowed with extraordinary gifts of prophecy, as were the prophets in
Israel, he was permitted and commanded by divine right to preach publicly in
God’s name. When the high priest forbade the prophet Amos to prophesy at
Bethel, Amos did not claim for the Israelites this liberty to preach God’s
word publicly in God’s name in sacred assemblies. Instead, he alleged his own
extraordinary mission, by which God had sent him to preach this word, Amos
7:14, 15. So perhaps it was also permitted to the sons of the prophets, who
were prepared, trained, and set apart for the ecclesiastical ministry, 1
Samuel 19:20. In the synagogues exhortations were added after the reading of
Moses and the prophets. If circumstances ever required it and those presiding
over the synagogues saw fit, particularly in corrupt times, certain people
were indeed permitted to teach and exhort outside the regular order. Yet these
were always people who possessed attestation of their gifts and whose
reputation for an extraordinary or ordi-

[Volume II; printed p. 84; ONB canvas 92]

nary mission had been made widely known through the doctrine they preached
and the deeds they performed. Thus Christ the Nazarene was permitted to read
and explain the writings of the prophets in the synagogue, Luke 4:16, since
his majesty and miracles obtained him a hearing everywhere, as Beza notes
here. By this right he also taught in the temple itself and everywhere else.
For this reason the ordinary teachers also asked him by what authority he did
this, Matthew 21:23. So too we see that Paul and Barnabas were given the
opportunity to speak and exhort publicly in the synagogue at Antioch, Acts
13:15, since their reputation was already known to the Antiochenes. For before
this time they had already preached God’s word there to many throughout an
entire year and brought many to faith in Christ. Agabus had joined them and,
by an extraordinary gift of prophecy, foretold to the Antiochenes the
impending famine. Many other prophets and teachers were also there who had
preached God’s word both to Jews and Greeks. See Acts 11:19–21, 25–28; 13:1, 2
and so forth. But in New Testament practice no one except prophets by gift and
office, whether extraordinary or ordinary, was permitted publicly to set forth
God’s word in Christ’s name in the assembly of believers, as is clear from 1
Corinthians 14:29–31 and so forth. The apostle”—Beza says on this passage—“does
not deal with just anyone from the assembly, but with prophets lawfully called
to instruct God’s church. Therefore, those who infer from this that anyone
from the assembly may speak in the church, and who censure the practice of
having a sermon by only one person, are not to be heard.”

X. More arguments could be adduced, but it is not necessary, since our
assertion is more than sufficiently confirmed from what has been said. As for
the objections, they are indeed numerous, but they can easily be dissolved by
anyone who recalls what must be concluded and knows well enough to distinguish
between extraordinary

[Volume II; printed p. 85; ONB canvas 93]

and ordinary people, between extraordinary and ordinary times, and between
private duties imposed on private persons and public duties enjoined with a
public office. Nevertheless, I will propose and resolve the one objection that
appears to be principal.

This principal objection is taken from 1 Corinthians 14:31: “For you can all
prophesy one by one, so that all may learn and all may receive consolation.” We
can answer it as follows.

The word *all* ought not always be taken in such a way that no one can be
excluded. This is sufficiently well known from the disputes against the
Arminians. Rather, it ought to be restricted according to the subject matter
being treated where the word is used. Here, therefore, it must be restricted
to the prophets about whom Paul speaks. This is abundantly confirmed from what
precedes and follows; see verses 29 and 32. That not everyone was a prophet is
clear from chapter 12:29, where the question has the force of a negation: “Are
all prophets?”—as if he said, “Not everyone is a prophet.” But a further
investigation is needed here so that we may know who these prophets were, for
not everyone agrees, even among those who oppose the liberty of prophesying
desired by the Socinians, Arminians, and Independents. Some say that these
prophets were private members of the church furnished with the gift of
interpreting Scripture for the church’s edification, instruction, and
consolation. That is, in addition to the ministers, they were leading or
especially approved men among the brethren, according to the gifts conferred
upon them. Ames says this in *Cases of Conscience*, book 4, chapter 26,
question 15, and Hoornbeeck in *Apology for the Christian Church, Which Does
Not Apostatize*, page 115 and following. But even these authors will deny the
adversaries’ inference, because it does not follow that, because certain
brethren may speak about divine things in private or quasi-private exercises,
they may therefore announce the gospel publicly and authoritatively. Ames
himself says in the same place that in these exercises there were people

[Volume II; printed p. 86; ONB canvas 94]

who modestly and in order proposed their questions so that they might be
taught by the more skilled, verse 35. But who would say it follows from this
that they may preach the gospel publicly and authoritatively and arrogate the
pastoral office to themselves? And to say this in passing: Why would women not
also be permitted modestly and in order to propose their questions in these
exercises so that they might learn in this way? But because women were
expressly forbidden to speak in these exercises, it is apparent that those
exercises were of another nature. Similarly, the most distinguished
Hoornbeeck, in the place cited above, page 120, suggests that those exercises
were a kind of catechizing and that in the same chapter, verse 19, the apostle
himself calls it catechism: *hina kai allous katēchēsō*, “so that I may
catechize others also.” Thus on page 122 he says that those prophetic exercises
were like catechetical exercises, but of the highest order. The adversaries’
conclusion can by no means be inferred from this, because the following
consequence is invalid: Private persons may speak in catechetical exercises;
therefore they may preach God’s word publicly. But to note this too in
passing: If modern catechetical exercises resemble those prophetic exercises,
though of a lower order, I do not see how women or young maidens may speak in
them, since this was expressly forbidden in the others. But this only in
passing.

There is also another opinion, namely that of those who say these prophets
were ordinary ministers of the church. But the adversaries’ conclusion follows
even less from this opinion, as is plain to everyone.

There is a third opinion, namely that of those who assert that these prophets
were extraordinary ministers of the church immediately inspired by God’s
Spirit. No small number of theologians and commentators defend it: the
*Synopsis of Purer Theology*, disputation 42, thesis 22; Calvin, *Institutes*,
book 4, chapter 3, section 4; Diodati on the passage; Dickson on the passage;
Martyr, *Commonplaces*, class 4, chapter 1; Aretius, *Theological Problems*,
commonplace 62; Bucanus, commonplace *On the Ministry*, question 16 and its
margin; Ravanel in the *Bibliotheca* under the word *prophet*; and no small
number of others, including

[Volume II; printed p. 87; ONB canvas 95]

certain fathers. Our own Rutherford and Gillespie and all the others who argue
against the Independents about this liberty of prophesying defend this same
opinion. We too will follow it as the more probable opinion, relying on these
reasons that our theologians propose and vindicate:

1. The apostle openly distinguishes these prophets from pastors and teachers
in 1 Corinthians 12:28, 29 and Ephesians 4:11, 12. They were therefore other
and extraordinary ministers, distinct from the former.

2. They are not only distinguished from pastors and teachers but are also
preferred to them and even to evangelists, who were themselves extraordinary,
Ephesians 4:11, and more distinctly in 1 Corinthians 12:28: “And God has
appointed some in the church, first apostles, next prophets, third teachers,
and so forth.” From this some conclude that the apostles held the first and
principal place, and by the same argument it can be proved that the second
belonged to the prophets. In any case, they are always placed before ordinary
ministers, as can also be seen in Acts 13:1.

3. The character of some of them is apparent from Acts 11:27, 28 and 21:10,
11: they were inspired by extraordinary revelations.

4. It is clear from verse 26 that those mentioned here were also
extraordinarily inspired: “Whenever you assemble, each of you has a psalm, has
a doctrine, has a tongue, has a revelation, has an interpretation”; and verse
30, “But if anything is revealed to another,” and so forth. Pareus, on the
passage, understands by *revelation* the revelation of God’s mysteries or of
future things, and by *interpretation*, the interpretation both of languages
and of doctrine and Scripture. On verse 30 he says that all these things are
understood of those inspired by the gift of prophecy. Beza says that this
revelation was an entirely extraordinary gift.

5. Just as the apostles were prophets and comprehended within themselves all

[Volume II; printed p. 88 (misprinted "98"); ONB canvas 96]

the lower orders, so that they could perform the same things as they, so too
the prophets, inferior only to the apostles, comprehended within themselves
all the other lower orders. They were therefore above ordinary pastors and
teachers.

6. It is certain and confirmed by experience that even after the apostolic
age some people under the New Testament were endowed with the gift of
prophesying and predicting. But from where can it be proved that Christ gave
such people to the church, if not from 1 Corinthians 14 and 12 and Ephesians
4?

7. The apostle distinguishes prophecy both from the word of wisdom and from
the word of knowledge in 1 Corinthians 12:8, 9. Pareus says on the passage that
it was the gift of predicting future things and revealing the secrets of
Scripture. Beza on the passage and Aretius on the passage hold the same.
Calvin says it was an extraordinary endowment for explaining God’s secret
will, so that the prophet is like God’s intermediary to human beings.

8. In the New Testament the word *prophecy* is frequently used for an
extraordinary gift in Matthew 26:68; Mark 7:6; Matthew 7:22; Acts 21:9; Luke
1:67; Revelation 1:3; 22:10, 19; 10:11; 1 Peter 1:10; John 11:51; 1 Timothy
1:18; Revelation 2:22; and 1 Timothy 4:14.

9. Indeed, this gift is numbered among the rarest granted to the apostles
themselves in 1 Corinthians 13:2.

10. First Corinthians 14:6 says, “Now, brothers, if I come to you—how will I
profit you unless I speak to you either by revelation, by knowledge, by
prophesying, or by teaching?” Here, according to some, there are two pairings,
as Pareus testifies on the passage. Revelation and prophecy cohere as

11. Throughout the whole chapter he prefers this gift of prophecy to the gift
of tongues. Therefore it denotes something extraordinary.

12. The apostle no more permits all the church’s members to prophesy than to
speak in tongues, as is clear from verse 26. Therefore it must be said either
that both these gifts were ordinary, which is absurd, or that they were
extraordinary, which is what we maintain.
