QUESTION VIII.

THE COMMUNICATION OF PROPERTIES.

*Were certain properties of the divine nature formally communicated to
Christ’s human nature through the personal union? We deny, against the
Lutherans.*

[Marginal heading: The effects of the hypostatic union with respect to the
human nature are the grace of eminence and habitual graces.]

I. The *effects of the hypostatic union* are twofold. Some concern *Christ’s
human nature,* and others the *Person* subsisting in both natures. Two things
are ordinarily referred to the former. First is the *grace of eminence,* the
dignity of the human nature above all creatures arising from its union with
the divine nature. Through this union it is the proper flesh of the Son of
God, which can be said of no other creature. Second are *habitual graces,*
namely, those remarkable gifts which the divine nature conferred upon the
human nature. Although these are the greatest and most perfect in their own
order, they were nevertheless not simply infinite, but proportioned to the
recipient’s capacity within the order of created gifts. Yet they were greater
than those in any angels or saints, both in the dignity of their subject and
in the perfection of their parts and degrees. Hence the Father is said not to
measure out the Spirit to him by measure (John 3:34).

[Marginal heading: With respect to the Person: 1. communication of properties;
2. communion of office; 3. communion of honor.]

II. With respect to the *Person,* the effects are again threefold: 1. the
*communication of properties,* the properties of each nature being
communicated to the Person; 2. the *communion of office and of effects,* by
which the mediatorial works pertaining to our salvation are attributed to the
Person acting according to both natures; and 3. the *communion of honor* and
worship owed to the θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man.*

[Marginal heading: The communication of properties.]

III. The *communication of properties* which we now discuss is an effect of
the union by which the properties of each nature have been made common to the
Person. From this arises the phraseology or manner of speaking about Christ
in which the properties of either nature are predicated of Christ’s Person,
however he may be denominated. This occurs either *directly,* when the things
belonging to the divine nature are predicated of the Person denominated from
the divine nature, and the things belonging to the human nature of the Person
denominated from the human nature. Thus the Word is said to have been with God
in the beginning and to have been God (John 1:1),

[Printed p. 284 | Internet Archive PDF 312 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and the Son of Man is said to be delivered up to death and crucified (Luke
9:22). Or it occurs *indirectly,* whenever things belonging to deity are
predicated of Christ the man, and things belonging to humanity are predicated
of Christ the God. Thus suffering is attributed to God (Acts 20:28), and
ubiquity, which is proper to deity, is attributed to the Son of Man (John
3:13).

[Marginal heading: It can be called real.]

IV. This communication is not only *verbal,* but is rightly called *real,* not
indeed with respect to the *natures,* as if the properties of one nature were
actually communicated to the other, but with respect to the *Person,* who
consists of the two natures really united and claims the properties of each
for himself. Although the union of the natures with each other is real, it is
not necessary for the properties of the natures to be communicated to one
another. It is enough that because of that union they are communicated to the
whole suppositum.

[Marginal heading: It is considered either concretely or abstractly.]

V. This communication can be considered either *abstractly* with respect to
the nature, or *concretely* with respect to the Person. The terms *concrete*
and *abstract* must not be taken in the Lutherans’ improper sense, according
to which the *abstract* is the human nature abstracted or separated from the
deity, while the *concrete* is the human nature as united to the Λόγῳ,
*Word.* This conflicts with the common usage of philosophers and with the
nature of the thing, according to which the human nature can never be
separated from the Word. Instead, the terms are employed here according to
the received usage of the schools. The *abstract* is the name of a nature or
form which inheres in another either essentially or accidentally, such as
deity and humanity. The *concrete* is the person or subject having that form
or nature, which is expressed by the concrete names *God* and *man.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

VI. The question here does not concern *communication in the concrete,* or,
as they call it, *of the nature to the person.* This is admitted on both
sides. Rather, it concerns *communication in the abstract,* or *of nature to
nature:* were the properties of the divine nature truly and really
communicated to the human nature through the hypostatic union? The Lutherans
affirm this, but we deny it.

[Marginal heading: Its origin.]

VII. The controversy had its origin in the other controversy about the
Supper. When the Lutherans did not find sufficient support in Christ’s words
for establishing a bodily presence, and felt themselves severely pressed by
the article of the ascension, they fled to ubiquity. Although Luther later
wished it dismissed, relying solely on Christ’s Word, *Brenz* and others who
renewed the sacramental controversy after Luther’s death recalled the
invention of ubiquity as the prow and stern of their cause. Because it was
objected against them that ubiquity is a property of God and therefore
incommunicable to others, they went so far as to say that some divine
properties were transfused into Christ’s human nature by the power of the
hypostatic union. Hence at the Colloquy of Maulbronn they wished the personal
union formally to consist in the communication of properties, or in the
communication of subsistence whose consequent effect is the communication of
properties. This view now prevails among nearly all of them. Yet it did not
please Luther, who insisted on the communication of properties only in the
concrete, not in the abstract. In tome 1 of the Jena edition, in the
*Disputation on the Two Natures in Christ and on the Communication of
Properties,* he says: “This is the catholic faith, that we confess one Christ,
true God and man. From this truth of a twofold substance and the unity of the
person follows what is called the communication of properties, so that the
things which belong to man are rightly said of God, and conversely the things
which belong to God are said of man. It is truly said, ‘This man created the
world,’ and ‘This God suffered,’ and so forth. Yet these statements are not
valid in the abstract, as when ‘the human nature’ is spoken of.” And in *On the
Last Words of David:* “By customary usage they call these forms of speech the
communication of properties, that is, when something which is proper to one
nature and is said of it separately in the abstract is nevertheless
attributed in the concrete

[Printed p. 285 | Internet Archive PDF 313 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to the person.” Paul Eber, the Wittenberg theologian, agrees with him in the
book he
published on this subject.

VIII. Nor, again, is the question whether the properties of the human nature
were communicated to the divine nature. The adversaries admit that this could
not happen. Nor is it whether all the properties of the divine nature were
communicated to the human nature, but only whether certain ones were, such as
omnipresence, omniscience, omnipotence, and life-giving power. They affirm
this; we deny it.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that there is no communication of properties
in the abstract. 1. Because the divine essence cannot be communicated.]

IX. The reasons are: 1. The divine essence cannot be communicated to a
creature, because a created thing cannot be made uncreated, or else it would
be God. Therefore neither can God’s essential properties, which are identical
with the divine essence, be communicated. For then the same nature would be
at once created and uncreated, immense and finite, and so forth, which is a
contradiction. Nor is the contradiction resolved by saying that by the *act
of the nature* it belongs to the human nature to be created and finite, but by
the *act of the Person* to be uncreated and immense. 1. Personality is not an
act, but the mode of a thing; and if the act of the Person belongs to Christ’s
humanity, it will certainly be a person. Nor do the propositions *The Word
became flesh* and *Man is God* cease to be true if the divine essence does not
communicate itself to the human nature. Their truth rests not upon a
communication of properties in the abstract, but upon the personal union of
the two natures in the concrete. Through regeneration we become partakers of
the divine nature (2 Pet. 1:4), not formally, but analogically by reason of
the gifts.

[Marginal heading: 2. Because properties are not communicated.]

X. Second, what is proper to one cannot be communicated to another, or else
it would cease to be proper and would become common to the one to whom it is
communicated. It is replied in vain that “it cannot indeed be communicated
*κατὰ μέθεξιν, by participation,* but can be *κατὰ σύνδυασιν, by conjunction,*
through the union of each, so that it has a place in subjects
*συνδεδυασμένοις, conjoined.*” For that *σύνδυασις, conjunction,* is not in
the united natures in the abstract, but in the Person, or the whole consisting
of the united natures, in the concrete. 2. It is also replied that
“properties can be communicated and yet remain properties, since they belong
to God alone by nature, but to the human nature by grace.” Yet what becomes
common to another, however it is communicated, can no longer be proper. Nor
can the difference of Christ’s human nature from other created things support
this communication, which can have no place in them. That difference consists
in the excellence of the gifts, the dignity of the office, and assumption
into the Person of the *θεανθρώπου, God-man,* not in the communication of
properties.

[Marginal heading: 3. Because either all must be communicated, or none.]

XI. Third, either all the properties of the divine nature were communicated,
or none, because they are inseparable and really one. When one of those
things which are really one is communicated, the other is necessarily
communicated. Add that if certain properties were communicated to the flesh
because of the hypostatic union, then since the Λόγος, *Word,* united his
whole self to the flesh, all the properties of the Λόγου ought also to have
been communicated, unless we wish to divide the Λόγον. No reason can be given
why some were communicated and others not. For if they say that it conflicts
with the human nature for it to be eternal, why would it not also conflict
with it to be omnipresent? Yet on the Lutherans’ hypothesis not all were
communicated. Therefore none were. Nor may it be answered that there is a
difference among God’s properties, some being *ἐνεργητικὰς, active,* which
manifest themselves outwardly in particular operations and whose
communication was necessary for the mediatorial office, while others are
*ἀνεργητικὰς, inactive,* which did not need to be communicated. Such a
difference obtains only with respect to the effects and to us, not in the
properties themselves and intrinsically, where no such difference can exist
because of the supreme simplicity of the essence. Therefore neither can
there be diversity in their communication. Nor does it help

[Printed p. 286 | Internet Archive PDF 314 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to invoke the distinction between *mediate and immediate communication,*
according to which
all the divine properties are indeed said to be communicated mediately in the
hypostasis of the Λόγου, which was communicated to the flesh, but only
certain ones immediately. Apart from the fact that a formal communication of
the hypostasis to the flesh is gratuitously imagined, as will be proved
later, the question does not concern mediate but immediate communication,
through which, on that hypothesis, all the divine properties ought to be
predicated of the human nature, since the account of all the properties must
be alike. I grant that they are distinguished into communicable and
incommunicable, but it is well known that this is not meant formally—in which
sense they are all equally incommunicable—but only analogically.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because the properties of the flesh were not
communicated to the Λόγῳ.]

XII. Fourth, if the divine properties were communicated to the flesh because
of the union, then in turn the properties of the flesh ought to have been
communicated to the Λόγῳ, because the union is reciprocal, which they are
nevertheless unwilling to admit. Nor can the distinction between the assuming
and the assumed nature remedy this difficulty. The basis of reciprocal
communication is not the assumption but the union itself. Just as the union
is reciprocal—for as the divine nature is united to the human, so the human
is united to the divine—so it would require reciprocal communication not only
in the concrete but also in the abstract. Nor can a distinction be sought in
the claim that the human nature needed the communication of these properties,
but the divine nature did not. The human nature indeed needed excellent gifts
to perform its work, but not the properties of God, which would instead have
destroyed the human nature and transformed it into deity.

[Marginal heading: 5. Because this would produce a confusion of the natures.]

XIII. Fifth, if there is a real communication of properties in the abstract,
there will also be an equalization and confusion of the natures, an error long
ago condemned in Eutyches. The consequence is clear from the nature of
confusion, which occurs when two or more things and their properties are
communicated, that is, made common. Nor does it help to reply that no
equalization takes place because the human nature does not have omnipresence
from itself. For one thing to be made equal to another, it is not necessary
that from itself and through itself it should not be equal to that with which
it is equalized. Communication is ineptly opposed to equalization and
confusion, which cannot occur without communication. Nor has anyone ever
heard that things which are already equalized are equalized, or that things
which are confused are confused. Nor was Eutyches ever charged with thinking
that Christ’s flesh was made divine by itself and from itself. Indeed, such a
thing cannot even be imagined by those endowed with reason, for how could it
have been made divine if it was already divine from itself?

[Marginal heading: 6. From the Lutherans’ hypothesis.]

XIV. Sixth, if the union was effected without confusion and the natures
themselves and their properties remained preserved and distinct, as the
Lutherans acknowledge, no communication of properties could have been made in
it. For what is communicated does not remain proper. Nor can it be said that
the properties always remain distinct because they belong to deity *ἰδίως,
properly,* and by nature, but to humanity by grace. Whatever the mode of
possessing them may be, they wish the human nature truly and really to possess
them, so that they can legitimately be predicated of it. Thus they no longer
remain proper to the Λόγῳ.

[Marginal heading: 7. Omnipresence is opposed.]

XV. Seventh, we argue as follows against the *omnipresence* of Christ’s human
nature. 1. Omnipresence was not communicated absolutely to one to whom it was
communicated neither in the state of humiliation nor in the state of
exaltation, because these are the only two states of Christ the Mediator. But
it was communicated in neither: not in the *humiliation,* because the Gospel
testifies that he was not everywhere, but now in this place and now in that,
and that he passed from place to place (John 6:24; 11:15),

[Printed p. 287 | Internet Archive PDF 315 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

(Mark 6:6); not in the *exaltation,* because it was not given to him while he
was still on earth: “I know you seek Jesus who was crucified. He is not here,
for he has risen, as he said. See the place where the Lord lay” (Matt.
28:5–6); and “See my hands and my feet, for I am he himself” (Luke 24:39–40),
and so forth. Nor was it given when he was carried into heaven, because he
foretold that he would depart from the world and no longer be present here:
“You will always have the poor with you, but you will not always have me”
(Matt. 26:11); “I came forth from the Father and came into the world. Again, I
leave the world and go to the Father” (John 16:28); and “I am no longer in the
world” (John 17:11), that is, soon I will no longer be here. 2. He actually
departed from this world (Luke 24:51; Acts 1:9). 3. He now dwells in heaven
and will not return until the day of judgment: “Whom heaven must receive until
the time of the restoration of all things” (Acts 3:21); “If he were on earth,
he would not even be a priest” (Heb. 8:4); and “The Lord will descend from
heaven” (1 Thess. 4:16–17).

XVI. It cannot be replied: 1. *The human nature possessed omnipresence in the
state of humiliation in first act, but did not exercise or reveal it.* For
this gratuitously assumes what had to be proved. Nor would Scripture deny
absolutely and simply that Christ was present everywhere if this were to be
understood only restrictively and with limitation. 2. *Christ left the world
with respect to visible and familiar intercourse with his own and to his
servile condition, but not with respect to real and substantial presence,
according to which he says that he will be with his disciples until the
consummation of the age.* For just as Scripture nowhere teaches an invisible
presence of Christ’s body, so Christ’s words exclude every presence of his
body when he is said to be leaving the world, that is, the earth, and
ascending into heaven. Even if it is said metaphorically of the Λόγῳ that he
came forth from the Father and descended from heaven, the account is not the
same for the flesh’s departure from the world, which is properly attributed to
it. He promises that he will always be with the disciples according to the
Spirit, not according to the flesh. 3. *There are three respects of Christ’s
body: first, according to the properties of its nature; second, according to
the glory of the resurrection; and third, according to the grace of the
personal union. In the first two modes it is in only one place, but in the
last it is everywhere.* For the omnipresence of the body follows no more from
the third respect than from the others, because although united to deity it
remained quantitative, as it remained a body.

XVII. 2. The omnipresence of Christ’s body cannot be reconciled with various
articles of faith. For if it is imagined to be omnipresent by the power of the
personal union, it could neither have been *conceived* in the womb of the
blessed Virgin, nor have been *born* outside it and come into the light, nor
have *died,* since the soul could not have been separated from the body, nor
have been *buried,* since it was already in the tomb, nor have *risen,* since
it was outside the tomb, nor have *ascended into heaven,* where it already
dwelt, nor will it be able to *descend* from heaven to earth for judgment if
it will be present everywhere. Nor may it be said that in the state of
humiliation Christ did not exercise the majesty communicated to his flesh,
but subjected it to locality and the other conditions of this age. The
communication of majesty is gratuitously assumed, not proved. Moreover,
locality flows from the necessity of the human nature and does not depend
upon freedom of the will. The history of the ascension teaches that his
departure was not merely a cessation of company or intercourse, but a
translation of the body from earth to heaven and from a lower place to a
higher one (Mark 16; Acts 1).

XVIII [printed XVII]. 3. It conflicts with the *nature of a body,* which is
circumscribed and finite, has parts outside parts, and ought in itself to be
visible and palpable. It cannot be excepted: 1. *It is unjust to subject a
glorious body to the laws of the common nature.* For glory neither destroys
nor changes the nature, but

[Printed p. 288 | Internet Archive PDF 316 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

only adorns and perfects it. 2. *Christ’s body is circumscribed by the property
of its nature and in a physical mode, but uncircumscribed by reason of the
union and in a hyperphysical mode.* This distinction has no foundation in
Scripture or in the nature of the thing. Indeed, it is absurd and ridiculous,
because one body is made capable of contrary properties, locality and
nonlocality. A mode is objected in vain where the thing itself is not
acknowledged. 3. *Christ’s body was sometimes ἄφαντον, hidden, and ἀόρατον,
invisible* (Mark 16:12; Luke 24:31; John 8:59). This did not occur by a
separation of visibility from the body, but partly through a miraculous
escape or withdrawal and partly through the restraint of their eyes so that
he was not recognized.

[Marginal heading: 8. Omniscience.]

XIX. Eighth, we argue as follows against *omniscience.* A nature which is
ignorant of something, progresses in knowledge, and is finite cannot be
called omniscient. But such is Christ’s nature, which was ignorant of the day
of judgment (Mark 13:32), progressed in wisdom (Luke 2:40, 52), and is finite
and limited and therefore incapable of the infinite.

[Marginal heading: 9. Omnipotence.]

XX. Ninth, against *omnipotence.* Omnipotence has not been communicated to one
who recognizes another as more powerful than himself and in whom a certain
passive potency exists, because one who is omnipotent is also impassible and
immortal. But according to the human nature Christ was passible and mortal,
and needed the Father’s support (Matt. 26; John 17). Nor may it be said that
Christ did this *κατ’ οἰκονομίαν, according to the economy,* because he had
emptied himself, but not from necessity or need. Although it is true that the
Son of God assumed our nature according to the economy and not from
necessity, it is nevertheless false that Christ’s human nature did not simply
need divine aid for its preservation.

[Marginal heading: 10. Life-giving power.]

XXI. Tenth, against *life-giving power.* Whoever has life-giving power in
himself is God, because it belongs to God alone to give life: “With you is the
fountain of life” (Ps. 36:9). The life of God is nothing other than his active
essence, which cannot be communicated to a created nature. Moreover, if the
human nature possessed that life-giving power, it could not be called dead,
because the life of God cannot be extinguished, nor can the personal union be
dissolved.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXII. The consequence does not hold *from the real union of the two natures in
Christ to a communication of properties between the natures.* The real union
of the natures does not abolish their distinction, but rather posits it,
since things which are not distinct in the union cannot be said to be
actually united. Nor does it imply a real communication of properties with
respect to the natures, but with respect to the Person, who consists of the
two really united natures and claims the properties of each for himself.

XXIII. An argument does not hold *from the whole Christ to the whole of
Christ,* or from Christ’s Person to the natures, nor can what is attributed to
him immediately be predicated of them. The Person indeed claims for himself
the properties of both natures, but one nature does not claim the properties
of the other which belong to the Person, or else the natures would be
confused. Hence an easy answer is given to Matthew 18:20 and 28:20, because
there the subject is the Person in the concrete, not the human nature in the
abstract.

XXIV. *An argument is not properly drawn from the assumption of the human
nature into the unity of the Person to a formal communication of the
hypostasis of the Λόγου, nor to a communication of properties in the
abstract.* The assumption of the nature can exist without a communication of
the hypostasis. Hypostatic sustentation is sufficient, so that the human
nature, which in itself is *ἀνυπόστατος, without a subsistence of its own,*
becomes *ἐνυπόστατος Λόγῳ, subsistent in the Word,* and is sustained by him.
It follows from this that it does not have its own subsistence, nor subsist in
the mode proper to a person, since even in the union it is not a Person. Yet
it does subsist, or rather is sustained, in the Λόγῳ.

[Printed p. 289 | Internet Archive PDF 317 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XXV. When the *fullness of deity* is said to *dwell in Christ σωματικῶς,
bodily* (Col. 2:9), a communication of properties in the abstract cannot be
inferred from it. 1. The passage concerns Christ in the concrete, not the
nature in the abstract. 2. Even if it concerned the human nature as the
temple of deity (John 2:19), nothing would be elicited from it in favor of the
communication of properties. It is one thing to *dwell* in the apostolic
sense and another to *communicate* in the Lutheran sense. For the apostle,
*to dwell bodily* means that the whole fullness of deity—that is, the divine
essence with the entire fullness of its perfections—is united to the human
nature, not shadow-wise or merely through efficacy or assistance, as in the
types of the Old Testament, in believers, and in other obscure and imperfect
revelations, but *truly* and *personally,* just as *body* is opposed to
*shadow* in verse 17 and *σῶμα, body,* often signifies a person among the
Greeks. But *to communicate* in the Lutheran sense denotes really giving and
transfusing the whole fullness of deity into the humanity. It is clear that
this cannot occur here, because the fullness of deity is nothing other than
God’s essence itself with all its attributes. If the fullness of deity was
communicated to the flesh, the flesh will be deity itself and all the
properties would have been communicated to it equally, contrary to their
hypothesis.

XXVI. A union which is *ἀδιαίρετος, indivisible,* and *ἀδιάστατος,
inseparable,* does not immediately require that wherever one of the united
things is, the other also be there, except when the things in question are
equal to each other and one does not exceed the other. This cannot be said of
Christ’s deity and humanity. Thus whatever is in a whole which is everywhere
ought indeed to be everywhere if it is in the omnipresent whole by
equalization, that is, if the union is between things which are equally
infinite. But this does not apply where a finite nature is united to the
infinite while retaining its finitude.

XXVII. If the Λόγος is somewhere outside the humanity by *separation,* so as
to be separated from it, it can truly be said that somewhere he would not be
man. But if somewhere he is outside the humanity by *noninclusion,* it cannot
properly be said that somewhere he is not man. The Son of God is man not
through a local coexistence of the humanity with the deity, but through the
hypostatic union of the human nature, made not with a place but with the
Λόγῳ. Therefore the Λόγος existing on earth is most truly man and has the
human nature united to himself personally and most immediately, even if that
nature is not present on earth. Thus where Christ’s humanity is not, Christ
the man is nevertheless properly said to be, insofar as the Λόγος existing
there sustains the humanity, though it exists not there but elsewhere.

XXVIII. *When the ubiquity of the human nature is denied, a separation* or
division of the two natures in Christ does not immediately follow. It is one
thing for the Λόγον to be outside the flesh by noninclusion, which is granted
in this sense because, being infinite, he is not included in it. It is another
thing to be outside it by separation, which is denied, because although he is
not included, he is nowhere separated from the flesh. The proposition *The
Word is somewhere not man* can have two meanings. Either it means *The Word
has somewhere abandoned the human nature,* and in this sense it is false,
because what the Word once assumed he never dismissed; or it means *The Word
is somewhere where the flesh of the Word is not.* In this way it is true
according to Christ’s word, *You will not always have me,* that is, according
to the humanity, while nevertheless elsewhere he says, *I will be with you
until the consummation of the ages,* namely, according to the deity.

XXIX. Although Christ sits at the right hand of God, which is everywhere, it
does not follow that Christ is everywhere according to the flesh. 1. God’s
right hand is one thing and sitting at the right hand another. The former is
God’s omnipotence and majesty; the latter is the Mediator’s royal office. What
is said of the right hand cannot immediately be said of the one who sits at
the right hand. Otherwise, as God’s right hand is eternal and immense, so the
one who sits at the right hand ought to be such, and the church herself,
which is said to sit at the right hand of her Bridegroom (Ps. 45:9), could
claim this for herself.

[Printed p. 290 | Internet Archive PDF 318 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

2. The phrase is not proper but metaphorical and figurative. It indicates the
*name of an office* proper to the Person, not *πανταχουσίαν, omnipresence,* or
a property of the nature: namely, the rule and dominion over the world and
the church conferred upon Christ, as the apostle interprets it, “*He must
reign*” (1 Cor. 15:25). Reigning belongs not to this or that nature, but to
the Person of the *θεανθρώπου, God-man.* 3. Christ is said to sit not
everywhere, but only in heaven (Eph. 1:20; Heb. 8:1). For this reason he is
said to have ascended into heaven. Here the phrase *in heaven* determines the
place which Christ occupied upon ascending (John 14:2–3; 17:13), rather than
signifying heavenly glory, which is sufficiently expressed by the word
*sitting.*

XXX. Christ is said to have ascended into heaven in order that he might *fill
all things* (Eph. 4:10), not places with his body, but the members of the
church with the gifts of the Holy Spirit. The preceding and following context
necessarily requires this sense. In the preceding verses, from Psalm 68, he
shows that the end of the exaltation is the outpouring of the gifts of the
Holy Spirit (v. 8). He immediately repeats this statement, illustrated by an
antithesis, in verses 9–10, and calls what he there named *giving gifts*
*filling all things.* In what follows, he explains the exaltation by its
effects, showing how the exalted Christ fills the members of the church,
specifically its teachers, with his Spirit.

XXXI. When “treasures of wisdom and understanding are said to be hidden in
him” (Col. 2:3), this pertains not to Christ’s human nature, but either to the
mystery of the gospel by reason of its meaning, which Paul had discussed in
the preceding verse, or to Christ himself. In the latter case, it pertains
not so much to him by reason of the subject, that is, the wisdom with which
Christ himself is endowed, as by reason of the object, because all saving
wisdom consists in the knowledge of him.

XXXII. The Spirit is said to have been given to Christ “*ἀμέτρως, without
measure*” (John 3:34), not absolutely, as if the gifts of the Holy Spirit
conferred upon him according to the oracle of Isaiah 11:2 were simply immense,
as the knowledge and wisdom of God are simply immense. Rather, it is
*comparatively* with respect to us and all Christ’s members, who from his
fullness as their Head receive grace for grace (John 1:16). Thus things which
are separated in others—one gift being given to this person and another to
that person, *κατὰ τὸ μέτρον τῆς δωρεᾶς τοῦ Χριστοῦ, according to the measure
of Christ’s gift* (Eph. 4:7), that is, the measure Christ wished to bestow upon
us—are found united in him, because Christ is all in all (Col. 3:11). Things
which are always imperfect in others are most perfect in him. Hence he is
said to be *anointed with the oil of gladness above his companions* (Ps. 45:7;
Heb. 1:9), because he must *ἐν πᾶσι πρωτεύειν, have preeminence in all things*
(Rom. 8:29 [sic]).

XXXIII. Since the universal judgment is an effect of the Mediator’s office,
it is attributed to the Person according to both natures, not according to
only one. Thus the distinct operations of each nature concur in it: the
divine through omniscience and omnipotence, and the human through the return
for judgment and the sensible and visible acts of judgment described in
Matthew 25. When it is said that “*authority was given to him to execute
judgment ὅτι υἱὸς ἀνθρώπου, because he is the Son of Man*” (John 5:27), the
particle *ὅτι, because,* is not so much to be taken *reduplicatively* for
*insofar as,* so that Christ is said to have been appointed judge by God not
only as God but also as man, as the apostle speaks in Acts 17:31. Rather, it
is taken *causally* as *because,* to indicate the cause of this authority’s
being conferred upon the Son, namely, the mediatorial office which he
undertook through the incarnation. Thus *Son of Man* here denotes not the
human nature in the abstract, but the Person in the concrete in relation to
his office. Nothing else is intimated than that such power was given to him
because he is the Messiah whom Daniel calls the *Son of Man* in chapter 7,
and to whom Daniel saw dominion and power given by the Ancient of Days, as the
Person chosen for so great and sublime a mediatorial office.

XXXIV. In Matthew 28:18 Christ does not speak of something communicated by the

to the human nature in the personal union. Rather, he shows only what was
given to him by God the Father for the execution of his office, not in the
union but after the resurrection. 2. He speaks not of *potentia, power,* which
is an essential property of God, but of *potestas, authority,* which is a
personal effect of the Mediator. He does not say *πᾶσα δύναμις, all power,*
but *πᾶσα ἐξουσία, all authority,* to indicate the right and authority to
command and judge. Hence he immediately adds, *Go therefore and teach,* and
so forth. Even if every authority requires omnipotence for its exercise, it
is not necessary for one who possesses all authority immediately to possess
omnipotence with respect to both natures. It is enough that it belongs to the
Person with respect to the other nature. 3. He does not say *to the human
nature,* but *to me,* so as to designate the Person in the concrete, not the
nature in the abstract. 4. Although properly speaking nothing can be given to
the deity by reason of the *essence,* nevertheless something can be given to
it economically, both by reason of the *office,* so that he undertakes and
executes it according to the Father’s will, and by reason of *manifestation,*
so that the glory of the deity, which had been hidden in the form of a
servant, might now be revealed and made conspicuous to all (John 17:5).

XXXV. One who performs miracles by his own physical power must be omnipotent.
But Christ as man did not perform them by his own power, but in the manner of
a moral instrument. Therefore he had to concur in the miraculous work by
contributing what belonged to him, but the infinite power by which the
miracle was properly produced belonged to the deity alone (Mark 5:30).
Miracles are attributed to Christ in the concrete, not to the humanity in the
abstract.

XXXVI. Christ’s flesh is called life-giving (John 6:48), not *efficiently,* by
producing eternal life through itself, but *meritoriously,* by acquiring for
us through his death a right to life. Moreover, in similar expressions a
synecdoche must be recognized, by which an effect that properly belongs to
the Person according to both natures is attributed to the flesh or blood by a
synecdoche of the part.

XXXVII. Although what belongs to the other nature is attributed to the Person
denominated from one nature, this does not introduce a false conception by
attributing to the subject what does not belong to it. For example, suffering
and death are attributed to God, though they do not belong to him (Acts
20:28). They are not attributed to God as the subject of inherence, but to the
Person who is God as the subject of denomination, which is sufficient to
remove the false conception.

XXXVIII. The various distinctions offered by the Lutherans to reconcile the
contradictions occurring in their opinion cannot untie the knot and involve
many contradictions. The first distinction is between the *act of the Person
and of the nature:* the human nature is finite with respect to the latter,
but omnipresent with respect to the former. But this is in vain. 1. The act of
the Person cannot belong to a nature which is not a person. And if the
personal act belongs to Christ’s flesh, it will actually be a Person. 2.
Personality is not a divine act, but a mode of a thing distinct from its
essence and existence. 3. A divine act cannot be attributed to the human
nature, which is not God. 4. This personal act either destroys the act of the
nature or it does not. If it does, the flesh would cease to be flesh, because
the act of the nature is the nature itself. If it does not, Christ’s flesh can
always be said to be finite and circumscribed by a definite space, which is
*ἀσύστατον, incompatible,* with its ubiquity.

XXXIX. The account is the same for the *physical and hyperphysical or
majestic mode.* 1. A hyperphysical mode does not destroy the nature, but
adorns it, while a mode of being everywhere destroys it and is therefore
antiphysical. 2. Christ’s body did not lose quantity through the union, and
therefore did not lose its mode or dimension. 3. If by the *mode of majesty*
is understood only that most excellent glory by which the assumed flesh was
taken into the hypostasis of the Λόγου, this indeed implies a prerogative of
the flesh above every other creature, but not a communication of divine
properties. But if it means the sum of all

the divine attributes, since that majesty is nothing other than deity, it
would follow that deity itself was communicated to Christ’s flesh.

XL. 3. They customarily distinguish a twofold *being* in Christ: *local,*
according to which he is in a definite space, and *nonlocal,* according to
which he has been everywhere from the beginning of the incarnation. But this
is absurd. 1. Since the relation of time is no less connected to a body than
the relation of place, there will also be in Christ a twofold being, temporal
and nontemporal or eternal, which they nevertheless deny. 2. Local and
nonlocal are contradictories which cannot belong to the same thing. Nor do
different respects help at all, because the hypostatic union does not abolish
the nature of a body and therefore does not abolish local being. Thus it
cannot give it nonlocal being, because what leaves one side of the
contradiction in place cannot simultaneously attribute the other to it.

XLI. 4. They distinguish *κτῆσιν and χρῆσιν, possession and use,* being so
*through oneself* and *κατ’ ἄλλο, through another.* Christ’s deity is
omnipresent through itself, *κατὰ κτῆσιν, by possession,* and with respect to
both first and second act together, but the humanity only *κατ’ ἄλλο, through
another,* and *κατὰ χρῆσιν, by use.* But this is erroneous, because such
distinctions abolish the very subject in question. They all tend to make
omnipresence belong in some way or other to Christ’s body, which cannot remain
a body if it is omnipresent. 2. In earthly things, *κτῆσις,* or ownership of a
thing, can often be distinguished from *χρῆσις,* or its use, because one who
has the use of a thing does not always own it. But it is not so in divine
things. One who is omnipotent by use is no different from one who is such by
property, for God does not give his glory to another. 3. If being everywhere
*κατ’ ἄλλο, through another,* means only omnipresence with respect to the
concrete or the Person, the sense will be true, but the expression
inconvenient. But if it intimates an omnipresence given to the flesh by the
power of the union, the distinction is absurd and involves *ἀσύστατα,
inconsistencies,* so that the same subject is at once circumscribed and
uncircumscribed.

XLII. The examples of the soul, which communicates life to the body, and of
fire, which communicates to heated iron the faculty of burning and shining,
do not support the communication of properties. The life produced in the body
by the soul is not the soul’s own property but an effect, and the power of
shining and burning in heated iron is not in the iron, but in the fire hidden
and enclosed within the iron’s pores.
