Eurydice, wife of King Arrhidaeus, was born to Cyna, Alexander’s sister, and
Amyntas, son of the elder Perdiccas, King Philip’s brother. A woman of a
restless and proud disposition, she harassed Python with so many daily quarrels
and insults that he thought it expedient to resign his office as guardian and
protector. From then on, the ambitious Eurydice breathed nothing but the
arbitrary administration of the entire empire in her husband’s name; but she
soon fell from this hope.

> Marginalia, printed p. 207: *Eurydice harasses Python*.

Antipater, summoned by the army to undertake the care of the kings, immediately
forced Eurydice away from manly and public affairs and confined her again to
women’s concerns. He made a new distribution of the provinces according to his
own pleasure: he assigned Media to Python and the command of the royal army to
Antigonus, thereby committing to him the war against Eumenes. Antipater himself
departed for Macedonia with the kings and Eurydice.

> Marginalia, printed p. 207: *He rebukes Eurydice too*.

Antigonus moved vigorously against Eumenes. Alcetas, Perdiccas’s brother, and
Attalus, his brother-in-law, envied Eumenes’s command and very foolishly
separated their forces from his, for after Eumenes had been defeated Antigonus
crushed them with little trouble. But subduing Eumenes was a greater labor once
they came to blows. Although the flower of Macedonian strength stood with
Antigonus, Eumenes’s valor nevertheless appeared likely to prevail, had not
Apollonides, corrupted by Antigonus, deserted him in the midst of battle with
his wing of cavalry. Eumenes did not allow the deserting Apollonides to escape,
but overtook him and rewarded his treachery with a public hanging. Eumenes
himself, however, weakened by this betrayal, was defeated by Antigonus.
Nevertheless, he returned immediately after his flight by a roundabout route,
took possession of the battlefield that same night, and buried his slain men.
Although he could have captured Antigonus’s baggage, he secretly warned
Menander to hurry to safety, for he did not wish the remnant of his soldiers to
be burdened in their flight by too much spoil. By these stratagems he won great
praise even among his victorious enemies. Yet, perceiving that his forces were
too small for him to dare stand against Antigonus in battle, he dismissed his
soldiers with commendation. He himself fled with a few men to Nora, a fortress
in Cappadocia, confidently foreseeing that after a short time disturbances
would arise in the state that would open for him a path to new enterprises;
this prudent man’s prediction was not in vain.

Antigonus hemmed Eumenes closely within Nora for an entire year of siege. Yet
through conversations and intermediaries he often tried to entice Eumenes into
friendship with him. But Eumenes’s loyalty to Alexander’s house was so firm
that even Antigonus’s greatest promises, amid his own extreme danger, could not
corrupt him.

> Marginalia, printed p. 207: *Antigonus breaks Eumenes*.

Meanwhile Antipater died at home, more than eighty years old, entrusting the
government of the kings and Greece to Polysperchon, the oldest surviving one
of Alexander’s commanders. Cassander, indignant that his father had preferred
Polysperchon to him, left no stone unturned to dislodge the man and seize the
reins of empire himself. Although Alexander had felt great reverence for his
mother Olympias, he knew her fierce and savage disposition and had therefore
never allowed her any part in public government. Antipater too,

> Polysperchon*.

as he lay dying, had instructed Polysperchon not to permit any woman to lay her
hands upon affairs of state. Nevertheless, when the imprudent old man perceived
that Eurydice’s devices were turning the favor of the Macedonians away from
him, he drew in a greater evil to remedy this one. He recalled Olympias from
Epirus, to which Antipater had banished her, into Macedonia. As soon as she
arrived, the woman’s monstrous pride and cruelty brought everything crashing
down. She did only this one thing rightly: she placed Eumenes, now escaped from
Nora, over Asian affairs with supreme command under the kings.

Immediately after Antipater’s death, Antigonus, commander of the royal army in
Asia, aspired to Alexander’s throne. To make a friend of Eumenes, whose valor
he feared above everything, Antigonus offered him generous terms of peace in
writing. Only one thing in them offended Eumenes: he seemed to be bound far
more strictly to Antigonus than to the kings. Those besieging Nora were
Macedonians. While Antigonus was absent from the camp, Eumenes subscribed to
the terms offered, with only a slight change to the binding clause and with the
good favor of the Macedonians: he was to be bound to obey the commands of the
kings before Antigonus and all others.

Freed from confinement and Antigonus’s contrivances by this honorable ruse, he
immediately gathered a great body of veterans. Instructions came to him from
Olympias and Polysperchon that he should take as much as he thought necessary
from the royal armies and treasury to suppress Antigonus’s tyranny. Without
delay he first made his way into Cilicia to the Silver Shields. At first they
submitted to his command reluctantly, but so great were the man’s humanity,
prudence, industry, and courage that they soon cooperated amicably enough.

At that time Seleucus governed Babylon and Python governed Media. Both rejected
Eumenes’s authority, since they were themselves panting after supreme power.
Python had removed one or two neighboring satraps from his path, and so all the
others attacked him with their combined forces. Unequal to them all, he joined
Seleucus. By presenting the royal letters to these confederates, Eumenes
enticed them to his side—the rest easily, but Peucestes, governor of Persia,
with greater difficulty, because he too aspired to empire.

Meanwhile Antigonus arrived, and Python and Seleucus joined him. Peucestes and
his eager allies accordingly submitted to Eumenes’s leadership, and the great
forces on each side met on the banks of the Tigris. With such skill and
vigilance did Eumenes guard the eastern bank from the river’s source to the sea
that whenever Antigonus attempted to cross, he was driven back with great loss
of his men. As the war dragged on, and Antigonus made no progress because of
Eumenes’s many stratagems, he finally bore down upon Eumenes by night marches
in the middle of winter. Since most of the forces were far away in distant
winter quarters, Peucestes and everyone else present began to panic. Eumenes
told them to be of good courage, for he would impede the enemy’s march for as
many days as were needed for their own forces to return. He accomplished this:
that night he arranged many fires upon the crests of the mountains overlooking
Antigonus’s camp. The sight terrified Antigonus, as if Eumenes were ready with
all his forces to attack his own weary troops on the road. He retreated with
the utmost speed and, while he delayed, gave Eumenes time enough to summon his
forces.

At last, however, they came to battle. The Silver Shields—many of whom were in
their seventies and none younger than sixty—put Antigonus’s entire phalanx to
flight after great slaughter. Peucestes, however, withdrew from the battle
with his cavalry and handed Antigonus victory among the horsemen. Taking
advantage of the opportunity, Antigonus used a wing of cavalry to seize the
Silver Shields’ baggage, and all Eumenes’s stratagems were powerless against
this setback. The wives and children of the Silver Shields came into
Antigonus’s power, together with all the spoil they had gathered from defeated
nations along their entire march. Antigonus promised to return all of it to
them untouched, provided they delivered Eumenes alone into his hands. They
agreed to this and handed him over in chains. After a few days’ imprisonment,
Eumenes was consigned by Antigonus to an unworthy death, although his son
Demetrius strongly counseled against it. But Antigonus conceived such
indignation against the treacherous Silver Shields that he handed them over to
Sibyrtius to be led into Arachosia and other distant provinces, with explicit
instructions to ensure that none of them ever again saw his ancestral hearth
or the Greek sea, even from a distance. He also removed Peucestes from the
governorship of Persia and forced him to persist in an obscure and ignoble
life until death; and he deprived Python of Media and his life when Python
plotted against him. When Antigonus came to Babylon and was treated very
amicably by Seleucus, he ordered Seleucus to give him an account of the
province’s revenues. Seeing that no charm of words would satisfy Antigonus,
Seleucus fled with a few cavalrymen to Ptolemy in Egypt for fear of immediate
death. Antigonus, now master of all Asia without an adversary, turned his mind
to adding Macedonia and Greece to Asia.

All affairs among the Macedonians were then in the worst condition because of
Olympias’s implacable anger against Eurydice. Cassander, indignant that his
father had preferred Polysperchon to him in the care of the kings, fled to
Asia, and obtained from Antigonus a fleet and soldiers with which to draw the
empire of Macedonia and Greece to himself. Polysperchon feared that the Greek
cities,

in each of which Antipater had placed aristocrats in command after dissolving
the democracies,
would defect to Cassander. He therefore restored democracies everywhere by
royal edict, displaced the aristocrats whom Antipater—and also Alexander and
Philip—had established throughout Greece, and punished a great part of them
with either death or exile. To carry out this bloody edict, he led Arrhidaeus
with an army throughout Greece. When he came into Attica, the Athenian people
cast all their aristocrats down from power. Foremost among these was Phocion, a
man laden with every praise, a very close friend of Philip, Alexander, and
Antipater. Now eighty years old, he had held the highest office more than fifty
times, never through any canvassing of his own, and had always discharged it
to the state’s great advantage and honor. He fled from the people’s fury to
Polysperchon. In order to bind the Athenians to himself, Polysperchon sent
Phocion back to the city in chains. By public vote the ungrateful and furious
people ordered this most excellent man to drink hemlock. After that day, in
fact, no man distinguished in any field ever arose at Athens.

> Marginalia, printed p. 209: *Phocion, the last of the illustrious Athenians*.

The Megalopolitans fortified their city against Polysperchon’s attack. By no
art and by no means could Polysperchon subdue it; after great slaughter of his
men he was forced to withdraw from the siege, and all the more hastily because
Cassander, with a strong army from Asia, was bringing many cities of the
Peloponnese over to himself. Indeed, as Polysperchon was despised as a sluggish,
cruel, and luckless man, most of Greece had begun to favor Cassander.

> Marginalia, printed p. 209: *Polysperchon after the siege of Megalopolis is
> broken*.

Meanwhile Olympias advanced from Epirus into Macedonia, and Eurydice met her
with a hostile army. Before battle, the Macedonians passed from Eurydice to
Olympias out of remembrance for Philip and Alexander. When Olympias saw that
the Macedonians depended upon her, she seized Eurydice and Arrhidaeus and shut
them in a very close prison. Eurydice wrote of her straits to Cassander, who
was far too close and intimate a friend of hers and was then deeply occupied
in the Peloponnese, begging him to come to her aid as quickly as he could. But
Olympias’s fury outran all Cassander’s haste. After having Arrhidaeus long
tortured in prison, she finally had a Thracian stab him to death; soon after,
she forced Eurydice to hang herself with her own girdle. She delivered one
hundred of Cassander’s leading friends to death together with his brother
Nicanor; and after violating the tomb of his brother Iollas, whom she regarded
as Alexander’s poisoner and killer, she scattered his bones. The woman’s
monstrous cruelty turned the hearts of the Macedonians away from her. Thus,
when Cassander approached with an army, Olympias had to shut herself within
Pydna. Cassander enclosed the city in a close siege and, after several months,
forced its surrender through the most terrible famine. Polysperchon hastened
from Greece and Pyrrhus’s father Aeacides from Epirus to aid Olympias, but both
without success. Most of Polysperchon’s soldiers, corrupted by gifts and
promises, deserted to Cassander and forced Polysperchon to retreat. Aeacides’s
army raised a mutiny and not only deserted him, but on returning home expelled
him from the kingdom and placed Neoptolemus in his stead. Thus, with all her
hope gone, Olympias came into Cassander’s hands. By a public decree of the
Macedonians he condemned her to death, but those sent to kill her revered the
woman’s majesty and returned without harming her. The relatives of the men she
had killed, however, carried out the murder at Cassander’s second command, and
she bore it magnificently, as befitted Alexander’s mother.

Now safe from Olympias, Cassander committed Roxane and her son to the most
secure custody, forced Thessalonica, Philip’s daughter, to marry him, and
possessed almost all Macedonia and Greece in peace. But behold, a new storm of
ambition and greed set all Alexander’s commanders against one another and at
last enveloped most of them in mutual destruction.

> Marginalia, printed p. 209: *Roxane too, with her son*.

Antigonus, now enjoying all Asia without a rival, plotted against Ptolemy in
Egypt, Lysimachus in Thrace, and Cassander in Greece. So that they would not be
picked off one by one, these prudent men resolved to forestall him by combining
their counsels and forces. Through envoys they openly demanded from Antigonus a
division of Alexander’s treasuries and of the provinces of Asia, and Seleucus’s
restoration to his possessions. Antigonus laughed at the envoys and dismissed
them after sprinkling them with a great deal of verbal salt.

> Marginalia, printed p. 209: *The others conspire against Antigonus*.

From this torch the war’s flame was kindled. Ptolemy extended his dominion
throughout Syria with little labor, for most of its peoples defected to him
almost voluntarily. The Jerusalemites did not, indeed, receive him within
their walls. But Ptolemy observed that the Jews abstained from military duties
on the Sabbath, and on that day entered the city under arms as though in the
midst of profound peace. He overturned everything at his pleasure and led one
hundred thousand citizens away with him into Egypt. Yet he treated them as
friends rather than captives and entrusted to them the custody of most of his
fortresses in Egypt and Cyrene.

> Marginalia, printed p. 209: *Ptolemy’s actions against the Jews*.

Antigonus decided to begin war with Ptolemy in Syria through his son Demetrius,
who was indeed quite young but surrounded by many excellent commanders.
Demetrius wrested most of Syria from Ptolemy as easily as Ptolemy had acquired
it. To pour something cold upon the young man’s ardor, however, Ptolemy came to
meet him near Gaza with Seleucus and great forces. There Demetrius, elated by
youthful fervor and determined to fight against his commanders’ advice, was
broken with great slaughter and

himself escaped only with difficulty. Ptolemy took possession of Gaza and many
other cities through his victory, and returned to Demetrius, with generous and
friendly words, all his friends and his entire household equipment together
with a great part of the booty. Demetrius, not at all broken in spirit,
obtained new forces from his father and prepared himself for a new battle with
Ptolemy’s commanders. There he gained a signal victory and, as he desired above
everything, repaid Ptolemy in kind: he amiably returned Ptolemy’s brother and
many of his friends as prisoners, together with great spoil, and added to his
father’s empire almost all the Syria that had been taken from Ptolemy.

Meanwhile, while Antigonus and Demetrius were wholly occupied against Ptolemy,
Seleucus slipped into Babylon with a very small band. The people, having
experienced his greater humanity than Antigonus’s, immediately joined him.
Antigonus then had few forces in the upper provinces, and so Seleucus defeated
them with little trouble and joined Media and Persia to Babylonia. Demetrius
soon arrived in Mesopotamia with a great army, but because Seleucus offered him
no opportunity for battle, he had to return to his father without result; the
great forces of Ptolemy, Cassander, and Lysimachus were harassing Antigonus in
Asia. Safe from Antigonus and Demetrius, Seleucus added all the upper provinces
as far as the interior of India to his empire. Not content with those vast
kingdoms, he turned his mind to occupying Antigonus’s entire dominion, which he
soon acquired in the following manner.

After Alexander’s five surviving commanders—Ptolemy, Antigonus, Lysimachus,
Cassander, and Seleucus—had harassed one another in fruitless wars, all of
them, weary, agreed to peace. Its terms were that each should possess what he
had occupied, the Greek cities should be free, and the empire should be handed
over by everyone to Roxane’s son Alexander as soon as he came of age. This
peace was far removed from all sincerity, for these ambitious and greedy men
played with treaties and oaths as boys play with knucklebones. Perceiving that
the freedom of the Greeks and Alexander’s placement upon his father’s throne
would be his ruin, Cassander had the youth killed with his mother as soon as he
could; he held most of the Greek cities in check with his own garrison troops.
Polysperchon, who had long lain hidden among the Aetolians, learned that the
Macedonians bore the ruin of Alexander’s house very bitterly. He summoned
Barsine and her now-adult son Heracles to himself and marched with a well-
equipped army toward Macedonia against Cassander. Cassander, foreseeing the
Macedonians’ desertion from him to Heracles, secretly agreed with Polysperchon
through private messengers upon Heracles’s murder. The traitorous hypocrite
carried it out, having bargained for the rule of the Peloponnese as his reward.
Thus the whole family of Alexander, the whole family of Philip, was uprooted.
