# CHAPTER XII

## The covenant made with the Israelites was not a subservient covenant

John Cameron asserts in his *Theses on the Threefold Covenant*—an opinion
followed by his student Amyraut in his *Theses on the Three Covenants*, where
he calls it the *legal* covenant—that the covenant made with the Israelites
was a *subservient covenant*, distinct both from the covenant of nature and
from the covenant of grace.

In thesis 45 he says that it agrees with the covenant of nature in these
respects: 1. In each covenant God is one contracting party and a human being
is the other. 2. Each has a stipulation annexed to it. 3. The stipulation is
the same with respect to the moral law. 4. The promise is the same in its
general kind. 5. Each leads to Christ. Then, in thesis 46, he says that it
differs from the covenant of nature as follows. *First, the covenant of nature
was contracted with absolutely all human beings, but this covenant only with
the Israelites. 2. The covenant of nature was entered into immediately with
humanity when created and had no preliminaries; the old covenant came much
later and had many preliminaries. 3. The covenant of nature binds only to the
obedience owed by the law of nature, whereas the old covenant also binds to
ceremonies. 4. Although life is promised in each covenant, here it is
designated by the enjoyment of the land of Canaan, but there by the enjoyment
of paradise. 5. Although each covenant leads to Christ, the covenant of
nature does not do so in itself, but incidentally; the old covenant does so in
itself, for this is its true and proper aim. God did not enter into the
covenant of nature with human beings so that, crushed by its weight, they
might sigh for Christ. Its last and chief end was that what belongs to God
might be rendered to him by humanity. But in the subservient covenant God
requires his right for no other end than that people, convicted of their own
weakness, may flee to Christ. 6. The covenant of nature rests upon creation
and general preservation; the subservient covenant rests upon the election of
the Israelite people, their deliverance from Egypt, and their preservation in
the land of Canaan. 7. The covenant of nature was instituted so that people
might be led gently by it, for it was inscribed upon their hearts; but the
subservient covenant was instituted so that people might even be compelled,
for it begot to bondage (Galatians 4:24). 8. The covenant of nature is
eternal; the old covenant is temporary. 9. The covenant of nature did not aim
at restraining outward crimes, either as its principal aim or as its less
principal aim; the old covenant did aim at this as its less principal aim.
10. The covenant of nature was inscribed upon the heart; the old covenant
upon tablets of stone. 11. The covenant of nature was made in paradise; the
subservient covenant on Mount Sinai. 12. The covenant of nature had no
mediator; the subservient covenant had a mediator, namely Moses. 13. The
covenant of nature was entered into with humanity in its integrity; the
subservient covenant with a certain part of fallen humanity.*

Then, in theses 67 and 68, he compares this covenant with the covenant of
grace. He first says that they agree in the following respects: 1. God is the
author of each. 2. Each is contracted with sinful humanity. 3. Each reveals
sin. 4. Each restrains from sin. 5. Each leads to Christ. 6. Each is a symbol
of God's church. 7. Each is made through a mediator. 8. Life is promised in
each. But he says they differ: *1. With respect to the relation of the author,
for in the subservient covenant God is considered as reproving sin*

[Printed p. 121 | IA PDF 129 | IA leaf 129 | IA decausadei02brow]

*and approving righteousness alone, but in the covenant of grace as
forgiving sin and renewing a new righteousness in a person. 2. In their
stipulation, for the stipulation of the old covenant is, “Do this and you will
live” (Galatians 3:12), while that of the new is, “Believe and you will not
come into judgment” (John 3:18). 3. In antiquity, for the subservient covenant
was added to the promises of grace which preceded it (Galatians 3:16, 17,
19). 4. In the way they reveal sin, for the subservient covenant does not
reveal sin primarily, but through the experience of human weakness in keeping
that compact (Romans 7 throughout), whereas the covenant of grace does this
primarily, for it teaches explicitly that a person is a sinner (Romans 3:9
and following, and verse 23) and that human happiness consists in the
remission of sins (Romans 4:6 and following). 5. The subservient covenant
restrains from sin, but by compulsion (Romans 7:22–24), while the covenant of
grace does so with a voluntary inclination of the mind (Romans 6:16). 6.
Although each leads to Christ, the covenant of grace does this directly and
the subservient covenant indirectly. 7. Although each is a symbol of the
church, the old covenant is a carnal symbol of the Jewish church, while the
covenant of grace is a spiritual symbol of the church of both Gentiles and
Jews. 8. Although each covenant is made through a mediator, the mediator of
the old covenant is the man Moses, while the mediator of the new is not a
mere man but the God-man, Christ. 9. In the old covenant the Spirit of bondage
is given (Galatians 4:24–25), but in the covenant of grace the Spirit of
adoption is given (Romans 8:15). 10. The old covenant was the means to the
end; the covenant of grace is the end. 11. The old covenant terrified
consciences; the new comforts them. 12. The object of the old covenant is a
person lulled to sleep in sin; the object of the new is a conscience terrified
by sin. 13. The old covenant revealed the way to worship God, but did not
supply it; the new covenant does both. 14. The old covenant is a written bond
against us (Colossians 2:14), but the new is a release from burdens (Matthew
11:28). 15. The old covenant is from the terrifying Mount Sinai (Hebrews
12:18–24); the new is from the lovely and pleasant heavenly Zion (Psalm 2:6).
16. The old covenant excludes the Gentiles; the new admits them. Finally,
some add this difference: although life is promised in each, there a life to
be lived in the land of Canaan seems to be promised, but here a life to be
lived in heaven.* Thus Cameron explained his opinion, which does not please
us in the least and which we judge must not be admitted, for the following
reasons.

1. He does not hold that the administration of the covenant which obtained
under Moses was merely a distinct dispensation of the same covenant of grace,
which can be called subservient because it explained the earlier
administrations of the same covenant and prepared the way for the later ones.
Instead, he holds that this administration was of a covenant specifically
distinct both from the covenant of nature and from the covenant of grace. He
thereby forges for us a third kind of covenant between God and humanity which
Scripture does not mention.

2. Scripture mentions only two specifically distinct kinds of covenant. In
one, life is promised under the condition of works; in the other, under the
condition of faith. Nowhere in Scripture do we read of a third condition
specifically distinct from these. Indeed, if some third condition existed,
the Apostle's argument in Romans 10:5–11, 11:6, and 4:4–5 would not be
sufficiently sound.

3. In every covenant, a reward is promised to the person who fulfills the
condition. In this subservient covenant, the reward is imputed either by
grace or as a debt, for there is no third way (Romans 4:4–5). If it is by
grace, that covenant is not distinct from the covenant

[Printed p. 122 | IA PDF 130 | IA leaf 130 | IA decausadei02brow]

of grace. If it is as a debt, it will be the same as the covenant of works.
It is therefore not a third, specifically distinct covenant.

4. He himself says in thesis 43 that this covenant is called *subservient* in
almost the same sense in which it is called *old* by the Holy Spirit, because
it had to grow old, give way to the succeeding and more excellent covenant,
and finally be abolished (Hebrews 8:3). It follows that under the gospel we
are completely freed from this covenant and from everything contained in it,
whether promises, threats, or commands. Thus the law of the Decalogue will
now be wholly abolished, contrary to what was said above in Chapter V. See
Matthew 5:17 and Romans 3:31.

5. It further follows that under the law, while that subservient covenant
continued, there was no covenant of grace. The more excellent covenant
succeeded it, and while that subservient covenant had not yet grown old, given
way, or been abolished, but remained in force, the more excellent covenant
had no place and could have none. What succeeds, and is only to succeed,
another cannot have a place while that other covenant has not yet given way
and been abolished. Yet it is sufficiently clear from what was said above in
Chapter IX that the covenant of grace was made with the Israelites.

This argument is confirmed from his own definitions of the old covenant and
the covenant of grace, which he proposes in theses 81 and 82. There he defines
the former as follows: [*The old covenant is that by which God requires from
the Israelite people obedience to the moral, ceremonial, and political law,
promises every kind of blessing in possession of the land of Canaan to those
who perform it, and most sternly declares curses and death against those who
refuse, with the end of leading them to the coming Messiah.*] But he defines
the latter as follows: [*The covenant of grace is that by which God, upon the
proposed condition of faith in Christ, promises remission of sins in his
blood and heavenly life, with the end of displaying the riches of his mercy.*]

It is clear from this that both of these covenants could not have had a place
among the Israelites, because every kind of blessing could not be conferred
both under the condition of obedience to the moral law and so forth and under
the condition of faith. For according to the Apostle in Romans 11:6, *If it
is by grace, it is no longer by works; otherwise grace is no longer grace. But
if it is by works, it is no longer by grace; otherwise work is no longer
work.* And in chapter 4:4 he says, *Now to the person who works, the reward is
not imputed by grace, but as a debt.* Therefore, since according to this old
covenant every kind of blessing was promised to those who performed
obedience, the reward was not imputed by grace, but as a debt. For if it had
been imputed by grace, righteousness would have been imputed not to those who
work but to those who believe in him who justifies the ungodly, according to
the Apostle in verse 5 of the same chapter.

But if this is so, why did David pronounce blessed those whose iniquities are
forgiven and whose sins are covered? And why did Peter say in Acts 15:11 that
they were saved by the grace of Jesus Christ? Why also did the Apostle say in
Hebrews 11:33 that by faith they conquered kingdoms, worked righteousness,
and obtained promises, and so forth? But enough was said about this above in
Chapter IX. Indeed, in theses 21, 22, and following, Cameron himself
acknowledges that no one's sins were ever forgiven except in Christ alone,
that sins were truly forgiven on his account, that he taught people by his
Spirit, and that through him the church was truly directed. In the following
theses he shows that the principle of this mediation was set forth more
obscurely before Christ's first advent and that its efficacy was less. Hence:

6. If in the time of Moses there was the same mediator and the same principle
of mediation as

[Printed p. 123 | IA PDF 131 | IA leaf 131 | IA decausadei02brow]

there is under the New Testament, the covenant which was made with the
Israelite people was not specifically distinct from the covenant of the New
Testament, or the covenant of grace. The consequence is firm, because a
covenant includes mediation, and through a covenant mediation is proposed and
applied. Therefore, where the principle of mediation is specifically the
same, the covenant is specifically the same. He concedes the antecedent in
the theses just named, where he shows that the difference between Christ's
mediation under the Old and New Testaments consists only in these three
things: 1. The manner and principle of that mediation was proposed more
obscurely. 2. Its power and efficacy was less. 3. It reached fewer people
under the Old Testament than under the New. But this difference in degree is
not a difference in kind. It cannot be inferred from it that this covenant was
specifically distinct from the covenant of grace. On the contrary, it is
clear from this that the covenant was specifically the same as the covenant
of grace; otherwise the difference between them would be much greater than a
merely gradual one.

7. Furthermore, since the administration of that covenant is called a
*testament*, so that the old covenant is the same as the Old Testament, it is
clear that this covenant cannot be specifically distinguished from the new
covenant. Each is a testament, and in each good things are given to heirs in
the manner of a testament. Now good things conferred in the manner of a
testament cannot be said to be granted on another specifically distinct
principle. The good things given in a testament are given absolutely and
freely, not from merit; and they are given by virtue of the death of Christ,
the author and maker of the testament, not from merit. Therefore, since the
Old and New Testaments are the same with respect to their essence and
substance, and since the division of the testament into Old and New is not a
division of a genus into species but of a subject according to its accidents,
it is clear that the old covenant was not specifically distinct from the
covenant of grace. Otherwise it could in no sense be called a testament.

8. If the covenant made with the Israelites was specifically distinct from
the covenant of grace and only subservient to it, then before the time when
that covenant was made there was no covenant of grace. What this covenant
served ought not to precede it, but to follow it: that for which another
prepares the way necessarily follows and does not precede. Yet it cannot be
denied that the covenant of grace existed before Moses, according to the
Apostle in Galatians 3:17. Indeed, Cameron himself will not deny that the
principle of this mediation was set forth before Christ's first advent,
although more obscurely. In thesis 23 he says this is established from reading
the books of the sacred canon called the Old Testament, which treat the person
of the mediator, the way his office is carried out, and thus the mediator's
office itself and its benefits. He explains these things more fully in the
following theses. See also Amyraut, as cited above, theses 57, 59, and 69.

9. The insertion of this subservient covenant disturbs and obscures the order
and sequence of the administrations of the covenant of grace which Holy
Scripture presents to us. Immediately after the fall, the covenant of grace
was promulgated and indicated in these words: *I will put enmity between you
and this woman, and likewise between your seed and her seed* (Genesis 2:15).
The same covenant was renewed with Noah and afterward with Abraham, and all
the fathers were saved according to this same covenant, as Cameron himself
says in thesis 21. Hence the Lamb of God is said to have been slain before the
foundations of the world were laid. I grant that this covenant was proposed
more obscurely before Christ's advent, but always more clearly as the times
drew nearer to Christ's advent, as Cameron himself acknowledges in thesis 28:
[*But, he says, within that obscurity certain degrees may be observed.*

[Printed p. 124 | IA PDF 132 | IA leaf 132 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Before the law was given through Moses, the promise was more obscure; from
the giving of the law until the time of the prophets, less clear; from the
time of the prophets until John the Baptist, clearer; after John's preaching,
open and most clear, when our Lord Jesus Christ succeeded John.*] Cameron
himself gives reasons for this sequence in theses 30 and 31.

We see, therefore, that the covenant of grace originated immediately after
the fall and that its explanation became clearer day by day through distinct
degrees. But this order is completely disturbed by the subservient covenant,
for according to the explanation given of it, it cannot be regarded as a
clearer explanation of the covenant of grace. It is distinguished from that
covenant in many respects and approaches more nearly the nature of the
covenant of works, or covenant of nature.

10. He says that the subservient covenant agrees with the covenant of nature
in that each has a stipulation annexed to it and that this stipulation is the
same with respect to the moral law. From this it is clear that the Israelites
not only could but ought to have pursued righteousness by works of the law and
thus to have established their own righteousness. They ought to have pursued
righteousness according to the nature of the covenant God had made with them,
and this covenant required the same thing with respect to the moral law as the
covenant of nature required, namely perfect obedience. Therefore, they ought
to have pursued righteousness through obedience rendered to the law.

One will not remove the difficulty by saying that ceremonial and political
precepts were annexed and stipulated in this subservient covenant, for their
righteousness would still be legal righteousness. Amyraut acknowledges this
in thesis 23 of *The Three Divine Covenants*: [If, he says, the Israelites had
fulfilled their *ceremonial and political* observances, a part of legal
righteousness would have consisted in them.] He tries to demonstrate this at
length there. But the Apostle expressly charges it against them as a fault
that they pursued righteousness in this way by works (Romans 9:31–32; 10:3).
He does not say that they failed to attain the law of righteousness because
they did not observe those laws perfectly, but because they pursued
righteousness not by faith but as if by works of the law, and because they
were not subject to God's righteousness, and so forth.

11. We proved above that the covenant made with the Israelites was the
covenant of grace. It follows that it was not a covenant specifically distinct
from the covenant of grace.

12. He says that both the covenant of nature and the subservient covenant lead
to Christ, but with this difference: the covenant of nature does so not in
itself but incidentally, whereas the old covenant does so in itself. But how
are we led to Christ incidentally by the covenant of nature? He responds in
thesis 48: [Insofar as it shows what a person owes God and what punishment
awaits one who does not pay the debt, it compels a person to look to the
mediator when he sees himself capable neither of paying the debt nor of
bearing the punishment.]

But since Cameron here considers the covenant of nature according to its
first institution, when it was instituted with uncorrupted humanity—as he
speaks in thesis 58—how can it be said that even incidentally it led to Christ
at that time, when no mention of a mediator had yet been made, there was no
need of him in a state not yet corrupted, and that covenant admits no
repentance? I know that this covenant now leads us incidentally to Christ,
because it shows what we owe God, and so forth, and that it was proposed more
vividly to the Israelites for this end. But Cameron is speaking here about
the covenant of nature considered in itself and according to its first
institution.

13. He says that this subservient covenant had many preliminaries. But all of
them

[Printed p. 125 | IA PDF 133 | IA leaf 133 | IA decausadei02brow]

must be referred to that dispensation of the covenant of grace.

14. He says that life in the enjoyment of paradise was promised in the
covenant of nature, but life in the enjoyment of the land of Canaan in the
subservient covenant. Yet our theologians condemn this way of speaking in the
Socinians, because it is false that the fathers under the old covenant were
fattened only with earthly or outward benefits. Paul affirms something very
different in Hebrews 11:9–10, 14–16, as does Peter in Acts 15:11.

14. He says that this subservient covenant is contracted with sinful
humanity. Yet Amyraut, as cited above in thesis 21, says that the Israelites,
considered as human beings, had almost the same relation as the relation of a
person contracting the natural covenant: [Not only, he says, because they
were endowed with intellect and will, but also because, although they were in
reality sinners, they are nevertheless assumed to be free from sin. For it
would certainly be absurd to require by virtue of some covenant an absolutely
complete moral perfection, free from every stain of sinful desire as required
in the law, from someone whom you openly acknowledged in that same covenant
to be corrupted by sin.]

Thus it is apparent that this subservient, or legal, covenant was in reality
the covenant of nature renewed, and not some other kind of covenant distinct
from the covenant of nature.

15. He says that in the subservient covenant God is considered as reproving
sin and approving righteousness alone, but not as forgiving sin or renewing
new righteousness in a person. Yet it is clear from Scripture that in the
Israelite covenant God presents himself as one who forgives sin (Exodus
24:6–7). He also actually forgave their sins repeatedly (Numbers 14:19–20),
and the sacrifices they offered daily foreshadowed this forgiveness (Hebrews
9:12–14). He also repeatedly promised the renewal of new righteousness, or
something equivalent (Deuteronomy 30:6; Exodus 19:5–6, compared with 1 Peter
2:9 and Revelation 1:9).

16. He says that the subservient covenant does not reveal sin primarily, as
the covenant of grace does, which explicitly teaches that a person is a
sinner. But the Apostle says expressly that through the law comes the
knowledge of sin. See Romans 3 and 7. He also says that the law was added
because of transgressions (Galatians 3:14). Furthermore, since the covenant
of grace primarily presents to us the grace of God in Christ the mediator, it
is certain that sin is revealed in the covenant of grace not primarily but
secondarily. The covenant of grace presupposes sin, just as the Israelite
covenant did, as is clear from the sacrifices and other ceremonies.

17. He says that this covenant leads to Christ only indirectly. But since the
person of the mediator, his office, and the way his mediatorial office was
carried out were described even under this subservient covenant, though more
obscurely, as Cameron himself acknowledges in theses 23, 24, and following,
it seems absurd to say that this covenant led to Christ only indirectly. The
Apostle expressly says in Romans 10:4 that *Christ was the end of the law*,
and in Galatians 3:24 that *the law was a tutor leading to Christ*.

18. He says that the old covenant was a carnal symbol of the church. But how
can that covenant be called carnal when it proposed a spiritual law, required
spiritual obedience, and contained spiritual promises, and so forth?

19. He says that Moses was the mediator of the subservient covenant. Yet
Moses was only a typical mediator, while Christ was the true mediator
portrayed by that type (Deuteronomy 18:15–20). That covenant was also
confirmed

[Printed p. 126 | IA PDF 134 | IA leaf 134 | IA decausadei02brow]

by Christ's blood, foreshadowed in the type, and so became a testament. As for
Galatians 3:18, very many understand the mediator mentioned there to be
Christ himself. See Schoock's *Disquisition concerning the Decalogue*, thesis
9 and following. Amyraut, as cited above in thesis 30, says that in this
covenant the Israelites had no need of another mediator beyond someone who
was merely a go-between or interpreter.

20. He says that the Spirit of bondage is given in the old covenant, as if
the believers under that covenant had not shared in the Spirit of adoption.
He thus takes without qualification what must be understood in a qualified
sense.

21. Nor can it be admitted that the old covenant was a means to the end while
the covenant of grace is the end, because each was instituted to reveal
salvation through Christ.

22. He makes it peculiar to the old covenant to terrify consciences and to
the new covenant to comfort them. Yet very many things in that dispensation
served to create and foster comfort: general and particular promises,
manifestations of God, motives, and even the ceremonial law itself. All these
and similar things served to ease the pain of consciences. Nor is the new
covenant without things that terrify consciences, for that covenant also has
its sufficiently dreadful threats. See Romans 2:16; 2 Thessalonians 1:8–10;
Hebrews 10 and 12.

23. He says that the object of the old covenant was a person lulled to sleep
in sin, while the object of the new covenant is a conscience terrified by
sin. But the old covenant also had as its object a conscience terrified by
sin; nor were those people the only ones who fled rightly to Christ. And under
the new covenant there are very many people lulled to sleep in sin who must be
awakened.

24. He says that the old covenant revealed the way to worship God but did not
supply it. He thus assumes that the fathers under that covenant did not have
the Spirit and that in it there was no promise of the Spirit or of divine help
to worship God rightly. But these claims are utterly false.

25. Amyraut says in thesis 25 cited above: [Neither *faith*, which embraces
the Redeemer, nor repentance is properly required by the law, because, he
says, it is contracted with persons who, although actually contaminated by
sin, are nevertheless assumed to be uncorrupted. Therefore, *an utterly
complete duty consisting in piety, righteousness, and holiness is required
from them*, which undoubtedly excludes every need of repentance and all
knowledge of redemption.]

These things are true of the covenant of nature. We do not deny that this
covenant was in some way proposed to the Israelites so that, convicted in
this way of their sins and their own weakness, they might flee to the
mediator, who was truly though more obscurely proposed in the covenant God
made with them. But they must by no means be said of the covenant Jehovah
entered into with them. As we proved above, that was not the covenant of works
but the covenant of grace, by virtue of which the Jews were bound to exercise
both faith and repentance, for without them they had no salvation or hope of
salvation. Yet they were saved by faith no less than we are, as we showed
above.

26. Nor can Amyraut's statement in thesis 31 be admitted: namely, that this
covenant neither made nor could ever make anyone blessed. First, this assumes
that this covenant really was the covenant of works, made with the Israelites
considered as without sin. Second, it can readily be inferred from this that
all those under that covenant perished,

[Printed p. 127 | IA PDF 135 | IA leaf 135 | IA decausadei02brow]

because according to their principles the covenant of grace did not yet
exist.

27. Amyraut says in thesis 65 that *the legal covenant was instituted for the
sake of the gospel on account of its typological function, and that this
legal covenant was a type of the evangelical covenant, as the land of Canaan
was a type of heaven and Moses of Christ.* But how, I ask, can this be shown,
since the legal covenant as he explains it is the same as the covenant of
nature? See what he says in thesis 53. Where is the correspondence between the
figure and the reality? How could justification by works represent the
remission of sins or justification by faith?

28. By this legal covenant Amyraut does not mean the entire dispensation which
obtained among the Israelites, but only some part of it. For if he had
included the whole dispensation, he would differ little from us, as is clear
from what he says in thesis 72: [Therefore, he says, it should by no means
seem strange if men eminent in learning and piety taught that the nature and
essence of both covenants, the legal and the evangelical, was the same. By
the *legal covenant* they meant that entire dispensation which obtained in
the times of the Old Testament, including both the *evangelical promises* and
the law considered precisely in itself. They saw that the nature of things
must be judged from their principal efficacy. Since the principal force of
that dispensation considered as a whole must be regarded as consisting in the
fact that believers obtained from it comfort, sanctification, hope, and, in
short, everything absolutely necessary to obtain salvation, what objection
is there if that which accomplished the same thing as the gospel is judged to
have had the same nature as the gospel?]

And in thesis 73, after identifying one or two differences between the Old
and New Testaments, he concludes: [But that difference, whatever it is, since
it consists only in the manner and degrees of the things, does not alter
their kinds. Therefore they are rightly thought to depend upon the same
cause.]
