XL. *Paradox* was saying in § 4 of the same chapter 4: *[He worthily and duly
sustains the Province of interpreting the Sacred Books who is able to search
out the truths of the passages to be explained, to detect the falsehoods of
expositions made upon them by others, and to establish by arguments in no way
doubtful that he has detected them.]* Because the *Exercitator* had said
parenthetically that he did not deny that there might perhaps be other
Interpreters besides those whom he describes here, *Wolzogen* concludes from
this on page 124 that his Disputation is empty, since those others must be the
same, he says, as those whom he rejects, and therefore that the new Interpreter
is wholly unnecessary. I confess that I do not sufficiently perceive what
purpose these things serve, nor yet grasp what they have to do with the matter.

[Page 159 | ONB canvas 213 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000213]

For the *Exercitator* says in § 7 that *[his Interpreter must far excel all
the others and be set many parasangs ahead of them at the present time.]* If
these things were true (though they are utterly false), even if he does not
deny that there can perhaps be other Interpreters, I do not see how *Wolzogen*
will prove what he asserts here. But since I do not undertake to defend
*Paradox*, I proceed. *Wolzogen* further tries on this basis to prove the
disputation of *Paradox* useless. Although I indeed judge this exercise of
*Paradox* not only useless but also pernicious and scandalous, I would
nevertheless wish that, when *Wolzogen* sought to prove it useless, he had
employed stronger arguments, lest readers think that he does not treat the
matter seriously but is merely playing at his task. But let us hear the
argument he employs. 2. *[To make this manifest*, he says, *I take first what
the adversary freely grants: that the actual penmen of the divine Writers
cannot be consulted, who, if they could be heard, would be the best
Interpreters of their own Writings. Yet I would not readily grant even this in
the present dispute, that each person is the most certain Interpreter of his
own writing. I will not assert this. Rather, I believe that the sacred
amanuenses themselves, even if they were still alive, could not be regarded as
certain Interpreters of all their own Writings, since they were often ignorant
of what they were writing and at times poured forth oracles with an alienated
mind, etc. I nevertheless confess that, with these and certain other similar
cases excepted, the living voice of all the Prophets and Apostles would be most
efficacious for the truth of Interpretation if it could be heard in the
Church.]* *Reply.* *Wolzogen* is far too ready to take this up, since the
Reformed acknowledge that, just as some are said to speak through their
writings after death, so the Prophets and Apostles can be consulted in their
writings, although they cannot now be heard as they were formerly when alive.
See the most distinguished *Essenius*

[Page 160 | ONB canvas 214 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000214]

in his response to this passage of *Paradox*. Next, he does not speak
hesitantly here, but clearly and positively asserts that the Amanuenses were
often ignorant of what they were writing; indeed, that they sometimes wrote
with an alienated mind or poured forth oracles. The Orthodox certainly do not
speak so, nor did the Ancient Church think so. But let us see by what arguments
he confirms this in *Censure of the Censure*, pages 129–130. He cites Daniel
12:8; 1 Peter 1:10; Matthew 13:17. But these do not confirm what he says. For
although God had not yet seen fit to communicate to them a perfect and
particular knowledge, with respect to every circumstance, of the things which
he sometimes revealed to the Prophets; and although the Prophets themselves,
moved by a pious desire arising from that more general revelation, eagerly
wished to know more about those mysteries—for example, about the advent of the
Messiah, whom they keenly awaited, and about the Evangelical state—yet
*Wolzogen* will never thereby prove that they were ignorant of what they were
writing or that they wrote what they wrote with an alienated mind. He next
cites *Maresius*. But Maresius says only that *[they do not appear to have
perceived the meaning and signification of their Prophecies beyond the mode and
measure of the revelation made to them. Therefore, just as they were generally
taught about future things obscurely and enigmatically, so they saw them only
obscurely and in an enigma, not so distinctly and clearly as those to whom it
later fell to behold the foretold events themselves before their eyes.]* But no
one can say with any appearance of truth that those who perceived the meaning
of their Prophecies only according to the measure of the revelation made to
them, and not so clearly and distinctly as others to whom God granted a
clearer revelation when they were permitted to behold the foretold events
before their eyes, were ignorant of the things they wrote, much less that they
uttered these oracles with an alienated mind. As for the testimony of
*Josephus* speaking about *Balaam* in *Antiquities*, book 4, chapter 6, and
saying: *[Do you suppose that, whenever the fates are to be disclosed, it lies
within us what

[Page 161 | ONB canvas 215 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000215]

we say or do when breathed upon by the divine Spirit? He brings forth whatever
words and Oracles he wills, while we neither know nor think. As soon as he
enters our breast, nothing of our own remains there any longer.]* We set no
great store by this, since it is confirmed by no authority of Scripture, and
*Wolzogen* himself admits that Josephus proceeds further here than he wishes
to go. Moreover, we said certain things about this above and about the
assertion that *each person is the best Interpreter of his own Writing*; there
is therefore no reason to say more here, unless *Wolzogen* either denies that
God is the author of Scripture or that God is the best Interpreter of
Scripture. But more on this matter afterward. It is sufficient to have said
here only that *Wolzogen* openly contradicts himself and emasculates his own
statements, which becomes no one but a betrayer; but a liar ought to have a
good memory. He says that he cannot readily grant that *each person is the
most certain interpreter of his own writing*. Yet afterward he says that the
sacred Amanuenses, with certain cases excepted, would be the best and most
efficacious Interpreters of their own Writings. But if they are supposed to
have been ignorant of even a whit, they could not have been certain, much less
the most certain, interpreters of their own Writings, according to *Wolzogen*
himself in *Censure of the Censure*, page 131. Next, on page 125, he adds this
reason for being unwilling to make each person the most certain Interpreter
of his own writing: the person who wrote or spoke could have been ignorant of
what the Discourse signified according to the usage of speakers, and could
therefore have uttered things contrary to his own intention. But can this be
transferred to the Scriptures? *It cannot*, he says, *because they were
delivered divinely, and in them the author's mind agrees with his words.*
Rightly said: therefore, in this case, where the author has expressed his mind
in the most suitable words, which are not only not contrary to his mind but
agree with it perfectly, and there is no danger that through the passage of
time his failing memory will keep him from recalling what his mind formerly
was, the author of the writings will be their best interpreter. Yet he adds,
*unless God had used amanuenses*.

[Page 162 | ONB canvas 216 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000216]

Thus, because God used Amanuenses in composing the whole of Scripture, that
statement can be transferred even to the Scriptures. And so there is no need
to inquire what the author's mind was, because the author could have uttered
things contrary to his own intention. What more blasphemous thing could be
imagined about the Scriptures and their author? Is this man Orthodox? Is he a
faithful champion of the Orthodox doctrine received hitherto? Because God used
Amanuenses, must it therefore be said that in the Scriptures, although
delivered divinely, the author's mind agrees hardly at all with his words?
What Orthodox man would say this? But perhaps he will say that he thinks this
only with respect to the Amanuenses. Yet let the entire text be read. *[Yet I
would not readily grant even this in the present dispute, that each person is
the most certain interpreter of his own writing. I will not assert this, since
a man's memory can be so liable to failure that, through the passage of time,
he no longer remembers what his mind formerly was when he took up his pen and
reads his own writings as though they were another's. For I deny that the
question here concerns the author's mind. The sole inquiry is what the
Discourse itself signifies according to the usage of speakers. But the person
who wrote or spoke could have been ignorant of this and could therefore have
uttered things contrary to his intention. Let him say, then, what he wished to
signify; every reader and hearer acquainted with the language will declare
with equal judgment what he did signify. This nevertheless could not be
transferred to the Scriptures, in which it is established that, because they
were delivered divinely, the author's mind agrees with his words, unless
(note well) God had used amanuenses.]* Who does not see that these words,
*unless God had used amanuenses*, demonstrate clearly enough that all the
foregoing can and must be applied no less to the Scriptures, because God used
the labor of Amanuenses in composing them, than to other writings; and that,
consequently, it must not be said that the author's mind corre-*

[Page 163 | ONB canvas 217 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000217]

*sponds to his words. This is dreadful. Next, let us grant—what no sane person
reading this can grant—that his meaning is that the mind of the Amanuenses does
not always agree with their words because they sometimes poured forth Oracles
with an alienated mind. Yet, just as this has nothing to do with the point,
since no one says that the Amanuenses must be regarded as the author of
Scripture, so by this reasoning he makes clear that by the words *to be of an
alienated mind* he understands something other than being carried into an
ecstasy, as he affirms in *Censure of the Censure*, page 133, and that he thinks
the Apostles and the other amanuenses uttered words which did not agree with
their own mind, or were perhaps contrary to it. In this respect there is no
difference between the sacred books and other profane ones. But let us pass
over these things, which *Wolzogen*, compelled by no necessity, produced only
to give in passing some specimen of his piety and of a pious—or rather an
impious—mind, and let us come to the argument itself. *[But that* (namely, the
voice of the Prophets and Apostles), he says, *having fallen silent at their
departure, I find only a threefold way of instituting Interpretation about
which dispute can be held. The first might be thought to be opened for us by
the Holy Spirit;
the usage of speech appears to open the second for us; the truth of the things
themselves, according to which the Scriptures are examined, is believed to
explain the third.]* *Reply.* He would have spoken more clearly, more
distinctly, and in greater agreement with the writings of the Orthodox if,
when speaking about the Interpreter, he had distinguished between the Supreme
Interpreter and the Subordinate or ministerial interpreter. But since
*Paradox* appears to be speaking about the subordinate Interpreter, the
question properly and strictly proposed would have been: What is that
infallible norm by which one who Interprets the Scriptures, whether for
himself or for others, best Interprets them? (Although the supreme interpreter
and the infallible and supreme norm are taken promiscuously for the same thing,
because the sentence or pronouncement of the supreme judge or Interpreter, in
the Scriptures or through the Scriptures, is the very supreme and infalli-*

[Page 164 | ONB canvas 218 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000218]

*ble norm for interpreting the Scriptures, or that according to which we must
interpret them, in accordance with the teaching of the Reformed received
hitherto.) That the *Exercitator* intended this is evident both from the title
of his own little book—which is, *Philosophy the Interpreter of Scripture: A
Paradoxical Exercise in which True Philosophy is Apodictically Demonstrated to
be the Infallible Norm for Interpreting the Sacred Writings*—and from his aim.
But *Wolzogen* speaks as if the only question were this: What is the better or
more universal method, or which instrument is more useful for investigating
the true meaning of the Scriptures? But one who thus perverts the question
plays with the reader, wastes his labor, and betrays the cause. Let us see,
then, what he says about this threefold way, as he calls it. *[And the Holy
Spirit*, he continues, *because he still exercises a certain power in the
souls of men, was believed by some to open the meaning of the Scriptures and
to Interpret them for believers. The Exercitator rightly explodes this
opinion and determines that it contains pure Enthusiasm. The last two ways
therefore remain. He himself embraces one, while the other belongs to the
Reformed, who, although they disagree in certain minutiae and acknowledge some
use of Reason and some use of the Spirit, nevertheless return to this: that
they judge the Scriptures must be Interpreted from scriptural usage of
speech.]* *Reply.* 1. Who will now regard *Wolzogen* as a faithful patron or
champion of orthodox doctrine, when he denies plainly enough that he believes
the Holy Spirit opens the meaning of the Scriptures, and indeed expressly
denies in *Censure of the Censure*, page 135, that he acknowledges this opinion
as his own? 2. Who does not see that he excludes the Holy Spirit altogether
from Interpretation here? But he says in *Censure of the Censure*, page 134,
that *he expressly repudiates only that Spirit which belongs to the
Enthusiasts, and does not reject every Spirit*. Yet I now see it confirmed that
*Wolzogen* is hardly a competent, much less the best or most cer-*

[Page 165 | ONB canvas 219 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000219]

*tain interpreter of his own writings, and that he has sometimes uttered
things which, if they are not contrary to his intention, are far removed from
it. But since the question here ought not to concern the author's mind, as he
just told us, but what the Discourse signifies according to the usage of
speakers, let us examine the Discourse itself again. He says that he finds
only three ways of instituting interpretation, the first of which may be
thought to be opened by the Holy Spirit. He rejects this way and determines
with *Paradox* that it contains pure Enthusiasm. It is therefore clear that he
does not distinguish the Spirit of the Enthusiasts, though he names them, from
another Spirit, or the operations of the Holy Spirit which the Enthusiasts
claim from his other operations which the Orthodox acknowledge. Instead, he
regards it as Enthusiasm to say that the Holy Spirit opens the meaning of the
Scriptures. Next, he expressly denies that *the Holy Spirit can be said to open
the Scriptures through that power which he still exercises in the souls of
men*. Therefore, if he could in any sense be said to open the meaning of the
Scriptures, he would open it in some way other than through any power which he
still exercises in the souls of men. Is this what the Reformed teach? Is this
what all who condemn pure Enthusiasm say or assert? Furthermore, is he not
speaking about every operation of the Spirit which is distinct from the other
two ways mentioned? Are not these three ways of instituting Interpretation
distinguished from one another? Does not *Paradox*, who embraces the third way
which the truth of the things themselves is said to explain, exclude the
Spirit, as *Wolzogen* himself says more than once? Why not also the person who
embraces the second way after rejecting the other two? Finally, does the
*Paradoxical Exercitator* acknowledge any Spirit who is said, through any
power which he exercises in the souls of men, to open the meaning of the
Scriptures and Interpret them for believers? Or does he reject only that
Spirit which belongs to the Enthusiasts? Let *Wolzogen* teach us where he says
this. I think that I have read the contrary from *Wolzogen* himself. In what
man-*

[Page 166 | ONB canvas 220 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000220]

*ner, then, can *Wolzogen* say that he does not reject every Spirit but
repudiates only that Spirit which belongs to the Enthusiasts, since he says
the same thing as the *Exercitator*, and indeed approves the *Exercitator* by
saying that *the Exercitator rightly explodes this opinion and determines that
it contains pure Enthusiasm*? 3. *Reply.* How can he say that the second way
belongs to the Reformed? Who, I ask, are those Reformed writers who embrace
it? Let him name even one who asserts that usage of speech is the infallible
norm for interpreting the Scriptures. But since he can name no one, who will
affirm that he stands on the side of the Reformed? The Reformed indeed do not
deny that usage of speech must be regarded as a very useful means or
instrument; nor do they therefore exclude Reason, which they also acknowledge
to be an utterly necessary instrument. But the question here does not concern
a means or instrument; the norm is at issue. 4. What are those minutiae in
which the Reformed disagree among themselves? Or does he think the things in
which he differs from them are mere minutiae? The distance between a *Means*
and a *Norm* is certainly greater than can be filled by any minutiae
whatsoever. 5. He is worthy of the same credence when he says that the Reformed
acknowledge some use of the Spirit and some use of Reason. Do they not
acknowledge a greater use of the Spirit than of Reason? Or do they value usage
of speech more than Reason or than the Holy Spirit? Let *Wolzogen* teach us who
and what sort they are who assert this. 6. Of the same stamp is his statement
that *they nevertheless return to this, that they judge the Scriptures must be
Interpreted from scriptural usage of speech*. Do they say that the Scriptures
must be Interpreted from that usage as from a Norm, or according to a norm?
Let him tell us who they are. They can indeed say that the Scriptures must be
interpreted from scriptural usage of speech, but they cannot say that the
Interpretation of Scripture depends princi-*

[Page 167 | ONB canvas 221 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000221]

*pally upon this usage of speech, much less that they must be interpreted
according to this usage as an infallible norm, or that this usage must be
regarded as an infallible interpreter. Who is like *Wolzogen*, wishing to drag
the Orthodox, even against their will, onto his side and for this reason
inventing that they say and assert things which their own writings testify
they neither said nor ever thought? 7. Finally, it is not our business to
defend *Paradox*; nevertheless, we would wish that *Wolzogen* pressed him more
forcefully. *Wolzogen* promised to prove the *Exercitator's* disputation
useless because he had said that there might perhaps be other Interpreters.
But as long as *Wolzogen* opposes so faintheartedly that Interpreter whom the
*Exercitator* regards as the chief, he strengthens the adversary rather than
defeats him. For on page 128 he employs only this single statement as his
reply: namely, that *it was not worth the trouble to summon this chief
Interpreter from afar if there were others nearer at hand who could perform
the same service*. Is this how *Wolzogen* refutes the adversary? Did he not
know that the adversary could reply that, although there might perhaps be
other interpreters, this one alone is infallible? And we all know that this
*Paradox* leaves no stone unturned to prove it. But we proceed to other
things.
