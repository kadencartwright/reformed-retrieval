QUESTION IX.

THE TWOFOLD STATE OF CHRIST.

*Was it necessary for Christ the Mediator to discharge his office under a
twofold state? We affirm.*

[Marginal heading: Christ’s twofold state: humiliation and exaltation.]

I. Thus far we have treated the *Person* of Christ. We must now treat his
*state,* in which he had to fulfill the office committed to him by the Father.
It is commonly held to be twofold: one of emptying and humiliation, the other
of exaltation and majesty; of sufferings and glory; of conflict and triumph;
of the course and the goal; of death and life.

[Marginal heading: The truth of it.]

II. The *truth* of each is demonstrated by: 1. The oracles of the Old
Testament in which it was foretold. Thus Psalm 8 mentions being made lower
than the angels, which is the emptying, and the crown of glory and honor,
which is the exaltation. In Psalm 110:7 it is said of the Messiah, “He will
drink from the torrent along the way; afterward he will lift up his head.”
Here the torrent denotes the multitude, severity, and brevity of Christ’s
sufferings, just as waters are commonly symbols of calamities (Ps. 69:2,
14–15; 32:6; Job 21:20; 34:7; Isa. 51:17), in contrast with

the torrent of delights with which God gives his own to drink in grace and
glory (Ps. 36:9). The expression *τὸ bibere de torrente, the drinking from the
torrent,* denotes the sharing in the sufferings which Christ had to bear
during the course of this life, which was the way to glory. The lifting up of
the head, however,
indicates the glory that followed his sufferings according to God’s order and
the necessity of our salvation (Luke 24:26; Phil. 2:9). Thus in Psalm 118:22,
the “stone which the builders rejected” is said to be “placed at the head of
the corner.” That is Christ, who is often compared to a stone and foundation
(Isa. 28:16; 1 Cor. 3:10). Rejected with contempt by the scribes and priests,
to whom the care of building the church had been entrusted, as useless and
harmful to the building, he was appointed by God as the Head of the church and
as the cornerstone which unites and supports both walls, Jews and Gentiles.
In Isaiah 53 the Messiah’s twofold state is depicted so vividly that Isaiah
seems more an evangelist recounting accomplished events than a prophet
foretelling future ones. Hence in Luke 24 Christ demonstrates from the
prophets the truth and necessity of each state; and Peter, in chapter 1 of his
first epistle, says that the prophets diligently inquired into the sufferings
and the glory that would follow. 2. Various passages of the New Testament in
which the fulfillment of the oracles is presented (Phil. 2:7–9; 2 Cor. 13:4;
1 Cor. 15:4; Heb. 2:9; 1 Pet. 3:18; 1 Tim. 3:16).

[Marginal heading: Its necessity: 1. on God’s part;]

III. The *necessity* is established on the part of *God,* on *our* part, and
on the part of the *salvation* to be conferred upon us. 1. On *God’s* part,
two things had to be accomplished in order to reconcile God to us and obtain
the fruits of saving grace. First, offended justice had to be satisfied
through Christ’s suffering and death. Second, the gifts of grace had to be
poured out upon men, which was accomplished in the exaltation (Eph. 4:8).

[Marginal heading: 2. On Christ’s part;]

§. 2. On *Christ’s* part, he had to sustain a twofold relation: that of a
*surety,* who would make satisfaction for us, and that of a *Head,* who would
give us life and rule us by uniting us to himself. With respect to the former,
he had to act *for us before God,* doing and suffering all that was owed by
us. With respect to the latter, he had to act *in God’s name toward us,* so
that by imparting the benefits of the covenant to us he might bring us into
its communion. His threefold office also required this. The priestly office
is completed in two parts: satisfaction on earth and intercession in heaven.
With respect to the royal office, he first had to acquire a people for himself
through conflicts before he was raised to the throne and took up the reins of
the kingdom. As Prophet, he first had to be a minister of the circumcision
among his brethren before, being taken up into heaven, he became the Light of
the Gentiles and Teacher of the world.

[Marginal heading: 3. On our part;]

IV. 3. On *our* part, we were oppressed by two evils brought upon us through
sin: *guilt* before God and *stain* within us. Guilt could not be removed
except through the blood of Christ, which was the ἀντίλυτρον, *ransom,* for
our souls. The stain could not be washed away except through the grace of the
Spirit, which was the fruit of his exaltation (John 16:7).

[Marginal heading: 4. On the part of salvation.]

V. 4. On the part of *salvation,* two things were required for its enjoyment:
1. *acquisition;* 2. *application.* For acquisition the state of humiliation
was necessary, because salvation could not be acquired except through death.
For application, however, the state of exaltation was necessary, because it
could not be applied except through the efficacy of life. In the former,
Christ merited for us the right to life; in the latter, he actually confers
life upon us. In the former, Christ’s righteousness exerted itself toward our
reconciliation with God; in the latter, his power exerted itself against the
devil and sin. In both he completes the work committed to him by the Father.

VI. Although Christ was in the state of humiliation, he was not thereby so
abased that he did not from time to time emit various rays of his divine glory
for the ἀσφάλειαν, *assurance,* of our faith. He did so both in the miracles
he performed and in the wonderful events which befell him, such as those seen
at his nativity, baptism, transfiguration, and death.

VII. If it is asked according to which nature emptying and exaltation are to
be attributed to Christ, the answer is that this is properly said of the
Person and referred to both natures, but with a great distinction. It is
referred to the human nature with respect to its real abasement and exaltation
in itself. It is referred to the divine nature only by reason of concealment
and manifestation, in relation to the flesh as a veil by which it was covered
and from behind which it revealed itself. Emptying properly belongs to the
human nature insofar as it took up our infirmities, suffered, and died.
Exaltation belongs to it insofar as in rising again it put off all infirmities
and assumed a glory which it did not have before. With respect to the divine
nature, however, no diminution was made in the emptying and no increase in
the exaltation. Rather, emptying is attributed to it with respect to the
concealment and withholding of glory and majesty beneath the form of a
servant. Exaltation is attributed to it *κατὰ φανέρωσιν, by way of
manifestation,* or with respect to manifestation and exercise, when the veil
of the flesh’s infirmities was removed and that glory which he had possessed
from eternity, and which had been concealed for a time beneath that veil,
shone forth in the Person of the Mediator exalted above all the heavens (John
17:5).

VIII. The Son of God is said to have emptied himself (Phil. 2:6–7), not by
renouncing his deity, for he always remained equal to God (John 5:17–18), but
by concealing it beneath the form of a servant. He also became poor (2 Cor.
8:9), not by losing the heavenly riches which he always retained in his
possession, since the fullness of deity was in him and the treasures of wisdom
and knowledge are hidden in him (Col. 2:3, 9), but by concealing them beneath
weak and needy flesh (Matt. 8:20; Eph. 4:9).
