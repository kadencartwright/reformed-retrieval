I know that our adversary rejects this distinction on pages 20 and 21. Yet his
arguments carry little weight and do not demonstrate that it labors under any
absurdity.

The first is this: “That which is moral in the common sense has such slight
moral character that it has no other than the fact that it is commanded by
God concerning conduct.” Reply: I judge even this sufficient to ground that
designation, since there is nothing in the word itself that can suggest the
contrary. “And thus,” he continues, “all ceremonial, forensic, and positive
precepts—indeed, every precept of God—will be moral. In this sense, therefore,
the question to be debated hereafter is not whether a precept is moral, but
only whether it is a precept of God. Whatever kind of precept it is, it will
always be moral.” Reply: All those precepts too can be called moral, with the
word taken broadly, without any absurdity. For whatever is commanded by God,
insofar as it is commanded, is considered not in the physical category but in
the moral category. It is therefore a morally good action, not an evil or
indifferent one, because God’s command removes indifference, as has now been
explained. 2. It is true that if the question is whether any action is good in
the moral order, it suffices to ask whether or not it is commanded by God.
This notwithstanding, we can debate the nature of God’s precept and ask
whether or not it binds perpetually, whether or not it is founded in the law
of creation, and whether it is a moral precept in the strictest sense or not.
3. He himself will acknowledge that the precepts of the Decalogue are moral,
properly speaking, although not in the strictest sense. It is nevertheless
permissible to compare them with one another and ask whether they all flow
immediately from the same source. We saw above that he himself did this.

He next argues as follows: “It is strange that what is moral *par excellence*
(κατ’ ἐξοχήν) and most moral is, according to him, that which has been
prescribed to the whole human race in perpetuity, from the beginning of the
world to its end, as the Sabbath has been. He therefore does not think that
precepts which flow immediately from God’s nature are more moral than that
positive precept concerning the Sabbath—as it is for him—which prescribes
something concerning conduct to the whole human race. Therefore loving God
with all one’s heart, having no other gods, not worshiping God through images,
not profaning the Lord’s name, not coveting, and loving one’s neighbor as
oneself are not greater virtues than observing the Sabbath? And although the
Sabbath is inferior to the very precepts of the second table, and indeed to
other ceremonies, it is nevertheless not inferior to the precepts of the
first table, since it is moral *par excellence*, beyond which nothing higher
is found—unless once again he covers this wound with a new term and a
plaster.” Reply: These things smack more of childish play than of theological
gravity. The adversary is certainly not ignorant that this term designates
only those things commonly called properly moral. If we say that “You shall
not swear” must be referred to the ten words so called *par excellence*, does
it follow that there is no greater precept, or that loving God with all one’s
heart and so forth is not a greater virtue? He should know that this term is
used to designate the precepts which properly and immediately belong to the
Decalogue, or those ten words which God spoke on Mount Sinai. No comparison is
thereby established among them, nor does this term designate any difference
among them. Rather, they are all included alike under this term because they
agree in this: that they were prescribed to

[1674 logical printed p. 188 | IA leaf 206 | PDF p. 206]

the whole human race in perpetuity, from the beginning of the world to its
end, and that they are founded in the law of creation.

Third, he argues as follows: “The Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 13 that love
is greater than faith and hope and will never cease, while the weekly Sabbath
will cease in the life to come. Is there therefore something more moral than
what is moral *par excellence* (τῷ κατ’ ἐξοχήν morali)?” Reply: This argument is of
the same flour as the preceding one and concludes nothing, as is evident from
the answer to the earlier argument. Does he intend nothing to be moral *par
excellence* unless it will never cease but remain in the life to come? I do
not believe so. Let him therefore concede that the Sabbath is moral in the
same way as certain other things which will have no place in the life to come,
and we will not anxiously contend over the name to be imposed upon it.

Fourth, he continues: “Whereas everyone until now has concluded that something
is perpetual because it is moral, our author, on the contrary, wants something
to be moral *par excellence* because it is perpetual—as though the perpetuity
of a thing, which is also found in nonmoral things, conferred moral character,
rather than moral character implying perpetuity.” Reply: What does he say?
“Let it be positive and indifferent in itself. Yet by the very fact that it is
determined by the Lord’s authority and God’s free command, to that extent it
has ceased to be indifferent and has become necessary upon that hypothesis.
And if it has been determined and prescribed universally—not individually to
some particular polity or only until the time of reformation—it is also
eternal, necessarily to be observed throughout all ages, so that even the
eternal judgment must be rendered in accordance with it; and it is therefore
not only moral but also moral *par excellence*.” He does not say that the
perpetuity of the thing gives it moral character; rather, from perpetuity as a
certain mark—for it is not yet evident what those nonmoral things in which
perpetuity is found might be—he infers the thing’s moral character. Does this
seem absurd?

Fifth, he argues as follows: “What does its perpetuity contribute to its being
moral *par excellence*, since that perpetuity does not depend upon the thing’s
own nature, as though it could not be otherwise, but upon God’s positive
command, which could also have made an indifferent thing perpetual if he had
wished? Is the thing itself therefore by that very fact more moral in its own
nature than when it is commanded only for a time, since its perpetuation is
external to its nature?” Reply: 1. From whatever source that perpetuity
depends, whether from the nature of the thing itself or from God’s positive
precept, it can be a mark of a precept that is moral *par excellence*. 2. We
concede not only that God could, if he had wished, have made perpetual by his
positive command a thing indifferent in itself and capable of being
otherwise, but also that he has made certain such things perpetual, as we will
hear later; and we affirm that they are properly called moral. 3. Even though
that thing is not more moral in its own nature antecedently to the positive
precept, it is more moral than what is commanded for a time only, because it
has a greater congruence with human nature, although it has no moral character
before God’s good pleasure.

He further asks, “What difference was there formerly for Jews who did not
reach the time of reformation between those moral things which were made
necessary for a time because of a precept and those which were to remain such
perpetually? Were the former too,” he says, “moral *par excellence* for that
time? Did a Jew under the Old Testament circumcise with the same obligation
and religious devotion with which he kept the Sabbath? Indeed, did he keep the
Sabbath with the same devotion with which he loved God?” Reply: 1. There was
more than one difference between these two kinds of precepts, as is clear from
what was said above. It suffices now to say that the things made necessary for
a time by a precept

[1674 logical printed p. 189 | IA leaf 207 | PDF p. 207]

were instituted only until the time of reformation and then to be abolished,
a fact which they either did know or ought not to have been ignorant of.
Therefore such things were moral *par excellence* for that time only through
their error. 2. We concede that they were bound both to circumcise, to keep the
Sabbath, and to love God. But we do not say—and need not say—that they were
bound to perform all these with the same degree of religious devotion. I am
not bound to love my neighbor with the same obligation and religious devotion
with which I love God; yet the adversary will not deny that I am morally bound
to both.

In the sixth place, he says that this was contrived solely for the Sabbath’s
sake. “For,” he says, “what is moral in itself does not need to be defined in
this way, since it flows from the nature of God and man and does not need to be
derived from a precept, and specifically from one that is perpetual and
universal, since by its own nature it precedes the precept.” Reply: But since
it is not sufficiently evident to everyone what it means to flow from the
nature of God and man, what harm is there if something more easily understood
is used to describe and explain the moral? Moreover, no moral good precedes
every precept of God. “But now,” he continues, “let someone consider whether
this moral category, which is the sole one of its kind, is not deservedly
suspect.” Reply: 1. Then we ought to regard the very love of God as suspect,
because he said above that, properly speaking, nothing is moral except the
love of God. 2. Who says that this is the sole moral category?

“There are so many ceremonial and forensic precepts,” he continues, “so many
moral precepts of the second table, and so many of the first which are truly
moral *par excellence*. But this moral category, which depends upon a
universal and perpetual positive precept and is nevertheless moral *par
excellence*, is the sole one of its kind: namely, the precept concerning the
Sabbath. It has no affinity with ceremonial or forensic precepts, since it is
universal and perpetual; nor with the remaining precepts of the Decalogue,
which flow from the nature of God and man, since it is positive and yet moral,
indeed moral *par excellence*.” Reply: 1. Who has ever said that ceremonial
and forensic precepts are moral *par excellence*? 2. Who has said that the
precept concerning the Sabbath is the only positive precept—positive to God,
that is—which is universal and perpetual? Can nothing which is positive in
relation to God be natural in relation to us? 4. Although it is positive in a
certain respect, it can nevertheless have this affinity with the remaining
precepts of the Decalogue: that it pertains to the law of creation and is
prescribed there, just as the others are.

“How many kinds of moral things, then,” he goes on, “does the Decalogue
contain? One which is moral by its own nature and is contained in various
precepts. A second which is positively moral, such as the second precept
concerning images, and likewise the precepts concerning the magistrate,
incest, polygamy, divorce, and ownership of things. A third which is likewise
positive but has nothing in common with the preceding kind, and yet is moral
*par excellence*; it is the unique (μονογενὲς) and solitary moral category of
its kind, like the precept concerning the Sabbath.” Reply: 1. We know that all
ten precepts of the Decalogue are distinct from one another, and consequently
that the Decalogue contains ten kinds of moral things. We also know that they
are all reduced to two heads, namely, love of God and of neighbor, and that
the Sabbath is included among these. 2. He himself set forth just as many
kinds of moral things above, indeed more, and he will not deny that they are
all contained in the Decalogue. “It is shameful for a teacher,” and so forth.
3. Indeed, he suggested above that various kinds of moral things occur in one
and the same precept of the Decalogue. For he wished the love of God alone to
be called properly moral, yet there are other things in the first precept of
the Decalogue itself which, though not properly moral, are nevertheless moral
improperly. 4. The precept concerning the Sabbath is not solitary, but has a
sufficiently close fellowship with the other precepts of the Decalogue.

Finally, he concludes this argument as follows: “Who does not see that this
definition of the moral is not drawn from the nature of the moral, but that
this moral category was devised for the Sabbath’s sake, so that the Sabbath
too might appear moral in some way, and that what was sought at the beginning
(ἐν ἀρχῇ) was thereby assumed? It is just as if someone wished to define an
animal which was neither beast nor man and then to infer from that definition
that some single animal of that sort existed somewhere, when instead a
definition of this kind should have been inferred from the existence of such
an animal. Just as this cannot occur between a rational and an irrational
animal, so too no middle thing sharing in both can exist here between moral
and nonmoral precepts.” Reply: 1. Not every description or designation is a
definition. 2. Although this definition or description is not taken from the
nature of the moral which he presents to us, it is nevertheless drawn from
the true nature of the moral. 3. If there were an animal which was neither man
nor beast, ought there not to be a definition of that kind of animal which
would also include this animal? Yet our adversaries leave no stone unturned to
make us admit a definition of the moral which would exclude the Sabbath,
although we can prove in many ways that the precept concerning the Sabbath is
moral, as will become evident in its proper place. 4. Whatever he may say, we
will shortly show that something moral and something positive can occur in one
and the same precept.

Seventh and finally, “Taken as a whole” (κατὰ τὸ ὅλον), he says, “the Sabbath
does not universally concern all people. Indeed, according to eminent
theologians, it concerned no one before the law was given, since it was given
only in the wilderness and was given to the Jews as a sign, in which manner
it was not given to the Gentiles. It endured only until the time of
reformation, like the other shadows. Therefore it is neither moral nor moral
*par excellence*.” Reply: Later, with God’s help (σὺν Θεῷ), we will confirm by
firm arguments the truth opposed to all these claims. Let us therefore
proceed to other matters.
