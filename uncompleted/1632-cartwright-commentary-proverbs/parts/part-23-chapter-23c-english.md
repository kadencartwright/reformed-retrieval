Verse 6. Do not eat the bread of one evil in eye, and do not desire his delicacies.

Verse 7. For as he measures out for himself, so he is. “Eat and drink,” he will say to you; but his heart is not with you.

Verse 8. You will vomit up the morsel you have eaten; moreover, you will have wasted your most pleasant words.

In רעעין (*evil*) there is a metonymy of the effect for a miser, who looks sourly upon the person who eats his food. In לחם (*bread*) there is a synecdoche for an entire meal. In תקיאנה (*you will vomit up*) there is a metaphor from bodily vomiting to disgust of mind.

The purpose is that we should exercise choice in the exchange of kindnesses; specifically, that we should not rashly accept anyone's hospitality.

Just as, in accepting gifts, we must not accept just anything or from just anyone, as is clear from the examples of Abraham, David, and Elisha, Genesis 14; 2 Samuel 24; 2 Kings 5:16, so in regard to banquets, lest we show ourselves too ready to become guests,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 514 | printed columns 1012-1013]

we must take care. And just as our houses ought not to stand open to receive anyone whatsoever in hospitality, for there are people to whom we should not even say farewell or give a greeting, so neither are we permitted to enter every house that stands open.

Here, therefore, we must consider first the kind of people who invite us, and then the intention and end which we set before ourselves when about to feast. As regards people, it is chiefly the godly whom we ought to invite as guests and to whose banquets we ought to be invited, so that we may mutually strengthen charity and, aided by their company, make greater progress in godliness. Although it is not lawful to frequent the banquets of the wicked, yet it is not wholly forbidden to attend them sometimes or invite such people to our own, provided this serves their salvation, as in Matthew 9:10–13, or our peace and tranquillity, as in Genesis 26:30. To this is added Paul's authority, who does not altogether deny Christians permission to feast with idolaters if invited, 1 Corinthians 10:27. And in 1 Corinthians 5:10–11 he warns that association is not to be avoided entirely, except with those who, admitted to the communion of the Church, are held guilty of some more serious crime joined with obstinacy. Thus, since the miser's wickedness is not the cause of our being forbidden his table, we must investigate other reasons. The first is that, through a certain malignity of spirit and excessive attention to property, he cannot bear calmly for guests to taste his dishes somewhat freely. From this it follows that the words of those who encourage one another to go to a stingy man's table, even though uninvited, and devour whatever food he has, are irreligious. They say it is most just that things he does not know how to use should be taken away from him. Yet although he may fully deserve to be despoiled, it would still be unworthy of us to do it. We should rather imitate David's moderation in this matter: although he suffered a shortage of provisions, he nevertheless sent servants to Nabal to ask for something, not to take everything away.

He confirms this reason by a comparison from the greater. For the miser commonly begrudges even himself, much more others, a more liberal meal, especially of delicacies. If someone should object that he is too stingy to furnish a banquet with great abundance or sumptuousness, so that he has little to fear from a more plentiful consumption of dishes, I answer that a miser's banquet is commonly, as the saying goes, by far the most exquisite. Thus Nabal's feast, which he provided for his shearers, is described as magnificent and like a prince's.

An anticipation of an objection follows. The objection is that the miser is wronged when accused of this malignity, since he quite often addresses his guests courteously and as befits a generous man. The reply is drawn from things that differ: he does indeed display this in words, but thinks otherwise in his heart, and will regard you with greater hatred for this very reason, that you comply with his merely verbal encouragement to eat more freely.

The second reason why we should abstain from a miser's hospitality is drawn from the attendant regret felt by one who has reclined at his table. For when he perceives that he has thereby incurred hatred, or sees some favor extorted from him by importunate requests in repayment for the food he ate, what wonder if he wishes either that he had eaten nothing or that he had immediately vomited it all up?

The third reason is that you will have wasted all the conversation by which you desire to entertain both yourself and your host. For he is so miserably afflicted and tormented by fear that his food will be consumed that he has neither the inclination nor the leisure to attend to your words, however pleasant they may be.

As this is truly said of misers, so it may no

[1632 witness | PDF p. 515 | printed columns 1014-1015]

less be affirmed of those who, although sufficiently ready and inclined to give, are nevertheless burdened beyond their means by importunate guests and cannot help feeling ill-disposed toward those who thus oppress them. This may be applied to the corrupt custom of the islanders, who repeatedly visit the house of a woman who has given birth, every day for the sake of feasting, until she presents herself again to the Church.

### Translator's note

The paired Latin retains the witness's *mensa* and *invitenter* in the sentence on going uninvited to a miser's table; the English renders their evident contextual sense. *Insulanorum* is translated literally as “the islanders”; this passage itself does not identify a particular island. The return to church is stated without adding a ceremony not named in the source.
