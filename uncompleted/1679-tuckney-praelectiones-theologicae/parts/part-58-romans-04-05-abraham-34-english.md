It remains only for us to weigh what they bring forward from the opposing
side—taunts, I would say, rather than arguments.

Let the first of these be that he regards it as ridiculous for people to be
cleansed from sin only then, namely at death, when they can no longer sin.
What a splendid triumph!

I give two answers.

1. If they are speaking of wicked people or souls, this is not entirely
true. The scholastics indeed tell us that after death, when they have
reached their terminus, they cannot merit; let them answer for that. But
they do not have my agreement that they cannot also sin. The obstinacy of
the damned against God, their blasphemies, impenitence, hatred and loathing
of God, and the many other things that Aquinas and others acknowledge in
them will always be counted as sins by me. Aquinas himself counts them this
way, though he does not think them demeritorious, but rather belonging to
the punishment of damnation.

2. If they are speaking of believers dying in Christ, who are the subject
of this question, it is indeed true that after death they are raised to
heaven and thus established in glory, so that they can sin no more. But
this is so far from weakening

[1679 printed p. 280 | IA leaf 288]

our position that it greatly confirms it. For if they cannot sin, then the
pollution of sin's remnants has been removed together with the ability to
sin. If any internal habit or tinder of sin were to remain even then, it
would at some time break outward into act, which even they grant does not
happen.

Their second objection is that those who assert that believers are first
cleansed from the remnants of sin at death attribute more to the
believers' own death than to Christ's death.

Away with these blasphemous absurdities! On the contrary, they attribute
this entirely to Christ's death and freely credit it to his merit. But it
must be communicated to believers by degrees: initially in this life, but
perfectly after death in the future life. This accords with Justin Martyr's
*Questions and Responses to the Orthodox*, response 75: “Immediately after
departure from the body, the righteous are separated out, for they are led
by the angels to places worthy of them”—namely into paradise, and into such
a paradise that, as he continues there, “they enjoy the fellowship of
angels and archangels, and even the sight of Christ the Savior himself,
according to the saying, ‘We are absent from the body and present with the
Lord.’” Heaven must receive him “until the times of the restoration of all
things,” Acts 3:21. Departure from this life provided their entrance into
that heaven and consequently rendered them free from sin. But whether this
attributes more to their death than to Christ's own death, I call even our
adversaries themselves to judge. Because they are carried into heaven by
the ministry of angels, as Lazarus's soul was in Luke 16:22, do they
therefore owe more to the angels than to Christ, who alone merited this by
his death? Or if, as Castellio and the Roman theologians think, believers
can through their greater care become free from sin in this life, is
something therefore attributed more to their own exertions than to Christ
and his death, when they credit the whole thing to his death? Thus this
happens to them with death intervening, as it did also to Christ, Luke
24:26, but solely through Christ's merit, to which they attribute everything
without remainder.

Third, they charge that our position forges a new purgatory—not indeed the
Roman purgatory, which they invent after death, but one at death, perhaps
less credible to them.

It is well that they at least do not attach the Roman purgatory to us, for
it is wholly inconsistent with our position. If we are cleansed from the
remnants of sin at death, we have no need of a further purgatory after
death. Our sole purgatory is Christ's blood, 1 John 1:7. In justification it
cleanses us completely from the guilt of sins; in sanctification, through
the work of Christ's Spirit, 1 Corinthians 6:11, the ministry of the word,
John 15:3, and even the assistance of afflictions, Daniel 11:35 and 12:10,
it washes us little by little from their remnants and refuse, 2 Corinthians
7:1, until at death the body of sin and death is abolished together with
the mortal body. The surviving soul then immediately flies to heaven pure
and unstained. Though while yoked to the dusty tent of the earthly body it
had slept among the Chyropodes, darkened by the mold and soot of sins, as
soon as it flies from this earthen cottage it will be like “the wings of a
dove covered with silver, and her feathers with yellow gold.” If Castellio
regards this as a new purgatory, he will be the innovator; we meanwhile
will retain the ancient faith.
