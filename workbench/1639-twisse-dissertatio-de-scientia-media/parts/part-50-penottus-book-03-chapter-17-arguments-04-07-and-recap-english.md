PENOTTUS.

Fourthly, grant that this coexistence of things were possible; nevertheless, it cannot serve Thomas's
purpose at all. For he intends to give the reason why, although things come to be successively and at
some time do not have certain and determinate being, God nevertheless sees them without succession
and sees them certainly and determinately. The reason for this question cannot be given from the fact
that the existence of things is measured by eternity, but can be given only from the fact that God's
knowledge is measured by eternity. Since that knowledge is all at once, it represents to the divine
intellect all at once and immovably the things which come to be successively and in the course of
motion. This is explained still further: just as eternity does not remove succession and variability
from those things which are measured by it as by an extrinsic and inadequate measure, but removes
them only from things which are measured by it as by a proper and intrinsic measure, so the fact that
eternity is the measure of variable and successive things cannot be the reason why things are
understood by God without succession and variation. That can arise only from the fact that eternity
is the measure of divine being, the divine intellect, and divine knowledge, in which there can be no
succession.

RESPONSE.

I acknowledge that Thomas's purpose is to show that future contingents are seen by God certainly and
determinately, while—as you should have added—the contingency of those same things is preserved safe
and sound. And because the consequent necessity attending a thing's existence is commonly thought to
do no injury whatever to its contingency, Thomas therefore holds that contingent things are seen by
God as they actually are in themselves, and that from eternity. The Dominicans think that a reason
for this question can be given from the fact that the existence of things is measured by eternity
and coexists with it as a whole, because eternity has no parts. From this they infer that things have
coexisted with eternity from eternity, since otherwise they would coexist not with the whole of
eternity but only with a part of eternity—which cannot be said, because eternity has no parts. Thus
far I have not found this Dominican explanation of Aquinas's opinion shaken, or

[Book I, printed p. 90; BSB canvas 00116]

weakened, by any probable refutation he has produced. But let us examine your explanation. You say
that the reason is instead given by the fact that God's knowledge is measured by eternity and, since
it is all at once, represents all things
to the divine intellect all at once and immovably. Here, however, I do not know whether you are
deliberately playing with your readers by means of a manifest ambiguity—perhaps even playing with
yourself. For it is uncertain whether you attribute to God's knowledge, or rather to his eternity,
the fact that, since it is all at once, it represents all things to the divine intellect all at once
and immovably. You can easily see how you have been reduced to extremities, so that it would be no
wonder if you summoned some equivocation to your aid. For perhaps, if your position were distinctly
set out, it would immediately fall apart and cut its own throat with its absurdity. What then? Does
God's knowledge, because it is all at once, represent all things to the divine intellect all at once
and immovably? And will you thereby supply us with the reason why God knows all things certainly and
determinately? But what else is this than to explain the same thing by itself? If someone doubts how
it can happen that God knows all things certainly and determinately, will he not doubt with entirely
equal uncertainty how it can happen that God's knowledge represents all things to the divine
intellect at once? Certainly, because God's knowledge is eternal, it follows that no knowledge can
arise anew in God. Hence, if he now knows something certainly and determinately, it follows that he
also knew it certainly and determinately from eternity. Durandus long ago argued in this way to prove
that God knows future contingents with respect to their actual existence.

[Marginalia: 1. dist. 38. q. 3. num. 11.]

“It is established,” he says, “that God knows a contingent when it is present—for example, that
Socrates is running—for otherwise I would know something of which God was ignorant. Therefore, if he
did not know this before it came to be, while it was future, it would follow that something had
accrued to his knowledge, which is impossible.” But who does not see that a proof of this sort is
only *a posteriori*, not *a
priori*, since it does not give the reason whence this comes about? Yet this argument can also be
readily dissolved. For before Socrates was running, “Socrates is running” was not something knowable,
and accordingly could not be known even by God. But you will say from Durandus: therefore, when
Socrates was running and God knew that Socrates was running, he knew something which he had not known
before. I answer: on the contrary, God did not know something he had not known before; he knew one and
the same thing, but as having itself in different ways. For, as I have often warned, it is one and the
same thing for a thing to be present when the time of its production and existence arrives, future
until it arrives, and past as soon as that time has ceased to be, although it is enunciated in one
way and another and is thus known by God. You will reply further: But God does not know things done
differently from things to be done. I answer that this too is true: not differently on the side of
the knower, but differently on the side of the things known. For the things themselves are subject to
this change, but God's cognition is not, since by it he contemplates all things, whether future,
present, or past—all things, I say, in willed being before himself. But if your words are to be
referred not to knowledge but to eternity, as though eternity, because it is all at once, represents
all things to the divine intellect all at once and immovably, then, I say, this is precisely the
Dominicans' opinion: while striving to avoid it as a finish line with your wheels at full heat, you
crash most unhappily into it and owe your spectators a spectacle of ridicule.

For eternity represents only those things which are in it and coexist with it, and with all of it.
Because contingents coexist with eternity, and with it as a whole, the Dominicans infer that they too
have coexisted with eternity from eternity—not indeed in time but in eternity. We have seen you try
this distinction more than once, but have not yet found that you have shaken or undermined it. It is
also far too absurd to say that eternity represents anything to God besides itself. And why would
God's eternity represent things any more than his immensity does? God's essence, which is eternal, is
rightly said to represent all things to God at once and immovably, either insofar as it is the
likeness of things or insofar as it is their active principle. So Aquinas, 1, q. 14, art. 11, in the
body. The essence considered simply represents all possibles; considered as willing, as Durandus
speaks, and as the active principle of things, it represents all future things, and does so at once
and immovably. As for the illustration appended, I answer that, although eternity does not remove
succession and variability from things—nor must those features be removed in order that God may know
them more readily and without succession—it nevertheless provides a reason why they are understood
by God without succession, a reason at least in syllogistic terms. For since all knowledge in God is
eternal, it is impossible for one cognition to succeed another. Yet it is not true, as I note in
passing, that eternity removes variation from things measured by it as by their proper measure. The
divine essence, volition, and knowledge are not without succession because they are measured by
eternity; rather, because they are without succession in God, they are measured by eternity.

PENOTTUS.

Fifthly, in Thomas's opinion, in the same way that all things are present in God in every *now* of
eternity, they are also present to God in every *now* of time. Therefore, that distinction between
presence in the *now* of time and in the *now* of eternity is contrary to Thomas's mind. I prove the
antecedent. Thomas says that all things which come to be in time are present to God from eternity
because the divine gaze is borne from eternity upon all things as they are in their presence. Now,
just as this proposition is true, “God's gaze is borne from eternity upon all things as they are in
their presence,” so this too is true: “In every *now* of time the divine gaze is borne upon all things
as they are in their presence”; and yesterday the divine gaze was borne upon all things as they are
in their presence, and tomorrow it will be borne upon all things. Therefore, in the same way,
contingent things are present to God in every *now* of eternity.

RESPONSE.

An argument of this sort, though advanced at some point with greater fatality, was answered by
Alvarez, disputation 8, number 6. He grants not only that in this instant of time—and thus in every
instant of time—God's gaze is borne upon all things as they are in their presence, but also that in
this instant of time future things are present in eternity, and so too in every instant. But he
distinguishes a twofold sense of such a proposition. “The first,” he says, “is that the determination
‘in this instant,’ or ‘in this time of ours,’ imports the measure of the truth of that enunciation;
and in this sense that proposition is most true, for it is now true to say that

[Book I, printed p. 91; BSB canvas 00117]

Antichrist is present to GOD in the measure of eternity; likewise, yesterday that enunciation was
true, and it will be true for every future instant. The second sense is that the determination ‘in
this time’ imports not the measure of the proposition's truth but the measure of the actual existence
of Antichrist or of some other future thing; and then the proposition is false. For its sense is that
in this present instant all future things exist in themselves and are beheld by God in eternity
itself as coexisting with him and having being in their proper measure.” The man of Novara does not
attempt to oppose any refutation whatever to this Alvarean reply. Navarrete, however, goes further
and acknowledges that determinations of this kind—“yesterday,” “today,” and “tomorrow”—whether they
import the measure of the truth of the propositions to which they are attached or the measure of
actual existence, however the matter stands, nevertheless make true propositions. That is, just as
from eternity, so also in every instant of our time, contingent things actually exist, though not in
time but in eternity. Nor does the man of Novara oppose any refutation to this reply, but rests
content with merely setting out the argument.

PENOTTUS.

Sixthly, Thomas himself declared his opinion in express words when, in 1, q. 16, art. 7, he taught
that truth is eternal only in the divine intellect; nor through this is anything other than God
eternal, because the truth of the divine intellect is God himself. And in q. 20, art. 2, reply to the
second, he teaches that nothing is from eternity except in God himself.

RESPONSE.

Navarrete also proposed to himself the task of solving this Suarezian argument, controversy 52,
section 9, final section. He replies that Aquinas speaks only of truth taken formally, which is found
only in an intellect; and since no intellect except the divine intellect is eternal, Aquinas infers
from this that, if there were no intellect, no created truth would be eternal. As for the second part
of Penottus's argument, everyone readily concedes that nothing except God is eternal. But from the
actual existence of things in eternity it by no means follows that things are eternal. Indeed, it by
no means follows that they have being absolutely at all, but only in a qualified respect.

PENOTTUS.

Seventhly, the argument runs thus. The authors who admit this existence of things in the *now* of
eternity assail Scotus severely because he said that creatures have in GOD from eternity a certain
known being, which according to him is nevertheless a being of reason and close to nothing. Some,
among them Dominic Báñez at 1, q. 14, art. 6 and q. 13, art. 1, and Biel at 1, d. 35, q. 3, art. 1,
attack him so strongly that they call his opinion rash and the error of John Wyclif. But if it is an
error to grant that creatures have this known being in God from eternity, it seems much more
incongruous to say that all creatures are coeternal with God according to their true, real, and
proper existences. For Scotus says that creatures exist from eternity in God himself, not in the
*now* of time but in eternity, and he will answer objections in the same way as they do.

RESPONSE.

Alvarez declares that the existence of things in eternity is brought about by no divine action other
than that by which they are produced in time. Hence there is no fear that they constitute some
eternal entity outside God. This existence of things in eternity is deduced from eternity's peculiar
condition: since it is indivisible, it must be all at once, and consequently whatever at some time
coexists with eternity must coexist with its whole. Therefore, they say, it coexists from eternity,
not indeed in time but in eternity. Navarrete too is accustomed to explain this point here, not as
though temporal things extend themselves to eternity, but because eternity, since it is all at once,
extends itself to all time and all temporal things and comprehends them all in its indivisible *now*.

II. It would have been fair for Penottus to take account of what Navarrete says on this matter, since
Navarrete's controversies appeared in print almost twenty years before Penottus's *Bulwark of Human
Liberty* was published. Navarrete concludes his disputation against Scotus in this way: “Scotus would
have explained the character of the intuitive knowledge presently under discussion better through
the coexistence of things in eternity together with divine cognition than he did through that
production of things from eternity in known being. For whatever Scotus devised so that creatures
might be present to God by a presence sufficient to terminate the knowledge of vision, or the
cognition of creatures in themselves, is understood much more easily and truly in this coexistence
of creatures in eternity. Therefore, we follow a middle way between Scotus's opinion, which holds
that creatures terminate God's cognition according to their being produced in known being, and the
opinion of those who hold it sufficient for intuitive cognition that creatures are in the divine
essence as in the most perfect exemplar. We say that neither is this second condition sufficient nor
is the first required; rather, it is sufficient and required that creatures, according to their own
proper being distinct from the divine essence, coexist with eternity, so that they may be attained in
themselves and according to their own being.”

Whatever may be true of the other authorities produced by Penottus, I take it as established that
Cajetan is dragged by him with a twisted neck and by force into the opinion which the man of Novara
tries to thrust upon him. Penottus certainly put a bold face on it when he dared to say that Cajetan
expressly admits that “things coexist in eternity only when they exist in their proper measure,
because to coexist presupposes existence.” These are Cajetan's words, which Penottus had cited in the
previous chapter as especially pertinent here: “The coexistence of a thing with eternity is made
equal in duration to the measure of that thing's existence, since it coexists only according to its
actual existence. But the existence of the instant in which the sun rises tomorrow is not measured
by some preceding or succeeding instant; therefore, it is not measured by every instant.” All these
words tend only to show that temporal things are not present to eternity in every *now* of time, such
that each thing's coexistence with eternity is measured by every instant of time. Thus Cajetan
affirmed the coexistence of a thing with eternity in time as in its proper measure—not at every
instant of time, but only at a definite one, namely, the one in which it is found to exist. Penottus
twists this as though Cajetan had asserted universally that temporal things do not coexist with
eternity. Again, when he attributes to Cajetan the view that he acknowledged no coexistence of
temporal things with eternity other than one in the proper measure of the things, he attributes to
Cajetan something whose contrary Cajetan asserts with the greatest clarity in the very same passage.
Cajetan proposes a distinction concerning the coexistence of temporal things

[Book I, printed p. 92; BSB canvas 00118]

with eternity either in every *now* of time or in every *now* of eternity. Cajetan's explanation of
this distinction is quite long. Penottus considered it advisable to stop at the explanation of the
first member, which nevertheless contains the opinion Cajetan does not approve. To confirm it,
Penottus appends the following words, to which he adheres, but repeats them in exceedingly bad faith.
“Take note,” Cajetan says, “that temporal things' being present to eternity can be understood in two
ways. In one way, such that they are present to eternity in every *now* of time, so that each thing's
coexistence with eternity is measured by every instant of time. And it seems to me from Thomas's
words that the adversaries took this sense; but they erred, for St. Thomas never dreamed this. Indeed,
it manifestly implies a contradiction. For the coexistence of a thing with eternity”—these are the
words upon which Penottus relies, though he neither cites them faithfully nor understands them
correctly—“is made equal in duration to the measure of that thing's existence, since it coexists only
according to its actual existence.” The man of Novara takes these words as though they had been
spoken without qualification, although they concern a definite measure, namely time, and a question
framed in this way: whether, that is, if the measure of time is in question, temporal things coexist
with eternity in every instant of time or only in a definite one, namely, the one in which they
actually exist. Cajetan's opinion is explicit: temporal things coexist with eternity not in every
*now* of time, but only in the one in which they exist. He further proves it by the following
argument: “If the present instant always—that is, in every instant of time—coexisted with eternity,
then it would coexist with other instants of time, both past and future. Thus that instant would not
be passing away; all instants would in truth be one instant; several instants of the same time would
exist simultaneously; and the past and future would be present, and so forth—all of which are
manifestly impossible.” Thus far Cajetan has been engaged with a perverse interpretation of Aquinas's
opinion, aiming only to prove that it cannot be true with respect to the measure of time that
temporal things always coexist with eternity. But if the words concern the measure of eternity, he
acknowledges that temporal things always coexist with eternity—in eternity, though not in time. Let
Cajetan himself be heard: “In another way, it can be understood that in every *now* of eternity
temporal things are present to eternity, so that each thing's coexistence with eternity is preserved
in every *now* of eternity; and this sense is true and intended by Thomas.” And a little later he
says: “Therefore, to escape the snares more clearly, proceeding from things to propositions and from
propositions to things, the following propositions must be distinguished: ‘All things which come to
be in time are present to eternity’; ‘they are present to GOD from eternity’; ‘they are always
present to GOD’; and similar propositions. For their words either connote the *now* of time or the
*now* of eternity; in the first way they are false, in the second true.” From this you may see with
what effrontery and shamelessness Penottus professed that Cajetan expressly admits that things coexist
with eternity only when they exist in their proper measure. Yet when Penottus drew these words from
Cajetan's explanation of the first sense, he did not understand his author. For Cajetan means only
this in that passage: if we philosophize about the coexistence of things with eternity according to
the things' proper measure, it cannot be said that temporal things coexist with eternity in every
instant of time, but only in the instant in which they exist. Nevertheless, it is only by explaining
the latter sense that Cajetan presents to us the opinion of Aquinas which he declares to be true;
Penottus entirely passed over this sense. He displays the same good faith in reciting Ferrariensis's
opinion. He attributes to Ferrariensis the incredible view that “things are present to God in
eternity only for the time in which they will exist.” What else could this mean but that things are
not present to God in eternity before the time in which they exist arrives? Yet, I say, it is utterly
incredible that Ferrariensis thought any such thing, for this would strip God of all foreknowledge of
future things. But if the phrase means that things are indeed present to God in eternity from
eternity, though not so that they exist at every instant of time but only at the one in which they
will exist, this is precisely the Dominicans' opinion. Penottus confirms it rather than weakening it
by citing the present passage of Ferrariensis as though it opposed them, when in reality it does
nothing of the kind. What he brings forward from Ripa is more suitable to his purpose. Yet I do not
construe what Ripa sets out as being contrary to the Dominicans' opinion in the direction Penottus
drags it, but only as an explanation in an acceptable sense. I do not have that author at hand,
however, and so cannot
determine from the text itself, by sure indications, what he thought about this matter.

PENOTTUS.

When the defenders of the coexistence of things in eternity are confronted with the objection that,
if all things coexisted with God in eternity from eternity, two contradictories would be verified of
the same thing with respect to the same *now* of eternity, they are forced to say in answer that,
although eternity is formally one indivisible *now*, it nevertheless virtually contains several
instants and also virtually contains succession. Hence it can correspond successively, at least
extrinsically, to several distinct instants of time. For this reason, contradictories are not
verified of the same thing in the same respect and under the same description. For these two
contradictories—“Antichrist is in eternity” and “Antichrist is not in eternity”—are not verified with
respect to eternity insofar as it is intrinsically only one indivisible being, but insofar as it
extrinsically or virtually contains succession and several instants of time and can correspond to
several successive instants of time itself.

RESPONSE.

They do generally reason in this way, if not in every detail then at least in part. But Penottus has
not yet shown that this explanation contains any contradiction in their opinion. I freely confess
the truth, however, that the words Penottus appends come very close to doing so: “Things which are
contained in eternity only insofar as it virtually contains succession and several instants do not
coexist with eternity from eternity.” These words, I say, come closer than everything else presented
thus far to a clear refutation of the adversaries' opinion. Only eternity's indivisibility stands in
the way, for by reason of it, whatever things coexist with eternity, and in whatever sequence, must
coexist with its whole.

PENOTTUS.

Finally, Catholics reject Wyclif's opinion. According to Waldensis, book 1, *On the Doctrine of
Faith*, chapters 5 and 8, Wyclif said that creatures have a certain real and ideal being from
eternity, distinct from the being they have in God.

[Book I, printed p. 93; BSB canvas 00119]

RESPONSE.

This opinion which Waldensis attributes to Wyclif appears to be the same as the opinion thought to
be Scotus's. I have already set out above from Navarrete, in response to the seventh argument, how
the Dominicans' opinion differs from it.

PENOTTUS.

And in truth, grant that the opinion could be defended; nevertheless, its manner of speaking seems
entirely inadmissible. For the preposition *ab*, in strict usage, denotes the point from which a
motion or duration begins. Thus, according to the proper sense of the expression, the proposition
“Creatures are in eternity from eternity” means that creatures exist in eternity without a beginning
of duration, and so forth. Neither Augustine, Thomas, nor any other ancient Doctor appears to have
used this way of speaking.

RESPONSE.

Those points would hold if “to be in eternity” meant to be absolutely. Nevertheless, I acknowledge
that the phraseology is exceedingly unusual.

PENOTTUS.

Now, for the solution of the difficulties and reconciliation of the opinions, let the third
proposition be this: Creatures are present to GOD from eternity according to objective being, not
insofar as “objective being” signifies that known being devised by Scotus, but insofar as it signifies
creatures according to the true and real existences they have in time, which are from eternity the
object of divine cognition, at which divine cognition itself truly and properly terminates as at a
secondary object. In the sense of this conclusion are to be understood those who say that creatures
are present to God from eternity only according to objective being. In this sense the conclusion is
in substance the opinion of St. Thomas, for in meaning it is the same as the first proposition
established in chapter 16; it can be proved by the same reasons by which that proposition was proved
there and by which the second proposition was established above in chapter 4.

RESPONSE.

This proposition, not only in substance but also in form, is altogether one and the same as the
opinion of those who acknowledge no presence of future things before God except objective presence.
But to be present before God in this way means nothing other than to be known by God as something
which will at some time come to be. This is made manifest both by the very statement of the opinion,
provided it is stated candidly, and by the way Penottus himself sometimes represents it. In this
passage, the true and real existences of things, which he makes the object of divine vision from
eternity, are called the existences which creatures “have in time.” Yet in chapter 16, number 3,
these same real existences of creatures are called those which “they will have when they have
actually been produced in the nature of things.” No one doubts that God knew perfectly well from
eternity those real existences which creatures were going to have in time. But this is not to know
something under the notion of the present; it is rather to know it under the notion of the future—as
future in reality, I say, though also as present intentionally. To see creatures in this way is not
to see them as determined to one outcome but rather as things to be determined, at least as regards
their existence outside God. I do not deny that God can see them as determined to one outcome within
God, insofar as he beholds them as they exist before God in willed being; but I readily see how little
a determination of this kind suits the palate of our Novarian. Penottus says that this proposition,
which he makes the same as the first—so that he does not so much propose a new proposition, though he
calls it the third, as serve up cabbage twice cooked—can be proved by the same reasons by which the
first was proved there. It is by no means necessary for us to examine them here, lest we too repeat
what has already been completed above. He adds that it is established by the same reasons with which
he says the second proposition was established above in chapter 4.

Come then, let us submit to examination what Penottus argues in chapter 4 concerning that second
proposition. First let us hear Penottus himself. “Second proposition: God knows creatures as they are
in themselves, as the secondary and inadequate object of the divine intellect, in such a way that
divine cognition and the divine gaze truly terminate at the being which a thing has in itself outside
its causes, which is the true and real being of actual existence.” This proposition could have been
dispatched in far fewer words, but whatever else Penottus's abundance may be capable of, he certainly
does not usually suffer from a lack of words. “The divine gaze,” you say, “terminates at the being of
the thing, that is, at its actual existence.” Thus far I acknowledge what you say. But when you add
further that the thing “has it in itself,” I cannot agree at all. For you are speaking of the divine
gaze from eternity. Yet from eternity creatures by no means had being or any real existence; rather,
they were going to have it, as you yourself maintained in chapter 16, number 3. From eternity God had
seen the real existence of all things, but as future, not present; for from eternity it was not
present but only future. Nevertheless, I do not deny that, insofar as this future existence of things
was known to God from eternity, it was also present to God from eternity, namely in known being. But
this is to be present only intentionally, not really. If, when a thing is future, it is seen by God
as present, why should it not, when it is present, be seen by God as past? Indeed, why should it not,
when it is future, also be seen by God as past just as much as present? For it is no more unbecoming
to God to know anew that something is present than to know anew that something is past. Nor is it
more unbecoming to attribute to God a cognition of some thing which begins than a cognition of some
thing which ceases. Therefore, by whatever reasoning God must be said to know from eternity that all
things are present, lest he at some time begin to know something which he did not know before, by
entirely parallel reasoning God must be said to know eternally that all things are future—though they
long ago ceased to be—lest he at some time cease to know something which he knew before. Those reason
more soberly, therefore, who hold that God knows creatures as future only while they are future, as
present only while they are present, and as past only after they have become past. Yet he will not
know things done differently from things to be done—not differently, I say, on the side of the
knower, though differently on the side of the things known. To speak more plainly: God knows that
things have themselves in one way and another, but he does not know in one way and another that
things have themselves in one way and another. I have explained this more than once and made the
argument repeatedly pressed by Penottus and others at their pleasure disappear as exceedingly
superficial and vanish into nothing.
