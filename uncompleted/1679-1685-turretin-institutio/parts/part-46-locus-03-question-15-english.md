QUESTION XV

Is the will properly distinguished into the will of decree and of precept,
the will of good pleasure (εὐδοκίας) and of approval (εὐαρεστίας), the will of
good pleasure and of the sign, and the secret and revealed will? Affirmative.

I. Although there is one and a most simple will in God, through which he
comprehends all things in one and a most simple act, just as he sees and
understands all things in one intuition, nevertheless, because it is directed
toward various objects in different ways, it comes about that, in our manner
of conceiving, it is apprehended as manifold. It is not manifold in itself and
intrinsically, on the side of the act of willing, but extrinsically and
objectively, on the side of the things willed.

II. From this have arisen the various distinctions of God’s will. The first
and principal distinction is that by which it is divided into the will of
decree and of precept. By the former God determines what he himself wills to
do or permit; by the latter, what he wills to be done by us. The former
concerns the futurition and event of things and is the rule of God’s external
actions; the latter concerns precepts and promises and is the rule of our
actions. The former cannot be resisted and is always fulfilled, Romans 9:19:
“Who has resisted his will?” The latter is often violated by human beings,
Matthew 23:37: “How often I willed to gather you, and you were unwilling?”

III. Just as there are various passages of Scripture in which God’s will is
taken either for the decree, as in Romans 9:19 and Ephesians 1:11, or for the
precept, as in Psalm 143:10 and Romans 12:2, so there are also some in which
both wills of God are signified together. Thus in John 6:38, when Christ says
that he descended to do the will of the one who sent him, he means that he
descended to fulfill what God had decreed and to obey the Father’s command.
And when we say in the Lord’s Prayer, “Your will be done,” we ask that his
precepts be obeyed and his decrees fulfilled.

IV. Although the precept also falls under the decree with respect to its
proposal, it does not do so with respect to its execution. They are therefore
properly distinguished from one another: the will of decree is that which
determines the event of things, whereas the will of precept is that which
prescribes his duty to a human being. Thus without contradiction God can will
with respect to the precept what he does not will with respect to the decree,
insofar as he wills to prescribe something to a human being but does not will
to effect that very thing. Thus he willed Pharaoh to send the people away but
nevertheless did not will the people actually to be sent away.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 200 | IA leaf 254 | PDF p. 254]

V. Hence, although these wills are conceived by us as diverse on account of
the diversity of their objects, they are nevertheless not contrary, because,
as already stated, they do not concern the same thing. Certainly, if by the
force of his decree God drove human beings to do what he prohibited by the
law, or if he placed an obstacle before someone trying to obey the law and
called him back from obedience, he would will conflicting things and would
himself oppose his own will. But God’s decree does not conflict with his
command when he commands a human being to perform the duty that person owes but
does not will to give him the strength to perform it, because he indeed wills
the thing with respect to the proposal of the duty but does not will it with
respect to the execution of the event.

VI. The commanding will has two kinds of objects. Some are affirmative, with
respect to which the will itself can also be called affirmative, when the
effecting of a thing is commanded. Others are negative, with respect to which
the will itself can also be called negative, and it consists in the
prohibition of a thing. So too the decreeing will has affirmative objects,
with respect to which it is called effecting and affirmative, both with
respect to its terminus and with respect to its principle. It has other
objects that are negative, with respect to which the will can be called
ceasing and negative—if not with respect to its principle, at least with
respect to its terminus. It is then called permissive, and through it God
determines not to prevent the creature from sinning. For although this
volition is positive with respect to its principle, insofar as God wills not
to prevent, it is nevertheless properly called negative with respect to its
terminus, which is non-prevention.

VII. The effecting will cannot coexist with the negative commanding will,
for God can never will to effect by himself what he has forbidden by his law
as evil. But it can coexist very well with the affirmative commanding will,
for the same one who commands faith decrees to give it to the elect. The
affirmative commanding will can coexist with the negative decreeing will, so
that God commands of the creature what he nevertheless does not will to
effect in the creature. Thus he commands everyone to observe the law,
although he does not effect this in them; he commands those who are called to
have faith in Christ, although he has decreed to deny it to many.

[Marginal heading: The will of good pleasure (εὐδοκίας) and of approval
(εὐαρεστίας).]

VIII. To this distinction of the divine will is added another, by which it is
divided into the will of good pleasure (εὐδοκίας) and of approval
(εὐαρεστίας), a distinction often used by theologians. The will of good
pleasure (εὐδοκίας) corresponds to the will of decree, but the will of
approval (εὐαρεστίας) to the will of precept. This distinction appears to be
drawn from Scripture, which often calls the former εὐδοκίαν and the latter
εὐαρεστίαν. Thus, speaking of the will
of decree in Matthew 11:26 with respect to those from whom God either hides or
to whom he reveals his mysteries, Christ says, “So it is, Father, because
thus it became good pleasure before you” (ὅτι οὕτως ἐγένετο εὐδοκία
ἔμπροσθέν σοῦ), because such was your good pleasure. And in Ephesians 1:5 Paul
says that God predestined us “according to the good pleasure of his will”
(κατὰ τὴν εὐδοκίαν τοῦ θελήματος αὐτοῦ). Likewise, in verse 9 the form
εὐδοκίας is mentioned in this sense. As for approval (εὐαρεστίαν), it is not
rarely referred to the
will of precept, which is also called the will of approval and complacency.
Thus in Romans 12:2 the will of God to which we ought to be conformed is
called good and acceptable (εὐάρεστος). And Ephesians 5:10 says,
δοκιμάζοντες τί ἐστιν εὐάρεστον τῷ Θεῷ, “discerning what is pleasing to
God”; Colossians 3:20 says, τοῦτο γὰρ ἐστιν εὐάρεστον τῷ Κυρίῳ, “for this is
acceptable to the Lord.” In this sense εὐαρεστία indicates the commanding
and approving will, by which God declares what is pleasing to him and what he
wills human beings to do. But εὐδοκία indicates the decreeing will, by which
God declares his good pleasure concerning the things he has determined to
execute himself.

IX. The distinction, therefore, must not be understood as though what depends
upon God’s εὐδοκία were not also acceptable (εὐάρεστον) to him. For whatever
God has decreed to be done is truly pleasing to him in that respect, nor can
he be said to will to execute something that it does not please him to
execute. Rather, the distinction can be received only insofar as it reduces
to the will of decree and of precept, so that the will of good pleasure
(εὐδοκίας) designates

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 201 | IA leaf 255 | PDF p. 255]

precisely that will by which God decrees to do or permit something and for
which, in most cases, you can give no other reason except that it so pleased
God. The will of approval (εὐαρεστίας), by contrast, is that by which he wills to set
before the creature its duty as a thing pleasing to himself and in which he
takes pleasure.

X. Although we sometimes confound good pleasure (εὐδοκίαν) with approval
(εὐαρεστίᾳ) when it is spoken
of those in whom God delights because there is in them some quality or
condition that accords with God’s nature and therefore wins his favor—as the
Father says of Christ in Matthew 3:17, “in whom I am well pleased” (ἐν ᾧ
εὐδόκησα), that is, in whom I delight most intensely—and although this can
also be referred, not improperly, to the will of good pleasure, which Christ
most perfectly fulfilled by satisfying God’s justice, nevertheless the former
is here taken strictly for the decree, for which no reason outside God’s good
pleasure is to be sought.

XI. Approval (Εὐαρεστία), when contrasted with good pleasure (εὐδοκίαν) in
this argument, signifies
nothing other than the bare complacency by which God approves some thing as
just and holy and delights in it, and therefore wills to command it to the
creature as its most just duty. Hence it does not properly include a decree
or any volition in God but indicates only the thing’s agreement with God’s
nature, according to which he cannot fail to love what is consonant with his
holiness. For the approval of something is not immediately a volition of it,
nor, if I approve something, do I therefore at once will it. Consequently,
this is less properly called God’s will.

XII. Although promises of salvation to be given to believers, which are
proposed together with the evangelical precept, also belong to the will of
approval (εὐαρεστίας), it does not follow that this will must connote some decree or
properly so-called conditional volition to give salvation to all. For such a
decree could not coexist with the decree of reprobation or with God’s wisdom,
since it is contrary to his wisdom to will something under a condition that
is impossible and that God himself has decreed to deny to the creature, he
alone being able to give it. Only this can be gathered from it: that there is
an inseparable connection between faith and salvation established by God
himself, so that no one who does not have faith can obtain salvation, and no
one who has faith can fail most certainly to attain salvation. Thus the
promises added to the precepts signify only what God will give to believers
and the penitent, not what he wills to give to all those to whom the precept
is proposed.

[Marginal heading: The will of the sign and of good pleasure.]

XIII. The third distinction of the will is into the will of the sign and of
good pleasure. This distinction was first introduced by Hugh of St. Victor,
then adopted by Lombard in book 1 of the *Sentences*, distinction 45, commonly
used by all the scholastics and especially by Thomas in part 1, question 19,
articles 11 and 12, and retained by our theologians with a proper
explanation.

XIV. The scholastics call the will of good pleasure that which lies hidden
in God for a time and is left to his most free power until it is disclosed by
some oracle or by the event itself. They call the will of the sign that which
becomes known to us through some sign, such as a precept or a promise, and
which denotes some effect outside God that is a sign of his will. Hence the
scholastics customarily enumerate five signs by which God’s will becomes
known: precept, prohibition, counsel, permission, and operation. They are
comprised in this little verse:

He commands and prohibits, permits, counsels, fulfills;

But this is wrong: 1. because there are more such signs, such as promises and
threats,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 202 | IA leaf 256 | PDF p. 256]

prophecies, and narratives; 2. operation is not a sign of the will but its
effect, and belongs to the will of good pleasure; 3. permission does not fall
under the sign but under good pleasure; and 4. counsel is either referred to
good pleasure or included in the precept.

XV. The will of good pleasure is therefore better defined by us as what is
elsewhere called the decreeing will, which is nothing other than the decree
of God’s good pleasure concerning things outside himself that are to be
future. But the will of the sign is what is called the commanding and
approving will, which prescribes a human being’s duty and explains what is
pleasing to God.

XVI. It is called the will of the sign not because it signifies what God
wills to do concerning or with a human being, for in this sense it would not
be distinguished from the will of good pleasure. Rather, it is so called
because it signifies what God wills to command a human being as pleasing to
himself and as that person’s just duty. Thus this will is properly said to
fall under the decree and good pleasure with respect to the command of the
thing, but not with respect to the thing commanded, because God commands
nothing in time that he did not decree from eternity to command.

XVII. When the will of good pleasure is contrasted with the will of the sign,
the term “good pleasure” is not taken broadly, insofar as it denotes simple
complacency in and approval of the thing or the decree to command it. For in
this sense the will of the sign can also be called the will of good pleasure,
because it concerns things God approves and decrees to command the creature.
Rather, it is taken strictly, insofar as it denotes God’s pleasure or decree
concerning the effecting or permitting of something, just as decrees of a
court are called judgments.

XVIII. There can be no contrariety between these two wills, because they do
not will and refuse to will the same thing in the same manner and respect.
The will of good pleasure is the will of event and execution; the will of the
sign is the will of duty and of obligation to it. Thus by the will of the sign
God willed the sacrifice of Isaac with respect to its being commanded—that
is, that he should command it to Abraham in order to test his obedience. But
by the will of good pleasure God did not will it with respect to the event
itself, because he had decreed to prevent that slaying. Now although these two
acts of the divine will are diverse—“I will to command Abraham to sacrifice
his son” and “I do not will this son to be sacrificed”—they are nevertheless
not contrary. For both were true: that God had decreed to command this to
Abraham and likewise had decreed to prevent its effecting. Thus without
contrariety God willed Isaac to be offered and not to be offered: he willed
it with respect to the precept but did not will it with respect to the
effect. God’s entire will concerning this deed was neither only to command
Abraham to make that offering nor only to prevent it, but must comprise these
two diverse acts. The former is affirmative and concerns the command of the
thing; the latter is negative and concerns its prevention. Nor does it follow
from this that a human being is commanded to believe what is false, because
we are commanded to believe only what is revealed and as it is revealed. But
the event is not revealed through commands of this kind; only the duty and
obligation to it are revealed.

XIX. Although God is said to will the salvation of all by the will of the sign
and not to will it by the will of good pleasure, there is nevertheless no
conflict. For, besides the fact that the universal proposition must be
understood not so much of every individual in the classes as of the classes
of individuals, the former will concerns only God’s approval and his command
of the duty, whereas the latter concerns its futurition and fulfillment. The
former denotes what is pleasing to God and what he has determined to command
a human being for obtaining salvation; the latter denotes what God himself
has decreed to do. These two things do not conflict: to will to call to faith
and salvation and nevertheless not to will to give faith itself or salvation;
that is, to will to command a human being to believe and not to will—that is,
to decree—not to give him the faith by which he might believe.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 203 | IA leaf 257 | PDF p. 257]

XX. The will of the sign that is proposed externally must correspond to some
internal will in God, lest it be a false and deceptive sign. But that internal
will is not a decree to give salvation to this or that person; it is a decree
to command faith and to promise salvation if the person believes. This rests
both upon the connection established by God between faith and salvation and
upon God’s internal disposition, by which, as he loves himself, he cannot
fail to love his image wherever he sees it shining forth, and thus to delight
in the creature’s faith and repentance to such an extent that he endows it
with salvation.

XXI. The promised salvation proposed through the will of the sign does not
properly and directly fall under the precept, because in their formal
character a promise and a precept differ: the former declares a benefit, but
the latter a duty. Yet by reduction and secondarily it can pertain to the
precept, insofar as it is added to the precept as a spur, a means and motive
for arousing the obedience of the person to whom the precept is given. And
although the promise must have some foundation for the certainty of its event
in order to move a person, this foundation must not be sought from God’s
decree concerning particular persons to confer salvation upon all, which
cannot coexist with the decree of reprobation. Rather, it must be sought from
his decree concerning the things themselves—that is, from the ordering and
inseparable connection made by God between faith and salvation as between
the means and the end. For this is why salvation is promised with complete
certainty in the gospel to all believers: by God’s good pleasure there is so
close a connection between faith and salvation that no one can have the
former without also possessing the latter.

XXII. To that external word which is the sign—for example, “Everyone who
believes in Christ will be saved”—there corresponds some internal word or
thing signified, namely, God’s will inseparably to connect faith in Christ
with salvation and to propose such an order and way of salvation to a human
being. But it cannot be a conditional will to save each and every person
under this condition, because God would thereby testify that he wills
something he truly does not will toward those passed over, to whom he denies
the condition.

XXIII. Although the will of the sign signifies that God is merciful, it does
not follow that he must be merciful with respect to each and every person,
but only with respect to those who will have the condition expressed by that
will, namely, believers. Since these are none other than the elect, the mercy
expressed by this will properly pertains only to them. Add that, since this
will of the sign has never been universal with respect to each and every
person, even though it has been general and common with respect to all peoples
and conditions, the mercy signified through it cannot be universal.

XXIV. If through this will God signified that he wills the salvation of each
and every person, he would signify that he wills what he by no means wills,
since by passing over many he was unwilling to endow them with salvation. But
when he signifies that he wills the salvation of all believers and the
penitent, he signifies that he wills what he truly wills, and nothing is
truer or more sincere than this declaration.

[Marginal heading: The secret and revealed will.]

XXV. The fourth distinction of the will is into the secret and revealed will.
The former is commonly referred to the will of decree, which for the most
part lies hidden in God; the latter, to the will of precept, which is revealed
and made plain in the law and the gospel. Its foundation is drawn from
Deuteronomy 29:29: “The secret things belong to our God, but the revealed
things belong to us and to our children, that we may do the words of this
law.” The former is called a profound and unsearchable abyss, Psalm 36:6 and
Romans 11:33, 34. The latter is set before everyone and is not far from us,
Deuteronomy 30:14 and Romans 10:8. The former has as its object everything
God wills either to effect or to permit, and what he wills in particular to
do concerning individual human beings,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 204 | IA leaf 258 | PDF p. 258]

which things are therefore absolute and determined in one direction. The
latter concerns the things that are our duty and that are proposed
conditionally. The former is always done; the latter is often violated.

XXVI. The will is called secret not because it always lies hidden from us and
is nowhere revealed. For God not rarely discloses to human beings in the Word
certain secret things of his counsel, and it also becomes plain from the
event itself. Rather, it is so called because it lies hidden in God until it
is disclosed by some indication, such as an oracle or the event.

XXVII. Although the secret will concerning our election remains hidden in
God, it does not follow that we can have no certainty of salvation. For
although it cannot be had *a priori*, it can nevertheless be had *a
posteriori*.

XXVIII. Whatever Christ willed to be done in time by human beings as a duty
according to the law and the gospel, he also revealed in time. But he did not
immediately reveal whatever Christ decreed by his good pleasure to be done by
himself, which God for the most part reserves to himself and does not disclose
to us except from the event, Acts 1:7 and Mark 13:32.

XXIX. Although God is supremely good, it is not necessary that by an
antecedent will he will well toward everyone unto salvation, because the
exercise of his goodness depends upon his most wise will, which has mercy on
whomever he wills and hardens whomever he wills. Nor, if he wills to diffuse
his goodness toward the creature through the benefit of creation and
providence, must he therefore at once will well toward it unto salvation.
