## QUESTION XIII

### Whether and why Christ celebrated his last Passover one night earlier than the other Jews

The question of the true day on which Christ celebrated his last Passover is notable, ancient, and sufficiently difficult. Yet I would not profess with Spalatensis, book 5 *On the Republic*, chapter 6, paragraph 260, that this doubt is so immense that its knot, if any in the Gospels, is most difficult to untie.

There are three principal opinions on this matter. The first holds that Christ anticipated the legal day of Passover, which was the fourteenth day of the moon, or the fourteenth day of the first month, Nisan, so that Christ celebrated his last Passover on the thirteenth day of this month, one day before the other Jews. The second is the opinion of those who teach that the Lord celebrated his Passover on the ordinary day, the fourteenth of the moon, together with the other Jews. The third, which is ours, holds that Christ indeed anticipated the Jews by one day, but in such a way that he observed the legal fourteenth day, while they observed the following day, the fifteenth.

The authors and defenders of the first opinion are the more recent Greeks. I say the more recent because the older writers Chrysostom, Epiphanius, Theophylact, and others hold the third opinion. The most ancient Greeks, moreover, were so far from this view of their successors that for their persistent celebration of Passover on the fourteenth day of the moon they were branded with the names Quartodecimans and Τεσσαρεσκαιδεκατιτῶν, and were even placed by Philastrius and Augustine in the catalogue of heretics. This was not so much because they celebrated Passover with Christ on the fourteenth of Nisan as because, in accordance with the Mosaic law, they imposed on all Christians the necessity of celebrating on that day.

For very many years an exceedingly bitter dispute on this matter prevailed and nearly separated the Eastern churches from the Western by a shameful schism. Indeed, had the great moderation and no less great authority of Irenaeus of Lyons not restrained the proud fervor of Victor, the Roman pontiff, when he was ready to strike all the Easterners with the weapon of excommunication, the whole church seemed about to be consumed at once by that fire. At last the Council of Nicaea—

—extinguished that flame by framing a canon (which, however, does not survive today) that Passover should be celebrated only on the Lord’s Day on which the Lord rose, whether it fell on the fourteenth or immediately followed it.

Some sparks of this discord were nourished long after the Council of Nicaea, especially in the more remote churches and in Britain. There our forebears, the Scots and Picts, tenaciously followed the Easterners’ fourteenth day, while the English, then recent arrivals on the island, championed the Westerners’ Sunday no less vigorously. In his observations on Epiphanius’s heresy 51, Petavius appears, out of his Jesuit favor for the Roman party, which the English then followed, not to present the opinion and arguments of the Scots with sufficient fairness in his narration of this controversy. It is certainly regrettable that disputes about ceremonies troubled the churches for so long, since they could very easily have been suppressed at their first appearance by the complete omission and neglect of those altogether unnecessary ceremonies, as occurs today among many of the orthodox.

In any case, five or six centuries ago the modern Easterners called this ceremonial controversy back from the dead, with the state of the dispute somewhat altered, and prosecuted it against the Westerners with excessive bitterness. I do not know whether the dispute continues to this day. I do see, however, that its continued life against the Westerners is suggested plainly enough in the censure of chapter 13 of the Augsburg Confession by Jeremias, patriarch of Constantinople, whom Socolovius defends at length against the Easterners in his notes there. Yet in the recent confession of Cyril the patriarch there is no word about this matter. For that ὀρθοδοξότατος, most orthodox, patriarch, who was most closely united with the Reformed Protestant churches against the Roman papists, strove with the greatest effort to calm and extinguish by his notable and successful labor all the controversies between Eastern and Western Protestants, not only in rites and matters of lesser moment, but especially in doctrine and weightier matters. Yet through this labor the wicked arts of the Jesuits procured for him a cruel death but a most glorious martyrdom.

The first form of the present controversy, which the more recent Greeks prosecuted against the Latins, concerned the quality of the Eucharistic bread: whether it ought to be unleavened or leavened. At first the Latins as well as the Greeks used leavened bread, while the Ebionites and other Judaizing heretics contended too strongly for Mosaic rites. But when those heretics were dead and buried, most, if not all, everywhere in the church celebrated the Supper with unleavened bread. There was silence about this as an indifferent matter until after the year one thousand. Soon, however, the Roman Church, not content to celebrate with unleavened bread herself, far too imperiously commanded all others to follow her in this rite. Hence the Greek Church, which had long begun to recoil from the arrogance of the Roman party in many other matters, commanded all her people with no less imperiousness to use leavened bread. To establish this thesis with a firm argument, she also began to teach that Christ had celebrated his Supper with leavened bread. Both Greeks and Latins agreed on this principle: the Supper should be celebrated with the kind of bread the Lord himself used at his first Supper. Would that they had carefully observed this rule in the most necessary and supremely essential parts of the Supper. In that case the Roman party would never have dared to cast from the Supper its principal constituent parts, namely the cup and the substance of bread itself. In any case, no necessity compels us to imitate Christ in merely accidental circumstances of the first Supper. Thus, although Christ celebrated with unleavened bread—as he indeed did—this imposes no necessity of unleavened bread on all Christians. And although the bread of the first Supper had been leavened, that first practice would have compelled no one to use leavened bread afterward.

The Greeks were more fervent here than the Latins. In the Councils of Florence and even of Trent, the latter do not condemn the use of leavened bread outright, provided liberty is permitted to their churches. But the Greeks wished all to follow their custom as necessary and denied that the Latins’ unleavened bread was true bread. Certainly, for several centuries now the unleavened breads of the masses have been such thin sheets that one who denied they were bread would not greatly err. Yet to deny that all unleavened things are bread because they are unleavened contradicts sense no less than reason.

The principal argument by which the Greeks proved the necessary use of leavened bread was Christ’s practice, which they thought they could demonstrate irrefragably from the matter before us. Everyone grants that from the first day of Unleavened Bread until the seventh day thereafter there was no use of leaven among the Jews, but all likewise grant that before the first day of Unleavened Bread, leavened bread, ἔνζυμον, was common. Thus, if the Greeks proved that Christ celebrated his Supper and last Passover before the first day of Unleavened Bread, they thought they had established their entire position firmly enough. Aquinas and others deny this consequence to the Greeks. They affirm that although Christ celebrated his last Passover and last Supper on the thirteenth day, it does not therefore follow that he celebrated with leavened things, ἐνζύμοις. For although leavened bread, ἔνζυμον, was common to the whole people and the law did not require unleavened bread until the following day, they could easily have found unleavened bread among the things commonly leavened, ἐνζύμων. If it had seemed good to the Lord to celebrate his Passover before the days of Unleavened Bread, nothing would have been easier for him or anyone else than to find, on the day before Unleavened Bread, unleavened bread among ordinary leavened things, ἐνζύμω, with which he could have celebrated his Passover according to God’s inviolable law.

But the antecedent is what all the Latins and we—

—deny and the Greeks attempt to prove. First, from John 13:1, where it is affirmed that Christ celebrated his last Passover, and thus the sacred Supper, before the feast of Passover. Reply: John is speaking there of the feast of Passover as it was celebrated by the Jews that year, not on the legal fourteenth day but on the fifteenth, as will appear afterward. That Christ celebrated his Passover on the legal fourteenth day, disregarding the Jews’ tradition concerning the postponement of Passover to the fifteenth, is evident from the other three evangelists, Matthew 26:17, Mark 14:12, and Luke 24:7. There it is expressly stated that Christ celebrated his last Passover on the first day of Unleavened Bread, which in different respects was the fourteenth and the fifteenth, but in no way the thirteenth. The Greeks reply that the first three evangelists, who erred in the circumstance of time, were corrected by John. Rejoinder: this is manifest blasphemy against the sacred Scriptures. It is true that many very learned and candid Latin writers charge the Greeks with it, Thomas Aquinas among others. But since they cite no Greek author of this blasphemy, and since Bellarmine and the other most bitter adversaries of the Greeks confess that nothing of the kind is found in Euthymius and other Greek principal defenders of this controversy, this undeserved Latin charge—if not a slander, certainly an excessively rash and injurious oversight—may be passed over.

Second, the Greeks argue from John 18:28. When Christ was judged by Pilate, the Jews had not yet eaten their Passover, which according to the law was to be eaten on the fourteenth of Nisan, whereas Christ had celebrated his on the preceding day, which must therefore have been the thirteenth. Reply: if the Jews that year had observed the legal day and had not, according to their traditions and contrary to the law, transferred the celebration of Passover from the fourteenth to the fifteenth, the argument would have been insoluble. But we shall soon see that the Jews that year celebrated not on the legal fourteenth but on the fifteenth.

Third, the Greeks argue from John 19:31. The day on which Christ was crucified was the παρασκευὴ, or preparation, for Passover, which was celebrated on the fourteenth day. Therefore Christ’s Passover and Supper, which were celebrated on the preceding day, necessarily fell on the thirteenth. Reply: as above, this παρασκευὴ was not for the true legal Passover but for the Jews’ traditional Passover, which that year was one day later than the lawful one.

We, however, argue against the Greeks as follows. As Chrysostom has it on Matthew 26:2, Christ always observed God’s law exactly, on the day of Passover as in all things. He would have sinned against the law if he had drawn the celebration of Passover back from the fourteenth day to the thirteenth. The Greeks reply that Christ was compelled by necessity, because his death was imminent, to anticipate the day of Passover, and also did so to fulfill the Passover type, so that he, the true Passover, might be sacrificed on the cross at the very time when the typical Passover was offered in the temple. Rejoinder 1: he in whose hand are all things disposes each one at so opportune a time that no necessity arising from any time presses him to violate the law. 2: since Christ in fact observed the law of Passover exactly on the fourteenth day between the two evenings, he arranged everything suitably so that his death not only occurred on the day of Passover itself, which began at the second evening of the fourteenth day and continued to the end of the fifteenth, but also so that he hung on the cross during the very hours when the Passover lamb was sacrificed by the priests in the temple. Yet this was not required in the correspondence of antitype to type, for Christ’s death was the most perfect antitype of the daily morning sacrifice and of all the sacrifices offered on any day of the year and at any hour of any day. Christ was not therefore obliged to die in the morning or at every hour when any ordinary or extraordinary sacrifice was slain.

We argue again that the first day of Unleavened Bread was the fifteenth day of the month, although it is sometimes called the fourteenth day, as at Exodus 12:18. This must be observed carefully in elucidating various passages of Scripture, for sometimes the day of Passover and the first day of Unleavened Bread are joined as one and the same day, the fourteenth. At other times they are separated as two distinct days, with the fourteenth assigned to Passover and the fifteenth to the first day of Unleavened Bread. The reason is this: the Hebrews’ days began with the nights, and so the fifteenth of Nisan began at sunset on the fourteenth day. What was therefore done at sunset on that fourteenth day is rightly referred either to the fourteenth or to the fifteenth. Now the Passover was commanded to be slain in the evening of the fourteenth day, which was not the beginning but the end of that day, for immediately after the lamb was eaten, in the early morning of the fifteenth day, the people of Israel were driven out of Egypt, Exodus 12:33. When at Leviticus 23:5 the Passover is said to be prepared between the two evenings, take care not to understand this so broadly that it could have been prepared at any time between the first evening of that day, which began at sunset on the thirteenth day, and the second, which ended at sunset on the fourteenth. For בין הערבים must be understood strictly of the second evening of the fourteenth day and the first of the fifteenth. As Josephus, Philo, and Maimonides describe the Passover, at the ninth hour, or three in the afternoon on the fourteenth day, three hours before sunset, the lamb was slaughtered, offered, and roasted, so that, thus prepared—

—it might be eaten at the twelfth hour, at sunset, and at the first hour of the fifteenth day after sunset. Thus it would be clear how the Passover, which was to be prepared at the end of the fourteenth day and eaten at the beginning of the fifteenth, is rightly referred both to the fourteenth and to the fifteenth. Certainly, the celebrations of Passover and the first day of Unleavened Bread were so close and joined to one another that, although in the cited passages they are distinguished as different days, elsewhere they are joined without distinction into one day, as may be seen at Luke 22:7, Mark 14:1, and Matthew 26:17.

We now add the minor premise: but Christ’s Passover was celebrated on the first day of Unleavened Bread, as is evident from the passages already cited. How these agree with John’s Gospel was stated above.

In his disputation against the Greeks, book 4 *On the Eucharist*, chapter 7 and the following chapters, Bellarmine seems less concerned to press strong arguments against them than to construct slanders against Calvin. Certainly, our writers do not join with the Greeks in this question, but assail them with stronger arguments than any of the papists do. As for our thinking it free and indifferent whether the sacred Supper is celebrated with leavened things, ἐνζύμοις, or unleavened things, ἀζύμοις, the Council of Florence before us—which all papists approve as ecumenical—determined precisely the same thing. As for some of our churches using unleavened bread, ἄζυμα, and others leavened bread, ἔνζυμα, they do so by mere choice, without any necessity. Where leavened bread is common, they do not seek unleavened bread; and where unleavened bread is common, as among us and through most of Scotland, they do not seek leavened bread. Would that all our disputes with the Roman Church were so easy to settle. No pious and learned man would saw this dispute back and forth for long.

The second opinion places Christ’s Passover at the same time as the Jews’ Passover, namely on the evening of the fourteenth of Nisan, on Thursday, the fifth day of the week.

Constantine the Great and other Christian emperors after him, to demonstrate their religion and zeal for Christ’s name to the world, enacted by their civil laws that not only the entire Passover week, as it was among the Jews, but also the week nearest it should be festive. Thus fourteen days in all from the Sunday of the resurrection were celebrated as solemn holidays throughout the Roman world. In this arrangement, in Passover week Sunday, or the day of the sun, was the first day; Monday, the day of the moon, the second; Tuesday the third; Wednesday the fourth; Thursday the fifth; Friday the sixth; and Saturday, which was the Jewish Sabbath, the seventh. On account of this nomenclature for the days of Passover week, after the time of Constantine the days of every week throughout the year were called *feriae*, numbered from the first to the seventh, or from Sunday to Saturday, in the Latin Church.

The present opinion holds that Christ’s Passover was celebrated on the fourteenth day of the moon, on Thursday, the fifth day, just as the preceding day, the thirteenth of the moon, was Wednesday, the fourth day. Many very learned men among Protestants as well as papists support this second opinion. Among the latter are Aquinas, Toletus, Bellarmine, Baronius, a Lapide, Estius, and many others. Among the former are Beza, though doubtful, Grotius, Hammond, the latest English annotators, and others. The opinion has two parts. The former is that Christ ate his Passover on the fourteenth day of the moon, on the fifth day, Thursday night. The latter is that the Jews also ate theirs that same night. We readily grant the former part, for it is amply proved from the passages of Scripture we have already adduced against the first opinion. But the whole difficulty lies in the second part. Various arguments to prove it are gathered by Maldonatus, Bellarmine, and especially Baronius at the year of Christ 34, number 23. We shall answer here the arguments Baronius offers, since they seem to be pressed by him more forcefully and clearly than by anyone else.

The first is from the first three evangelists in the passages cited above. It is clear that Christ celebrated his Passover on the first day of Unleavened Bread, that on the same day the Jews sacrificed the Passover, and that Luke asserts it was necessary for them to sacrifice on that day. Reply: Mark says nothing about the Jews, but merely says, “they were sacrificing.” This is sufficiently preserved by the action of Christ and the apostles, whatever the other Jews did. The necessity of which Luke speaks refers to the duty prescribed by the law of celebrating on that day, which Christ fulfilled exactly with his followers, not to the practice of the other Jews, who were drawn away here from the law by the traditions of the Pharisees.

The second argument. The law bound the celebration of Passover so closely to the fourteenth day that if any could not observe that day for the most necessary reasons, they could not therefore postpone it to another day, but were commanded by divine precept to celebrate on the same fourteenth day of the following month, Numbers 9:10 and 2 Chronicles 30:13. Reply: this divine command did not prevent the scribes from adding their traditions to God’s law, which Christ often refuted by word as well as deed.

Third. It was fitting for Christ, the true Passover, to be slain on the day when the people celebrated the typical Passover. Therefore the people celebrated on the day Christ was crucified. Reply: even if we granted the entire argument, it would militate in no way against us. Moreover, as was said above, perfect correspondence of type and antitype does not require correspondence in every respect of time. Finally, Christ suffered on the natural day on which Passover was to be celebrated according to the law and on which he himself ate his Passover—

—although the Jews postponed their Passover until the day after his passion.

What the most excellent Joseph Scaliger means in *On the Emendation of Times*, pages 550 and 551, [Greek: κ’ ἐναντίαν; reading uncertain], I for my part do not grasp. There, from Hilarion, he presses—and correctly—that Christ suffered on Friday, the fifteenth of Nisan, and sacrificed his Passover on Thursday, the fourteenth day, according to the law and not, as the Jews did, on the following day. Yet in the same place he asserts again and again that Christ suffered on the fourteenth day and celebrated his Passover on the thirteenth. I do not grasp how these statements are consistent. But since I do not see that others have noted this contradiction, I prefer to ascribe it to my own dullness than to any error in so great a man.

Baronius adds at great length to Maldonatus on Matthew 26:2 arguments whose two principal points seem to be these. The former is from John 18:39. Barabbas was released on the day of Passover on which the people celebrated Passover. But Christ was crucified on that day. Therefore, on the day Christ was crucified the Jews celebrated their Passover. Reply: grant that Barabbas was released that very day and not on the following day, which the words do not prove, as Maldonatus rightly observed. For although on that day the Jews received from Pilate the power to release Barabbas, the execution of that power could properly have been postponed until the following day, when they began to celebrate the feast. But leaving this aside, we say that the argument concludes nothing for the thesis. We grant that the people ate Passover on the day Christ was crucified, but for this very reason we contend that the people did not eat it on the same day as Christ, who had eaten it on the preceding night.

The other argument is this. If Christ had anticipated the common day of Passover, he would have offended his disciples and given the Jews just cause to accuse him as a deserter of the law. Reply: he had sufficiently armed the apostles, and the disciple who was his host in whose house he celebrated Passover, against taking offense, since two days before and at other times he had spoken much among them about this last Passover of his. As for his enemies, they had no just cause from this to accuse Christ, since he observed the letter of the law exactly against their traditions; nor, while they pressed other capital charges against Christ, was it advantageous for them to dwell on these ritual minutiae, which were themselves disputed.

The third opinion, which we follow, is that of most Protestants—Calvin, Scaliger, Calvisius, Casaubon, Diodati, Piscator, the Belgian annotators, and others—and also of many of the most learned papists and Jesuits—Jansenius, Maldonatus, Salmeron, Petavius, and others after Paulus Burgensis. It holds that the Jews did not celebrate Passover until after Christ’s passion, at the end of the fifteenth day and the beginning of the sixteenth, as Friday, the sixth day, ended and the seventh day, which was the Sabbath, began.

We prove this first from John 18:28. On the day of the Lord’s passion the Jews refused to enter Pilate’s judgment hall lest, being defiled, they be barred from eating Passover. Therefore on that day they had not yet eaten Passover, although Christ had celebrated it on the preceding night. The insolubility of this argument is evident from the absurdity of the answers offered thus far. The common solution, to which Bellarmine and Baronius adhere after Toletus, is that there was a twofold eating of Passover: one of the Passover lamb, and another of the other sacrifices offered throughout the seven days of Unleavened Bread. The Jews completed the former on the preceding night with Christ, the latter on the following night after Christ had suffered, and John speaks only of this latter eating. They base this distinction on Deuteronomy 16:2: “You shall sacrifice Passover to the Lord your God from the flock and the herd.” Here the offering not only of the lamb but also of sheep and cattle is called the sacrifice of Passover. Rejoinder: to refer the purity John mentions not to the Passover lamb and the first day of Unleavened Bread but to the sacrifices made on the other days of Unleavened Bread is contrary to the law. The law placed the solemnity of the feast chiefly in eating the unblemished lamb and on the first day of Unleavened Bread, when there was always a holy assembly of the people. On the remaining days of Unleavened Bread such purity and holiness were not required of the people, as is confessed. Second, although burnt offerings and peace offerings of cattle and other animals besides lambs were offered on Passover and the first day of Unleavened Bread, Scripture never calls those sacrifices Passover itself, nor are those eating them ever said to eat, as Casaubon notes emphatically, τὸ πάσχα, “the Passover.”

As for the passage of Deuteronomy, it was corrupted by the Latin translator. For the Hebrew has וזבחת פסח צאן ובקר, according to Ana, “you shall sacrifice as Passover a sheep and an ox.” The Latin wrongly translated, “you shall sacrifice Passover from sheep and cattle,” for the Passover offered was not from sheep and cattle but only from lambs, although ovine and bovine sacrifices were joined with the Passover lamb. The Greek interpreter follows the truth of the Hebrew here: θύσεις τὸ πάσχα πρόβατα καὶ βόας, “you shall sacrifice as Passover sheep and cattle.” The Chaldean paraphrast, to distinguish the other sacrifices from the Passover, renders it paraphrastically thus: “You shall slay Passover between the evenings, and cattle and sheep on the following day.”

—that at the last Supper Christ did not eat the Passover lamb or unleavened bread at all, but only a common supper. This invention is absurd, and contrary not only to the understanding of all other Christians, ancient as well as recent, but also to the express Scripture at Luke 22:15: “With desire I have desired to eat this Passover with you.” Was Christ frustrated in this so vehement a desire? Did he speak these words in the very act of celebrating Passover, in the upper room itself, which had been prepared at his command expressly for this purpose, that he might eat the Passover there with his disciples, Matthew 26:17–19?

There is no difficulty in the arguments Spalatensis brings for this absurdity. First, he says, Passover was eaten by people standing and hurrying, but Christ’s last Supper was prolonged and celebrated by people sitting. Reply: standing and hurrying were accidents of the first Egyptian Passover which we never read were repeated. Second, at Christ’s Supper there was a meat sauce into which the bread given to Judas was dipped, but at Passover all things were roasted and no meat was boiled. Reply: that ἐνέμβαμμα into which Christ dipped Judas’s bread was not a meat sauce, but vinegar with lettuce and bitter herbs, with which God commanded the Passover to be eaten. Third, the disciples thought Judas had been ordered to hurry so that he might buy what was necessary for the feast. Therefore the feast of Passover had not yet come. Reply: the feast of Unleavened Bread lasted seven or eight days. The first day had begun, the others followed, and the disciples had to buy many things for their celebration.

Few opinions are so absurd that they do not find some followers. At Matthew 26:18 Hugo Grotius follows Spalatensis here, although he does not name him, so that he might celebrate himself as the author of this novelty too. His follower Hammond is more candid. Here as usual, on Mark 14:12, letter C, he acknowledges that the truly incomparable Grotius was the forerunner of his absurd novelties. They distinguish between a sacrificial Passover, θύσιμον, and a commemorative one, μνημονευτικὸν. They hold that Christ ate the latter on the thirteenth of Nisan, one night before the Jews, without violating the law, because this commemorative Passover was not commanded by the law but was a voluntary rite in which unleavened loaves with bitter herbs, but no lamb, were eaten in memory of Passover. A daring invention! No example proves that the Jews ate such a Passover without sacrifice in the land of Babylon. However much superstition the unbelieving Jews commit after their rejection, what is that to Christians? Indeed, what Grotius brings from Procopius plainly destroys his opinion of a Passover without a lamb. In any case, as is customary for innovators, in resolving a lighter doubt they fall into a much graver one. To prove that the Jews celebrated their Passover on the legal fourteenth day, they affirm that Christ ate his on the illegal thirteenth day. Indeed, they affirm that Christ’s Passover was not Passover except homonymously, ὁμωνύμως, and was not at all instituted by God, but voluntary—that is, a voluntary superstition. Whatever may be invented about Jews living in Canaan, no Scripture can prove that any Jew dwelling in Jerusalem ever celebrated Passover with unleavened bread and bitter herbs but without a lamb, and did so on the thirteenth day, when God did not require unleavened bread.

They press that no Passover lamb outside the lawful day was to be expected for Christ from the priests, who slew all the Passover lambs in the temple. Reply: every day the priests slew in the temple lambs and very many other animals, which they returned to any who offered them to be eaten at home. How easy it was, therefore, for the head of the household with whom Christ ate his last supper to obtain on the day before Passover a lamb slain in the temple by a priest who inquired nothing about the purposes for which that lamb was to be eaten. Moreover, if we denied that every Passover lamb was slain by the priests in the temple, we would have Philo as our fellow voter, σύμψηφον, in this matter. He expressly asserts that at the feast of Passover God permitted every head of household to slay a lamb within his own home. We admit that sometimes Passover lambs slain by the priests in the temple were distributed to families to be eaten, as is evident from Scripture as well as Josephus, but Philo denies that this was always done. Grotius’s censure of Philo the Alexandrian for ignorance of his country’s custom is merely dogmatic assertion. Philo, who lived before the destruction of the second temple, rightly does not appear capable of being ignorant of the circumstances of Passover that were very well known to all Jews wherever they lived.

Our second argument for the third opinion is from John 13:1. Christ ate his last Supper before the feast day of Passover. This cannot be understood otherwise than that Christ ate his Passover before the Jews’ Passover. For if one supposes that Christ and the Jews celebrated their Passovers on the same night, which was the lawful one, John’s words could not be understood without a manifest contradiction: namely, that Christ celebrated Passover on the legal day and yet before the legal day of Passover. Baronius and Bellarmine, after Toletus, reply that “the day of Passover” is sometimes taken for the natural day of twenty-four hours, from evening to evening, and sometimes for the artificial day of twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset, and that John speaks of the artificial, not the natural, day. I rejoin, first, that the distinction does not remove the contradiction, ἐναντίωσιν, from this place: Christ celebrated Passover on the legal day before the legal day. —could remain until the following morning. Further, the artificial day after Passover was the first day of Unleavened Bread, the fifteenth of Nisan, manifestly distinct from the preceding day of Passover, the fourteenth of Nisan.

The third argument is from John 19:14. Christ died on the preparation, παρασκευῇ, of the Jews’ Passover. Therefore he celebrated his own Passover a complete day and night before the Jews’ Passover, which always followed the preparation, παρασκευὴν. The three cardinals reply that Passover had no preparation, παρασκευὴν, but every preparation was for the Sabbath; and so Christ died on the preparation of the Sabbath, not of Passover, which had no preparation. Yet the Sabbath’s preparation was called that of Passover because that year the Sabbath fell on the second day of Unleavened Bread, which was the sixteenth of Nisan and the third day of Passover. I rejoin, first, that it is false that no Jewish feast except the Sabbath had a preparation. Casaubon demonstrates the contrary from so many passages of the Talmud and the rabbis that the Jesuit Petavius, much more learned in all these matters than the cardinals, openly yields and joins us. And who indeed would dare deny that Passover had its preparation when John affirms it in the most formal words without any qualification? Second, it is false that the Sabbath which occurred that year on the third day of Passover is called Passover. Although we admit that the whole feast of Unleavened Bread, of seven or eight days, is called Passover, no passage of Scripture can prove that all those seven days and each one of them, from first to last, are called Passover. Third, leaving all these points aside, our argument holds, for Christ’s Passover preceded by one day even that preparation for the Sabbath on which he was crucified.

The fourth argument. If the Jews had eaten Passover with Christ on the fourteenth of Nisan, then the following fifteenth, namely Friday, the day on which the Lord was crucified, would have been the first day of Unleavened Bread for the Jews and therefore holy. Although the preparation of food was lawful on it, all servile works were unlawful. But that Friday was not such a day; it was entirely a working day on which the Jews publicly conducted capital cases, constructed crosses, killed and buried the condemned. Indeed, the most religious and holy among them bought linen and spices and procured everything pertaining to the burial of the dead. They abstained from these things through reverence for the Sabbath as soon as the sun set, and rested from them throughout the holy day. They reply that matters pertaining to burial of the dead were works of charity which the most holy day permitted; moreover, that the Jews were so profane that they made light even of reverence for the Sabbath. Rejoinder: works pertaining to burial were so profane that Joseph and the holy women abstained from them throughout the Sabbath out of reverence for the day. And although the Jews were indeed most profane in heart, they nevertheless acted so religiously in externals that they admitted not even the smallest things which seemed in any way to defile the sacred day.

The fifth argument is from John 19:31. The Sabbath after Christ’s death is called a great day. This designation does not arise from the Sabbath itself, for then it would belong to every Sabbath, but from some great solemnity added to the Sabbath. In the feast of Unleavened Bread there was no great solemnity or holy convocation except on its first and last day, and no one holds that the last fell on the Sabbath that year. Therefore the first day fell on the Sabbath and acquired for it the title of a great day. The true first day of Unleavened Bread was indeed the preceding day, namely the fifteenth of Nisan, on which Christ suffered after his legal Passover. But that first day of Unleavened Bread which made the Sabbath on which Christ lay in the tomb a great day was, although not the true first day, nevertheless the Jews’ first day of Unleavened Bread. So that two great solemnities would not follow one another in immediate succession, they transferred it to the Sabbath by the prescription of the Sanhedrin. Thus this Sabbath, augmented by another very great solemnity, was deservedly called a great day. The three cardinals reply that this Sabbath was called great because the third day of Passover, or the second day of Unleavened Bread, coincided with the Sabbath. They say, however, that the transference of feasts was a fable unheard of in Christ’s days and while the second temple stood. Rejoinder: the days of Unleavened Bread between the first and last were not solemn and added no greatness to the Sabbath. As for the transference of feasts, Paulus Burgensis demonstrates it so firmly, and Casaubon and others confirm it with so many testimonies of the ancient rabbis, that Petavius himself embraces it. I do not indeed think that this transference of feasts was divine, although both Calvin and Beza expressly affirm it. For if it had been divine, why did Christ not follow it? Yet I do not approve Ludovicus de Dieu or his source Johannes Coch, who denies this transference of feasts against the most learned both of the Reformed and of the Jesuits. Certainly, our third opinion is defended sufficiently well without it, for it affirms only the fact that Christ celebrated his Passover on the day before the other Jews celebrated theirs, not any single particular manner of that fact. Still, this manner of transferring one feast to another day, namely Passover to the Sabbath, seems more expedient than any other I have yet happened to see. The manner Petavius seems to have learned from Ludovicus de Dieu is certainly much more encumbered: namely, that Passover fell on the Sabbath that year because of the priests’ ignorance and the uncertain proclamation of new moons. Petavius does not prove this manner with suitable arguments, and it would have rendered the entire reckoning of all the Jews’ feasts so uncertain and perplexed—
