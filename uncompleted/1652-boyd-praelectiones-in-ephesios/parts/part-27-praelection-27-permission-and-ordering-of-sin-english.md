Lecture XXVII.

As for reprobation, sin has the principal place in its execution, and the
permission both of that first sin, I mean, and of all the others that flow
from it is especially serviceable to this matter. Someone might therefore
quite suitably refer to this place the whole discussion of sin—whether of
Adam’s first sin, and in him the sin of the whole human race, or of the other
sins that follow it—and of their permission. Among all those whom we have
happened to see treating this point in the divine decree, that truly holy man
of God Perkins discussed this subject so abundantly and accurately that
nothing could seem to be lacking on this side of the matter.

[Marginalia: Lecture XXVII. The latter member. Perkins.]

Nevertheless, to avoid confusing different loci, we must draw from the
subject only those points which the heterodox commonly bring forward lightly
against the doctrine now before us, although to them those points seem by no
means slight or weightless. Thus they like to argue from means appointed to an
end. Whoever wills an end also wills the means that serve its execution. But,
on your own account, God wills the end, that is, the just condemnation of the
reprobate because of their sins, and sin itself is its principal, or at least
its most powerful, means. Therefore, God also wills sin itself, contrary to
the express testimony of sacred Scripture in Psalm 5: “You are not a God who
wills iniquity,” to omit the innumerable other absurdities that follow from
this. Or they put it this way: whoever decreed the end decreed the means as
well. But God decreed to condemn the reprobate because of sin. Therefore, he
also decreed sin itself, and consequently we will have asserted that God is
the author of sin, which is absurd and blasphemous.

[Marginalia: The principal objection. Psalm 5:5.]

Indeed, this objection can be extended to attack not only the decree of
reprobation but also the decree of election, and thus predestination itself
taken generally, at least insofar as it concerns that first and original sin.
For we advised above in the proper place that the permission of that sin is a
means common to both parts of the divine decree, no less than humanity’s
creation itself, although it serves them in different ways. To satisfy this
difficulty plainly and fully, both the various and manifold consideration of
the divine will and that of sin itself must be recalled to your minds and
memories: whether that first sin in which we all sinned in Adam or the other
sins that followed from it, insofar as each consideration bears upon our
present purpose. As regards the former, we may adapt to the same will—inasmuch
as it concerns creatures and either coincides with providence itself

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 152 | De Prædestinatione p. 108]

or certainly has a great affinity and kinship with it—what an ancient author
in the *Suda* asserts about divine providence, applying it, I say, to the will
as it relates to human actions. “The providence of God,” says that author,
“takes place in three ways: according to good pleasure, according to
dispensation, and according to concession.” God’s providence, or his will as
our subject requires, that is, as it concerns human beings and their
government, admits of a threefold difference, or exerts itself in three ways:
according to good pleasure, according to dispensation, and according to
concession or permission. Things which come to pass through God’s good
pleasure or approval are said by some of the Greek Fathers to be done by God
“according to what is antecedent,” that is, from an antecedent will, meaning
God’s general and simple decree and counsel. For in this way they oppose “the
things that come to pass simply” (they also express this first mode in that
way), things which simply come to pass through God’s providence, to “things
that come to pass dispensationally.” They commonly restrict “dispensation” to
the mystery of the incarnation itself, as I remember advising above when
explaining section 10, chapter 1, on Ephesians. But we take this second “mode
of divine providence,” which is “according to dispensation,” in another sense
here, as will soon be evident. We also interpret the first mode, “according to
good pleasure,” somewhat more narrowly, as that action of God by which he so
wills a work that he also approves it, effects it in a special manner, and
therefore delights in it. This mode belongs only to good works proceeding
from the Holy Spirit at work within us.

[Marginalia: The ancient author in the *Suda* on the threefold sense of the
divine will. Ephesians 1:10. Mode 1.]

The second mode is that of sustaining. By it divine providence sustains and
preserves even a nature that is sinning—its existence, powers, faculties,
acts, and all its passions—by that general concurrence, according to the order
and law established in the nature of things. It does this as that first cause,
without the immediate influx of whose power secondary causes could not
subsist for a single moment, still less operate or move anything. In this
sense the apostle said in Acts 17, “In him we exist, live, and move.” This mode
of operation, by which God sustains and preserves even a nature that is
sinning by that ordinary power and concurrence of his, including in the very
act of sinning, in no way prevents God himself from being free and immune from
every fault, or prevents every defect from residing in the creature itself.
For that divine power preserves and sustains it, not insofar as it is evil and
sinful, but insofar as it is a creature and possesses the existence divinely
assigned to it in some rank and order of things. Likewise that power sustains
its act insofar as it is an act, not insofar as it is an irregular and vicious
act, that is, one that falls short of and turns aside from the norm of divine
law. So it is in the familiar, well-worn example of a limping person. The limp
itself must be said to come only from the defective instrument, namely a
dislocated leg, and not from the soul or any power of the soul, which meanwhile
remains immune from every defect and fault. The motion itself, or walking,
however, comes not only from the leg as the instrument, but also from the soul
itself, or its locomotive faculty, as the principal and higher cause. Likewise,
when a person sins, the fact that he does or performs something comes not only
from himself as the instrument and proximate cause, but also from God who
preserves and sustains as the higher and principal cause. But the fact that he
acts wickedly, sins in acting, limps, stumbles, and strays from the rectitude
of the divine will comes from himself, from the defect implanted within him
and from his native depravity, and must not be imputed to divine goodness.
Thus, by this ordinary dispensation, so to speak, of his power, influx, and
assistance, God preserves only the order established in nature. He does not
assist or impel humanity’s depraved will as it rushes into things forbidden by
the moral law. This is no more the case than it is in that dispensation of his
will by which he fosters, forms, and gives shape in the womb to children
conceived from unlawful and furtive intercourse no less than to those who
come from a lawful, that is, a conjugal bed.

[Marginalia: Mode 2. Acts 17:28. Example.]

Finally, the third mode relates to evil human works. God’s wise and powerful
providence intervenes in them by permitting, directing, and ordering even the
worst actions of the worst instruments, so that he himself meanwhile executes
the holy work of his counsel most holily on his own side. This mode can in
turn be considered in three ways. First, in divine permission, when in
accordance with his counsel he allows a creature that still stands to its own
choice, so that, without adding the new grace that would confirm it, he
permits it to fall into sin; or when he justly leaves a person already fallen
and guilty of sin to himself and to Satan, deprived of his grace. The result
is that the sinner bursts out into that freedom of sinning from which God had
previously restrained him by his hidden power. This permission is often
attributed to God in sacred Scripture under words of acting or effecting—for
example, when he is said to harden the impious in their sins or to command
them to do something which nevertheless conflicts with the rectitude of his
own law, as when he commanded Shimei to curse David, and other things of that
kind. In the same way, according to common usage, we will say that a rider
urges on a running horse when he lets out its reins, and likewise that a
hunter does so when he releases a dog straining after prey from its restraint,
that is, from its hunting leash.

[Marginalia: Mode 3. Subdivision of the third mode. 1. Note carefully.
Examples supplied.]

Second, it occurs by the presentation of various occasions, whether they are
good in themselves or morally indifferent. God’s hidden power so uses them
toward the sinner, justly treated according to his deserts, that by the
sinner’s own agency it draws into the open either the sin that still lies
covered and concealed within him or increasingly provokes and sharpens a sin
already known and manifest, until its measure is filled and that cup of
seething wrath is emptied upon his head. In this way the apostle in Romans 7
says that the law itself increases and provokes sin. This occurs because of
the defect of the nature itself, which is perverse and corrupt, and because
of the depravity implanted within it, by which it is borne more violently and
eagerly toward what the law forbids. So winds roar with a great noise around
the barriers of a mountain, or torrents swollen by winter snows rise and dash
all the more violently against an obstacle or embankment opposed to them. A
physician who administers even a prophylactic draught, otherwise harmless in
itself and in its own nature, will similarly disturb, arouse, and inflame the
putrid and corrupt humors of a diseased body which had previously been calm
and quiet. In the same way God presents to the destined impious person law,
commands, admonitions, threats, counsels, blows and scourges, gifts and
benefits, things thought, seen, read, and heard, things encountered, obvious,
and accidental, and infinitely many other things of this kind, either good or
indifferent and of a middle nature. Abandoned to themselves and deprived of
his grace, the impious abuse them for sin. They eagerly and without delay
seize from them handles and occasions for sinning, and advance and execute
God’s just and wise counsel concerning them, whether unknowingly or against
their will. Thus the supreme goodness of God does not instill or infuse even
the smallest amount of malice into them, although sacred literature sometimes
says that he even arouses them to things which, considered in themselves, are
evil. Thus in 1 Kings 22 he is said to have commanded that lying spirit to
seduce Ahab’s false prophets. Likewise, he is commonly said to have aroused
and brought the Chaldeans, Assyrians, Babylonians, and others of this kind to
execute his judgments upon the Israelite people, although they themselves

[Marginalia: 2. Romans 7:8. Similar examples. Anticipation. 1 Kings 22.
Jeremiah 34:22.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 153 | De Prædestinatione p. 109]

intended nothing less, but were driven headlong only by a depraved lust for
dominion. Thus in Isaiah 29 and 19 Jehovah is said to have drenched that
stubborn people with a spirit of deep sleep, to have closed their eyes, and,
in chapter 19, to have mixed a spirit of perversity among the Egyptians. In
Jeremiah 13 he is likewise said to be about to fill all the inhabitants of the
land of Israel and of Jerusalem—king, prophets, and priests alike—with a
spirit of drunkenness. Similar statements occur everywhere in the prophetic
Scriptures. They plainly impress upon us God’s efficacious providence even in
governing the most wicked works of the impious: in supplying their occasions,
preparing objects, instruments, aids, and other things of that kind, removing
and obstructing contrary impediments, and in this way opening the path both
for his glory and, consequently, for the malice and punishment of the
reprobate.

[Marginalia: Isaiah 29:10 and 19:14. Jeremiah 13:13.]

Third, it occurs by ordering. God orders the evil work of an evil instrument
to the best end, and in this way executes his own just, holy, and wise work no
less justly, holily, and wisely, whatever kind of instrument he deigns to use
in it. Thus he not only permits Joseph’s sale into Egypt, but disposes and
turns it to the common good of Jacob, Joseph, and Jacob’s whole family, that
is, of his Church. Hence come those sayings of the ancients, especially
Augustine, which in this difficult and subtle matter are no less true and
solid than elegant and acute: “Just as God is the best creator of good
natures, so he is the most just orderer of evil wills.” Again, “God fulfills
certain wills of his, which are certainly good, through the evil wills of
evil people.” Again, “Concerning those who do what he does not will, he
himself does what he wills.” Again, “By the very fact that they acted against
God’s will, his will was accomplished concerning them.” Again, “Who would not
tremble at those divine judgments by which God does whatever he wills in the
hearts of evil people, rendering to them according to their deserts?” Again,
“It is manifest that God works in human hearts to incline their wills wherever
he wills, whether to good according to his mercy or to evil according to
their deserts, by his own judgment, sometimes open, sometimes hidden, but
always just.” Other such statements occur in *The City of God*, the
*Enchiridion*, *On Rebuke and Grace*, *On Grace and Free Choice*, *Against
Julian*, and elsewhere. So too Fulgentius: “Although God is not the author of
evil thoughts, he is nevertheless the orderer of evil wills; and from anyone’s
evil work he himself does not cease to work good.”

[Marginalia: 3. Example. Sayings of Augustine. Fulgentius.]

Here, therefore, is the sense in which sacred literature says that God
hardens, blinds, and deceives the impious, commands some evil thing to them,
and himself does that very thing which is done most wickedly and criminally
through them, and so forth. It speaks in this way because of these various and
manifold actions of God concerning sin: not only permitting, abandoning, and
withdrawing his grace, nor even merely disposing and ordering to an end, but
also drawing and bringing forth the act itself through various occasions, in
the sense and manner explained above. Consequently, what Augustine concludes
in book 1 of *Questions to Simplicianus* from the apostle’s statement in
Romans 9—“Therefore God has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he
wills”—namely, that God’s hardening is his unwillingness to show mercy, so
that he does not bestow something by which a person becomes better but does
not bestow anything by which he becomes worse, we admit as true. We do not,
however, acknowledge it as complete, because it does not express or explain
fully enough the third mode of divine providence which we are discussing,
well employed in the evil works of evil people.

[Marginalia: Conclusion. Prior application. Romans 9:18.]

Therefore, to compress this first distinction into a few words for explaining
the present difficulty, it is evident that God’s will is sometimes taken more
strictly, precisely, and specially for his complacency, so to speak. By this
will he so wills something that he both approves and effects it as a good
thing pleasing to him. Consequently, the object of this will, that is, the
thing itself which God wills in this manner simply, depends upon that will
simply and follows from it as an effect from a cause. In this way we say that
God wills only good and does not will evil. Sayings of the Fathers which deny
that God preordained works of impiety but assert that he merely foreknew them,
and which say it is the same to speak of God as author and God as willing,
must be understood of this sense of the will, which is its special and most
proper sense.

[Marginalia: Member 1. Augustine, *Hypognosticon*, book 6.]

At other times God’s will is taken more broadly and loosely for his voluntary
permission, direction, and disposition to a good end. By it he determines not
to prevent certain things from happening and consequently wills in some
manner that they happen, even though he neither simply approves nor effects
them, but permits them to be done by others. It is that decree of God by which
he wills something, not as himself approving what is done—for he always
approves his permission as a most just and holy thing—nor as about to effect
it himself, but only as about to permit others to effect it. Meanwhile, by
his power in that ordinary concurrence, he sustains both their being and their
activity; indeed, he also directs their evil work most excellently and
disposes it most wisely. Consequently, the thing to be effected in this
manner, or the object of the divine will taken in this sense, does not properly
depend upon that will as its cause and source, but only upon the evil will of
the failing creature. To treat that will a little more accurately, it seems
that the following order must above all be observed. First, for certain
reasons God wills or determines not to restrain an evil or prevent its
occurrence. Then, only as a consequence, he wills that it occur, insofar as
whatever of this kind happens in the nature of things contrary to the divine
law and justice revealed in the law happens because God does not prevent it.
For just as nothing good is or can be done unless God does it, so no evil can
be avoided unless God prevents it. We say that nothing, not even in the life
of the worst people or in the audacious malice of Satan himself, escapes this
will which at least permits, directs, and orders; nothing happens outside it.
Otherwise we would be compelled to confess that God’s providence is idle,
whether by denying this divine permission or by referring it, as some of the
Scholastics do, not to some counsel or will that exists in God, but to a kind
of idle and inert negligence or lack of care. These writers assert that with
respect to evil, that is, the evil works about which we are speaking, God has
no positive act either of willing or of not willing, but only the negative act
of not willing to prohibit them. This is nothing other than to remove a good
part of divine providence insofar as it concerns most human affairs and to
introduce God as an idle spectator of many things done in the world. It is to
substitute and obtrude an Epicurean idol in place of the true God who governs,
directs, and administers all things, and therefore in place of one who in at
least some manner and sense wills, determines, and orders absolutely all
things that occur in the world.

[Marginalia: Member 2. A corollary to be noted. The order of willing in this
sense. Confirmation. Lombard’s error.]

Sacred Scripture, on the contrary, openly proclaims that all these things,
even though they are most wickedly done by evil instruments, nevertheless
pertain to God’s will and counsel. That single passage in Acts 4 establishes
this clearly and will serve us in place of all the rest: “They were gathered
together in this city—namely

[Marginalia: Refutation. Acts 4:28.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 154 | De Prædestinatione p. 110]

Herod, Pilate, the Romans, and the Jews—to do whatever your hand and counsel
preordained to be done.” There we see subjected to God’s decree and will not
only Christ’s passion but also the very works which, in relation to those who
did them, were evil and
indeed most criminal, insofar as God willed them to occur for just ends.
Therefore, even if we assert the same concerning Adam’s fall and express it
in the same way as the Holy Spirit, speaking through the mouth of the Church,
expressed Christ’s betrayal and crucifixion and the other things the Jews and
Gentiles wickedly dared against him—namely, “By eating from that deadly and
fatal tree, Adam did what God’s hand and counsel preordained to be done”—what
else do we do but imitate God’s own Spirit in this respect, who carries before
us the brightest torch of sacred Scripture in this form of speech? Likewise,
1 Peter 3 expressly mentions God’s will in the evil works of the impious, that
is, the persecutors of his Church: “It is better,” Peter says, “if God’s will
so wills, that you suffer evil while doing good than while doing evil.” What
was it to persecute and afflict God’s servants, who profess Christ’s faith,
for the sake of justice and the gospel, except to sin grievously against God’s
law? Yet Peter teaches in that passage that this did not occur without God
willing it.

[Marginalia: A corollary to be noted. 1 Peter 3:17.]

Neither the ancients nor, indeed, more recent writers refused to speak in this
way. It will be enough for us to bring forward one or two statements.
Augustine says, “It must be understood that all things are either completed
with the Lord’s assistance or permitted when he abandons, yet so that you
understand that absolutely nothing is admitted against God’s will.” Again,
“Nothing occurs unless the omnipotent wills it to occur, either by allowing it
to happen or by doing it himself.” Again, “Although things which are evil are
not good insofar as they are evil, nevertheless it is good that there should
be not only good things but also evil ones. For unless it were good that there
should also be evil things, the omnipotent good would by no means permit them
to exist.” Among more recent writers Hugh likewise says, “It is an easy thing
and is said without a scruple of conscience that God wills good. But if it is
said that God wills evil, this is grievous to hear and a godly mind does not
readily receive that one who is good wills evil. For it then seems to be said
that one who is good loves evil and approves what is depraved. The godly mind
therefore rejects it, not because what is said is not said correctly, but
because what is said correctly is not understood correctly.” He explains in
what follows how it must be understood: the statement means only that “God
wills evil to exist, but does not will the evil itself.” Again, “He wills evil
to exist, and in it he wills nothing but good.” Elsewhere, “When he does good
and permits evil, his will appears in them, since he wills that what he does
or permits should exist.” Again, “God’s will is his good pleasure, his will is
his operation, and his will is his permission.” This accords with the
threefold notion brought forward above from the *Suda*.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On Predestination and Grace*, chapter 15;
*Enchiridion*, chapters 95 and 96. Hugh, *On the Sacraments*, book 1, chapter
12, part 4.]

We now return to sin itself and set forth, in the second place, its diverse
consideration. We will first lay down these two foundations as principles or
hypotheses. First, evil insofar as it is evil cannot be the object of the
will. Even a human being cannot will evil except under some appearance of
good, insofar as it seems good to him and by his perverse judgment appears to
be what it truly is not. How much less can God Most Good and Great will evil
under the character of evil, insofar as it is such? He does not will it except
insofar as it bears the character of good. Second, this difference between
good and evil must be noted: although there is a supreme and infinite good to
which no evil adheres or is joined, namely God himself, who is goodness itself,
there is no corresponding supreme evil which has no good joined to it or is
good in no respect. Every evil is founded, so to speak, in good, rests and
adheres in it, and has its existence in good. Whatever existence is properly
to be attributed to evil, we must confess that it belongs to evil only by
reason of a good subject. If that subject did not exist, evil would not exist
either. Hence Augustine acutely says in the *Enchiridion*, “If there were no
good in which evil existed, evil could not exist at all, because corruption
would have neither a place in which to subsist nor a source from which to
arise.”

[Marginalia: Part 2 of the solution. 1. 2. Augustine, *Enchiridion*.]

With these points set out and assumed, it is evident, and has now become
common teaching, that sin has a twofold relation or consideration. I mean sin
taken concretely for an evil action, affection, motion, desire, thought,
habit, custom, inclination, or evil and disordered propensity itself, and
this insofar as it is sin—that is, not as a motion or action but as a depraved
motion or depraved action. Otherwise this would be sophistry, since anything
is said simply to belong to someone when it belongs to him as such. A consul,
for example, will be said to command the citizens, not insofar as he is a
musician or physician, although that too might happen to belong to him, but
insofar as he is a magistrate and consul. Hence it would be inept to say that
the physician or musician issued this or that edict, but proper to say that
the magistrate or consul did so. Likewise, since that lawlessness, as a kind
of form inhering in the action, both constitutes and denominates the action
as lawless—for from it the action is and is called sin—to consider sin is to
regard that lawless action not as an action but as sin. Thus we say it is
regarded in two ways: in one, as it is related to God willing and decreeing
its permission; in the other, as it is related to the person committing it.
In the former way it bears the character of good, as wisely permitted,
directed, disposed, and ordered by God to a good end. In the latter, as
perpetrated and committed by an evil person, it is evil and damnable. These
two considerations concur in one and the same work of the Devil or an impious
person in such a way that neither impedes or injures the other, nor, so to
speak, affects the other with its own qualities: an admirable harmony in
things of such discordant and dissenting natures. God’s goodness and wisdom,
executing his own good work through those worst instruments in the best way
and to the best end, does not diminish or excuse even the slightest degree of
their malice or the depravity of the work they perpetrate. Conversely, their
depravity and malice and the damnable act they commit do nothing but commend
and illustrate God’s admirable wisdom and goodness. So far are they from
blowing upon, affecting, or infecting it in any way with their foulness.

Hence we need not distinguish sin here into what they call its material and

God and to the evil instrument. It is related to God insofar as he sustains,
permits, directs, and orders it. In the former way, the action which we call
sin is a natural good; in the latter, it is even morally good, that is,
conforming and consonant with God’s hidden will. It is related to the human
being as the proximate agent and perpetrator; in relation to him, his action
is in every way damnable, depraved, and evil, drawing and provoking God’s
wrath upon his head.

Finally, as regards the means serving and subordinated to the execution of the
divine decree, it is evident that they are not all of the same kind or order.
Some are means in themselves and by their own nature; others are means only
accidentally, that is, insofar as, beyond their own nature, they happen to be
directed and disposed by the first and supreme cause to a good end. This is
true of sin, both of that first sin and of the rest of its offspring and
progeny. It does not have the character of a means insofar as it is committed
and effected by the person himself, but only insofar as it is permitted and
directed by God toward the manifestation of his glory, both in his mercy and
in his justice, in a most excellent and entirely divine manner.

[Marginalia: Part 3 of the solution.]

Once all these points have been established and explained, anyone will find
it very easy to dissolve from them the objection proposed at the beginning by
applying the distinctions already provided to its terms. First, when it is
said that whoever wills the end also wills the means, we must answer with a
distinction both of the will and of the means. God wills those means which are
simply such and are means in themselves—namely, all things which are good and
such by their own nature—both simply and with an approving will. But he wills
those means which are such only accidentally and in a certain respect, not
simply by their own nature, with a will which is likewise not simple, pure,
and approving, but only permitting and ordering or directing. He wills them
in a certain respect, that is, insofar as they are means. Yet they are means,
not insofar as they are done by human beings, but insofar as they are related
to God permitting and directing them. He therefore wills them in a similar
way, only by permitting and directing them. Under this consideration they
cease to be evil and assume the character of good, for every means appointed
by God to some end is good insofar as it is such. The same must be said of the
decree. The matter comes to the same thing and the force of the objection is
always the same, whether it employs the name of will or decree.

[Marginalia: Application of the preceding distinctions to the solution of the
objection. Part 1.]

Next, as regards the minor premise, first, it can rightly be denied that the
creature’s punishment or eternal condemnation is the end of the divine decree
or even the end of the condemned person himself. The end is only the
manifestation of God’s glorious justice in him. God does not intend punishment
insofar as it is punishment, that is, as the creature’s evil or destruction,
but only insofar as it is the execution of justice. Second, sin is a means
subordinated and serviceable to this end, not insofar as it is related to the
human being but insofar as it is related to God. Consequently, it is a means
not as it is evil in itself and in its own nature, but as it assumes some
character of good: not insofar as it is committed, but insofar as it is
permitted and ordered. In the former way, as committed, it is by itself and
by its own nature the meritorious cause of punishment and condemnation. In
the latter it is a means for executing divine justice, and only accidentally,
insofar as it is permitted, a means for executing the divine decree. The
permission itself can and must be called a means in itself, as we taught above
in the proper place when speaking for the first time of Adam’s fall.

[Marginalia: Part 2. Conclusion of the application.]

Once these points have been observed and so distinguished, nothing absurd
will follow in the conclusion, namely, that God wills sin—not simply, but in
a certain respect and as regards its occurrence—or that he decreed sin, not
to effect it himself so that it comes from him or he may be called its author,
but to permit it to be done by others, so that it properly comes as from a
cause only from the Devil and evil people and recognizes them alone as its
authors. Consequently, God willed and decreed it as something good, not evil,
namely, as directed by him to the best end.

But suppose someone still presses and urges this point against what has been
said: Sin insofar as it is sin is evil and detestable. But insofar as it is
permitted by God, it is sin. Therefore, insofar as it is permitted by God it
is evil, and not only insofar as it is committed by an impious person,
contrary to the solution given above. The major premise is admitted; the
minor is deduced from the preceding statements, in which it was asserted
that sin, even insofar as it is such, has a twofold relation: one to God who
permits and orders it, in which respect it is good, and the other to the
Devil or human being as its author, in which respect it is evil. God therefore
permits and orders sin even insofar as it is sin.

[Marginalia: Counter-instance.]

We must answer from the foundation already laid that, as far as our purpose
is concerned, the word “sin” can be taken generally in three ways:
materially, formally, and totally; or in the material sense, the abstract
sense, and the concrete sense, insofar as it is a kind of whole composed of
matter or a subject and its form. It is taken materially or formally when in
thought and reason I separate either the subject or matter from malice or
iniquity itself, which is the form, or, conversely, this malice or lawlessness
from the subject and matter in which it exists and inheres, although in
reality and actuality they can never be separated in sin, as is evident from
the second hypothesis prefixed above to sin’s twofold consideration. In the
first sense, we say that sin is an action, motion, or inclination. In the
second, we say that sin is a defect or deviation, disorder and lawlessness
itself, and the absence or privation of divine rectitude in a thing in which
it ought to have existed. Finally, in the third, we say that sin is, for
example, blasphemy, theft, adultery, parricide, and so forth.

[Marginalia: Answer. The threefold sense of sin.]

Now when we say that sin insofar as it is sin is regarded by us here and is
considered in two ways, it is evident that we have taken the word “sin” in the
first way, from the very explanation we added there: we said not insofar as
it is simply a motion or action, but insofar as it is a depraved motion or an
irregular action. For we consider here the action which is and is called sin,
that is, the thing viewed under the form from which it has the name of sin,
although in the thing itself, namely that action, many other things besides
that form of sin could be considered, as is evident. In a word, therefore,
sin is taken in the third way and the concrete sense when we institute this
twofold consideration of it. Again, sin taken concretely for an action which
actually includes and denotes that lawlessness can be considered either under
the character of that lawlessness, in which case it is evil and from human
beings, or under the character of its divine permission and ordering to a
good end, in which case it is good and from God, not from evil authors.
Therefore, to the major premise, “Sin insofar as it is sin is evil,” the
answer is made by this distinction. If sin is taken in the first way, the
proposition that it is evil insofar as it is sin is denied universally and
absolutely, because sin taken in the first sense with this reduplication,
“insofar as it is sin,” constitutes nothing other than sin in the third way
or concrete sense;

but sin in the concrete sense has a twofold relation, one to the person
sinning and the other to God permitting, as has now been repeatedly impressed
upon us. But if we take sin in the third sense and meanwhile add that
reduplication by saying, “Sin insofar as it is sin,” we admit the major
premise here, because it signifies that evil action considered under the very
character of its lawlessness and malice, and thus we confess that it is evil.
But we deny the minor premise in that sense. We asserted nothing of the kind
above, if our words are weighed attentively. We confess that God permits sin
in the first sense, even insofar as it is sin, that is, an action which is
actually lawless and actually formed, or rather defiled, by malice. But we
utterly deny that he permits sin in the third sense insofar as it is sin—that
is, that he permits that depraved action under the character of its depravity
and malice, so that the action’s depravity and lawlessness is the formal
reason for the divine permission which God regarded in permitting the sin.
We assert that sin, even in the third sense, although always actually depraved
and lawless in itself, nevertheless has another consideration on God’s side,
under which it is both the means and matter for illustrating his justice. In
this respect it is good, not so much from its own nature as accidentally. For
this same reason it is permitted by God and subjected to the divine will and
decree, and comprehended in them not only by a negative but also by a positive
act. In a word, when we say here that sin insofar as it is sin is considered
in two ways, we signify only that, in speaking of sin, we do not regard the
bare action in which viciousness exists as in its subject matter according to
the character it might have as an action not subjected to such a form.
Instead, we take that action as actually vicious, that is, we speak of the
whole concrete thing, the evil action, I say, not insofar as it is evil but
rather insofar as it is a certain concrete whole which we called sin in that
third sense. We teach that this whole in turn has that twofold relation under
which it assumes the character both of good and of evil.
