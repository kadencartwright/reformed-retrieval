CHAPTER XII.

Verse 1. Whoever loves instruction loves knowledge; but whoever hates correction [is] brutish.

Everyone persuades himself—and obtains without difficulty that others should put the same trust in him—that he is led and held by zeal for learning and knowing things that are useful and necessary to know. Nor is anyone so wild or savage that he does not delight in skill in the most excellent things. By this proverb of Solomon, however, they are convicted of falsehood, of being destitute of true and solid knowledge, and thus of being strangers to the zeal for right knowledge. For since zeal for knowledge extends no farther than love of discipline and chastisement, when this latter is confined within narrow bounds, the former cannot extend and enlarge itself to embrace many things. Folly is innate in us, and the rod of correction will drive it away. In order that this may be perceived more easily, let us examine each point more carefully. In this passage, therefore, discipline or chastisement is restraint or severity by which the desire to sin is hindered.

It is inflicted by God:

- Immediately by his own majesty.
- Mediately through:
  - Angels:
    - Good,
    - Evil,
  - Human beings:
    - Privately:
      - In the family,
      - In the school,
    - Publicly:
      - In the Church,
      - In the commonwealth.

Therefore, in order to prove ourselves lovers of knowledge, we must embrace and love all these chastisements. Proverbs 3:11 commands us not to despise correction or disdain it with a certain nausea. But here it is further required that we love it, if we wish to be suitable hearers and disciples of heavenly and saving doctrine. Scripture, moreover, appears in this manner to establish certain degrees of advancement and progress in bearing evils. First, care must be taken that we neither spurn nor disdain it; thus it will come about that at length we ascend to the point that we are willing to love the affliction imposed upon us. Yet perhaps it would be better if we said that it is an act of love, although of a more remiss love, not to despise or disdain correction.

Let us therefore test ourselves by this standard and rule of the Prophet, to see whether we are sons or disciples of wisdom. And yet loving correction may seem foreign and abhorrent to human nature, so that you must first strip off what is human before you can value it. Nevertheless, when we consider first the efficient cause—namely, God and his paternal love—and when we regard the end and the excellent effects indicated in very many passages of Scripture, Proverbs 3:12 and Hebrews 12, it will not be difficult to induce our mind to love it. For why should we not love chastisements, although they are bitter, and those who apply them, when experience teaches that people greatly embrace physicians and surgeons, and their remedies, however much they cause pain? Indeed, they regard as insane and mentally deranged those who are angry with a physician who burns or cuts. The love of correction, from reflection upon these things,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 165 | printed columns 304–305]

once begun, will be very greatly confirmed if we review the disadvantages that flow from neglected discipline and correction.

But if someone asks by what signs he may know that he is a lover of correction, the answer is: let him desire to live under those tutors and overseers, and to pass his life in those families, schools, churches, and commonwealths where discipline and correction flourish. If he reveres and loves correctors and chastisers, he will sometimes even prefer mourning to joy, Ecclesiastes 7:3, 4, 5, 6. See an excellent example of this matter in David, who compares reproof to precious ointment and considers it unjust that it should be withheld, Psalm 141:5.

In the latter half-verse he confirms the former doctrine from its contraries. What sort of thing this is can easily be discovered from the comparison of the preceding statements. For if anyone asks who hates discipline and correction, the ready answer is: the person who prefers to live in those families, schools, churches, and commonwealths where he may do everything at his own discretion and according to his desire; the person who bears being rebuked badly and unfairly hates the one who rebukes him. Finally, as Job 36:21 says, it is the person who chooses vanity or sin rather than affliction. By attaching the designation of a brutish and bestial person, he marks as though with black charcoal the person who hates correction. First, because, like a brute, he is destitute of knowledge of excellent things and has no more understanding than an ox and an ass. By contrast, just as one who loves discipline is zealous for knowledge, so he obtains knowledge: *Correct a wise man, and he himself will be wiser*, Proverbs 9. Next, just as brute animals attack with mouth or hooves those who lash them with blows, so haters of discipline attack the person who tries to chastise them. For just as brute animals, when they are bled or have medicine applied so that they may be relieved of disease or otherwise cared for, leap upon their masters who wish them well, so do those who hate correction. To this is added Proverbs 9:8.

It must further be noted that these people seek a vain reputation for wisdom and manliness by the very fact that they refuse to bear chastisement, and for this reason are marked by the Holy Spirit with the black charcoal of a bestial nature.

Finally, it must be noted by what kind of correction the brute who hates correction is chiefly identified. For although in the former half-verse he had treated correction generally, in the latter he brings forward into the midst one species of correction. If anyone does not bear even this, he appears to be beyond hope of salvation. For one who does not endure words of rebuke—how will he endure blows? By this very argument Nabal’s servant convicts his master of folly and brutish stupidity, because he would not bear anyone who admonished him even with a word, 1 Samuel 25:17. By contrast, the godly and those who love correction should prepare themselves for even the bitterest chastisements and should not show themselves rebellious or refractory, even if the physician applied iron or fire for the cure.

Verse 2. Favor from Jehovah advances a good man; but he condemns a wicked man.

The rendering adopted by nearly all interpreters—*the good man has elicited favor*, and so forth—appears unable to stand, for this meaning of the verb does not correspond to the meaning found in Psalms 1 and 40:9 and Isaiah 58. Next, a nominative case is rarely placed before a verb among the Hebrews. If it were placed first, a pronoun would have to be used, and the translation would run in this manner: *the good man himself elicits or brings forth*, and so forth. Finally, the antithesis of the latter half-verse declares that טוב (*good*) is in the accusative case, as is איש מזמות (*a man of crimes*). The purpose, moreover, is to declare God’s constant and effectual love toward the good, and, by contrast, his hatred toward the wicked.

In the former half-verse he consoles the godly, who tremble and shake because of their exceeding weakness,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 166 | printed columns 306–307]

especially when they consider Satan’s fury and the world’s hostility toward them, by pronouncing that it will be well with them, and indeed that day by day God will provide for them, that they will be heaped with benefits from God, and blessed both in soul and body. For he not only does not permit the godly to fall away from salvation, but also further adorns and increases them with all his excellent gifts. An outstanding testimony to this is Psalm 119, section 9, where he also assigns the cause of God’s benignity toward human beings: namely, that he himself is good and therefore delights to do good, especially to the good. For since he was good and kind toward us when we bore a hostile and alien mind toward him, what will he do toward those who have been reconciled? If he conferred blessing upon us when we were evil, what will he do toward those who are good and have turned from wickedness? Romans 5. And although human beings do not advance the good but rather press them down, God will nevertheless advance them: *Those who honor me I will honor*, 1 Samuel 2:30.

And just as this passage is full of consolation against our distrust, so it contains an admonition that every person should examine and test himself to see whether he is making progress in piety. For if we are good and godly, we ought to grow in piety: Romans 1, *from faith to faith*; 2 Corinthians 3:18; and Psalm 84.

Next, it must be noted what cause of this advancement the prophet assigns: namely, unmerited favor. By this we are taught that not only the first grace is conferred upon us freely, but also the second and the third, and whatever good is conferred upon us until the final day of life. This is against the papists, who say that God’s subsequent graces and gifts are conferred upon us because of our own condign merit.

He argues and illustrates the former part of the proverb from the contraries. Here, first, he opposes to a good man a *man of crimes*, or a criminal—that is, one devoted and addicted to crimes. For even a good man may sometimes commit a crime, yet he is not addicted to crimes as a man of crimes is. Next, when he says that the Lord will condemn him, he teaches by this that day by day the Lord will strip him of every good by taking away and diminishing what he has—as happens to those who are condemned to death by the sentence of a judge—and will declare him condemned. And although people lift him up and proclaim every good thing about him, and either are unwilling or, because of his remarkable skill in concealing crimes—which appears to be indicated by the word מזמות, *crimes*—are unable to do otherwise, God will nevertheless crush and overwhelm him with the sentence of condemnation. See Luke 8, where even what the wicked do not have, or what they appear to have, will be taken away.

Here ask how Paul’s statement can stand, in which it is affirmed that God justifies the ungodly, Romans 4:5, when here, by contrast, God is said to condemn the wicked person. The answer is that this passage teaches what God does as the just judge of the world, but the other what he does as mediator and savior of those whom, from sheer, pleasing, and gratuitous goodwill, he has resolved to deliver from the battle line of divine wrath.

Verse 3. A man is not established by wickedness, but the root of the righteous is moved.

The word *root* is a metaphor drawn from trees, which, when sunk and fixed deeply and profoundly in the earth, are not easily moved. The purpose of the proverb is to teach that the prosperity of the wicked—which presents to everyone’s eyes a great appearance and likeness of the firmness and duration of all things—is fleeting and perishable; but that the condition of the godly, although exposed to perpetual agitation and most turbulent tossings, is fixed and immovable.

In the former part of this proverb there is a tacit concession, for it is just as if

[1632 witness | PDF p. 167 | printed columns 308–309]

he were saying that, by wickedness and ingenious devices—now by fraud, now by force—a person can obtain a condition outwardly happy and sumptuous, but cannot preserve and sustain it for a long time, much less for eternity. Psalms 73 and 37 teach that everything flows for the wicked according to their soul’s desire, and that God sometimes surpasses their coveted wishes. Examples are set before us in the Assyrian, Persian, Greek, and Roman tyrants, whose ambition and ambitious arts succeeded so well that their empire [extended] to the same bounds by which nearly the whole world was contained. See Daniel 5:19: all peoples and languages feared him, and so forth; whom he wished he put to death, whom he wished he preserved alive; whom he wished he exalted, and whom he wished he cast down. Therefore, people should not consider themselves any more righteous or any less hateful to God if their counsels have been successful, since up to this point God advances not only the wicked, but also those who strive after gain and honors by crooked methods and byways. But if they wish to determine and deliver a judgment concerning anyone’s piety and righteousness from the judgment of external things, they must look closely at what his end and final outcome will be. For it will be advantageous to turn the mind and eyes, led away from the condition of present things, toward the end and final state of people of this sort. Let them put their heads and counsels together, join hand to hand, gather forces, and apply every engine; nevertheless, they will never bring it about that they add permanence to affairs conducted according to their desire. Psalm 37:10: [*Yet a little while, and the wicked is not; even if you consider his place diligently, nevertheless he is not.*] The same examples commemorated above are proofs of the truth proposed here. The example of Ahab, however, is illustrious. When he had received an answer from the Prophet—or rather from God through the Prophet—that he would be torn out root and stock and that he would not obtain a son whom he might designate as heir, he thought that by his own counsels and inventions he could render that counsel of God void. First, therefore, he took many wives and concubines, by whom seventy children were begotten, hoping that at least one would survive to succeed his dead father. Next, he committed the guardianship and education of each of these to men of the highest nobility who dwelt in the most fortified city of Samaria, so that, bound to him by his favor and strengthened by their own defense and the strength of the city, they might stand invincible against the enemy in defense of his children. But how far he fell from that hope, the history in 1 Kings 10 bears witness. Hence in Psalm 1 they are compared to the husks of chaff. See Job 20:4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and Jeremiah 17 concerning what lies on the summit of a mountain. Nor is it contradictory that [text damaged in image] is that which breaks forth and with its own green [text damaged in image] unfolds itself. For the former passage teaches what they are in the beginning, and the latter what they are at the end; the former what they are in God’s judgment, and the latter how they appear in human judgment. Nevertheless, that מעט (*a little while*) in Psalm 37:10 must be understood prudently, since it is evident from Psalm 17 that the happiness and prosperity of the wicked is sometimes transmitted to their sons.

In the latter half-verse he indicates the firmness of the righteous person’s condition by the illustrious likeness of a tree that has driven deep roots into the earth. Here, as in the former part, there is a certain tacit concession, implicit and wrapped up, which can be expressed and unfolded in this manner: the righteous can indeed be shaken and tossed by the storms of temptations and the winds of afflictions, but they cannot be moved from their place or uprooted. For whether we consider the hope of another life or think of the enjoyment of happiness in the present age, the righteous person is sometimes attacked and beaten. Yet he never falls away entirely from the former, and he recovers to some extent the advantages of the present life that he had lost; especially in blessing his posterity is by God

[1632 witness | PDF p. 168 | printed columns 310–311]

enriched. For it is said that *the righteous person is scarcely and with difficulty saved in this life*, 1 Peter 4:18. Jacob, Abraham, Job, and David are examples; yet by the incredible power and wisdom of God they were delivered and vindicated from destruction. As for the hope of the future life, Satan is sometimes permitted to winnow and sift them, Luke 22:31, but faith does not fail, verse 32, nor do the gates of hell prevail against the faithful, Matthew 16. Therefore, what Psalm 34:20 says is most true: *The evils of the righteous are many; out of them all the Lord delivers him.* See also Job 4:7. Hence they are compared to trees planted beside watercourses, Jeremiah 17 and Psalm 1. But Zechariah 12:3 is an outstanding passage, since what is said of the Church universally is rightly applied to each of its members.

Verse 4. A vigorous wife [is] the crown of her husband; but the woman who causes shame [is] like rottenness in his bones.

By *a wife of strength* he understands a woman who diligently performs the duties of the mistress of a household and who, because of the weakness of her sex, does not abandon the duty incumbent upon her. She is described copiously and precisely in Proverbs 31. Here איש חיל (*a mighty man*) in Exodus 18:20 means one who is endowed with those virtues by which he is equipped to discharge duly the office committed to him. Therefore, those whom verse 21 describes, by a particular enumeration of the virtues that pertain to administering that management, as fit to undertake the commonwealth, and upon whom in verse 25 we read that charge laid for execution, he calls only אנשי חיל (*mighty men*).

The noun עטרה (*crown*) is a metaphor for adornment and dignity. In בעצמותיו (*in his bones*) there is a synecdoche for the whole body.

In the beginning God gave a wife to man so that she might be his helper in all the consolations of the present life. Since a good portion of these consists in the man’s adornment and dignity, it is plain that she was also created and formed for this end: that she might be an honor and adornment to him. But the Apostle teaches this matter even more precisely when he says that the woman is the glory and image of the man, 1 Corinthians 11:17, and that she ought in some measure to serve the man’s glory, as the man serves Christ’s glory. He also assigns the reason for this exactly: she was taken from the man and brought into the light for his sake. And since she is the image of the man and represents him, the more piously, prudently, and justly she conducts herself, the more esteem she adds to her husband; and the contrary if she acts otherwise. Therefore, the Apostle teaches that a woman’s immodesty, when she prays in the Church with uncovered head, redounds to the reproach of her husband, 1 Corinthians 11:5; on the other side, her modesty blossoms into the man’s praise. See Proverbs 31:23. Of all the crowns that wives weave for their husbands, however, none is more excellent than when, by their own virtue and piety, they allure husbands who are enemies of both to the love of piety and virtue, or advance those already imbued with them. If someone asks how it happens that a wife’s vigor or languor, her virtue or vice, so closely embraces her husband’s honor or dishonor, the answer is ready: because of the singular union and incomparable bond of wife with husband. For if honor returns to parents or masters from a well-instructed son or even a servant, how much more from a wife? Hence it also happens that we often see idle husbands covered with crimes treated better and more gently than their condition warrants, out of regard for their wives, through whose virtues they shine.

By calling a good and prudent wife her husband’s crown, moreover, he teaches that she not only honors the man, but is the sum and most precious of all ornaments. For if she had furnished only a ring, it would nevertheless have been a great

[1632 witness | PDF p. 169 | printed columns 312–313]

ornament to a man: If he had represented her by a golden necklace, he would have designated a greater accumulation of glory; but when he adds the splendor and dignity of a crown to his head, he denotes an honor greater than which none can be bestowed. From this it also follows in what place a dutiful wife is to be held and how highly she is to be valued. In 1 Peter 3 she is compared to a vessel very choice and precious, but fragile and perishable; for that reason a certain honor must be accorded to her, lest she be broken by rough and harsh handling. In this passage, however, she is compared to a crown, which those who are able to walk crowned with it value very highly. For we see to what safe guardians and faithful men the crown of a kingdom is entrusted for safekeeping. Therefore, since a good wife is a crown made not of gold or silver, nor adorned with inset gems, but of piety, chastity, and prudence drawn down from heaven out of God's treasury in heaven, it is right to exercise so much greater care for her as this latter crown is preferred by infinite degrees to one that will perish.

To this woman he opposes another who brings disgrace upon her husband by her lustfulness, ignorance, levity, intemperance, drunkenness, avarice, and other defects to which the weakness of that sex is liable. The former was a crown; this one makes her husband appear to everyone like a branded criminal. The former increased her husband's honor; this one greatly detracts from the honor accorded him. Although this is grievous in itself and full of distress, the woman's wickedness does not rest in this ignominy of her husband; but just as she makes him an object of open shame, so she also brings along an inward evil within his very entrails and bones. For partly by public infamy, and partly by domestic insolence and obstinacy toward her husband, she makes the man so bloody that his bones are dried up with grief and anguish, from which decay and certain death follow. An example of this is established in the first chapter of Esther, where Vashti's refusal to come to the king obscured the kingdom more than all the king's boundless expenditures, banquets, and drinking parties—prepared for the enlargement of his own honor—had adorned it. From this it also follows that a pious and prudent woman not only adds outward splendor and dignity to her husband, but is like a continual feast by which he is inwardly refreshed in his soul and cheered to discharge all his duties more conveniently. From all these things it is further established how much care and effort people must devote—in prayers before God and in cautious and circumspect conduct toward other people—so that they may make the best choice of a spouse.

Verse 5. The thoughts of the righteous [are] just; the cunning counsels of the wicked, deceitful.

In the words משפט (*judicium*, judgment) and מרמה (*dolus*, deceit) there is a metonymy of the adjunct, which nevertheless is not without emphasis.

By this proverb, as by very many others, the righteous are separated and distinguished from the wicked, so that, having recognized God's love toward them through the operation of the Holy Spirit in their hearts, they may receive consolation and joy and give thanks to God for so great a bounty. The wicked, on the other hand, beholding the condemnation in which they lie buried, are to be struck with dismay and anxiously seek a remedy. How useful this is to people appears from the fact that the godly, on account of the common stain of sin that adheres to them and the many fruits that spring from it, are often brought to doubt their own condition; the wicked, by contrast, flattering themselves with the deceitfulness of sin, thrust far away from their own thought—and away from themselves—the condemnation in whose jaws they stand. Yet the two are separated and distinguished not, as that man prescribed, by a fence five feet high, but by far the greatest interval. In this proverb, however, they are distinguished by their interior and inwardly hidden effects, namely their thoughts.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 170 | printed columns 314–315]

Therefore this must especially be observed: it is people's duty to seek knowledge of themselves from their very thoughts, so that they may judge what sort of people they are—good or evil, righteous or unrighteous. Thoughts too are fruits of a tree, from which a lawful judgment concerning the tree may be rendered. Others, indeed, are able to learn what a person is like only from outward works, words, or deeds; but let us form a judgment about ourselves not only from our external works but also from our inward ones. Indeed, from the thoughts that leap forth during dreams, let us form a conjecture about what disposition we have toward God and what disposition toward our neighbor. Therefore it belongs to the godly to strike with a censor's rod their thoughts during sleep, if they are evil and vicious, so that they may be instructed by them even toward humility; conversely, they are to be raised and stirred by good thoughts to proclaim God's kindness. Next, it must be noted that the first thoughts that arise and spring up in the godly tend toward judgment and justice. For just as, from the depraved stain of sin implanted in us, before we are reborn in the Gospel by the power of the Spirit, thoughts flow forth not unlike their parents, Genesis 6:5, so, when that stain has been cleansed by the Spirit, we produce analogous fruits of every kind. From this is refuted the error of those who think that thoughts are free and, as they say, pay no tax. They are not arraigned, indeed, in a civil or ecclesiastical court, but they are compelled to appear before God's tribunal. When Daniel and his companions understood that snares were being set for them at the Babylonian king's table, they immediately resolved among themselves to preserve themselves from that pollution. These things, however, are not said as though corrupt thoughts do not enter the minds of the righteous, but so that the godly may strive for this: that they be pure even in heart, Matthew 5.

*The prudence of the counsels of the wicked*, and so forth. Not only thoughts that spring up suddenly, but the things they have concocted by employing craft and meditation, are referred and directed toward deceit. It is as if he were saying: the first thoughts of the righteous may be vicious, yet when they consider them more deeply, they unweave them and do not continue; the thoughts of the wicked, once conceived, are strengthened, and when they proceed to deliberate about some matter, their thoughts and counsels become all the worse for it. For since they draw whatever counsel they take from nowhere other than their own depraved and corrupt mind, the things that spring from so bitter a root must necessarily retain the same flavor as that from which they derive their origin. From this it is also clear that the deliberations and counsels of the righteous, which they form after taking time, have more of the taste and fragrance of judgment than chance thoughts that can easily turn aside.

Verse 6. The words of the wicked lie in wait for blood, but the mouth of the upright rescues them.

In the word פי (*os*, mouth) there is a metonymy of the subject for the words of the mouth. This proverb has the same aim as the preceding one: to establish a distinction between the righteous and the unrighteous, and that by matters from which the common mass of people do not seek the distinction between good and evil. For just as each person claims great freedom for himself in thinking, and in that respect deems himself ἀνυπεύθυνον (*anypeuthynon*, unaccountable), so also in words and speech he promises himself great impunity. For as long as they are only words—which people call wind and breath—they can scarcely be brought to believe that guilt arises from them. Nevertheless, Christ, in order to meet this error, plainly affirms that an account must be given for every idle word. Hence it is that by these two proverbs he checks and constrains thoughts and words, passing over deeds, which, if they are evil, are condemned by everyone. His judgment of this matter is sought not from a violation of the first table, because almost everyone wishes to appear to draw near to God with words and tongue, so that it is more difficult

[1632 witness | PDF p. 171 | printed columns 316–317]

to detect their impiety from their speech. But why does he seek a judgment of this matter from the sixth commandment rather than from the other commandments of the second table, when nevertheless people more frequently strike against other commandments in speech, especially through vanity and falsehood? Answer: the distinction is most fittingly taken here, because the wicked man nowhere more openly betrays himself than in drawing his tongue against the righteous and godly man, and that not merely to overthrow him in goods or reputation, but to take his life from him. Indeed, he will attack with his tongue others who are wicked as he himself is, but he hates the godly and righteous in a singular manner. See the testimonies from Scripture in Romans 3. See also Psalm 37:12, how the wicked gnash their teeth.

They lie in wait for blood with their mouth in two ways, namely, in order to take away life:

{
  separately { the present,
               the future,
  jointly both.
}

For the wicked man lies in wait for the life of the righteous and the blood of souls when he endeavors to join the righteous man to the fellowship of his own wickedness, which is full of peril. If he does not accomplish this, then he attacks his present life, being unable to endure the conduct and way of life which, even in the greatest silence, convicts his wickedness and makes it hateful to everyone. See John 1.3.12. Sometimes by one and the same work he lies in wait for both, namely when he entices him to a crime of such a kind that by perpetrating it he would be liable to a capital charge before the magistrate and guilty concerning his soul before God. An example of the former is found in the Pharisees, who first tried in every way to draw the Baptist away from his duty toward Christ. When they could not accomplish that, they laid snares for his life, as is established from Christ's testimony in Matthew 17. An example of the latter snares is in Proverbs 1:11, 12, 13, 14.

From these things it is clear what the duty of the righteous is, wholly opposed to those earlier crimes. Hence the mouth of the righteous was previously called a fountain of life and a tree of life. Examples of these things are found in Reuben, who exhorted his brothers not to inflict violence upon their brother; in Daniel, who rescued himself and his companions from death; and in Ebed-melech, who opened his mouth for Jeremiah. See Proverbs 24:8, 9. Therefore the righteous must take care that the wicked are not found readier and thirstier to shed blood than they are to draw people in danger out of the jaws of death.

Verse 7. The wicked overturn themselves so that they do not exist, but the family of the righteous shall stand.

In the word בית (*domus*, house) there is a metonymy of the subject for the persons attached to it. In יעמד (*stabit*, shall stand) there is a synecdoche of species, for by the word *standing* he understands every kind of firmness.

This overthrow, which the Prophet denounces against the wicked, is external and is one that God executes in this present age, not that which is to come after this life. This is clear from the adjunct of this overthrow (“so that they do not exist”), for the destruction that follows them when they have expired takes the wicked away.

Next, from the other half-verse this is plainly confirmed by the opposites. For since the penalty opposite to this reward and hurled against the wicked is contained only within the boundaries of this life, it is plain that the gracious promise toward the godly is circumscribed by the same bounds. For in the age to come there is no question of propagating or extinguishing a family. Just as there is no husband or wife there, so there is no father or son, grandfather or grandson, and so forth. From this it follows that in the Scriptures the Holy Spirit, by the promise of happiness in the present age, spurs the godly to cultivate piety, and by the threat of troubles in this life deters the wicked from their impiety. In this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 172 | printed columns 318–319]

matter God's counsel is not obscure. For since the godly do not aspire to the future life in such a way that they take no account of the present one, and since they desire that it may go well with them on earth, God has willed by this promise of the present life's advantages to confirm them in duty and sharpen their zeal. But since the wicked are wholly fixed upon the happiness of the present life, they are rightly to be frightened by this threat of God, brought to their senses, and recalled to duty. For although they are not led by desire for the future life, nevertheless, in order that provision may be made for them in this very age, they must see that they do not depart from the rule of God's word. How much force this one matter has, even among the wicked, to hold them in duty is sufficiently declared by the example of the Sadducees, who for that reason alone were zealous for the Mosaic law: that by this means it might go well with them in the present life and abundant provision might be made for them. In this matter God, the most merciful father of parents, imitates toward his own tender children a prudence joined with indulgence: parents, in order to keep their children in duty, entice them not only by the promise of an inheritance but also by promises of trifling things, especially those by which they have understood them to be captivated and delighted.

That God is to be understood in this passage as the one who overturns the wicked is easily gathered both because he administers everything that happens in the world and also from the opposite antithesis. For since no one, not even the most righteous, establishes his own family except insofar as it is made firm by God, it is certain that the overthrow of families flows from God, the avenger of wickedness. To this pertains the fact that punishments exacted for sins are attributed to God as the most righteous judge of the world, and not to the wicked themselves, who, so far as it lies within them, shrink from punishment. To the same point belongs the fact that in the compressed and contracted statements of Scripture, where something must necessarily be understood, recourse must especially be had to things that are most obvious and that admit the fewest exceptions of all. From this it is gathered that God is the author of the overthrow and destruction of the wicked in this world; that is, the punishment justly due to sins must be credited to him as the most righteous judge of the world. This is clear not only from various testimonies of Scripture (such as that there is no evil in the city that the Lord has not done, and so forth), but from the opposite establishment of the righteous. Since everyone acknowledges that this proceeds from the Lord, the same must necessarily occur in the overthrow of the wicked. Therefore, in the destruction of the wicked and in the continuance and prolongation of the good through many generations, let us turn our eyes away from those who were the instruments of each and cast them upon God as the fountain of justice on the one side and of mercy on the other.

Since he designates an overthrow proper to the wicked, it must be observed that God destroys them as though struck by a thunderbolt. He does not strike them with a light arm, but with an outstretched hand; he does not flick them with one or another finger, but drives his fist into them. Or, to come to what is more proper to this passage, he does not merely throw down the pinnacle and roof, but utterly tears the whole building away from its very foundations. To this pertains the passage of the Prophet where the godly are said to be struck by God as upon the branch or vine shoot, but the wicked to be cut as at the root. See further this distinction in Isaiah 27:9, 10 and Malachi 3:19: “he shall not leave branch or root.” Therefore, although they are supported by the favor even of the highest men, fortified by the defenses of friends, and buttressed by the force of money, they nevertheless cannot vindicate themselves from overthrow and utter demolition. For God, who threatens and effects it, is too powerful for anyone to resist him. Therefore, although the families of the wicked may sometimes appear to people—even to the godly—to be so firmly established and to have fixed roots, they nevertheless are

[1632 witness | PDF p. 173 | printed columns 320–321]

in reality like a spider's house and like the hut that a watchman made, Job 27:18. But what follows is graver, when he adds that their families are cast down in such a way that they do not exist. For houses are demolished from their foundations and afterward are rebuilt and restored. Trees, likewise, are torn out by the roots and afterward, when transplanted, flourish more happily. But the family of the wicked is so cut down that God never has pity upon their dust; it is so uprooted that it never afterward puts down roots. Seek examples of this from the wicked kings of Israel; hence Psalm 52:8 concerning Doeg.

The two half-verses give mutual assistance to one another in their own proof and confirmation. For the fact that the house and family of the righteous remains is an argument that the wicked are to be overthrown; conversely, the fact that no remnants of the wicked exist is an indication that the family of the righteous will survive. The examples of Abraham, David, and others teach that this is indeed true. And this is all the more wonderful because, though the families of the wicked are overthrown, their destruction is scarcely ever sought by the godly, while the family of the godly nevertheless stands, although its punishment by death is continually demanded by the wicked. This is illustrated still further by the fact that the generally numerous offspring of the wicked, who give hope of a future posterity, are suddenly removed from the midst, while the posterity of the godly hangs suspended by the slenderest thread of a single son; yet he is rendered so fruitful that from him alone a nation often arises and multiplies. To this is added that the wives of the godly have been found to be rather advanced in age and sterile for many years, while those of the wicked, by contrast, have been both fruitful and young. See on the one side the example of Ahab and others, and on the other those of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Anna the mother of Samson, and so forth. Finally, it must be noted that both these promises toward the righteous and these threats toward the wicked—because they concern perishable things—are not universally accomplished by God, but only insofar as it serves God's glory and the salvation of the godly. For it is possible that the stock of the godly, such as Solomon and Josiah, may be extinguished, while the families of the wicked are extended through more generations and ages. Yet this very thing occurs for the good of the godly, whose sterility is compensated by other benefits, Isaiah 56:4, 5; and likewise for the evil of the wicked even in this life, when posterity is propagated to them so that they become a proverb among everyone because of their beggary, as in Psalm 109, or because of shameful and foul diseases, as is related of Joab's posterity in 2 Samuel 3:29. Sometimes also they are propagated for a longer time not only to their own evil but to the singular advantage of the godly, Psalm 59:12.

Verse 8. A man shall be praised according to the measure of his understanding, but one perverse in mind shall be held in contempt.

By this proverb is refuted the error of those people who measure the goodness of each matter by prosperous success, by an apparently good outcome, or by the opinion of others. Hence they praise only those for whom matters have fallen out according to their desire, and they condemn those for whom matters do not succeed according to their wish. This error, however, is refuted by the fact that the goodness of matters must be judged not from the outcome but from the causes. Thus, if something has proceeded from sound and wholesome counsel, it should be regarded as praiseworthy and at length attain praise, whatever danger or harm may result from it. By contrast, although cunning and crafty counsels may obtain popular favor and seem to promise their advisers something great, they will nevertheless at length be objects of contempt and ignominy. For this proverb does not speak of the praise and ignominy that are due to people according to their merit, but of those which are at length actually rendered, although for a time it may be concealed

[1632 witness | PDF p. 174 | printed columns 322–323]

and lie hidden, or even contrary appearances are displayed. Therefore, in this passage not just any understanding is meant, but that which the Holy Spirit has created in the mind through the intervention of the preaching of the word. For nothing merits true praise (such as is understood in this passage) unless it flows from that praiseworthy source. See Psalm 119, where he affirms that by the word of God he is more prudent than princes, elders, and enemies. Next, the understanding noted is not one that is held enclosed and silent within the mind, but one that, declared by certain effects, breaks forth before the eyes and ears of others. For how can that which has not come to their knowledge merit praise from men?

This Proverb, however, has a twofold use:
{
  the former regards others,
  the latter ourselves.
}

In the former, it must be considered that, if something has been done by others which is not wicked in its own nature, we should not immediately assent to its condemnation because God not only follows that thing with no blessing but even burdens it further with misfortunes. Especially, however, if an action has been commanded by God, the itching desire to speak evil of it must be restrained merely because that action has as companions certain inconveniences which seem to strip it of its innocence. It will profit us ourselves very greatly, when deeds rightly done incur the censure of malevolent men because they have not attained so favorable and happy a success, to reckon within ourselves that they were prudently undertaken according to God's prescription, and therefore that there must be no doubt that God will sometime give us the outcome we desire; and that they are to be heaped with all the greater praise, the more mockeries cast upon us on their account we have experienced. For since, as Solomon testifies in Ecclesiastes 9, all outward things happen to us just as they do to the wicked, nevertheless we shall readily distinguish all our things from the deeds and pursuits of the wicked if the things we do have been administered from understanding, that is, by the prescription of the word.

If anyone should wish to judge Abraham's departure from his homeland—when famine pressed him in that very land in which he was a sojourner—or Jacob's return from Laban into his homeland, when he was surrounded by both dangers and inconveniences; if anyone should wish to judge Israel's battle, first against the citizens of Ai and then against the Benjamites, from the beginnings of those battles, they will seem to everyone to be accused and condemned together for folly and lack of consideration. But in the end, just as all these were always worthy of praise in the very beginnings of their departures and battles, so at the outcome and conclusion they were heaped with praises, because these things were undertaken from skill and knowledge of the sacred Scriptures and by the prompting of the Holy Spirit. Here the example of Mordecai has a place. Because he withstood Haman to his face when he possessed the highest authority, he might seem capable of being condemned for ignorance. But if this deed of his is judged from the word of God—because God had commanded that divine honor be given to no one, much less to such an enemy, against whom together with his whole nation God had decreed that an ἄσπονδον (*implacable*) war be waged by the Jews)—what, then, is lacking in it that is worthy of a wise man? By contrast, if anyone should wish to assess and judge from the outcome, rather than from the deliberation and prudent counsel involved in undertaking those wars, the battles of the Assyrians and Babylonians, who subdued entire kingdoms merely by their arrival and appearance, he may seem to himself to see things that extol them with praises. Nevertheless, because of their ambition and insatiable avarice, like engulfing chasms, they must be condemned by all men of sound judgment. Although their splendor and the dignity of their empire rescued them from contempt for a time, yet when stripped and dispossessed of that dignity and splendor they are held in contempt by all.

There are, moreover, others to whom this Proverb applies. For very many are found who

[1632 witness | PDF p. 175 | printed columns 324–325]

adopt such methods that, as though by a trumpet blast, their lavishness in refreshing the poor may become known. These indeed are esteemed for a time and proclaimed by everyone, although this expenditure upon the poor has not proceeded from an understanding mind. For that reason the time will come, when the light of the Gospel dispels the darkness of ignorance, that men of this kind will be held in contempt. By contrast, those who, at Scripture's command, send portions to the poor and do not pursue petty glory in relieving the poor, but make their gift prudently, as circumstances permit, although they may be held in contempt for a while, will nevertheless at last be honored with the praise due them from the word. Everyone condemns Mordecai at first sight because he withstood Haman to his face; but because he governed the whole matter according to Scripture's judgment, he at length obtained singular praise from that very thing. But if this reward comes less from men, it certainly comes from God; if not in this age, then certainly in the future. To be praised at last by him and adorned with commendations will be glorious, just as, by contrast, to be despised by him and marked with reproach must be deemed shameful, 2 Corinthians 10, last verse.

In the latter part, it is taught that one who does not direct either his sayings or his deeds from the word of God, however much he may initially obtain praise from men, will nevertheless at last be held in contempt. Thus Ziba, who by lying acquired his master's inheritance, was regarded as prudent, but afterward became hateful to all posterity.

Verse 9. Better [is] he who acknowledges himself lowly and has a servant than he who exalts himself with honor and lacks food.

In the words לחם (*bread*) and עבד (*servant*) there is a synecdoche. By the word עבד he understands things suitable for passing life conveniently and honorably. By the word לחם, however, he understands those things without which this life cannot in any way be endured, not even inconveniently. For a servant is a support of this life which someone can lack, although inconveniently. Bread, however, or sustenance, is of such a kind that those deprived of it must depart from life.

The aim of this Proverb, therefore, is to teach that the modest man, who thinks moderately of himself, passes this life more conveniently than those who, taking lofty spirits upon themselves, aspire to things which they do not reach and are then deprived of all the things by which they might sustain this life. This is contrary to what men think within themselves when, thrusting themselves into great affairs, they hope that they will obtain something preeminent and great.

The fact that someone thinks modestly and humbly of himself is the reason he does not seek higher things for himself, but, content with the common lot, pursues nothing peculiar or extraordinary. Hence it happens that, since he does not embrace more things than he can contain and grasp, as it were, in his fist, he obtains them with less difficulty and then preserves what he has acquired. For when our powers and efforts, divided among more things, extend more widely, they are known to encompass less and acquire less for themselves. We must therefore take care not to carry desires too loose and unbelted. When they grow without measure, we consequently lack things possessed even by porters. From this arises incomparable torment: both because an insatiable desire is not satisfied and because we also lack those things which the necessity of nature demands. See Isaiah 5, where those who gape after the fields and buildings of others are at last reduced to the utmost poverty, even to the point that they are compelled to beg alms, Lamentations 5. The truth of this Proverb in both its parts is illustrated in two kings, father and son, Josiah and Jeconiah. For the one, content with suitable and fitting clothing and food, enjoyed those same things, while the son, led by an insatiable

[1632 witness | PDF p. 176 | printed columns 326–327]

desire to have many things and to enlarge his condition, was for that very reason thrust down into extreme starvation when he was carried captive to Babylon. See the example of Absalom and Adonijah, who, exalting themselves and assembling a great retinue of servants, at last fell into the utmost misery.

This vanity is found among men of our age. You may see women who dress themselves so delicately and go about attended by such a retinue in order to display themselves that meanwhile the husband is compelled to feed himself on black bread and yesterday's broth. There are also many men who, in order to maintain and advertise the lineage and nobility they possess—or which they do not possess—make such great expenditures both in maintaining attendants and in acquiring more exquisite clothing that, after contracting heavy debt from it, they are cast into prisons and perish through hunger and starvation.

Verse 10. A righteous man cares for the life of his beast, but the mercies of the wicked are [mercies] cruel.

In אבוסי (*of a beast*) there is a synecdoche of number; in יודע (*knows*) a metonymy of the adjunct.

He establishes another distinction between the righteous man and the wicked, drawn from an explanation of the sixth commandment which embraces both the part of obedience and that of transgression.

First, he locates obedience to the commandment in this: if someone cares for the life of a beast and is solicitous that it suffer no harm. It might perhaps appear strange that we refer either the care or the neglect of a dumb beast to the source of the sixth commandment, since that commandment is wholly occupied with protecting the person of one's neighbor, under whose compass a beast cannot be included. But it must be noted that the commandments contain not only those things by which they are directly kept or violated, but also those which are connected with this observance or transgression. For God surrounds his commandments with certain hedges, by whose protection they are not so easily broken through. And just as the fence and wall by which an estate is enclosed is included under the name of the estate and coalesces into one thing with the estate itself, so it also happens in the commandments of God and in those things which are indirectly related to the commandments. Therefore, although savagery toward brute animals does not properly or most nearly touch the person of a man, nevertheless, because by these beginnings one proceeds to cruelty against men, this cruelty toward beasts is not unfittingly treated as a kind of inhumanity. To this pertains the command about not taking the mother together with the young and about not boiling a kid in its mother's milk. Likewise: *You shall not muzzle the ox*, Deuteronomy 25:4. Balaam's beast once for all ages opened its mouth, so that by that speech it might condemn every savagery toward dumb beasts. Hence in Exodus 23 God wishes the proof of our charity toward our neighbor to consist in the help which is rendered to him in the case of an ass or ox. Moreover, in Jonah, when God wished to leave his mercy toward men attested, among other tokens of his mercy he adduces the fact that beasts were found in so great a number in the city of Nineveh, Jonah 4:11. For if someone, from consciousness of the divine commandment, has been slower to shed the blood of a beast, we will consider him certainly much more averse and alien to violating a man.

Moreover, from the περιβόλω (*enclosure*) of this one commandment, by which it is hedged about, there emerges an excellent and distinguished rule to render us cautious and circumspect in observing the whole law: namely, that we should withdraw as far as possible from the borders of sin and should not only not carry it in our hands and bosom but not even touch it with the smallest finger. Jude 23: μισοῦντες κὴ τὸν ἀπὸ τῆς σαρκὸς ἐσπιλωμένον χιτῶνα, “hating even the garment stained by the flesh.” Therefore the righteous man will take care not only to avoid gross sins and those falling under the censure of

[1632 witness | PDF p. 177 | printed columns 328–329]

everyone, but also those which are slighter than the common people can recognize: not only beams, but specks; not only camels, but gnats. He will take care not only to observe the weightier matters of the law, but even to tithe mint. Add to this what is written in Luke 16: one faithful in the least will prove himself faithful also in the greatest. Here David's example is illustrious: his own conscience struck him because he had cut off a small piece of Saul's garment.

In the latter half-verse it is taught that the condition of the wicked is far different. For they exercise cruelty not only in those things which are condemned by the judgment of nearly all men; even in those very things in which they think they have shown mercy both to themselves and to others, they do not depart from cruelty and savagery. Thus Pharaoh seemed thoroughly humane to himself and others because at last he consented to dismiss and release the people with their children, except only for the herds and flocks. But to Moses and others who judged rightly, he seemed to have consulted too little for the advantage of the Israelites, who were not to leave so much as a hoof in Egypt. Thus Gamaliel seemed to almost everyone, even to many good men, to have been gentle and mild toward the apostles, since he consented that they not be delivered to death but beaten with scourges. This is seen most clearly in Pilate. For in order to rescue Christ from death, he handed him over to be beaten with thongs, with the result that through this crafty and cruel counsel of his Christ paid both penalties, that of scourging and that of death. Of this kind are those who bestow their alms upon the poor with insults. For it happens rather often that the poor man receives more sorrow from the reproaches than consolation from the money given.

Verse 11. He who tills his ground is satisfied with food; but he who pursues vain things [is] senseless.

In עבד אדמתו (*tilling his ground*) there is a synecdoche of one species—and that the chief—of lawful calling for the whole genus of a just and praiseworthy calling, as is established from the corresponding member, in which he generally opposes farmers to the idle and vain. In לחם (*bread*) there is also a synecdoche of a member for the whole.

The aim is that all, frightened away from idleness and vain occupations, should exercise themselves in a lawful calling permitted by the law of God.

In the former part two things are contained:
{
  duty,
  reward.
}

In the duty, it is first required that they devote themselves to labor. By God's command the same obligation rests upon all on account of Adam's fall, Genesis 3:19. Likewise, in 1 Thessalonians 4:11 the Apostle admonishes that one must labor with one's own hands. To this belong the things Solomon writes against idleness. This holds convicted beggars and the chiefs of beggars—the mendicant friars, monks, and so forth.

Next it is required that they not merely work, but do work worth doing and exercise themselves in an honorable art and occupation. For that reason he sets down the example of cultivating the earth, an occupation first and most ancient of all, and at the same time the most excellent of those performed by bodily strength. This πρωτόπλαστος ♂ (*the first-formed man*) practiced; this the Patriarchs practiced; this the greatest kings practiced, as is expressly recorded of Uzziah. To this pertains the Apostle's testimony, ἐργαζόμενος τὸ ἀγαθὸν ταῖς χερσί, “working what is good with his hands,” Ephesians 4:28. By this clause usurers are condemned: although they could trade with their own money, nevertheless, in order to provide both for their leisure and for certain returns, they devote themselves wholly to lending at interest. To this should be summoned all the merchants and artisans who are introduced as wailing in Revelation, and who once ministered by their trades and crafts to the spiritual fornication and luxury of the whore of Babylon. To this are referred all actors and wandering musicians who roam here and there.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 178 | printed columns 330–331]

Finally, all who serve their pleasures or games.

The third point in this half-verse is that they ought to cultivate their own proper field if they wish to share in the attached promise, and not put their sickle into another's harvest. For there are those who are so occupied in undertaking work that they are even ἀλλοτριεπίσκοποι (*meddlers in other men's affairs*). Indeed, there are very many who, abandoning their own affairs and the things which rest upon them by a certain peculiar duty, manage the affairs of others. Of this kind are those who seize offices and duties in the commonwealth and, in administering them, allow their own families to be deserted. Of this kind are not a few ministers among us who, abandoning their own flocks, fly over to feed the flocks of others—and civil flocks at that—thereby revealing themselves as true ἀλλοτριεπίσκοποι, 1 Peter 4:15.

But if someone asks how servants, who possess no field of their own and proper to themselves, are to keep this commandment—or, if they have one, cannot be free to cultivate it, because they are assigned to their masters' fields as long as they are bound by agreement to serve them—the response is that the master's field is rightly reckoned the servant's field, because by God's prescription the cultivation of it rests upon him.

The promise follows: he will be satisfied with all the good things necessary for preserving this present life; that is, God will so bless his labors that he will not lack that from which he may live conveniently. This promise seems to be contained in the threat declared to Adam, that in sorrow and labor he would eat his bread; by this it is indicated that, through undergoing these hardships, he would sustain this miserable life. Paul seems to promise something greater and more ample: God will not only abundantly supply what the laborer lacks but will further provide something for him to distribute to others in need, Ephesians 4:28. It must be noted here that God accommodates the promise in this passage to the nature of the preceding duty. For although under the law the advantages of the present life rather often foreshadow and seal the happiness of the future as well, nevertheless the promise made in this passage, being bounded by the advantage of this life, does not extend itself to the life that follows. For since the duty delineated in this verse is equally common to good and evil men and does not distinguish the upright from the wicked, as most other duties do, it follows that the reward appointed is of the same kind. (It must nevertheless be considered whether being satisfied with bread is not proper to the righteous man, because he rests in what has been acquired, while nothing satisfies the wicked.) (For the wicked man tills his own field just as the upright man does. Yet it should add a great spur to the godly for undertaking honorable labor that God has promised even wicked men that they will live from their labor. For if provision has been made for the wicked from their labor by God's blessing, much more will provision be made for the godly who conduct themselves honorably. Therefore, let the godly be ashamed of that fetid and rotten saying by which it is claimed that they cannot live from their honorable labor unless they mix false and corrupt things with true and upright ones. For if God has granted this to the wicked, who neither seek a blessing from him nor refer it to him, how much more will he provide it to the godly, who strive to honor him in both respects as the giver of strength and success? Although the benefit is common, nevertheless in its use there is by far the greatest difference. For in Psalm 128 the man who fears God is pronounced blessed when he eats from what his labor has acquired; but it can never fall or come to the wicked man to be blessed in the enjoyment of the things he has acquired.

But he who pursues vain men is senseless.

He illustrates the former part of the proverb by opposites. Here the first thing worthy of observation is that a principal enticement to idleness and vain occupations is the fact that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 179 | printed columns 332–333]

we see others pursuing that kind of life; whence these words often sound in our ears: Why should this not be permitted to me just as much as to him? Therefore the Apostle, in order to draw his people back from idleness, when he warns them to withdraw themselves from the company of those who are idle, also sets before their eyes his own example of labor for them to imitate. But as for who are vain, see Judges 9:4 and 11:3, and 2 Chronicles 13:7. For if those who follow others are fools, how much more leaders and chief priests. For this reason he makes mention instead of the followers, because they think themselves absolved and blameless on the ground that they have people whom they follow.

But in saying that the person who pursues such people is witless, this is true in both directions, whether we consider wisdom for this life or foresight for the life to come. For although that former duty of cultivating the earth does not establish the distinction between the good and the evil, nevertheless joining oneself to the idle and pursuing their practices distinguishes an evil man from a good one. Nor indeed is one to be numbered among the faithful who is worse than an unbeliever; such are those who take no account of their own people, so that provision may be made for them concerning the advantages of this life.

Verse 12. The wicked man desires the protection of evil men, but the root of the righteous gives [it].

Supply: that which the righteous desire.

In מצוד (I would translate it “net,” not “protection”; see whether *root* corresponds to *protection*) there is a metaphor of a net for those things which it draws in and entangles. The sense, moreover, is that the wicked, not content with the things obtained by their honorable labor, extend their desires to grasp other things; by these immoderate desires they are entangled in many evils. By contrast, the desires of the righteous are satisfied by those very fruits which God bestows upon them and upon their labors, an allusion being made to the cultivation of the earth, of which he made mention in the verse immediately preceding. The first part is confirmed by James 4:1, 2, 3; likewise 1 Timothy 6:8, 9, 10. Hence it is that Christ compares our immoderate desires and appetites to thorns, Matthew 13.

The latter part is afterwards confirmed from Romans 13:14, where the godly are said not to make provision for their flesh to fulfill its desires, but, having food and clothing, to be content with these, 1 Timothy 6:8. From this it comes about that their desires have not been extended without measure, and by that very fact a sufficiently abundant yield from their own fields, which they have cultivated, is supplied to them, so that they desire no more than that with which God follows their labors by his blessing. Thus in Psalm 103 he is said to satisfy the soul of the righteous with good things. Hence it is that we are exhorted to be content with present things, Hebrews 13:5; though these might seem in human judgment not to be sufficient for the sustenance of their life, nevertheless they rest by faith upon God’s promise: *I will not forsake you, nor will I ever abandon you.* [The Lord is my helper; I will not fear what man may do to me.] See an outstanding example of this in Philippians 4:18.

Verse 13. In the failure of the lips [is] a snare of evil, but the righteous man comes out of distress.

In the word שפתים (*of the lips*) there is a metonymy of the cause for the effect, *lips* for speech. In מוקש (*snare*) there is a metaphor drawn from bird-catchers. According as each bodily member is an instrument fitted either for godliness or ungodliness, integrity or wickedness, so God furnishes those same members with precepts, so that by them, as though by bridles, they may either be restrained from evil, or by spurs be urged toward good. Hence it happens that, since a person’s speech has very great importance for life or death, God takes the tongue for himself to be

[1632 witness | PDF p. 180 | printed columns 334–335]

diligently trained and instructed. See James 3:7, 8. Everyone bears an untamed tongue, to such a degree that no human being can tame it; God alone knows how to subject it. He does this in no other way than by the precepts and teachings of Scripture, by which, as though with a halter, we bind the tongue lest it leap upon us.

First he shows the tongue’s poison and venom; afterwards, in the following proverb, its excellent use. He sets its malice before us to be considered under the figure of a snare and trap, by which metaphor he declares that ambushes are laid for us in speech. By this statement he rouses us to exceptional care and watchfulness. For greater danger presses upon us from a concealed and hidden evil than from one which is open and obvious. For besides the fact that we are more often unprepared when a hidden evil escapes us, it also suddenly flies upon us and so strikes us that it is not easy to turn the mind toward flight from the evil. Third, in a single moment we are held entangled in a snare, so that by a single word we are ensnared in a moment. Finally, bird-catchers place snares and traps not in deserted or desolate places, but in those places which birds frequent for the sake of food. So also ambushes in words are placed in the busiest parts of that station or calling in which God has placed us: for ministers, in the ministry; for a magistrate, in those things which concern his governance. Therefore in this matter especially we ought to be circumspect and wide awake.

Next, because he calls the failure of evil lips not only a snare but an evil snare, we ought to redouble our watches and guard-posts. He calls it an evil snare because it is artfully knotted and woven by Satan, both to capture people ingeniously and to hold those captured firmly until it kills them by suffocation.

This failure of the lips proceeds either from our own lips or those of others. For this reason, just as a guard must be set upon our mouth, and a door erected, against the snare of our own lips, Psalm 141:5, so against the snare of others’ lips we ought to bear ears of horn, deaf ears.

In the latter half-verse it is taught that the righteous man is not wholly immune from this snare, as though he were never ensnared by either his own words or those of others; nevertheless, when brought into distress by this evil, he is not deserted or abandoned by God, but either disentangled from the snare or, the snare being broken, flies away as it were. See 2 Corinthians 4:8, 9. See 1 Samuel 21:12, how David was rescued from the words of the bloodthirsty council of the Philistines. See 1 Samuel 23. Likewise see how God preserved him from the failure of his own lips by Abigail’s timely intervention. And when he affirms that the righteous man comes out of the affliction or distress of the tongue, at the same time he intimates that the wicked man perishes through the failure both of his own lips and of those of others. Thus the man who told David that he had killed Saul perished through the failure of his own lips. Adonijah did the same by his petition for Abishag as wife. Thus many papists perish daily through their senseless prayers poured out without understanding, and through rash vows.

Verse 14. From the fruit of his mouth a good man is satisfied; a man also receives for himself the recompense of his works.

Namely, the fruit of the mouth: unless it is good, even those things acquired by our hands dissolve and perish. (See whether the latter half-verse should not be referred more broadly to deeds, just as the former is referred to words.)

In פרי (*fruit*) there is a metaphor; in פי (*mouth*), a metonymy of the efficient cause; in ישבע (*will be satisfied*), a metaphor transferred from the food of the body to the nourishment of the soul. In וגמול there is a synecdoche. In chapter 11, verse 30, the righteous man

[1632 witness | PDF p. 181 | printed columns 336–337]

is likened to a tree whose fruit is life. Here, however, the Prophet compares one member of a righteous person to a choice tree bearing the best fruits. What is said here concerning the mouth can rightly be affirmed of all the members of body and soul; yet in such a way that, just as the vine bears the most precious fruit among trees, so among the members of the body the mouth obtains the esteem belonging to the foremost and most precious tree. And as it produces the best fruit, so also it produces the most abundant; hence he says that a man is to be satisfied with the fruit of the mouth. For although the feet, hands, and other members of the body are quickly wearied in doing work, the single member of the tongue or mouth does not easily grow tired; and by the right use of the tongue we are rendered more fit to continue using it rightly.

In the latter half-verse he appears to teach that through the right use of the tongue we enjoy the things obtained by our labor. For as a rudder governs the whole ship and a bridle turns a horse, so by the right use of the tongue the other members of the body are kept, as it were, in their duty. Next, by the lawful use of the tongue we lead a peaceful life, Psalm 34:13. And just as by a slanderous and unserviceable tongue our resources are rather often transferred to other masters, so by a proper use of the tongue those possessed are either preserved, or those transferred and alienated are restored. For when someone rashly becomes surety for another and by this depravity of the mouth makes himself another’s debtor, he transfers away the things obtained by his labors, Proverbs 6. Likewise, slander against a prince is punished by the loss of property. See 1 Kings 21, where it is taught that a blasphemer against God is to be put to death, but one who slanders the king is to be stripped of his resources. Therefore, since a most abundant fruit springs from the lawful use of the mouth, and since such manifold fruit is gathered from it, it would be worthwhile for us to bestow labor upon cultivating, pruning, and diligently preserving this tree, just as a farmer is solicitous by every means for the cultivation and protection of that tree which produces good and abundant fruit in due season, although its fruit is not certainly assured and although in the seventh year there is an intermission and cessation from the gathering of fruit.

Verse 15. The way of a fool [seems] right in his own eyes, but he who listens to counsel is wise.

In the word דרך (*way*) there is a metaphor for the course and custom of life. Likewise, in the words שמע (*listens*) and בעיניו (*in his eyes*) there is a transference from the external senses to the faculties of the mind: the former to the obedience of the will, the latter to the light of understanding.

By the name *fool* is understood one who has not been regenerated by the Holy Spirit. For since God’s will is perfect wisdom, against which we strive with all that we are, Romans 8:7, it is necessary for us to be wholly deprived of wisdom, Proverbs 30. Hence also in Scripture such a person is called בער, Psalms 73:22 and 92:6. He is compared to a horse and mule, in which there is no understanding, Psalm 32:9. For no idiot is so estranged from the common sense and intelligence of human beings as all of us are by nature destitute of the knowledge of the divine will.

Nor indeed is there in us merely an emptiness and privation of wisdom. Rather, a spirit of stupidity is implanted in us by nature, by which, just as we are drawn away from wise counsels, so we are swept off to rash and futile thoughts. Original sin is defined not only by the privation and absence of good, but further by the impression and engraving of evil. For it is not like a scraped tablet, suited to receive any image, but one which is not merely spattered all over with the ink of perversity, but thoroughly coated with it. For just as the erasure of the divine image is introduced, so the likeness and figure of the devil is set in its place, Genesis 6. What more? Indeed, that which yields nothing to the preceding two

[1632 witness | PDF p. 182 | printed columns 338–339]

in wickedness and virulence: namely, that our nature delights and acquiesces in this its unhappiness; I add that it even glories and triumphs in it, Philippians 3:19. For our thoroughly corrupted nature, like a bawd, applauds itself in its wickedness, as if someone endowed with a beautiful form, thrust and sunk into the mire, should rejoice in this deformity.

And this is the unhappy and mournful condition of all human beings. But the fool who is described here exists still more wicked and more ruined. For, as is clear from the latter half-verse, here he designates as a fool the person who drives away and rejects the wisdom offered by God. *For in this the world is liable to condemnation, that light has shone upon human beings, who nevertheless loved darkness rather than light*, John 3:19; and, *If I had not come, they would not have had sin*, John 9:22, 24. Finally, such is the power of sin inhering in us that, when the law comes, sin gathers still greater strength, so that by the intervention of the law sin becomes more sinful. In our eyes it is too little to spurn offered grace unless we oppose ourselves wholly to it. In this way the saying celebrated as a proverb is confirmed: an idiot values his trifles or fly-whisk more than the Tower of London. And these are those whom the Prophet in Psalm 5 calls not fools but plainly insane.

From these things is refuted the opinion of those who exalt the free choice of man so far that, with a good thing proposed and set before his eyes, he can either reject it or, if he pleases, embrace it; for the Prophet teaches that the folly implanted in human beings is so great that they do not of their own accord admit what is good. And the Apostle confirms that, when the law intervenes, sin grows under the guidance of nature. Here we must remember what is commanded in Deuteronomy 6:18: *You shall do what is good and right in the eyes of Jehovah*; and in chapter 12, verse 8 we are forbidden in the worship of God to do *what is good in our own eyes*. Therefore here is a broad field for speaking against a good “intention,” as they call it, John 16, Romans 10. What is more, in obedience toward human beings they are often deceived when they follow what seems good to them, as in the Amalekite who said that he had killed Saul, and in those who killed Ishbosheth.

In the other half-verse the wise person is defined, just as the fool is described in the former. And although among human beings the highest degree of wisdom is said to consist in someone’s being wise of himself, and the second degree in his listening to others who possess wisdom when he himself is not wise, the Prophet teaches that no one is wise of himself, but that the highest degree of wisdom is to hear another person warning him.

Here the first benefit of the ear is proclaimed. How do they believe concerning one whom they have not heard? But it is not enough for someone to hear unless he listens, as is expressly stated here. Next we must see what we hear and to what thing we listen, as Christ warns—namely, to counsel. He means God’s counsel, which is called counsel κατ’ ἐξοχήν (*par excellence*), as in Proverbs: he who despises the word or precept—that is, God’s—as though other counsels, precepts, and so forth did not exist in comparison with these. Finally, counsel in this passage is not of the kind left to us and to our choice, as in human counsels.

Verse 16. The fool’s own indignation is known the same day, but a shrewd man conceals disgrace.

In יום, *day*, there is a synecdoche of species, namely, a day for a brief time. Likewise in ערום there is a synecdoche of genus for a shrewd person who is praiseworthy. The sum of the proverb is that he is wicked who is wholly unable to restrain his anger; by contrast, it determines that he is godly who knows how to rule it.

It must first be asked whom Solomon designates here with the mark of folly. It is the person who has nothing either of right or

[1632 witness | PDF p. 183 | printed columns 340–341]

authority for checking his indignation and binding it with the chains of patience, but who loosens the reins upon his affections, so that as often as he conceives indignation, he belches and pours it out; whose indignation has no door, no bars, no bolts; whose affections have no guard set upon them and around whom no wall is built to delay and hold back the disordered and untamed affections of his mind, which are ready to leap out. He boils like a seething pot and throws out foam. Human beings especially have this affection of indignation in a raging and exultant state, upon which bridles are imposed with the greatest difficulty. Hence also arose the elegant Hebrew proverb that a person is known in a certain especial way by these things, אדם נכר בכיס בכוס ובכעס: in his purse, in his cup, and in his anger. Here, therefore, we learn not only to establish the distinction between wicked and upright people, but also to examine our own advance and progress in godliness and uprightness. For if we do not wholly subjugate our indignation out of conscience toward God, there is no godliness or uprightness; if we have commanded that affection only a very little, it is an indication that our progress is exceedingly meager and small.

First it must be noted that folly does not consist in someone’s being indignant, but in his being unable to conceal his anger: *Be angry and do not sin*, Ephesians 4. In Matthew 5:22, the person who is rashly angry is condemned. And when he teaches that the person who does not conceal his indignation is a fool, he sufficiently indicates that indignation may arise in a prudent and deliberate person. Next, folly does not consist merely in someone’s being unable to conceal his anger, for Moses, indignant with the people and provoked by many of their treacherous crimes, poured it out with his lips, Psalm 106:32, 33; Numbers 26:10, 11. Nor is someone to be held a fool or wicked merely because he does not bridle his anger for a day or an hour; for in that case David would be counted among the fools or wicked. Upon the report of the messenger whom his envoys sent back from Nabal, he at once grew enraged and testified to his indignation by a rash oath and preparation of arms. But he is at last to be held a fool who does not oppose himself and the powers of his soul to this corrupt affection, but, as it were, opens doors and gates to it—much more, one who surrenders himself captive to it, and indeed advances it.

In the other part of the verse he teaches that the person who conceals disgrace is shrewd. Here by the word *shrewd* he means one who is praiseworthy. For since this word is by its nature μέσος (*intermediate*) and can be bent in either direction, toward either praise or reproach, here, as throughout the book of Proverbs, it is taken only in praise and is opposed to לאויל. Of this person it is affirmed that he covers disgrace. Here it must be noted that he does not say that he conceals indignation, but disgrace. By this he notes, first, that indignation itself, when it is unjust, is disgraceful; next, the pallor of the face or its suffusion with redness, the distortion of the mouth, and the other deformities of bodily parts which indignation produces; next, precipitancy of words and blasphemy in oaths; fourth, deeds which stray from the rule of reason. [For the anger of man does not work the justice of God], James 1.

Further, since he says *disgrace*, not *his own disgrace*, as in the former half-verse he said *his own indignation*, it must be noted that the person who restrains his indignation lest it break out also conceals the disgrace of the person with whom he is indignant. For although he warns the person with whom he is indignant of the sin committed, nevertheless in admonishing him he follows that moderation which especially looks to the offender’s reputation and standing. Nor does he conceal the offender’s disgrace only by not publishing his offense in the ears of all, but by preventing the offender, through a certain gentle manner of conduct and conversation with him, from adding sin to offense or heaping crime upon it. This indeed is not done

[1632 witness | PDF p. 184 | printed columns 342–343]

unless it is from charity, which covers a multitude of sins, it is clear that this restraint of indignation proceeds not from a cunning and crafty concealment of injuries, by which someone seizes and watches for a more suitable time of vengeance, as did Joab and Absalom. That they are not included in this class of prudent men is clear from the fact that they do not cover disgrace but finally expose it. Or else he passes over an injury with a deaf ear for another person's advantage, as Saul did in 1 Samuel 10: he concealed the signal injury done to him because he recognized that avenging it would be untimely for him, since he was not yet established in the kingdom.

Verse 17. He who declares what is just breathes forth truth, but a most false witness [breathes forth] deceit.

From the latter half-verse the antithesis of a false witness, namely a true witness, is carried over to the first; and from the first the verb *to breathe forth* or *to declare* is carried over to the latter.

This verse teaches how someone is to be distinguished as true or false. In יפיח there is a metaphor transferred from breath to free speech.

This proverb seems so obvious and evident that it might appear unworthy to be uttered by Solomon, so distinguished and artful a trumpet of God. For who does not understand that a true witness speaks things true and just, while a false witness does things contrary to them? But it must be understood that in this book God delivers not only things hidden and abstruse, but also those that lie before our feet and are situated in the common sense and understanding of men. He does this with this purpose: by thus making those things ὑπομνήσει (*a reminder*), he renders men more attentive and eager both to retain them in memory and to express them in life and deeds. Then in this way he fulfills the promise made at the beginning of the book and discharges his pledge, since he says that this book delivers not only things that the wise and learned may attain and comprehend, but also things that illiterate and uneducated men may attain and comprehend.

Yet to one considering the matter more attentively, it will be sufficiently clear that certain things lie beneath this passage which do not so easily occur to everyone. First, this is required in a truthful witness: he must say not merely things that are true, but also things that are just. For someone may say true things perversely and with a malevolent mind, as Doeg did concerning David, whom he defamed before Saul, and as did the false witnesses who were suborned and instructed against Christ.

Next, someone may be found who, because the lawsuit is between a wicked man and a righteous one, thinks that he may deviate a little from the truth in order to benefit the righteous man and cause him to prevail in his case. The same consideration might also draw a witness away from testifying to the truth so that he may assist an afflicted and wretched man against a wealthy one. Against men of this kind the proverb pronounces its judgment, teaching that a truthful witness will declare the truth even if his testimony should harm a godly or afflicted person. For just as a judge is rightly warned not to lean in court either toward a righteous man against a wicked one or toward a needy man against a wealthy one, so a witness is not vainly or inopportunely taught not to employ his tongue unjustly to procure either this man's loss or that man's advantage.

Next, when he says that a true witness breathes forth truth, he thereby teaches that a truthful witness speaks his testimony willingly and freely, so that he is neither hired with a price nor driven by fear to do it. For one who is enticed or compelled by such things to testify to the truth does not merit the name of a true witness. The same is true if he has spoken true testimony from love or hatred of one of the litigants. Moreover, if he does not speak the truth plainly, fully, and openly, but either

[1632 witness | PDF p. 185 | printed columns 344–345]

keeps certain things silent, adds something to the truth, or obscures the truth with doubtful and winding expressions, he will not be regarded as a truthful witness. To all these things is opposed what is said in Psalm 15: [the righteous man speaks truth from his heart.] Finally, the same applies if he speaks truth that he has not sufficiently perceived and ascertained for himself. From these points one understands who is a false witness: namely, one who either speaks falsehood or obscures the truth and does not willingly and readily advance the justice of the truth.

Verse 18. There is one who utters things like the thrusts of a sword, but the tongue of the wise is medicine.

In לשון (*tongue*) there is a metonymy of the subject for words. In מרפא (*medicine*) there is a metaphor transferred from medicine of the body to the healing of the soul.

By this proverb he designates the extraordinary harm or benefit of the tongue.

The particle יש, prefixed in this book at the beginning of proverbs, seems to designate a particular axiom, as in that proverb, *There is a way that seems right*, and so forth. For most wicked men are conscious of their own wickedness, and the same fact is confirmed in this proverb. Not all wicked men utter words as venomous as those indicated by this proverb. For although all, through their depraved nature, are inclined to this crime, and although these things lurk beneath their tongues, Romans 3:13, it is one thing to have them in the mouth or beneath the tongue, but another to pour them out and shoot them like poisoned arrows. Psalm 52 compares a deceitful tongue to a sharp razor; Psalm 120 compares it to the arrow of a mighty man armed with fiery hooks, burning more fiercely than coals of juniper. In this passage it is compared to a thrust, or rather to thrusts, of a sword. From these passages it follows that Christ not without cause extends the crime of murder to words that breathe anger and vengeance, so that there is even actual murder, so to speak, not only that committed with the hands but also that perpetrated with a venomous tongue. Those whose tongues are sharper than any two-edged axe are therefore represented before God as bloodthirsty and stained with blood. Nor is it remarkable that such should be the effect of a wicked tongue, since its fire is kindled by hell itself, James 3:4. From this it follows that we must use the utmost caution, lest either we abuse our tongues in perpetrating so great a crime or suffer such a thing from other wicked men (especially since this is done not only maliciously but with deliberation, as the force of the word which the Prophet whispers indicates), while we arm ourselves against this scourge with the weapons of patience. Above all, the truth of this statement is perceived in the impostures of false teachers, who by one sentence pierce several thousand souls with the arrow of errors. But if the words of a mortal and wretched man possess so much power that they strike the hearer, how great is the power of the divine word discharged and shot from the mouth of the Almighty? See Hebrews 4; and above all, how dreadful will be that voice in Matthew: *Depart, you accursed*, and so forth.

In the other half-verse the contrary virtue of a well-ordered tongue is indicated, which is no less powerful to heal than the other is to wound. From this it is clear that we must strive all the more in this age to possess health-giving tongues and lips, inasmuch as the wicked in this generation possess tongues more venomous than before. We should be ashamed to be less equipped for healing than the wicked are instructed for destruction. When diseases either arise afresh or grow worse with age, physicians devote all their effort to preparing medicines stronger than the diseases; and shall we not devote diligent effort in this most excellent art of healing, for which so ample a reward exists, so that we may cure wounded souls?

[1632 witness | PDF p. 186 | printed columns 346–347]

It is also established by this verse that the tongue of the wise is defined not merely as one that does not wound or inflame, but as one that also supplies medicine. It is not enough if the tree of the tongue does not bear harmful fruit, unless it also produces wholesome fruit; nor is it enough if there is no poison beneath the tongue, unless an antidote is also ready at hand. This is also what the Apostle notes: words of this kind ought to impart grace to the hearers, Ephesians 4:29.

Next, it is not enough for someone to utter good words unless he applies them seasonably. For the words of Job's friends were true and wise, but untimely and badly placed. And this is what the Apostle advises in the same place, πρὸς οἰκοδομὴν τῆς χρείας (*for the edification required by the need*), and especially in Colossians 3:26.

How great the power of each faculty is, both of cursing and of blessing, may be seen in Nabal's venomous words and Abigail's health-giving speech, by which the fire that Nabal had kindled was extinguished. It may likewise be seen in the words of the Ephraimites and of Jerubbaal, Judges 8:1, 2, 3. But if other words like daggers are set against biting words like swords, how great a conflagration will result! See Judges 12:1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and so forth.

Among all the wise, however, the ministers of this kind excel who correct errors. Of them, in a preeminent sense, is found true what Isaiah 50 says about the tongue of the learned given by God, that they may know how to impart a word in season to the weary. And according as they have been instructed, by one word like an arrow they will be able either to strike or to restore some thousand persons.

Verse 19. A truthful lip is established forever; until I pass through a moment, a false tongue.

In the words שפת (*lip*) and לשון (*tongue*) there is a metonymy of the effect, namely of the lip and tongue for speech; then there is a synecdoche of a member for the whole person. Likewise, in ארגיעה there is a synecdoche of the species *moment*, namely, for a brief and narrow span of time. By nature we live in great ignorance of all things, with the result that, unknowing and imprudent, we rather often stumble into the truth. But after the darkness of blindness has been dispersed, disturbances immediately surround our souls—love of this person, hatred of that one, and above all fear—and terror is cast into our minds. Consequently, from knowledge of the truth which we corrupt with lies, we are put in greater danger still than if we had remained in ignorance. Therefore by this proverb God both encourages us toward the truth and terrifies us away from speaking falsehood.

Men are wonderfully tormented by care and thought for continuation and eternity, partly in themselves and partly in the propagation of their offspring. But when they perceive that an irreconcilable hatred of the truth is deeply implanted and innate in the world, they are seized with fear lest by testimony to the truth they procure destruction for themselves or their own, especially by testimony to that truth which accords with godliness. Against this fear God strengthens us with this promise of perpetual continuance. Thus Christ teaches that the one who loses his life for the sake of asserting the truth will find it. For although they may be taken from life because of their testimony to the truth, yet being dead they live, not only with God but also in a most pleasing and delightful memory of themselves upon the earth.

For what is said in Hebrews 11 about Abel—that, though now dead, he nevertheless still lives and speaks through faith—may also be affirmed truly of all those who bear witness to the truth, especially to that of the Gospel. And in this manner it may fittingly be said that the lip of Daniel and of his three companions still lives and endures. But this saying will be fulfilled most completely when their dead bodies

[1632 witness | PDF p. 187 | printed columns 348–349]

on the last day are raised from the dust to life. Hence also that saying of Christ in John 3: *He who believes in me shall not die*; and in chapter 6: *He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood shall live forever, and I will raise him on the last day.* By this saying he establishes the eternity of life of the whole man, body and soul, in the resurrection to life.

In the other half-verse, in which he affirms that a tongue of falsehood lasts for a moment, it is taught that those who lie may appear for some time to gain something from it and to procure for themselves security in continuing; yet at last all that hope of their stability vanishes into smoke. There are many examples of this, whether we consider the lies of the wicked or those of the godly. The lie of Abraham and Isaac, by which they declared that their wives were their sisters, could seem for a time to relieve them and free them from danger. Yet the truth finally shone forth, and when it was disclosed, they were suffused with shame and disgrace even before profane kings. When Peter utterly denied that he had known Christ, it could at first be thought that he had gained a benefit from it, because by the advantage of that lie he was admitted into the priest's house, joined to the other attendants of the priest at the fire, and admitted to all those spectacles which he so greatly desired to see. Yet it lasted only for a moment, for not long afterward everything was turned into mourning and tears.

The same must be said of Gehazi, Elisha's servant. For when by his fabricated speech he had brought home from Naaman a great quantity of silver and precious garments, he seemed to himself to have profited by his lie. But behold, this joy lasted only a moment: as soon as he came to Elisha, his lie was discovered and he was struck with leprosy. To this may be brought the truth of religion, which endures, while heresy, though it prevails for a time, is afterward dispersed by the shining rays of truth.

Verse 20. Deceit [falls back] into the heart of those who devise evil, but joy [falls] to counselors of peace.

He uses the word *heart* metaphorically for the soul, because just as the heart is the principal fountain of life among the parts of the body, so the soul is the fountain of the whole man. The aim of the proverb is what is celebrated by the Greek proverb, κακὴ βουλὴ βουλεύσαντι κακὴ ἐστι (*evil counsel is evil to the one who counseled it*); just as, on the contrary, things go well for those who look to the good and advantage of others.

First, it occurs to us here to examine who is an architect of evil. As has been said, all of us by nature are disposed in soul and body to perpetrate what is evil. Therefore those who have not been freed from that common plague παλιγγενεσίᾳ (*by regeneration*) must certainly be occupied in the continual perpetration of evils. Yet it does not follow from this that all the wicked are artisans of evil of the kind described in this passage. All are indeed workers of iniquity who practice iniquity as an art and occupation; but just as in arts and sciences there are some beginners who, although wholly occupied in doing the work, nevertheless accomplish less than the more skilled do in only one hour, so it also happens among wicked men that some carry out more evils in one day than others do in a month. There are also many wicked men who do not think about inflicting injuries on others but are chiefly enemies and adversaries to themselves, while they squander everything they possess to serve their lusts. Therefore those are called architects of evil in this passage who, in order to harm others, are always turning something over in their mind and working it with their hands, just like artisans who either ponder within themselves the form and shape of a structure or at once apply their hands to the structure once it has been invented and conceived. These are those who, for the infliction of evils, have

[1632 witness | PDF p. 188 | printed columns 350–351]

sold themselves, who by night devise evil upon their beds to execute it in the morning, provided they have opportunity, as is said in Micah. Finally, these are the men whom the Apostle in Romans 1:30 calls ἐφευρετὰς κακῶν (*inventors of evils*). And just as in the arts the more skilled and industrious a man is, the greater the wages he receives, so the artisans of evil will receive from Satan twice as much punishment as other workers of iniquity, since they devoted their labor to him as the master craftsman; just as the architects of good will be rewarded with a greater prize by God, whom they served.

It is said of these artisans of evil that they always carry deceit and fraud within them in their mind. By this statement Solomon wished us to be warned never to believe them when they flatter and promise every good thing with the highest profession of fidelity and even with an oath interposed, because whatever they profess with their mouth, within themselves they nevertheless contemplate falsehood and deception. How suitably we are instructed in this by the Prophet is sufficiently declared by the fickleness and thoughtlessness of many godly men in this matter: though they have certain and established knowledge of a particular man's wickedness, they are nevertheless so beguiled by their flatteries and seductive words that they persuade themselves they should fear no evil from them. David was deceived in this way by his son Absalom. Although he had known him to be an extraordinary artisan of crimes, he was nevertheless ensnared by his feigned and simulated expressions of obedience. Above all, this was manifestly seen in the king of Israel, who at once believed Ben-hadad the Syrian when he addressed him most flatteringly, and released him at the same time from death and captivity, to his own great harm. Our own people commonly sin in this respect: although they know well that the Pontificians are bound by a certain diabolical religion not to keep faith with heretics (as those distinguished heretics call them), they are nevertheless led away by their flatteries so as to hope that faith will be kept by people of such perfidy. They are little mindful of this saying of Solomon: those men nurture deceit within, whatever they display without.

From this latter half-verse it is evident who the artisans of evil are: namely, those who deliberate and turn over in their mind how to perpetrate evil. Next is shown what kind of evil it is in the fashioning of which they occupy themselves: namely, in inflicting evils on others, by which they disturb and interrupt the peace and quiet of others, whether that peace is of soul or body. This ought to sharpen our zeal in piety and uprightness, so that we may become artisans in the worship of God and in our duty toward our neighbors. For it is unworthy that the wicked should bring more craft and skill to the fitting together of Satan's tent than the godly bring to the weaving of God's tabernacle; that they should be more clever and industrious in raising Satan's shrine than we are in building God's temple; and finally, that they should approach the building of Babylon better equipped than we approach the building of Zion. Therefore it will not be enough not to take peace away from others unless we also bring peace to them. Nor is it enough to do this in just any manner and perfunctorily, unless we also direct the mind's keen attention to it. Hence all the godly are commanded to seek peace and even pursue it as it flees, so that they may be called and may be sons of peace, and by this mark, as by a pledge, may recognize among themselves that they are sons of God.

The reward joined to these counselors is set forth as joy, and that joy is manifold. For even in the very devising of counsels of peace there is something that lightens the labor of deliberation. Next, consciousness of a deed rightly done wonderfully refreshes a man. Finally, God's blessing and reward in this life fill the soul with joy. Hence it is also gathered that grief and sadness of soul accompany artisans of evil: first from the very restlessness of devising evils, since while intent upon the matter they often have no leisure either to eat or to sleep. Next, from the sting of an evil

[1632 witness | PDF p. 189 | printed columns 352–353]

conscience. Finally, because an evil counsel at last becomes most evil for the counselor.

Verse 21. The righteous shall not be cast into any affliction, but the wicked shall be filled with evil.

God, mercifully turning occasions of evils away from the godly, by his just judgment sets evil of every kind before the wicked.

Since the word רע has a twofold signification, the one of guilt and the other of punishment, the fact that both significations are comprehended in this passage renders the proverb more majestic. Especially since it was accomplished only by the singular wisdom of God that both meanings should be contained in a single word, so that it might designate both as removed or both as imposed at the same time—contrary both to what the profane maintain, who imagine that punishment will not be the companion of guilt, and to the papists, who invent the tale that, though sin has been removed, its punishment must nevertheless be expiated by works and satisfactions. The same can be affirmed of the words עון and חטא.

When man once existed incorrupt in mind and body, nevertheless, when allurements and enticements were set before him from without, he was driven into the abyss of destruction. Now, therefore, when he carries sin planted deeply within and clinging to him, like tinder that easily catches flame, how great is the danger of damnation in which he lives, unless the deceits and snares of sin, which the world on one side and the devil on the other would prepare and arrange, were kept off by God’s clemency toward us. Therefore, although there is great inward force for preserving both the integrity of life and the safety that comes from integrity—in the stripping off of the old man and the clothing of the new man—yet that inward sanctification is so weak and languid that it continually needs the aid of God’s singular providence to drive away those occasions and opportunities outside us by which the desires still clinging within would be baited. This, then, is the benefit that Solomon celebrates in this passage. An excellent and illustrious example of this matter occurs in Exodus 13, where God is said, when leading the people out of Egypt, not to have led them by the road through the country of the Philistines, which was by far the shortest, but by the road through the desert, which wandered very far out of the way, lest, if they had gone straight on to the land of Canaan, they should repent on engaging in battle with the Philistines and turn back and return to Egypt, from which they had been rescued. 1 Kings 14:12, 13. Another example of this matter is found in Abijah, the son of Jeroboam, and in the godly king Josiah. Both are said to have died an untimely death for this reason: that they might not see and experience the evils about to follow, and so be drawn away from the piety they cultivated. 2 Kings 22:20. For although the godly can never be driven or moved away from the faith and salvation they have received, either by the enticements of desires or by the fear of evils, nothing nevertheless prevents God from using certain means and supports to foster and preserve this faith and constancy in them.

But what appears to stand against this is that, while leading the people through the desert, God tested them repeatedly by want of water and other things, as is expressly noted in Deuteronomy 8. Reply: first, this evil was the more tolerable of the two for the Israelites; and for this reason he chose to expose them to the difficulties of the desert rather than to battle against the Philistines. Next, God tests his own people both so that they may come to know the corruption of their own mind and so that they may experience God’s help in delivering them in those trials. Thus in 2 Chronicles 32:31 God by his providence first sees to it that the Babylonian envoys are sent to Hezekiah. It is then expressly added that God abandoned Hezekiah, so that in this way he might come to know the inconstancy of his own mind in what is good. So also the sight of Bathsheba naked was set before David, and David, ensnared by the sight of her, was held fast, not

[1632 witness | PDF p. 190 | printed columns 354–355]

only so that he might perceive his own perversity, but also so that from both his fall and his restoration an example of human misery together with divine mercy might stand forth, to be transmitted to every succeeding generation. The same judgment must be made concerning Peter’s denial (the occasion for which was supplied by John’s being known to the high priest), and concerning other examples of that kind.

But since God alone is the one who turns away the occasions and opportunities of evil, both of guilt and of punishment, he not only accomplishes this by himself without any work of ours, through his bare providence and governance of all things, when we are not even thinking of it; he also employs us as ministers of this grace and benefit, when we keep watch so that, so far as it is granted us, we flee every occasion of sinning and do not thrust ourselves into unnecessary dangers that do not fall upon us by reason of our calling. Hence that saying of the Savior: *Watch and pray, lest you enter into temptation.* Matthew 26. That we may avoid adultery, brothels, familiarity and conversation with prostitutes, idleness, drunken revelry, and so forth must be kept far away. That we may flee drunkenness, we must abstain from the company of drunkards, from false companions who entice us to drink, and so forth.

But the wicked are filled with evil, within and without, with guilt and punishment. For God deservedly leads them into temptation, in which they are dashed down and perish. Job 18:9: [*a snare shall seize the heel*]; and 22:10, 11: *Therefore snares surround you, and sudden terror disturbs you there, or darkness, so that you cannot see, or a mass of waters will cover you.* For these things are attributed by Job’s friends to him as though he were wicked. And Proverbs 23:5: [*stings and snares are in the way of the perverse.*] But in Amos at one point, and more often in other prophets, God is said to array his manifold battle lines against the wicked: a bear, a lion, a serpent, and likewise the sword, famine, plague, and iron, so that if they escape one or another of these, they may nevertheless be crushed by another. An illustrious example of this teaching occurs in Judges 4:6, 7, where Barak is commanded to proceed into battle with several thousand soldiers, so that by this means he may draw Sisera, commander of Jabin king of Canaan, into battle and destroy him once drawn out. Thus Joshua is commanded to flee before the citizens of Ai, so that by this device, being drawn away from their city, they might perish together with the city itself. But most remarkable is what is found in 1 Samuel 3 and 4. For there, in order that God might most severely avenge the Israelites upon the house and family of Eli, he ordains a just war in which the Israelites were to be routed by the uncircumcised.

Verse 22. Lying lips [are] an abomination to Jehovah, but those who act faithfully are acceptable to him.

In אמונה (*truth*) and רצונו (*delight*), שקר (*falsehood*) and תועבת (*abomination*), there is a metonymy of the adjunct. In שפתי, *lips*, there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole person, and a metonymy of the subject for the adjunct, *speech*. By *lips of falsehood* he does not mean everyone who speaks things that are false, but one who becomes accustomed to uttering falsehoods and devotes himself to producing them. For there is no one who does not at some time say something false, whether through error, imprudence, or, finally, weakness, or for the sake of obtaining good or avoiding evil; and yet they are not therefore regarded as abominable to God. But those who are addicted to lies, even if they pour out lies as a joke, can rightly be reckoned in this number. So also can those who speak the truth but do it in order to harm, or who do it without being moved by any conscientious regard for truth. Of these it is said that they are abominable to God. It would be serious if we were only slightly pleasing and acceptable to a mortal whose favor and goodwill we seek and who is useful to us; it would be more serious if we were hated by the same person; it would be most serious if he regarded us as an abomination—that is, if he

[1632 witness | PDF p. 191 | printed columns 356–357]

utterly abhorred us and judged us detestable, so that he could not bear the sight of us. How much more troublesome would we be when the immortal God is so disposed toward someone? That the matter truly is as stated is clear from the fact that we see people most inflamed against those who delight in the things by which they themselves are most grieved. Thus, if anyone most religiously cultivates marriage and rests content with his own wife, he is most violently carried against adulterers; if anyone behaves soberly and drinks sparingly and moderately, he pursues drunkards with great and almost Vatinian hatred. Since, therefore, God is most true and is truth itself, it is necessary that his mind should be offended at liars. From this it will also follow that people can gather no testimony of God’s love toward them when they approve lies that he disapproves, love falsehood that he hates, and indeed delight in a lie that he abominates. For if we have an estranged disposition toward those who stand furthest from our manner of life, let us consider that God has the same disposition toward us when we are separated and disjoined by a great distance from his character and, as it were, his manners. Further, we must beware lest by lying we obtain a person’s favor at the loss and expense of God’s favor.

Next, it must not be passed over that he affirms that *lips* are an abomination to God. For although the synecdoche is transferred to the whole body, as has been said, nevertheless God’s hatred is carried particularly against that part of the body by which God is provoked, and it will also pay a punishment corresponding to the sin. It must also be noted at the same time that God abominates not merely falsehood but the person who employs falsehood—something sinners cannot bear to be persuaded of. In the latter half-verse it seemed that he ought to have said that a lip of truth would be acceptable. But Solomon speaks otherwise, for it is not those who speak but those who act, and do so sincerely and from the heart, who are God’s delight. If one evil obtains tyranny over us, it is sufficient to bring upon us God’s hatred and even abomination (although evils are intertwined and connected with one another); but in order for anyone to prove himself pleasing to God, he must present himself whole and uncorrupted in all things, not only in words but also in deeds.

Moreover, when we consider that what we do is acceptable to God, this is an excellent spur to doing good by which we may shake off our sluggishness. The Apostle uses this argument in Romans 12:2 and 1 Thessalonians 4:3. This passage is illustrated here when he says not only that it is acceptable and pleasing, but exceedingly acceptable. See the example of Joab, who, when he perceived David’s desire and will concerning the recall of Absalom, immediately offered himself as minister for accomplishing the matter. If, therefore, we are ever in danger of being drawn away from what is right through zeal to win the favor of some mortal, even of a prince, let the thought at once occur to us how precious it is to retain the goodwill of God.

It must further be noted that God equally abominates the wicked and delights in the godly, and the reverse: something mortals are persuaded of only with difficulty. For although they can be induced to believe that the godly are highly regarded by God, they do not believe that the wicked are borne with the greatest hatred.

Verse 23. A prudent man conceals knowledge, but the mind of fools proclaims folly.

In לב, *heart*, there is a metonymy of the cause, because the fool’s mind is the cause of his betraying his folly with his mouth. Likewise in the verb יקרא, *proclaims*, there is a metaphor taken from heralds, who proclaim the edicts of princes with a loud voice and powerful lungs; thus the fool, with the greatest confidence, exertion, and eagerness,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 192 | printed columns 358–359]

proclaims his folly. Since it was said above that ערום is used in this book only in a good sense, it can be asked how it rightly applies to a good and godly person that he conceals knowledge which could be useful to many. For if one who withholds grain is worthy of a curse, how much more must he be judged accursed who keeps stored and concealed within himself the knowledge of good things? Hence in Matthew 25 the man who buried his talent in the ground is set down for reproach; and in Luke 19:20, the one who kept it concealed in a napkin. By contrast, in Job 32:18, 19, Elihu says that he was full like a wineskin which, unless an opening is given it, is in danger of bursting and being torn apart. It must be understood that one kind of concealment is praiseworthy, just as another is blameworthy. For so to conceal the knowledge of good things that we begrudge others acquaintance with them, or reserve it as our peculiar possession for the sake of our own honor, advantage, or pleasure, deserves just rebuke. In this we imitate greedy and ill-disposed craftsmen who, if they know something more ingenious in their art, conceal it for their own advantage to such a degree that they scarcely, if at all, disclose those mysteries of the art to their own novices and apprentices, who have been handed over to them for instruction. But if anyone conceals his knowledge in such a way that he renders it more fruitful when it is afterward to be brought to light, he will rightly be praised. He is just like that most commendable head of a household who stores grain, wine, oil, and other supports of life in storerooms and granaries, so that he may bring them forth in their season for the use of his guests and household.

What has been said generally and without distinction must therefore be explained more fully and particularly. First in this class, those are to be praised who, although they have laid excellent foundations for knowing some matter, nevertheless do not scatter it among the public or communicate it to others before they have fully known and grasped it. Those who act otherwise imitate servants who, having received part of a message from their masters, immediately hurry off to carry out that part and do not wait until the master has related the whole message. Above all, blameworthy among these are those who undertake the ministry of the church when they have not yet grasped even a hundredth part of the knowledge suitable for discharging that ministry.

Further, a prudent person does not produce his knowledge at every time, but applies discrimination and judgment in choosing the time. Thus Abigail did not admonish Nabal of his folly before he had slept off his drunkenness. It also pertains to this that he does not interrupt another person’s speech. The fool’s tongue so itches that he cannot restrain it until the other has finished speaking. For he labors no less to bring forth his foolish speech than a woman in labor strives to bring forth her child. Thus, when Christ disputed with the Pharisees and teachers, although he had foreknown both their objections and their answers, nevertheless he allowed them their alternating turns of objecting and answering. It further belongs to this point that we grant the first places in speaking to those older by birth or more learned.

Thus Elihu, although better instructed to speak than all Job’s friends, and so eager to speak that he could scarcely contain himself, nevertheless did not begin speaking before he discovered that those more advanced in age had finished. Those sin most grievously against this prudence and skill in the choice of time who undertake the ministry without the church’s due call, however instructed and prepared they may come to that office.

There is also sin with respect to place when someone publicly babbles out what ought to be uttered privately, and the reverse. Moreover, just as prudent people do not set forth their knowledge at every time, so neither do they set it before everyone, but only before those who are fit hearers of it. For they do not

[1632 witness | PDF p. 193 | printed columns 360–361]

cast what is holy to dogs, or pearls to swine. See Proverbs 9. Itinerant ministers sin against this caution when, deserting their own flocks, they wander about feeding the flocks of others.

From these things it is readily clear what is taught in the second half-verse, which is set opposite by the law of contraries to the first: namely, that the fool babbles out, not wisdom, which he does not possess, but his own folly, in which he abounds—that is, he speaks of matters insufficiently ascertained by him. Even if he has something certain, he does not produce it in its proper place or time, or before those in whose presence it would be fitting. Next, whereas empty vessels make more noise, the fool confidently and without any hesitation publishes what the skillful and prudent person brings forth slowly and sometimes with a mind held in suspense, just as a herald publishes the decrees and edicts of princes. Solomon appears to have indicated this by the word *proclaiming*. Finally, he is so inflamed with zeal to bring forth his folly that even when silence is imposed upon him by those whose concern it is, and his mouth is bound as by a halter through law or punishment, he nevertheless continues in mind and desire. Though silent, he speaks, as it were.

Verse 24. The hand of the diligent shall rule, but the deceitful shall be tributary.

By *hand*, a synecdoche of the part, he designates the whole person, because the hand is the instrument by which someone punishes the wicked and rewards the godly, and likewise the instrument by which tribute is paid. In מס (*tribute*) there is also a synecdoche of species for servitude of every kind and an abject condition. 1 Samuel 2:3. The Lord says, *Those who honor me I will honor*; but in this passage people are said to be advanced to honors through diligent management of the affairs entrusted to them. Thus the statement that subordinate things do not conflict or impede one another applies here. See the last verse of Proverbs 22.

Examples of this are found in Jacob and Joseph, who became great through constancy in their occupations. But it is especially true of Mordecai, who, since he was continually at the gate in accordance with the nature of his office, was afterward made a prince. Esther 2:19. The contrary can be seen in Issachar. Genesis 49:14.

From this we must learn not to seek honors and pant after them in the manner of unbelievers. Rather, let us take care to walk diligently in our calling; let us leave the rest to God, who keeps watch so that he may stand by his promises. Beware, however, of placing your hope in these means. See Ecclesiastes 9:11. God wills that this happen so that we may not be excessively attached to these outward means.

It might appear that an idle hand ought to be opposed to this hand, but Solomon opposes a deceitful hand. He does so by a twofold abbreviation, because the person who is idle is generally also deceitful. For through idleness he fabricates frauds and, to spare himself labor, employs deceit. Next, idle persons defraud both themselves and others of the labor owed to themselves and others, and such a person is a thief who sometimes ought to be punished more severely than one who practices robbery on the public road.

Verse 25. Solicitous anxiety in the mind of a man weighs it down, but a good matter makes it glad.

Kimchi in his מכלול says that this very sudden change of gender arises from the fact that a relative of the other gender agrees with a synonym of the antecedent, which synonym is of the same gender as the relative.

In ישחנה there is a metaphor taken from earthly things which, because of their heaviness, weigh down by their own weight. Although there are many things that weigh a person down and drive and dislodge his mind, as it were, from its own place into the lower parts of the body—such as labor and occupation, Ecclesiastes 1; likewise diseases, prisons, famine, heat, cold, and other external scourges of God of that kind—nevertheless nothing appears

[1632 witness | PDF p. 194 | printed columns 362–363]

more hostile to a person’s soul than when he has grief within and enclosed in his soul. For the more closely it clings to the soul, the more it strikes and consumes the soul, and the more difficult it is to tear away from the soul. This is the distinction between external and internal afflictions. See 2 Corinthians 11:23–29, but especially verse 28. It is not that external afflictions, which take their beginning from the body and extend and reach to the soul, do not strike the soul; rather, those pains and sorrows are sharper and more biting which take their beginning from the soul and afterward afflict the body with wasting and decay. See Proverbs 15:10.

In the latter half-verse he prescribes the remedy for this. People devoted to the joys and cheerfulness of this life scarcely dream that times can befall them in which they will be saddened; or, if grief catches them unguarded and unwise, they imagine that nothing is easier than to put sorrow to flight and that they can drive it away in a moment. But if grief settles more deeply than to depart at their desire, they immediately employ the delights of this life—games, jokes, wine, incense, and ointments—by which they may banish far away the grief lodged within. Yet all these things either do no good, or, if they soothe the grief for a time, it afterward returns sharper and more violent; and, like a wound that has skinned over but has not been healed, it breaks out afresh. In external evils that befall us, these remedies are more usefully applied: food to calm hunger, drink for thirst, rest from labor for weariness, and so forth. But in these sorrows fixed in and clinging to the soul, the one remedy is what Solomon adds in this place: namely, a good word, or consolation drawn from the word of God. Hence David professes in Psalm 119:92 that he would have perished in his afflictions if he had not been sustained by God’s promises. See Job 33:14–24. For so in Jeremiah 29:10, as often elsewhere, a “good word” is put for gracious promises. From meditation upon the divine promise comes our committing ourselves to God and casting all our cares upon him, Psalm 55:23. This casting of ourselves upon God, moreover, is done through prayer, Philippians 4:6–7. Once these things have been done first, only then should wine, music, and so forth be employed. For this Proverb does not forbid us to resort in the third place to those external aids, which are even commanded, provided they are lawful, as in Proverbs 31: “Give wine to those sorrowful in soul.” And Elisha called for the aid of a musical instrument so that in this way, with the sadness of his soul dispelled, he might prepare himself for his office, 2 Kings 3:15.

From this it is further clear that those who are not of the best spirit in honorable causes, and are not refreshed by good hope, are blameworthy; indeed, that they are to be blamed if, when chastened for their sins, they set no limit to their sorrow, or if through patience they do not await deliverance from evils. Such people sin grievously against the first commandment, in which hope is commanded.

Verse 26. The righteous man observes from his neighbor, but the way of the wicked leads them astray.

This Proverb may seem to contain nothing that is not set before our feet and present in everyone’s common understanding. For what sort of claim, I ask, is this: that a righteous person is superior to one who is not righteous, and one who fears God to a profane person? Who is there, especially among those who profess godliness, who does not know this? But if we attend a little either to the common and widespread customs of people or to the temptations by which the godly are especially exercised and tested, we shall understand that what is taught by this Proverb is not nothing. For among mortals, whenever a comparison is made of the excellence of people, godliness and righteousness are cast behind their backs and never

[1632 witness | PDF p. 195 | printed columns 364–365]

rise, as it were, into the balance. For whether the contest is between us and others, or whether we enter a contest with others on behalf of those connected to us by some bond, we are accustomed to give them first place—not to those who excel in virtue and godliness, but to those who surpass others in possessions. For if the question is who is superior and should be placed above others, or who should be raised to a magistracy, people immediately resort to riches, nobility, friends, and learning, and the whole matter is decided by these external and adventitious ornaments alone. Is there a contest about the excellence of two people? The dispute is immediately brought and recalled to this: Which of them is richer? Which more noble? Which more learned? Which older? Which stronger among young men? Which woman is more beautiful or better endowed? See James 2:12. Against this practice Solomon delivers his judgment when he teaches that a righteous nobleman is superior to an unrighteous one, and a godly rich man to an ungodly rich man, and so forth. Indeed, a righteous man deprived of all these ornaments and disfigured by the contrary hardships of life is superior to one who is adorned jointly and heaped high with all these advantages of the present life. Further, even if he spoke with the tongues of people and angels, and even if he moved mountains from their place, yet if he is destitute of saving grace and of the righteousness that arises from it, he is not worthy to be compared with a righteous person. The righteous person is superior to anyone in the unrighteous nobility of his lineage, since he has God for his Father, Psalm 16; in inheritance, since he is an heir of heaven and earth; in food, since he is fed with heavenly manna; and in clothing, since he is clothed with Christ’s righteousness—whereas the unrighteous person does not deserve even the name of man, Jeremiah 5:1.

This also wonderfully supports the godly in that temptation in which they are deeply distressed, on the one hand by their own afflicted condition, and on the other by the prosperity of the wicked and their abundance of every good thing, even to the point that they are tempted to defect from godliness. Psalms 73 and 37. See Malachi 3:14, where God complains of that ungodly saying which the Israelites used to boast: that it goes well with the ungodly, while those who fear God waste labor and oil. Against them God sets that saying in Isaiah: “Tell the righteous that it will be well with him,” and contrariwise that it will be ill with the wicked, chapter 57:12–21; Matthew 6:33. Let this saying of Solomon also come to our aid: that the righteous person is superior to his neighbor, even if that neighbor were the greatest philosopher and emperor. Therefore righteousness and godliness must be acquired before everything else.

In the latter half-verse he gives the reason for this saying: the way of evil people—whether it is a way of nobility, of riches, or of inward knowledge—drives them into error, that is, makes them worse: “[To the unclean all things are unclean.]” Then it also deceives them concerning those advantages of life which they eagerly pursue, when they fall away from the happiness which they hope to attain.

Verse 27. The deceitful man will not roast what he has taken by hunting, but the diligent man [enjoys] the precious substance of a person.

In the word יחרך (*will roast*) there is a synecdoche of the species for “will not enjoy.” Likewise in צידו, where hunting is put for every kind of prey or spoil.

Human nature, being its own procuress, is induced in a moment to loosen for itself the reins of sin which God draws tight. Since it hopes that it cannot do this under God’s righteousness unless it removes what it does from the reckoning and estimation of sin, it diligently strives to do so. And although there are many kinds of sins which, as though masked, pretend to be virtues, almost no sin appears more whitewashed and painted over than that by which one intercepts another person’s property through deceit and cunning. See 1 Thessalonians 4:6; Ephesians 5; Colossians 3. For if anyone can overthrow another in his possessions by deceit and crafty counsels, then, because he is not held liable by civil law, he applauds himself as though for a deed well done; so far is he from

[1632 witness | PDF p. 196 | printed columns 366–367]

being suffused with shame over it. And although they shrink from deceit and cunning in no matter, they indulge themselves especially if they exercise it in hunting and in the pursuit of wild animals. For how few there are who do not think that everything is lawful for them in fowling and hunting, because they judge wild beasts to be common to all. But even if civil law had not provided that things enclosed by fences and walls are not common property, what else is it than to seek prey deceitfully and unlawfully when one invades another’s field and tramples the crops while pursuing a wild animal or a partridge, or, finally, uses that exercise to the point of satiety? Since, therefore, God forbids prey and game to be intercepted by deceit and guile, and imposes a penalty upon the person who circumvents his neighbor, it is plain that in every human business and commerce he regards fraud as hateful and displeasing. This manner of speaking is taken from ordinary experience, because it often happens that dogs devour the captured game, cats the catch of fish, and, finally, that the birds which a fowler has taken by fowling fly away before they are roasted; or, if they are put to the fire, they are nevertheless snatched away from it. It is also taken from the law, which threatens sinners and violators of the divine law that they will plant a vineyard from whose wine they will not drink, sow fields whose harvest they will not reap, build a house in which they will not dwell, and press oil with which they will not anoint themselves. From this it must be noted that the punishment here inflicted for violating one commandment is the punishment which the law inflicts for transgressing the whole law: even if you observe the whole law in other respects but are found utterly neglectful in one, you are held guilty of violating the entire law.

But let us explain the words. When he says יחרך, he indicates clearly that the unrighteous and greedy may obtain the power to acquire by fraud the prey they seek, to carry it home with them, to strip off its skin, and even to place it at the fire for roasting; yet before it is fully roasted it is forcibly taken away from them. Here it must be observed how people deceive themselves: if only they hold enclosed within their hands what they desire and seek, they immediately think the entire business completed and themselves in safe waters. They do not consider that the prey may be taken from them, or they from the prey, before they are permitted to use and enjoy it. Those who store acquired money in a perforated bag act in precisely the same way. See the parable in Luke 16, which teaches that prolongation of this life does not consist in possessing many things, since—as the proverb has it—many things fall between the cup and the outermost lips, and many between the mouth and the morsel. For although he had gathered and carried everything into barns and granaries, he was nevertheless not permitted to taste even the smallest bit. See Job 18, where it is said that a hungry man will make a harvest from another’s sowing. So in Exodus 16:20, what they craftily reserved for the next day contrary to the command rotted and swarmed with worms before it came to be either boiled or roasted. Sometimes a person’s deceit succeeds to the point that he puts into his mouth what he obtained by deceit. See Proverbs 20:17: “The bread of falsehood is sweet, but afterward it fills his mouth with gravel.” See an example of this in Numbers 11:33 and 1 Samuel 30:16–17.

It must be noted, however, that God takes vengeance upon human frauds all the more severely the closer he permits people to come to the use of possessions acquired for fraud. For the closer crafty deceivers come to the enjoyment of embezzled money, the more they rejoice and judge that its keeping will be secure and safe; and so, when they fall from that hope so near at hand, they are tormented more grievously and bitterly. It would have been more tolerable for such a person if he had been deprived of those things when the building was only begun, the vineyard not yet harvested, and the crop not yet carried into the barn, and so forth. Therefore let the crafty and fraudulent always tremble; for though they catch prey, perhaps they will not bring it home; they will not roast it; if they roast it, nevertheless, while it is still in their mouth, they are compelled to

[1632 witness | PDF p. 197 | printed columns 368–369]

vomit it back up. Indeed, what is more, even if they have swallowed it, yet by God’s just judgment they will be compelled to vomit up what they ate, as it is written in Job.

In the latter half-verse he opposes the diligent person to the deceitful, and possessions justly acquired to those amassed by fraud. He affirms that the diligent person’s possessions are precious, that is, at once of great and lasting use. Hence the saying in Psalm 37:16. To this also pertains what was recalled above from Luke 16: “A person’s life does not consist in his possessing many things.” Therefore, if anyone desires to prolong his life on earth and to pass it pleasantly, he must take care not so much—as is commonly done—to accumulate many things as to ensure that they have been obtained and acquired justly and lawfully, without fraud. For fraud gnaws and eats away possessions like a moth eating garments or rust eating money. Nor will it profit to drive thieves away outside when within your own house, and indeed in the innermost chambers of your soul, you nurture fraud like a band of thieves. That fraud alone, like a κλεὶς (*key*), will open all your gates, doors, and chests to thieves and robbers.

Verse 28. In the road of righteousness [there is] life, and the way of its path is immortal.

In the words דרך ארח נתיבה there is a metaphor obvious to everyone. Then in the words חיים (*life*) and מות (*death*) there is a synecdoche for prosperity and adversity.

Depraved nature within, and the devil and the world without, continually dictate to us and to our souls that life and health are endangered if anyone devotes himself to righteousness and is guided by zeal for it; that no one can readily endure this life unless, by loosening the reins of righteousness, he grants a place to fraud; and that it is a great shortcut through life if one serves the times and the stage, as it is said. This temptation is answered when it is said that life is prepared for those who persist in the track of righteousness. Almost all travelers are so disposed that they seek out, enter, and tread those roads in which no danger to life is perceived, whether from flooding waters, precipices, or robbers. Since, therefore, we must necessarily enter upon a journey, and since no other road in conducting our dealings is safe and free from danger except righteousness, let us embrace it under so great a guide, author, and counselor as Solomon—or rather, God in Solomon.

We must do this all the more because, in making journeys along roads and tracks, it is counted worthwhile if they bring no loss or danger. Since, therefore, on the road of righteousness there is not only no loss or danger, but life itself further offers itself to us as we complete the journey, it is a mark of singular perversity to depart from that road or, as they say, to turn aside by the breadth of a finger.

In the latter half-verse he teaches that all acts of righteousness are safe routes and paths. For almost everyone thinks that right and righteousness should be cultivated, and they judge that in most cases it is honorable and useful to conform oneself to the norm and rule of righteousness. But they also think there are certain cases, like paths, upon which they cannot persist without immediate danger: for example, if doing what is right would bring them into danger of reputation, possessions, or even life itself; if a prince commanded something unrighteous to be done; or if preserving faith would bring the loss of everything.

Then, when he says there is no death in the path of righteousness, he answers another difficulty. For it occurs to our minds to think that there is indeed some advantage in cultivating righteousness, something that pertains to life, but that there is also some disadvantage, something that threatens danger and loss. Solomon answers these thoughts when he says that in the path of righteousness there is life alone, not death,
