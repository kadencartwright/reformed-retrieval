QUESTION XXXII.

ON ECCLESIASTICAL DISCIPLINE AND EXCOMMUNICATION.

Do sacred ministers possess the spiritual power to excommunicate stubborn and
scandalous sinners? We affirm against Erastus and his followers.

I. The third part of ecclesiastical power concerns the exercise of discipline.
It consists in the spiritual power by which public and scandalous sinners, on
account of impurity in doctrine or life and after disregarding and rejecting
the church’s private and public admonitions, are barred by ministers, on the
order and authority of the presbytery, from the signs of divine grace. If they
persist in the same stubbornness, they are at last declared before the whole
church, in God’s name, to be excluded from the church’s communion until they
are reconciled to God and the church through true and earnest repentance.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 249 | IA leaf 283 | PDF p. 283]

[Marginalia: Our doctrine concerning discipline and excommunication.]

II. Apart from God’s institution, the necessity and justice of this discipline
are gathered from the nature of the thing itself. For if no society, indeed no
household, can be kept in a right condition without discipline, it is evident
that discipline is much more necessary in the church, whose condition ought to
be most orderly. Thus, just as Christ’s saving doctrine is the soul of the
church, so discipline serves as its sinews, by which the members of the body,
each in its own place, cohere with one another. Therefore all who either desire
discipline to be abolished or hinder its restoration or exercise seek the
church’s dissolution. For what will happen if everyone may do as he pleases?
Discipline, then, is the foundation of order and bond of unity, the bridle by
which those who rage against Christ’s doctrine are restrained and subdued, the
spur by which the insufficiently willing are roused, and, as it were, the
fatherly rod by which those who have fallen more seriously are chastened with
clemency and in keeping with the meekness of Christ’s Spirit.

III. The ends the church regards in discipline of this kind are these. First,
that those who lead a shameful and wicked life not be named among Christians,
to God’s dishonor, as though his holy church were a conspiracy of depraved and
criminal people. For since she is Christ’s body, she cannot be defiled by
putrid members of this kind without some disgrace falling upon the head.
Account must also be taken of the Lord’s Supper, lest it be profaned by
indiscriminate administration. Second, that the good not be corrupted, as
commonly happens, by constant association with the wicked, because nothing is
easier than for evil examples to draw us away from the right course of life.
Therefore Paul commands the Corinthians to banish the incestuous man from
their fellowship, because “a little leaven corrupts the whole lump.” And later:
“If anyone among you is called a brother but is either a fornicator, a covetous
person, an idolater, a drunkard, or a reviler, do not even eat with such a
person” (1 Cor. 5:6, 11). Third, that they themselves, being put to shame, may
begin to be ashamed of their disgrace. It benefits them for their wickedness
to be chastened, so that those whom indulgence would have made more stubborn
may be awakened by the feel of the rod.

IV. Here, however, it must especially be observed that this discipline
exercises a severity that is always joined with a spirit of meekness. For, as
Paul commands, diligent care must always be taken that the person against whom
discipline is exercised not be swallowed up by sorrow, because in that way the
remedy would be turned into destruction. Since what is sought in its exercise
is that the sinner be brought to repentance and evil examples be removed from
the midst, lest Christ’s name either be spoken ill of or provoke others to
imitation, it is easy to judge from this how far severity ought to proceed and
where it ought to stop. For when the sinner gives the church evidence of his
repentance and by this evidence blots out the scandal as far as lies in him,
he must not be pressed further; otherwise rigor exceeds its proper measure.
Hence the excessive austerity of the ancients cannot be approved. It both
departed from the Lord’s prescription and was exposed to considerable dangers.
For when they imposed upon a sinner solemn penance and deprivation of holy
communion, now for seven years, now for four years, and now for his whole life,
what could follow from this but either great hypocrisy or the greatest
despair? Therefore this spiritual sword must not be drawn for a trivial cause,
as commonly happens among the Anabaptists. Instead, everything must first be
tried, and recourse must be had to extremes only by degrees—that is, from the
lesser excommunication to the greater—as the Jews formerly proceeded from
*niddui* to *cherem* and from there to *maranatha*.

V. This power of correction consists in two keys, the closing and binding key
and the opening and loosing key, as is gathered from Matthew 16:19. By the
former, persistent heretics, disturbers of the church’s peace, and scandalous
and incorrigible sinners are suspended from the use of the sacraments after
one and a second admonition; and if

[1847 vol. III printed p. 250 | IA leaf 284 | PDF p. 284]

they persist in their stubbornness and rebellion, they are cast out of the
church so that they are permitted to participate in no exercise of piety with
the rest of the faithful. This must not be extended without qualification to
civil society, charitable and moral duties, or other duties owed and necessary
from our calling in the domestic or civil state, as the papists would have it,
as though wives were forbidden to perform for excommunicated husbands,
children for parents, servants for masters, and subjects for princes the
duties to which they are bound by natural law. For excommunication cannot
sever the natural and moral bonds by which people are bound to one another.
Rather, this separation must extend only to those parts of social intercourse
that might seem to imply connivance or approval. By the other key, which is
called opening and loosing, those who repent and grieve sincerely for their
sins are received again into the communion of the church and sacred things
after a suitable reparation of the scandal given, which is called canonical
satisfaction.

VI. Furthermore, this casting out from the church must be understood
differently according to its twofold state. With respect to the external
state, it denotes an actual separation from the external communion of the
church and the use of sacred things, yet not permanently but for a time, until
the person repents. With respect to the internal state, however, it is not an
actual expulsion from Christ’s mystical body, for one who has once been
incorporated into that body can never be cast out of it. Rather, it is only a
threat or declaration of the intrinsic desert of the fault: namely, that such
an impenitent and stubborn sinner deserves to be cast out of that society
according to God’s sentence pronounced against such sinners (1 Cor. 6:9), and
therefore rightly to be deprived of the sense of God’s grace present in this
life and to be deprived of the enjoyment of glory in the next unless he
repents. Hence one must not immediately believe that through excommunication a
sinner is simply and absolutely cut off from the body of Christ. For he often
does not cease to be a member of the church in secret and with respect to the
internal state, although with respect to external discipline he is removed for
a time from the society of the faithful. When a father casts a stubborn son
out of his house and deprives him of his presence and the tokens of paternal
favor, he has not on that account wholly disinherited him or divested himself
of every paternal affection toward him. Indeed, by then using this remedy to
bring him to repentance, the father displays his love toward him even by this
action, though the son does not recognize it then and will only acknowledge it
in earnest when through true conversion he is restored to his father’s favor.
Thus excommunication is the rod of a pious mother against a wayward son. She
does not regard him as no son because she strikes him, but chastens him
severely so that he may be corrected and not become no son at all; not to
destroy him, but to heal him; not to cast him away from herself, but to bring
the wanderer back to herself. Nor is it an objection that God says he will
ratify in heaven what has been bound on earth. God ratifies it in the manner in
which it was pronounced, but this cutting off is never absolute; it is
conditional, as was said.

VII. This is our doctrine concerning ecclesiastical discipline and its
exercise in excommunication, which is received and fruitfully exercised in
most Reformed and evangelical churches. It must nevertheless be admitted that
some churches very closely joined to us have not yet received its use—not
because they judge it unjust and unlawful, but because they believe it is not
so necessary under Christian princes and that, when a Christian magistrate
rightly performs his duty and restrains every scandalous person according to
the norm of the divine Word, the church can safely be without it. Meanwhile,
they not only do not disapprove and condemn its use in other churches, but
also judge that it can and ought suitably to be retained. This was done in the
preceding century by the most distinguished magistracy of Zurich and the
venerable assembly of that church. When asked by our people about consistorial
laws, the exercise of ecclesiastical discipline, and especially the use of
excommunication—which wicked and factious people were striving in every way
to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 251 | IA leaf 285 | PDF p. 285]

overthrow—they answered: “We have long heard of the consistorial laws of that
church and recognize that they are pious and approach the prescription of the
Word of God. Therefore it does not seem admissible that they should be changed
by innovation. It is better that they be preserved intact, especially in this
age in which people continually become worse. And although their discipline
does not correspond to ours in every respect, it is nevertheless tempered
according to the circumstances of times, places, and persons, and therefore we
do not wish ours to be overthrown.” And later: “We do not send the form of our
discipline to prescribe even the least thing. It is preferable that the
churches persevere in what has once been received, and indeed piously received
from Scripture, etc.” See Bullinger’s letter to Calvin at the year 1553,
December 12.

VIII. But others to whom excommunication was displeasing went further. They
not only did not receive it as unnecessary, but condemned it as unlawful and
unjust: as a purely human invention having no foundation in Scripture and
encroaching upon the rights of the magistrate, to whom the restraint of the
guilty belongs. Thomas Erastus, M.D., held this position and strove to prove it
by various arguments in his theses and treatise on excommunication, which
aroused various controversies at Heidelberg for this reason. Some followed
him here, especially L. Molinæus, whom we mentioned earlier. He dares not only
to denounce it as an invention of the human mind and an empty terror, but also
contends that it is a plague of the human race, a scourge of the earth, the
origin of every evil, the sole foundation of papal tyranny, the mystery of
iniquity, and the mark of Antichrist; that it must be shunned more than a dog
or snake and banished from the church’s borders. Indeed, this man of unsound
mind advances to such madness that he determines the words read about it in
the liturgy—“In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, I excommunicate all
idolaters, heretics, etc.”—must be expunged and replaced with these: “In the
name of John Calvin,” as though Calvin had been the first author of this holy
institution and it had not existed before him in the primitive church. But it
is less surprising that a man favoring Independency and fanaticism struck this
rock and was so bewitched by this error that until his death he did not cease
to fight for its defense as for hearth and home. Indeed, he dared to attack
those who disagreed with him with taunts and bitter sarcasms and to press them
with inept cavils. In doing this he became a laughingstock to pious men and
lovers of order, and was condemned by his own relatives and connections as “a
head scarcely curable by three Anticyras,” as he complains about them more
than once in his letters.

[Marginalia: The justice of excommunication is proved. 1. From the law of
societies.]

IX. It is not difficult to show from the various solid arguments by which it
is established how unjustly the doctrine received in our churches is denounced
by him and others who share his opinion. (1) As we said before, it is the
natural right of every well-ordered society to be able to separate from its
flock harmful and noxious people who are stains, disgraces, and cancers upon
its assembly. For the same power by which they possess the right to assemble
with one another gives them authority to make laws and constitutions for the
preservation of the body and for expelling those who are disobedient to these
laws and could infect or corrupt the whole body by their rebellion. This order
is so necessary that no assembly can subsist long without it. If this is
granted to other societies, it ought much more to belong to the church, which
is both holier and more orderly. Nor can those with whom we now deal deny
this. They acknowledge that when the magistrate is alien to the faith or
neglects his duty to restrain and punish criminals, any assembly can through a
confederated discipline and mutual compact assume for itself a certain power
of the magistrate, compel the disorderly into order, bar the impious and
faithless from itself, order them to keep their possessions to themselves, and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 252 | IA leaf 286 | PDF p. 286]

provide for other things conducive to its preservation. But it matters little
whether this is called a natural right or authority flowing from Christ,
because natural right is derived from none other than God himself. Indeed,
since the church is a sacred and religious society instituted by Christ
himself, no one can deny that it has received from Christ himself whatever
power it has, as it has all other things. For the same one who willed to erect
it in the world also equipped it with everything necessary for its
preservation.

[Marginalia: 2. From the practice of the Old Testament.]

X. Second, from the practice of the Old Testament, because excommunication had
a place in the Old Testament. Not to mention the casting of fallen man out of
Paradise and the prohibition against eating from the tree of life—Paradise was
a type of the church, while the prohibition had the nature of a sacrament—or
that Cain is said to have been driven from the Lord’s presence (Gen. 4:14),
that is, from a place dedicated to divine worship and sacred assemblies, a
divine judgment which can be an example to us with respect to ecclesiastical
judgment, it is evident that under the law the uncircumcised, lepers, and those
legally unclean were prohibited from the temple and ceremonies. (1) God
commanded not only that those unclean according to the law voluntarily abstain
from the communion of sacred things (Lev. 5, 6, 7, and 10), but also that the
priests and Levites above others take care of this matter—namely, that
according to God’s Word they distinguish and judge between clean and unclean,
holy and profane, as may be seen concerning every uncleanness (Lev. 19:8;
Ezek. 44:9) and concerning those unclean from leprosy (Lev. 13:2), and that
they bar those whom they had judged unclean from the communion of the temple
and sacred things until they were purified again through particular
sacrifices. Necessary expiation supposes uncleanness, and uncleanness was
necessarily followed by exclusion from the altar, which, besides the singular
example of Uzziah (2 Chron. 26), is indicated universally (2 Chron. 23:18):
“Jehoiada distributed the duties in the house of God among the priests and
Levites, etc., and stationed gatekeepers at the gates of the house of God so
that no one unclean in any respect might enter.” Although this is said of
ceremonial uncleanness, it can and ought no less—indeed, much more—to be
understood by an argument from the lesser to the greater of moral uncleanness,
since the former bore a type of the latter (Hag. 2:12, 14). (2) In Ezekiel
44:9, God joins the uncircumcised in heart with the uncircumcised in flesh in
exclusion from the sanctuary: “No foreigner uncircumcised in flesh or
uncircumcised in heart shall enter my sanctuary.” Although this is understood
mystically of the sanctuary of the New Testament, it nevertheless denotes
what was to be done in the external sanctuary, as is clearly demonstrated by
the rebuke of the priests in verses 6 and 8: “Let it be enough for you. Depart
from all your iniquities, O house of Israel, who have brought in foreigners
uncircumcised in heart and uncircumcised in flesh to be in my sanctuary and
profane my house.” (3) In various places the prophets rebuke those who entered
God’s house with hands full of blood and the priests for not distinguishing
between clean and unclean (Isa. 1:12; Jer. 7:9; Ezek. 23:38; 22:26; Zeph.
3:4). Thus in Leviticus 5:21 those who were unclean on account of fraud and
theft could not have access to sacred things unless they had been cleansed by
the sacrifices prescribed by God. Now where there is uncleanness, there is
also exclusion from sacred things held in common (Num. 19:20). Pertinent here
is the frequent reference in Moses to the cutting off of a soul from the
people (Exod. 12:15, 19; 30:33; Lev. 7:15, 17; 17:4; Gen. 17:14; Num. 9:13;
15:30–31; 19:20; and frequently elsewhere). With our Calvin and many other
learned men, we judge that this should be referred to excommunication rather
than to some divinely inflicted judgment or eternal death.

XI. Nor is there any doubt that excommunication had a place among the Jews and
was often exercised, as is evident even from Ezra 10:8. Hence the frequent

[1847 vol. III printed p. 253 | IA leaf 287 | PDF p. 287]

mention of “separation” in the Gospel and of those who became “put out of the
synagogue” by following Christ (Luke 6:22; John 9:22, 34–35; 12:42; 16:2).
Pertinent here are the three kinds of separation that existed among them:
*niddui*, *cherem*, and *shammatha*, or *maran atha*. Learned men establish
this distinction among them: *niddui* was the first and lighter kind of
excommunication, for a fixed time; *cherem* was more severe and punished one
who had not repented under the first, and was performed with curses and
imprecations; the third, *shammatha*, was the most severe and offered no hope
of return to the communion of the commonwealth or church, as the most learned
Buxtorf observes in his Talmudic lexicon. But if this discipline was a mere
invention of human ingenuity destined to establish the mystery of iniquity,
how is it that Christ, who so sharply rebuked the Pharisees’ scrupulous
religion in tithing herbs and cleansing vessels, did not say even one word
about this deadly custom? This assuredly indicates clearly enough that he did
not disapprove this rite, provided it was rightly exercised.

[Marginalia: 3. From Christ’s prescription.]

XII. Third, because Christ confirmed and prescribed this order, which already
existed in the Jewish church, by handing over the keys of the kingdom of
heaven to Peter and, in his person, to the whole church. By these keys the
kingdom is not only opened but also closed so that the profane have no entrance
into it. He also confirmed it through the power of binding and loosing granted
to the same church, which, as was seen before, must not be referred to mere
preaching but also includes the exercise of discipline. This is confirmed from
Matthew 18:17, where Christ wills that an offending brother who refuses to
acquiesce in the pious admonitions of the injured brother be brought before the
church, or presbytery, and if he refuses to hear it, that we regard him as a
Gentile and a publican. This alludes to the procedure of the synagogue, for it
declared those whom it excluded from its communion to be regarded as Gentiles
and publicans—that is, as aliens from the covenant and people of God, as were
all Gentiles and publicans, who were held in the greatest reproach among the
Jews and with whom the people of God ought to have had no association.

XIII. The following objections are of no force here. 1. “Binding and loosing
denote the remission and retention of sins that occurs through the preaching
of the Word.” The affirmation of one does not exclude the other; indeed, these
accompany each other: general binding and loosing, which occurs through
preaching, by which pardon is promised in general to the penitent and
punishment announced to the disobedient; and particular binding and loosing,
which pertains to the exercise of discipline, by which the promises of grace
are applied to this or that penitent, while the retention of sins and exclusion
from the kingdom of heaven unless he repents are announced to this or that
scandalous and stubborn sinner. 2. “The church which the injured brother ought
to hear is the Jewish Sanhedrin, or civil magistrate of the same religion. If
he refuses to hear even this, he must be brought before a Gentile magistrate,
as is customary when a matter is with a Gentile and publican.” The vanity of
this gloss is evident from various considerations. (1) Nowhere is the Jewish
Sanhedrin designated by the name “church.” It is absurd that Christ would send
his disciples when injured to the civil Jewish Sanhedrin, because it was most
hostile to Christians and was soon to be abolished with the commonwealth.
Furthermore, either such a Sanhedrin was the supreme tribunal, from which no

[1847 vol. III printed p. 254 | IA leaf 288 | PDF p. 288]

appeal was permitted, yet here they wish an appeal to be made to the pagan
magistrate; or, if some inferior forum is understood, an appeal ought to have
been made from it not to the pagans but to the supreme Jewish Sanhedrin. But it
is clear that Christ understands that church to which he willed to give the
keys of the kingdom of heaven, so that whatever it bound on earth would be
bound in heaven (v. 18), the church of which he says in verse 20, “Where two or
three are gathered in my name, there I am in the midst of them.” Everyone sees
that this is said of the Christian church. (2) It is unheard of for an appeal
to a Gentile magistrate to be designated by the words, “Let him be to you as a
Gentile and a publican.” Thus Christians would be allowed—not merely allowed,
but enjoined—to flee to a Gentile tribunal to contend and litigate with a
brother, contrary to Paul, who forbids this and therefore sharply rebukes the
Corinthians for going to judgment against their brothers before Gentiles
(1 Cor. 6:1–3). Furthermore, on this account Christ would teach the method not
of gaining an offending brother, but of recovering losses and obtaining
reparation for injuries, which ought not to be the primary aim of Christians.
3. “Christ does not say, ‘Let him be to the whole church as a Gentile and a
publican,’ as he ought to have said if excommunication were performed by it,
but, ‘Let him be to you.’” (1) But it is wrongly supposed that according to us
the sense of the words “Let him be to you as a Gentile” is “Let him be
excommunicated,” as though Christ had given every private person the power to
excommunicate a stubborn brother. According to us, these words denote nothing
other than withdrawal from the brother, so that we no longer regard him as a
brother, live with him as a member of the same religious society, or have
association with him, just as the Jews wished to have nothing in common with
pagans and publicans. (2) In this way we do not contend that Christ gave every
person the right to excommunicate a brother by regarding him as a Gentile, but
only that Christ wills us to regard as a Gentile a stubborn brother who has
refused to hear the church and therefore has been cut off from the church’s
communion by the church, or ecclesiastical presbytery. Thus regarding a person
as a Gentile and publican does not constitute the excommunication; it follows
the excommunication and supposes it to have been performed by the church, or
assembly of governors, on account of stubbornness. (3) Christ could rightly
say, “Let him be to you as a Gentile,” because he was dealing with what the
private injured person was to do toward the hardened and stubborn brother who
refused to hear the church and was therefore excommunicated by the church’s
judgment. But by parity of reasoning, what is said to one is said to all. Hence
he immediately adds in the plural, “Whatever you bind,” not, “Whatever you
bind” in the singular. (4) “Power was indeed given to the church, but only as
long as it was under the cross, and it lacked that power under Christian
emperors.” This also is assumed without warrant, because the order constituted
by Christ in the church ought not to have been temporary but perpetual. It was
afterward confirmed by perpetual use under both Christian and pagan emperors.
The emperors’ reception of Christianity ought not to have abrogated this
power, but only to have ensured that it did not derogate from civil power or
become confused with it. So far was the pious magistrate from abolishing this
order that he ought instead to have confirmed and sanctioned it. It is evident
that it also continued steadfastly under the emperors.

[Marginalia: 4. From Paul, 1 Corinthians 5:3.]

XIV. Fourth, Paul confirms the same institution more than once, especially in
the punishment exercised against the incestuous Corinthian (1 Cor. 5:3). After
describing the atrocity of the crime and rebuking the church’s negligence in
casting that man out of its communion, he decrees by apostolic authority that
they finally do what ought to have been done long before: namely, that when
gathered in the name of Christ, from whom they had received this power, they
deliver this unclean man to Satan and thus remove him from their midst. This
was not so that the wretched man cast out of the Lord’s house would perish in
Satan’s kingdom, but so that, worn down by this punishment, he might utterly
destroy that criminal affection of the flesh and thus be preserved on the day
of the Lord; and so that the Corinthian church would not be infected by the
contagion of such impurity, but, with this leaven purged out as was established
for the legal Passover, might truly celebrate the feast of unleavened bread,
that is, worship God purely and holily in both hearing the Word and approaching
the Lord’s table, and thus enjoy Christ our Passover. Verse 4 says: “In the
name of our Lord Jesus Christ, when you and my spirit are gathered, deliver
such a person to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that the spirit
may be preserved on the day of the Lord Jesus.” Various arguments occur here.
(1) The apostle rebukes the Corinthians in verse 2 because

[1847 vol. III printed p. 255 | IA leaf 289 | PDF p. 289]

they had not “mourned so that such a sinner might be removed from their
midst.” Surely this was not by capital punishment, as though he wished to
ascribe to the presbytery the magistrate’s proper right, but by
excommunication, by which the sinner is removed from the midst of the
assembly—that is, excluded from the church, as Cyprian speaks. Because the
church, when it was about to exclude someone from its fellowship, assumed the
garb of mourners and “mourned him as dead,” as Origen says in *Against Celsus*,
book 3, “mourning” is therefore put for “excommunicating” by a metonymy of the
adjunct. Thus in verse 13 Paul prescribes that they “remove the wicked man from
their midst,” which he surely could not have done unless he recognized that
the church has the right to cast the wicked out of its communion. (2) He
describes this punishment as the “purging of leaven” (v. 7). For just as
leaven had to be cast out of the house for the celebration of Passover, so the
impious incestuous man who, like leaven, could corrupt the whole lump—that is,
infect the whole church, as the contagion of one wicked person easily spreads
to all—had to be cast out of the church lest he corrupt the others by this
evil example. (3) Delivery to Satan can be referred to nothing better than
excommunication and its effects. The expression’s rationale is taken from the
fact that one who has been cast outside the church and thereby deprived of the
goods proper to the church—such as prayers, sacraments, public sermons,
pastoral care, and God’s special protection—is left exposed to the tyranny and
temptations of the devil, who reigns outside the church. Paul thus wished to
indicate more effectively how dreadful it is to be cast out of the church’s
assembly. Pertinent are these words of Theophylact on 1 Timothy 2:10: “They
punished the unbelieving and incorrigible and delivered him to Satan. How? He
was cast out of the church, cast away from charity, and delivered naked to the
wolf.”

XV. In vain Erastus and others make the following objections to evade the
force of this argument. (1) “If to deliver to Satan is to cast outside the
church, outside which there is no salvation, Paul would not have said he did
this so that the spirit might be saved on the day of the Lord.” Why not? A
person cast outside the church is considered delivered to Satan, not as one
who will remain with Satan and perish with him, but on the contrary so that,
moved by the sense of his most miserable condition, he may repent. Thus, when
the flesh—that is, the corruption of the old man that previously ruled
him—has been destroyed, the spirit may be saved; that is, the old man may be
mortified and the new made alive. (2) “Delivery to Satan must be understood as
the miraculous delivery of the incestuous man to be tormented and killed, as
was done by Peter with Ananias and Sapphira and by Paul with Bar-Jesus.” Many
considerations show that Paul speaks simply of the church’s ordinary duty, not
of a miracle. 1. He rebukes the Corinthians because they had not removed him
from their midst (v. 2), but he would not rebuke them for failing to perform a
miracle, a gift particular to the apostles and not to all, nor would the
apostle have commanded them to work a miracle. 2. Removing this man requires
the solemn consent and assembly of the church. This punishment had to be
inflicted by those whose duty it was to assemble together for this purpose,
whom the apostle commands to purge out the old leaven and remove the impious
man from their midst. But what need was there for the church’s consent and
assembly to perform a miracle, which the apostles are not known ever to have
required in any extraordinary work? 3. The end of this punishment shows that
it cannot be understood of death miraculously inflicted upon him, both because
it is evident that the incestuous man survived that delivery and because he is
delivered to Satan so that his spirit may be saved—that is, so that he may
repent in this life and finally be saved. This is confirmed by the parallel
passage, 1 Timothy 1:20, where Paul is said to have delivered Hymenæus and
Alexander to Satan “so that they might learn not to blaspheme,” that is, so
that, affected by this punishment, they might desist from blasphemy through
earnest repentance. 4. The example of Ananias and Sapphira is not pertinent
here because it did not belong to the exercise of discipline and their
correction, but to the just punishment of their lie, so that

[1847 vol. III printed p. 256 | IA leaf 290 | PDF p. 290]

apostolic authority might thus be held in greater esteem. 5. Although “remove
from the midst” is sometimes understood of killing, it does not follow that it
must be so used here, because the apostle does not simply say that such a
person must be removed from the midst, but “from their midst,” which clearly
indicates ejection from the church. 6. Even if we granted what some of the
fathers held, that this delivery to Satan entailed some bodily torments, the
doctrine concerning excommunication would not therefore be overthrown,
because being cast out of the church and being tormented by Satan, who reigns
outside the church, do not conflict with one another, just as an evil spirit
invaded Saul after he was rejected by the Lord. Chrysostom joins both together:
Satan’s scourge and ejection outside the church. On 1 Timothy 1:20 he says,
“They cast him out of the common assembly of the faithful; he was cut off from
the flock and made naked, and thus, left destitute, he lay exposed to wolves.”
Although this delivery to Satan had something extraordinary insofar as it
proceeded from apostolic authority—just as it is certain that Christ gave the
apostles a distinguished power above others in the government of the church,
so that they would be held in greater esteem among the faithful—this entire
action was not therefore extraordinary and singular, proper to the apostle
alone to the exclusion of the church, which is expressly included here. Nor,
if what was extraordinary had to cease, must what was common and ordinary
therefore be abrogated, which he wisely prescribed to his church as permanent
and immutable. The most learned Saldenus observes this very well in his
excellent dissertation *On the Apostolic Rod*, where he treats this argument
solidly and accurately, just as he does all the other matters he discusses in
his recently published learned work *Theological Leisure*, in which he
brilliantly demonstrates that he is doing anything but idling.

XVI. (4) Pertinent here is what the apostle says in 1 Corinthians 4:21 about
the rod he could exercise against the Corinthians: “What do you wish? Shall I
come to you with a rod, or in charity?” Although I am not unaware that quite a
few learned men think this apostolic rod should be ascribed to the apostles’
peculiar prerogatives and was nothing other than a certain extraordinary power
divinely granted to them, by which they could punish disobedient and stubborn
sinners not only with words, threats, and rebukes, but also with a certain
sharper remedy—that is, some bodily injury—which they sometimes exercised
against the impious and incorrigible, so that the good would be moved by their
example, the wicked deterred from blaspheming, and God’s most severe vengeance
against evildoers manifested; and although we do not deny that examples of
this severity occur in Peter against Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5:5) and in
Paul against Elymas the sorcerer, nothing compels us to go so far. This rod can
suitably be referred to the severity of discipline by which, as a father—which
he had said he was toward them in verse 15—he could chastise their faults
through ecclesiastical censures and sharp correction, such as the one he was
about to exercise against the incestuous man. He opposes it to a spirit of
gentleness and charity, not because it too does not proceed from charity, but
because charity was concealed by such severity: by sadness of countenance and
harshness of words, by which he would not so much console and confirm the
repentant as rebuke offenders, as the same reverend doctor well observes.

[Marginalia: 5. From Romans 16:17.]

XVII. Fifth, from various passages in which the apostles designate this
discipline not obscurely, as in Romans 16:17: “I beseech you, brothers, to mark
the authors of dissensions and offenses contrary to the doctrine you learned,
and turn away from them”; 2 Thessalonians 3:14–15: “If anyone does not obey our
word through this letter, mark him and have no association with him, so that he
may be ashamed”; Titus 3:10: “Reject a heretical person after one and a second
admonition”; and 2 John 10–11: “If anyone comes to you and does not bring this
doctrine, do not receive him

[1847 vol. III printed p. 257 | IA leaf 291 | PDF p. 291]

into your house or bid him greeting.” This signifies that familiarity and
fellowship with such people must be avoided, because one who bids him greeting,
and much more one who receives him as a guest in a sign of familiar goodwill,
“shares in his evil works,” that is, takes a share in the guilt as an approver
or supporter. He alludes to the custom of the Jews, who avoided the
excommunicated as Gentiles and publicans and neither offered nor returned to
them the greeting common among people. But these and other things of this kind
are said not only to private persons, but also to the whole church. The point
is easily gathered by an argument from the lesser to the greater. For if every
believer separately ought to separate himself from the impious, how much more
ought the church itself to cast them out of its communion? Pertinent is the
praise of the Ephesian church for being “unable to bear the wicked” (Rev. 2:2),
that is, for rebuking and casting them out of the church, tempering meekness
with zeal, and not conniving at sins.

[Marginalia: 6. From Matthew 7:6.]

XVIII. Sixth, from Matthew 7, where Christ forbids “giving what is holy to
dogs.” By “holy things” the mysteries are understood, of which Christ’s
servants are stewards (1 Cor. 4:1). The names “dogs” and “swine” fit none
better than those who, after being admitted into the church, have been proved
such by experience and judged such by the church. Therefore the Lord forbids
giving his mysteries to such people and consequently administering the
sacraments to them. Although this is not said directly with respect to the
Eucharist or excommunication, a general rule is nevertheless drawn from it
against tolerating the profanation of sacred mysteries that is committed by
unclean swine and dogs. Nor should it be replied that the wicked cannot infect
the good with whom they commune. Great scandal is thereby created for pious
souls, who cannot without holy indignation see these sacred mysteries polluted
by the profane, themselves associated with such people at the same table, and
no distinction made between the good and the wicked, the penitent and the
impenitent. Furthermore, the mysteries themselves are cheapened when holy
things destined for the holy are indiscriminately distributed to the profane.
Third, although no harm would thereby return to the good and pious, there is
nevertheless the greatest evil in this: the profane pollute the sacred table
for themselves and summon most immediate destruction upon themselves by
eating and drinking their own judgment. It is the pastors’ duty to prevent
pollution of this kind as far as lies in them, lest they too be guilty of
sacrilege when sacred things are polluted by others through their negligence.
Would it not be cruel mercy to give a profane person free access to the sacred
mysteries, by which he would summon new guilt upon himself? Indeed, compassion
alone, by which it is right to be moved toward a wretched person increasing
his crimes and in this way hurling himself headlong into the abyss of
destruction, would suffice to deny him the sacrament even if no other reason
were available.

[Marginalia: 7. From the practice of the ancient church.]

XIX. Seventh, from the practice of the ancient church, which customarily
removed public sinners from the communion of sacred things until, through
various public exercises of humiliation usually expressed by the common name
*exomologesis*, the church’s scandal had been suitably repaired and they were
again received and admitted publicly by the same power. Tertullian teaches
this excellently in *Apology*, chapter 39: “Certainly we nourish faith with
holy words, raise hope, establish confidence, and no less strengthen
discipline by repeated inculcations of the precepts. In the same place are also
exhortations, chastisements, and divine censures. For judgment too is
conducted with great weight, as among people certain of God’s presence, and it
is the highest anticipation of the coming judgment if anyone has so sinned
that he is banished from sharing prayer, the assembly, and all sacred
fellowship, etc.” Many things of this kind occur in Cyprian concerning the
exercise of this discipline. Pertinent are the four distinct grades of penance
that existed among them. (1) *Proklaūsis*, or weeping, at the door of the
oratory or temple, where the sinner stood and tearfully entreated the faithful
entering to pray for him. (2) *Akroasis*, or hearing the divine Word, in the
portico of the oratory, from which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 258 | IA leaf 292 | PDF p. 292]

they had to depart as soon as prayers began, since it was forbidden for them
to participate in these. (3) *Hypoptōsis*, or prostration, inside the temple
door, where he stood with the catechumens and was bound to leave with them.
(4) *Systasis*, or standing, when he had the right to stand among the faithful
themselves and no longer to leave with the catechumens. This was finally
followed by *methexis*, or participation in the sacraments. In *systasis*
there was communion in prayer; in *methexis*, communion in the holy things.
How steadfastly and rigorously this discipline was exercised is evident from
the penitential canons, and no one was so exempt from it that even princes,
together with commoners, did not submit themselves to bearing it. Rightly so,
since it was acknowledged to be Christ’s, to whom it is right that every royal
scepter be submitted. Thus when Theodosius was deprived by Ambrose of the
right of communion on account of the slaughter perpetrated at Thessalonica, he
laid aside every royal insignia with which he was surrounded, publicly
bewailed his sin in church, and with groaning and tears prayed for pardon. He
did not judge that he ought to regard it as a disgrace if he humbly prostrated
himself before Christ, the King of Kings, nor that it ought to displease him to
be judged through the church. Although that ancient severity was relaxed over
the course of time and those strict canons fell into disuse when Christian
morals began to soften through worldly wealth and pleasures, the whetstone of
persecutions failing upon which the fervor of zeal and love of virtue were
accustomed to be sharpened, discipline must not therefore be abolished
entirely, but suitably exercised for edification according to the circumstances
of place and time.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XX. Although a private person cannot separate himself from the sacred Supper
without the crime of schism on account of faults prevailing among many in the
church, provided the doctrine is pure, it does not follow that the church may
not separate anyone from the Supper, provided his confession is pure. The
condition of a private person is one thing and that of the church’s governors
another. The latter are commanded to keep watch lest the sacraments be
profaned and to distinguish between clean and unclean with public authority,
which no private person may assume for himself. For the church’s separation
is a remedy against faults, while the private person’s separation violates
charity.

XXI. Even if Christ had admitted Judas the traitor to communion in the
sacrament, which is rightly doubted, it would not follow that everyone must be
admitted indiscriminately, because Judas was still a secret traitor and
hypocrite, known to Christ alone. But Christ willed to act not as God from
divine authority, but as a man and the Father’s minister. No one ought to be
barred from the Supper except on account of a public and scandalous sin proved
by suitable witnesses, of which the sinner has been convicted and condemned
by public authority.

XXII. The Word and sacraments do not stand on the same footing. The Word is
directed to everyone, even the impious and profane, because it is the means of
people’s conversion. But the sacraments are destined only for believers,
because they are seals of the covenant which ought to seal grace to those in
covenant and presuppose a particular application that requires the condition
of faith in those who use them. The Word is destined to give faith and
repentance, but the sacraments to confirm both. The Word is directed to
unbelievers so that they may become believers; the sacraments are given
because they are believed to be believers. The Word does not create a bond of
society among those to whom it is preached; the sacraments are given as bonds
of our union, so that they may be seals of our justification. Thus they cannot
rightly be given to those who have broken the bonds of society and are not in
a state of justification.

XXIII. Although Paul expressly excluded no one from the sacred Supper in
chapters 10 and 11 of 1 Corinthians, because he leaves this duty to the
ecclesiastical council, content to have prescribed the rule, he nevertheless
teaches sufficiently how scandalous sinners and heretics must be treated when
he says that no one can partake of the Lord’s table and the table of demons,
signifying that those who

[1847 vol. III printed p. 259 | IA leaf 293 | PDF p. 293]

share in the sacred rites of idols cannot approach the Lord’s Supper. The same
thing is testified not obscurely in both 1 Corinthians 5 and 2 Thessalonians
3:14–15.

XXIV. The parable of the tares, which must be left until the harvest, is not
pertinent here, because it concerns secret hypocrites who cannot easily be
separated from true believers. Nor does it discuss so much the duty of the
magistrate or pastor as that of believers themselves, whom Christ here exhorts
not to lose heart because they are compelled to bear the wicked with them.
Christ does not intend to deny that pastors ought to apply themselves to
purifying the church and that they must be assisted in this by all the pious
so far as each person’s calling permits. But after everyone together has
contributed mutual effort, if they cannot advance so far that the church is
entirely cleansed from every stain, the ministry is not therefore to be cast
aside or the whole church disturbed with unusual harshness. Thus this parable
of the church’s universal purification, which will never occur before the last
day, does not prohibit particular removals performed either by the magistrate
or by ecclesiastical judgment.

XXV. Although Christ did not expressly bar anyone from the use of the
sacraments, he nevertheless designated this sufficiently when he forbids
anyone to approach the altar unless he has first been reconciled to his
brother; when he prohibits giving what is holy to dogs; when he wills that
stubborn brothers who rebel against the church’s voice be regarded as Gentiles
and publicans; when he wills the one who lacks a wedding garment to be cast out
of the banquet; and when Christ institutes and celebrates his Supper only
among his disciples. By this very fact he sufficiently shows that celebration
of the Supper belongs only to those who profess themselves Christ’s disciples,
not to those who have been excluded by the church from the communion of sacred
things on account of impurity of life and public crimes. Who would think that
when Christ instituted the Supper, which is the token of our mystical
communion with him, he willed anyone to approach his table otherwise than
worthily? Therefore, in inviting those who approach worthily and commanding
everyone to eat, he certainly did not command the unworthy to approach as they
were. Rather, he exhorted them in such a way that they would prepare
themselves for that feast by repenting, sufficiently warning them, with the
apostle as interpreter, not to approach while otherwise disposed.

XXVI. There was no reason for John the Baptist to reject the Sadducees and
Pharisees who approached his baptism, since they came with confession of sins,
as is expressly stated in Matthew 3:6; they had not been excommunicated by
others, nor did he himself have power to excommunicate them, even if they had
been worthy of excommunication. Therefore he did what belonged to his office
when he exhorted all who approached to repentance, setting before them God’s
most severe judgment from the law and at the same time offering the remission
of sins in the Christ who was present.

XXVII. Just as the Anabaptists rave when, in order to overthrow the
magistrates’ tribunals, they pretend that criminals must be punished by no
penalty other than excommunication, so others groundlessly think that censures
of this kind prejudice the magistrates’ authority. Ecclesiastical correction
and civil punishment differ in many ways: the latter pertains chiefly to
punishment, but the former to correction; the former regards the soul’s
salvation and conversion, but the latter the body’s destruction and penalty.
Therefore, although the magistrate punishes criminals with bodily punishment,
it does not follow that there is no place for spiritual and ecclesiastical
censures by which sinners are brought to godly sorrow, repentance, and
salvation.

XXVIII. No special revelation of the Holy Spirit is needed to decree
excommunication. It is sufficient that pastors act from the Word of God and
the power granted to them in it. For just as it is evident by right from the
Word of God for what causes excommunication must be exercised, so in fact it
is evident from suitable testimony concerning scandals committed that must be
corrected by the church.
