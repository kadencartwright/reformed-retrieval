CHAPTER XL.

The Arguments of Those Who Pledge Silence Are Refuted.

First, you are accustomed to cite Solomon's statement that there is a time for
silence just as for speech, Ecclesiastes 3:7. How little this favors you is
plain from the same passage, verse 7, where it is said that there is no time of
peace in a time of war. For in our spiritual warfare silence answers to peace,
and confession to war. And that the time especially calls for speaking on
behalf of discipline is clear when, as you know, a great war is being waged
over discipline. Thus Paul says in 1 Corinthians 16:9: “A great and effective
door has been opened to me, and there are many adversaries; therefore I shall
remain at Ephesus until Pentecost.” It is as though one must remain at one's
post and fight most of all when truth itself is assailed by many adversaries.
So too elsewhere: “There are many insubordinate people whose mouths must be
stopped,” Titus 1:10–11. It is as though God's servant must speak most of all
when the insubordinate set themselves most strongly against the truth. Do you
believe that their mouths can be stopped by the silence of your mouths, and
will not instead be opened by it? Thus God himself says: “There is no one who
cries out for justice, and no one who pleads for faith,” Isaiah, chap—

[1638 control | printed p. 137 | BSB canvas 147 | bsb10507786_00147]

—ter 59, verse 4. “They do not prevail in faith in the land,” Jeremiah 9:3.
“There was no helper; I was astonished that there was no one to support me,”
Isaiah 93:5. Is it not plain from every one of these passages how greatly God
is displeased by the sloth of those who bring no aid to truth in its struggle?
Canon law rightly says from Augustine: “When the welfare of the whole Church
is at stake and the faith is attacked, the prelates must ascend against the
enemy and, on the day of battle, set themselves like a wall before the house
of the Lord and lay down their lives for their sheep, so that by the example
of their suffering they may kindle those whom they can no longer strengthen
by the word of doctrine.” How apt these words are against those who do not set
themselves against the corruptions of discipline.

Second, you are accustomed to allege the example of Paul, who, you say, kept
silent at Ephesus for three years about the expressions of its idolatry so
that he might gain an opportunity to preach to the Jews.

First, the Gospel had to be preached to the Jews in the first place and then
to the Gentiles. Therefore, if Paul abstained for a while from addressing the
Ephesian Gentiles until he had first instructed the Jews, this still does not
favor your silence, since no such dispensation has been entrusted to you.

Second, it follows from that reasoning that silence is as permissible about
all papist idolatries as it is about discipline.

Third, when a conflict arose over Ephesian idolatry, Paul did not then refuse
to fight with the beasts at Ephesus, 1 Corinthians 15:32; Acts 19:30.
Therefore, since the conflict over discipline in England is fierce, you have
no time for delay if you wish to imitate Paul's example.

Fourth, it is false that Paul remained silent about Ephesian idolatry for so
long. The town clerk's statement in Acts 19:37, that “these men have not
reviled your goddess,” was a political falsehood intended to calm the riot.
Demetrius himself contradicts it in Acts 19:26: “You hear and see that Paul,
by persuasion, has turned away a great crowd not only at Ephesus but throughout
almost all Asia, saying that those which are made by hands are not gods.”

Fifth, Paul teaches by his own example what God's servants must do. In Acts
17:16 his spirit was provoked within him at Athens as soon as he saw that the
city was given over to idols. Samson must be joined to him, for the Spirit of
Jehovah came upon him as soon as his ears perceived the sound of the
Philistines shouting as they came to meet him, Judges 14:15. Do these examples
not teach that the very sight of discipline violated in England ought to have
provoked your spirits, and that the Hierarchists' outcry against it ought to
have awakened God's Spirit within you?

Third, you claim that through your silence about discipline you gain an
opportunity to preach Evangelical doctrine. I say this differs little from the
craftiness of Pilate, who judged that Christ should be scourged in his body so
that, once the Jews were pacified, he might gain Christ's life, John 19:1.
Moses could have gained the people if he had yielded some of the animals to
Pharaoh, but he judged that he must not yield even a single hoof, Exodus 10:26.
Daniel could have gained a long—

—er period in which to benefit the Church if he had been willing to refrain
from praying to his God for even a very short time. But he also judged that he
must open the window of his chamber so that he might make a clearer confession
of his piety, Daniel 6:11. John the Baptist could have gained an opportunity
to preach the Gospel longer if he had been willing to refrain from reproving
Herod, Mark 6:18, but he ordered such carnal prudence to be kept far from him.
Nor was the Lord Jesus Christ himself willing to spare that same Herod so that
he might preach the Gospel more freely. Instead, resting upon the Father's
providence—as you also ought to have done—he said: “Go and tell that fox that I
shall make my journey today, tomorrow, and the day after, until I am brought to
completion,” Luke 13:32–33. Socrates likewise says of Meletius: “For a time at
Antioch, Meletius did not involve himself in matters of faith but taught his
hearers only those things which pertained to morals. At last, however, he
unfolded the clause concerning one substance and so allowed himself to be sent
into exile.” Thus Meletius at last grew weary of your prudence.

Fourth, you say that those who preach about discipline today labor in vain and
are plainly washing a brick. A brick, indeed? What if they are also washing an
Ethiopian? Augustine rightly says: “No less pay is owed to the barber when he
washes an Ethiopian, though he leaves him darker, than when he washes a Greek,
whom he nevertheless renders fairer.” You know very well how many replies
could be made to this objection. For the present I shall keep within the hide,
as it were, of this cause.

Saint Bernard therefore meets this objection. When Eugenius refused to
evangelize for this very reason, Bernard spoke to him as follows: “You tell me
to feed dragons, you say, and scorpions. For this reason, I say, attack them all
the more.” He alludes to Ezekiel 2:6: “But you, son of man, do not fear them,
because they are insubordinate and thorny toward you and you dwell among
scorpions. Do not be dismayed at their faces, because they are a rebellious
house.”

Emperor Louis once convened an assembly at Regensburg against Pope John. In it
the Bishops made the following decree against this objection: “They are
accounted *Semmystae*, though they are messengers of Antichrist. Their
insatiable thirst for honor, power, and money betrays them, as do their luxury,
avarice, and pomp, which have become proverbial. We candidly confess that our
times do not bear censure. Christian simplicity has long been sordid amid
corrupt morals, and custom of longer standing, more powerful than truth, has
prevailed. Nevertheless indignation breaks silence. Placed by heaven upon the
loftiest watchtower of human affairs, we cannot keep silent, lest we be dumb
dogs which, in the sacred prophet's words, cannot bark. And although, because
of the unhappiness of the times, we cannot drive away the wolves covered in
sheep's clothing, yet not to resist such men is to betray our office. Although
we cannot do as much as we wish, nevertheless, even without attaining it, to
have desired it is abundantly beautiful and magnificent.”

The words of the Bishop of Civita are similar: “What shall I say, or what more
shall I add, about the condition of the clergy? For, as I see it, this people
is stiff-necked, sold to its iniquities, and obstinate in its crimes. I called
him, and there was no one who would hear, Isaiah 63. But if I presume to treat
luxury, avarice, pride, simo—”

“—ny, and the other vices of the clergy more deeply, I shall everywhere be
proclaimed a visionary and presumptuous man, accused of falsehood, and shall
pour out a discourse where there is no hearing, contrary to the wise man's
teaching. But woe to me, on Isaiah's testimony, if I keep silent. For this
people—the priestly and clerical order, that is—provokes wrath, and they are
lying children unwilling to hear God's word, who say to the seers, ‘Do not
see,’ and to those who perceive, ‘Do not perceive for us the things which are
right. Speak pleasing things to us; see errors for us; let the Holy One of
Israel cease from before your face.’ Therefore, because you have rejected my
word and have put your hope in tumult, this iniquity shall be to you like a
breach falling from a high wall, for its destruction shall come suddenly when
it is not expected, so that it is shattered as a potter's vessel is shattered
with a mighty breaking.”

I have not been afraid to copy out these words, however lengthy, because,
brothers, they not only set your pastoral office before your eyes even though
there is no hope of making progress, but also because arguments press upon you
which you cannot answer so long as you remain silent. The same evils recounted
here now hang over England because of the corrupted discipline of the clerical
order. Those who share in the sin will also share in the punishment; and,
specifically concerning England in that passage about Laodicea, Christ
threatens to vomit its Angels out of his mouth unless they grow fervent and
repent, Revelation 3:16.

What compassion is there in your hearts, brothers, if you are not moved at the
drawn sword itself like that natural mother? What faith is there, if you do not
sound the trumpet while the enemy stands at the gates? Finally, what concern
have you to deliver your own souls, if you do not warn your country of the ruin
already at hand? And this, drawn from both the holiness and the necessity of
reformed discipline, is the fourth corollary addressed to the silent ministers
in England.
