Let it be granted that the bishop was not only the overseer but also the
pastor of the distinct parishes in the city, and that he both was and is so by
virtue of his office. It must now be considered whether he was also such in
the contiguous and adjoining region. The word διοίκησις, when referred to a
bishop, is unknown to Eusebius and the earlier centuries. It is taken in that
sense in the African Council. In the Councils of Chalcedon and Constantinople
it signifies the dominion or circuit of jurisdiction of a greater archbishop
or patriarch, of which we spoke in chapter 2. Eusebius uses the word
παροικίας, and in various ways. In Eusebius παροικία signifies the church
which inhabited a city and the city’s suburbs, when that word was referred to
a bishop—or also to a presbyter, where the church had not been divided into
partial churches—but not those churches which were in the region. For he very
often uses the same expression as Scripture, as when he says “bishop of the
parish in this or that city.” How, I ask, can churches ἐν ταῖς χώραις be said
to be ἐν τῇ πόλει? Downham says that Eusebius sometimes also uses the
preposition κατὰ, as when he says of Dionysius, τῆς προστασίας, τῶν κατ’
Ἀλεξάνδρειαν ἐκκλησιῶν τὴν ἐπισκοπὴν ὑπολαμβάνειν, History, book 7, chapter
35. But since Downham himself interprets κατὰ as “in” or “around Alexandria,”
and says that Eusebius sometimes uses κατὰ in the same sense as ἐν, Eusebius
means nothing other than that Dionysius undertook the episcopate—the
προστασίας—of the churches which were in the city of Alexandria and the
suburbs around the city. And his saying *churches* rather than *church* arose
from the fact that the Alexandrian church had been divided into distinct
parishes and churches, as we learned above from Sozomen, although that
division must not be referred to an institution of Mark or to the time of the
apostles. Downham says that Eusebius means the same thing when he calls a
bishop παροικίας or παροικίαν, ἐκκλησίας or ἐκκλησίαν. I take up the premise.
But Eusebius says that Demetrius was bishop τῆς αὐτόθι παροικίας, book 6,
chapter 8. Therefore, when in book 6, chapter 1, he says that the same
Demetrius undertook τῶν δὲ αὐτόθι παροικιῶν τὴν ἐπισκοπὴν, he means nothing
other than the distinct parishes which were in the great parish of the
Alexandrian church, not parishes throughout the countryside and the adjoining
region, as though Demetrius were a diocesan bishop, nor episcopal parishes
throughout all Egypt, as though he were a metropolitan. There αὐτόθι signifies
“in” or “within,” not “outside” or “around.” Thus the phrases ἡ παροικία and
αὐτόθι παροικίας indicate that

[Marginalia: *Def. lib. 2.* cap. 7. p. 125. pag. 105.; Lib. 2. pa. 11.]

that parish was contained within the city and its suburbs. Thus the apostle
uses κατὰ at Romans 16:5: “Greet Priscilla and Aquila, καὶ τὴν κατ’ οἶκον
αὐτῶν ἐκκλησίαν,” the church which is in their house. Eusebius also uses
another expression which conveys the same thing, as when he calls Demetrius,
in book 6, chapter 26, τῆς Ἀλεξανδρέων ἐκκλησίας ἐπίσκοπον, bishop of the
church of the Alexandrians. It would be inept, I think, if someone were to
call the diocesan church of the bishop of London the church of the Londoners.
For the rural parishes in the diocese belong not to the citizens of London
but to the bishop of London. He uses the words “parish” and “church” in the
singular when he writes about the Alexandrian bishops even after Demetrius and
Dionysius. The word παροικία itself also properly signifies the neighborhood
of those who inhabit adjoining houses or houses situated not far from one
another. This is said ineptly of a single city together with its diocesan
region. At length this word “parish,” and in corrupt form “parochia,” was
applied by an improper manner of speaking to an entire diocese, but after the
age of Eusebius.

But suppose that before the age of Eusebius the bishops of cities had spread
their wings beyond the city and its suburbs, and their churches in the
countryside had been designated by another expression, such as ἐν ταῖς χώραις
or κατὰ χώραν. I answer first that it was not so from the beginning. For it is
known to everyone that ἐν ταῖς χώραις there were others besides the bishops
of cities, whom they called chorepiscopi. But they reply that the chorepiscopi
were only suffragans of the bishops and supplied their place, and that they
were called chorepiscopi for this reason. Yet the customary opposition between
πόλιν κὲ χώραν shows that they were called chorepiscopi for another reason,
namely, because they were bishops ἐν ταῖς χώραις, as Downham admits. When it
was decreed in canon 10 of the Council of Antioch that chorepiscopi should in
future be ordained by one bishop, like presbyters, and not as before by the
metropolitan and three bishops, like the bishops of cities—as Balsamon
interprets it, and Downham himself—it is evident that the chorepiscopi had not
been subject to the bishops of cities but were equal in power. For they had
received episcopal imposition of hands from the metropolitan, just as the
city bishop himself had, and by their own right had ordained presbyters and
deacons, which this synod also forbade for the future unless the bishop
permitted it. Hence Bellarmine concludes that those chorepiscopi were true
bishops, since they also could ordain presbyters and deacons only with the
permission of the city bishop, because they were consecrated according to the
rite of the pontiffs. He says that there were other chorepiscopi who had
received episcopal consecration but were called chorepiscopi because they had
no church of their own and ministered in another’s diocese,

[Marginalia: *Def. li. 2.* pa. 27.; Li. 2. pa. 29.; *De Clericis* cap. 17.]

[1623 printed p. 292 | IA leaf 304 | PDF p. 304]

as certain titular bishops do at the present day, who are also commonly
called suffragans. He says, moreover, that there were other chorepiscopi who
were nothing but presbyters, yet in villages and hamlets or even small towns
represented the bishop in some fashion, and that Damasus affirms in letter 3
that they were not permitted to ordain even subdeacons. It is indeed true
that the Council of Antioch established a law that henceforth a chorepiscopus
should be ordained by one bishop and consequently be a presbyter, not a
bishop; and it established this concerning absolutely all chorepiscopi. For
that canon intends that no chorepiscopi at all should be ordained as they had
previously been ordained by the metropolitan and other bishops, because that
ordination was a sign of the equality between city bishops and chorepiscopi
which they wished to abolish. For, being already then filled with a spirit of
pride, they could not endure rival chorepiscopi or allow their dignity to be
cheapened, as they thought would happen if the dignity of a bishop were
communicated even to rural men. All the chorepiscopi had their own churches
and jurisdictions, just as the city bishop did; they were not titulars or
suffragans. For why were they ordained like the city bishop by a metropolitan
and not by the bishop whose suffragans they are said to have been? Why did
they subscribe the Council of Nicaea like the bishops of cities? Why did they
ordain presbyters and deacons until it was forbidden by canon? Whether the
Damasus is genuine or spurious matters little: in letter 4 he maintains that
they were merely presbyters and could not ordain even subdeacons. But Downham
himself doubts the trustworthiness of that decretal letter. Nevertheless, it
can be gathered from that letter that the chorepiscopi performed the duties
of episcopal authority, although this Damasus therefore charges them with
arrogance and ambition. Why were their ordinations not invalid if they did
not possess the power of ordaining? The Council of Ancyra only forbade them
to do it in another parish, as we showed above. The chorepiscopi had already
begun to be depressed. For in the Council of Neocaesarea, which many of those
who had subscribed at Ancyra attended, they are said to be like the seventy
disciples. But the canon of Antioch mentioned above shows that the canon of
that provincial council—if it is genuine—had no effect on the chorepiscopi;
they nevertheless discharged the same office as the bishops of cities until
they were restrained by canons. Indeed, letters 4 of Damasus and 86 of Leo
establish that serious disputes arose between the bishops of cities and the
chorepiscopi. If from their first institution they had merely been the
suffragans of the bishops, and not bishops of the same right and power, they
would not have sustained disputes with the urban bishops for so long. But at
length they were forced to yield in rank and dignity, and even in the name
“chorepiscopus.” Downham says that their jurisdictions were like rural
deaneries in England. And Mocket says, *The rural deans are not unlike the
chorepiscopi and regional archpresbyters of the ancient church.* We shall show
in its place that they were altogether unlike them in power. As to the
circuit

of jurisdiction, Mocket says the same thing as Downham.

How narrow the boundaries of episcopal dioceses were is evident from the fact
that they held provincial councils of bishops every year, something the
English now complain cannot be done because of the breadth of dioceses and
provinces. It is also evident from the fact that, for examining the case of an
accused presbyter, six bishops from neighboring places were joined to the
bishop, and for the case of a deacon, three—African Council, canon 20. They
lived so near one another that they could come together quite conveniently.
Cyprian relates that *Privatus, an old heretic in the colony of Lambesa, had
almost many years before been condemned for many serious offenses by the
sentence of ninety bishops.* You see ninety bishops in a single synod at
Lambesa almost many years before Cyprian wrote that letter. *Eighty-four
bishops attended the synod held at Carthage against Novatus concerning the
granting of peace to penitent lapsi,* as the author of the *Synodicon* reports
in chapter 22. Around the year 308 a council of 270 Donatist bishops was held
at Carthage, which Augustine mentions in letter 48. The sixth Council of
Carthage was held by 217 bishops. If so many attended the councils, how many
were there throughout all Africa? Augustine, against Gaudentius, records that
there were innumerable orthodox bishops in Africa. Victor of Vita reports
that 660 bishops saved themselves by flight in the Vandal persecution. In
certain churches there were only two presbyters or one, and sometimes none,
as Bilson himself proves from Ambrose on 1 Timothy, chapter 3, the third
Council of Carthage, canon 45, and the African Council, canon 56. Where there
were so few presbyters, the diocese was not broad. When a bishop was elected,
he was elected in the presence of the people and by the whole brotherhood, as
Cyprian reports concerning Sabinus: *We see that this was also done among you
in the ordination of our colleague Sabinus: by the vote of the whole
brotherhood and by the judgment of the bishops who had assembled in person
and who had written letters about him to you, the episcopate was conferred on
him and hands were laid on him in the place of Basilides.* In the same place
he says that it descends from divine tradition and apostolic observance that
an election be made in the presence of the people. The people were not so
numerous that they could not come together as one to elect the bishop. These
and similar things show that the jurisdictions of bishops were not extended
as widely as they now are, and that bishops were appointed not only in cities
but also in towns, villages, and hamlets throughout the countryside and the
region. Writers also expressly say that bishops exercised the episcopate in
towns and villages. *Gregory, moreover, was made bishop of Nazianzus, a very
small city of Cappadocia—in which his father had governed the church before
him,* πολλῆς εὐτελείας, as Socrates says. A certain Alexander, who pretended
to be a charcoal burner, was appointed bishop over the village of Comana by
Gregory Thaumaturgus. See Baronius at the year 233, number 10. That it was a
village is evident from Eusebius, book 5, chapter 16, where he says Ζωτικὸν
ἀπὸ Κομάνης κώμης, that Zoticus was from the village of Comana.

Theodoret reports that a certain man named Maris was appointed by Eusebius
over Doliche, which is there called a πολίχνη, a small town. Gennadius, in *On
Illustrious Men*, says that Asclepius was bishop of a village that was not
large. Spiridion was bishop of the town of Trimithus, as Sozomen reports, and
they relate that even after he was placed in the episcopate he continued to be
a shepherd. Cartwright observes that the names of four or five towns whose
bishops are recorded as having attended the Council of Carthage held in
Cyprian’s time do not appear on geographical tables. In his letter to
Evagrius, Jerome compares certain chilly little towns with Rome and
Alexandria, yet they too had bishops. The town of Three Taverns was near
Velletri, yet it had its own bishop, although Pope Gregory later subjected
the remnants of that devastated church to the bishop of Velletri. He likewise
joined and united several other churches to their neighbors. The fortress of
Cumae had its own bishop. Gregory writes to Bishop Benenatus: *Both the
condition of the times and the nearness of the places invite us to unite the
churches of Cumae and Misenum, since they are not separated from one another
by a long journey and—through the effects of sin—the people are not so
numerous that they ought to have individual priests as in former times.
Therefore, because the priest of the fortress of Cumae has completed the
course of this life, know that by the instrument of the present authority we
have united both churches and committed them to you; and know that you are
the proper pontiff of both churches.* Therefore, before hostile impiety had
laid waste those churches of Cumae and Misenum, as Gregory says in letter 35,
they had their own pontiffs, although they were not separated from one another
by a long journey. *At Laon, Remigius appointed a bishop at Clavatum, which
was a fortress of his diocese. Hincmar adds, when he relates this, that Blessed
Remigius did it supported by the authority of the African canons and the
apostles,* says Filesacus in his commentary on the authority of bishops. *Yet
among other nations you may find bishops ordained even in villages, as I have
learned occurs among the Arabs and Cypriots, and among the Novatians and
Montanists who dwell in Phrygia,* says Sozomen. Grant that bishops were not
appointed everywhere in villages, hamlets, and towns throughout the
countryside, but that in many places the bishops of cities governed those
rural parishes. It nevertheless does not follow that the practice of the
church was the interpreter of the apostles’ intention, as Downham maintains.
For Downham maintains that the apostles intended the bishops of cities to
govern the adjoining region, and he proves their intention by the practice of
the churches after the apostles’ time. But practice after the apostles’ time
was varied, as we have now shown, even if we grant that diocesan churches were
immediately established in certain provinces. Moreover, we deny that this was
done immediately after the apostles’ time. It was done later, and the
boundaries of their churches were enlarged either by the negligence or the
ambition of bishops, who appointed presbyters in villages and towns and made
them subject to themselves and to their presbyteries

[Marginalia: Lib. 5. cap. 4.; Lib. 1. c. 10.; *Repl. 1. part.* pag. 535.; Lib. 2. ep. 35.; Lib. 2. ep. 31.; Epist. 35.; Pag. 300.; *Hist.* lib. 7. cap. 19.]

in the city, but took no care to erect presbyteries or appoint bishops. For
since they, their presbyters, and the Christians of the city church were the first to win
those who were also in the countryside, before others came from elsewhere to
undertake that work, they did not take care that presbyteries and episcopates
should be established throughout the countryside as the number of the
faithful increased. Instead, as people were gradually converted, they
gradually brought them under their own jurisdiction. When the bishops of
other cities perceived this, in order that they might equal the bishops of
other cities in dignity, they gradually began to depress the chorepiscopi and
the bishops of towns and villages until at last they were wholly subdued and
the very name was erased. Hence the following canons were enacted: *Bishops
ought not to be ordained in villages, but visitors—that is, those who travel
around—ought to be appointed; and we judge that those who were ordained
previously are to do nothing without the knowledge of the city bishop.*
Balsamon says on this canon: *Other canons determined that ἐν ταῖς κώμαις καὶ
ἐν τοῖς χωρίοις χωρεπισκόπους—chorepiscopi and presbyters—were to be appointed
in villages and rural districts. But the present canon commands that
περιοδευτὰς be made, and not bishops, lest episcopal dignity become cheap and
contemptible, since the village in which a bishop has been ordained does not
have a multitude of people for the honor of God and of the episcopate. But
those bishops who,* he says, *were appointed in these places before the canon
are not to be expelled; rather, they are to exercise all their functions with
the knowledge of the metropolitan who ordained them.* From this canon and
Balsamon’s interpretation it is evident that in the East bishops were
appointed in villages and rural districts, not in cities alone, and that
those who had already been appointed before this canon discharged all their
functions without the knowledge of the city bishop. But who are these
*periodeutae*—visitors, or, as others say, roving inspectors or circuit riders?
*Periodeutae,* says Balsamon, *are those who are promoted today by bishops as
exarchs. For they travel about, observe the offenses of the soul, and perfect
the faithful.* *They are called periodeutae,* says Zonaras on the same canon,
*because, having no proper see or cathedral anywhere, they wander here and
there to keep the faithful in their duty.* Filesacus says: *If we wished to
follow the canon law of the Latins in this matter, we would say that the
periodeutae signify none other than archpresbyters or rural deans.* Justellus
says on this canon: *Therefore περιοδευταὶ in the church are circuit riders
or visitors who, like vicars of the bishops, visit and travel around the
regions subject to the cities and have no proper see, as Zonaras says.* This
is what Downham falsely said above about the chorepiscopi, namely, that they
had no proper church. For the chorepiscopi are distinguished from the
periodeutae here in Balsamon and in Justinian, *Code*, book 4, title 1, *On
Bishops and Clerics*, where he places περιοδευτήν after chorepiscopus.
*Gennadius, patriarch of Constantinople,

[Marginalia: Conc. Laod. cap. 57.; vide Gratian. dist. 80.; *De Origin. Paroeciarum.*; pag. 570.]

in an encyclical letter to St. Leo the Great, Roman pontiff, enumerating the
ecclesiastical dignities and offices, lists περιοδευτήν after chorepiscopus,
that is, a circuit rider or, you might say, a visitor. And at the end he asks
the pontiff to transmit copies of this letter to the bishops of the West and
to the περιοδευταῖς, namely, those whose duty is to make circuits of the
churches and visit them,* as Filesacus shows. In some manuscripts ἢ
περιοδευταῖς is read; in others, ἀλλὰ. Photius interprets the Laodicean canon
in the *Nomocanon* in this way: *Let there be no bishop at all ἐν πόλει
βραχείᾳ ἢ κώμῃ, in a small city or village.* From the equity of this law—I
would have said its iniquity—Balsamon gathers by equal reasoning that *it is
much more contrary to God’s honor if a prelate goes on foot because of his
poverty and is deprived of necessities. Therefore it is not safe to elect
bishops in the Eastern churches, in which not very many Christians are
found.* It tends to the disgrace of the episcopate, you see, if a bishop
presides over a small people or walks on foot. This provincial Council of
Laodicea was held around the year 364. Anacletus says, *Bishops ought not to
be appointed in fortresses or modest cities,* and below, *he is not to be
titled to and named from a modest city, lest the bishop’s name become cheap,
but from an honorable city.* It is known even to the bleary-eyed and barbers
that the decretal letter attributed to Anacletus is spurious. In the Council
of Sardica it was decreed *that a bishop may not simply be appointed in some
village or small city for which even one presbyter suffices. For it is not
necessary to appoint a bishop there, lest the bishop’s name and authority be
held in contempt. But the bishops of the province ought to appoint bishops in
those cities where bishops also existed previously. If, however, some city is
found so populous that it is judged worthy of its own episcopate, let it
receive one.* Balsamon says on this canon: *Because, when cheap men had been
elected bishops in small and cheap parishes—where there were often scarcely
any bishops, and those who existed went on foot and were otherwise
contemptible—episcopal dignity was held in contempt, the fathers established
that a bishop should not be appointed in a village, rural district, or little
town where even one priest suffices, lest episcopal dignity be held in
contempt. Rather, bishops should be appointed in those cities which abound in
men and which had bishops long ago. But as though someone had said that it
was unjust for a populous city to have no prelate because no bishop had been
appointed there long ago, the fathers added that a new bishop can also be
appointed in a city of this sort.* Sutcliffe himself considers certain canons
of this council suspect. Yet it is well that they concede that a numerous
people requires a bishop. Therefore, wherever there is a numerous people,
whether in a city or in the neighboring rural districts and villages, a
presbytery ought to be erected. For those fathers did not wish a bishop to be
appointed anywhere without a presbytery. In letter 1 Gregory Junior writes to
Bishop Boniface

in this manner: *We command that, according to the statutes of the sacred
canons, wherever the multitude of the faithful has increased, you ought by
the authority of the apostolic see to ordain bishops—yet with the pious
consideration that the dignity of the episcopate not be cheapened.* Pope
Zacharias writes to Bishop Boniface: *Let us remember what we are commanded to
observe in the sacred canons, that we by no means ordain bishops in little
villages or modest cities, lest the bishop’s name become cheap.* Nevertheless,
he permitted Boniface to establish an episcopal cathedral in the fortress
called Marseburg. Pope Leo writes to the bishops of Africa, letter 85, chapter
2: *As to that which properly concerns priestly dignity, among all things we
wish the statutes of the canons to be observed, so that bishops are not
consecrated in any and every place, nor in any and every fortress, and where
there were none before.* Below: *lest the priestly summit be granted to
villages and estates or obscure and solitary municipalities, which the
divinely inspired decrees of the holy fathers forbade.* These decrees of the
fathers in the Councils of Laodicea and Sardica were inspired not by God but
by a spirit of ambition. For neither those fathers nor the Roman pontiffs who
promoted the execution of the decree by their authority give any reason for
the decree except that the bishop’s name and authority should not become
cheap. They had regard not to the church’s edification and convenient
government but to the bishop’s secular splendor and magnificence. A bishop is
of the same worth and priesthood wherever he may be. The power of riches and
the humility of poverty do not make a bishop either higher or lower, as
Jerome says. The sun shines in rural districts and country towns just as it
does in cities, and it is no dimmer on that account. By this reasoning they
could have denied presbyters to rural districts and villages—the men whom the
apostle says are worthy of double honor—lest a crowd or an ignoble place make
them contemptible. The man honors the place, not the place the man. But by
these means Satan advanced the mystery of iniquity, and because he wished the
lesser bishops to be princes, he took care by every means that the head of one
be exalted by despoiling others, as that most distinguished man Thomas
Cartwright rightly observed. Villages and rural districts did not supply
enough to support the pomp which they affected. At length the twelfth Council
of Toledo, canon 4, as Downham cites it from Burchard, pronounced an anathema
on anyone who arranged for a bishop to be appointed where there had not been
a bishop before. Eventually the Africans themselves were seized by the same
desire to amplify their own dignity and extend their diocese. *For they too
decreed that dioceses which have never had a bishop are not to have one, and
that the diocese which once had one is to have its own. And if, with the
passage of time and the growth of the faith, the multiplied people of God
desire to have their own ruler, let them have a bishop, of course with the
consent of the man under whose power it was established.* Do you see how the
mules scratch one another? The same decree is found as canon 20 of the
African Council, as it survives in Gratian. So far were those in whose power
it lay from permitting the erection of new episcopates that they even tried
to invade the people

[Marginalia: Tom. 2. Conc. epist. ult.; 2. Repl. part. 1. pag. 515.; Li. 2. p. 26.; Can. 2. ca. 5.; Caus. 16. qu. 1. cap. *Si multis.*]

of neighboring dioceses. Hence the disputes between bishops over enlarged
dioceses, as is evident from canons 84, 85, and 86 of the African Council.
Nevertheless, all these things demonstrate that the ancient custom of the
church was to appoint bishops and presbyteries throughout the countryside and
the region in towns, villages, rural districts, fortresses, and
municipalities, just as in larger cities; and that, after they had struggled
for a long time, entire regions at last yielded to these monopolists as the
ambition, power, and authority of the polybishops prevailed. Thus one who had
been bishop of one parish in a city eventually became bishop of the whole
region as well; and the name “parish,” which had been distinguished from ταῖς
χώραις, was applied both to the city parish and to ταῖς ὑπ’ αὐτὴν χώραις,
embracing them in a single conception. *How great do we think the labor is,*
says Erasmus, *when, besides the villages and rural districts, twenty large
and populous cities obey one prelate?* Again, *The dominion of many prelates
extends so widely that, even if they were as free as possible from all worldly
business, they could not preach in all the towns, since today one city
requires several ecclesiastical ministers.* Musculus says, *Let the bishops
consider this: although they do not lawfully minister to even one church, they
extend the power of their episcopate not to a few churches but even to entire
provinces.* Bernard says, *Since they preside over very populous cities and
enclose, so to speak, whole countries within the compass of their own diocese,
when occasion is found from any old privilege they labor to subject neighboring
cities to themselves as well, so that two cities, for which two prelates
scarcely sufficed, may be reduced under one prelate.* So great is the bishops’
shamelessness that with a most capacious maw they devour many cities. *And
certainly, if that ancient love for the people still flourished, they would
acknowledge that there is more business in one city than can easily be
discharged by the best men. They know well enough that the primitive church
had no bishops of this kind before the time of Sylvester I,* said Bishop
Hooper. In the same place he said that bishops and presbyters scratch one
another. Bishops permit presbyters to hold several benefices; otherwise the
presbyters could have retorted to the bishops that a bishop ought to be the
bishop of one city. *And it ought to be so: and once the magistrates have
brought them back into this order, it will be as impossible for a bishop to
pass through Scripture piously and soberly in every matter of religion as to
drive a camel through the eye of a needle.* Epiphanius, in heresy 68, *writing
