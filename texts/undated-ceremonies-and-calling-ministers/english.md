# On Ceremonies and the Calling of Ministers

*A fresh translation of the complete French text at CO X/1 208–210. CO's
French is the fuller source-language witness; its freely made historical Latin
version translates only part of the work and lacks the French conclusion. The
English below was translated directly from the French.*

[CO X/1, p. 208]

On the matter of ceremonies, and especially the observance of feast days:

Although those who pursue some conformity or other without regard to the
edification of the church will have to give an account to God for having
nourished the evil and obstructed the remedy, and although it is a hard and
grievous thing for all

[CO X/1, p. 209]

good and faithful servants of God to subject themselves to what they see to be
neither good nor useful, nevertheless, because we have to bear many
imperfections when we cannot correct them, I do not advise any brother to press
the matter to the point of leaving his church if the greater part pulls in the
opposite direction. For in such a matter it seems to me enough to have sought,
so far as it lies in us, what we know to be best.

For although what they wish to have accepted entails either scandal or a bad
consequence, it may nevertheless be conceded because it is not in itself
contrary to the word of God—especially when one sees that the plurality of
votes prevails, since one who is only a member of the body has no further means
of persisting.

On the second matter, whether those who have been improperly appointed ought
to be regarded and acknowledged as brothers:

I do not see that it is lawful to approve their calling as legitimate.
Therefore, it seems to me, there are only two ways to answer this: either they
are left in their position and office by toleration, or they acknowledge their
faults and are then received. The first answer has an honorable excuse: today,
no more than in the past, it is unnecessary to ascertain whether those who had
no freedom to elect or receive them approve what another person did.

It may therefore be required that if the prince did it by command and
authority, he not compel those who desire an equal order to be observed in the
church to praise and approve an act in which they were not heard, but were
called only as witnesses. The second way would be better, more edifying, and
more consistent with the church's order: that those who were introduced in
this manner peacefully declare that they regret having intruded themselves
without a better foundation for their calling, and that it be expressly
declared that it is neither good, useful, nor reasonable to proceed hereafter
in so confused a manner.

But I wonder why, in such interrogations, no mention at all is made of
doctrine.

[CO X/1, p. 210]

For it is a strange thing that everyone is asked to declare the faults that
need correction while the principal one is omitted. It seems exactly as
though they are there to say not a word except to the extent that they are
compelled. For my part, I solemnly state that henceforth I would have a
conscientious objection to giving advice to those who scruple to preach on
feast days, yet, when they are asked what they find objectionable in the
church, remain silent about the blasphemies pronounced from the pulpit against
God's election and providence, and allow the pure doctrine of the sacraments
to be condemned in the examination of preachers.

This 25th of December 1557.

John Calvin.

## Translator's notes

1. The French deliberately moves between collective and individual voices:
   “we have to bear” and “as far as it lies in us” occur beside “I do not
   advise,” “it seems to me,” and the signed first-person protest. The English
   preserves those shifts.
2. CO prints the anomalous forms *Diou* and *si. confuse*. The French
   transcription does not silently correct them to *Dieu* and *si confuse*;
   the English renders their evident contextual sense.
3. Neither printed-page turn divides a word. The markers therefore follow the
   exact French page changes, even where p. 208 ends and p. 209 begins in the
   middle of an English sentence.
4. CO's two p. 209 footnotes quote and describe the older partial Latin
   translation. They are edition history, not part of the French response, and
   are discussed in the README rather than translated into the body.
5. The English was translated afresh from [`french.txt`](french.txt) and
   checked against the facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not
   consulted or used for wording.
