QUESTION X.

THE BEGINNING OF THE CHRISTIAN ERA.

*What were the year, month, and day of Christ’s birth?*

[Marginal heading: The dignity of the Christian era.]

I. Although there are various epochs, both sacred and secular, by which times
are customarily marked, none approaches the dignity of the Christian era. The
*nativity of the Savior* is easily the chief of all epochs. All preceding
times look toward it as their auspicious and blessed center, while all
subsequent times flow from it as from a fountain to the end of the age. “The
generation of Christ,” says Leo, “is the origin of the Christian people, and
the birthday of the Head is the birthday of the body; and while we adore the
Savior’s birth, we find that we are celebrating our own beginning.”

[Marginal heading: When it began.]

II. Although this era has such excellence and usefulness, it is nevertheless
certain—and no less astonishing—that Christians thought rather late about
establishing and using it. For before the seventh century, no one is recorded
as having applied the nativity of the Lord to the dating of any civil or
ecclesiastical business. Certainly Justinian, who reigned five hundred years
after Christ, in Novella 47, his edict on the notation of times and the dating
of public acts, wished a fourth designation, taken from the emperors, to be
added to the older designations of times taken from the founding of the city,
the names of the consuls, and the indictions. Yet he says nothing of the
Christian era. Nor is there any mention of it among the Fathers and councils
held in those earlier centuries. Dionysius Exiguus, a Scythian by nation and a
monk or abbot at Rome, was the first. Displeased that in his own and earlier
centuries the Paschal cycles, which indicated the dates of Easter and the
feasts, marked the years above all from the persecution of Diocletian, he
erased this designation of a heathen man and

[Printed p. 295 | Internet Archive PDF 323 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

most savage persecutor and placed Christ’s nativity in his private reckoning
around the time of Justinian, AD 532. The Venerable Bede followed this
invention in the seven-hundredth year after Christ, as did not a few others,
but privately. For the era of Christ did not begin to be employed in any
public acts, whether sacred or civil, before the time of Charlemagne, after AD
800.

III. Even though Christians have since agreed in using the era introduced
from Christ’s birth, it cannot be said how greatly both chronologists and
theologians disagree among themselves in fixing the certain and true beginning
of that era, some referring it to one year and others to another. This raises
a twofold question, which must be examined here distinctly: first, *the true
date of the year of his birth;* second, *its month and day.*

[Marginal heading: Various opinions concerning the year of the nativity.]

IV. As to the first, it would be lengthy to recount here all the opinions of
the chronologists, and far more laborious, if not altogether impossible, to
reconcile them with one another. We note only the principal ones in passing.
Scaliger, Calvisius, and Helvicus refer the year of Christ’s birth to the year
of the world 3947, the Julian Period 4711, the city’s founding 750,
Nabonassar 745, Augustus 41 from the murder of Julius, the calendar reformed
by Julius 43, Herod’s reign 33 and his life 70, in the consulship of Lucius
Cornelius Lentulus and Marcus Valerius Messalla. Lansbergius agrees on the
years of the city, the Julian Period, and Nabonassar, but adds three to the
years of the world by referring it to 3950. Petavius holds it to be 3979.
Salianus, extending still farther, carries it to the year of the world 4052.
Torniellus subtracts one. Not to mention others who depart more or less from
these, the common era of Dionysius places it around the year of the world
3949.

[Marginal heading: Three scriptural marks of the year of the nativity.]

V. Since these matters are chronological rather than theological, we believe
we ought not to labor anxiously over them. Nor do we think it contributes
much to the ἀσφάλειαν, *assurance,* of our faith that we know precisely in
which year Christ was born, provided we hold that the marks of time noted by
the prophets truly concur in his nativity. Therefore, setting aside the
various and highly intricate questions raised here, for now it is enough to
observe that the safest course is to rest in what Scripture wished to give us
on this subject to confirm our faith. It presents common rather than singular
marks of Christ’s nativity. They circumscribe the time of his birth to some
extent but nowhere designate the precise year. Three marks above all are
noted in the New Testament, from which a judgment may be made concerning
Christ’s nativity. The first lies in the *notation of Herod’s reign,* in whose
days he is said to have been born: “When Jesus was born at Bethlehem, in the
time of King Herod” (Matt. 2:1). The second lies in the *designation of the
registration* of the whole world, instituted by Cyrenius, governor of Syria,
when Augustus was emperor (Luke 2:1–2). The third lies in the *concurrence of
Tiberius’s fifteenth year and Christ’s thirtieth,* when he was baptized (Luke
3:1 together with v. 23). Christ is said to have been ὡς ἐτῶν τριάκοντα,
*about thirty years old,* after he was baptized by John, to whom the word of
God is said to have come in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar. But all
these marks are general and indefinite. They do teach that Christ was born
*in the days of Herod* and *at the time of the first registration* made by
Cyrenius, but they do not designate the precise year either of Herod’s reign
or of that registration. Only two things are certain and indubitable here.
First, Christ was not born either before or after the empire of Augustus and
the reign of Herod, but during them. Second, his nativity fell in Herod’s last
years, since after Herod’s death Christ returned from Egypt and was still
described as a παιδίον, *little child* (Matt. 2:20), that is, as being of
tender age. Then Herod’s death is immediately appended as an ἐπίλογον,
*epilogue,* after the plots laid against Christ and the most cruel slaughter
of the infants carried out

[Printed p. 296 | Internet Archive PDF 324 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

at Bethlehem.

VI. If it is asked why the Holy Spirit wished to stop at these more general
marks and not to report something more specific about the year of the
nativity, which would have been easy for him, the answer is easy. This was
undoubtedly done, first, so that it might be evident that the hinge of neither
faith nor salvation turns on a scrupulous calculation of times. It is enough
for us that Christ was truly born at the time designated by the prophets,
even though the particular notation of that year is not known. It is not for
us to define with danger what can be unknown without danger. Second, he also
wished in this way to set the harmony of the two Testaments more clearly
before our eyes. For just as in the Old Testament Christ’s advent is
designated by the setting of the Jewish scepter and of Jewish αὐτονομίας,
*self-government,* without a more specific and precise designation, so his
nativity is said to have occurred under Herod’s reign and Augustus’s empire.
This expressly indicates that the scepter had been taken away from Judah and
that the fulfillment of the times by which the advent of the Messiah had been
circumscribed had appeared.

VII. Although the year of the nativity itself is not precisely designated, it
must not therefore be thought that the faith of the gospel history is
endangered, because an exact determination of the time may be unknown without
loss of truth when the question *whether it occurred* is acknowledged by all,
as it is here. For although the doctors disagree about the notation of the
time, they all confess, even the Jews themselves, that Christ was born at a
certain time. Scripture’s general designation can therefore suffice here,
and there is no need to labor greatly over anything further. It is better
here to ἐπέχειν, *suspend judgment,* and speak soberly than to affirm anything
rashly.

[Marginal heading: The question concerning the month and day of the
nativity.]

VIII. The other question concerns the *month and day* of the nativity, about
which there are three principal opinions. The first places it on December 25.
This is the common opinion of the Roman Catholics; it has also been received
among us and is followed by the Christian churches. The second places it at
the end of September. This is the opinion of Scaliger, Calvisius, Beroaldus,
and others. But the arguments for neither are demonstrative, only probable,
and rest largely upon conjectures.

[Marginal heading: The first opinion, which places it on December 25.]

IX. The principal foundation of the first opinion is taken from Chrysostom on
Luke 1: the conceptions of both John and Christ occurred at the two
equinoxes, while their nativities occurred at the two solstices. John was
conceived at the autumnal equinox and born at the summer solstice, namely in
June. Christ, however, was conceived six months later at the vernal equinox
and born at the winter solstice, which according to the calendars of Eudoxus
and Meton was thought to fall on December 25. He infers that John was indeed
conceived in September at the autumnal equinox from the fact that the angel
Gabriel announced John’s conception to Zacharias on the tenth day of the
seventh month, called Tisri, which corresponds to our September. This was the
Day of Atonement, on which Zacharias—who is assumed to have been the high
priest—entered the Holy of Holies according to the law and burned incense
before the ark (Luke 1:9). When that sacred rite was completed, Zacharias went
home and Elizabeth conceived at the beginning of October. Then, after six
months, Christ was conceived at the beginning of April (Luke 1:26) and born
in the ninth month near the end of December.

X. But it is not difficult to demonstrate that this foundation is weak. It
rests upon the false hypothesis that Zacharias was the high priest who was
obliged to burn incense on the Day of Atonement, an assumption refuted in more
than one way from the text itself. For if Zacharias was the high priest, why
does Luke say nothing about it, not calling him ἀρχιερέα, *a high priest,* but
only ἱερέα τινα, *a certain priest,* namely one from the body of priests? How
is he said to have performed the priesthood in the order of his ἐφημερίας,
*rotation,* which belonged to the ordinary priests, who by the right

[Printed p. 297 | Internet Archive PDF 325 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of their rotation served at the altar and went home after their week of
ministry, but not to the high priest, who was attached to no order and could
perform any sacred rites at any time? Why is he said to have been allotted
this λειτουργίαν, *service,* of making incense, when that solemn ceremony
belonged by office to the high priest alone without a lot?

XI. Nor is the contrary established from the statement that he entered the
temple to make incense. For burning incense outside the veil and sanctuary
belonged to any priests chosen for it by lot, just as the altar of incense
was placed outside the veil and sacred chamber (Ex. 30). Nor is it established
from the statement that the people waited outside the temple (v. 21), because
it was customary for the people to wait in the court for the priest’s return,
since they were not given access to the holy place, but only the priests were.
The people therefore waited for Zacharias according to custom while he
performed the sacred rites in keeping with his lot. Nor is it established
from the statement that the angel appeared to him at the right side of the
altar of incense. For this was not within the veil beside the ark, but outside
the veil at the side of the altar of incense, where the priest chosen by lot
burned incense daily, morning and evening.

[Marginal heading: The second opinion refers it to the autumnal equinox or
the end of September.]

XII. The second opinion is that of *Scaliger, Calvisius,* and others, who
believe that Christ was born at the autumnal equinox or around the end of
September. To prove it, Scaliger thinks that he can derive a demonstration
from the ἐφημερίᾳ, *rotation,* mentioned in Luke 1:5, namely from the
distribution of the priestly families, each of which in its own order had to
devote itself by turns to divine worship according to the order established
by David (1 Chron. 24). By this distribution they were divided into
twenty-four courses, and by the lot cast by David the first course fell to
the family of Joiarib, the second to the family of Abijah, to which Zacharias
belonged, and the rest to the other families noted in the passage cited. And
because in 224 years all the rotations returned to the same days, the
rotations of the individual families can be determined with complete
certainty by the aid of this sacred cycle. But besides the calamity of the
Babylonian captivity, the idolatry of various kings of Judah and the neglect
of divine worship, and so also of the priestly orders, in the temple brought
confusion upon this order. Therefore, when Judas Maccabaeus cleansed the
temple from the pollutions of Antiochus, he restored this order of rotations
in such a way that, as Josephus testifies, it was never interrupted down to
the final siege of Jerusalem by Vespasian. According to this calculation of
the priestly cycle, Zacharias, in the eighth course of Abijah, had to begin
his rotation on July 21, 160 years and 192 days after the Dedication by
Maccabaeus. It was completed on July 28, after which day Zacharias returned
home and Elizabeth could conceive John the Baptist at the beginning of
August. She hid herself for five months according to Luke; and as the sixth
began, namely January, after Elizabeth had been visited, Mary conceived
Christ. Nine months later, near the end of September or the
beginning of October, close to the autumnal equinox, she gave birth to him.

XIII. Yet this demonstration, although subtle and ingenious, does not appear
sufficiently sound. For it rests upon so many hypotheses that, if one fails,
the whole edifice falls, and so it cannot readily obtain assent. The chief
assumption here is that Judas Maccabaeus restored this order and that it was
never again disturbed or interrupted. But apart from the fact that nothing is
said about the restoration of this order in 1 Maccabees 4, by what argument
can we be persuaded that, as Josephus maintains, no interruption ever
occurred in it afterward, when so many events could have disrupted this
order? For example, this could occur if any family was defiled by legal
pollution, or if any of the twenty-four

[Printed p. 298 | Internet Archive PDF 326 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

families died out or was not present, and in other like cases. And how will
it be certainly established that the families always remained in the same
order when they are not always listed in the same rank? Add that it is also
assumed without any sure foundation that Elizabeth conceived immediately,
one or two days after Zacharias’s return, although Luke says simply that this
happened μετὰ ταύτας ἡμέρας, *after those days,* an expression which implies
some latitude of time.

XIV. Besides this demonstration by Scaliger, other arguments are advanced to
confirm this opinion, but they carry no greater weight. Thus it is said that
Christ had to be born in the season when shepherds could ἀγραυλεῖν, *live in
the fields,* and διανυκτερεύειν ἐν ὑπαίθρῳ, *spend the night in the open air;*
but winter is not such a season. Casaubon regards this argument as less than
firm (Exercitation 2 on Baronius’s Annals, year 1, no. 14), because a judgment
about Judea and other warmer and more southerly places ought not to be made
from Gaul or Germany, especially since the same thing also occurs in colder
and more northerly places, such as England, where, since there is no fear of
wolves and other wild animals, livestock customarily spend the night in the
fields all year. Why, then, could the shepherds in far warmer Judea not
ἀγραυλεῖν, *live in the fields,* keeping watch over their flocks?

XV. Although the time of the census instituted by Augustus may seem less
convenient if it was in winter, it does not follow that it must have been at
another time. For that time is to be sought from no source but the ruler’s
will, by whose decision everything was done. Indeed, such subjection to those
harsh commands was part of Jewish servitude. Nor were the Jews so highly
regarded by the Romans that they would attend to their convenience or
inconvenience.

XVI. It was not necessary for Christ to be born in the season of the year in
which the world was created, namely autumn. For nothing prevented the world
from being reborn through Christ even though his nativity is assigned to a
different time. Nor did the proclamation of the evangelical jubilee have to
occur at the same time as the legal jubilee, for every year and every time
under the Messiah is the acceptable year of the Lord and the day of
salvation.

[Marginal heading: The opinion ἐπέχει, suspends judgment.]

XVII. The third opinion is that of those who ἐπέχουσι, *suspend judgment,*
maintaining that no one can or ought to determine the month and day of
Christ’s nativity, since Scripture is silent about them and nothing is
recorded as having been determined about them in the first centuries of the
Christian church. We also join them, so that we do not rashly define what
cannot be soundly defined, but remain ignorant of what Scripture wished us to
remain ignorant, since it would have been easy for it to note that year,
month, and day if this had been judged necessary for our consolation and
faith. Hence we believe that no controversy should be raised with anyone over
this subject. And while we judge the annual celebration of the solemnity of
the nativity to be most pious and useful, we nevertheless believe that the
observance of December 25 precisely rests upon custom and the long practice
of the church rather than upon any apostolic precept or example. Whoever
wishes to know more should consult Ussher’s *Annals,* Spanheim’s *Dubia,*
Baillie’s *Chronology,* and others among our doctors who have treated this
subject at length.
