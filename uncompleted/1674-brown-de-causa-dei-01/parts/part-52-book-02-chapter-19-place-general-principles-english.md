CHAPTER XIX

Certain General Points Concerning Place in Relation to Religion Are Set Forth,
and Its Necessity and Usefulness Are Shown

It is a refrain among the Adversaries that *Time* and *Place* have the same
relation in regard to Religion; that is, that each is a mere circumstance of a
religious action. They repeat this often in all their writings and press it, as
though it were something certain and indubitable. But although our men often
deny it, and although the burden of proof rests upon the one who affirms it,
they never gird themselves to prove it.

And although it is enough for us to deny what has been asserted confidently
enough but not proved—indeed, not even made probable—nevertheless, for good
measure we shall show how manifold is the difference between *Time and Place*
in relation to Religion, so that the reader may see that not everything which
the adversaries assert with sufficient confidence is to be received without
examination. But first we shall premise certain general points concerning
*Place,* and concerning *Place* in relation to Religion.

*Place* is considered in two ways, according as it is in two categories: namely,
either according as it is in the category of *Quantity,* or according as it is
in the category of *Where.* In the first way it is called either *long, broad,*
or *deep,* and is considered in itself; in the latter way it denotes a relation
to what is located, or to that which is in such a Place or where. Then,

*Place* can be called either *Natural, Artificial,* or *Accidental.* A *Natural*
place is one given by nature, as when something is said to be in Earth, Water,
or Air. An *Artificial* place is one which an artisan makes, as when someone is
said to be in a House, a Palace, a Courtyard, and so forth. It is called
*Accidental* with respect to the *Thing Located,* as when we say that someone is
in that place where such a thing is being done, or where such company is held,
and so forth. Furthermore,

*Place* is either *indeterminate* or *Determinate.* An *indeterminate place* is
one in which

[1674 logical printed p. 365 | IA leaf 383 | PDF p. 383]

we happen to be when we do such a thing, and which for that reason we do not
choose; nor are we commanded to do it in such a place, nor perhaps do we think
about it while we are in it. A *determinate* place is one which a person
prescribes for himself, or a superior for an inferior, so that such a thing may
be done in it. Accordingly, it comes into consideration no less than the action
itself and forms part of obedience when it is enjoined by a superior that the
place be observed, or that something be performed in it. Moreover,

*Place* is either *Common* or *Sacred.* A *common* place is one in which worldly
affairs are commonly conducted, and for the sake of distinction it can be
called a *Profane Place.* A *Sacred* place is one in which religious affairs are
conducted, and is a *Consecrated Place,* or one *Dedicated* and set apart for
Religious exercises. This is done either *totally and perpetually,* or only *in
part,* for some *Time.* A *perpetually consecrated Place* can be consecrated
exclusively in two ways: either because it is not lawful to conduct any worldly
work in it, or because nowhere else is it lawful to perform those spiritual
duties which are to be performed in that place. In both these ways the Temple
at Jerusalem was an exclusively consecrated place, because it had been so
dedicated to divine exercises that it was not lawful to turn it to other uses
or to conduct ordinary business there. Nor was it lawful to immolate and offer
sacrifices elsewhere, or to perform other typical duties affixed to that place.

Place, whether *Common* or *Sacred,* can be spoken of with respect either to
*Public* Worship, *Domestic* Worship, or *Solitary* Worship.

As for the Necessity of *Place,* it is considered *physically and naturally,*
and thus is indeed necessary to Religion—or rather, to the creature which does
anything. For all creatures, even incorporeal Angels, have their Place and
*where,* in which they exist and operate; for they are in some place in such a
way that they are not at the same time outside it. All bodies also have an
*internal Place,* which bounds the body itself, and an *External* one in the
air, for example, or in water, which they fill and require by natural and
physical necessity. And this Place is exactly proportioned to the body itself,
corresponding to every dimension of the thing located—that is, to its *length,
breadth, and height,* or *depth*—and it is unchanging, always remaining the same
as long as the body remains the same, even though it changes its *where.* Place
considered in this way is indeed equal to Time, whether with respect to Religion
or to any other actions, because each is physically necessary. For whether we
wish it or not, we exist *in some Place and Time,* since these are necessary and
inseparable adjuncts. But this relation of *Place* together with *Time* is merely
Physical, in no way Religious or Moral; for *Time and Place* so considered have
no Ethical, Moral, or Theological relation to virtue or vice, as is evident.

Moreover, Place is also naturally necessary to all human actions, whether
Ordinary or Religious, in this respect: such proportion and size of place is
required that in it a person can breathe and perform any action with sufficient
convenience and without danger to life. Accordingly, a multitude requires a
larger place than one person does. In this consideration too, *Place* can be
called equal to *Time,* because just as *Time* applied to Religion ought not, by
excessive duration or frequency, to be prejudicial to our life, so neither ought
*Place* to be so confined or narrow that it is harmful rather than convenient.
But this consideration is purely and simply

[1674 logical printed p. 366 | IA leaf 384 | PDF p. 384]

Natural, Vital, or Medical, not Moral or Theological; and it chiefly regards the
Body, not the Soul, except indirectly.

Furthermore, place is Naturally necessary in this respect: when several people
assemble together to worship God and to hear sermons, the Place must be large
enough to receive everyone conveniently and made convenient in other ways, so
that the Preacher can easily be heard by everyone, and so forth. With respect to
this consideration too, it can be granted that *Place and Time* are equally
Necessary. Yet this consideration, like the former, is *Negative* or *Privative*
rather than *Positive.* And although it denotes a relation to Religion more
properly than the preceding consideration, nevertheless it does not properly
denote a *Moral and Theological* necessity, but rather an *Animal* one, since it
has regard only to the senses, so that they may better perform their office.
Religion, however, is exercised chiefly in the soul and in the heart; and
sometimes it can be exercised in solitude, without any use of the external
organs. And when it is exercised publicly, although the place may be suitably
accommodated to the senses, the soul can nevertheless be distracted in many
ways and thereby rendered unfit to worship God.

But all this Necessity of *Place* considered in itself—and no greater necessity
can be imagined in relation to Religion—is in no way to be equated with the
Necessity of *Time,* as can be evident to anyone by comparing what was said
above about *Time* with what is said here about *Place.* For nothing certain
appears here, nothing affirmative; rather, all this necessity, as great as it
is, is negative or privative with respect to Religion, and bears more directly
upon people themselves while they are occupied with external actions than upon
religion itself.

As for *usefulness:* *Place* can indeed be said to be useful to Religion in
various ways, but all that usefulness is Accidental, not Substantial, as will
appear.

*First,* then, a place can be called useful—or rather, convenient—when it is
suitable for repelling injuries from the sun, sky, and air, and large enough to
receive all those who ought to assemble together to worship God. But this
convenience contributes little to Religion, for sometimes we can worship God in
an open and sunny place, even when we are exposed to injuries from the air, as
we see done in *Ezra 10:9, 13.* The convenience of benches, seats, and similar
things can also be referred to this, as can the situation of the place, so that,
namely, it is not excessively far from the houses of those who ought to worship
God together. But this Usefulness or Convenience is merely accidental as
regards Religion, and removes some impediment rather than advancing Religion
itself, since a place can be prepared in the most convenient way while all
Religion meanwhile perishes. Therefore no benefit thereby accrues to Religion.
And in this respect, *Place* can be equated with *Time,* which also is useful
accidentally; for *Weather,* as was seen above, is not substantially useful to
an action, but only accidentally.

*Second,* If a Place is very greatly adorned, whether with respect to its
situation, form, or other accidents, it can perhaps please some people. But for
the most part this convenience, if it can be called a convenience, is an
inconvenience to many, naturally suited to diverting the mind and thereby
sufficiently effective at hindering the pious exercise of Religion, as
experience establishes. For often in Temples adorned with tapestries,

[1674 logical printed p. 367 | IA leaf 385 | PDF p. 385]

pictures, and other ornaments of this kind in which some people perhaps take
delight, it can be seen that the eyes of many are occupied by these objects,
and at length the mind wanders and is drawn entirely away from the present
business. And in this respect, no sane person will say that *Place* is equal to
*Time,* since we cannot similarly abuse Time and render it hostile to Worship,
or make it an effective impediment.

*Third,* it can be called not useless but fitting that a proper Place be
dedicated and reserved for pious uses, or divine worship. We make nothing of the
consecration employed by the Papists and Hierarchists, since it is not founded
upon God's word. For here civil propriety is evident; and in this manner those
who construct such buildings and apply them to this use testify in some measure
how they are disposed toward Religion and the worship of God. Therefore civil
propriety requires that those buildings not be applied to other uses without
necessity. But this convenience contributes little or nothing to advancing
Religion, for a person can worship God as piously and devoutly in the open air
as in such a house, just as the Christians did in the primitive Church, who
worshiped God in crypts, cemeteries, and other places of this kind. Christ also
worshiped God publicly outside a house, on mountains, on the shore, and in
private houses. Therefore in this matter *Place* is not to be equated with
*Time,* but is to be placed far behind it, as is evident from the foregoing and
will also appear later.

*Fourth,* there is also an accidental Usefulness of *place* in this: that it is
set apart for divine use in order to preserve a firm memory of some signal deed
of God. For this end God formerly ordained that the Temple be built on Mount
*Moriah.* For this end also, before the Temple at Jerusalem was built, others,
with God's approval, constructed certain altars which afterwards afforded an
occasion for superstition, just as did the assemblies of the primitive
Christians held beside the monuments of the Martyrs, as experience establishes.
In this regard *Place and Time* are in some measure equal, because God also
determined certain times for such an end—the Time of Passover, namely, and
similar times—which additionally had something typical annexed to them. Men
also instituted certain anniversary Times which are not to be approved, as is
evident in the feast days observed by the Papists. But since God has now
instituted no Place as he formerly instituted the Temple, and there is a certain
day called the *Lord's Day,* for celebrating the memory of Christ's resurrection
and the other works of redemption, as is commonly said, it is evident that
*Time* is to be preferred far above *Place* and is more useful to Religion.

*Fifth* and finally, there is also another accidental usefulness of *Place:*
namely, when a place is typical, having been instituted by God for ceremonial
use, as the *Tabernacle* and *Temple* formerly were. These foreshadowed Christ,
whose human nature was the true temple in which the fullness of deity dwelt
bodily, as the Apostle says to the *Colossians,* and taught that all sacrifices
are to be accepted in Christ and through Christ alone. In this respect *Place*
can be equal to *Time,* because God also formerly instituted typical times which
regarded Christ, as the Apostle teaches in *Colossians 2.* And we acknowledge
that all these, both *Times* and *Places,* have now been abolished.

From all these things it is evident that nothing appears in *Place,* in relation
to Religion, which could prove it to be substantially useful to Religion, unless
perhaps accidentally,

[1674 logical printed p. 368 | IA leaf 386 | PDF p. 386]

namely by force of God's free institution, making it a type or annexing
something ceremonial to it. But with *Time* the matter stands far otherwise,
both with respect to its whole *proportion,* to its continuous *duration,* and
to its frequent *recurrence.* For with respect to these aspects, as is evident
from the foregoing, the Ethical, Moral, and substantial usefulness and indeed
necessity of Time for advancing Religion is apparent; indeed, also its
Theological usefulness and necessity. Thus it is to be placed far before
*Place.* But this will be explained more particularly in the following chapter.
Meanwhile, anyone who wishes to have what is said here, and what is to be said
in the following chapter, more fully explained should consult the book named
above, *On the Sabbath,* written in English by the Englishmen *Daniel Cawdrey
and Herbert Palmer,* part 1, chapter 2.
