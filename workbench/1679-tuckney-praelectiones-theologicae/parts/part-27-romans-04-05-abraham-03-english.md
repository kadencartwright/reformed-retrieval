Before proceeding to explain the individual clauses, it remains for us to
consider, through an analysis of the whole passage, how and with what skill
Paul builds his argument upon this immovable foundation.

The sum of the whole is this: Abraham was justified not by works but by
faith alone; therefore all of us are as well. The antecedent is affirmed
rhetorically in verse 1: “What, then, shall we say that Abraham our father
found according to the flesh?” It is proved and confirmed by the following
four verses. Piscator compresses the sum of this proof—and of these four
verses—into this single syllogism:

“What is given for works is not credited from grace (this is stated in verse
4). But righteousness was credited to Abraham from grace in verse 3.
Therefore it was not given to him for works in verse 2.”

He then illustrates the proposition by the antithesis of contraries in verse
5. So Piscator. This is correct, but he seems to reverse and entangle the
order of Paul's text excessively and thus to make it more complicated.
Pareus does the same in his analysis of this passage, which runs as follows:

[1679 printed p. 175 | IA leaf 183]

“The proposition to be proved is that Abraham was justified not by works but
by faith. Its former part, ‘not by works,’ is proved: (1) from denial of the
effect to denial of the cause: if he had been justified by works, he would
have had matter for boasting before God, but he does not have it (verse 2);
(2) from correlatives: wages are given to a worker from debt, not from grace
(verse 4), but Abraham's reward was not given from debt but credited from
grace (verse 3): ‘For what does Scripture say? Abraham believed God, and it
was credited to him for righteousness.’ Here the proof of the latter part,
that Abraham was justified by faith, is also interwoven. Its proof is likewise
twofold: first, by Scripture's express testimony in Genesis 15:6, ‘For
what does Scripture say? Abraham believed,’ and so forth; second, by denying
the contrary already proved: ‘To the one who does not work’—that is, who does
not seek to be justified by works—‘but believes in the one who justifies the
ungodly, faith is credited as righteousness’ (verse 5). But Abraham did not
seek to be justified by works, as is understood from verse 4, but by faith,
as is proved in verse 3. Therefore faith was credited to him for
righteousness.”

Pareus finally concludes, “Thus Paul interweaves the parts of this
demonstration with great skill.” Perhaps, however, this interweaving belongs
not to Paul but to Pareus his interpreter. Paul's reasoning and method seem
plain, simple, and direct, following the sequence of his words, if arranged
in this way:

The proposition to be proved was that Abraham was justified not by works but
by faith.

Paul proves “not by works” in verse 2, because Abraham would then have had
boasting before God, which he did not have.

He proves “but by faith” in verse 3 by Scripture's express testimony:
“Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him for righteousness.”

Verses 4 and 5 expand these two points. Paul proves the former—that Abraham
was not justified by works—with a new argument and with the foundation of
the preceding one in verse 4: wages are credited to a worker according to
debt, which could provide matter for the boasting rejected by the preceding
argument. He repeats and affirms in verse 5 the latter point, which had been
proved from Scripture in verse 3.

Olevianus seems to me to arrange and connect these matters most clearly and
simply of all. His account is this: “Abraham was not justified by works in
verse 1: (1) because he has nothing about which to boast in verse 2; (2)
because he was justified by the gracious crediting of faith in verse 3. The
inference—if by faith, then not by works—is proved from the antithesis of
works and faith in verses 4 and 5, that is, from the nature of opposites. A
person cannot be justified by God's gracious favor and at the same time by
works, because it cannot be both freely given and not freely given but from
merit. If it is from debt, he has something about which to boast; if from
grace and the gracious crediting of faith, he has something for which to
glorify God.”

[1679 printed p. 176 | IA leaf 184]

This, in my judgment, is the simple and genuine connection and analysis of
the passage. In it Paul proves the whole principal controversy, as Adam
Sasbout understands him: neither Abraham nor consequently any believer is
justified by works. He proves it by two arguments: first, because Abraham
would then not have possessed glory before God, which he did possess (verse
2); and second, because he was justified by faith (verse 3). Or rather, Paul
establishes it by three arguments: first, because Abraham was justified by
faith (verse 3); second, because if he had been justified by works, he would
have possessed glory—or more properly, boasting (verse 2); and third,
because he would then have possessed it from debt (verse 4), whereas verse 5
ascribes the whole to grace.

Having set down these points about the passage's general scope and analysis,
let us now approach a detailed explanation of each verse, beginning with the
first, which reads:

VERSE 1

Τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν Ἀβραὰμ τὸν πατέρα ἡμῶν εὑρηκέναι κατὰ σάρκα;

What, then, shall we say that Abraham our father found according to the
flesh? — Vulgate

Or, as Beza renders it more correctly:

What, then, shall we say that Abraham our father obtained according to the
flesh?
