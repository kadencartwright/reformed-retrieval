We will nevertheless enter upon a certain concise method for refuting at least
all the principal tenets of his position and for establishing the truth
against every assault, both his and those of other adversaries—Libertines,
Socinians, Remonstrants, Erastians, and others. But first it will be pertinent,
indeed useful, to see how well he agrees with the Reformed churches. Anyone who
reads their confessions even cursorily will easily perceive this. We will set
out only certain passages here.

The Helvetic Confession drawn up in AD 1566, chapter 1, in *Corpus
Confessionum*, part 1, page 1, says: “We therefore hold that true wisdom and
godliness are to be sought from these Scriptures, and also the reformation
and—note well—the government of the churches and the institution of all the
offices of godliness.” And a little later: “Therefore, when this word of God is
today proclaimed in the church by lawfully called preachers, we believe—note
well—that the word of God itself is proclaimed and received by the faithful,
and that no other word of God is either to be invented or expected from
heaven. In the present case the word itself that is proclaimed is to be
regarded, not the minister who proclaims it. Even if he is evil and a sinner,
the word of God nevertheless remains true and good. Nor do we suppose that
this external preaching ought therefore to be regarded as useless because
instruction in true religion depends upon the inward illumination of the
Spirit, since it is written, ‘No one will teach his neighbor, for they will all
know me,’ and, ‘Neither he who waters nor he who plants is anything, but God,
who gives the increase.’ For although no one comes to Christ unless he is
drawn by the heavenly Father and inwardly illuminated by the Holy Spirit, we
nevertheless know that God certainly wills the word of God to be preached

[Volume II; printed p. 21; ONB canvas 29]

outwardly as well. God could indeed have instructed Cornelius in Acts by his
Holy Spirit or by the ministry of an angel, without Peter’s ministry. Yet God
nevertheless directs him to Peter, of whom the angel says, ‘He will tell you
what you must do.’ The same one who inwardly illuminates by giving the Holy
Spirit to men also gave this command to his disciples: ‘Go into all the world
and preach the gospel to every creature.’ Hence Paul outwardly preached the
word to Lydia, the seller of purple at Philippi, while inwardly the Lord opened
the woman’s heart. The same Paul, after setting out an elegant progression in
Romans 10, finally infers, ‘Therefore faith is from hearing, and hearing through
the word of God.’ We acknowledge that God can inwardly illuminate people even
without the external ministry, whom and when he wills; this belongs to his
power. We, however, are speaking of the customary method of instructing people,
delivered to us by God through precept and example.” Hence it is clear that
Scripture teaches how the church is to be governed; that preaching the word of
God is not useless, although illumination depends upon the Holy Spirit; and
that this inward illumination does not abolish the external preaching of the
word. All these things oppose Velthuysen, as will become evident.

And chapter 14, on page 24 of the same work: “Judging simply according to the
word of God, we say that all lawfully called ministers possess and exercise the
keys, or the use of the keys, when they proclaim the gospel—that is, when they
teach, exhort, comfort, and rebuke the people entrusted to their faith, and
keep them under discipline. For in this way they open the kingdom of heaven to
the obedient and close it to the disobedient. The Lord promised these keys to
the apostles in Matthew 16 and bestowed them in John 20, Mark 16, and Luke 24,
when he sends out the disciples and commands them to preach the gospel to the
whole world and to for-

[Volume II; printed p. 22; ONB canvas 30]

give sins. In his epistle to the Corinthians, the apostle says that the Lord
gave ministers the ministry of reconciliation. He immediately explains what
this is and calls it the word or doctrine of reconciliation. Explaining his
meaning still more clearly, he adds that ministers act as Christ’s ambassadors
in Christ’s name, as though God himself were exhorting the peoples through the
ministers to be reconciled to God, namely through faithful obedience. They
therefore exercise the keys when they urge faith and repentance. In this way
they reconcile people to God; in this way they forgive sins; in this way they
open the kingdom of heaven, and so forth.” This therefore does not belong to
everyone, as Velthuysen wishes.

And chapter 17, on page 32 of the same work: “There is likewise one head of the
body, which corresponds with the body. Therefore the church cannot have any
other head than Christ.” And a little later: “He most strictly forbade his
apostles and the apostles’ successors primacy and dominion in the church.
Therefore, who does not see that all who strive against this illustrious truth
and introduce a different government—note well—into Christ’s church should
rather be numbered among those about whom Christ’s apostles prophesy: Peter in
2 Peter 2, and Paul in Acts 20, 2 Corinthians 11, and 2 Thessalonians 2? When
the Roman head is removed, we introduce no *ataxia*, or disorder, into Christ’s
church, since we teach—note well—that the government of the church delivered
by the apostles is sufficient for us to keep the church in proper order.” The
church therefore is not permitted to institute whatever form of ecclesiastical
government she pleases, as Velthuysen dreams.

Moreover, chapter 18, page 36: “To gather or establish a church for himself,
and to govern and preserve it, God has always employed ministers, still employs
them, and will continue to employ them as long as the church is on earth.
Therefore the origin—note well—institution, and function of ministers are most
ancient and are from God him-

[Volume II; printed p. 23; ONB canvas 31]

self, not a new ordinance or one made by men. God could certainly by his power
join a church of human beings to himself immediately, but he preferred to deal
with people through the ministry of people. Ministers therefore must be
regarded not merely as ministers in themselves, but as ministers of God,
through whom God effects the salvation of human beings. Hence we warn that we
must take care not to attribute the things belonging to our conversion and
instruction to the hidden power of the Holy Spirit in such a way that we empty
the ecclesiastical ministry of effect. For we ought always to remember the
apostle’s words in Romans 10:14, ‘How will they believe?’ and so forth; and what
the Lord said in the Gospel, John 13:20, ‘Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever
receives anyone I send receives me,’ and so forth; and that the Macedonian man,
appearing in a vision to Paul while he was in Asia, admonished him and said,
‘Cross over into Macedonia and help us’ (Acts 16:9). Elsewhere the same apostle
said, ‘We are God’s fellow workers; you are God’s field and building’ (1
Corinthians 3:9).” All these things oppose Velthuysen.

On page 38 of the same work: “No one ought to usurp the honor of the ministry
for himself—that is, seize it for himself through gifts, any arts, or his own
choice. The church’s ministers are to be called and chosen by an ecclesiastical
and lawful election.” And a little later: “Not just anyone, but qualified men,
are to be chosen. Those who are chosen are to be ordained by the elders with
public prayers and the laying on of hands. Here we condemn all who run of their
own accord when they have not been chosen, sent, or ordained.” This office
therefore belongs not to everyone, but to those lawfully called, sent, and
ordained by the elders through the laying on of hands. Velthuysen acknowledges
no one sent and ordained in this way, and he rejects the laying on of hands.

And after certain other statements, page 39: “One kind of power is absolute and
unqualified, which is also called the power of right. By this power all things
are subject to Christ, the Lord of all.

[Volume II; printed p. 24; ONB canvas 32]

The Lord reserves this power for himself and does not transfer it to anyone
else. Another kind is the power of office, or ministerial power, limited by him
who possesses full power. It is more like ministry than dominion. For a master
grants his steward power over his household, and to that end also gives him the
keys with which he admits into the house or excludes from it those whom the
master wills to be admitted or excluded. By virtue of this power a minister
does by his office what the Lord has commanded him to do. The Lord ratifies
what he does and wills the act of his minister to be regarded and acknowledged
just as if it were his own. To this, of course, pertain those gospel sayings:
‘I will give you the keys,’ and so forth (Matthew 16:19), and, ‘Whose sins you
forgive,’ and so forth (John 20:23). Therefore the ecclesiastical power of the
church’s ministers is that function by which the ministers govern God’s
church.” Velthuysen is therefore wrong when he wishes absolutely no ministerial
power or authority in ecclesiastical or spiritual matters to be ascribed to
ministers.

A little later in the same place: “One and equal power or function has been
given to all ministers in the church. Certainly from the beginning bishops or
presbyters governed the church with a common care. No one set himself above
another or usurped for himself greater power or dominion over his fellow
bishops. Mindful of the Lord’s words in Luke 22:26, they kept themselves humble
and assisted one another through mutual services in governing the church.” The
church therefore is not free to institute whatever form of ecclesiastical
government she pleases, as Velthuysen supposes.

Similarly, page 41 of the same work: “The offices of ministers are various. It
belongs to ministers to assemble the sacred gathering, expound God’s word in
it, and adapt the whole of doctrine to the condition and needs of the church.”
This is very different from teaching according to a formula or con-

[Volume II; printed p. 25; ONB canvas 33]

fession proposed by the congregation, which is what Velthuysen wishes.

And a little later: “Since it is altogether necessary that there be discipline
in the church, and since excommunication was once customary among the ancients
and ecclesiastical courts existed among God’s people, in which prudent and godly
men exercised this discipline, it has also belonged to ministers to regulate
this discipline for edification according to the condition of the times, the
public situation, and necessity. Here the rule must always be maintained that
all things ought to be done for edification, decently and properly, without
tyranny and sedition. For the apostle testifies that power in the church was
given to him by God for edification and not for destruction.” Velthuysen
therefore errs when he wishes this discipline abolished and denies that this
excommunication is an ecclesiastical censure to be pronounced by an
ecclesiastical court, or that any such power was granted to ministers by
Christ.

Similarly, a little later on page 41: “Meanwhile there ought to be a proper
discipline among ministers. Their doctrine and life are to be diligently
examined in synods. Sinners are to be rebuked by elders and restored to the way
if they can be healed. If they are incurable, they are to be deposed and driven
away from the Lord’s flock like wolves by true pastors. If they are false
teachers, they are by no means to be tolerated. Nor indeed do we disapprove of
ecumenical councils if they are held after the apostolic example for the
church’s welfare, not her destruction.” The apostles therefore set before us an
example of synods together with a power of jurisdiction to be exercised by
pastors—namely, in examining and expelling them.

Moreover, chapter 19, page 43 of the same work: “Just as God is the author of
the sacraments, so he continually works in the church in which the sacraments
are rightly performed. Thus,

[Volume II; printed p. 26; ONB canvas 34]

when the faithful receive the sacraments from ministers, they acknowledge that
God is working in his own institution, and therefore receive the sacraments as
if from God’s own hand. The minister’s fault, if any notable one resides in him,
does not harm them when they acknowledge that the sacraments’ integrity depends
upon the Lord’s institution. Hence in the administration of the sacraments they
also clearly distinguish between the Lord himself and the Lord’s minister.”
They therefore will that the sacraments be administered by ministers, not by
just anyone, as Velthuysen wishes.

Similarly, chapter 22, page 53 of the same work: “Although everyone may read the
Holy Scriptures privately at home and mutually edify one another in true
religion by instruction, sacred gatherings, or ecclesiastical assemblies of
the faithful, are nevertheless entirely necessary so that God’s word may be
lawfully proclaimed to the people and prayers and supplications offered
publicly. For it is evident that in the apostolic and primitive church all the
godly attended gatherings of this kind. As many as despise them and separate
themselves from them contemn true religion, and are to be pressed by pastors
and godly magistrates not to persist more obstinately in separating themselves
and rejecting the sacred gatherings.” Yet Velthuysen, as we will hear, wishes
those who attend these public assemblies and gatherings to be superstitious.

Finally, chapter 30, page 60: “The magistrate’s principal office is to procure
and preserve public peace and tranquillity. He will certainly never do this
more successfully than when he truly fears God and is religious—namely, when,
following the example of the most holy kings and princes of the Lord’s people,
he promotes the preaching of the truth and sincere faith, cuts down lies and
every superstition along with all ungodliness and idolatry, and defends God’s
church. Indeed, we teach that care of religion pertains especially to the holy
magistrate.” They do not say that he ought or is able to seize for himself all
ecclesiastical government, as Velthuysen wishes.

[Volume II; printed p. 27; ONB canvas 35]

The Helvetic Confession drawn up in AD 1536, articles 16, 17, and 18, at pages
69–70 of the work cited above, says: “The authority of the word and of feeding
the Lord’s flock, which is properly the power of the keys, prescribes equally
to the highest and the lowest and is to be sacred and inviolable. Its
administration ought to be entrusted only to chosen and qualified men, either
by a divine vote, by the church’s sure and deliberate vote, or by the judgment
of those to whom the church has delegated this duty. For this function is to be
granted to no one whom the ministers and those to whom this business has been
committed by the church have not found and judged to possess knowledge of the
divine law, innocence of life, and singular zeal for Christ’s name. Since this
is the true election of God, it is rightly confirmed by the church’s vote and
the laying on of the minister’s hands. For Christ alone is the true head and
pastor of his church, who gives overseers, pastors, and teachers so that they
may rightly and lawfully exercise the power in the external administration of
the keys.” This office therefore is not common to everyone. Those who are
rightly appointed are ordained through the laying on of hands and possess the
power and authority to exercise the key of doctrine. Velthuysen is therefore
mistaken.

The French Confession, article 25: “We believe that, since we possess Christ
only through the gospel, the sacred and inviolable *taxis*, or order,
established in the church by his authority must be preserved. Consequently,
pastors are required in the church upon whom rests the charge of teaching the
word and administering the sacraments. They must also be honored and
reverently heard, provided that they are lawfully called and discharge their
office. This is certainly not because God needs such lesser props and aids, but
rather because it thus seems good to him to govern us by employing this as a
kind of bridle. We therefore detest those fanatics who, as far as they can,
desire to abolish the sacred ministry—that is, the preaching of the word and
the administration of the sacra-

[Volume II; printed p. 28; ONB canvas 36]

ments. Romans 1:16 and 10:17; Matthew 18:20; Ephesians 5:22 and 4:11; Matthew
10:40; John 13:10; Romans 20:14–15.” They would therefore detest Velthuysen
among the other fanatics, for, as far as he can, he not only desires the sacred
ministry to be abolished but strives with all his strength to abolish it, as we
will show.

And article 26: “We therefore believe that no one is permitted to withdraw from
the assemblies and rest content in himself. Rather, all together must maintain
and preserve the church’s unity by submitting themselves to the common
institution and Christ’s yoke wherever God has established that true
ecclesiastical discipline, even if the magistrates’ edicts oppose it. All who
separate themselves from this order resist God’s ordinance. Psalms 5 and 42;
Ephesians 4:11; Hebrews 10:25; Acts 4:19 and 5:29.”

Article 29: “We believe that the true church ought to be governed by that polity
or discipline which our Lord Jesus Christ established: namely, so that there
are pastors, presbyters or elders, and deacons in her; purity of doctrine is
maintained; vices are curbed; the poor and others in distress are cared for as
need requires; and sacred assemblies are held for the edification of young and
old. Acts 6:4; Ephesians 4:11; 1 Timothy 3:2; Titus 1:5.” Christ therefore
established polity and discipline in his church, and it is not permissible to
institute another for governing the church. Velthuysen, who teaches us the
contrary, is therefore wrong.

Article 30: “We believe that all true pastors, wherever they have been placed,
are endowed with the same and equal power among themselves under that one head,
the supreme and sole universal bishop, Jesus Christ. Consequently, no church is
permitted to claim command or dominion over another. Matthew 20:26 and 18:2; 2
Corinthians 1:27; 1 Peter 5:3.” Therefore not every ecclesiastical govern-

[Volume II; printed p. 29; ONB canvas 37]

ment is indifferent, as Velthuysen pretends.

Article 31: “We believe that no one is permitted to seize the church’s helm on
his own authority, but that each person ought to be admitted after a lawful
election, insofar as this can be done and as long as the Lord grants the
opportunity. We believe that this rule must always be followed: all pastors,
elders, and deacons must have testimony of their calling. Matthew 28; 10:16;
Mark 16:15; John 15:16; Acts 1:21; Romans 10:15; Titus 1:5; Galatians 1:15; 1
Timothy 3:7–8.” These things oppose Velthuysen’s position.

Article 32: “We believe it expedient for those who have been chosen to govern a
particular church to deliberate among themselves how the whole body may be
properly governed, yet in such a way that they never depart from what our Lord
Jesus Christ instituted.” Covenant therefore has no efficacy here, although
Velthuysen loudly extols it.

Article 33: “We exclude all human inventions and all laws introduced under the
pretext of worshiping God in order to bind consciences. We admit only those
that serve to foster concord and keep everyone in the obedience that is due.
In this matter we think we must follow what our Lord Jesus Christ established
concerning excommunication, which we approve and consider necessary together
with its appendages. Romans 16:17.” Velthuysen contradicts these things by
rejecting what Christ established about excommunication, for he judges it not
only unnecessary but altogether nonexistent.

Article 39: “We believe that God wills the world to be governed by laws and
polity—and not only this, but also everything that pertains to the state of
what they call justice, whose author he requires himself to be acknowledged.
For this reason he also placed the sword into the magistrates’ hands to repress
offenses committed not only against the second table

[Volume II; printed p. 30; ONB canvas 38]

but also against the first.” They do not attribute ecclesiastical power to the
magistrate, as Velthuysen does.

The English Confession published in AD 1562, in *Corpus Confessionum*, part 1,
page 90: “We believe that there is one church of God; that it is Christ’s
kingdom, body, and bride; that Christ alone is the prince of that kingdom;
Christ alone the head of that body; Christ alone the bridegroom of that bride;
and that there are various orders of ministers in the church—some deacons, some
presbyters, and some bishops—to whom—note well—the instruction of the people
and the care and oversight of religion have been entrusted. Yet there neither
is nor can be any one person who presides over the chief affairs of the whole,”
and so forth. They therefore do not wish every ecclesiastical government to be
indifferent. They will that the care and oversight of religion be entrusted to
ministers, and thus not be common to everyone.

After certain other statements on page 91 of the same work: “A minister must be
lawfully called and properly and orderly set over God’s church. No one can
intrude himself into the sacred ministry by his own choice and whim. We say
that the power of binding, loosing, opening, and closing has been given to
ministers by Christ. The office of loosing consists in this: the minister
reconciles those who have offended the minds of their brothers by grave
scandal and some notable public offense, and who have in some way estranged
themselves from the church’s common fellowship and from Christ’s body, when
they repent. We say that he exercises the power of binding and closing whenever
he publicly excludes the excommunicated from the church’s bosom. Whatever
judgment God’s ministers pronounce in this manner, God himself so approves that
whatever is loosed and bound on earth by their work, he himself wills to loose
and bind, and to ratify in heaven.” These statements stand diametrically opposed
to Velthuysen’s.

[Volume II; printed p. 31; ONB canvas 39]

Elsewhere, article 23 of the English Confession: “No one is permitted to take
upon himself the office of publicly preaching and administering the sacraments
in the church unless he has first been lawfully called and sent to perform
these things. We ought to consider those lawfully called and sent who have been
chosen and admitted to this work by men to whom the power to call ministers
and send them publicly into the Lord’s vineyard has been granted in the
church.”

The Scottish Confession, chapter 18; see *Corpus Confessionum*, part 1, page
119: “We believe, confess, and profess that the marks of the true church of God
are, first and above all, the true preaching of God’s word; then the right
administration of Jesus Christ’s sacraments; and finally ecclesiastical
discipline rightly administered, as God’s word prescribes, to repress vice and
foster virtue.” Yet Velthuysen goes about abolishing this mark altogether.

Chapter 20 of the same work: “The purpose of councils—for we are speaking of
those that deserve the name of councils—was in part to refute heresies and to
give a public confession of faith for posterity to follow. They did both—note
well—by the authority of God’s written word.”

And chapter 22, page 122 of the same work: “We judge that two things are
necessarily required for the sacraments to be rightly administered. One is
that they be administered by lawful ministers. By such men we mean only those
who are appointed to preach the word, in whose mouths God has placed some word
of exhortation, and who are such as have been lawfully chosen for this by some
church.” Velthuysen’s opinion is certainly very different.

Chapter 24, page 124 of the same work: “Moreover, we affirm that the purification
and preservation of religion belong chiefly and especially to kings, princes,
governors, and magistrates. Thus they are ordained by God not merely for civil
government, but also to preserve

[Volume II; printed p. 32; ONB canvas 40]

true religion and to suppress every idolatry and superstition.” Velthuysen,
however, ascribes a much greater power to them—namely, all ecclesiastical power.

The Belgic Confession, article 29: “The marks by which the true church is known
are these: if the church employs the pure preaching of the gospel; if she
employs the sincere administration of the sacraments according to Christ’s
prescription; and if ecclesiastical discipline prevails for the correction of
vices.” Yet although Velthuysen is Dutch, not only does he refuse to regard
ecclesiastical discipline as a mark of the true church, but he also wishes all
ecclesiastical discipline to be removed altogether from the church.

Article 30: “We believe that this true church ought to be governed by that
spiritual polity which God has taught us in his word: namely, that there are
ministers or pastors who proclaim God’s word and administer the sacraments;
that there are also elders and deacons who constitute, as it were, the church’s
senate together with the pastors; so that in this manner true religion may be
preserved and true doctrine propagated everywhere, and also that the vicious
may be spiritually rebuked and restrained. In this manner all things will
indeed be conducted rightly and in order in the church, when faithful men are
chosen according to the rule prescribed by the apostle Paul in his epistle to
Timothy.” All these things directly oppose Velthuysen’s statements.

Article 31: “We believe that ministers of the divine word, elders, and deacons
ought to be chosen for their functions by the church’s lawful election, with
the invocation of the divine name and according to the order taught in God’s
word. Everyone therefore ought to take care that he does not intrude himself
by unlawful means, but should await the time when he is called by God, so that
he may possess a testimony of his calling by which he is certain and persuaded
that it is from the Lord. As for ministers of the divine word, wherever they
may be,

[Volume II; printed p. 33; ONB canvas 41]

they possess the same power and authority, since they are all ministers of
Christ, who alone is that universal bishop and sole head of the church.” There
is therefore a particular order for electing ecclesiastical ministers,
prescribed by God, and this order must be observed. And if ministers everywhere
possess the same power by divine right, not every form of government is
indifferent. Velthuysen nevertheless speaks otherwise, and by speaking this way
contradicts this confession.

Article 32: “Meanwhile we believe that, although it is useful and good for the
governors of the church to institute and establish among themselves some fixed
order for preserving the church’s body, they must nevertheless diligently take
care not to depart from the things instituted by Christ, our only master. We
therefore receive only what is suited to preserving and fostering concord and
unity and keeping everyone in obedience to God. For this, above all,
excommunication employed according to God’s word and together with its other
appendages is required.” The things Velthuysen sets before us are scarcely in
accord with these statements.

Article 36: “Their office”—the magistrates’, namely—“is not merely to take care
and keep watch to preserve the commonwealth, but also to protect the sacred
ministry; to remove and avert every idolatry and corruption of God’s worship;
to destroy antichrist’s kingdom and advance Christ’s kingdom; and to take care
that the word of the gospel is preached everywhere, so that God may be honored
and worshiped by each person as he requires in his word.” Velthuysen attributes
to the magistrate a very different and distinct power.

The Strasbourg Confession, chapter 13; see *Corpus Confessionum*, part 1, pages
188–89: “Concerning the ministry and dignity of the ecclesiastical order, it is
first taught among us that there is no power in the church except for
edification. Then, no one of this or-

[Volume II; printed p. 34; ONB canvas 42]

der ought to be regarded otherwise than Paul wished himself, Peter, Apollos,
and others like them to be regarded—namely, as Christ’s ministers and stewards
of God’s mysteries, of whom it ought principally to be required that each be
found faithful. They possess the keys of the kingdom of heaven—the power of
binding and loosing, forgiving and retaining sins—yet in such a way that they
are nothing but Christ’s ministers.” Therefore, whatever Velthuysen may grumble,
there is a particular order of ministers. The power of the keys is also given
to them, and they are bound faithfully to exercise this power.

The Augsburg Confession, article 5; see *Corpus Confessionum*, part 2, page 11:
“Therefore Christ instituted the ministry of teaching the gospel.” And later:
“The fanatic spirits are also condemned who pretend that the Holy Spirit is
given or is effective without God’s word. For this reason they despise the
ministry of the gospel and the sacraments and seek illuminations without God’s
word and outside the gospel.” Velthuysen joins himself to these fanatics, as we
will hear, in order to abolish this ministry of teaching the gospel or render
it empty.

Article 14: “Concerning ecclesiastical order they teach that no one ought to
teach publicly or administer the sacraments in the church unless he has been
rightly called, just as Paul also commands Titus to appoint presbyters in the
cities.” This calling is very different and distinct from that of which
Velthuysen sometimes speaks.

On ecclesiastical power, page 43 of the same work: “There have been great
disputes about the power of bishops, in which some have improperly mingled
ecclesiastical power and the power of the sword. Therefore, in order to comfort
consciences, our theologians have been compelled to show the difference
between ecclesiastical power and the power of the sword. They understand the
power of the keys, or the power of bishops according to the gospel, to be the
power or mandate from God to preach the gospel, forgive and retain sins, and
administer the sacra-

[Volume II; printed p. 35; ONB canvas 43]

ments. For Christ sent the apostles with this command: ‘As the Father sent me,’
and so forth. Therefore, since ecclesiastical power grants eternal things and
is exercised only through the ministry of the word, it does not hinder civil
government. For civil government concerns things other than the gospel. The
magistrate protects not minds but bodies and bodily possessions against
manifest injuries, and restrains people with the sword and bodily punishments
in order to maintain civil justice and peace. Ecclesiastical and civil power
therefore must not be mingled. In this way our theologians distinguish the
offices of the two powers. Thus, when the jurisdiction of bishops is in
question, civil rule must be distinguished from ecclesiastical jurisdiction.
According to the gospel, or, as they say, by divine right, bishops—that is,
those to whom the ministry of the word and sacraments has been committed—have
no jurisdiction except to forgive sins, to judge doctrine and reject doctrine
that dissents from the gospel, and—note well—to exclude from the church’s
communion the ungodly whose ungodliness is known, not by human force but by the
word. Here the churches must necessarily and by divine right yield obedience,
according to the saying, ‘He who hears you hears me.’” Later, on page 45: “To
these things they reply that bishops or pastors are permitted to make
ordinances so that things may be conducted orderly in the church.” Also to be

The Saxon Confession, chapter “On the Church”; see *Corpus Confessionum*, part 2,
page 70: “Although the church is an assembly that can be seen and heard, she
must nevertheless be distinguished from political governments, or those who
bear the sword. By divine right bishops do not possess power to punish the
disobedient or to seize worldly governments. Yet there is order in her,
according to the passage, ‘He ascended and gave gifts to men: apostles,
prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers.’ The Son of God is the high
priest, anointed by the eternal Father. So that the church may not utterly
perish, he assigns her ministers of the gospel, some called immediately by
himself, such as prophets and apostles, and others chosen through a human
calling. For he approves the church’s election and in his boundless goodness is
effective even when the gospel sounds through those chosen by the votes or in
the name of the church. We therefore retain in our churches the public rite of
ordination, by which the ministry of the gospel is entrusted to chosen men
whose conduct and doctrine we first examine. We also faithfully teach the
churches about the ministry’s dignity. Nothing greater can be said than what the
Son of God declares: ‘As my Father sent me, so I send you.’ He both shows what
commands he has given—‘Preach the gospel’—and affirms that he will be effective
through their voice, just as the Father shows that he is effective through the
Son. We also set forth God’s commands: ‘He who hears you hears me’; ‘He who
despises you despises me’; and, ‘Obey your rulers.’ These sayings nevertheless
do not establish a kingdom outside the gospel, but command the obedience due
to the gospel’s voice. These things pertain to the ministry: to teach the
gospel; administer the sacraments; impart the word of absolution to those who
seek it and do not persist in manifest offenses; ordain rightly called and

examined ministers of the gospel; exercise the church’s courts in a lawful
manner concerning those guilty of manifest crimes in conduct or doctrine; and
pronounce a sentence of excommunication against the obstinate, while absolving
and receiving again those who are converted. So that these things may be done
rightly, consistories have also been established in our churches.” Velthuysen
therefore agrees poorly with these fathers, the authors of this confession. He
wishes ecclesiastical power to be indistinguishable from political power. He
acknowledges no ministers set apart or appointed by divine right, but only men
designated for the sake of order. He despises the rite of ordination; he
acknowledges no dignity of the ministry; he does not reserve the administration
of the word and sacraments to the minister; he does not wish ministers to be
ordained; he acknowledges no ecclesiastical courts by divine right; and he does
not regard excommunication as an ecclesiastical censure.
