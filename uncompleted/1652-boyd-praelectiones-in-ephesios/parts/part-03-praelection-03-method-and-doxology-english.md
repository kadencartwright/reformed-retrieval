On verse three and those following.

[Marginalia: Lecture III. The connection.]

The first item has now been completed—not so much a part of the epistle as a
preparation or preliminary treatment familiar to our Apostle in his epistles.
By it he is accustomed to prepare the minds of the faithful to whom he writes
to receive the doctrine of truth with greater fruit, not merely to read or
hear it but much more to perform it throughout life and, so far as possible,
to reduce it to use and practice. Through that truly Apostolic inscription and
salutation—one befitting a true ambassador of Christ, breathing and savoring
true faith and brotherly love, and thus altogether reflecting the Spirit of
Christ from whom it proceeded—he renders the minds of the Ephesians attentive,
teachable, and well disposed. Now that this threshold and vestibule has been
surveyed above, it is time for us, with God leading, to enter the building
itself, most august and splendid: the body of this epistle itself.

[Marginalia: Preliminary notice concerning the order of treatment.]

In unfolding and explaining it, we have determined, with God graciously
helping, to observe this order and sequence of teaching. First, we shall make
a general analysis of the subject set before us, showing the Apostle’s aim in
the individual parts and, so far as possible, how those parts cohere with one
another. Then we shall come to the verses themselves—as it were, the members
of the parts—and to each joint and thus, wherever necessary, to the words
considered individually. Third, from the members thus explained we shall
gather the dogmatic matters, that is, so to speak, doctrinal observations. In
the meantime, to the measure of the gift entrusted to us, we shall not neglect
other kinds of observations. Such are refutative or destructive observations,
which concern the refutation of some error or false dogma that conflicts with
God’s Word. Under this head of observations we rightly include vindication,
which is nothing other than the vindication of a passage of holy Scripture
from a false and erroneous interpretation. There are formative or exhortatory
observations, which concern the duties of the Christian, life and morals,
exhortations, admonitions, and precepts; admonitory, corrective, or deterrent
observations, by which we are recalled from contrary vices, chastened, and
corrected through dissuasion; and consolatory, encouraging, or comforting
observations, which supply consolations against temptations of every kind.

As for those proleptic observations, or solutions of difficulties, by which
answers are offered to doubts or contrary objections, passages apparently in
conflict are reconciled, contrary testimonies are resolved, or texts are
vindicated from distortion of their aim, and other such things: not without
reason do we refer them to destruction or refutation, as is self-evident to
anyone who considers the matter attentively. Finally, besides those particular
observations from didactic or doctrinal passages concerning God, God’s works,
and other heads of the Christian faith that may occur more briefly in the
individual members or verses, this care must include both an indication and a
fuller, methodical treatment of the commonplaces—at least the chief and more
notable ones—with which the Apostle deals directly and expressly. Such
treatment is not always deferred and relegated to the end of each chapter, as
some are accustomed to do, lest too great a delay be introduced and more
commonplaces than can adequately be treated should occur by the end of a
chapter before we may pass to the beginning of the next. Instead, it is added
at the end of each larger division or section, that is, of those parts which,
like the larger members of the human body—the arms, upper arms, thighs, legs,
and so forth—cohere distinctly and are joined by an evident articulation.
Thus what follows does not depend so closely upon what precedes that the two
cannot be parted and separated for a little by the insertion of some
commonplace.

[Marginalia: Proleptic reason.]

I freely admit that this custom of interweaving commonplaces with the exegesis
of the text is by no means necessary and is not suited either to the designs
of all teachers or to the uses of all learners. Yet I hope it will be
advantageous for us, for the sake of both knowledge and exercise, to follow
this method at present until we have once passed by and gone through all these
things and will no longer need to linger over them in the future. Because we
learned them, as it were, at the threshold and vestibule, it will afterwards
be sufficient merely to remind those who have advanced and matured of them in
passing and cursorily. Having been thus forewarned and prepared concerning
our teaching or analysis, let us now approach the subject itself.

First, since you heard above that the former part of this epistle is contained
in its first three chapters, in which the Apostle pursues the doctrine of
God’s benefits in Christ and the causes of our salvation in order to confirm
the Ephesians, and so all the faithful, in the faith, so that they would
steadfastly seek their whole salvation in the grace of Christ alone and
nowhere require outside him as revealed in the Gospel anything for eternal
life and full and perfect blessedness: the next step is to divide this former
part in turn into its members.

[Marginalia: Subdivision of the former part. 1.]

First, we have a full and lucid explanation of the causes of salvation, or of
those spiritual benefits, from chapter 1, verse 3, through verse 15.

[Marginalia: 2.]

Then he digresses to testify to his joy over the faith and love of the
Ephesians by thanksgiving and by prayers conceived for their progress and
fuller instruction. This may itself be regarded as an indirect exhortation to
perseverance and progress in the faith. For by showing that he gives thanks to
God with such joy for their progress and prays to God with such affection for
their progress, he signifies that they themselves should do this much more.
This extends from verse 15 through the end of chapter 1.

[Marginalia: 3.]

The Apostle had begun above to apply briefly to the Ephesians that excellent
power of God in making them alive with Christ, bringing them into the
communion of the Church, and joining them to Christ the head and, so to speak,
incorporating them. Throughout chapter 2 he applies it more fully to Jews and
Gentiles alike. He illustrates this condition of the faithful under Christ
and in Christ from the contemplation of their former misery and confirms it
by explaining the communion of all the saints and the cause and foundation of
that communion.

[Marginalia: 4.]

Fourth, lest the condition of the person teaching offend weaker minds, he
takes occasion from the end of the preceding chapter to meet the stumbling
block of his cross. He magnificently commends the dignity of his ministry,
both from the fruit and benefit that thereby flows to the Ephesians themselves
and the other Gentiles, and—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 47 | Caput I p. 5]

—he exhorts them not to grow sluggish because of his bonds, since he endured
those bonds both for their sake and to their notable benefit. This
occupies the former part of chapter 3, through verse 14.

[Marginalia: 5.]

Finally, he again digresses into a notable expression of benevolence,
signifying his vehement concern for the salvation of the Ephesians by
conceiving most ardent prayers for them. These are the five members into which
we have divided the former, didactic part of the epistle in general. To the
exposition of each we shall add, God favoring, that fuller doctrine of the
commonplaces treated in them which was discussed above.

[Marginalia: Return to the first member. Subdivision.]

Let us now return to the first member, concerning spiritual benefits
pertaining to eternal salvation, conferred upon the faithful by God the Father
in Christ and actually sealed in their hearts by the Holy Spirit, from chapter
1, verse 3, to verse 15. Here, for the sake of more distinct teaching, we may
again observe these three subordinate heads in passing. First comes the
general proposition of those benefits in verse 3, containing all that follows
within itself in a certain power and potency. Then comes an explanation of the
same proposition in the seven following verses, through verse 12. Finally
comes its brief application first to the believing Jews and then to the
Ephesians to whom he writes, in the four remaining verses, 11, 12, 13, and 14,
as will appear more distinctly in its place. Thus, descending now by degrees
and step by step, we have arrived at this notable and most memorable
proposition set forth in verse 3, whose words we shall first read.

[Marginalia: Verbal exposition.]

“BLESSED BE GOD,” and so forth; that is: Let that God who is the Father of our
Lord Jesus Christ be praised and celebrated by all. He has endowed us with
every kind of truly heavenly and divine beneficence in heavenly places—that
is, from his supreme heavenly throne, where he reveals his glory in a special
and ineffable manner and from which he heaps upon us every kind of spiritual
blessing, from which he sends down and showers upon us every good and saving
gift. He does this to us as regarded in Christ as our head, from whom those
blessings are derived into the members subjected and joined to his body.

[Marginalia: Incidental observation.]

Here note in passing an elegant *antanaclasis* in the verb *to bless*, that
is, a repetition of the same word in a different sense. It is a very pleasing
figure and deservedly commended by the rhetoricians themselves, but here it
principally presents us with another excellent use. We are to learn not only
the difference between that blessing by which we bless God and that by which
he first blesses us, distinguishing these two kinds of blessing from one
another. We are also to regard the one as the cause, fountain, and source of
the other. For the reason we bless God is that he first blessed us, just as we
love God because he first loved us and sent his Son as the propitiation for
our sins, that is, the appeasement of the wrath due to our sins, as John says
in chapter 4, verse 10 of his first epistle.

[Marginalia: 1 John 4:10.]

Moreover, God is said to bless us when he benefits us, makes us blessed, and
attends us with the gifts and effects of his immense kindness and goodness.
This is a Hebrew expression, for in the Old Testament the verb *barak* is
commonly used in the same sense when attributed to God in relation to men.
This may be seen particularly in Genesis 24, where Eliezer tells Rebekah’s
parents that Jehovah had greatly blessed his master Abraham. Then, by way of
explanation, he adds how God had blessed him: so much, he says, that he had
become great—

[Marginalia: Genesis 24:35.]

—and God had given him flocks and herds, silver and gold, male and female
servants, camels and donkeys. We, by contrast, bless God after first having
been blessed by him when we acknowledge his beneficence toward us, his free
favor, and his immense mercy, and therefore celebrate, praise, venerate, and
adore him: with heart and body, with a grateful and suppliant affection of
mind, with the voice that witnesses and reveals the mind, and with our whole
life and every work conformed to heart and tongue. Thus everywhere in the
Scriptures, especially in the Psalms, the faithful are said to bless God when
they celebrate, acknowledge, praise, and proclaim his goodness and
beneficence—indeed, his other divine virtues and attributes. Psalm 103 says,
“My soul blesses Jehovah, and whatever is within me—or all my inward
parts—blesses his holy name. Bless Jehovah, O my soul, and forget none of his
benefits.”

[Marginalia: Psalm 103:1–2.]

Having noted this in passing concerning the word *bless*, let us now explain
this proposition more distinctly. In it we shall consider first the form, as
it were, which it bears, and then the matter contained in it, or what are, as
it were, its material parts.

[Marginalia: A distinct consideration of this proposition.]

As for its form, it is clear that it possesses the form of a doxology or
thanksgiving.

[Marginalia: Explanation of the form.]

For the Apostle is about to set forth the Ephesians’ spiritual blessings in
Christ, and thus to expound the causes of eternal salvation and trace them
back to their very fountain and first source. He therefore breaks forth at
once into this notable doxology, that is, a glorification of God familiar to
him, whose counterpart and exact parallel you have in 2 Corinthians 1:3 and 1
Peter 1:3.

[Marginalia: 2 Corinthians 1:3; 1 Peter 1:3.]

First, if we ask for the fountain or root from which this notable doxology
arises, we shall find that it flowed from a twofold recognition: the
recognition of our natural misery and of our most wretched and accursed
condition outside Christ Jesus; and the recognition of God’s supreme mercy in
Christ and of his free benefits. By them he transferred us from so profound an
abyss, whirlpool, or prison of misery into such light, liberty, and glory,
into such dignity and so happy a lot, that—O marvelous change in our lot!—from
slaves of Satan and sons of night and hell we should become sons of light,
indeed sons of God himself, brothers of Christ, and heirs with him of the
heavenly kingdom, in him and through him.

[Marginalia: Statement of the cause.]

For who, seriously affected and cast down in spirit first by the sense and
consciousness of his sin and native misery, and first struck by the horrors of
hell and that terrible sight of eternal death, but then raised up again by
earnest remembrance of that free deliverance and lifted, as it were, from
hell itself into glorious heaven, drenched and refreshed by the inward sense
of so great a grace—who would not immediately break forth with this holy heart
and all his strength into the praise of God’s glorious grace?

[Marginalia: Application.]

The Apostle himself certainly presents us in his own person with a notable
example of this in Romans 7. He was first affected by so deep a sense of his
natural misery, because of the remnants of sin still dwelling in his mortal
body, that he exclaimed with great feeling, “Wretched man that I am! Who will
deliver me from this body of death?” But then he doubtless gathered and
refreshed himself by the thought of Christ. You see how at last he breaks from
the heart into that utterance of gratitude, that is, thanksgiving: “I thank
God,” he says, “through Jesus Christ our Lord.”

[Marginalia: Romans 7:24.]

We have another no less illustrious proof of this same affection in 1 Timothy
1. There, when Paul considers at once his most wretched condition—

[Marginalia: 1 Timothy 1:17.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 48 | Caput I p. 6 (through Lecture III close)]

—under the tyranny of sin and the supreme mercy that the most gracious God had
exercised toward him so magnificently, marvelously, and abundantly that he was
set forth as a conspicuous example to all who would afterwards be called to
the faith and grace of Christ and to the attainment of eternal life, he is
immediately kindled with a certain pious ardor of gratitude and exclaims: “To
the King of ages, immortal, invisible, to God alone wise, be honor and glory
for ever and ever. Amen!”

Next let us see who it is whom the Apostle thanks here. “BLESSED,” he says,
“BE THE GOD AND FATHER OF OUR LORD JESUS CHRIST”—that is, that God who is the
Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, whom we distinguish by this peculiar mark and
token, as it were, from the false gods of the Gentiles and from all idols:
that he is the Father of Christ Jesus our Lord. Consequently, just as he is
the fountain and origin of Deity itself, of life, light, glory, and goodness,
so he is also the fountain and origin of every blessing and benefit, whether
spiritual or bodily and earthly. Therefore all our praises, blessings, and
thanksgivings are in the first place to be referred and directed through
Christ the Mediator to him as the first cause and source of our salvation and
the principal author and giver of every good.

[Marginalia: 2. The one to whom thanks are given.]

Thus two different subjects are not denoted here by this conjunction, any
more than in David’s words in Psalm 18: “Jehovah lives, and blessed be my
rock.” Who would be so senseless as therefore to think that Jehovah is one and
the one whom the Psalmist calls his rock—that is, his strength and the firm
foundation of his salvation—is another?

[Marginalia: Psalm 18:47.]

Here, therefore, the first person of the Deity must in the same way be
understood *by preeminence*, without excluding the Son and the Holy Spirit, as
we explained clearly enough above.

[Marginalia: Further elaboration.]

I mean the person of the Father. Let us say something about him here so that
the various expressions by which the same person is ordinarily expressed in
holy Scripture may be understood more distinctly. Sometimes he is found
expressed separately by the name *God*, as in John 17, “that they may know you,
the only true God,” and so forth. Sometimes he is expressed separately by the
name *Father*, as everywhere in John’s Gospel. Sometimes the words *God the
Father* are joined, as in the immediately preceding verse and everywhere in
the Apostolic salutations. Sometimes the words *God and Father* are joined by
a conjunction, as here and everywhere else. Yet even here we understand the
other two persons together with the Father.

[Marginalia: John 17:3.]

For just as all three persons of the holy Trinity always concur in blessing
us—that is, in one and the same act of divine blessing, and thus in every
external act that concerns creatures—while preserving their own manner and
order of acting, namely, the Father as the fountain and source of every
operation, acting through the Son and Holy Spirit; the Son as the efficient
cause second in order, doing all things from the Father through the Holy
Spirit; and the Holy Spirit as the agent third in order, immediately executing
from the Father and Son all that the Father and Son do: so also they must
certainly always be joined in every blessing of ours, that is, in that
thanksgiving which we render to God. This occurs either by express mention of
all three persons, as when we give thanks to God the Father through the Son in
the unity of the Holy Spirit, or by tacitly understanding the other two in the
person of God the Father, who is expressly mentioned. The Apostle does this
both here and frequently elsewhere.

[Marginalia: Confirmation.]

From this only two things will come to be observed by us before we proceed to
the remaining part of this proposition. First, we should note the difference
between the Prophets and the Apostles in expressing, designating, and
circumscribing the true God for us, as it were. The former lived under the
pedagogy of the law before Christ was manifested in the flesh. When they gave
thanks to God, they had the solemn custom of giving thanks to the God of
Israel and celebrating Jehovah, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, just as
he once revealed himself to Moses in the bush, saying that this was “his name
forever, and his memorial to every age.”

[Marginalia: Exodus 3:15. First observation.]

Thus the Prophets rightly designated the true God and distinguished him from
all the idols of the Gentiles by this his memorial. They also pointed him out
as the God of hosts, Jehovah who made heaven and earth, and circumscribed him
from that marvelous and immutable work of creation or also from the
deliverance out of the land of Egypt. But once the mystery of redemption had
been disclosed and that incomparable secret hidden from the ages concerning
the incarnation of the Son of God had been fully and plainly revealed through
the Gospel, the Apostles designated this same true God—Jehovah, the God of
Israel, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—by another epithet or by another
new quality, so to speak, not proclaimed before Christ’s birth, when they
everywhere celebrate and proclaim him as the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.

The more clear-sighted among the Jews might perhaps have been willing to
confess in general and, speaking in the thesis, that God was the Father of the
Messiah or Christ whom they expected. Yet, speaking in the hypothesis, they
blasphemously denied that he was the Father of this particular person, Jesus
Christ, the son of the virgin Mary whom they had crucified, and, miserably
blinded by extreme obstinacy, they still utterly deny it.

[Marginalia: Statement of the cause.]

The Apostles therefore designate God in this way both to preach plainly and
clearly that mystery of redemption accomplished in this very Jesus Christ and
through him alone, and to show us all in whom alone God the Father is a
pacified and gracious Father to us, through whom alone we have access to the
Father and the throne of grace, and thus what knowledge of God will finally be
saving for us: namely, if we know him as the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ,
acknowledge him, and celebrate him when acknowledged. For our supreme comfort
and joy, therefore, they are accustomed to designate God as the Father of our
Lord Jesus Christ, and consequently as our most gracious and kind Father in
him and through him alone.

Second, from this same notable doxology observe, I ask, the memorable
difference between a regenerate and an unregenerate person. Although the
latter may overflow with an abundance of God’s benefits, he is so far from
therefore rendering or even conceiving heartfelt thanks to God that he rather
abuses those benefits to the dishonor of the divine name, without any sense,
memory, care, or thought of divine beneficence. By contrast, the devout mind
of a truly regenerate man and hearts imbued with love and reverence for God
never speak or think about God’s benefits and gifts, or enjoy them, without
being raised and kindled in spirit to praise him in Christ and proclaim his
grace. Indeed, even amid bonds and afflictions, they are so far from
complaining, wailing, and protesting with unbelievers that, by thinking on
those spiritual things, they rejoice with our Apostle in the bonds themselves,
glory beneath the cross, and bless God’s name with their whole heart. Let this
suffice concerning the form of this proposition.
