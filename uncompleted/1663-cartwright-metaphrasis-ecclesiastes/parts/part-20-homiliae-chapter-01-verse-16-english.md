VERSE 16. I spoke with my mind, saying: I enlarged and added wisdom beyond all
who had ruled Jerusalem before me, and my mind perceived much wisdom and
knowledge.

This appears to be a prolepsis, whose tacit objection is drawn from the
efficient cause of this vanity and vexation of mind: that the fault lay only
in Solomon's inexperience and childishness, because he was unable to procure
the delight of mind that the contemplation of natural things and of the whole
of Philosophy stands ready to supply. The reply is a denial of that
inexperience. He confirms it by comparison with lesser men, since he was
wiser than all who had ruled Jerusalem, where nevertheless the wisest of all
men had been found. In human knowledge of things, Adam alone surpassed him;
yet this wisdom did not render Adam blessed or preserve him in the happiness
he had received. Here someone may object that Solomon was too little mindful
of what Proverbs 27:2 teaches: Let another's lips praise you, and not your
own. But the answer is that this was done for the sake of confirming the
truth and is not to be condemned. Thus Paul is the herald of his own praise.
Here someone will ask: To what purpose is knowledge of natural things to be
acquired, and to what end are time and expense to be devoted to it, since it
is vanity and vexation of spirit? I answer that it greatly benefits the
ungodly, so that, knowing from these things the Deity, power, and other
invisible things that are in God, they may be rendered without excuse. But
the study is useful to the godly, both that they may be instructed in
humility by consciousness of their own smallness and that, being led to
admiration of divine wisdom, they may be stirred to proclaim his praises with
full voice. Yet how very few are found who have embraced the study of this
wisdom and set before themselves these ends, or even one of them; who do not
seek their own delight of mind; and who do not pursue this study more in order
to

become known than to know God, and seek praise from others more than to extol
God with praises.

By this inability of this wisdom, the goodness of God is wonderfully
illustrated in the preaching of the Gospel, by which even the unlearned and
those of the slowest and dullest intellect arrive at the saving knowledge of
God. See Psalm 19, where it is said to impart wisdom to the simple and
ignorant, and likewise to gladden the mind, whereas the wisdom sought from the
knowledge of natural things is vexation and torment of mind. Here they rise
up in objection: many have been found who perceived great pleasure and
advantages from their studies, and so they judge the labors undertaken to
have been amply compensated. But either Solomon is false—which cannot be—or
they are deceived. Certainly, when they placed their happiness in the
accumulation of wealth or the acquisition of honor, which have vanished, they
must have been deceived. Again, they may object that military discipline and
skill in governing lie despised unless one is permitted to reap glory and
honor from them, and that this does not prevent their practitioners from
being honored here in this world and blessed in the world to come. This is
indeed true, provided they do not set honor and wealth before themselves as
the end of their studies.
