### QUESTION III

#### What is the true end of the seventy years of the Babylonian captivity?

Answer. There is some controversy among learned men about the end of that
seventy-year period, although not so much as there was about its beginning.
Most end it in Cyrus’s first year, but many among the ancients—Clement of
Alexandria, Eusebius, Jerome, Augustine, and others—end it in the second year
of Darius Hystaspes. They base their opinion on Zechariah 1:12 (“This is the
seventieth year”), where the angel, speaking in Darius’s second year, calls
the present year the seventieth year of the captivity; but that year was at
least eleven years later than Cyrus’s first. Many customarily answer this
argument by the distinction that we first read in Theodoret: there were two
seventy-year periods, one of the captivity of Jerusalem and the other of the
desolation of Jerusalem. The former began in Nebuchadnezzar’s first year and
ended in Cyrus’s first; the latter began in Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth, when
the city and temple were desolated, and ended in Darius’s second, when, after
some interruption, the temple and city began to be restored anew. We think
that this distinction of the ancients, approved by many more recent writers,
should not be rashly rejected. Certainly the arguments by which D. Nisbett
attacks it as though Capellus had invented it seem to carry little weight;
and in fact exactly seventy years are reckoned from the devastation of the
city and temple to the second year of Darius Hystaspes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 148: *Theodoret’s distinction—not Capellus’s—between
> the seventy years of captivity and the seventy years of desolation stands
> firm against Nisbett’s arguments*.

However that may be, with most of the more recent writers, Protestant as well
as Roman Catholic, we think that the passage in Zechariah should be understood
without any distinction as referring to the seventy years of captivity
mentioned by Daniel and Jeremiah. The author of Chronicles and Ezra expressly
place their end in Cyrus’s first year. Indeed, the reasoning Jeremiah advances
in 25:12 demonstrates it: he intends his seventy years of captivity and
desolation to close with the overthrow of the Chaldaean Monarchy, but it is
certain that Babylon was captured long before the second year of Darius
Hystaspes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 148: *Yet it is wrongly applied to the passage in
> Zechariah concerning the seventieth year*.

The Latin translator of Zechariah seems to have led the ancients into his own
opinion, for he renders the prophet’s passage, “This is now the seventieth
year.” But Montanus, Torniellus, and all the vernacular versions render it
much more correctly, “now for seventy years,” thereby indicating the seventy
years of Jeremiah that had already passed. The angel sets this as the basis
of his prayers for the removal of the remnants of the desolations that still
remained after those seventy years had elapsed and the people had returned.
What misled the Latin translator is an idiom of Hebrew construction that
differs greatly from both Greek and Latin syntax. For the Hebrew text is
literally “this seventy years”: among the Hebrews plural numerals are
constructed with singular nouns and, conversely, singular numerals with
plural nouns, as may be seen in Buxtorf’s *Thesaurus*, Book 2, chapter 2, page
10. The passage should therefore have been rendered “for these seventy years,”
not “this is the seventieth year.”

> Marginalia, printed p. 148: *A bad Latin translation misled the Fathers*.

But the whole difficulty has not yet been answered. Grant that there is only
one seventy-year period, without any distinction between the Babylonian
captivity and desolation, and that in the second year of Darius Hystaspes
Zechariah is speaking of that seventy-year period which had ended in Cyrus’s
first. The further question nevertheless arises: which, ultimately, is that
first year of Cyrus? For most writers grant Cyrus two beginnings of his rule,
one in his own Persian kingdom, and the other in the Babylonian—or rather
Persian—Monarchy.

> Marginalia, printed p. 148: *Which was the first year of Cyrus’s kingdom and
> Monarchy?*

Scaliger reports from Tatian and Africanus in Eusebius that all the ancients
agreed that Cyrus’s first year fell in the first year of the fifty-fifth
Olympiad. This is readily accepted not only by our writers but also by the
Jesuits Petavius, Torniellus, Ribera, Henricus, and others. Indeed, there is
general agreement that the first year of Cyrus mentioned by the prophets
should be understood not of his Persian kingdom, but of the Babylonian
Monarchy. For it is certain that Jeremiah’s seventy-year period was not
completed before Babylon was captured, nor did the power to free the Jews
come into Cyrus’s hands before then. Nor is there much disagreement between
the parties either about the total sum of Cyrus’s rule or about its partial
sums, whether of his kingdom or his Monarchy. Herodotus gives Cyrus’s kingdom
twenty-nine years, Ctesias thirty, and Scaliger thirty-one or thirty-two;
while Ptolemy’s Canon assigns nine years to his Babylonian Monarchy, and
Xenophon seven. These figures can be reconciled by distinguishing whole and
fractional years, a sufficiently common device in this dispute. But Calvisius,
following Scaliger, and Scaliger, following the rabbis, assign only three
years to Cyrus’s Monarchy, contrary to the testimony of almost all ancient
and recent writers. It is true that Daniel mentions Cyrus’s first and third
years, but Daniel’s silence about more certainly does not prove there were no
more. Scaliger wrongly laughs at an argument of this kind in the case of
Belteshazzar.

> Marginalia, printed p. 148: *Scaliger and Calvisius wrongly assign only three
> years to Cyrus’s Monarchy*.

What is especially displeasing in Scaliger’s and Calvisius’s opinion, however,
is that they do not wish Cyrus’s first year to be referred either to the
beginning of his Persian kingdom or to that of the Babylonian Empire. Instead,
they say that the Jews, according to a new era established by themselves,

called the year in which they received from him the immense benefit of their
redemption “Cyrus’s first,” whether it was the fifth, sixth, or seventh year
after Babylon was captured. The
learned men not only assert these things without proof, but do so contrary to
the express testimony of Ezra 6:2. There the captivity of the Jews is said to
have ended in Cyrus’s first year, not only among the Jews, as in the preceding
chapter, but also among the Persians, in the royal decree deposited at
Ecbatana in the archives of the kingdom. What concern did Jewish eras have
for the Medes and Persians? And what the Persians and Medes themselves call
Cyrus’s first year in their most authentic archives—whose audacity is it,
finally, to declare it Cyrus’s sixth or seventh without any necessity?
Certainly nothing seems to have been more urgent or of earlier concern to
Cyrus, a man of the greatest spirit, than this: as soon as Babylon had come
into his power and he had learned from Daniel what Jeremiah and Isaiah,
inspired by the divine power, had foretold by name about him so many years
before, he should at once put it into effect. Therefore, with the multitude of
learned men, let the end of the Jewish captivity be established in Cyrus’s
first year—that is, the first year of his Monarchy—which is commonly said to
be the twenty-first or twenty-third year of his Persian kingdom and the
seventh or ninth year before his death.

> Marginalia, printed pp. 148–149: *Still worse, they deny that his first year
> should be reckoned either from his own kingdom or from his Monarchy*.
