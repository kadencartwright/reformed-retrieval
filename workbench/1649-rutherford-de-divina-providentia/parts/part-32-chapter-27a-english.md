CHAPTER XXVII

The arguments of Rodrigo de Arriaga, Suárez, Théophile Raynaud, and Guillaume
Gibieuf against GOD’s predetermination toward the material acts of sin are
examined. The arguments of James Arminius, Nicholas Grevinckhoven, and Diego
Ruiz, insofar as they contend that the act of sin itself is not distinguished
from its malice, are censured.

I.

Let us hear how Arriaga attacks us. He anticipates the objection as follows:
*A habit is simply a cause of an act, although it is determined to act.
Therefore general concurrence, no less than Calvinistic determination, can be
a cause of sin, even though GOD does not actually act unless determined by our
depraved will.* He replies: *A cause, considered in abstraction from a cause
in itself and an accidental cause, is defined as that which communicates being
to something. A cause in itself is defined as that which either concurs
determined in first act or determines itself. A habit of itself inclines
toward acts and draws back from the opposite acts, and so it is a cause in
itself. GOD, however, does not incline by his concurrence toward sin, but
recalls from it. Therefore his concurrence cannot be a cause of sin as
predetermination is, since the latter of itself inclines and bends the person
toward an evil act.*

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, philosophical course, disputation 10, physical
section 1, subject 5, no. 50.]

I reply: 1. The Jesuit defines cause in general badly. Being musical in a
sculptor in no way communicates being to the making of the statue, and yet
being musical is an accidental cause of the statue; and, according to the
Jesuit, accidental cause is included in this definition. 2. The Jesuit
recognizes no cause in itself unless it is determined to produce the effect.
The sun, then, is not a cause of sublunary effects—which is absurd—for the sun
is in itself an indeterminate cause, determined by these inferior things
toward generation and corruption, softening and hardening. GOD’s concurrence,
however, of itself inclines toward an act, because every cause in itself
inclines toward its effect. We do not say that GOD’s concurrence inclines
toward sin but toward this or that act as it pleases God, just as a habit
inclines toward this or that act as it is determined by the person who
possesses the habit. In this way God’s concurrence is the cause of the act
underlying-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 435; IA PDF page 493; BSB canvas 487]

*malice, just as a habit is the cause of an act. Nor is GOD’s predetermination
of itself a cause inclining toward malice; rather, the evil will abuses
predetermination.

Arriaga, at the same place: *The sun,* he says, *is truly a cause inclined
toward sublunary effects, although it needs some complement. But toward an act
of sin GOD not only gives indifferent concurrence, but gives it in such a way
that, as far as depends on him, he wills the act not to occur; and thus he not
only fails to concur with inclination, but concurs with repugnance.* Indeed,
Ruiz says, *he concurs as though accidentally and compelled by his office.*

[Marginal note: GOD does not concur toward entitative acts with repugnance,
hatred, or compulsion, as the Jesuits dream.]

I reply: Apart from the Jesuits and their pupils, the Arminians, no one would
say that GOD concurs toward material acts with repugnance and hatred. For he
either concurs with the same kind of hatred with which he pursues malice
itself, with a lesser hatred, or with no hatred but most freely. If the first,
then God hates his own concurrence as he hates sin, and it will be GOD’s office
as first cause to perform an act contrary to his own nature. If the second,
then he hates the act with one hatred and its malice with another. But since
God hates nothing but sin, the act as distinguished from malice will thus be
sin, and an infinite regress will follow. But God does not hate what he himself
effects, nor can he hate his own concurrence. Therefore he does not concur
with repugnance. Yet the Jesuits teach that God made a free decree to concur
with all causes. Hence he concurs with no repugnance. 2. If GOD’s concurrence
is the accidental cause of some positive effect, such as the material act of
sin, let them give us some other God besides Jehovah who is its cause in
itself.

Arriaga: *GOD wills the inefficacy of his concurrence. Furthermore, observe
that omnipotence is by the nature of the thing indifferent, so that it may be
subjected, as it were, to the power of the created will, just as a habit is
subject to the human will. And since this subjection depends on some act of
God’s free will—for dominion does not require that an act be inefficacious—it
follows that, once the first volition has been posited, GOD is not then a free
agent, but concurs with us just as if he were a necessary agent without
knowledge.*

[Marginal reference: Disputation 10, philosophical section 2, subject 3, nos.
87 and 91.]

I reply: 1. GOD’s inefficacious concurrence is either concurrence in potency
or actual concurrence. If the first, potential concurrence is no more
concurrence than an action in potency is action or an embryo is a human being.
Moreover, this concurrence is conditional, but cannot fail to be absolute in
one who by omniscience knows the condition as-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 436; IA PDF page 494; BSB canvas 488]

*fulfilled. GOD will then say: “I will that there be so many evil and so many
good acts—neither more nor fewer—if human beings and good and evil angels will
it. Otherwise, if they will that all be good, I will the same. Thus the glory
of punishing justice and sparing mercy will forever remain hidden from
creatures, and neither will ever be displayed, because it so seemed good to
rational creatures acting freely. The vilest creatures will thus make their
absolute decree and judgment concerning the glory of the infinite Creator.
And if they finally refuse to perform any good acts but sin to the end, I will
the same and by my concurrence will fulfill it at the creatures’ pleasure.”
The glory of sparing mercy will be buried forever, because the earthen vessels
so pronounce judgment and sentence upon the glory of the supreme potter. But
if the concurrence is actual, then the concurrence itself will be efficacious;
otherwise the concurrence of the first cause will actually be posited while
the second cause—namely, the free will—may do nothing. 2. It is unworthy of God
to say that he wills his concurrence toward good and supernatural acts, and
thus wills to display the glory of his mercy in the elect destined for glory,
by an optative and inefficacious wish. For then JEHOVAH would never bend the
king’s heart wherever he wills, but, like a person often powerless to attain
his wish, would only desire the king’s heart to be bent this way, while it was
never actually bent in the direction God willed. 3. If God concurs
inefficaciously, then after foreknowing the concurrence of the will which will
sin, he concurs efficaciously. Thus he wills the efficacy of his concurrence
and would will the sin to occur after it is at least foreknown that it will
exist. Yet it is as alien to GOD’s holiness to will existing sin as to will
sin that will exist. 4. God is said to do whatever he wills in heaven and on
earth. But if God’s omnipotence is subject to the power of the created will as
a habit is subject to the will, then at least on earth a human being can will
whatever he pleases. 5. If GOD’s dominion is preserved because GOD decreed to
subject his omnipotence and concurrence to the pleasure of created freedom,
then the Lord is Lord of our acts only because he created free choice with
full and absolute dominion over free acts and over the acts of GOD’s
omnipotence and dominion in his own concurrences. On this account GOD is Lord
of free acts in the same way as one who gives and alienates an inheritance to
another, resigning dominion and the right of dominion in the thing, can still,
according to Arriaga, be called its lord because he could have reserved the
inheritance and dominion-*

[Marginal note: Angels and human beings enjoying absolute freedom will issue
the decree and sentence about every free act and about God’s concurrences as
to their number and specification, according to the Jesuits.]

[Marginal note: GOD’s dominion in the freedom of the creature is an empty name
and title without the reality, according to the Jesuits.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 437; IA PDF page 495; BSB canvas 489]

*for himself. Adam today has dominion never to sin in just this manner, because
he once sinned freely. And God bends the king’s heart wherever he wills because,
of course, he resigned the absolute dominion of bending his heart wherever the
king himself wills, as well as of drawing the acts of divine omnipotence and
GOD’s concurrence to his own operation at his own pleasure, whether GOD wills
to concur or not. Thus GOD is Lord of our acts because he made himself our
servant and subjected his omnipotence to servitude and to the necessity of
concurring at the will’s pleasure. That is, God is Lord because he ceased to
be Lord and resigned dominion to the created will. 6. No dominion can belong
to GOD if the acts of that dominion are inefficacious. What dominion, I ask,
does one have who cannot do what he holily wills? 7. It is blasphemy contrary
to Scripture that, once his decree is posited, GOD does not act freely but
concurs toward the acts of the second cause as an agent without knowledge.
For *he works all things according to the counsel of his will*; therefore he
acts freely and from knowledge, Ephesians 1:11. *He bends the king’s heart
wherever he wills,* Proverbs 21:1. *He does whatever he wills in heaven and on
earth,* Psalm 135:6. Faith in GOD is vain, prayers are futile, and thanksgiving
for good procured in the acts of created freedom is empty if God’s omnipotence
and concurrence are subject not to GOD’s will but to the pleasure of the
created will.

2. Arriaga objects to himself as follows: *If GOD wills our salvation and
obedience only inefficaciously and by an act of complacency, it is no more
necessary that GOD prepare concurrence to procure these things than to make
more worlds, in which he takes pleasure.* He replies: *Inefficacious volitions
have a breadth. 1. One is pure complacency in the object. 2. Another desires
the object’s existence, yet not wholly as far as depends upon itself, because
it has other preponderating reasons why the things would not occur. 3. Another
is that by which GOD wills efficaciously as far as depends on him, but does not
therefore will absolutely, because the thing depends on free choice, upon which
GOD does not wish to inflict violence. Such complacency cannot exist unless
there were in GOD concurrence toward the good, though other inefficacious
volitions can exist without such a preparation of concurrence. This volition,
however, can be called virtually efficacious and virtually conditional insofar
as he so wills to concur with the created will as if he had efficaciously
willed to concur.*

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, at the same place, no. 94.]

I reply: 1. Inventing volitions and complacencies in God is-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 438; IA PDF page 496; BSB canvas 490]

*more readily inclined toward the great boldness of human ingenuity than
toward piety and conformity to Scripture, outside which it is not safe to
think about GOD. I do not deny that GOD wills the obedience of the reprobate
with greater complacency than he wills more worlds to be made, for God concurs
by some practical means toward procuring the former—namely, he employs
external means sufficiently adequate—while God employs no means at all toward
creating more worlds. Nor do I deny that God takes complacency in many things
he does not do. But the cause why God does not fulfill his complacencies is
GOD’s wise good pleasure, not preponderating reasons drawn from the natures of
things outside GOD. For every preponderating reason is from GOD’s wise will;
no reason outside GOD determines or circumscribes him. Either God could create
more worlds in opposition to those preponderating reasons, or he cannot. If
the first, GOD’s will is a more preponderating cause why more worlds would be
made than the reasons outside God are causes why they would not. No theologian
with a sound head can assert that outside GOD there is any cause or reason why
more worlds were not made, for the sole cause is GOD’s good pleasure. If the
latter, then GOD cannot even by absolute power do more than he does, since he
cannot do anything contrary to preponderating reasons and thus contrary to his
wisdom. But this would call GOD’s omnipotence into judgment if he cannot do
more than he does. It openly contradicts the Scriptures, which assert that all
things are possible to GOD. 2. There is no preponderating reason why God would
deny efficacious grace and concurrence to Judas rather than to Peter for final
repentance. *But,* says the Jesuit, *GOD refused to inflict violence upon the
free choice of Judas and others who finally resist GOD in impenitence.* Yet if
God could not efficaciously convert all the impenitent to himself without
inflicting any violence upon free choice, then God cannot give repentance unto
life to more people than those who actually repent. The determinate number of
the repentant and elect and of the unrepentant and reprobate is then from
preponderating reasons outside God, not from God’s absolute will. Thus it does
not lie with GOD to save more than he saves or condemn more than he condemns.
3. The fiction of indifferent concurrence—unknown to Scripture, the Fathers,
the earlier Schoolmen, and the Roman Catholic Doctors—is of such importance to
the Jesuits and Arminians that without it created freedom would be destroyed.-*

[Marginal note: There are no preponderating reasons outside God which
circumscribe GOD or move him not finally to convert the impenitent.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 439; IA PDF page 497; BSB canvas 491]

*4. If the will to concur is virtually conditional, then God wills to concur
toward parricides, blasphemies, and that flaming hatred of GOD with which the
demons seethe, if wicked human beings and demons so will. GOD must then be said
virtually to will such crimes. Hence God will have no complacency in the
obedience of demons and of human beings who have fallen into the unpardonable
blasphemy against the Holy Spirit unless concurrence toward good acts is held
ready by GOD for those demons and for people judicially condemned for
blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. 5. Finally, if GOD wills to concur if we
will him to concur, he wills us to will if we will; and God will say: “I will
love of myself, if there is no absence of love,” and, “I will hatred of myself
and the absence of love, provided there is no hatred of myself and love of
myself is absent.” This is absurd.

3. Arriaga replies: *It is indeed ridiculous to will or not will an object
dependently upon that very object, as if someone willed this to exist provided
that it exists, as it does: “I will my concurrence to exist if my concurrence
exists.” But this conditional statement—“I will a free act of the creature if
there is no free omission”—is not ridiculous. For there is a middle state,
namely, neither the existence of a free act nor the existence of a free
omission, if GOD’s concurrence does not occur.*

[Marginal reference: At the same place, no. 96.]

[Marginal note: The Jesuits teach that God disjunctively or conditionally
wills the creature’s free act as it shall seem good to created freedom, whether
with or without malice—which is contradictory.]

I reply: This is to trifle in a serious matter, for here GOD wills and does not
will the object dependently upon the object. The statement, “I will the
creature’s free act if there is no free omission,” is equivalent to, “I will
the creature’s free act to exist if the creature’s free act does not exist.”
For what else is freely omitting an act when GOD’s precept urges it than the
nonexistence of the creature’s free act? If God denies concurrence, the free
act must be omitted; and if GOD denies concurrence while being prepared to
give it if the person wills, or to deny it if the person wills not to act,
then from first to last this statement must be true in GOD: “I will the person
to will well if he wills well.” For according to our opponents GOD wills the
person to will well not absolutely but conditionally. Yet there is no condition
in God and none in the person except the person’s own free volition. I ask: on
what condition does GOD will love of himself? If the person loves, or wills to
determine his will to love GOD? But these are the same, for my freely
determining my will to love GOD is my freely loving GOD. Moreover, the free
non-omission-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 440; IA PDF page 498; BSB canvas 492]

*cannot arise from the absence of divine concurrence, because, as they teach,
GOD’s concurrence is held ready and exposed to the power of created free will.

2. Arriaga replies: *I say that willing an object disjunctively is one thing,
and conditionally another. Willing conditionally signifies that GOD wills one
part determinately while awaiting some condition, upon whose being posited the
thing loved is posited. But willing disjunctively only says that GOD of himself
would be content with either alternative, or compels toward neither, or
absolutely prefers neither to the other. It is not necessary that this be in
GOD: “I will sin, the creature’s determination having been foreseen.” But if
GOD’s willing to concur is God’s conditionally willing Judas’s volition, this
will necessarily be true in GOD: “I will Judas’s sin if he wills to sin.”*

I reply: 1. Disjunctive and conditional volition are not mutually opposed, so
our opponents must teach that both are in GOD. For they teach that God wills
us to will, provided we will, and this is a conditional volition; and he does
not will us to will if we do not will, but either wills to concur or not to
concur as seems good to us, and this is a disjunctive volition. 2. Here he
defines disjunctive volition as that *which is content with either alternative,
compels toward neither, and absolutely prefers neither to the other.* But the
Jesuits will deny that GOD’s will is disjunctive in this manner, since they
teach that GOD concurs toward evil acts with the highest repugnance, inclines
us to will well, abhors even the material acts of sin, is by no means content
with them, and far prefers good acts to evil. 3. The Jesuit—and with him Diego
Ruiz—denies this to be in GOD: “I will sin, the creature’s determination having
been foreseen.” For, as he teaches, *that expression would be unworthy of GOD,
because GOD can no more will sin after it exists than, if said of us, it would
be approving and rejoicing in sin after it exists.* It follows, according to
them, that God wills malice neither absolutely nor conditionally. Yet if God’s
absolutely willing to concur toward the material act of sin makes God will
malice, as they argue against us, then by equal reasoning God’s conditionally
willing to concur toward the act of sin will make him will the sin’s malice,
at least conditionally and on the hypothesis that sin exists. 4. The Arminians
teach that God did not will absolutely that the Jews crucify CHRIST, but only-*

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, disputation 10, physical section 2, subject 3,
no. 57.]

[Marginal note: According to the Jesuits, God wills sin conditionally.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 441; IA PDF page 499; BSB canvas 493]

*after foreseeing the Jews’ malice. But what, I ask, can be the other part of
the distinction unless they say that God conditionally willed the Jews to
crucify Christ? 5. They teach that it is the office of the first cause to
concur toward the act of sin after the depraved will has once determined
itself toward the act. Therefore God rejoices in his concurrence now
determined by the depraved will, and consequently in the act. But if God
rejoices in the act, he also rejoices in the malice which follows the act. For
this is their consequence: “Whoever wills the act wills the malice inseparably
annexed to the act.”

4. Arriaga: *GOD wills both concurrence and sin inefficaciously, as he also
wills concurrence toward a good work. Thus GOD’s willing to apply his
omnipotence is nothing but his having a pure, inefficacious volition to concur
or not concur with the creature. GOD indeed wills a good work inefficaciously,
but in such a way that, as far as depends on him, he wishes and desires it. Yet
he wills to concur toward sin in such a way that he does not will it except
because he cannot otherwise fulfill the office of the universal cause. Sin is
therefore simply involuntary to him, like casting merchandise into the sea.*

[Marginal reference: Subject 3, at the same place, no. 87.]

I reply: 1. GOD’s inefficaciously willing a good work is pure Socinianism—the
teaching of those who contend that God procures the obedience and conversion
of the elect only by bare desire and empty wish. Thus Vorstius and the
Arminians in the Hague Conference teach that, physically speaking, GOD effects
nothing in us of himself, but declares only what is to be done; that GOD wishes
and desires rather than necessarily and absolutely wills to effect our
conversion, godliness, and perseverance. In this way we petty mortals will the
conversion of people by wishes and desires as intensely and strongly as God
himself does. What could have been devised more unworthy of God’s dominion in
converting the elect, whether by Pelagius long ago or the impure Socinus today?
2. If God concurs unwillingly toward the material acts of sin, he performs the
office of first cause as unwillingly as a merchant terrified by shipwreck
casts his goods into the sea. Yet God no more unwillingly performs the part of
first cause than he unwillingly concurs with the moving sun, the ebbing sea,
the sprouting plant, the flourishing trees, or the burning fire. For God
exercises providence in the world most freely, not unwillingly. But in this
way GOD’s decrees are imagined loose and slippery.

[Marginal note: GOD’s inefficaciously willing the conversion and obedience of
the elect is Vorstianism and Socinianism.]

[Marginal reference: Vorstius, *On the State of GOD*, at note 6.]

5. Suárez comes forward, fiercely assailing this predetermination. I do not
address the arguments he has, since what has been brought forward so far has
already been carried away.-*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, opuscule, book 2, *On Concurrence*, chapter 2,
argument 4, § 18.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 442; IA leaf missing; BSB canvas 494]

*If GOD predetermines toward the material element of sin, it follows that the
first root and cause to which the depraved human will must be reduced is the
efficacy of the divine will. For the proximate root of the will’s entire
consent and conversion toward a mutable good is the human will itself; but
according to this opinion GOD is the first root of that consent. Therefore—.*
I reply: This proves nothing but that God is the first cause of the material
act, not of sin. The argument proves our thesis rather than disproving it. For
the deviant will is the cause of the malice which is in the positive conversion
to a mutable good, while GOD is neither its first cause nor any cause at all.
The Jesuit’s other consequences have already been discussed often.

[Marginal reference: Chapter 2, no. 10.]

Suárez: *Perhaps they will say that GOD is not the cause of sin because he does
not sin in thus determining the human will, since he is neither subject to a
law so as to be able to sin nor at variance with his own right reason. But
even if someone said that GOD is not the author of sin by sinning himself, but
only by causing another to sin, he would fall into heresy.* I reply: GOD’s
determining toward the material element of sin is a good act of the first cause
and does not conflict with right reason. 2. If God causes another to sin, he
cannot fail to be the author of sin. But we openly teach that GOD is not the
author of sin either by sinning or by causing another to sin. Indeed, the
Jesuit acquits us of this charge: *The heretics know well,* he says, *that GOD
can neither intend the formal element of sin nor incline the human will to
intend it.*

[Marginal reference: Book 1, *On Concurrence*, chapter 2, no. 5, argument 6.]

*It is not enough to say that GOD determines toward the material but not the
formal element of sin. For GOD prohibits not only the formality of sin, but
the act itself and the conversion toward a mutable good. Although it is true
that GOD prohibits and punishes that conversion on account of the formal
element, he nevertheless really prohibits the conversion itself, because it
is really evil, though denominatively, by the deformity it brings with it.*

[Marginal note: The Jesuits say, triflingly and foolishly, that GOD prohibits
only the act, but also the malice.]

I reply: If it is not enough to say that God predetermines toward the material
but not the formal, neither is it enough to say what the Jesuits and their
Arminian children say: that God causes by indifferent concurrence the material
act, but not the formal element of sin. For God is thus, as the Jesuit holds,
the cause of an act prohibited by law. 2. Nothing could be more empty or
unlearned than saying that GOD prohibits not only the formality of sin, but
also the act itself. For: 1. It is a merely identical tautology. For GOD’s-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 443; IA PDF page 500; BSB canvas 495]

*prohibiting an act insofar as he willed by his own decree that it be contrary
to his free will—that is, the law—is formally prohibiting an evil act.
Therefore, if God prohibits both the act and its malice, the Jesuit says
nothing but that GOD prohibits not only the formality of sin but also the
formality of sin. 3. The Jesuit holds that the act as distinguished from its
malice is prohibited. Malice, then, will be in the act as distinguished from
lawlessness, and in that act there will again be another malice, and so on to
infinity. And if both the act and its deformity are prohibited, even in every
most simple sin there will be two prohibitions and two illegalities, and every
numerically one sin will be two sins. 4. The bare act as distinguished from
lawlessness is a truly positive being, essentially dependent upon the first
being both in coming to be and in accomplished existence. Hence the act as
such is not prohibited by God. Nor is the act of eating the fruit of the tree
of knowledge forbidden by God because it is evil in itself prior to the divine
prohibition, but because God in his free and wise will saw fit to prohibit the
act. Thus the formal foundation of malice in every positive act arises from
GOD’s act of prohibiting it, and indeed freely, since God could either not have
prohibited the act or even commanded it. Therefore God does not prohibit the
act simply, but prohibits it insofar as it appeared inexpedient here and now
to GOD’s free will. In this manner God formally prohibits an evil act which
derives its evil character from the prohibition—or, what is the same, an act
freely judged and reckoned inexpedient by God. Every ground of prohibition is
therefore from a free act of the divine will, for God could have commanded the
act of eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge if it had so pleased him. The
Jesuit admits this when he asserts that the action itself is denominatively
evil. Thus God’s prohibition will be the same with respect to both the act and
its malice, because the malice itself results from the act of divine
prohibition. 5. It is consequently false that GOD punishes conversion toward
a mutable good by reason of the formal element and prohibits the conversion
itself. God prohibits the act for one reason and punishes it for another. He
does not prohibit the act formally because it was already an evil act prior to
the prohibition; on the contrary, the act is evil because it is prohibited.
But God punishes the act because it is evil; it is not formally evil because
it is punished, but is evil before every punishment.

7. It follows, Suárez says, that *if GOD predetermines toward the material
element of sin,-*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, at the same place, no. 21.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 444; IA PDF page 501; BSB canvas 496]

*he not only permits but wills and ordains that sin occur; but the consequent
is heretical. Therefore, etc.* He proves the consequence because *GOD not only
willed to permit Judas to betray Christ, but determinately willed it before
he foreknew that Judas would will it; for Judas willed it because GOD willed
him to will it.* I reply: 1. It follows very well that God willed and ordained
sin to occur while permitting rather than approving it. What is absurd in
that? Scripture expressly asserts it, Acts 4:27–28. 2. If it is evil for GOD
to will sin, God can will sin neither determinately nor indeterminately. Why,
then, does the Jesuit charge us as absurd with saying that GOD willed Judas to
betray Christ, and willed it determinately and before he foreknew Judas would
will it, unless he implies that their opinion is that GOD indeterminately
willed Judas to betray Christ and did so after foreknowing that Judas would
will it? But if it is evil to will sin, it is just as evil for God to will it
indeterminately as determinately. 3. If GOD’s foreknowledge is later than
Christ’s betrayal—which betrayal by Judas is prior in nature to its being
willed and ordained, as the Jesuit says—then CHRIST’s betrayal is future prior
in nature to God’s willing to permit it. Before the sin is permitted by God,
it is already future, because the sin’s futurition precedes every will of God,
and therefore also his permissive will. But this is a providence devoid of
Christian consolation, if all the persecutions of tyrants are future before
every act of the divine will. How wretched, then, are all of us whom the world
persecutes and troubles! It is vain thus to seek material for comfort and rest
in the wise and just divine will. 4. It accords with truly Christian comforts
that the world hates, betrays, kills, tortures, and defames us because our
heavenly Father willed us to drink such a cup. But if the Jesuit denies that
God’s will is the positive cause of every volition of Judas by which he willed
to betray the Savior, there will be many positive beings of which God, the
first being, is not the author. Both sound philosophy and theology reject this
absurdity.

8. The predeterminators, Suárez says, *say that GOD preordained acts as acts,
permitted their deformity, and in this permission knew that sin would occur;
but the first is contrary to divine goodness, and the second is not
consistently stated. For something’s being permitted to someone means that it
is so committed to his power and will that at his own choice he can will or do
it or its opposite.-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 445; IA PDF page 502; BSB canvas 497]

*Hence permission is sometimes opposed to a precept, or excludes one; in that
case to permit something is neither to command nor prohibit it. At other times
permission excludes punishment; then something is said to be permitted which
is neither punished nor rewarded. Thus, with respect to GOD’s will, what is
permitted is what by virtue of that will is neither absolutely and
efficaciously ordained to occur, nor hindered from occurring, nor denied the
influence necessary for it to occur. Therefore permission has no place where
there is predetermination toward the act, because not even by absolute power
can GOD prevent malice from following an act toward which the will is
predetermined by GOD. Thus it is not permission except in a highly improper
sense, as if you said that someone who applies water to fire permits it to lose
its coldness.*

I reply: 1. Our theologians say that God preordained the malice itself to occur
while he permitted it, and in this decree knew that the sin would be future.
2. Either it accords with God’s goodness to preordain acts as acts, or it does
not. But that acts should occur without God’s preordaining them is contrary to
God’s providence. Our opponents think they have acquitted themselves splendidly
when they say that GOD conditionally preordains acts to occur—namely, after
they are going to be, or, if they are going to be, God preordains that they
will be. Does this accord with God’s goodness? 3. Permitting something means
committing it to someone’s will so that at his own choice he can will or
perform it or its opposite—but always dependently upon God’s predetermination.
Otherwise God commits the fortune of all human beings and angels to their own
choice, so that they can will or perform either alternative at their pleasure.
4. The Jesuit wrongly holds that something is permitted in only two ways: (1)
when it is neither commanded nor prohibited; (2) when it is neither punished
nor rewarded. On that account all the actions of brute animals and all natural
actions—such as the heating of fire, the illumination of the sun, and the
motions of heavy and light things—are permitted, since they are neither
commanded nor prohibited by GOD’s law, and neither punished nor rewarded. But
human beings and devils are permitted to sin in a far different way. 5. His
claim that God wills the permitted thing not to occur by GOD’s efficacious
preordination contradicts his own statement elsewhere: *Concerning effects of
the latter kind, however, it is not inappropriate for them to be predefined
even before the foreknowledge of the act of sin.* Yet the Jesuit holds that
GOD’s permission intervenes in all free acts, without which our opponents
believe freedom cannot be kept safe and sound. He contradicts the truth, for
Scripture says that the crucifixion of the Son of GOD was from GOD’s definite
counsel, and therefore from his efficacious-*

[Marginal reference: At the same place, chapter 4, reply to objection 4, no.
15.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 446; IA PDF page 503; BSB canvas 498]

*ordination and decree. Although we teach that all sins occur from God’s
efficacious ordination and decree, we reject as execrable blasphemy that they
flow from a decree which effects sin, such that Peter’s confession and Judas’s
betrayal are equally works of the divine will, as the Tridentine Fathers with
an evil conscience most unjustly slander us. Efficacious and effective grace,
and efficacious and effective medicine, may differ little; nevertheless, in
this kind of causation an efficacious decree and an effective decree differ as
much as a cause of sin and something which is not a cause of sin. For an
efficacious decree concerning sin—once posited, sin infallibly follows—is not
a decree effecting sin. If GOD’s efficacious decree does not remove freedom,
as it certainly does not, even by our opponents’ admission, then sin flows from
no causal necessity out of an effective decree, nor is an efficacious decree a
cause of sin. 6. The question is not about the necessary result of malice from
an act, on the supposition that the act is forbidden. Anyone who raises the
question whether GOD by absolute power can prevent malice from following the
act in this case invents a question wholly equal to whether God by absolute
power can prevent the same from being the same, or prevent a human being from
being a human being yet rational. GOD’s gracious prevention of sin is displayed
not by preventing such a result of malice from the existence of a forbidden
act, but in the motion of efficacious grace by which the forbidden act would
not occur and the person would holily abstain from it.

10. Suárez: *It is clear by what will GOD confers general concurrence toward an
evil act: not by a promoting will, nor by an absolute will by which he wills
such an act to exist, but by a quasi-general will by which he wills to concur
toward that act and wills the act to exist—not absolutely, but under the tacit
condition that free choice applies or determines itself to effecting it. Yet
the meaning is not that GOD’s will is concerned only confusedly with the acts
of the created will, as though he willed to concur generally toward its acts.
Rather, just as he sees everything most distinctly, by his will he either
disposes or permits every particular. Thus GOD wills to concur toward this
particular act, even if it will be evil, when the person wills it. This will is
called general because, as far as depends on GOD, he does not limit his
concurrence to that act, but offers it to other good or evil acts, even in
particular, which the human will could have at the same moment-*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, opuscule *On Grace*, book 2, *On Concurrence*,
chapter 3, § 17.]

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 447; IA PDF page 504; BSB canvas 499]

*if it wished. Nor do I approve what some say: that at this instant GOD in fact
has no will to concur with this created free agent except toward this evil act
which he foreknows the created will is going to perform. For if GOD did not in
fact offer his concurrence toward other acts, those acts would absolutely not
be in the person’s proximate power to effect, and so he would not be free to
effect them.*

I reply: 1. Here we have God willing the evil act, and therefore sin, but under
an implied condition. Yet if God can conditionally will sin, then he wills sin.
2. It cannot be avoided that here there is a confused will in GOD, if there is
in him a will to concur toward good and evil acts at one and the same instant,
as the Jesuit imagines. 3. If this will is called general because GOD, as far
as depends on him, does not limit his concurrence to this act but offers it
toward good and evil acts, then God, as far as depends on him, no more limits
his concurrence to Peter’s act of confessing CHRIST, Matthew 26, than to the
act of not confessing him; nor does he intend supernatural acts by his
concurrence more than the most wicked crimes. 4. If GOD has a will to concur
with a created agent toward an act which he foreknows that the will will never
perform, lest freedom be injured, as the Jesuit says, then GOD’s foreknowledge
will conflict with freedom of the will—which, after Cicero, the impure Socinus
and Smalcius taught. 5. How foolish is a will to effect what it knows it will
never effect! Is something to be attributed to God which would not occur in a
prudent man?

11. Suárez, at the same place: *From these points the difference between this
will to concur and the predetermining will can easily be understood. The first
difference is that, in the will to concur in the manner described, GOD effects
nothing by himself alone concerning the act of sin. On this ground sin cannot
be attributed to him as author. But by the predetermining will GOD by himself
alone would effect in our will the determination toward an act of sin and the
way toward it, by reason of which the act of sin and whatever is joined with it
would deservedly be attributed to GOD. For if that predetermination is
something real and physical inhering in the will as a weight, it is like a
certain gravity dragging the will down into the depth of sin. Sin would
therefore deservedly be attributed to God, who gave such an impetus, as motion
is attributed to the generator of heavy things and the upward motion of a
stone to the one who throws it. But if that predetermination is not some prior
thing inhering in the will, but-*

*GOD’s efficacious motion, then whatever follows from that motion must be
attributed to GOD even more immediately, just as whatever follows from the
motion of the arm is attributed to the will, although it impresses nothing
prior but moves effectively by natural subjection.*

I reply: 1. If by Jesuit universal concurrence God effects nothing by himself
alone concerning the act of sin by which the act could be attributed to him as
author, then by equal reasoning he effects nothing by himself alone in good
acts by which those acts could be attributed to him as author, for the
concurrence is equal in each case. Indeed, since God supplies this concurrence
to all second causes, and does not act alone, neither gusts of wind, the rising
of the sun, rain, snow, nor any natural effects could be attributed to God as
author. 2. Consequently a human being, determining his own will by himself
alone, believes and repents. For since God’s concurrence is indifferent, it
effects nothing by itself alone in the physical act, and so the human will
will effect the supernatural character in the acts. 3. If God by himself
alone effects nothing whereby the entitative act of sin could be attributed to
him, I ask whether, by the intervention of free choice though not by himself
alone, he effects anything whereby the act of sin could be attributed to him
as author. God, of course, is not the sole author of sin but an efficient joint
cause with free choice. But if God does nothing here, why those words, “GOD
does nothing by himself alone”? If GOD effects nothing by himself alone and
does nothing by the intervention of free choice whereby the act of sin could
be attributed to him as author, there will be no divine efficiency here, no
act of causing. But that is exceedingly impious and unjust toward providence.
Whatever they say, if the Jesuit-Arminian doctrines stand, God will be either
the cause or the joint cause of sin. 4. I would not buy the question whether
God’s predetermination posits something real and physical after the manner of a
permanent quality even for a rotten nut. Yet this consequence is chimerical
and empty: “GOD’s predetermination toward an act of sin is a weight or impetus
inhering in the will; therefore if God predetermines the will toward an act of
sin, he will be the author of sin.” For the patrons of that opinion imagined a
weight tempered to the character and nature of freedom. This inference—God is
the author of the form, therefore he is the author of everything which follows
the form—is a physical axiom sufficiently valid in causes and effects in
themselves, but by no means holds in moral things. God is the author of free
choice, but it in no way follows that God is the author of the sin which flows
from free choice. 5. Predetermination-*

[Chapter XXVII, Part A; printed page 449; IA PDF page 506; BSB canvas 501]

*is a middle between the first act and second act of a free agent. Although
defining its mode would be beyond my powers, it is nevertheless something like
what a saw has from one who moves it, a staff from one who shakes and lifts it,
or a pen which is in potency to write has from a writer’s hand while actually
writing. I assert this in conformity with Scripture, Isaiah 10: *Assyria is the
rod of my fury.* Accordingly, an act may be attributed to God as principal
Agent inflicting evil in the class of distressing and penal evil. But it can no
more be attributed to God under the character of a morally deficient cause
than deformed and shaggy little letters impressed upon the paper are justly
imputed to a skilled and elegant writer. 6. Although GOD’s predetermination is
an efficacious motion toward an act, nevertheless the malice which follows the
act accidentally and in relation to GOD’s law ought not be attributed to GOD
in the same way that the arm’s motion is attributed to the will, to which the
arm is subject by natural subjection. 1. The arm of itself lacks reason and
freedom and is moved by the command of the will alone, but the created will is
so moved and premoved by God that it moves freely from its inward elective
power. 2. For the same reason, the arm is not subject to one law and the will
to another, but each is subject to one and the same law—the will properly and
the arm improperly. This is therefore no wonder. But God is above every law
given to the creature, while the human will is subject to GOD’s law. Thus all
the guilt in the arm is from the will which moves the arm, while the guilt in
the human will is from the human will alone, which is subject to law.
Consequently the motion of the created will toward evil is attributed to God
neither immediately, mediately, nor more immediately than it is to the created
will subject to law. For the argument supposes what is false—namely, that our
will is premoved by God like an inanimate instrument devoid of reason and
freedom.
