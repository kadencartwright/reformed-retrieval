QUESTION V.

Are the sacraments only marks and tokens of our profession? Or are they also
signs and seals of God’s grace concerning the remission of sins and
regeneration by the Spirit? We affirm the latter against the Socinians and
papists.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the Socinians.]

I. Our dispute is with the Socinians and papists. The Socinians indeed
acknowledge that the sacraments are distinguishing signs, or tokens and marks
of profession, by which we are distinguished from unbelievers. But they deny
that they can be called seals of God’s grace concerning the remission of sins
and regeneration. The *Racovian Catechism*, chapter 4 on baptism, question 1,
says that “baptism with water” is “an external rite by which people coming to
the Christian religion either from Judaism or from paganism openly professed
that they acknowledged Christ as their Lord.” Socinus had asserted this before
in his *Disputation on Baptism*. Völkel, book 4, chapter 22, denies that “the
Holy Supper is properly designated by the name sacrament, since,” as he says,
“it neither bestows nor seals any grace of God, but represents the sealing of
grace already bestowed. It was therefore instituted so that God’s kindness
itself might be celebrated with solemn commemoration and thanksgiving.”

[Marginalia: The opinion of the papists.]

II. The papists also deny that the sacraments possess a sealing power and are
seals of the divine promises. Bellarmine labors to prove this in book 1 *On the
Sacraments*, chapter 14: “The eighth clause, that a sacrament seals a promise
like a seal in order to arouse and nourish faith, is false and must be
diligently refuted.” He attempts to accomplish this in the following chapter,
and Becanus joins him in chapter 1 *On the Sacraments*, questions 4 and 5.
Gregory of Valencia, however, departs from them in tome 4, disputation 3,
question 3, page 1: “We acknowledge that the sacraments of the new law are in
some manner signs or seals of the divine promise.” Tirinius agrees with him in
controversy 18 *On the Sacraments*, for he holds that “a sacrament is not only
a distinguishing sign of the Christian people, a sign signifying
righteousness, and a sign sealing the divine promises, but also a sign causing
and effecting righteousness, and so forth.”

[Marginalia: And the opinion of the orthodox.]

III. The orthodox do not deny that the sacraments are distinguishing signs of
Christians and tokens of profession. But against the Socinians they deny that
the sacraments must be restricted to this. Besides this less principal use,
they judge that the primary use is not, indeed, that the sacraments are signs
causing and effecting righteousness, which we will treat later against the
papists, but that they are sealing and practical signs, which are pledges of
God’s grace and in their own sense exhibit the thing signified. To this end
they are called seals, with the term borrowed from human custom and commerce,
in which seals are used extensively to establish credibility. They are
customarily applied to covenants for their confirmation and to the charters

[1847 vol. III printed p. 300 | IA leaf 334 | PDF p. 334]

of princes for their certification, so that it may be evident that they are
true and authentic. Thus God willed to apply the sacraments to the word of the
promise of grace, like seals upon that heavenly charter, so that no one might
doubt its truth and certainty.

[Marginalia: The sacraments are proved to be seals. 1. From Romans 4:11.]

IV. (1) The first argument is drawn from Paul’s express words in Romans 4:11,
where he calls circumcision “a sign and seal of the righteousness of faith.”
He says not only that it is a *sign*, which has a simple signifying power, but
also a *seal*, which has the further power of sealing. It is a seal of the
covenant, of which it was the sign (Gen. 17:11), and of the promise of grace
concerning the righteousness of faith, which was included in the blessing by
which all the nations of the earth were to be blessed in the blessed seed. As
Jehovah our Righteousness, that seed was to bring the righteousness of the
ages, as Paul explains (Gal. 3:3; 2 Cor. 5:21). Since the principle of
circumcision and that of the other sacraments is the same, we rightly argue
from it to the others, for what is essential to one sacrament must belong to
the others. Although things belonging to one species are not essential to the
whole genus, nevertheless what is essential to one individual, such as
circumcision under the specific character of sacraments, must be attributed to
the whole species.

V. Nor can it be objected that this is indeed said about Abraham but cannot be
transferred to others by the same principle. It is attributed to Abraham in
such a way that it also pertains to others. This is both because Abraham is
considered here as the father of believers and is proposed to us as an example,
so that what was fulfilled in him may also be fulfilled in others, and because
the righteousness of faith and the promises of grace, of which circumcision is
the seal, are common to all and not peculiar to Abraham (Rom. 4:12; Gal. 3:9,
16). I acknowledge that the promise concerning Abraham’s fatherhood—that he
would be the father of many nations—is also included here. But circumcision
was not simply and properly the seal of that promise; it was the seal of the
righteousness of faith, which is common to all believers. Nor should it be
added that Paul distinguishes the seal from the sign and that circumcision was
a sign for all but a seal for Abraham. A seal is distinguished from a sign
only as an explanation from the thing explained, that is, as such a sign as is
also a seal, so that it may be distinguished from other signs that are indeed
signs but not seals.

[Marginalia: 2. From the parallel with other signs.]

VI. (2) The sacraments are related to the promises of grace in the same way as
other signs and seals added to a promise, which sealed it and rendered it more
fully attested. Thus the rainbow was related to the promise that the flood
would not be sent again—not merely to assist memory concerning the past flood,
as Bellarmine would have it, but to confirm faith that no flood would occur
again (Gen. 9:11–12). Gideon’s fleece was related to the future deliverance of
Israel (Judg. 6:37); the burning coal that touched Isaiah’s lips, to the promise
of God’s grace in the exercise of his calling (Isa. 6); and the washing of feet
(John 13), to the cleansing of the apostles. But all these signs and others of
this kind were related to God’s promises not only by signifying them but also
by confirming and sealing them.

[Marginalia: 3. From the interchange of names.]

VII. (3) The names, properties, and effects of the thing signified are given to
the signs. No other reason can be given for this predication except the
principle of sealing. Thus circumcision is given the name *covenant* (Gen.
17:11); the lamb, the name *Passover* (Exod. 12); the manna and water from the
rock, the names *spiritual food and drink* and *Christ* (1 Cor. 10:4); baptism,
the names *washing of regeneration* and *remission of sins* (Titus 3:5; Acts
22:16); and the bread, the name *the body of Christ* (Matt. 26:26; 1 Cor.
10:16).

[Marginalia: 4. From the fact that the sacraments are signs of the covenant.]

VIII. (4) The sacraments are signs of the covenant. But they cannot be signs
of the covenant except for its confirmation and the ratification of the
promises made on both sides, both on God’s part and on the part of human
beings. This is confirmed by the fact that they have the character of an oath,
by which God binds himself to the fulfillment of the promises. But an oath
seals one’s promises (Heb. 6:16–17).

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

IX. Although the Word and the Holy Spirit testify concerning God’s grace and
also seal it in their own manner, it does not follow that this cannot also
belong to the sacraments. These are subordinate, not opposed. The effect is
common to both causes, but the manner of producing it is different: there it
is verbal, here symbolic; there through hearing, here through sight and the
other senses. The Spirit is the internal seal, which seals by his own nature,
but the sacraments are external seals, which seal only by institution. The
Spirit is the principal and effective cause, which uses the Word and the
sacraments as instruments.

X. Although nothing is greater and more effective in itself than God’s Word,
it does not follow that it cannot be confirmed for our sake through the
sacraments. Nothing is thereby taken from the objective certainty of the
promises, since, on the contrary, the sacraments themselves draw all their
power and efficacy from God’s Word. But much is thereby added to subjective
certainty, insofar as our faith rests and is sustained upon this support, so to
speak. The Word is not confirmed in itself through the sacraments, but through
the use of the sacraments we are confirmed concerning the promises. Although
they are most certain in themselves, they are nevertheless often called into
doubt by us. The sacraments are therefore called seals not because the
certainty of the Word depends upon them, but because through them God impresses
promises that are not doubtful in themselves more strongly and affects the
mind with a sharper sensation.

XI. Although the sacraments are seals, it does not follow that their sealing
is often false, namely with respect to hypocrites and unbelievers. It is no
more false than the preaching of the Word, which is also directed to them,
because this does not occur absolutely, but conditionally, on the supposition
of the recipient’s faith and in lawful use. Otherwise no sealing occurs apart
from a stated condition. Thus it is one thing for the sacraments to seal grace
in a signified act by God’s institution; it is another for them to do so in an
exercised act and with respect to the person receiving them. The sacraments
seal nothing to hypocrites and unbelievers because the required condition is
lacking, but they do not cease to possess such power in a signified act by
God’s ordinance. Hypocrites and the ungodly therefore obtain neither a promise
nor the sealing of a promise in the use—or rather abuse—of the sacraments, but
contract a more grievous guilt from it. This does not overthrow the proper and
primary end of the sacraments. Otherwise neither would the gospel be a
testimony of saving grace, nor would Christ himself have to be regarded as the
author of life, since the gospel is to many a savor of death unto death and
Christ is to many a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense.

XII. When the sacraments are said to seal God’s promises, they need not be more
known and certain than the promises themselves in themselves. They need only
be so with respect to us and insofar as they are considered not in opposition
to the word of promise, but in composition with it. The sense is that the Word
sealed with seals is more known and certain with respect to us than the Word
alone, just as a charter to which a seal has been applied is more known and
certain than one that lacks it.

XIII. It is one thing to seal our justification and calling to us on our part;
it is another to confirm it on God’s part. Good works can be seals and
testimonies of our justification on our part, but the sacraments are seals on
God’s part. The former are fruits of our faith; the latter are seals of the
divine promises. Here, however, the question does not concern testimonies of

[1847 vol. III printed p. 302 | IA leaf 336 | PDF p. 336]

our faith, whether toward ourselves or toward our neighbor, but the testimony
of God’s grace toward us. Nor must these be opposed, but joined together.

XIV. If the sacraments are said to confirm the promises, this is not because
they teach that there are divine promises or because we believe the sacraments
more than the promises. Rather, it is so that we may believe both more, namely
the promises and sacraments together. Just as two bonds are said to be
stronger than one and two witnesses more certain than one alone, so the
obligation of the promise and the pledge of the sacraments are far more
powerful for confirming faith than either one separately. This is because the
intellect is moved directly through the words and through the senses in the
sacraments.

XV. It is one thing for the sacraments to be marks and tokens of our
profession, which we concede; it is another for them to be only or chiefly and
primarily such, which we deny, since they can have several other ends, as was
said before.

XVI. Although faith is required beforehand for the use of a sacrament, nothing
prevents it from being confirmed through the sacrament. Sight is required for
the use of spectacles and yet is strengthened through them. In the same way,
the faith that receives the sacraments can grow and be increased through them.
