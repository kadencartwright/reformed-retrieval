SUCCINCT AND CLEAR COMMENTARIES ON THE PROVERBS OF SOLOMON:

CHAPTER ONE.

Verse 1. The Proverbs of Solomon, son of David, king of the Israelites.

In the book of Proverbs Solomon, like a father, instructs the godly as his
children in the law of God in a familiar manner, adapting both its promises
and its threats to individuals, so as to render them wise and prudent in all
the affairs of this life and throughout the course of their whole life.

[Analytical diagram]

- This book contains two things:
  - a preface extending to chapter 10;
  - the remainder of the book, as its body, extending to the end.
- In the preface are considered:
  - its title, or the summary of the preface, in the first six verses;
  - a fuller treatment of the same through chapter 10.
- In the title the teaching of this book is commended from:
  - its subject matter, namely παροιμίαις, or sayings full of weight;
  - its cause:
    - its efficient author in:
      - the attached name, Solomon;
      - the relation, son of David, king of Israel;
    - its end:
      - knowledge:
        - wisdom;
        - instruction;
      - understanding the words of the prudent, verse 6.

The subject matter is commended because the whole book, every bit of it,
consists of nothing but weighty and illustrious maxims. Whereas books of other
people’s maxims (γνῶμαι) contain great sayings here and there, commended to the
reader as especially notable by marks placed in the book’s margin, this book
contains nothing that is not illustrious. Indeed, although other books of
Scripture are filled with weighty maxims, this book, by a certain singular
manner and, as it were, by a fuller and more densely packed measure, has
deservedly carried off this glorious title. Other books, although dictated by
the Holy Spirit and containing the most perfect wisdom and knowledge of the
subjects they set before themselves, nevertheless rarely allow the wisdom and
knowledge transmitted in them to be gathered and perceived except from the
context and continuous sequence of many verses. Thus, if you often stop at the
second, third, or even fourth verse, you will obtain scant and very meager
fruit. But in this book—especially once chapter 10 is reached, from which and
the following chapters this book obtained the name “Proverbs”—a complete and
perfect doctrine is delivered in almost every verse, or rather every little
verse. It is next commended by this name because such dexterity is employed
within such succinct brevity that the individual doctrines comprised in
proverbs are illustrated by some comparison—of greater, less, equal, similar,
or dissimilar. Thus, according to the old proverb, every one of these maxims is
spoken twice because it is expressed with an added similitude or other
comparison. In addition, by the very name παροιμίαι the doctrines are declared
to be dignities, as it were, because they deserve to be the mistresses and
governors of our life. Thus, while the rest of Scripture is deservedly called
by the name wisdom (σοφία), this book is nevertheless to be marked with that
title and distinction in a special manner.

The author or writer is described, first, by the attached name “Solomon.” By
this one word the reader is reminded of that incomparable wisdom by which he
surpassed by many parasangs every mortal who ever lived or ever will live. Of
his wisdom this work—to say nothing of building the palace and administering
the commonwealth—was a work worth the labor and by far the clearest testimony.
Here God’s liberality and munificence come before us for consideration: he
transmitted this monument of his wisdom in Solomon to every future generation
and did not allow this great luminary to set. Instead, down to this very age he
made it stand still like the sun at Gibeon, so that we might thoroughly avenge
ourselves upon our spiritual enemies. How dreadful, then, will our judgment be
if we do not listen to Solomon, a prince within our gates, and his most choice
wisdom, when the Queen of Sheba traversed such a long journey at such expense
and with such equipment merely to hear wisdom that cannot at all be compared
with this—indeed, that in comparison with this should scarcely be called a
filing of wisdom! Here, therefore, let the things said in Psalm 120 be applied.
Observe also the blindness of those who abandon Solomon and flee to
Machiavelli to learn wisdom and statecraft.

The author is further described from a relation: he is the son of David, the
most powerful and undefeated king, the most noble and renowned prophet, and a
most holy and wise man, by whom Solomon was educated in the most cultivated,
liberal, and religious manner. Had he been the son of a cowherd, an uneducated
man, or a traitor, he would nevertheless have deserved a hearing from everyone
because of the heavenly wisdom with which he was endowed. But now that he
comes equipped also with those ornaments which people value most highly, what
excuse will those offer who either do not hear him or do not rest satisfied in
his words?

The next relation follows: he was king, and king of God’s people, while they
were not yet divided as afterward but united as one both in commonwealth and
Church. Therefore, just as he was a prophet and a prophet’s son, so he was a
king and a king’s son. We know that the sayings of kings, however mediocre,
are eagerly seized upon; nor have people been lacking who committed to letters
the playful and sportive conduct of kings in sayings and deeds when they were
still boys. How great a benefit, then, it is to be able to converse daily with
a most exalted king!

[1632 witness | PDF p. 15 | printed columns 5–6]

This also exposes the madness of the world, which abandons the most exalted
King Solomon and betakes itself to Machiavelli, the ignoble clerk of a petty
city—just as if a suitor, abandoning a most choice lady, were to descend to
ugly little maidservants. For besides the far more excellent kind of wisdom,
this pen is golden in nobility, whereas the other is leaden through ignobility.
What dreadful madness possesses human minds, that they despise Solomon and
dote upon Machiavelli—except that the former’s wisdom is divine and the
latter’s diabolical, the former’s sent down from heaven and the latter’s
summoned from hell!

Verse 2. To know wisdom and instruction; to understand sayings of prudence.

[Analytical diagram: There follows a description from the end, which is set
out briefly as twofold in this verse: חכמה — לדעת — מוסר — להבין אמרי בינה.]

By the word חכמה he understands an exact knowledge of divine and human things
insofar as they embrace the duty of a Christian person; and it appears to
indicate the “word of knowledge” (λόγον γνώσεως) of which the Apostle speaks in
1 Corinthians 12. By the word מוסר he understands the “word of instruction”
(λόγον παιδείας), or the application of that knowledge to the reformation of
the deformity within us. Thus what 2 Timothy 3 attributes to the whole of
Scripture appears here to be attributed to this book.

From these things we learn, first, that human beings are by nature foolish and
mad, understanding none of the things by which one proceeds to eternal life;
and, further, that they are so refractory and stubborn that, even when they
know what is right, they do not permit themselves to be recalled to the right
standard. Hence in Job they are compared to an untamed colt and in Jeremiah to
an untamed young bull.

Next, from the former of these ends we perceive that no teaching exists,
whether from the books of philosophers, the precepts of political writers, or
the sayings of the wise, that can instruct us. It is indeed true that there is
a great difference between the education of the children of nobler and
ignobler people. Yet it is no less true that both alike are unfit and untaught
for the kingdom of heaven; indeed, it often happens that the more they are
cultivated by civil discipline, the further removed they are from God’s
kingdom. “Not many noble,” and so forth, 1 Corinthians 1.

From the latter end we learn that Scripture not only teaches sound and right
things but further instructs people how to apply them to their own use.
Without this, knowledge would not merely fail to help but would hasten divine
vengeance. Therefore Scripture not only supplies weapons but teaches how they
are to be put on and fitted, and how one must walk in them. Otherwise people
versed in Scripture would resemble those who carry fire in flint without
knowing how to strike it out. Hence appears the great power of the pastor and
of ecclesiastical discipline, through whose assistance these things are
fitted.

Verse 3. To receive the instruction of understanding, justice, right, and
everything upright.

In the Hebrew manner he develops the latter part of the former member,
concerning application. He establishes two parts of it: one of the
understanding, the other of morals or the will. Here we first learn that every
thing taught must be applied to us, Job 40:5, last verse; next, that the
understanding must first be reformed so that we do whatever good we do with
understanding and not by a kind of childish imitation, as most people do. For
although we may do what is just, nevertheless, unless it proceeds from
understanding, it is of no value. Further, this reformation must be translated
into life. Here three things are posited: first, that every action be just;
next, that its manner and measure, which is called מישור, be observed; and,
finally, that equity be employed to moderate the strict precision (ἀκρίβειαν)
of the highest right.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 16 | printed columns 7–8]

Verse 4. To give prudence to the simple, knowledge and discretion to the
young man.

Verse 5. Let the wise man, hearing these things, add instruction, and let the
understanding man acquire skillful counsels.

Verse 6. To teach a proverb and its interpretation, the words of wise men and
their riddles.

He returns to the former part of the former member. Here there is an
anticipation whose objection is left unstated but can be supplied in this
manner: “But since these things both are imbued with Solomon’s most recondite
wisdom and are delivered rather obscurely because briefly, we who are
untrained are excluded from studying them.” The answer is added: on the
contrary, even the dull person—who, because he believes everything, is easily
deceived—becomes from these things circumspect and discerning; those who are
like children in sense and judgment become prudent through them.

Here the difference can be perceived between knowledge drawn from Scripture
and that sought from the fabric of the world. Upon the doors of Plato’s school
was inscribed, “Let no one ignorant of geometry enter” (μηδεὶς ἀγεωμέτρητος
εἰσίτω); but upon these doors of Solomon it is far otherwise, for here is
written: “Let the untrained, simple, uneducated, and foolish enter,” as we shall
see more clearly afterward. This knowledge is set before infants, 2 Timothy 3.
David also affirms in Psalm 119:98 that knowledge of Scripture made him wiser
than teachers, elders, and enemies. How much more, then, is the rest of
Scripture accessible to everyone if this more recondite part of it is made
known to everyone!

From the seventh verse to the twentieth.

Verse 7. Reverence for Jehovah is the head of knowledge; fools despise wisdom
and instruction.

Verse 8. Hear, my son, the instruction of your father, and do not abandon the
teaching of your mother.

Having set forth in verse seven the kind of wisdom that Solomon will pursue in
this book, he exhorts his reader to embrace it and flee the contrary folly.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - the designation of that kind of wisdom delivered in this book;
  - an exhortation to embrace it and flee and turn aside from its contrary.

By the former it is taught that not every kind of wisdom is imparted here, but
only that whose summit and head, as well as basis and foundation, is the fear
of God. For everything commanded in this book is either the true worship of
God or proceeds from true worship of God as its fountain and parent. By
synecdoche he puts the fear of God, of whatever kind it is, for his true
worship, because it is not only the chief part of it but keeps all the other
affections of the mind in their duty, as it were. Whatever thoughts, words, or
deeds flow from the corruption of our mind and will, it seizes, as it were, by
the neck and brings to examination, so that if they are insufficiently
agreeable it may restrain them from proceeding further. Thus Paul judges the
absence of the fear of God to be the fountain of every evil, Romans 3. Thus
Abraham, while lodging in Gerar, feared both the violation of his wife and his
own murder because he supposed the fear of God to be absent there, Genesis 20.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 17 | printed columns 9–10]

He proves that the fear of God is wisdom by the fact that fools or the ungodly
hold it in contempt. What he had said generally as חכמה in the former
half-verse, he explains in the latter as מוסר חכמה. Here he meets the tacit
objection that very many despise this wisdom; he replies that they are fools
or madmen, and therefore their judgment should not be valued highly. For who
would despise a pearl or gem because a fool prefers his fan to it? Yet the
ungodly are as unfit to judge heavenly things as buffoons are to discern the
affairs of this life.

[Analytical diagram: The other part follows, in the exhortation to embrace
this wisdom and flee its contrary.]

He commends this wisdom from its efficient instrument, namely parents, as is
clear from chapter 4, verse 1. For true doctrine proceeding from parents is
less suspect than what proceeds from masters: they will be thought to command
right things for their own advantage, such as not stealing, not eating or
drinking excessively. Add to this that tender age is especially fit for
learning these things. It appears, then, that this doctrine of wisdom must be
formed in the parents’ home, and therefore that parents must be fit to instruct
in it before they become parents or contract marriage. Hence that statement in
1 Corinthians 14: if a wife doubts anything, let her inquire of her husband.
Moreover, it is plainly a sign that the person who makes no progress in his
parents’ home has been given over to a reprobate mind. Nor should even the
mother’s work in this business be despised, since she can contribute very much
to her children’s godliness or ungodliness, 2 Timothy 3. Hence in the books of
Kings and Chronicles the mothers’ names are added for both godly and ungodly
kings, since they share in whatever praise belongs to their virtue and
whatever blame to their vices. This clause is also added because many are
found who profess to listen to their fathers’ admonitions yet reject their
mothers’. Certainly, if they heard their father out of a desire to obey God,
they would without doubt admit their mother in the same act as their guide,
since it is God who commands both the one and the other. Parents, however, are
not therefore to be obeyed unless they command things godly and just, 1 Samuel
22:17; John 2. This is further clear from chapter 6, verse 23, where what verse
20 calls the command and teaching of father and mother is called a lamp and a
light.

Verse 9. For they will be a graceful addition to your head and chains for your
neck.

This confirms the preceding exhortation from the attached ornament and
dignity that God will add to students of true wisdom. Thus elsewhere in
Proverbs wisdom is said to adorn a person’s face and make it shine. See
Christ’s example, Luke 2:52. Indeed, did this not happen also to Joseph and
Moses even among the profane Egyptians, so that they had more favor than any
other of Pharaoh’s courtiers? The reason is not only that this wisdom is
lovable in the course of common life and in people’s mutual duties toward one
another, but that God, who bends and turns human minds at his pleasure,
advances and attends it by his power and activity (ἐνεργείᾳ).

He illustrates this doctrine by an argument from similitude, because it is
like a chain placed around the neck and a crown with which the head is
encircled. For just as chains and diadems win favor in the eyes of the common
people for the person who wears them, so this doctrine does for one who
possesses it in the minds of the wise. For it causes both the counsels that
have their seat in the brain within the head to be sharper and better advised,
and speech, which flows from the throat, to be sweeter and more agreeable.
Thus we see that those endowed with true wisdom possess the highest nobility,
as persons descended from heaven and a heavenly Father, Psalm 16.

Verse 10. My son, if sinners wish to entice you, do not consent.

Verse 11. If they say, “Come with us; let us lie in wait for blood; let us hide
ourselves against the innocent with impunity.”

There follows a dissuasion, comprised generally (γενικῶς) in verse 10 and
explained more particularly in the remaining verses.

First it must be observed here that sinners will not be lacking who seek to
drive and thrust away from the fear of God those instructed in it. For although
the matter is proposed as contingent (ἐνδεχομένως), it is nevertheless the kind
of thing that befalls all the godly and from which no one who fears God is
exempt. Since hypocrites are mingled with the faithful in the Church, there is
no doubt that they will bring forth with all their might the wickedness they
carry shut up within, since even in private houses scarcely anyone is permitted
to be free from this temptation. So effective is the diligence of our
adversary the devil, who goes about like a roaring lion and ranges over the
earth seeking someone to devour.

Next it must be observed whom Solomon calls sinners. Someone may say, “Is
there anyone who is not a sinner?” There certainly is. There is indeed no one
who does not sin, but there are many who are not sinners. For God does not hear
sinners, Psalm 9, though he does hear those who sin. Thus the one who has not
stood in the way of sinners is called blessed in Psalm 1; this beatitude would
be nothing if everyone who sins were excluded from it. Who the sinners are,
then, is clear from this passage: they are those who both give themselves to
wickedness and become counselors to others to do the same, willingly rendering
their service to Satan. The godly, although they are sometimes driven by Satan
and caught unawares, nevertheless take earnest care that others not be caught
in the same snares of sin.

The first remedy against these frauds of Satan through ungodly people is to
determine completely in our mind that we will not listen to enchantments and
enticements of this kind. Hence the statements, “I said that I would observe,”
and so forth, and again, “I have sworn,” and so forth, Psalm 119; and Job 31:1,
“I made a covenant with my eyes.”

There follows the sinners’ enticing speech, explained more particularly.
First consider their exhortation, then the reasons upon which it rests. The
exhortation is: “Come with us; let us lie in wait,” and so forth. What could be
more shameful or unworthy to say? Yet Solomon does not seem here to imitate the
actual words of such sinners—for in order to entice others into participation
in the crime, they generally allege that those whom they intend to despoil are
excessively intent upon wealth, or that their ill-gotten things are deservedly
taken from them. Rather, he uses these words so that their speech may be
represented in vivid colors as it truly is, as if he said: Let the person whom
they will ambush be greedy, a plunderer, or a robber; nevertheless, in relation
to you he is innocent. Let us learn from this that whenever the wicked attempt
to soften the atrocity of a crime with gentler words, we should set it before
ourselves in terms as hateful as it deserves. Thus, when they say, “Come with
us, that we may refresh and cheer ourselves,” let us remember that their
meaning is to drink themselves into drunkenness and assail God with
blasphemies—for that is exactly what they say. See the example of Joab, who
used the word “play” when he wished the young men to meet one another in
combat.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 19 | printed columns 13–14]

So when they invite you to erect statues for the Floralia, regard it as
exactly the same as if they said: Come with us to steal, fornicate, and profane
the Sabbath.

Verse 12. We shall swallow them alive like the grave, and whole like those who
descend into the pit.

The reasons for the exhortation follow. The first is drawn from the attached
inhumanity and from their acting with impunity and without danger. To one who
objects, “How can that be, since spilled blood and discarded corpses will
arouse the magistrate and investigators to seek out the murderers?” they reply
that they will take care that no trace of spilled blood appears, just as if
they had completely swallowed the people, and that they will be no more liable
than if the earth had gaped open and devoured them alive or if the dead had
been buried by corpse-bearers and undertakers. Yet the wretches do not
understand that the blood will cry out to God and that the earth will uncover
the blood of the slain, as it says in Job, as though it regurgitated by vomiting
the blood it had drunk.

Verse 13. We shall obtain every precious substance; we shall fill our houses
with spoils.

The second argument is drawn from the attached reward, namely spoils, by which
they may provide for themselves and their families. For Satan knows that human
minds are deeply infected by the contagion of greed, and therefore he fashions
a corresponding enticement for them. No bait can be found so universal, or one
that more quickly gnaws at or even devours the greatest part of humanity, than
a mouthful of profit. Hence Solomon selects it here.

Verse 14. You shall cast your lot among us; let us all have one purse.

There is an anticipation in this verse, whose objection is gathered from the
opposite answer: “But you are more numerous, bound among yourselves by an old
covenant of association, and therefore you will either exclude me completely
or at least not admit me to an equal division of the spoils.” “No,” they say,
“the division will be equal; and to preserve greater equality, it will be
decided by lot who chooses first and who second.” Another anticipation is
added, whose objection can be framed thus: “But before we arrive at the spoils,
expenses must be met, and I have no money.” They answer: “Nor will that prevent
you from joining us, for we shall all have one purse. You will live at our
expense until you obtain money, just as we will live at yours when money is
available.” By this speech, however greedy and unjust they are, they display an
appearance of justice and liberality, so that they may more easily ensnare the
unwary youth and disguise their crimes with some appearance of virtue.

Verse 15. My son, do not walk in a common way with them; restrain your foot
from their path.

Solomon now prepares an antidote to the poison dripped by wicked scoundrels.
Here it must first be observed that, to restrain the young man from evil,
Solomon opposes God’s authority to their wicked counsels. Therefore let this
voice always resound in our ears. Its authority will easily keep us in our
duty—unless we wish to subordinate Solomon’s most wise counsel, or rather
God’s, to the suggestion of errors. For if Christ’s authority must be preferred
by many parasangs to the most ancient, weighty, and learned opinions of human
beings, Matthew 5, how much more must it be preferred to the wicked little
words of frivolous little men! Second, when he warns that the foot must be
restrained, he appears to indicate that even the godly have a certain
inclination to enter destructive ways of this kind. Third, we are taught from
this that it is insufficient for someone to resolve firmly within himself to
abstain from sin unless he also prepares weapons against the devil’s snares,
opposing sword to sword and a solid and firm reason to one that merely appears
good. Otherwise that affection, supported by no arguments, will easily vanish.

Verse 16. For they run with their feet toward evil and hasten to shed blood.

The first reason by which he dissuades is drawn from the effect: they
perpetrate evil. This is amplified by the attached circumstances both of haste
and of the impetus with which they are borne toward evil. Their hurrying with
both feet denotes their impetus and violence in evil. He then explains this
running toward evil more particularly when he says they hasten to shed blood,
as if to say: they think that they hasten to obtain riches, but it can be said
more truly that they hasten to shed blood. That is, they propose to shed blood
in order to acquire spoils; but in reality—such is their perversity—they
acquire spoils in order to shed blood. Let Paul’s passage in Romans 3:15,
therefore, be compared with this one of Solomon.

Verse 17. For a net is spread in vain before the eyes of every bird.

Verse 18. Moreover, they lie in wait for their own blood; their hiding is
against their own lives.

The other argument is drawn from the effect of their own slaughter. For while
they think they lie in wait for the blood of others, they set snares for their
own. He also proves this by an argument from parity. Just as a net spread out
does not make the birds more cautious so that they cease to hurry to the bait,
and in relation to the birds can thus truly be said to have been set in vain,
so these men, captivated by desire for spoil, do not notice the snare that
either God himself or the magistrate sets for them. If, then, the Apostle
dissuades from sins on the ground that sinners deceive themselves, they should
be moved all the more by this argument: that they are, as it were, killers of
themselves (αὐτόφονοι).

Verse 19. So are the companies of all who are devoted to gain; gain takes away
the life of those devoted to it.

This verse is an epiphonema by which he declares the certainty of the ruin
that greed draws upon them.

Verse 20. Supreme wisdom cries aloud outside; in the streets she gives forth
her voice.

Verse 21. At the head of the crowds she proclaims; at the entrances of the
gates, in each city, she speaks her words, saying:

Verse 22. How long, O fools, will you love folly? And will mockers desire
mockery for themselves, and the stupid hate knowledge?

Verse 23. Turn back at my correction. Behold, I shall pour forth my spirit to
you; I shall make my words known to you.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 21 | printed columns 17–18]

In these verses, while endeavoring to lead those who have repudiated the
preceding doctrine to repentance, he announces certain ruin to the despisers
both of the doctrine and of the rebuke.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - an attempt to lead those who despise the preceding doctrine to repentance;
  - the final ruin of the despisers both of the exhortation and of the
    chastisement.
- The attempt to lead to repentance has two elements:
  - one of confidence and patience (μακροθυμίας);
  - the other of severity.

In order that this speech may possess greater authority, it is attributed to
wisdom most wise—that is, to Christ. For although every discourse comprised in
the Scriptures belongs to Christ, God’s Son and the Father’s wisdom,
nevertheless, so that certain statements may obtain greater force than others,
according as they are adapted to the salvation of those before whom they are
set, the former are uttered in the prophets’ name, but the latter in the words
of God himself and of Christ. See Proverbs 9. From this it is clear that the
names and designations attributed to God in Scripture are derived from the
character of the things and persons concerning whom and before whom the
discourse is delivered. Thus he is called the God of consolation and mercies
when the discourse concerns the afflicted, and the just God when he executes
vengeance. For that reason Solomon introduces supreme wisdom speaking here,
because he is dealing with people who reject the preceding doctrine as though
it were insufficiently wise. Ministers should imitate this.

It must next be observed that the wisdom contained in Scripture, however much
people condemn it as folly, is nevertheless the most exact wisdom and easily
the chief of every other wisdom.

In the rebuke these two things are considered: first, it is proposed openly
and clearly without any circumlocution; second, it is severe. The former is
illustrated from the place serving as its setting, where the greatest gathering
of people exists. Thus Christ says, “What you have heard in the ear, publish
abroad upon the housetops.” To this belongs Isaiah’s statement: “Cry with the
throat; lift up your voice like a trumpet.” Sinners of this sort will not wake
from the sleep of sin unless the minister thunders with a sonorous voice. Thus
Elijah did to Ahab, John the Baptist to Herod, and others to others. This
rebukes both ministers who reprove sinners covertly, slowly, and doubtfully,
and sinners themselves who unjustly bear every frankness (παῤῥησίαν) in the
reproof of their vices. Further, let it be observed that pains must be taken to
make the preaching of the Word as public as possible.

The severity of the rebuke appears first in the shamefulness of the names he
attributes to them—for he calls them simple, mockers, and fools, and proves
that they are such from their effects, partly their love and desire for evil
and partly their hatred of good—and second in the attached circumstance of
time: they have done these things for a long time and set no limit to their
sinning.

First observe that the ungodly and those who despise God’s teaching are called
fools and simple. By this deliberate manner of speaking, the insult of folly
so often heaped by the ungodly upon the godly is hurled back upon themselves.
Next observe that the same persons are called fools and simple who are also
called mockers; likewise, mockery and folly are said to fall upon the same
person. This teaches that these are not buffoons deprived of common reason,
but people who, trusting their own intelligence and wisdom, hold God’s wisdom
in ridicule and derision as folly. Therefore those numbered here among
mockers are not witty satirists and humorous jesters who speak everything at
others’ pleasure, but people who make wholesome admonitions an object of
ridicule.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 22 | printed columns 19–20]

He next declares from their effects who they finally are: namely, those who
love folly, injustice, and mockery and pursue with hatred the wisdom contrary
to them, whereas the godly hate evils, although they are not free from them,
and love good things, Romans 7.

But someone will object that fools are unjustly required to learn wisdom and
are blamed for loving folly. For what else is to be expected from them? I
answer that this is most rightly required of them because by their own fault
they brought that folly upon themselves. From this the error of the papists is
clear, who commonly infer free will from exhortations of this kind.

By the attached circumstance of time, עַד־מָתַי, it is indicated that one must
not descend immediately to sharp rebukes, but only after people have for some
time rejected the grace offered by God. See 1 Peter 4:2–3.

The exhortation to repentance in verse 23 follows in support of the preceding
chastisement, as if he said: Although you have profited nothing from wholesome
doctrine and gentle admonitions, nevertheless let this chastisement now work
repentance in you. Here we see how gracious God is toward those whom he
chastises, since he does it with the intention of correcting them. We are also
taught with what spirit we ought to receive chastisements, whether they occur
in words or deeds.

In the same verse he confirms this exhortation from the attached promise that
the Spirit will be conferred most abundantly, as if they had never transgressed.
See John 7; Romans 8.

From verse 24 to the end of the chapter. Having recounted the history of both
the exhortation and the correction, he announces to their despisers a ruin
that is horrible, inevitable, and most just, and anticipates what might impede
this judgment.

- The parts are:
  - the announcement of the dreadful, inevitable, and most just divine
    judgment against the ungodly;
  - an anticipation of what might oppose the truth of this announcement.

Verse 24. Since I call and you refuse, I extend my hand and no one pays heed.

Verse 25. But you withdraw yourselves from all my counsel and do not acquiesce
in my correction.

Here observe the order God follows in executing judgments and by which, as
though by writs served upon sinners, he finally proceeds to pronounce
sentence. First he teaches; then he exhorts most gently; next he applies
sharper correction both in words and deeds; finally, against sinners who reject
all these things and make them ineffectual, as it were, he promulgates the most
severe sentence. For earth that often drinks in rain but produces no fruit
worthy of the farmer who has expended cost and labor upon it is near to burning
and destruction, Hebrews 6. This order must be noted the more diligently
because, although it is desirable to know the method and procedure of the
civil court, it is far more advantageous to have the custom and practice of
the divine court thoroughly known and understood.

Verse 26. I also shall laugh in your calamity; I shall mock when that which
you dread comes.

Verse 27. When that which you dread comes like raging devastation, and your
calamity arrives like a whirlwind; when oppression and distress rush upon you.

[Analytical diagram: God’s judgment against ungodly despisers is described
from its adjuncts: terror; justice.]

The attached terror that precedes is the dread of the ungodly, by which,
pricked by the goads of their sins, they are often compelled to fear every
extreme thing, even hell itself. Hence the statement, “There is no peace for
the ungodly.” For just as they have conscience as a witness and accuser, so
they have the same as judge unless they obey the accusation. See Job 15:21–22.
Those, therefore, for whom God is no dread, Genesis 31:53, are compelled,
whether willing or unwilling, to fear the devil and other things of that kind.

In the time of judgment itself let its attached circumstances be considered.
First is the joy of God who inflicts it, namely laughter and mockery. By this
he declares that he will no more pity them than they customarily pity those at
whose death they laugh and jest; and he says that their own example—holding his
teaching and admonition in ridicule—has provoked him to do this. Moreover, he
is said to laugh at and mock them because he offers them to others to be
mocked. Similar to this is the passage of Job where God is said to pursue the
wicked man with clapping of hands.

There follows a description of divine judgment against the ungodly from its
attached circumstances and effects in verse 27. First, it is said to assail
them with a dreadful noise, such as that of waters falling from a high place,
or a cataract (καταῤῥάκτου). Thus God is said to roar when exercising
judgments, presenting himself to people as dreadful as a roaring lion is to
little beasts, which are said to be so terrified by it that they cannot take
flight. For after God has made no progress by consuming them gradually, like a
moth, he bursts upon them in one assault after another and charges them like a
strong man, Job 16:14. Therefore, the person who turns away from God’s
correction because it is a little more bitter will fall into that which is the
most bitter of all things that can be said or conceived.

Another attached circumstance declaring the judgment dreadful is that it is
sudden. This is illustrated by the likenesses of a whirlwind, the pain of
childbirth, and a nocturnal thief. For the ungodly dwell in darkness, where
they cannot foresee the coming evil; indeed, they sleep so soundly in their
sins that they scarcely ever think of it.

The effect of this judgment is oppression and such distress that they do not
know where to turn. For these are the things Paul notes await the ungodly,
Romans 9:2. But the godly are delivered from all their distresses because they
have Christ, to whom they may resort in every trouble and difficulty.

Verse 28. Then they will call upon me, but I shall not hear; in the morning
they will seek me, but they will not find me.

He intensifies the distress and oppression by another effect, namely calling
upon God, which he teaches will not merely be fruitless for them but harmful
and indeed fatal unto destruction. Here, therefore, those are refuted who do
not doubt that they will obtain pardon for their sins provided they find one
quarter of an hour in which to call upon God, since here God says that he will
not hear them even if they cry to him from morning until evening. It is also
clear that those err utterly who think everyone who calls upon God while dying
is saved. For this passage sufficiently shows that many descend into hell with
“Lord, have mercy on us” upon their lips.

“Then,” someone will say, “how does the truth of that promise stand—that the
person who calls upon God’s name will be saved?” Joel 2. I answer that this is
understood of those who call upon God truly and sincerely: first, with faith,
which these people cannot do, for they have no faith and very often do not know
what it is; second, with a desire to glorify God, while these in their cries
regard only their own salvation; third, by departing from iniquity, 2 Timothy
2:19, which these people do not do.

—knowledge and did not choose reverence for Jehovah.

Verse 30. They did not acquiesce in my counsel but despised all my correction.

Here, as occurs in pronouncing sentences, the causes of the judgment rendered
are discovered, chiefly so that the following sentence concerning his most
just judgment may be inferred. Here it must be observed that those who do not
choose—that is, do not eagerly desire—the fear of God are condemned just as
those who hate it; and those who did not acquiesce in God’s counsel just as
those who haughtily rejected his correction.

Verse 31. Thus they shall eat of the fruit of their way and be filled from
their own wicked counsels.

God confirms his justice by the testimony of a proverb much circulated among
people. For just as it is just that a person eat the food he himself prepared
and reap what he sowed, so it is just that the person who sowed to the flesh
reap corruption, and that the one who prepared it with hands of iniquity taste
the bread of affliction. This teaches that no one besides themselves must be
accused for the destruction of mockers. “Your destruction is from yourself, O
Israel,” Hosea 13:9. Observe further that they are to be satiated with their own
wicked pursuits, which formerly they followed so greedily and with which they
could never be satisfied.

Verse 32. For the ease of fools will kill them, and the tranquility of the
stupid will destroy them.

Verse 33. But the one who listens to me will dwell securely, tranquil from the
dread of evil.

Thus far has been the announcement of the dreadful, inevitable, and most just
divine judgment against the ungodly. There follows an anticipation of what
might seem to oppose the truth of the announcement. The tacit objection is
gathered from the answer: it could enter the minds of both godly and ungodly
people that God by no means executes the judgments he threatens against the
wicked, since they sit calm and quiet in their dwellings. But, he says, that
very ease and prosperity kills them, both accelerating God’s judgments and
rendering them more atrocious. He illustrates this from the contrary condition
of the godly: if any evil befalls them, it nevertheless works together for
their good, so that no true evil, nor even the dread of evil, assails them.
