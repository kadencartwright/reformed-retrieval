QUESTION II

Whether the era of Christ, used commonly for a thousand years, or at least for many years now, is rightly established *a posteriori*? Or, whether today we rightly number 1,657 years from Christ’s birth, neither more nor fewer?

We have discussed the year of the Nativity in relation to what came before, comparing it with all the intervals back to the beginning of the world. We now inquire about that same year in relation to what came after. That is, is the Nativity rightly compared with the following years down to the present one? This comparison is not so difficult or controversial as the former. That may be because the multitude of writers in history, chronology, and astronomy described everything more clearly and correctly in periods nearer their own than the few surviving ancient writers did in periods more remote and distant. Or it may be because modern chronologists have not yet labored as much over the minutiae of more recent centuries as over earlier ones. If the chronological markers of later times were examined by the learned as exactly and fully as those of earlier times, it is possible that no fewer controversies would be found in the former than the latter. At any rate, since Scaliger’s correction of chronology almost all learned men, Protestant as well as papist, agree that the common era was wrongly constituted. It cannot be defended that the present year is truly the 1,657th from the birth of Christ.

Some of the less learned papists cling tooth and nail to the common era, because the Roman Church, which cannot err, and the supreme pontiffs, foundations and rocks of truth, constantly employ it. The crowd of Jesuits, however, now no longer frightened by this bogey of the infallibility of church and pontiffs, admits that the calculation is certainly and plainly false. They excuse the pontiffs and church only by the ridiculous comment Petavius gives in *On Time* 12.4: their supreme majesty supposedly did not lower itself to discuss chronological minutiae but unhesitatingly employed the common people’s calculation, whatever it might be, without examination. Eagles, to be sure, do not catch flies, nor has Jupiter leisure to attend to trifles. This is indeed a most worthy lid for the papal dish. We have no reason to wonder that Petavius excuses his party’s present anachronism of a few years, since in the same chapter he attempts to conceal the much grosser acknowledged error of the pontiffs and church by more than six hundred years. The Jesuit admits—contrary to his party’s custom—that for many centuries the majesty of the Roman Church did not lower itself to any solid examination of chronology. He should have added: nor of any other controversies whatsoever. Whatever errors it found rooted in the schools or among the people, it offered them all to the populace to drink down upon its immense and divine authority.

The veil of Paulus Middelburgensis recalled by Petavius is more honorable. That is, the pontiffs had never added the year of the Lord’s birth to the years of their pontificates in their bulls until Blondus, recently secretary of the papal chamber, introduced this novelty on his private authority; afterward the pontiffs did not attend to an examination of this newly imported calculation.

It is true that learned men of every party no longer think it advantageous to remove this corrupt calculation, now too well established by the use of many centuries. Yet no candid person ought, under any pretext, to sell a known falsehood as truth or refuse to supplement that era by however many years certain and clear reasons demonstrate are justly due.

Some think something should be subtracted from this common era. The more recent Greeks—Theophanes, Maximus the Monk, George Syncellus, Cedrenus, and others—judge the common calculation to err by a prochronism of seven or nine years. Thus one sees Greek reckonings invariably seven or nine years shorter than Latin ones. How destitute of reason they are will soon appear, when we have proved that the common era, far from being condemned for prochronism or excessive length, really errs by metachronism and shortness and must be corrected by adding and supplying what is lacking. The whole difficulty lies in determining precisely how many years it lacks. Spanhemius follows Petavius too closely here, for both strive with all their might against Scaliger and Calvisius to show that the addition cannot be certainly and precisely determined. At any rate, it is established that the addition of twenty-two years by Sigebert, Marianus Scotus, and most Latin writers for many centuries was utterly barbarous, as Petavius and after him Spanhemius rightly observe. For if Christ’s Nativity anticipated the common era by twenty-two whole years, his death must have preceded the death of Augustus. But that is—

—plainly contrary to the Gospel, for at Luke 3:1 and 23 Christ was baptized in Tiberius’ fifteenth year and then preached the Gospel for some years before his death. He therefore could not have been born so early that he died before Tiberius’ first year.

Paulus Middelburgensis not only defends this palpable error at length but professes to have learned it from the apostle Paul in a nocturnal vision. Baronius rejects this dream as a ridiculous sophism. That is no wonder, since elsewhere Middelburgensis himself, forgetting his dream and vision, insists that the common era is so far from following the true Nativity by twenty-two years that it even precedes it by two. Spanhemius rightly notes Petavius’ oversight in sharply censuring our Casaubon as though he had absurdly corrected Baronius and defended Middelburgensis’ dream. Yet Casaubon’s irony condemns Middelburgensis’ nonsense no less than Baronius’ own statement does. Having therefore rejected so gross an addition of so many years, we must see how many years ought justly to be added to the common era.

Joseph Scaliger, Calvisius, Torniellus, Pererius, Baronius, and others add two. Although Baronius agrees with him on this two-year interval, Scaliger refutes him at length in his *Prolegomena*, but with great modesty toward a very learned man—far otherwise than the shameless and insolent Jesuits are accustomed to treat our writers in any literary disagreement.

Baronius’ error is that, although he places the Nativity two years earlier than the common era, he does not think himself to dissent from that era but thoroughly confuses his Scaligerian computation with the common one. Upon this foundation he places many sacred and political histories in the wrong time, as Scaliger and Calvisius each demonstrate excellently and fully in their *Prolegomena*, and from them Petavius and Spanhemius. Indeed, Baronius himself finally perceives his oversight and attempts to correct it. From the beginning of his annals he marks most things by years two years earlier than the common era—for example, Augustus’ death in Christ’s sixteenth year, although according to the common era of Dionysius it happened in the fourteenth. Nevertheless, when he reaches the beginning of Diocletian’s reign, he places it in Christ’s 284th according to the common era, which he follows thereafter to the end of the *Annals*, just as before that point he had followed Scaliger’s computation. Lest he be observed disagreeing with himself, and so that he might slide as cautiously and silently as possible from the former computation to the latter, he subtracts two from Emperor Probus’ six years, which every historian assigns him, and deletes four consuls from the fasti of two years. Petavius does not dare defend this splendid cardinal’s theft, demonstrated visibly by our writers, but admits it candidly enough in his observations on Epiphanius’ fifty-first heresy, that of the Alogi.

This could have been forgiven Baronius more easily had he not pursued others’ oversights here with excessive harshness. He is so intent upon flogging the excellent writer Josephus that, without reflection, he throws himself into many foul errors. Contrary to every learned man’s opinion, he contends that Herod’s reign must begin only after Augustus’ victory at Actium, that nine whole years omitted by Josephus must be added to Herod’s life, and that Herod’s life must be extended to Christ’s ninth year, as our Casaubon rightly notes in his *Exercises*.

We nevertheless gladly admit what Baronius proves at length: Christ’s Nativity cannot be later than the consulship of Messalinus and Lentulus, the 43rd Julian year, 28th from the battle of Actium, 751st of the city according to the Varronian Palilia, but 750th according to Cato, and the second year of the 194th Olympiad. According to Calvisius, those markers agree with year 3,947 of the world, only two years after our Scriptural calculation stated above. It is nevertheless two complete years before the common era, which supposes Christ was born in world-year 3,949, the 30th from the battle of Actium, 29th from the capture of Alexandria, 45th Julian, 43rd Augustan from Julius’ death but 42nd from Augustus’ first consulship, fourth of the 194th Olympiad, 753rd of the city according to Varro, and 752nd according to Cato. Almost all the learned now admit this error, but few attempt what ought chiefly to be done: to demonstrate it by clear and solid arguments. Petavius, and after him our most learned Spanhemius, labors with every effort to evade everything Scaliger and Calvisius assert rather than weaken and overthrow it—far too insolently and contrary to his custom, as Nisbetius observed before us. At any rate we now present these arguments chiefly from Calvisius.

First, Christ’s thirtieth year coincides with Tiberius’ fifteenth (Luke 3:1, 23), and therefore Christ’s fifteenth with Tiberius’ first and Augustus’ last. Augustus died in his seventy-fifth year on August 19—not, as Baronius has it, on the Kalends of September—in the 59th Julian year from the calendar’s correction, 58th from Julius’ death, and 57th from his first consulship, when Sextus Pom—

—peius and Sextus Appuleius were consuls. From that day, according to the Roman custom of numbering successors’ reigns immediately from their predecessors’ death, Tiberius’ reign began. Thus Tiberius’ first, Augustus’ last, and Christ’s sixteenth coincided. On September 27 of that year there occurred a universal lunar eclipse according to Dio, Tacitus, and other historians of undoubted credibility in this matter. In the preceding year there was a solar eclipse, as Petavius has from Dio and Eusebius at 12.4. According to the astronomers’ calculation, that lunar eclipse is found in Christ’s sixteenth year and the solar one in his fifteenth. According to the common era, however, Tiberius’ first, Augustus’ last, and the lunar eclipse fall in the fourteenth year, and the solar eclipse in the thirteenth. The common era therefore follows the true Nativity by at least two years.

Furthermore, since Christ’s sixteenth coincides with Augustus’ last or 57th and with the 59th Julian year, Christ’s first will coincide with the 43rd Julian, 41st of Augustus, second of the 194th Olympiad, and the consulship of Lentulus and Messalinus. Yet the common era’s first year does not coincide with these, but with the 45th Julian, 43rd Augustan, the consulship of Cossus Cornelius Lentulus and Calpurnius Piso, and the fourth year of the 194th Olympiad.

To evade this argument Spanhemius, after Petavius, raises a great cloud of dust.

He says first that Luke does not at all connect Tiberius’ fifteenth with Christ’s thirtieth, but only with John’s preaching; that there is a great interval between the first and 33rd verses of chapter 3; and indeed that it is utterly unknown how much time elapsed between John’s preaching and Christ’s baptism.

Reply. I would have forgiven a Jesuit any troublesome fictions for defending the Roman Church’s errors. I cannot so easily forgive the far more excellent Spanhemius for borrowing them. Before Petavius, so far as I understand, no ancient or recent writer, Protestant or papist, called into doubt whether Luke 3:1 cohered with verse 23, whether Tiberius’ fifteenth corresponded with Christ’s thirtieth, or whether John’s preaching and Christ’s baptism occurred at least within the same year. Luke’s principal purpose in that passage is to arrange the chronology of Christ alone. At 2:42 he marks Christ’s twelfth year, and at 3:23 his thirtieth. To illustrate the latter, in verse 1 he notes many highly distinguished chronological markers: the Roman emperor’s year and contemporary officeholders—not only the governor of Judea but also three tetrarchs of neighboring regions and finally two high priests. The evangelist joins these chronological markers to John’s baptism not for John’s sake but Christ’s. The connection of these chapters demonstrates this, as does Luke’s own explanation of himself at Acts 1:20, where the time of Christ’s baptism, preaching, and appearance in public ministry is plainly joined with the time of John’s baptism. To separate them, especially by the interval of a whole year, would appear too manifest a contradiction in Luke. We do not approve that in moving this stone an excellent man followed an insolent Jesuit.

He says second that Tiberius’ fifteenth need not be connected with Christ’s thirtieth, because we do not know whether the evangelist numbers Tiberius’ years in the Hebrew or Roman manner. Granted that the Romans count an emperor’s first year immediately from his predecessor’s death, the Jews nevertheless counted their princes’ years from Passover and the spring month Nisan. Josephus thus counts Herod’s years, and Calvisius himself teaches that Holy Scripture customarily counts royal years in this way among Jews as well as Israelites.

Reply. Spanhemius magnifies this troublesome and novel objection from Petavius too much. Herod, a tyrant succeeding no one, might have begun the years of his reign from whatever time he pleased in order to gratify the Jews. It nevertheless appears utterly improbable that the kings of Judah or Israel began their reigns only from Nisan or Passover. Although Nisan was the first month in sacred matters, it was not so in civil matters, nor did any command of God or custom of the Jewish people require civil reigns to begin from Nisan or Passover. Calvisius’ instance does not prove this. What then? If a king died on Nisan 16, did his reign always continue for a full year in the interim until a new Passover returned on the fifteenth day of the following Nisan? Some kings of Israel and Judah reigned only a few months, ending after their predecessors’ final Passover but before the next. Does Scripture delete those kings from its royal catalogues? Third, however Hebrew writers count the years of their own princes, did they attempt to conform the times of foreign monarchies to the Jewish model? Could or would the Jews have dared, contrary to the common practice used in every public act throughout the empire, to alter the years of Nebuchadnezzar, Cyrus, Artaxerxes, and the other Assyrian, Persian, or Greek emperors according to their nation’s private custom? Spanhemius will not persuade us of so great a novelty without credible witnesses, even if he has persuaded himself.

He says third that Christ’s thirtieth year may extend—

—from his twenty-fifth to his thirty-fifth. Indeed, every whole number in Scripture—tens, twenties, thirties, hundreds, thousands, and so forth—embraces a latitude of at least ten.

Reply. We acknowledge that in Scripture whole and sometimes fractional numbers possess some latitude. But to suppose, without any necessity demonstrated from Scripture itself, that every whole number possesses the latitude of an entire decade appears manifestly to batter and overthrow the deepest foundations of all sacred chronology. Thus, even if all Spanhemius’ many other engines were broken, with this one alone he could demolish every computation drawn from whole numbers in Holy Scripture.

He says fourth that Luke does not express Christ’s thirtieth year, but another—namely, *about* the thirtieth—which is not the thirtieth itself.

Reply. Scaliger on his own side, and Petavius and Spanhemius against him, dispute far too much and too subtly about Luke’s words, “He was beginning to be about thirty years old.” As Spanhemius rightly admits, these words obscure rather than illuminate the evangelist’s sense. As to the present exception, our Nisbetius learnedly and soundly repels it from Scripture itself, as he does many other things in this diatribe of Spanhemius. Thus Luke 23:44, “it was about the sixth hour,” is explained by the Holy Spirit himself at Matthew 27:45 as “from the sixth hour.” Acts 10:3 has “about the ninth hour,” which verse 30 explains as “the ninth hour.” Mark 8:9 likewise has “about four thousand,” while Matthew 15:38 has “four thousand.” Again Matthew 27:46 has “about the ninth hour,” while Mark 15:34 has “at the ninth hour.” Thus in our passage of Luke, “about thirty years,” is rightly understood as truly thirty years, whether beginning, ending, or complete.

He says fifth that Calvisius’ astronomical markers are not true, because Petavius proves that no solar eclipse occurred in the year of Augustus’ death.

Reply. Spanhemius does not dispute Calvisius’ primary astronomical markers, one of which, if true, demonstrates the conclusion. He does not deny the lunar eclipse in the second month after Augustus’ death or the solar eclipse in Claudius’ fifth year, from which he professes chiefly to prove his whole case. Nor does he himself offer anything against the solar eclipse that preceded Augustus’ death, but merely appeals to Petavius, as though Petavius, fighting a shadow, proved that it either did not occur or could not then have occurred. I do not know how to excuse here the carelessness of Spanhemius, elsewhere most diligent. Not only does he cite no passage from Petavius, but the most celebrated chapter in this matter, book 12, chapter 4, often cited elsewhere by Spanhemius, expressly contradicts Spanhemius’ assertion, affirming from Eusebius and Dio that a solar eclipse occurred in Augustus’ final year. If Petavius denies it anywhere else, which I doubt, that is no wonder. In this very passage Spanhemius observes Petavius’ manifest contradiction: here he insolently argues against Scaliger that nothing certain and determinate concerning Christ’s thirtieth year can be deduced from Luke 3, but elsewhere affirms that Christ’s thirtieth is demonstrated with utmost certainty from this same passage. I conjecture that Spanhemius’ pen slipped inadvertently from Scaliger to Petavius here—through a double inadvertence. First, it is not Petavius but Scaliger, in his observations on Eusebius at number 2,030, who asserts that the solar eclipse noted by Dio before Augustus’ death neither occurred nor could occur in the year where Eusebius places it. Second, although Scaliger asserts this of the year before Eusebius’ year of Augustus’ death, in the same place he expressly affirms from Dio that the eclipse most truly occurred the following year, namely the year of the death itself.

Finally, the object of Petavius’ and Spanhemius’ whole disputation is that Luke’s passage be understood so loosely and with so broad a latitude that it may be extended to Christ’s 31st, 32nd, or 33rd year. In that way they save the opinions of those who think the common era follows the true Nativity by three years, one year beyond Scaliger’s view. They also save the opinion of Decker and Susliga, who add four years to the common era, and even Kepler’s, who exceeds Decker’s four years by one and the common era by five. There was certainly no need to stir so many and such great waves for this purpose, for our mind by no means shrinks from so great an extension of the Nativity. Indeed, Kepler’s five-year addition would agree beautifully with our opinion in the preceding chapter, for the true Nativity would then coincide with world-year 3,945. At any rate, I do not think Luke’s passage should be vexed with such foreign glosses. I do not pursue Petavius’ replies to Calvisius’ first argument any further.

Calvisius argues second from Herod’s death. The common era supposes Christ was born as Julian year 45 ended, in the fourth year of the 194th Olympiad, the consulship of Cossus Cornelius Lentulus and Gaius Calpurnius Piso, and the 753rd year of the city. Yet Herod, who was alive when Christ was born, died two complete years before the stated time. The common era therefore places Christ’s birth too late by at least two years.

That Christ was born before Herod’s death is clear from the evangelists. At Matthew 2:1 he is said to have been born in the days of King Herod and to have fled into Egypt until King Herod, Archelaus’ father, died (verse 19). That Herod died at least two years after Christ’s birth appears from Matthew 2:16, where He—

—rod is said to have killed the children two years old and under, reckoned from the time when the star of the already-born, not yet-to-be-born, Christ appeared to the Magi. By carefully inquiring of the Magi, Herod discovered that this was two years before he ordered the children killed, and he lived for some time after they were slain. Christ was therefore born two years and some additional time before Herod’s death. Herod did not live beyond Julian year 43, for Josephus repeatedly testifies that Herod received the kingdom of Judea from the Romans in Julian year 6, when Gnaeus Domitius Calvinus and Gaius Actius Pollio were consuls, the fourth year of the 184th Olympiad, and that he continued in the kingdom thirty-seven years. Added to six, these make forty-three.

If these things are true, then just as Herod died two years before the common era, Christ, who was born two years before Herod’s death, must have been born four years before that era.

Many make many replies to this argument, but none more absurd than Baronius, although he agrees with Calvisius on the two-year interval between the true Nativity and the common era. Not only Scaliger and Casaubon but also others of our writers heap great praises upon him for his incomparable diligence in compiling ecclesiastical history. Yet all testify that he not only very frequently sins grossly against truth, the true life of history, but almost always errs childishly in chronology. Nowhere is this singular Baronian ignorance of chronology more evident than in the present argument. A good part of the argument rests on Josephus’ testimony concerning Herod’s times, and Baronius, as we noted in the preceding question, affirms that Josephus counts them very badly. First, he will not place the beginning of Herod’s reign with Josephus in Julian year 6, but in year 15, when he has Antony defeated all the way at Actium. Second, he will not allow Herod to have lived seventy years with Josephus, but only sixty-seven, and yet extends his life to Christ’s eighth year—that is, as he teaches, Julian year 49, only two years before the exile of his son Archelaus in Julian year 51. Not only our writers but even the Jesuit Torniellius refutes at length these and many other absurd and unhistorical Baronian claims here. What seems to have given Baronius his chief occasion for stumbling here was a typographical error in Josephus, as Spanhemius rightly noted from Torniellius: it said that Herod was only fifteen when his father appointed him governor of Galilee, where it should have said twenty-five. Scaliger was preparing to wash out this stain of a ten-year anachronism, along with many hundreds of other scribal errors, in a new edition of Josephus, as Casaubon notes, but death prevented him from completing it. By rejecting many thoroughly true chronological designations in Josephus and accepting this false one as true, Baronius imposes upon himself the necessity of making many errors, which no one of the papal party itself attempts to defend.

Although Scaliger and Calvisius use this argument, when they see that by its force the true Nativity is drawn not merely two years beyond the common era, as their hypothesis requires, but four, they attempt to weaken it. Scaliger would like to convict Josephus of error in the years of Herod’s reign, as though he ought to have extended them not merely to thirty-seven but thirty-nine. Yet so many things resist this Scaligerian correction that Calvisius himself abandons it.

Spanhemius, unwilling to be bound by this argument, makes two replies. First, where Josephus says that Herod’s kingdom extended thirty-seven years from the Romans’ original grant and thirty-four from his first possession after Antigonus was crucified, there was a scribal error. The scribes wrote the Greek numeral 37 for 39 and 34 for 36.

Reply. This correction of Josephus’ text by Spanhemius is rash and contrary to the evidence not only of every printed copy but of the manuscripts. Second, since Scaliger himself only suspects this emendation, does not assert it firmly, and Calvisius never embraced it—for he nowhere here extends Herod’s reign beyond thirty-eight years—Spanhemius ought not to have put it forward. Third, the emendation does not untie the knot. If Herod died at the beginning of Julian year 43, shortly before Passover, six months before Christ’s birth at the beginning of September, as Scaliger holds, or nine months before it, if Christ was born on December 25 according to most, this fiction will contradict Scripture, which places Christ’s Nativity before Herod’s death. But if Herod died at the beginning of Julian year 44, three or six months after Christ was born, that likewise contradicts the Scripture cited above, which makes Herod’s massacre of the children occur two full years after Christ’s birth.

Apparently foreseeing these difficulties, Spanhemius distrusts his first reply and flees to a second. Namely, this argument indeed presses defenders of the common era and even Scaliger and Calvisius, who teach that the true Nativity exceeds the common one by precisely two years. It does not, however, press the Jesuits Decker and Susliga, who antedate the common era by four years, and least of all Kepler, who does so by five. Indeed, Spanhemius appears to surrender, as Petavius did before him, and to stand with both feet in Kepler’s opinion. I would gladly follow such great authorities, for then my preceding hypothesis concerning the true Nativity’s coincidence with world-year 3,945—

—would fit exactly, though far contrary to Kepler’s and Petavius’ opinion about world-years. But I fear that two contrary arguments cannot be answered. The first concerns the lunar eclipse Josephus notes shortly before Herod’s death. According to the astronomical computation Scaliger affirms that it occurred at the beginning of January in Julian year 45 or 44, in whose March Herod died. The other is that, according to Kepler’s opinion, Luke’s passage concerning Christ’s thirty years must be extended to thirty-three.

Kepler appears to answer the first conveniently enough. He teaches that Josephus noted the lunar eclipse not in January, a full three months before Herod’s death, but in March on the very night when Herod burned the Jewish teachers with their disciples for pulling down his golden eagle from the temple gate. He says that according to his tables he finds an eclipse of six digits of the moon in Julian year 42 on March 13, which preceded Herod’s death by only a few days. If this is true, the matter appears settled and Kepler victorious.

Spanhemius’ objection intended to weaken this astronomical argument is not solid: namely, that the eclipse Josephus noted was total, while Kepler’s was only partial, reaching half the moon. Had Spanhemius inspected Josephus’ passage, he certainly would not have offered such an escape from Kepler. Josephus wrote not one jot about a total obscuration, and indeed the obscuration of half the moon is most properly designated a true eclipse.

What Kepler might answer to the passage of Luke I suspend until his and Calvisius’ opposing writings in this argument come into my hands. For the present I do not press the argument beyond Calvisius’ view—namely, for a two-year anticipation of the true Nativity before the common era. If Kepler could demonstrate a five-year anticipation by this or any other argument, he would do it with our warmest thanks, for we would judge the reasons for our calculation beautifully confirmed.

A third argument is commonly offered for Calvisius’ conclusion from the registration by Quirinius, governor of Syria, in Luke 2:1. But when I weigh all the many things written about this registration that I find in Baronius, Casaubon, Montacutius, Spanhemius, Petavius, Torniellius, and others, it does not seem to me that a certain argument can be drawn from them for the stated conclusion. First, let us grant what few learned men now deny, that Quirinius registered Judea twice: the latter time after Archelaus was exiled, in the tenth year after Herod’s death, a registration Josephus discusses fully; and the first time before Herod’s death. These registrations were wholly different and thirteen years apart, contrary to the opinion of Eusebius, Bede, Calvin, Piscator, Baradius, and other learned men who confused both into one.

Second, let us grant that the first registration was of persons only, not property, contrary to the opinion of Suidas long ago and more recently Baronius and others.

Third, let us also grant that in that first census of persons Quirinius discharged an extraordinary commission from Augustus and by no means performed the ordinary office of governor of Syria. Tertullian, who has that earlier census carried out by L. Saturninus, the ordinary governor of Syria, may thus be reconciled with Luke, since Saturninus’ name could have been prefixed in the public records to every act performed by any official, whether subordinate or extraordinary, within the limits of his prefecture.

Fourth, for Spanhemius’ sake let us grant that Quirinius conducted the census while he discharged the office of ordinary governor of Syria, immediately after his first consulship in Julian year 36 and before Saturninus governed Syria.

Finally, contrary to Chemnitius’ opinion on Luke 2, let us grant that this census of Quirinius was Augustus’ second universal census, at least a whole decade after his first merely geographical survey mentioned by Aethicus, in which he had the roads and routes of the whole Roman world described so that he might proceed more easily to the census of persons—just as he registered persons in order to prepare the way for a census of property to supplement the treasury.

With all these and still more points granted, nothing can nevertheless be concluded about the certain year of those censuses. The census tables of Quirinius, together with all the other accounts of the commonwealth’s censuses, were once deposited in the Roman archives. Hence Tertullian and Justin Martyr confidently appealed to that registration of Mary and the infant Christ under Quirinius, and neither Porphyry nor Julian the Apostate nor any other enemy of the Christian name contradicted anything in it. But all those public tables, with most other writings of that age, have long since perished. Since no author surviving today says a word about Quirinius’ first census, its true year cannot be certainly defined. It is certain, as Spanhemius proves at length, that the census was conducted between Julian year 37, when Quirinius as proconsul pacified the disturbances in Cilicia, and year 42 or 45, when Herod died or was approaching death. Whether it was completed exactly five years before the common era, as Kepler attempts to demonstrate by many arguments, is—

—not clear to us, since we have not yet seen that writing of Kepler.

We would pass over this argument and conclude the question here, were we not detained a little by Montacutius’ perversity. The great difficulty in the evangelist’s passage concerning Quirinius’ registration has produced many interpreters’ expositions, as each strives according to his ability to bring light into the darkness. Among others the excellent Calvin and Beza offer their labors, on account of which not only they but the whole Protestant nation is most wretchedly flogged by the papists. Here, as very often elsewhere, Montacutius vomits as much black bile against both Calvin and Beza as any papist, in order to demonstrate the inclination of his mind toward the papal party. In the first edition of his notes Beza modestly proposed a conjecture that Luke’s verse was corrupted by scribes, or that a marginal note had slipped into the text and could return to the margin without any loss of sense. In the very next revision he corrected and wholly deleted this conjecture. The Jesuit Salmeron, and after him Cardinal Baronius—distinguished defenders, to be sure, of Scripture’s most perfect perfection—assail Beza and all the “innovators,” as they are accustomed to call us, with wagonloads full of curses, as though they were the most audacious corrupters of the divine word. Would that papists always wished to join strength with us in defending the most perfect integrity of the divine Scriptures; this would be an excellent foundation for resolving many controversies. At any rate, since Casaubon had answered abundantly for this modest and retracted conjecture of Beza and Montacutius had nothing left to accuse in Beza on this point, he could not restrain his claws from digging more deeply into the wound inflicted by the papists. In part 1, page 5, of the *Origins*, he lacerates the man without cause as follows: “Who would deny that Beza could err? I acknowledge that in many places throughout those *Annotations* and their later editions he erred gravely and childishly.” It is well that this cautious censor, mindful of his reputation, did not dare produce even one of those many grave and childish errors.

Besides various solutions to the difficulties in this passage of Luke, Calvin also offers this: it could have happened that the registration proclaimed and begun by Quirinius when Christ was born was interrupted by bloody revolts of the people rioting over the census and completed only after Archelaus was exiled and Judea was reduced to a province by Quirinius. Baronius attacks this solution as inept and ridiculous. Montacutius, as often elsewhere a better champion of Baronius than of Casaubon, insults Calvin even more insolently than the cardinal himself at the page cited above: “Calvin responds and reconciles the passages ignorantly and ridiculously. They reveal in their author an immense ignorance of history and of the memory of ancient events. This exposition is consequently not only bad but impious.” A slanderous mouth against an unavenged man of the first greatness, and more abusive than the most abusive of the papal quacks! The English Laudians were lately accustomed to vomit their bitterest venom upon the leaders of the Reformation in this way.

But what, finally, is this ignorance of Calvin that is so ridiculous, inept, and foolish? Certainly it should not be turned into reproach that men of the highest excellence in his age, being appointed to greater and better studies, did not have so much leisure for history and chronology as others who devoted themselves wholly to such lesser matters. Besides, the Herculean labors of many scholars have today supplied numerous aids for resolving intricate chronological questions which were lacking in the preceding century. Yet there is nothing in the present commentary of Calvin that deserves so black a theta. What Baronius and after him Montacutius mock as inept in Calvin, Baronius himself embraces, as Montacutius does not dare deny at page 16: namely, that the registration proclaimed by Quirinius was completed only after some years. Further, Calvin conjectures that the interrupted registration was caused by the wars that disordered the Jews for some time after the registration. Is his ignorance so foolish in this? Suppose that Quirinius’ registration was joined with the imposition of tribute, as many most learned men highly skilled in history, both ancient and recent, believed with Calvin: Eusebius, Suidas, Bede, Baronius himself, Baradius, Chemnitius, Piscator, and others. Montacutius would not have dared brand them as ignorant of history, although our censor is bold enough. Given this, I say, no one will doubt that Quirinius’ registration excited great, bloody, and protracted disturbances in Judea. Gamaliel testifies at Acts 5 to the tumults of Judas the Galilean and Theudas, and Josephus relates fully the long-continued seditious arms raised over the imposition of this tribute.

I do not defend the truth of Calvin’s opinion here. Indeed, any practicing chronologist can apparently see various errors of history and chronology in Calvin’s commentary, though Montacutius’ acumen did not observe them. What I contend is that nothing in those oversights appears so absurd as to have given any cause for Baronius’ great insolence or Montacutius’ greater insolence.

Finally, we observe that although our separate calculations in this and the preceding question differ from the most illustrious Scaliger, there is no discrepancy between our computation and his if both calculations are combined. In the year of the world we are two years shorter than he, for he places the Nativity in world-year 3,947 while we place it in 3,945.
