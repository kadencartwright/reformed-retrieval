### Chapter VI

#### Fifth argument

Page 189.

If reconciliation with God and remission of sins has been procured for all
and each of mankind, with no one excepted, then the word of reconciliation
should also always be proclaimed to all and each of mankind, with no one
excepted. The reason is that no one becomes a partaker of this reconciliation
except through faith (Rom. 3:25). Faith is not engendered except through
hearing (Rom. 10:14, 17). But not only experience but also Scripture teaches
that this does not happen: Psalm 147:19–20; Acts 14:16 and 16:6. Therefore …

The Remonstrants do not deny that this argument has an appearance of validity,
but they say that it vanishes at once for those who weigh the matter properly.
Let us therefore examine their scales, in whose weighing they go before
others.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 139 | printed p. 129]

1. They say that this argument has equal force against us, who confess that
reconciliation has been procured for all nations and peoples, because the
word is not always proclaimed to all peoples and nations. Here they admit more
than one fallacy. 1. When our men say that reconciliation is procured for all
nations and peoples, they know that this is understood in a common sense, not
universally or generally. That is, no nation is excluded by name, yet not
every nation is so included that, when any sort of people assembles on some
island, there must be some among their number to whom Christ’s redemption
pertains. 2. The word can be proclaimed to all peoples and nations in such a
way that, as a common word, it is coextensive with redemption, although it is
not always preached to all. For it is not said that in every people, at every
time, there are some for whom Christ procured salvation. But those who say
that reconciliation has been procured for all and each of mortal men teach
that in every place where people live, there are always found such persons as
Christ redeemed and reconciled to the Father. Therefore, although no reason
requires that the gospel always be preached to all nations on account of the
common redemption of all nations, the universal redemption of all and each of
mankind nevertheless necessarily demands that the gospel always be available
to all.

2. They concede that the word of reconciliation is the ordinary means by
which the benefit obtained by Christ’s death is administered to people; but
they think that the time and manner in which this ought to be done must be
left to divine wisdom and justice. Then let it be left to them—who forbids
it?—provided that meanwhile nothing is maintained which conflicts with
wisdom, justice, and right reason. But it plainly conflicts with divine wisdom
that God should equally will, intend, and cause the salvation of all to be
procured as an end, and yet grant only to a few the means without which
salvation is expected in vain; that such a procurement of salvation should be
obtained for all

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 140 | printed p. 130]

from a will to save all, a procurement which brings no good to anyone except
those who know it, yet should be known only by a few. It conflicts with
justice that a universal law should be established which obliges and affects
all but is not promulgated in such a way as to become known to all and each.
Finally, it does not sufficiently accord with reason and common sense that
something should be called the ordinary means of salvation when it is not
ordinarily granted to those for whom salvation itself is intended. I will not
press these points in my own words, but instead will take from their own mouth
what may stop their mouth. We will return to them, slightly altered, the
comparisons which we received from them as the most suitable for illustrating
this mystery. A physician advertises that he has a remedy against every
illness which he would wish to communicate to all the sick, so that they may
be healed by its benefit; meanwhile, however, he signifies in no way—except
to a very few—that such a remedy has been prepared for them. Similarly, some
powerful prince says that he has prepared money to redeem all captives and
that he earnestly desires their liberation. But although he professes this,
he has nevertheless firmly determined within himself to permit no captives,
except a certain few, ever to be informed either of his intention or of this
kind preparation. Would the boasting of such a physician or prince be just?
No more can it stand that Christ died for all with respect to the divine will
or intention unless this very ready will is made known to all. With the aid of
this comparison the argument which they turn back upon us can also be blunted:
“For those to whom God wills the word of reconciliation to be proposed,
reconciliation has also been procured; but God wills it to be proposed to all
(1 Tim. 2; Mark 16); therefore …” For if that prince or physician were to say
that the price of redemption or a universal medicine should be offered
generally to all, while knowing that its report would reach no one’s ears
unless he took care that it be carried by messengers

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 141 | printed p. 131]

appointed to each person, yet sent servants only to a few for whom he had a
special regard, should he properly be said to will salvation or its means
equally for all and each? So the matter stands in the dispensation of the
word.

3. They observe that the assumption of the argument is not correctly framed,
because it says that “the word has not been proposed to all,” when it ought to
say that “the word should not be proposed to all,” which is false. But it
would have been better to say nothing than to quibble about nothing. It was
not hidden from them that when, in the major premise, it is said that, given a
general reconciliation, the word of reconciliation ought to be proclaimed to
all and each, this is understood not of the office of men—as though for this
reason it were incumbent upon them to preach to all and each—but of divine
providence, by which God would have provided and brought it about that this
word was actually proclaimed to all, if only he had prepared redemption and
salvation for all and each as surely as he had caused them to be procured.
Accordingly, and in keeping with the rules of dialectic, the consequent part
of the major premise is denied in the assumption—“this word has not been
proclaimed,” or “God did not bring it about that it was actually
proclaimed”—which is the same thing, so that the antecedent may be denied in
the conclusion: therefore God did not prepare and cause redemption and
salvation to be procured for all and each.

4. At last they come to the assumption itself and first affirm that Scripture,
with respect to the time of the New Testament, speaks against this proposition
of the brethren. Against this proposition? Which one, I ask? Namely, “the word
of reconciliation is not proclaimed to all.” I do not at all doubt that the
propositions of our men will be made at least apparently contrary to Holy
Scripture, provided that free power to fashion and refashion them is granted.
What was assumed in the argument was something else, namely that the word of
reconciliation has not always been proclaimed to all and each of mankind,
with no one excepted. They saw that Scripture nowhere spoke against this
proposition

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 142 | printed p. 132]

even as to its wording; therefore, by a figure of transfiguration, they
cast the wording into an uncertain form against which certain sayings of
Scripture seem to stand. But what do they bring forward from Scripture? That
God commands all to repent (Acts 17:30); that the sound of the Apostles went
out into all the earth (Rom. 10:18); that the gospel was preached to every
creature (Col. 1:23); and that the saving grace shone upon all people (Titus
2:11). By all these statements the Apostle means nothing other than that the
sound of the gospel is no longer confined to some corner of the world, but is
proposed commonly and publicly to every kind of person throughout the whole
world, and has already traversed a great part of it. What has this to do with
the proposition of the gospel made as a thing owed to all and each of mankind,
for whom they say that reconciliation has been procured through Christ? From
this, rather, let them learn how those more general expressions which are
customarily used in the matter of redemption can be explained from other
passages: namely, that Christ is said to have died for all and for the world
in the same way as the gospel is preached to every creature and the Spirit is
poured out upon all flesh. For Christ’s merits, the efficacy of the gospel,
and the indwelling of the Spirit are joined by God and ought not to be
separated by man.

2. After this they confess that there are certain peoples, even of the present
time, who still know absolutely nothing of this word of reconciliation. They
therefore concede the entire assumption. But although they cannot deny that it
is true, since heaven itself and earth testify that this is how the matter
stands, they nevertheless do not allow it to pass untouched. They therefore
say that from the beginning of the world, then among Noah’s posterity, and
finally through Christ’s Apostles, God proposed this word of reconciliation to
the whole world so that it might be propagated to all. This has absolutely
nothing to do with the matter. For since the benefit of reconciliation, as

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 143 | printed p. 133]

a sort of privilege, is granted not to people joined in a society but to
individuals privately, each separately for himself, and since no one becomes
a partaker of it by another’s faith, what point is there in relating to some
wretched man—who perhaps has never heard of Adam, Noah, and the Apostles—what
was done to them or by them several thousand years ago? The question about
each person in himself is whether they have the word of reconciliation
preached to them. They say that God willed it to be propagated to all. How
meager this is: to flee to an imperfect inclination when providence and its
effect are being disputed! For they understand such an inclination when they
say that God wills this. But if they say that God truly and properly willed
this, they can be sufficiently refuted by Aristotle’s words in *Rhetoric*,
book 2: “If he willed and was able, then he also does it; for all, when they
are able and willing to do something, also do it.” They are also refuted by
Christ’s express words in John 3:8: “The wind blows where it wills; so is
everyone born of the Spirit.” The Remonstrants saw, as it were, a blow of this
kind threatening their preceding statement, and they also strove to guard
against it, so far as could be done. Therefore they saw fit to inquire into
the cause why the word is not preached everywhere. But, at least in this
place, they stir up this entire dispute in vain. For nothing was said in the
argument about the cause of this matter; nor is it possible for that cause
which does whatever it wills, both in heaven and on earth, to be hindered by
any cause. Nevertheless, they eagerly bring forward something about the cause
of this matter. And so, after declaiming against the position of our men on
predestination—a matter which does not belong here—they admonish first that,
in every outcome, this statement of the Apostle in Romans 11:32 must
steadfastly be retained, that God “shut up all things under sin, that he might
have mercy
upon all.” In this matter they have me as very much an assenter, but with this
condition: that the genuine meaning of this saying be sought from the same
Apostle as his own best interpreter. He explained it in these

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 144 | printed p. 134]

words in Galatians 3:22: “Scripture shut up all things under sin, that the
promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to believers,” where he shows
that by “all” he understands all believers. Having laid this down, they add
that the reason for this fact is for the most part hidden from us. We freely
accept this, provided that by “cause” they understand something prior to the
will of God; for this cannot be hidden from us: that the will of God itself is
at least some cause both of the withholding and of the communication of those
things which depend upon it. And in hidden matters of this sort it is always
best and safest to rest in the will of God as the supreme cause. Christ
himself commends this modesty to us by his own example in Matthew 11:26: “Yes,
Father, because it so pleased you.” They next descend to the point that they
give us true hope of hearing the cause; but as before, they disappoint our
expectation. 1. They say that it could be that the gospel was propagated more
widely than we suppose, although it was later obscured and collapsed. We do
not deny this, but we say that it could not have reached all and each, with no
one excepted, which is the point in question. 2. They say that blame for this
could have lain partly in the negligence of the heralds and partly in the
ingratitude of the people who hindered the gospel’s course. There is no doubt
that some blame inheres in them. But 1. it would be wretched for the church if
its salvation depended upon other people’s choice, so that the only ones to
come to the knowledge of God were those whom negligent and ungrateful people
do not wish to defraud. 2. When the question concerns heralds and prophets,
that old saying in 1 Samuel 10:12 must be called to mind: “Who is their
father?” Namely, it is he who knows how so to establish Saul among the
Prophets and Saul among the Apostles that they perform their office with the
greatest care. 3. Finally, who gives force to the gospel or opens a door to
his own unless it is the Spirit of God? When God prepares a way for himself,
the loftiest hills give way (Isa. 40:3).

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 145 | printed p. 135]

3. They say that when God pleases, he can use another manner. Undoubtedly he
can; but if, among all those who are without the gospel, he uses other means
sufficient for salvation, then the preaching of the gospel is not the
ordinary means of salvation. For since their number is far the greatest, the
manner of obtaining salvation which prevails among them must be called
ordinary, and all who have the gospel must be saved by an extraordinary way. 4.
They admonish that the cause must be sought in the individual’s own preceding
sins. We do not deny this altogether, but both Scripture and experience
compel us to deny that people’s preceding works are the law and measure of
divine calling: Deuteronomy 9:4–6. 5. They say that God does not allow himself
to be without testimony among those who have neither the gospel nor the
written Law, and indeed testimony of such weight that every excuse is taken
away from them. They confirm this with various testimonies. But it is
astonishing that assertions of this kind are heaped up by them, since they
neither contribute anything to the proposed argument nor have ever been
called into doubt by our men. We are not permitted to be so idle as to seek
by guesswork where such things are tending.
