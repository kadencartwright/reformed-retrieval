# CHAPTER I OF THE EXAMINATION OF ARMINIANISM

## On Holy Scripture and its subject matter; on fundamental and
non-fundamental articles; likewise on ignorance and error, and on knowledge
and faith concerning them

### John 5:39

*Search the Scriptures; they testify about me.*

It is asked: Is Scripture—or the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture—the judge
of controversies? Or has God left us in his Word no rule that coercively binds
consciences, but only a rule that judges directively?

The Remonstrants say in their *Apology*, chapter 7, folio 3, that Scripture
cannot be the judge, because the law and the judge are distinct when the
dispute concerns the meaning of the law. The Roman Catholics appoint the
Church as the peremptory and infallible judge of Scripture, of the meaning of
Scripture, and of all controversies of-*

[Printed p. 2 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 55 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*faith. The Arminians, on the contrary, do not wish it to be even a ministerial
judge, or one invested with any coercive ministerial power, but merely a
directing rule—and directing in such a way as the meaning of Scripture might
suggest itself—so that one who embraces a heterodox meaning does not sin. They
then deny that there is any infallible judgment of the Church, even when it
judges according to Scripture. They teach that the very phrasing of Scripture,
or arguments elicited from Scripture by our labor, is only a rule that judges
directively, insofar as each person can form from them his own judgment about
salvation, as from the most certain rule left to us by God. Thus, one who does
not assent to them cannot be constrained by any other infallible judgment as
an indubitable rule.

But we teach that Scripture is sufficient for coercively binding decisions of
controversies. 1. Because Christ appeals to Scripture, John 5:39: *“Search the
Scriptures, for in them you think that you have eternal life; and they are what
testify about me.”* Likewise John 5:47: *“If you do not believe the writings of
Moses, how will you believe my words?”* Paul appeals to Moses and the Prophets
in his controversies with the Jews, Acts 24:14–15 and 26:22.

2. Because conscience would perpetually waver and would have nothing on which
to stand unless Scripture were the endpoint of controversies.

3. Because *“Scripture is a lamp to the feet and a light for the-*

[Printed p. 3 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 56 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*steps,”* Psalm 119:105.

4. Because if Scripture were mere law and nothing more, the judge and the law
would necessarily be distinguished. But because Scripture is the rule of
judgment and at the same time has the Holy Spirit joined to it—who decides the
case for those from whom the Gospel is not hidden and convicts those who speak
against it—it is therefore unnecessary for law and judge to be distinguished
here as human law and an earthly judge are distinguished.

5. It is absurd for the judgment of the Church when it judges according to
Scripture not to be the rule of controversies. For this is to deny God and the
Holy Spirit speaking from Scripture, which the Church sets forth as a
minister. If it cannot decide controversies of faith as a ministerial judge,
it would leave everyone in doubt.

6. If no rule has been left to us in the forum of conscience that judges
coercively, but only one that directs and leaves people to the private judgment
of their consciences, no one would be bound in conscience to believe Scripture.
Instead, all believers would live amid conjectures and private dreams.

7. No one would sin by *“twisting the Scriptures to his own destruction,”*
although many are said to do so, 2 Peter 3:16. For who sins by twisting a law
that does not coercively bind the conscience at all? If Scripture does not
compel people to receive the true meaning intended by the Holy Spirit, then
one who attaches an erroneous and false meaning to it does not act against a
due obliga-*

[Printed p. 4 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 57 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*tion. Consequently, one who is destitute of the truth and twists Scripture
does not sin.

## On fundamental and non-fundamental articles

### Hebrews 6:1–2

*Therefore, leaving behind the elementary discourse for those who are
beginners in Christ, let us press on to maturity, not laying again a foundation
of repentance from dead works and of faith in God, of teaching about baptisms
and the laying on of hands, and of the resurrection of the dead and eternal
judgment.*

In these words the apostle enumerates the fundamental heads and catechetical
elements of the Christian religion, namely, the doctrine concerning God and
Christ the Savior. He enumerates teaching: 1. On repentance. 2. On faith,
which concerns the one God in essence, distinguished in three persons, and the
Savior's person and offices. 3. On the sacraments. 4. By the synecdoche of the
laying on of hands, on ecclesiastical discipline. 5. On the resurrection of
the dead. And 6. On eternal judgment.

This disputation on fundamentals is necessary: 1. For an exact definition of
the Church, for the true Church is that which holds the foundation. 2. For
cultivating communion with the true Church or separation from a false
synagogue. 3. For examining that invention of the Arminians concerning freedom
of prophesying. 4. For determining something about the salvation of the
heathen. 5. For refuting

[Printed p. 5 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 58 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

that impious doctrine of the innovators concerning the reconciliation of
Evangelicals with Roman Catholics and Arminians, and with other impure sects.
6. For overthrowing the atheism of those who maintain that a person *can be
saved in any religion.* 7. For fixing the limits of mutual toleration and
brotherly love among godly people who disagree about things that are not
necessary. 8. For investigating the marks of the true Church, and so forth.

The Arminians err miserably concerning fundamentals. I set out their theses on
these matters here.

1. Thesis. Because Scripture is perfect and clear, only those things are
fundamental *which are in Scripture expressly and in so many words, or by a
consequence as necessary and obvious to everyone as this: “It runs; therefore
it moves.”* See the Remonstrants' *Apology, Examination of the Censure of the
Confession*, folios 6–7 and chapter 1, folio 3, and *Against Vedelius*, book 1,
chapter 6.

But to the contrary: 1. Although Scripture is clear, *“the Gospel is
nevertheless hidden from those who perish.”* The Jews neither see nor are able
to see in Scripture—whether expressly or by a consequence clearer than the
sun—the Messiah who is Θεάνθρωπος, God-man (God's own only-begotten Son, who
was to be incarnate in the fullness of time; to be born in a lowly condition
from the Virgin Mary at Bethlehem; to be despised and subjected to an
ignominious, accursed, violent, and bloody death, thereby atoning for our sins;
to fulfill and abrogate the sacrifices and ceremonies of the Mosaic law; to
rise from the dead on the third day; to ascend into heaven and sit at the
right hand of God the Father until he returns to judge-*

[Printed p. 6 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 59 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*the living and the dead; whose kingdom was to be not earthly and bodily but
spiritual, heavenly, and eternal; who was promised and prefigured as such in
the Old Testament and manifested in the New Testament). For *“a veil lies over
their hearts when the Old Testament is read,”* 2 Corinthians 3:14–15.
Therefore, according to them, the article concerning *Christ incarnate, dead,
raised, and glorified* was not fundamental.

2. Before the Prophets were committed to writing, the resurrection of the dead
was likewise not a fundamental article. For Christ's inference from Exodus
3:6—*“God is the God of the living, not of the dead; therefore the dead will
rise”*—is not as obvious as this: *“It runs; therefore it moves.”* 3. There is
no fundamental article which some heretics have not assailed, as experience,
Scripture, and histories testify. 4. This is said by Arminians, because they
teach that Socinians, Anabaptists, anti-Trinitarians, Arians, Tritheists,
Sabellians, and Roman Catholics do not err at all in fundamentals. Is Christ
consubstantial and ὁμοούσιος—of one essence—with the Father? Is there one God
and three distinct persons? Does God's providence care for, provide for, and
determine all things? The Arminians boldly dare to assert that these are
contained in Scripture neither expressly nor by a consequence beyond all
exception.

2. Arminian thesis. The doctrines necessary to be known for salvation are very
few, namely, only those

[Printed p. 7 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 60 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

few things which, as Casaubon impiously said in his letter to Cardinal Perron,
*“are approved almost equally by all who demand to be called Christians.”*
They say that Athanasius exceeded the limit of his jurisdiction because he
said of his creed, ὅστις βούλεται σωθῆναι—*“whoever wishes to be saved must
believe,”* folio 6 of the *Apology*. They also say that controversies among the
godly and learned are not fundamental. They number Socinians, Arians, Jesuits,
Pelagians, and Semi-Pelagians among the godly and learned, as they teach in the
*Apology*, chapter 25, folio 280, and chapter 3, folios 53–54. They then measure
piety by a civil life before other people, and faith in the fundamentals not
by God's Word but by a life that is civilly upright, whereas piety ought to be
measured by true faith. This is the piety of the Pharisees: those who are not
adulterers, murderers, or poisoners are godly to them, even if they are Arians
or Socinians. But God's Word is the sole rule of piety.

2. Even if they are so few that they can all be comprehended under two general
heads—faith and obedience—or perhaps under the single word *theology*, yet the
things to be believed, done, and sought are not few when enumerated in detail,
as Gisbertus Voetius rightly notes in his *Treatise on Fundamentals*. In this
way someone could say that the fundamentals in astronomy are few: namely, to
know the nature and motions of a movable body around the center of the world;
that the fundamentals in Latin grammar are few: namely, only this one thing,
to speak Latin elegantly; and-*

[Printed p. 8 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 61 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*that the fundamentals in agriculture are few: namely, only to sow and reap.
But *a deceiver deals in generalities.*

3. To sew cushions beneath all mortals—as people called, equipped with
universal grace and the ability to believe, redeemed by Christ's death, and
eternally loved by God unto salvation—they have stretched the true Church
with so capacious a bosom that Arians, Socinians, Roman Catholics, Pelagians,
anti-Trinitarians, and all heretics *“who demand to be called Christians,”* as
Casaubon speaks, are numbered among the true Church. Only Jews and Turks are
excluded from the orthodox churches—and perhaps not even they. For the Jews,
who do not see that the Old Testament teaches in express words, or by a
consequence obvious and conspicuous to all like the noonday sun, that Mary's
son is the promised Messiah, err by a simple error. Yet such an error is not
damnable according to them, namely, the Arminians.

3. Arminian thesis. No agreement and no confession of faith should be sworn,
believed with divine faith, or adhered to any longer than until, by the spirit
of free prophesying, the fundamentals seem horrible to them and discordant
with human reason.

But to the contrary. We think that one must swear to a confession not formally,
insofar as it has been drawn up by the Church, but materially, insofar as it is
God's Word; and conditionally, not absolutely. The formula as such is not the
object of faith, but God's Word is.

[Printed p. 9 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 62 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

2. But here the Remonstrants imply that there is no lawful confession of faith
to which one should swear *unless it expresses in the bare words of Scripture
only those things necessary to be believed for salvation, and does not express
different judgments about religion concerning doctrines that are not
precisely necessary to be believed.* For on this basis one ought not even swear
to Romans 8 and 9 except in this general fashion: *“I subscribe and solemnly
swear to those chapters according to the meaning—whatever it may finally
prove to be, whether known or unknown to the orthodox Church—which the Holy
Spirit intends.”* In this way a Jew, Turk, heretic, child, ignoramus, or madman
to whom nothing about Scripture has been revealed can swear to the Apostles'
Creed, the Decalogue, and the Lord's Prayer, while the person swearing is an
ἄθεος—atheist—an Arian, Papist, Socinian, Anabaptist, and so forth. For no one
swears from faith to a formula expressed in no words other than the bare words
of Scripture. Indeed, no one even swears to the New Testament unless it is
understood, expounded, and believed in this or that manner according to the
analogy of faith. Otherwise, all heretics who acknowledge Scripture to be the
Word of God can swear and subscribe to a confession of the only kind the
Arminians acknowledge as an object of faith. They nevertheless can be ἄθεοι,
people without any faith, Arians, and heretics allied by oath against Christ
and his truth. Such were the Jews, who knew that Christ was the Son of God and
had truly been raised from the dead, but maliciously buried that knowledge and
his resurrection by their lies, bribes, and pay-*

[Printed p. 10 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 63 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*ments.

3. I cannot divine what they mean by doctrines that are *precisely necessary*,
or what the other member of the distinction opposed to the word *precisely*
is. For they undoubtedly imply that many who have heard nothing about Christ
are saved without knowledge of the fundamentals, and they extend this
knowledge only to those who are in the visible Church.

4. They cry out that confessions are established as judges of controversies.
We say that orthodox confessions are materially Scripture itself. But is it a
crime to establish Scripture as the judge of controversies?

5. The apostle says in 1 Corinthians 3:11: *“No one can lay another foundation
besides the one laid, which is Jesus Christ.”* One foundation is personal or
real and is Christ himself; another is dogmatic and is the fundamental
doctrine concerning Christ, the common subject of all theology. It follows
from this passage that some things are fundamental and necessarily required
for salvation, while some things built upon the foundation are not necessary.
These are *“curious questions,”* as Calvin says, *“not false doctrines,”* as
Beza observes at the same place, *“which are not properly said to be built on
this foundation except perhaps in outward appearance.”* Or, as Pareus notes in
his commentary on 1 Corinthians, Paul *“means vain, curious, alien, frivolous
doctrines, by which they did not indeed completely remove the founda-*

[Printed p. 11 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 64 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*tion, but nevertheless greatly obscured it.”* Bellarmine says in *On
Purgatory*, book 1, chapter 5: *“It is not understood as heretical or evil
doctrine, but nevertheless as curious, useless, and superfluous doctrine.”*
These superstructures seem to me to be what Paul calls ζητήσεις καὶ
λογομαχίας—“questions and battles over words”—in 1 Timothy 6:4.

6. A distinction must be made between matters or articles of faith and
fundamental articles. For matters of faith to be believed with divine faith
are fourfold: 1. Fundamentals. 2. Supra-fundamentals. 3. Around-fundamentals.
4. Beyond-fundamentals. (Permit these designations for the sake of instruction.)

[Editorial erratum, PDF 52: at p. 11, line 7 the source prints `articulus`; the
errata directs `read articulos`, which is adopted here.]

Fundamentals are those things that concern faith's vital parts, soul, and life.
Simple ignorance of them condemns—not indeed all mortals, but everyone in the
visible Church, if we except the mentally incapable, the deaf, and infants.
They are parts of the Church materially and, from God's side, often
intentionally and in first act, but not formally and in second act, because
they are capable neither of God's active calling nor of being passively
called. But this ignorance does not formally condemn those outside the visible
Church who have heard nothing about Christ; rather, as punishment, it renders
them incurable. Just as salutary medicine, when rejected, actually kills the
body and does so culpably, so also salutary doctrine concerning God and Christ
his Son, the Mediator and Savior of humanity, when delivered and rejected,
actually and culpably kills the soul, and this contempt casts the soul into
eternal death-*

[Printed p. 12 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 65 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*. But salutary medicine neither offered nor rejected renders people incurable
as a loss; it does not kill them culpably. So too salutary doctrine about God
and Christ, when not communicated or rejected, kills the soul, but not as
fault.

The second kind consists of supra-fundamentals, which follow from the
foundations by a plain and necessary consequence. These are also fundamentals
secondarily and materially, and error concerning them and implicit ignorance
of them condemns. For those who deny such things overturn the foundation;
those who deny clear and evident consequences deny their antecedents, John
8:19.

3. Around-fundamentals are all things revealed in the Word, including
occasional, chronological, and historical matters—for example, that Paul left
his cloak, books, and parchments at Troas, 2 Timothy 4:13; that Abraham begot
Isaac; and astronomical matters concerning Orion and the Pleiades, Arcturus,
and the northern constellations, Job 38:31–32. These are indeed matters of
faith that must be believed by necessity of command, but they are not
fundamentals necessary by necessity of means, such that no one could be saved
without knowing them. Three sins are to be considered in these matters: 1.
Ignorance. 2. Weakness. 3. Stubbornness or obstinacy. Ignorance of these
around-fundamentals is a sin in many people and condemns by its desert and
potential, but not actually. Everyone in the visible Church is bound—when God
gives ability, aids, and opportunity—

[Printed p. 13 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 66 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

especially teachers—to know these things. But many who are ignorant of them
are saved. 2. Many, clouded by weakness, passions, and temptations, sin in these
matters; this sin condemns in power and by its desert, but not actually. 3.
There is an obstinate and malicious opposition to these matters. For example,
if someone maliciously denies and attacks the miracles performed by Moses,
Elijah, Christ, and the apostles, his error is fundamental—not because the
subject matter of these things is the foundation of faith, but because he sins
against a fundamental principle and denies that whatever God clearly and
openly reveals in the Word is true. Such obstinacy ought to be retracted by a
confession; when it is not so retracted, it actually condemns.

4. Beyond-fundamentals are also ἀδιάφορα—indifferent things—which in themselves
and by their nature are neither forbidden nor commanded, such as eating meats
or marrying this woman or that one. Their practice, which without faith is
bounded by the Word as regards the general rules that they serve edification,
decency, and piety, is likewise bounded by the Word. Ignorance of them in this
manner condemns by its desert, while obstinate ignorance, which is called a
depraved disposition, actually condemns. When malicious obstinacy is also
added, it is a fundamental error, just as obstinacy concerning things revealed
in the Word is, because to resist the light of the Word clearly set forth even
in the most indifferent matters is to deny God's authority in the Word.

7. Things of the third and fourth kinds must, however, be believed with divine
faith because of that same divine authority-*

[Printed p. 14 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 67 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*. For the same God who says that Christ came into the world to save sinners
says that eight souls were in Noah's ark; yet the former is fundamental and
the latter is by no means so. It therefore follows that the essential
rationale of a fundamental article is not to be taken from the authority of
God who commands—at least not as its formal rationale—but from the necessity
and weight of the proposed article. For a fundamental is that without
knowledge of which no one can be saved; but people can be saved without
knowledge of many historical and chronological matters. It also follows that
most who are saved can be ignorant of many miraculous acts of God, whereas we
cannot be ignorant of very many of our own moral acts, such as faith and
repentance. Consequently, certain human acts belong among the fundamentals
while certain divine acts do not. Thus, the rationale of a fundamental article
is taken not from the excellence of its object but from the necessity of the
thing that must be known for salvation.

8. The necessity of knowing the fundamentals is twofold: one for obtaining
salvation and another for external communion with the true Church. This
twofold necessity must be discussed here.

Davenant says in *On Ecclesiastical Peace*, p. 28: *“I hold that an article is
to be regarded as fundamental when, by the will of God who reveals it, it is so
necessary to be known and believed for obtaining salvation and eternal
blessedness that ignorance of it—and much more opposition to it—incurs a
manifest danger of losing eternal life.”*

[Printed p. 15 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 68 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

Estius defines fundamentals in book 3, distinction 25, section 2, as things
*“distinct knowledge of which is necessary for ordering one's life rightly.”*
This definition is much too narrow, because true faith is required in addition
to a rightly ordered life.

Potter, in *Charity Mistaken*, chapters 8 and 9, response to section 7, defines
catholic truths as *“those which principally and essentially concern faith,
which principally constitute the Church, and which in the ordinary course
must necessarily be believed by every Christian who wishes to be saved.”* But
Potter gravely errs in setting over against these the non-fundamentals, which,
he says, are disputable on either side and can admit of a *non liquet*—“it is
not clear.” For many things are non-fundamental which are not disputable and
are not, as he says, indeterminable by Scripture. That eight souls were in
Noah's ark is such a matter, since it is openly decided in Scripture.

For Roman Catholics generally, however, fundamentals are those things *which
are of the faith, and whatever things the Church proposes as such and as
already defined.* But to the contrary: 1. A fundamental article is fundamental
in itself before it is defined as fundamental by the Church, just as God's
Word is not God's Word because the Church defines it to be God's Word, but
because God has revealed it to us as his Word. 2. If the Church can make
non-fundamentals into-*

[Printed p. 16 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 69 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*fundamentals by its ecclesiastical definition, then it can make fundamentals
into non-fundamentals. For there is an equal power to make fundamentals and to
make non-fundamentals, just as there is an equal omnipotence to create and to
annihilate. But this is absurd, for in this way the Church could legitimize
Arminianism and define that *Christ is God, consubstantial with the Father,*
not to be a fundamental article. 3. The defining Church does not have greater
power than God defining in Scripture. But not everything God defines in Holy
Scripture is fundamental. Thus, he defined that *Eve, having been deceived by
the serpent, deceived Adam,* which is not fundamental.

Bellarmine says in *On the Authority of Councils*, book 2, chapter 12: *“When
councils define, they do not make something to be of infallible truth, but
declare it.”*

Vincent of Lérins says in *Against Heresies*, chapter 32: *“In short, what has
the Church ever attempted through the decrees of councils except that what was
formerly believed simply should afterward be believed more diligently?”*

Laud says in *Against Fisher*, sections 32–33, numbers 4–5, pp. 226–27: *“An
ecumenical council cannot easily err in fundamentals; or if it does err, it is
nevertheless valid, and external obedience must be shown to it, at least
insofar as obedience consists in silence, patience, and abstention, until it is
made plain by Scripture or a demonstration to the contrary that it has erred,
or until another coun-*

[Printed p. 17 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 70 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*cil of equal authority overturns it; nor is a general council to be rescinded
by a private spirit.”* I answer: He holds heretically that a general council
cannot easily err in fundamentals; or, if it does err, that faith must be
suspended—perhaps until the end of the world—until another general council is
convened.

Gerson says in his *Treatise on the Declaration of Truths Which Must Be
Believed*, and so forth, number/section 4, part 1, p. 414: *“Not everything the
Church delivers under a judicial definition”* (in a council) *“must be believed
by necessity of salvation, but only those things which it so delivers with the
universal consent of the whole Church concurring, implicitly or explicitly,
truly or interpretatively.”*

9. Fundamentals seem to me to be defined as follows: *They are the principal
heads concerning Christ, the sole foundation of salvation, which are either
expressly contained in Scripture or, by a consequence evident to everyone not
maliciously blinded, necessarily pertain to those two adequate means of our
communion with Christ—namely faith and repentance, or Christian obedience—
without knowledge of which a person cannot be saved.*

They are: 1. Principal heads concerning Christ, such as most of the articles
contained in the creed they call Apostolic. Many occasional, chronological,
genealogical, topographical, and historical matters are therefore not
fundamental, for they are not the first and most common elements of the
Christian reli-*

[Printed p. 18 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 71 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*gion. 2. Doctrines about Christ the Savior, or matters very closely pertaining
to our communion with him, which do not necessarily cohere with this
foundation are not fundamental, even if they can be matters of faith. For
Bellarmine says not badly in *On the Church*, book 1, chapter 14, section 5:
*“Many things are of the faith which are not necessary for salvation.”*

Hence the ignorance of the formalists is astonishing. They contend that the
English ceremonies must be embraced from a duty of conscience toward
superiors because they are not fundamental or necessary for salvation, but are
in themselves ἀδιάφορα—indifferent things. It is as though this were an
adequate division of things to be believed and done: some are fundamental and
others indifferent; likewise, some are fundamental and necessary for salvation
and others are not necessary. For believing that Paul left his cloak at Troas,
that Saul persecuted David, and similar things is neither fundamental nor
necessary for salvation. Yet everyone who reads God's Word must believe them
unto salvation. Willing to be ignorant of them is not indifferent, but a sin;
or, when these things have been set before someone either by reading or by the
public preaching of pastors, not believing them is a sin that condemns by its
desert. Therefore, although the ceremonies are not fundamental, it does not
follow that they are indifferent or that practicing them is lawful.

3. Fundamentals are either expressly in Scripture-*

[Printed p. 19 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 72 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*or follow by evident consequence. Hence things which are disputed in
themselves, as far as godly and learned men can see—such as questions about
degrees among the glorified and about the new heaven and new earth after the
dissolution of the world—are not fundamental.

Hence, 2. All traditions of the Roman Catholics and Jews are struck from the
register of fundamentals.

3. From this it follows that more remote and obscure conclusions, which are
deduced less evidently and more obscurely from the cardinal elements of faith
and obedience, are not fundamental articles.

4. Nor are any conclusions by which the doctrine concerning Christ, faith, and
obedience is not overthrown, as Cameron observes in *On the Church*, pp.
272–73. Hence:

4. Fundamentals necessarily pertain to the doctrine of faith and obedience.
See Beza, *Minor Works*, volume 2, *On the Marks of the Catholic Church*, p.
141. For without faith and repentance no one can be saved.

5. I say, *without knowledge of which no one can be saved.* That is, no one can
be saved without explicit knowledge of certain first principles; but implicit
knowledge suffices for certain fundamentals deduced from those principles, as
we shall hear.

Hence the following questions concerning fundamentals must be discussed for a
clearer unfolding of the definition. 1. Are things deduced by consequence from
the most common principles believed with true faith?-*

[Printed p. 20 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 73 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*2. What evidence of consequence is required for an article to be called
fundamental? 3. Since all fundamentals necessarily concern faith and
obedience, can the number of fundamentals therefore be determined? 4. What
knowledge of the fundamentals is required? What explicit faith, and what
implicit faith, suffices for salvation? 5. What is a fundamental error, and
what is vincible and invincible ignorance? 6. Since knowledge of the
fundamentals is necessary for salvation, what kind of necessity is this, and
how many forms does it have? 7. To what extent can those who err in
fundamentals be tolerated? 8. To what extent is communion with, or separation
from, a Church which does not err in fundamentals lawful, and to what extent
is it damnable? Here also: Is the Roman Church a true Church?
