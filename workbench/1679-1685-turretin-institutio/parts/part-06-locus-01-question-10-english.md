QUESTION X

Is Human Reason to Be Permitted a Judgment of Contradiction in Matters of
Faith? Affirmative.

[Marginal heading: Occasion of the question.]

I. This controversy too is conducted between us and the Lutherans, especially
the ubiquitarians. Because we are accustomed to object that their opinion
concerning the multilocation or omnipresence of Christ's body is
contradictory, they are accustomed to reply that judgment concerning
contradiction in articles of faith belongs not to reason but to Holy
Scripture. See Osiander, *Enchiridion against Calvin*, chapter 1, question 3;
Mentzer, *Elenchus of the Errors of Sadeel*, on argument 6; Stegmann, *Against
Photinus*, disputation 2, question 3. Hence Meisner, *Metaphysical Questions*
3, against Calvin, says that this axiom—“Contradictories are altogether
impossible, because for the same thing to be and not to be is impossible”—is
most dangerous in divine matters. To establish the state of the question,
three things must be distinguished here: the reason that judges, the
principle from which the judgment is formed, and the norm of consequence by
which the judgment is formed. 1. Here *reason* means reason not blinded and
depraved through sin, but restored and illuminated by the Holy Spirit. 2. The
principles are axioms not known by nature or established by human authority,
but delivered in Scripture. 3. The norm by which reason is directed and
confirmed in drawing out and applying truth from Scripture consists of the
rules of good consequence implanted by God in the rational creature. This
norm, however, is not the norm of truth itself, which is solely the Word of
God and the first normative truth, but only the norm of consequence. Through
its intervention, what follows from the truth and what does not is known and
discerned more certainly.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question is not whether reason can by itself attain the mysteries of
faith, for no one denies that very many of them far surpass the grasp of
reason. Rather, it is whether reason can recognize the contradiction between
propositions, which can be discerned only from the laws of nature and reason.
2. The question is not about an absolute and unlimited judgment of decision,
but about a judgment of discretion, which is limited and bound to the Word
and must always be proved from it, 1 Thessalonians 5:21; 1 John 4:1. Even if
this judgment does not always bring conviction to another, it nevertheless
suffices for one's own conviction.

III. We therefore state the matter as follows: judgment of contradiction
belongs to reason, but 1. always with revelation presupposed, together with
its truth, which is of itself worthy of faith; 2. organically and
ministerially, not despotically and authoritatively; 3. with recourse to
Scripture itself, which sufficiently interprets itself and requires no
interpreter outside itself by whom its meaning may be established. Thus
reason, illuminated by the Holy Spirit through the Word, can from the Word
itself, according to the rules of good and necessary consequence, consider
and judge how the parts of doctrine cohere with one another, and what follows
from them and what does not.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that judgment of contradiction belongs to
reason.]

IV. The reasons are: 1. Scripture commands this judgment more than once,
Matthew 7:15 and 16:6; Colossians 2:8; 1 Thessalonians 5:21; Hebrews 5:14. 2.
The examples of the saints confirm it, such as those of the Bereans, Acts
17:11, and the Corinthians, 1 Corinthians 10:15. 3. The end of Scripture
teaches it. That end is the perfection of the man of God in the knowledge of
truth and the conviction of those who contradict, 1 Timothy 3:16; Titus 1:9,
and it cannot be attained without this judgment. 4. The use of reason proves
it, because just as

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 31 | IA leaf 85 | PDF p. 85]

the knowledge of the affirmation or negation of propositions and the doctrine
of contradiction belong to reason, so too does judgment concerning
contradiction belong to it. Unless this is established, a very wide door is
opened for heretics and fanatics of every kind to thrust upon us their
fabrications and monstrous opinions, nor could there ever be any true
contradiction.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

V. Although this judgment is made subjectively by a human being, it must not
therefore be considered *human*, as though it were conceived originally in
the human manner from human reasonings or inclinations. Rather, it is
*divine*, because it is brought forth from the light and impulse of the Holy
Spirit. In this sense Paul says in 1 Corinthians 2:14 that the spiritual
person judges all things—that is, spiritually, from the light of grace, not
from the reason of the natural person.

VI. Reason cannot judge the power of God from natural principles. Yet nothing
prevents it from judging that power from the Word, Matthew 19:26; 2 Timothy
1:12; Psalm 115:3. Thus judgment of contradiction is dangerous with respect
to blinded reason when it judges from corrupt principles, but we speak of
illuminated reason pronouncing from the Word.

VII. Reason must be taken captive, 2 Corinthians 10:5, insofar as it exalts
itself against Christ and his gospel, but it can be heard when it serves him
and judges from the gospel.

VIII. The darkness of the human intellect does not prevent sound reason from
being able to judge the truth of connections and thus contradiction. We
indeed grant that it cannot judge the truth of propositions, since it is
ignorant of that truth by itself and it must be sought from the Law and the
Testimony. But it does not follow from this that reason cannot judge the
contradiction of statements, opinions, and interpretations that human beings
form concerning these mysteries.

IX. Reason can judge not only a *direct and formal contradiction*, which
consists of the same terms and an express affirmation and negation, but also
an *indirect and implicit contradiction*, which is deduced by necessary
consequence. The following is such a contradiction: Christ's blood cleanses
us from every sin; therefore, there is no purgatory. Christ ascended into
heaven; therefore, he is not everywhere.

X. To recognize a contradiction, it is not necessary to know distinctly the
things themselves and their essence. It is sufficient that the truth of the
connections be known, from which a judgment concerning the contradiction of
a proposition can easily be rendered.

XI. Although many things appear contradictory to various people when they
are not so in reality, it does not follow that something which is not
contradictory will appear so to people who use reason well and think rightly.

XII. It is one thing to speak of sound and regenerate reason in the abstract;
another to speak of it in the concrete, as it exists in this or that corrupt
subject. We grant judgment to reason not in the latter sense, but only in the
former.

XIII. It is one thing to take every judgment away from reason and establish
that it is absolutely not to be trusted; another to establish that not only
those things reason knows are to be believed, but that in a more perfect
school those things which reason cannot deliver and attain are to be learned.
We do not affirm the former, but the latter.

XIV. Although reason is permitted the judgment of contradiction in matters
of faith, it does not follow that the human intellect becomes the rule of
divine power, as though God could not accomplish more than human reason can
conceive. For it is one thing for God to be able to do something *above
nature* and human conception, which is stated most truly in Ephesians 3:20;
another for him to do something *contrary to nature* and the principles of
natural light, which is utterly false. Nor is the power of God thus limited
to the rule of our intellect. Rather, our mind judges from the Word what,
according to the nature of the thing established by God, must be called
possible or impossible.

XV. Reason cannot judge the power of God so as to be able to comprehend it
thoroughly, or so as to regard as impossible the things that are above nature,
or so as to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 32 | IA leaf 86 | PDF p. 86]

introduce something into theology under the pretext of divine power unless
that thing is first established from the Word—much less so as to flee to the
omnipotence of God in order to overthrow something which the Word of God
establishes certainly and clearly. But reason judges the power of God rightly
insofar as it weighs that power according to the nature of things and his
Word, so that something is called impossible and contradictory not because it
appears so to this or that person, but because it is so in reality, since it
conflicts with the nature of the thing and with the truth revealed in the
Word.

XVI. It is one thing for reason to be able to judge what is false,
contradictory, and opposed both to the most universal principles of reason and
to revelation—for example, in the matter of transubstantiation, ubiquity, the
adoration of creatures, and the like. It is another for reason to have to be
the judge and indeed the norm of revealed truth and of the mysterious, which
is exalted far above its sphere, delivered by God, whose thoughts infinitely
transcend human thoughts, and who wishes reason to be taken captive to the
obedience of faith, 2 Corinthians 10:5. We deny the latter, but affirm the
former.
