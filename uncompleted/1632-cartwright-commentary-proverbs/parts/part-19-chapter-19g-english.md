V. 23. Reverence for Jehovah has regard to life; and one who is satisfied will lodge for the night, so that he will not be visited by evil.

In יראת (*reverentia*, reverence) there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole worship of God. In ילין (*pernoctabit*, will lodge for the night) there is likewise a synecdoche of the part—namely, night—for the whole time of life, whether by night or by day. In יפקד (*visitabitur*, will be visited) there is a metaphor, or rather a personification, by which the person of a friend is assigned to penal evil, who comes to visit the mind because of an inward connection.

V. 24. The sluggard hides his hand in his sleeve; he will not even bring it back to his mouth.

The purpose is to affect the sluggard with shame, so that both he himself may repent of his sloth and others may flee sloth as a shameful thing.

In chapter 6 of this book the idle man is reproached because he snores into broad daylight and is with difficulty drawn away from his bed. It would have been fitting, moreover, that after remaining fastened to his bed and bedstead longer than is proper, then at last, when he had awakened and risen from bed, he should apply vigorous effort in discharging his business, so that he might make up with excessive, untimely labour for what was left undone through his more prolonged sleep. But behold how, after leaving his bed and rushing forth, he may rightly be seen not yet to have shaken off sleep completely, but to sleep while awake.

When he says that the man hides his hand in his sleeve or bosom, he seems to allude to the gesture of the slothful, who are very commonly accustomed to put a hand into the bosom or sleeve, or to hold it under the armpit. In this matter the sluggard seems to display an unusual and extraordinary manner of life, because by nature and the law of all nations the face and hands are customarily left bare, not clothed and covered like the other parts of the body. The hands are especially bared when someone girds himself to undertake some work. By this concealment of the hand in the sleeve, therefore, Solomon designates the highest degree of idleness and cessation from all business, because almost all honourable and lawful crafts are practised by the hand. Hence in Prov. 13.11 riches acquired by the hand are praised and are opposed to riches obtained through vanity; and the same is confirmed in Ps. 90. In the last verse, the Prophet, asking from God a prosperous outcome for all pursuits and labours, earnestly entreats him to direct and confirm the work of the hands, Eph. 4.28. Therefore, just as the excellent woman in Solomon, Prov. last chapter, is commended because she puts her hand to the spindle-whorl and the spindle, so the praise of every man is that he applies his hand to the plough, the hoe, and so forth.

*Even to his mouth*, and so forth. It is a most elegant hyperbole by which the utmost sloth of the idle man

[1632 witness | PDF p. 420 | printed columns 814-815]

is depicted. For he affirms that the man shrinks from all labour to such a degree that he is scarcely willing to move his hand to feed himself; like infants wailing in cradles, he would not take it amiss if food were put into his mouth; and if he once attempts to do this, nevertheless afterward he wishes to draw back his hand only with difficulty. By this hyperbole he signifies that the idle man is unwilling gladly to undertake even the smallest and lightest labour. Hence it is burdensome for such people to comb their head, wash their face and hands, and make their bed. And certainly they shrink from labour to such a degree that they are unwilling to gird themselves for work even for the sake of their sustenance and necessary nourishment; although, with nature as guide, *let him who is unwilling to labour not eat*, 2 Thess. 3.10, and it has been established by God’s eternal decree that each person should *eat his bread in the sweat of his face*, Gen. 3. God wills this to be observed with such exactness that he does not grant milk from their mothers’ breasts even to nursing infants unless they draw it out with a great exertion of strength. From this it comes about that infants, wearied by drawing and sucking, pause for breath and, after taking time to refresh themselves, repeatedly seek their mother’s breast and teat.

But this idleness is the more intolerable because there are several causes which impel the man to labour. He ought to care for his own people, whether members of his household or relatives, and for their sake to exercise himself in doing work, since one who does not provide for his own, especially the members of his household, is worse than a heathen or unbeliever, 1 Tim. 5.8. Next, he ought to contribute something to the worship of God, so that God’s honour and glory may be advanced and enlarged, Prov. 3.9. Finally, he will have to labour so that he may distribute something to the needy, Eph. 4.28. For although one who is unwilling to labour ought not to eat, nevertheless there are many who cannot labour because their age is either tender or decrepit, or because of diseases and other infirmities; they must be aided by the industry of the strong and robust.

V. 25. You will strike the scoffer, and the simple man will become shrewd; and by reproving the prudent man he will understand knowledge.

By the word פתי (*fatuus*, simple man) something different is signified from what we often translate as *fool*. For by the latter word we understand a wicked man who opposes a law he acknowledges, but by the present word one who, being imprudent and incautious, easily slips into that which is evil. See 9.4. As for who the scoffer is, see above.

The purpose is to teach the excellent use of punishments and chastisements of every kind when they are employed at the proper time and with discrimination.

There are various ends of chastisements and punishments. One is to remove evil from the midst of the place where evil is committed, Deut. 7.13–14 and 9.19–20, because, in the case of sins openly committed, God will never be reconciled to that family, city, or Church where the crime was perpetrated unless the offender is punished. Another use is that they may strike fear into all to whom the report of the punishment has come, lest they commit anything similar. To these two ends prescribed by Moses, Solomon adds a third in this Proverb, and one far more excellent: namely, that by these chastisements the simple are brought to amendment and repentance. Two causes are assigned for this very excellent effect. The former is when the scoffer is punished more severely with beatings; the latter is when a prudent and understanding man is reproved with words.

And indeed, as concerns the former, Solomon very fitly and suitably counsels that the scoffer is to be restrained and confined by beatings and blows, whether to prison, to the cudgel, or finally to death. For in chapter 9 he had taught that the man was not to be admonished with words, verses 7–8. Therefore the scoffer is not of such lost and hopeless expectation that he cannot by harsher remedies

[1632 witness | PDF p. 421 | printed columns 816-817]

be brought to amendment, although he is not moved by words.

From this we are taught what course we must maintain in inflicting punishments: those who offend more grievously should be the first to pay the just and due penalty. Yet the contrary is almost always done, so that discipline vexes the doves while the ravens are pardoned, especially when the one who commits the crime is powerful or wealthy. There is an old Proverb that the dog should be beaten in the lion’s presence; but we are instead taught here that the lion should be beaten with blows so that the dog may beware for himself. For the better the standing of the one who has sinned, the more the punishment of the crime committed in his person instructs all others toward caution.

An example of this matter is presented in Num. 25.4, where, when the Israelites fornicated with the daughters of the Moabites and were enticed to the worship of Baal, God commands Moses to seize all the leaders and put them on the cross, although it is not altogether established that the leaders were themselves the fornicators. For by the punishment of the abject and poor, nobles and the rich are neither kept within their duty nor deterred from the commission of crimes. By contrast, if punishment is established against the rich and more powerful, people of the lower order remain within their duty, or certainly desist from perpetrating evils.

Then idolaters, and so forth, and adulterers are scarcely summoned, while petty thieves are punished with death, and so forth, although the latter ought to have been taught caution by the severe punishment of those criminals.

The simple man, moreover, is rendered more cautious by the blows inflicted on the scoffer, when he considers the atrocity of the crime from the severity of the punishment and when he is kept from perpetrating it by fear of the punishment. See 1 Sam. 6.6.

Therefore those do not act with sufficient consideration who refrain from punishing wicked scoffers because the scoffers are not amended by it; nevertheless, others are led to amendment.

Another method of forming the simple man and instructing him toward repentance is that you reprove with words a prudent man who has declined a little. For the simple man will then reason with himself in this manner: If one so excellent in virtue is worthy of correction, what will become of me, who must be placed far behind him in integrity of life? If he is reproved for so slight an offence, how much more worthy of censure am I, who commit graver things than these? If he bears it calmly and even loves the person reproving him (as in Prov. 9.8–9), how much more does it fall upon me to do this? Thus a twofold fruit arises from the reproof of the wise man. For both he himself is amended—as one who does not need blows—and the simple and incautious man repents.

From this it is also clear that the more instructed each person is, and the richer in both knowledge of the divine will and its practice and obedience, the more readily he accepts correction. This deservedly convicts those who, when they are reproved, immediately object that, so that it is vain to admonish them, they know well what belongs to their duty; and likewise that they have not departed so far from their duty that they need this correction, especially when it takes place in the presence of others who are not, as they are, so advanced in piety. But let them remember that by this very thing they betray themselves as fools, since the prudent man embraces the one who reproves him, Prov. 9.8. Next, grant that you do not need this correction: nevertheless, it had to be borne patiently and calmly for the sake of others who stand around, especially the simple. And when you suppose yourself to have been treated unjustly, the matter is far otherwise. For the fact that you are blamed while others are passed over is a mark of your piety.

From each part of the Proverb we gather a remarkable doctrine: namely, that with blows and the gravest stripes sinners, every one, ought to be first; but in verbal chastisement, when several sin, the godly who repent at admonition alone, so that you do not strike them, and those

[1632 witness | PDF p. 422 | printed columns 818-819]

who deviate least from duty are to be corrected first, because by their reproof others are convicted. [The construction of the printed Latin is irregular here.]

Next, this also must be observed: people ought to advance toward repentance not only from their own chastisements but also from those of others. This convicts those who are so far from advancing that they hurl the correction of their companions—whether domestic or public, Ecclesiastical or Civil—against them as a reproach to themselves.

Finally, it must be noted that the simple and ignorant man is only rendered more cautious by blows, not truly converted to God; but by the reproof of the wise man he can be brought to amendment. For punishments cannot instruct a person toward salvation, which is effected only by the power of the divine word, as is confirmed in this place.
