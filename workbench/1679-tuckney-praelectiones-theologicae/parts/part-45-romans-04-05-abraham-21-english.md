Let us now finally approach the two other grounds for this necessity which
Bellarmine undertakes to refute, and see how successfully he does so.

1. The first is that after the forgiveness of sin a man is still truly a
sinner, his sin covered but not destroyed. Bellarmine, by contrast, labors
to prove from several scriptural phrases and manners of speech that in true
forgiveness sins are wholly taken away, destroyed, and abolished, so that
imputation is no longer needed. He presents our ground rather spitefully and
maliciously. If we were permitted to express it in our own words, we would
state it thus: the imputation of Christ's righteousness is necessary for our
justification because we are sinners both before and after justification
and forgiveness. Where there is sin there is guilt, where there is guilt
there is unrighteousness, and there can be no true justification unless aid
comes from elsewhere and Christ's righteousness is substituted and credited
in place of ours: before the first forgiveness so that pardon may initially
be granted, and afterward so that it may continue and be renewed when guilt
is renewed. What does Bellarmine say against this? He denies that after
forgiveness we are still sinners and asserts that our sins are not only
covered but wholly abolished.

Several things must be answered.

1. Sin

[1679 printed p. 255 | IA leaf 263]

can be considered in two ways: as to its guilt or as to its existence in
the subject and its inhering stain. As to guilt, after justification and
forgiveness a justified person is not called a sinner in the sense in which
Bathsheba said in 1 Kings 1:21 that she and her son Solomon would become
“sinners,” that is, guilty. Thus the justified are not sinners—namely, not
guilty. This is not because the sins they formerly committed or presently
commit contain no guilt in themselves, or because they were not in
themselves worthy of death, but because they are not guilty with the guilt
overflowing upon their persons. Forgiveness and justification remove it.
Although this occurs through imputation, it is not imaginary but real and
most true; it is not imperfect but simple, absolute, and most perfect.
Sinners become perfectly righteous in the sense in which righteousness is
taken in justification, for the whole guilt is fully and perfectly removed.
Many of the manners of speech Bellarmine adduces to establish his position
apply here. In accordance with Scripture we acknowledge and freely confess
that in justification sins are taken away, destroyed, abolished, purged,
and washed away as to guilt. The washing away of sins concerns removal of
both guilt and the stain and filth of sin, as is evident from Psalm 51:9–12
and 1 Corinthians 6:11, “But you were washed, sanctified, justified,” and
also from baptismal washing, by which the guilt of original sin is removed
from believers although its existence is not immediately uprooted
altogether. In justification, I say, sins are fully washed away as to guilt,
removed from us as far as east is from west, cast behind God's back and even
into the depths of the sea, so that in this respect they cannot be found
when sought. Justified men are cleansed and made whiter than snow. Similar
phrases display the complete destruction and forgiveness of sins as to
guilt. Such washing away, removal, and abolition of sins is so far from
destroying the imputation of Christ's righteousness that it necessarily
presupposes it, for it depends entirely upon it. Through the logically,
though not temporally, prior imputation of Christ's righteousness and
satisfaction to us, we are absolved and justified from sin's guilt and curse,
as a debtor is freed from his obligation by the price his surety pays. Thus,
once truly forgiven and justified, we are no longer sinners in this respect,
namely as to guilt overflowing upon our persons. But what must be said about
the continuing existence of sin?

2. We answer, second, that a man can also be and be called a sinner in two
ways: insofar as sin reigns in him or merely dwells in him. The former sense
occurs when “the Son of Man is said to be delivered into the hands of
sinners,” Matthew 26:45, and when it is affirmed that “God does not hear
sinners,” John 9:31. The latter occurs in Luke 15:7, “I tell you there will
be joy in heaven over one sinner who repents,” and 18:13, “God, be merciful
to me, a sinner.”

A truly justified man is not a sinner in the former sense. Sin does not
master him because he is not under law but under grace, Romans 6:14.

Thus, according to Augustine, “the one in whom sin does not reign does not
sin,” and in this respect is not a sinner. Yet this does not remove the
imputation of Christ's righteousness but presupposes it. For through this
imputation of Christ's righteousness

[1679 printed p. 256 | IA leaf 264]

sins are not credited but forgiven. Once they are forgiven, we are
reconciled to God, 2 Corinthians 5:19, become God's children, and God's
Spirit is sent into our hearts, Galatians 4:6. This Spirit removes sin's
dominion, Romans 8:13 and 1 Corinthians 6:11. We therefore owe it to this
imputation of Christ's righteousness that we are not sinners in this sense.
When Bellarmine spitefully presents our position as though we held that our
sins are merely covered and by no means destroyed, he plays the slanderer
in his customary manner. For we:

1. Do say they are covered, with Scripture asserting it in Psalm 32:1–2:
“Blessed is he whose sin is covered; blessed is the man to whom Jehovah does
not credit iniquity.” By not crediting sins, God covers them and wholly
removes them as to guilt, which alone is considered in justification. But
sanctification is always joined to justification, and the same faith which
justifies the sinner also purifies the heart, Acts 15:9. Therefore:

2. We say that dominion is removed together with guilt, so that sins do not
reign, and to this extent are destroyed even here.

3. Their power is gradually diminished each day until:

4. Finally, their very inherence and indwelling is utterly expelled and
abolished. On the last day Christ will “present all his saints blameless
before the presence of his glory,” Jude 24, and “will then finally present
the church to himself glorious, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing,”
Ephesians 5:27. Indeed, before then, as soon as the spirits of the righteous
ascend to heaven after death—where nothing defiling enters, Revelation
21:27—they are made perfect, Hebrews 12:23.

Indeed, at the very moment of death they are freed with this mortal body
from this body of sin. Thus one calls death the culmination of sin and
Ambrose calls it the burial of sins. We openly confess and freely profess
all this. Yet Bellarmine will accuse us as though we held that in the
justified sins are only covered and by no means destroyed.

Finally, as to sin's actual inherence after justification—its indwelling,
as the Apostle calls it in Romans 7:17—if Bellarmine denies that any
justified person is a sinner while living here, he thereby makes God a liar
while lying most shamelessly himself, contrary to the plainest testimonies
of Scripture, the opinion of the church and all the fathers, the sense and
experience of every godly person, and even, I venture to say, his own
conscience, if he would attend to it.

As to actual sins, we all sin daily. Although through renewed faith and
repentance the sinful act passes away together with its guilt, sometimes,
if not a habit, at least some stain or filth remains which pollutes the soul
and is not entirely washed away without great labor and a long interval.

As to original sin, although its guilt is wholly removed in justification,
as I showed above, some remnants of the indwelling of sin remain. They often
assert themselves far too forcefully and provoke actual sins; indeed, as
the Apostle complains about himself, they make even the godly captives to
the law of sin, Romans 7:23.

Is there therefore no necessity at all for the imputation of Christ's
righteousness to remove these sins, which reigned before justification but
afterward still remain in the justified in the manner and sense just
described?

[1679 printed p. 257 | IA leaf 265]

There is indeed the greatest necessity, for three reasons.

1. For the first justification itself. Even if it were conceded that in
justification both original and actual sin are thoroughly and radically
removed as to indwelling, justification is so far from declaring that the
person to be justified is not a sinner that it necessarily presupposes he
is one. Legal justification, which could have occurred according to the
covenant of works, does not admit this, since it pronounces righteous only
the one who is absolutely righteous in himself and free from every sin. But
the evangelical justification of faith is different. Since it justifies
the ungodly, Romans 4:5, and consists in the forgiveness of guilt and
punishment, verses 7–8, it presupposes that the person to be justified is a
sinner in himself. In this respect he could no more be accounted righteous
in himself than a sick man healthy or a dead man alive. The one who needed
forgiveness was therefore guilty and a sinner. Sins once done cannot be
undone and can consequently be destroyed only through forgiveness. By God's
nature and decree this forgiveness could be obtained only through Christ's
righteousness and satisfaction, which would profit us nothing unless
credited to us and applied to our case. This imputation was therefore
absolutely necessary for it.

2. For the continuation of this justification. Since justification does not
immediately uproot original sin as to indwelling or every actual sin as to
its operations, these new sinful operations would altogether extinguish the
former justification by their new guilt unless the one newly wounded by the
fiery serpent looked again to the same serpent which still and always
remains lifted up, and unless the same plaster were applied to heal the new
wound. That is, this requires the continuing imputation of Christ's
righteousness, since he “is able to save completely and perpetually those
who approach God through him, because he always lives to intercede for them,”
Hebrews 7:25.

3. Finally, this imputation is necessary, as we showed above, so that the
Holy Spirit may be obtained and sent. He forestalls actual sins after
justification and daily diminishes the poison of original sin hidden within
until at last he purges it altogether.

The necessity of this imputation therefore still stands intact against
Bellarmine's attempt to undermine it with his second argument. Both before
and after justification we are sinners properly so called who need
continuing or renewed forgiveness; therefore we need a renewed or continuing
imputation of Christ's righteousness.
