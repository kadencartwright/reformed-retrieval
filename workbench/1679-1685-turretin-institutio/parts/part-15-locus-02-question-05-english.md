QUESTION V

Do True Contradictions Occur in Scripture, or Certain “Insoluble” Passages
(ἄλυτα), Which Can Be Solved and Reconciled in No Way?—Negative.

I. Although, once the divinity of Scripture has been asserted—which was
treated in the preceding question—its infallibility necessarily follows,
nevertheless in every age the enemies of true religion and Scripture have
thought that they found in Scripture not a few “seeming contradictions”
(ἐναντιοφανῆ), and they have boasted of those they diligently gathered in
order to overthrow its “authenticity” (αὐθεντίαν). Such were Porphyry,
Lucian, Julian the Apostate, and others among the pagans in the past, and
today various atheists, who loudly proclaim that there occur in it not a few
contradictions and “perplexities” (ἄπορα) which can be reconciled in no way.
Therefore we must deal with them in a particular question, so that the
integrity of Scripture may be preserved safe and sound against their impious
weapons.

II. Here our dispute is not only with open atheists and pagans, who do not
admit Sacred Scripture, but also with others who, although they seem to
receive it, nevertheless indirectly overturn it in this way. Such are the
enthusiasts, who allege the imperfection of the written Word in order to lead
people away to their hidden word, or private revelations; the papists, who,
although they otherwise defend the divinity of Scripture against the
atheists, nevertheless do not fear to fit the atheists’ weapons to themselves
and, so far as in them lies, to assail their own cause and indeed that of all
Christianity and betray it to their enemies, by pressing the corruption of
the original texts so that they may secure authority for their Vulgate
version; and finally various libertines, who, while dwelling in the bosom of
the church, do not cease to object to those various “perplexities” (ἄπορα)
and “seeming contradictions” (ἐναντιοφανῆ) in order to undermine the
authority of Scripture.

III. To meet them, the theologians follow different ways. Some think that the
matter can easily be resolved if it is said that the sacred writers could
have suffered a lapse of memory or erred in lesser matters. Thus Socinus in
*On the Authority of Scripture*, Castellio in the *Dialogues*, and others.
But this is not to defend the cause against the atheists, but shamefully to
betray it. Others admit that the Hebrew and Greek original texts have been
corrupted in some passages by the malice of Jews or heretics, but that a
ready remedy can be sought from the Vulgate version and the infallible
authority of the church. This is the opinion of very many papists, against
whom we will dispute later when we treat the purity of the original texts.
Others judge that a few errors slipped into Scripture and still remain which
cannot be remedied by the evidence and comparison of any manuscript codices,
but which are not to be ascribed to the sacred writers themselves, but partly
to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 67 | IA leaf 121 | PDF p. 121]

the injury of time and partly to the fault of copyists and scribes. Yet they
hold that the “authenticity” (αὐθεντία) of Scripture cannot thereby be
undermined, because the errors occur only in less necessary and lesser
matters. So Scaliger, Cappellus, Amama, Vossius, and others maintain. Finally,
others defend the integrity of Scripture and do not deny that various
apparent contradictions occur, but deny that they are true and real; they
hold that certain passages are “difficult to understand” (δυσνόητα), but not
altogether “insoluble” (ἄλυτα) and hopeless. This is the more common opinion
of the orthodox, which we follow as safer and truer.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

IV. The question is not about irregular spelling or punctuation of words, or
about various readings, which everyone admits occur not infrequently. Nor is
it whether the apographs which we possess agree with the first autographs in
such a way that they do not depart from them in any point or little letter at
all. Rather, it is whether they differ from them in such a way that the
genuine sense is corrupted and they can no longer be regarded as the original
texts and the rule of faith and morals.

V. The question is not about the particular corruption of certain codices,
or about errors which slipped into the codices of this or that edition
through the negligence of copyists or typesetters, for no one denies that
there are various corruptions of this kind. Rather, it is whether there are
universal corruptions and errors so diffused throughout all the codices, both
manuscript and printed, that they cannot be remedied and corrected by any
comparison either of various codices or of Scripture itself and parallel
passages; and whether there are certain true and real contradictions, and not
only apparent ones. We deny this.

[Marginal heading: Scripture is proved not to be corrupt.]

VI. The reasons are: 1. Because Scripture is “God-breathed” (θεόπνευστος), 2
Timothy 3:16. The Word of God does not know how to lie, Psalm 19:8–9; Hebrews
6:18; it cannot fail and be broken, Matthew 5:18; it abides forever, 1 Peter
1:25; and it is truth itself, John 17:17. For how could such things be
predicated of it if it were liable to contradictions, and if God had allowed
either the sacred writers to err and suffer a lapse of memory or incurable
errors to slip into it?

VII. 2. Because unless unimpaired integrity is attributed to Scripture, it
cannot be regarded as the one rule of faith and morals, and a wide door will
be opened to atheists, libertines, enthusiasts, and other profane people of
that kind to overthrow its “authenticity” (αὐθεντίαν) and overturn the
foundation of salvation. For since what is false cannot underlie faith, how
could a Scripture liable to contradictions and corruptions be held authentic
and regarded as divine? Nor should it be said here that the corruptions occur
only in lesser matters which do not touch the foundation of faith. For if the
“authenticity” (αὐθεντία) of Scripture is once overthrown, which happens even
through the incurable corruption of a single passage, how will it be able to
ground our faith any further? And if corruption is admitted in lesser
matters, why not in other, weightier ones? Who will be able to assure me that
no stain or error has slipped into fundamental passages? Or what could be
answered to an atheist or subtle heretic who stubbornly asserts that this or
that passage less favorable to him lies under an error? Nor should it be
replied here that divine providence willed to render Scripture immune from
weightier corruptions but not from lesser ones. For besides the fact that
this is said without proof, it is asserted with some injury to providence, as
though it failed in those necessary things which were required for the full
“self-authenticating character” (αὐτοπιστίαν) of Scripture. Nor can it easily
be believed that God, who dictated and inspired each and every word to
“God-breathed” men (θεοπνεύστοις), took no care that they all also be
preserved. If people take care that their own words be carefully preserved so
far as they can, especially if they are of some importance—for example, if it
is a testament or contract—lest anything be changed or corrupted, how much
more should God be thought to have taken care that nothing be corrupted in
his Word, which he willed to stand in place of a testament and the public
tablets of his covenant entered into with us,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 68 | IA leaf 122 | PDF p. 122]

especially since he could easily foresee and prevent such corruptions in
order to confirm the faith of his church?

VIII. There are four principal arguments for the integrity of Scripture and
the purity of the original texts. 1. The primary one is the providence of
God. Just as he willed to provide for our faith by inspiring the sacred
writers with what they should write and by preserving Scripture against the
attempts of enemies, who left nothing untried to destroy it, so he was bound
to guard it pure and untainted against the corruptions of falsifiers, so that
our faith might always have a firm support on which to rest. 2. The religious
scrupulousness of the Jews, who were always anxious, even to the point of
superstition, about the faithful custody of the sacred codices. 3. The
diligence of the Masoretes, who by their notes placed, as it were, a fence
around the Law so that it could in no way be changed or corrupted. 4. The
number and multitude of copies, through which it came about that, although
some codices could be corrupted, not all could be.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

IX. Whatever “contradiction” (ἀντιλογία) is found in the Scriptures is
apparent, not true. It is to be understood only with respect to us, who cannot
see and grasp Scripture’s agreement everywhere, but not as a contradiction
in the thing itself. And if the laws of a legitimate contradiction are
observed—namely, that the opposites apply “to the same thing” (τῷ αὐτῷ),
*eidem*; “in the same respect” (κατὰ τὸ αὐτό), *secundum idem*; “in relation
to the same thing” (πρὸς τὸ αὐτό), *ad idem*; and “at the same time” (τῷ αὐτῷ
χρόνῳ), *eodem tempore*—the various “seeming contradictions” (ἐναντιοφανῆ) of
Scripture will easily be reconciled. For either the subject is not the same,
as when James ascribes justification to works while Paul takes it away from
them: the former speaks of the declarative justification of the effect *a
posteriori*, but the latter of the justification of the cause *a priori*. So
in Luke 6:36 mercy is commanded, “Be merciful,” while it is forbidden in
Deuteronomy 19:13, “You shall not pity.” The former concerns private persons,
but the latter magistrates. Or the things are not said in the same respect,
as in Matthew 26:15 the presence of Christ in the world is denied, “You will
not always have me,” while in chapter 28:20 the same is promised, “I will be
with you until the consummation of the age.” The former is with respect to
his human nature and bodily presence, but the latter with respect to his
divine nature and spiritual presence. Or they do not relate to the same
thing, as when one thing is said absolutely but another comparatively: “Honor
your father,” and Luke 14:26, “If anyone does not hate his father.” The former
is to be understood absolutely, but the latter comparatively, as meaning to
love less and place after Christ. Or they are not said at the same time;
hence the saying, “Distinguish the times and you will reconcile the
Scriptures.” Thus circumcision is sometimes extolled as a great privilege of
the Jews, Romans 3:2, and sometimes depreciated as a thing of no value,
Galatians 5:3. But the former concerns the time of the Old Testament, when it
was an ordinary sacrament and seal of the righteousness of faith, whereas the
latter concerns the time of the gospel after the ceremonial law had been
abrogated. At one time the apostles are sent by a special mission to the Jews
alone before Christ’s passion and are forbidden to go to the nations, Matthew
10:5, “Do not go into the way of the nations”; at another they are sent by a
general mission to all nations after the resurrection, Mark 16:15.

X. Although we attribute absolute integrity to Scripture, we do not therefore
judge that copyists or typesetters were “God-breathed” (θεοπνεύστους), but
only that God’s providence watched over the copying of the sacred books so
that, although various errors could slip in, they did not slip in, or did not
so enter the codices that they cannot easily be remedied from a comparison of
other codices or of Scripture itself with itself. Thus the foundation of the
purity and integrity of the original texts is not to be placed in human
“sinlessness” (ἀναμαρτησίᾳ), but in the providence of God, which, however much
people occupied in copying the Sacred Letters could mingle in various errors,
nevertheless always diligently took care that they should be corrected or
could easily be corrected either from a comparison of Scripture itself or
from the better-attested codices.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 69 | IA leaf 123 | PDF p. 123]

It was therefore not necessary that all scribes be rendered infallible, but
only that they be so directed that the true reading could always be found and
this book might far excel all others in purity.

XI. Although we may not be able immediately to find a clear reconciliation
free of every difficulty between passages of Scripture which treat names or
numerical and chronological indications, they are not immediately to be
placed among the “insoluble things” (ἄλυτα). Or if they are called
“insoluble” (ἄλυτα), they will be so only “because of the inability of the one
solving” (τῇ ἀδυναμίᾳ τοῦ λύοντος), not in themselves, so that here it is
better to acknowledge our ignorance than to admit any contradiction. For
those histories were not written in such detail that every circumstance was
noted. Without doubt many things were reduced into a summary, while others
which did not seem so necessary were omitted. It is also possible that those
passages have various “relations” (σχέσεις) which were very well known to the
writers, although now hidden from us. Hence Peter Martyr, on 2 Kings 8:17,
says very well: “Although obscure passages occur in chronological matters, in
reconciling them one must not concede so much that we say the sacred codex is
erroneous. For God, who in his mercy willed the Sacred Letters to be preserved
for us, also granted them to be integral and uncorrupted. Therefore, if
sometimes we cannot give an account of the number of years, the ignorance
under which we labor must be confessed, and attention must be paid to the
fact that the Sacred Letters speak so concisely that it is not readily
apparent where the calculation should begin. Therefore let Scripture remain
uncorrupted; for if it is undermined in one or another passage, it will also
be suspect in the others.” And on 1 Kings 15:1: “It happens not infrequently
that in this history the numbers of years attributed to the kings seem to
agree only slightly with one another. Yet doubts of this kind can be resolved
in many ways. For it sometimes happens that one and the same year is assigned
to two kings because it was not completed and finished by either. Sometimes
sons reigned with their parents for several years, and those years are now
ascribed to the reign of the parents and now to that of the children.
Interregna also sometimes occurred, and a vacant period is attributed now to
the earlier and now to the later king. There are also at times some years in
which princes reigned tyrannically and impiously, and therefore they are
passed over and are not reckoned with the other years of their rule.”

[Marginal heading: On Cainan.]

XII. The passage in Luke 3:36 about the younger Cainan, who is inserted
between Arphaxad and Sala contrary to the truth of the Mosaic history in
Genesis 11:13, is indeed “difficult to understand” (δυσνόητος), and it has
exercised the minds of the learned in different ways. Nevertheless, it should
not be regarded as wholly “insoluble” (ἄλυτος), since various modes of
solution can be given. Passing over other opinions, we judge the most suitable
to be the one which maintains that Cainan is an “interpolation”
(ὑποβολιμαῖον) and spurious, having slipped in from the Septuagint through
the carelessness of copyists, or through a certain pious zeal lest Luke be
made to conflict with the Septuagint version, which was then of great
authority. Demetrius’s chronology in Eusebius, *Preparation of the Gospel*,
book 9, testifies that it existed in the Septuagint even before the time of
Christ. This, however, is plainly proved by: 1. The authority of Moses and the
book of Chronicles, which make no mention of him in the genealogical accounts
they set forth in three passages—Genesis 10:24 and 11:13, and 1 Chronicles
1:18—where he surely ought to have been mentioned. 2. The Chaldean
paraphrases, which consistently omit Cainan both in Genesis and in the book
of Chronicles. 3. Josephus does not acknowledge him, nor does Berosus as
produced by him, nor Africanus, whose words Eusebius cites in the *Chronicle*.
4. In this way sacred chronology is thrown into confusion and called into
doubt in the history of Moses if Cainan’s years are inserted between Arphaxad
and Sala; and Abraham will not be the tenth from Noah, as Moses asserts, but
the eleventh. 5. He does not occur in all the codices. Our Beza testifies that
he is absent from his most ancient manuscript, and Ussher, *Dissertation on
Cainan*, page 196, asserts that he saw a Greek-Latin book of Luke written on
very ancient parchment in capital letters without breathings and

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 70 | IA leaf 124 | PDF p. 124]

accents. It had been brought from Greece into Gaul and formerly deposited in
the monastery of St. Irenaeus in the suburb of Lyons; found in 1562, it was
afterward brought to England and given to Cambridge University, where Cainan
does not appear. Scaliger, in the prologue to Eusebius’s *Chronicle*, affirms
that Cainan is lacking in the most ancient copies of Luke. Whatever the case,
although the passage in Luke is said to lie under an error, his
“authenticity” (αὐθεντία) cannot therefore be called into doubt: 1. because
the corruption is not universal; and 2. because little rests upon this error,
and a ready remedy for correcting it is supplied from Moses. Thus there was
no need for the most learned Isaac Vossius to disturb the purity of the
Hebrew codex in order to secure “authenticity” (αὐθεντίαν) for the Septuagint
version.

[Marginal heading: Matthew 1; Luke 3.]

XIII. If a great variation occurs in Christ’s genealogy as woven by Matthew
and Luke, both with respect to the persons and their number, it ought not to
seem surprising, because it is not the same genealogy but a different one.
For Matthew sets forth the genealogy of Joseph, whose lineage he derives from
David through Solomon, while Luke traces Mary’s lineage through Nathan,
David’s other son, back to the same David. In accordance with Hebrew custom,
Matthew leaves the lineage of the bride to be sought in that of the
bridegroom. But Luke wished to supply what had been omitted by describing
Mary’s family tree, so that a full and perfect genealogy of Christ from both
parents might stand forth; so that there might be no place either for the
scruples of the weak or the cavils of the enemies of the gospel; and so that
the former might be confirmed and the latter convinced that, according to
the prophetic predictions, Christ was the true and natural son of David,
whether account was taken of Joseph the bridegroom, into whose family he had
passed through marriage to Mary, or of Mary herself. This is especially so
since it is certain that heiress virgins (ἐπικλήρους), who possessed a dowry
in family inheritances, such as the Blessed Virgin, were not permitted to
marry outside their tribe and family. The genealogy in Luke is nevertheless
referred to Joseph, not to Mary, because it was not the custom to weave a
genealogy through women, for they were reckoned either in their parents or
brothers if unmarried, or in their husbands if betrothed. Hence the Jewish
axiom, משפחת אם לא משפחה, “The mother’s family is not a family.”

XIV. Although Jacob is called the father of Joseph in Matthew, but Heli in
Luke, there is nevertheless no contradiction, because this must be understood
“in one respect and another” (κατ᾽ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο). First, there is no
absurdity in one son having two fathers of a different kind, so that one is
the natural father, who begot the son from himself, and the other the legal
father, who adopts into all his rights one begotten by another according to
law. Thus Manasseh and Ephraim were the natural sons of Joseph alone, but the
legal sons of Jacob through adoption. David’s grandfather Obed had one
natural father, Boaz, but also a legal father, Mahlon, the first husband of
his mother Ruth, for whom Boaz, the later husband, raised up seed according
to the law. Thus Jacob was Joseph’s natural father, while Heli, who was Mary’s
natural father, can be called Joseph’s father either legally, as Africanus
held, because, when Heli died without children, Jacob took his wife according
to the law, Deuteronomy 25:5, and by her fathered Joseph, Mary’s husband; or
civilly, by reason of the marriage contracted with his daughter Mary, through
which he became his son-in-law, in the way that Naomi calls her daughters-in-
law her own daughters, Ruth 1:11–12, a usage received among all peoples. Or
it can be said that Joseph is not called Heli’s son, but Christ is, so that
the words “as was supposed, the son of Joseph” (ὡς ἐνομίζετο υἱὸς Ἰωσήφ) are
enclosed in parentheses, rather than the parenthesis ending after the verb
“was supposed” (ἐνομίζετο), as is commonly done. The sense would be: “being
(as was supposed, the son of Joseph) of Heli” (ὢν [ὡς ἐνομίζετο υἱὸς Ἰωσήφ]
τοῦ Ἠλὶ), that is, Jesus existing as the son of Heli—namely, the grandson
through the Virgin Mary, Heli’s daughter—although he was thought to be the son
of Joseph. Nor is this transition from grandfathers to grandsons unusual,
especially where the fathers are dead, and all the more in this example,
because Christ was “without a father” (ἀπάτωρ) according to his human nature.

XV. If it is asked why, in Matthew 1:8, Joram is said to have begotten Uzziah,
although

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 71 | IA leaf 125 | PDF p. 125]

it is evident from 2 Kings 8:24 and 1 Chronicles 3:11–12 that Joram begot
Ahaziah, Ahaziah Joash, and Joash Amaziah, so that three kings are omitted and
a leap is made from Joram the great-grandfather to Uzziah the great-great-
grandson, it can be answered that it is not unusual in Scripture for distant
grandsons to be called the sons and daughters of remote ancestors and
forebears from the farthest antiquity. Thus the Jews call themselves sons of
Abraham in John 8:39; Elizabeth is said to be of the daughters of Aaron in
Luke 1:5; and Christ is called the son of David in Matthew 22. Thus Joram is
said to have begotten Uzziah through mediate generation, on account of which
grandsons and great-grandsons descended from ancestors are called sons. But
why these three kings rather than the others were omitted is not so readily
apparent, nor can the reasons ordinarily offered by various people obtain
assent. It is more suitable to say that this was done in order to round the
number, because Matthew wished to arrange all those generations into groups
of fourteen, a convenient abridgment for assisting memory. To accomplish
this he thought little of passing over certain less notable persons, as other
historians also do not infrequently when they reduce a matter to a summary.
These three in particular can be said to have been omitted so that Ahab’s
posterity down to the third or fourth generation might also be marked with
this sign of the curse, 1 Kings 21:22, by completely erasing these three
kings, who were of Ahab’s posterity, and, as it were, diminishing them by the
head, so that they would be regarded as though they had never lived or
reigned. Joram is not excluded because he did not spring from Ahab’s accursed
blood, nor is the great-great-grandson Uzziah, although he descended from
Ahab, because the divine curse does not ordinarily proceed beyond the third
or fourth generation.

XVI. If it is asked how Josiah is said in Matthew 1:11 to have begotten
Jechoniah, when it is evident from the books of Kings, 2 Kings 24:6, and 1
Chronicles 3:15–16, that Josiah had four sons “of different names”
(ἑτερωνύμους)—Johanan, who was also Jehoahaz; Jehoiakim, who was also Eliakim;
Zedekiah, who was also Mattaniah; and finally Shallum—whereas Jechoniah was the
son of Jehoiakim, various answers can be given. Either the text was corrupted
by the scribes and “Jehoiakim” (Ἰαχείμ), the son of Josiah and father of
Jechoniah, must be supplied between Josiah and Jechoniah. This is confirmed
by the authority of an ancient codex used by Robert Stephanus and his son
Henry, which Stapulensis and Bucer commend; and thus the text should more
truly be restored: “Josiah begot Jehoiakim; Jehoiakim begot Jechoniah”
(Ἰωσίας δὲ ἐγέννησε τὸν Ἰαχείμ, Ἰαχείμ ἐγέννησε τὸν Ἰεχονίαν). Or Josiah is
called the father of Jechoniah mediately because he was his grandfather,
while Jehoiakim was omitted because, just as he was not worthy of mourning
and the burial of the kings of Judah, Jeremiah 22:18, so neither was he worthy
of being inserted into Christ’s genealogy. Or there were two Jechoniahs who
are mentioned in verses 11 and 12: the first was Jehoiakim, Josiah’s son, who
is also called Eliakim and Jechoniah. Nor ought this to seem strange, since
not a few of the kings of Judah bore two or even three names, as Zedekiah was
also called Mattaniah and Uzziah was called Azariah. The second, properly
Jechoniah in 1 Chronicles 3:16, who was also Jehoiachin, was Josiah’s grandson
and is treated in verse 12. He is rightly distinguished from the former both
by the mention of brothers, whom the latter lacked, Jeremiah 22:18–19, and by
the mention that Shealtiel was begotten by Jechoniah “after the deportation”
(μετὰ μετοικεσίαν), verse 12.

XVII. Although Shealtiel is said to have begotten Zerubbabel in Matthew 1:12,
while in 1 Chronicles 3:17–18 Zerubbabel is called the son of Pedaiah, there
is nevertheless no contradiction. For when Shealtiel died without children,
his brother Pedaiah could raise up seed for him and have Zerubbabel by his
wife according to the law of levirate marriage. Thus Zerubbabel is called the
son of Pedaiah in 1 Chronicles 3 because he was truly begotten by him, and the
son of Shealtiel in Matthew 1:12 and Haggai 1:1 because he had been raised up
for him by his brother. Or Zerubbabel was Shealtiel’s successor because
Pedaiah died before his father Shealtiel, so that Zerubbabel was the immediate
son of Pedaiah, but only the mediate son of his grandfather Shealtiel.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 72 | IA leaf 126 | PDF p. 126]

XVIII. There is no contradiction among 1 Chronicles 3:19, Matthew 1:13, and
Luke 3:27, when Zerubbabel’s sons are said in the book of Chronicles to be
Meshullam and Hananiah, but Abiud in Matthew and Rhesa in Luke. This is easily
reconciled if we say that three different lines of Zerubbabel are described:
one in the book of Chronicles, that of his eldest son Meshullam and the
others, whose offspring was distinguished and from whom governors and leaders
were appointed over the Jewish nation brought back from Babylon for several
centuries; a second in Matthew, that of Abiud, from whom came Joseph; and a
third in Luke, that of Rhesa, from whom came Mary. The latter were private
persons whose lives were obscure among the people and therefore seem to have
been omitted in Chronicles. Or one must say with others that there were
different Zerubbabels and Shealtiels; consequently, it ought not to seem
strange if their descendants are different.

XIX. When Josiah is said to have begotten Jechoniah “at the time of the
deportation” (ἐπὶ τῆς μετοικεσίας), these words should not be translated
strictly “in the deportation,” since it is evident from the particular divine
promise that Josiah did not see that evil and had died some years before the
captivity, 2 Chronicles 34:28 and 35:24. Rather, they mean “at” or “around”
the time of it, when that deportation was imminent, so that ἐπὶ coincides
with ἕως, “until the deportation,” just as μετὰ does with ἀπὸ in verse 17.
Thus ἐπὶ will denote the time in which the event occurred, but taken not
strictly, rather broadly, as in Mark 2:26 and Luke 3:2, so that it is the same
as the Hebrew ב, which often means “around” or “near,” Genesis 2:2; Exodus
12:15. It must be translated “under” or “around,” that is, “when the
deportation was at hand.” Or the word ἐπὶ should not be referred to the more
remote verb “begot,” but to the nearer “his brothers”: ἐπὶ μετοικεσίας for ἐκ
μετοικεσίας through a very frequent interchange of prepositions, standing
for τοὺς ἐκ μετοικεσίας, those who were of the deportation, that is, among
those who were carried away to Babylon.

XX. Although Jechoniah is said to have begotten Shealtiel in Matthew 1:12,
while Shealtiel is called the son of Neri in Luke 3:37, there is no
contradiction, because there were different Shealtiels: one the son of
Jechoniah, of whom Matthew speaks, and the other the son of Neri, of whom Luke
speaks. The former was from the line of Solomon, the latter from the line of
Nathan. And since Shealtiel’s ancestors back to David in Luke were far
different from Shealtiel’s in Matthew, it is probable that the passages speak
of different persons. Nor is it an obstacle that Shealtiel is called the
father of Zerubbabel both in Matthew and in Luke, because there could likewise
be two Zerubbabels, just as there is a similar concurrence of two or more
people succeeding in the same order in Luke 3:34, Mattath and Levi; in verse
29; and in 1 Chronicles 6:7–8, and the same thing is repeated concerning four
of their descendants in verse 12.

XXI. The passage in Psalm 22:17 concerning כארי is not “insoluble” (ἄλυτος).
For although the common reading is retained, corruption would not therefore
have to be posited there, but only an anomaly of writing, in which א was added
by epenthesis, as not infrequently elsewhere—ראמה for רמה, “it will be
exalted,” Zechariah 14:10—and ו was changed into י, since these letters are
easily interchanged with each other—נשאי for נשאו, “they took,” Ezra 10, last
verse. And Marinus, in *Noah’s Ark*, calls that letter not a *yod* but a
diminished *vav*, and thinks it was placed there to indicate clearly the
marks of Christ’s nails. Hence in the royal codices it is written with a
pointed *yod*, as though it were the shureq *yod* כארי, in place of a shureq
*vav*, as in Jeremiah 50:11. 2. This is confirmed by the fact that the Small
Masorah notes at this passage that כארי with a *qamets* under כ occurs only
twice in Scripture, and indeed with a different

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 73 | IA leaf 127 | PDF p. 127]

meaning. But in Isaiah 38:13, where it occurs once, it can only be expounded
“like a lion.” Therefore here this word must be expounded in another sense,
which, from the “sequence” (ἀλληλουχίᾳ) of the words, can be no other than the
one commonly given, namely, “they dug.” 3. In the final Great Masorah an
alphabet is set forth consisting of pairs of words which agree in spelling
but differ in meaning, among which this word occurring in Isaiah 38 and here
is listed; hence it must have a different meaning in the psalmist than in
Isaiah. 4. The most ancient versions expound it thus: the Septuagint has “they
dug” (ὤρυξαν), as do the Arabic, Syriac, and Ethiopic. 5. The aim of the psalm
is to treat the design of Christ’s enemies concerning his crucifixion, as is
evident from the division of the garments, the casting of lots for the tunic,
and the like. Who, indeed, would believe that what was chief and the head of
the other things was passed over in silence? 6. The sequence of the words
requires this. For if we read “like a lion,” the sentence will gape and have
no meaning: “my hands and feet like a lion.” Therefore there must be some verb
to close the period, which can be no other than כרו, “they dug,” to which
another oracle, Zechariah 12:10, looks: “They will see whom they pierced.” 7.
Although we granted that the passage had been corrupted by the Jews, it would
not follow that the corruption was universal, because the Masoretes testify
that they found כארו written in the better-attested codices, the marginal
Masorah on Numbers 24:9. And Jacob ben Hayyim, the compiler of the Venetian
Masoretic work, found in certain corrected copies כארי written in the text,
but כארו read—that is, noted in the margin. Johannes Isaac, a Jew who became
a Christian, testifies against Lindanus that he found the same in his
grandfather’s psalter, which Capito confirms in *Institutes of Hebrew*, book
1, chapter 10. Nor can the papists deny it, since the Complutensian Bible
reads thus, and Genebrardus proves from various testimonies of the Jews that
it was so read.

XXII. In 2 Samuel 24:24, David is said to have bought from Araunah the
threshing floor and oxen for fifty shekels of silver. But in 1 Chronicles
21:25, six hundred shekels of gold are mentioned. The reconciliation is easy
from the subject of the purchase: he gave fifty shekels for that part of the
threshing floor on which he first built the altar; but after he learned from
the fire fallen from heaven that this was the place God had chosen for
building the house, no longer content with that small threshing floor, he
bought the entire field and mountain for six hundred shekels.

XXIII. In the same place, verse 13, seven years of famine are proposed by Gad
to David as a punishment to be inflicted by God. But in 1 Chronicles 21:12,
only three are mentioned. These are easily reconciled if we say either that
Gad first proposed a seven-year period, which afterward, when David recoiled,
was reduced to three years at the prayers of Gad or David, just as in Genesis
18 the number of fifty righteous people is reduced to ten; or that the seven
years treated by the book of Samuel include the three years of famine which
had already passed, imposed because of the Gibeonites, as treated in 2 Samuel
21:1, together with the current year, while in the book of Chronicles only
the three years which were to come are mentioned. So Kimchi, Martyr, and
Junius hold.

XXIV. In 2 Kings 24:8, Jehoiachin is said to have come to the kingdom when he
was eighteen years old. But in 2 Chronicles 36:9, he is said to have been only
eight. That is, in his eighth year he began to reign with his father, in
accordance with the frequent custom of kings at that time by which fathers
assumed their sons into partnership in the kingdom, so that the legitimate
heir to the kingdom might then be designated and acknowledged. But only in
his eighteenth year, when his parent died, did he begin to reign. Thus
Wolfius, Sanctius, and others after Kimchi reconcile the passages. Abarbanel
wishes the ten days which are added in the book of Chronicles to be taken for
ten years, as not infrequently elsewhere. He thinks this all the more
probable because otherwise the days in which kings reign are not ordinarily
recorded, but only the years or months.

XXV. Although various people think there is an insoluble knot between 2
Chronicles 22:2 and 2 Kings 8:26—where Joram, Ahaziah’s father, is said to have
begun to reign in the thirty-second year of his age, 2 Chronicles 21:5, and
to have reigned eight years, so that he died in the fortieth year of his age,
verse 20; but Ahaziah is said in chapter 22 to have begun to reign at age
forty-two, and thus the son would seem to be two years older than his father;
whereas in the book of Kings he is said to have begun to reign at age twenty-
two, not forty-two—nevertheless, this is not

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 74 | IA leaf 128 | PDF p. 128]

everyone’s opinion, nor are there lacking various modes of reconciliation
which suitably untie the knot. Some, such as Junius and Tremellius following
Rabbi Levi ben Gershon, hold that the book of Kings relates the true year of
Ahaziah’s age in which he began to reign and the duration of his life, while
the book of Chronicles relates not the duration of his life but that of the
maternal house of Omri, from the time Omri began to reign to its end, which
occurred in this very year by the most illustrious judgment of God. In memory
of that remarkable wonder, this calculation is attributed to Ahaziah, who was
removed in the same year by Jehu’s sword along with the rest of the house of
Ahab and Omri. For Omri reigned six years in Israel, 1 Kings 16:23; his son
Ahab twenty-two years, 1 Kings 16:19; Ahab’s son Ahaziah two years; and his
son Joram twelve years, which together make the number forty-two. In the
twelfth year of Joram king of Israel, this Ahaziah king of Judah began to
reign, 2 Kings 8:25. Those who hold this opinion understand a transposition in
the words of verse 1, so that they are inverted thus: “His mother’s name was
Athaliah, born of Omri”—that is, his granddaughter, namely, the daughter of
Ahab the son of Omri—“who was in his forty-second year when Ahaziah reigned.”
But because the transposition seems rather forced and obscure, others think
it better to refer the notation “forty-two years” not to Omri but to Athaliah
herself and to read: “His mother’s name was Athaliah, born of Omri, aged
forty-two years.” For nothing would touch Ahaziah or the condition of the
Jewish kingdom more closely than the birth of the woman Athaliah, who was born
for the destruction not only of her husband and son, but also of the whole
commonwealth and especially of the royal seed. Others, to whom even this
reconciliation seems rather harsh, add another: that beyond the years of his
reign and life recorded in Scripture, Joram, Ahaziah’s father, lived and
reigned for another twenty years, but is said to have reigned only eight
years בחזקתו, “in his strength,” that is, well and successfully. From the
time he fell into the hands of the Arabs and was afflicted by the severe
disease treated in 2 Chronicles 21:16, 17, and 19, he still lived for several
years, but these are not reckoned to his reign but to that of his son Ahaziah
in the book of Kings, because he lived without glory. Hence he is said to
have left behind no desire for him and not to have been brought into the
tombs of the kings, 2 Chronicles 21, last verse. Ahaziah, however, who began
to reign with his father at age twenty-two, after his father’s death entered
the kingdom alone at age forty-two and reigned for only one year, as is noted
in 2 Chronicles 22:2. Thus, after Kimchi, Martyr, Vatablus, Lyranus, and
others reconcile it; this was Jerome’s opinion on Chronicles. Others admit
that there is an error in the book of Chronicles which did not proceed from
the sacred writers but from the copyists, and which could easily slip in
through their carelessness in “abbreviated writing” (βραχυγραφίᾳ), which was
often used in listing numbers, so that מב, which denotes forty-two, was put
for כב, which signifies twenty-two. But they deny that it follows that the
passage is “insoluble” (ἄλυτον), because the knot can easily be untied and the
true reading restored by comparison with the book of Kings, which records the
number corresponding to historical truth and the years of Joram and Ahaziah.
It is also evident that various versions of the book of Chronicles retain the
number twenty-two, such as the Syriac and Arabic and various Greek
manuscripts, including the Oxford, Cambridge, and Roman codices.

XXVI. In Exodus 12:40, the sojourn of the children of Israel in Egypt is said
to be four hundred and thirty years. Yet in Genesis 15:13, the seed of Abraham
is said to be afflicted by foreigners for four hundred years, which is
confirmed in Acts 7:6. The answer is easy: in Genesis 15 a round number is put
for an uneven one, as often occurs in both sacred and profane writers.
Although, therefore, 430 years actually had to pass from the time this oracle
was given to Abraham, from which that number must begin, only 400 are counted
to round the number, and no account is taken of the thirty years. And if the
Israelites are said in Exodus 12 to be going to dwell in Egypt 430 years, it
does not follow that they had to remain in Egypt for that many years, since

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 75 | IA leaf 129 | PDF p. 129]

it is evident that their stay was only 210 years. Rather, the Israelites’
affliction is referred to Egypt because Egypt was its beginning from Genesis
12:10 and its end; or because it was made more notable there by its greater
length, harshness, and lateness, by Joseph’s rule, by the multiplication of
the Hebrews and their most grievous oppression, and by their astonishing
deliverance.

XXVII. In 1 Kings 9, last verse, Solomon’s servants are said to have brought
420 talents of gold from Ophir. But in 2 Chronicles 8, last verse, 450 are
listed. These are easily reconciled if we say either that the passages do not
speak of the same expedition but different ones, since Solomon sent ships
there every three years according to 1 Kings 10:22; or that thirty talents
were not brought to the king but consumed for the use of the fleet.

XXVIII. In Matthew 27:9, an oracle cited concerning the thirty shekels of
silver and the field bought from them is attributed to Jeremiah, though it is
certain that it occurs in Zechariah 11:12. Hence a universal error and a
“failure of memory” (σφάλμα μνημονικόν) in Matthew seem to be inferred. But
the knot can be solved in various ways. Some hold that the name “Jeremiah”
(Ἱερεμίου) slipped into the text from a copyist who did not understand the
scribal abbreviation, when ζοῦ had been written by abbreviation for
ζαχαρίου. But they hold this to be an error of small importance, namely, in a
proper name, and that correction is easy from a comparison of the Old and New
Testaments. Others, which is more likely, maintain that here there is a
“combination” (σύγχυσιν) of two prophetic passages from which this testimony
was formed, namely Jeremiah 32 and Zechariah 11. Various similar examples
occur: Matthew 21:8 from Isaiah 62:11 and Zechariah 9:9; Matthew 21:14 from
Isaiah 56:7 and Jeremiah 7:11; Acts 1:20 from Psalms 69:26 and 109:8; and 1
Peter 2:7 from Psalm 118:22 and Isaiah 8:14. Nor is it unusual, when the words
or testimonies belong to two prophets, for one to be omitted and only the
other’s name expressed, as in Mark 1:2–3, where the earlier part of the
testimony is Malachi’s and the later Isaiah’s, yet with Malachi omitted,
Isaiah alone is named. This can happen all the more when the one cited is
older than the other and the later drew many things from him, as happens
here. For Jeremiah is earlier than Zechariah, and Zechariah is accustomed to
borrow many things from him and, as it were, imitate his sermons, as can
readily be shown from Zechariah 1:14, taken from Jeremiah 18:11 and 35:15, and
Zechariah 3:8, taken from Jeremiah 23:5, and the like. Hence the Jews are
accustomed to say that the Spirit of Jeremiah was in Zechariah. To these can
be added that, according to the rabbinic tradition in the Babylonian Talmud,
*Bava Bathra*, folio 14, cited by Rabbi David Kimchi in his preface to
Jeremiah, Jeremiah occupied the first place in the volume of the Prophets;
hence he is named first in Matthew 16:14. Matthew could therefore cite these
words in the volume of the Prophets under Jeremiah’s name alone because he
held the first place there, just as in Luke 24:44 the book of the Hagiographa
is expressed by the name of the Psalms because it is first among them.
Finally, Jeremiah alone could have been named here by Matthew because Matthew
wished to give an account not so much of the thirty shekels, or the price for
which Christ was bought, which is treated by Zechariah, as of the field bought
with the price, which is treated by Jeremiah. Thus what is said here about the
price is taken from Zechariah, but what is said about the field from Jeremiah.

XXIX. A weighty and intricate knot indeed arises from a comparison of Genesis
11:26 and Acts 7:4. In untying it, Joseph Scaliger himself “suspends judgment”
(ἐπέχει) and attempts no answer, *Notes on Eusebius*, page 17, while Jerome
in *Hebrew Questions* and Augustine in *The City of God* 15.16 profess it to
be an insoluble doubt. Nevertheless, suitable ways of reconciling it are not
lacking which can satisfy modest minds. The knot is this: Moses in Genesis
11:26 wishes Abraham to have been born in the seventieth year of Terah, and
Stephen in Acts 7:4 expressly asserts that Abraham was transferred from Haran
into Canaan after Terah died. But it is certain from Genesis 12:4 that Abraham

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 76 | IA leaf 130 | PDF p. 130]

departed from Haran in the seventy-fifth year of his age, and that his father
Terah lived to 205, Genesis 11:32. Now if it is evident that Abraham was born
in Terah’s seventieth year and departed from Haran in the seventy-fifth year
of his own age, he did not depart after his father’s death, as Stephen says,
but sixty years before it, that is, in the 145th year of Terah’s age. Some
hold that Stephen suffered a lapse of memory here in calculating the time,
and that Luke’s history is not for that reason less “self-authenticating”
(αὐτόπιστον), because it was sufficient for Luke faithfully to report the
things Stephen had said, and he was not concerned to correct Stephen’s error.
But since it is certain that Stephen was full of the Spirit and also
“God-breathed” (θεόπνευστον), he cannot be charged with error without the
“self-authenticating character” (αὐτοπιστία) of the Word tottering. Others
maintain that Abraham’s birth should not be assigned precisely to the
seventieth year of Terah’s age, but only to his 130th year, and that the
contrary cannot be inferred from the statement in Genesis 11:26 that “Terah
at age seventy begot Abraham, Nahor, and Haran.” For although Abraham is
placed first, this is done by privilege not of age but of dignity, just as in
Genesis 5:32 Noah is said at age five hundred to have begotten Shem, Ham, and
Japheth—that is, to have begun to beget his three sons, of whom Shem was the
most worthy, although he was born one or two years after the older Japheth.
Thus Terah at age seventy began to beget his three sons, of whom Abraham was
the most worthy and was therefore placed first, though he was not the
firstborn. According to this opinion, Abram, born in the 130th year of Terah’s
age, lived with him partly in Chaldea and partly in Haran for seventy-five
years until his death, after which, to obey the divine command he had received
in Chaldea, he migrated with his household from Haran into Canaan. But others
find it harsh to defer Abram’s begetting to Terah’s 130th year, since
Scripture says nothing about it and thus Abram’s entire genealogy is rendered
uncertain, although Moses intended to record it above all others. They untie
the knot in another way, positing two calls of Abram: the first from Ur of the
Chaldeans to Haran, while his father was still living and in his 145th year
and Abram himself was seventy-five, of which Moses speaks in Genesis 11:3 and
12:1; and the second after his father’s death, from Haran into Canaan, which
seems to be suggested in Genesis 12:4–5. Stephen certainly indicates this not
obscurely in Acts 7:4, when he says that Abraham, called by God, went out from
the land of the Chaldeans and lived in Haran, and afterward, following his
father’s death, God transferred him into the land of Canaan. There God is
said to have called him out twice, first from Ur of the Chaldeans into Haran,
and afterward from Haran into Canaan. This seems a suitable and easy way of
reconciling them. Finally, some hold that there is an error in the listing of
the years of Terah’s life, which should not be extended to 205 years but only
to 145, as the Samaritan Pentateuch has it. Eusebius noted this in *Greek
Chronological Canon*, book 1, page 10: “According to the Samaritan Hebrew,
Terah, being seventy years old, begot Abraham, and lived seventy-five years,
until Abraham’s seventy-fifth year” (κατὰ τὸ Σαμαρικὸν Ἑβραϊκὸν, Θάῤῥα
γενόμενος ἐτῶν ο γεννᾷ τὸν Ἀβραάμ, καὶ ἐπέζησεν ἔτη οε μέχρις οε ἔτους
Ἀβραάμ). They conjecture that the common error could easily have arisen from
this: in calculation by letters, the letter מ, which has the value forty, was
changed into ק, which denotes one hundred, whence the difference of sixty
years arose. But since Jews and Christians commonly hold that only the Hebrew
Pentateuch, not the Samaritan, is authentic, one could not easily say that the
former should be corrected from the latter.

XXX. If it is asked how Genesis 46:27, where seventy Israelites are said to
have gone down into Egypt, can be reconciled with Acts 7:14, where seventy-
five are numbered, various answers are given. 1. Some hold there is no
discrepancy because the passages are not parallel. Moses, they say, sets forth
the genealogy of Jacob, or a catalog only of the descendants who came from
his thigh and entered Egypt with him, excluding his sons’ wives. Thus they
are said to have been sixty-six, verse 26. To find this

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 77 | IA leaf 131 | PDF p. 131]

number, Joseph and his two sons must be excluded because they did not enter
Egypt with Jacob, since they were already there; Er and Onan must also be
excluded because they died in Canaan; and his daughter Dinah must be added.
For the complete number of Jacob’s sons and grandsons rises to seventy-one,
from which, if you subtract five, sixty-six will remain. But in verse 27,
Moses weaves a catalog of the whole family, both those who entered Egypt with
Jacob and those already there, that is, Joseph with his two sons. If you add
Jacob, the head of his family, to them, you will have the number seventy.
Stephen, however, wished to teach whom Joseph called into Egypt, and therefore
excluded Joseph with his two sons and the two grandsons of Judah, Hezron and
Hamul, treated in verse 12, who were born only in Egypt, while counting Jacob
separately by distinguishing him from his “kindred” (συγγενείᾳ). Thus eleven
brothers, Jacob’s sons, remain, with their sister Dinah and fifty-two
grandsons. If eleven wives of the sons, who belonged to “the kindred” (τὴν
συγγένειαν), are added, the number of seventy-five souls is obtained. Others
hold that the passage has been corrupted and that Stephen did not say, or
Luke write, “five” (πέντε), but “all” (πάντες), a word which Moses also
expressed in Genesis 46:27, “every soul,” and so forth. The passage should be
translated, “who all”—that is, who joined together—“were seventy souls,” that
is, persons; or perhaps the word was πάντως, “altogether.” Through the
carelessness of scribes, from πάντες or πάντως there could easily arise πέντε,
both in the Septuagint and in Luke. Nevertheless the authenticity of Scripture is
not thereby undermined, since the passage can easily be emended from a
comparison of other passages and the analogy of faith, and the credibility of
both doctrine and history remains safe. Others hold that Stephen here did not
follow the Hebrew text but the Septuagint version, which has the number
seventy-five, as being more common and everywhere received, and because he
did not think that the reckoning of the number pertained to the matter under
discussion. They derive this number in the Septuagint by adding five sons of
Joseph from 1 Chronicles 7 whom Moses does not mention and who were born after
the entry into Egypt, namely Machir, Gilead, Shuthelah, Toben, and Eden. But
whether Stephen here followed the Septuagint or an error entered both the
Septuagint and Luke through the carelessness of copyists, it is certain that
the error is not incurable, since it is easy to correct it from the Hebrew
text; for the Scriptures must be expounded by the Scriptures.

XXXI. In Acts 7:16, Stephen speaks thus of the patriarchs: “They were carried
over into Shechem and laid in the tomb which Abraham bought for a sum of money
from the sons of Emmor τοῦ Συχέμ.” But it is evident from Genesis 23:9 that
Abraham bought a tomb from Ephron near the city of Hebron, while from Genesis
33:19 and Joshua 24:32 it is certain that not Abraham but Jacob bought a field
from the sons of Emmor, not for a sum of money but for one hundred lambs.
Hence an insoluble knot seems to arise. Nevertheless, it can be suitably
untied if we say that Stephen does not look to Abraham’s purchase treated in
Genesis 23:9, but to Jacob’s treated in Genesis 33:19: “Then Jacob came safely
to the city of Shechem and bought a portion of that field from the sons of
Emmor, the father of Shechem, for one hundred lambs,” where Joseph is said to
have been buried, Joshua 24:32. Nor is it an obstacle, 1. that Stephen says
Abraham bought the field, not Jacob. For either “Abraham” can be a genitive
here, so that it reads ὁ τοῦ Ἀβραὰμ, with ἔκγονος understood, “the descendant
of Abraham”; or, which is preferable, “Abraham” is taken patronymically for
“Abrahamide,” that is, Jacob the grandson of Abraham, as the names of fathers
are often put to designate descendants, even where individual persons are
treated. Thus David is put for his grandson Rehoboam in 1 Kings 12:16, “What
portion have we with David?” and so forth; Rehoboam for Abijah in 1 Kings
14:15; and David for Christ in Jeremiah 30:9 and Ezekiel 34:23. So also in
Virgil, *Aeneid* 4, Aeneas is called “Dardanus” for “Dardanides.” Or one must
say with Beza that the name “Abraham” is a gloss which, written in the margin
by an ignorant person, finally

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 78 | IA leaf 132 | PDF p. 132]

slipped into the text. Thus not Abraham but Jacob is said to have bought this
field. Nor is it an obstacle, 2. that the fathers are said to have been
carried over into Shechem, although Joseph alone is read to have been buried
there. For although Moses mentions Joseph alone because he alone was a
prophet, foresaw the deliverance, and by faith requested that his bones be
carried into Canaan, Hebrews 11:22—which he also secured by oath, Genesis
50:28, and hence this had to be narrated because in this way the Israelites
discharged themselves of the oath, Exodus 13:19—nevertheless Stephen could
speak in the plural through an interchange of number or a synecdoche of the
whole, as Scripture often loves to speak in this way. In Matthew 26:8 the
disciples are said to murmur, which is understood of the one Judas Iscariot,
who alone was indignant at the ointment being poured out, as John 12:11
explains; and in Matthew 27:44 the thieves are said to have reproached Christ
on the cross, which is referred to the one unrepentant thief, as Luke 23:39
explains. Further, although Moses did not expressly indicate this, it can
readily be inferred that Joseph’s other brothers were buried in the same
place, because all the patriarchs had equal standing among the Jews, the same
dignity, and the same cause which moved Joseph to make this request: faith in
God’s promise, a possession in the land, and hope of the resurrection. To
this pertains Jerome’s report in the *Epitaph of Paula* that, when she turned
aside at Shechem, she saw there the tombs of the twelve patriarchs. His
testimony, as that of an eyewitness, seems preferable to the testimony of
Josephus, who in *Antiquities* 2.4 relates that the patriarchs were buried at
Hebron. 3. It is no obstacle that the text does not have “the father of
Shechem,” as in Genesis 33:29, but simply τοῦ Συχέμ. For the word “father” can
rightly be understood, as more than once elsewhere: in Luke 14:10, “Mary of
James,” that is, James’s mother; Herodotus, *Thalia*, “Cyrus of Cambyses”
(Κῦρος ὁ Καμβύσεω), that is, Cyrus the father of Cambyses; Aelian, book 13.30,
“Olympias of Alexander” (Ὀλυμπιὰς ἡ Ἀλεξάνδρου), namely Alexander’s mother.
Finally, it is no obstacle that Jacob is said in Moses to have bought the
field for one hundred lambs, while in Luke the field is said to have been
bought for a sum of money. For the word קשיטה denotes a coin no less than a
lamb, because coins were then stamped with the image of a lamb, just as in
ancient times all money bore the image of livestock, because before coins
merchandise was exchanged for livestock. See Rabbi David Kimchi’s *Roots* on
the word קשיטה; Buxtorf, *Chaldean Dictionary*; Bochart, *Hierozoicon*, page
436; and so forth.

XXXII. There is no contradiction between 2 Corinthians 11:4 and Galatians
1:8, because in the latter it is said that no gospel actually and truly
different from the one preached by the apostles exists. But in the former it
is posited only hypothetically through an impossibility, not because another
gospel actually exists or can exist, but to take away every excuse from the
Corinthians for the fickleness they displayed in receiving pseudo-apostles.
That is: “If matters stood so that someone coming to you could bring another
Jesus and another, more perfect gospel, you would rightly receive and bear
with such a teacher, while despising us. But since this is utterly impossible,
as you acknowledge and the pseudo-apostles themselves cannot deny, you cannot
offer anything in your own excuse.”

XXXIII. Although 1 Kings 8:9 says that nothing was in the ark except the
tablets of the Law, while in Hebrews 9:4 the apostle says that other things
were there—when he says that “in it,” namely the ark of which he had
immediately spoken, “were the golden jar containing manna, Aaron’s rod that
budded, and the tablets of the covenant”—no contradiction occurs, and the
difficulty is easily solved. Either we may say that “in which” (ἐν ᾗ) is
referred not to the nearest word, “ark” (κιβωτὸν), but to the more remote
word, “tabernacle” (σκηνὴν), verse 3, since it is well known that a relative
is sometimes referred not to the nearest but to a more remote antecedent; or
that the preposition ἐν is not put here for “in,” as though the manna and
Aaron’s rod were in the ark itself, but for “with,” “near,” or “at,” and thus
corresponds to the Hebrew preposition ב, which does not always denote a thing
inside another, but also one attached and adhering to another, and sometimes
means “with,”

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 79 | IA leaf 133 | PDF p. 133]

as in Deuteronomy 6:7, “you shall speak בם, with them”; 1 Samuel 1:24, בפרים,
“with bullocks”; Hosea 4:3, “in the beast,” that is, “with the beast”; so also
Matthew 16:27, “in glory” (ἐν δόξῃ), that is, “with glory”; 1 Thessalonians
4:10, “in a voice, in a trumpet,” for “with.” At other times it means “at” or
“near”: Joshua 10:10, “in Gibeon”; Judges 18:12, “in Kirjath-jearim,” that is,
near those places. In Matthew 21:12, those buying and selling are said to have
been “in the temple,” that is, in its porches, as also in John 10:23.
Elsewhere Christ is said to have suffered “in Jerusalem,” that is, near it.
Thus it can best be translated “in which” or “near which,” namely the ark,
were the jar and the rod, so that not insertion but placement beside it is
signified. Or “with which,” that is, the jar, rod, and tablets of the Law were
joined to the ark, but in different ways: the tablets within the ark, but the
jar and rod outside or beside the ark.

XXXIV. When Christ in Matthew 5:33 forbids swearing “altogether” (ὅλως), he
does not wish absolutely and simply to condemn an oath, which elsewhere is
permitted, approved, and commanded by God, Exodus 22:8, 10–11; Leviticus 5:4;
Numbers 5:19–20; Proverbs 18:18; Hebrews 6:16. Rather, he condemns particular
kinds of oath which the Jews were accustomed to use and which are treated
there, namely by heaven, earth, Jerusalem, one’s head, and other creatures of
that kind, all of which are condemned by Christ as rash and unlawful. Thus
universal terms are often restricted to a particular kind, John 10:8, “All
who came before me are thieves,” namely, those not called or sent, or who say
that they or anyone else are the door of the sheep. And in 1 Corinthians
10:20, “All things are lawful for me,” and chapter 9:22, “I became all things
to all,” namely, in lawful and indifferent matters; for evil and vicious
things are never lawful for anyone.

XXXV. In Acts 13:21, Saul is said to have reigned forty years, but in 1 Samuel
13:1 he is said to have reigned only two years. The answer is that 1 Samuel
13 does not say that Saul reigned for only two years, but only that he had
then reigned two years when he chose the three thousand attendants treated
in the following verse; after he had reigned two years, only when that two-
year period was completed did he gather those military forces, as the Syriac
and Arabic note.

XXXVI. From these things it is evident that those various “seeming
contradictions” (ἐναντιοφανῆ), which are brought forward to overthrow the
“authenticity” (αὐθεντίαν) of Scripture and have been presented by us as
examples, although difficult and “hard to understand” (δυσνόητα), are
nevertheless not wholly “insoluble” (ἄλυτα). There are some other passages
which the papists are accustomed to bring forward to prove a corruption of
the original texts made by Jews or heretics; but they will more suitably be
vindicated in what follows, when we dispute about the authentic edition.
