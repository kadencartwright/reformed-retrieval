CHAPTER XIV.

The Fourth Argument: Separation Would Then Be Required from Churches Which Do Not
Receive Excommunication.

John Whitgift states the fourth argument thus: “I cannot persuade myself that any
form of government is so necessary that it cannot be changed. One reason is that,
if excommunication—which is a certain kind of government—were necessary, it would
be lawful to separate from Churches which do not use it; but we now see the
contrary.” He wrote this before our separation arose—would that it had never
arisen! Otherwise he would doubtless have fixed his nail here, as all the more
recent Hierarchists do.

Thus Matthew Sutcliffe says, “If your discipline is part of the Gospel, the
Barrowists have just cause to withdraw and separate themselves from us; you have
conspired with them to defame the English Church.” Lous similarly asks why our
reformers oppose Barrow so vehemently and inveigh against Greenwood’s impure
offspring. Either, he says, they must diminish what they have so often written
about the holiness of their discipline, or defend the shameless efforts of the
man who breaks the bonds of peace and concord. Unless schism is approved, that
discipline falls. Thomas Bell rolls the same stone: “The patrons of the English
presbytery make it an essential part of the Gospel, so necessary to salvation
that no Church can exist without it.” What can one do with a man who lies so
shamelessly? But let him continue. “I prove it secondly, because it is lawful to
remain in a Church destitute of excommunication, which we certainly could not do
if excommunication were part of Christ’s Gospel and necessary to salvation. One
must flee a Church which lacks anything necessary to salvation.” Bell therefore
agrees with the rest that one must separate from England because discipline is
lacking, provided only that discipline is necessary.

It was not enough for them to supply an occasion for separation by corrupting
discipline, unless they also continually fostered what they had aroused. Indeed,
even this was not enough unless they transferred the blame to those whose debates
and struggles against the schismatics are too well known to need an oration.
Bancroft says: “Your struggles against the schismatics are splendid indeed, since
an intercepted letter of Greenwood shows that this schism is nothing other than
a consequence of your doctrine, obtained by adding minor premises to your major
premises.” Perhaps your struggle is more splendid, resting on this foundation:
No form of discipline is prescribed in God’s Word; therefore no separation is to
be made on account of discipline. This is your celebrated defeat of the
Brownists, valued so highly that T. B. could not refrain from telling us that his
assertion—that election by the people’s votes is not a correct form prescribed in
the Word—was useful for refuting the Brownists, who deny that we have any
ministers because they are not elected by the people’s votes.

[Marginal references: Whitgift, tractate 2, chapter 1, division 2, page 81;
Sutcliffe, *Treatise on Discipline*, chapter 15, page 165; Lous,
*Ecclesiastical Complaint*, pages 59–60; Bell, *On Ecclesiastical Government*,
chapter 12, pages 151–152; Bancroft, chapter 33, pages 430–432; Bilson, *On the
Perpetual Government of Christ’s Church*, chapter 15, page 412.]

[1638 control | printed p. 39 | BSB canvas 49 | bsb10507786_00049]

But if such are your hopes, you have no hopes of Troy. So far are the Brownists
from being struck down by this weapon that they are instead armed with it, simply
by adding true minor premises to your false major premises. Whitgift says: If
discipline is necessary and delivered in the Word as immutable, separation must
be made because of its corruption. They now add the true minor: But discipline is
necessary and delivered in God’s Word as immutable; therefore separation must be
made from the English Church which corrupts it. Sutcliffe says: If discipline is
necessary and a part of the Gospel, the Barrowists have just cause to separate
themselves from our Church. They add the true minor: But discipline is necessary
and part of the Gospel; therefore we have just cause to separate from the English
Church. Lous says: Either the reformers’ praises of the holiness of their
discipline must be diminished, or schism must be approved. They add the true
minor: But the reformers’ praises of their discipline’s holiness must not be
diminished; therefore our separation is to be approved. Bancroft says: Schism is
a consequence of the discipline which the reformers seek. They add the true
minor: But the discipline which the reformers seek is true, instituted by Christ,
and nothing but truth follows from truth; therefore our separation fights for
divine truth. You, then, supply them with true minor premises; we supply none at
all. They do try to derive certain minors from our majors, but they are both
absurd and inconsequent.

For even if discipline is necessary and delivered in God’s Word, it by no force
of consequence follows that separation must immediately be made wherever it is
corrupted. This is clear from John Calvin. He judges that discipline is prescribed
in God’s Word as regards its substantial elements, yet proves by sufficiently
solid arguments that departure because of its corruption is Novatian and
Donatist. Thomas Cartwright did the same against Harrison. Sutcliffe vainly calls
his reasons weak and childish, since Bancroft not only cites but approves them.
Does he approve them? What, then, has he left himself to answer? This: that
Cartwright’s refutation of Harrison contradicts Cartwright himself, because here
he contends that discipline is necessary not to the Church’s existence but only
to its well-being, although elsewhere he allegedly calls it necessary to the
Church’s very existence. Where? Bancroft says it was in the Puritan assembly held
at Coventry in 1588, where Cartwright in his subscription called the approved
form of discipline essential. Essential to what? He did not call it essential to
the Church, but—as Bancroft himself reports—essential to true discipline
prescribed by Christ. They subscribed in Cartwright’s presence that the form of
discipline there drawn up was the essential form of discipline and necessary at
all times.

[Marginal references: Calvin, *Institutes*, book 4, chapter 1; Cartwright,
*Treatise on Discipline*, chapter 15; Bancroft, chapter 34, page 448, and chapter
4, page 67.]

[1638 control | printed p. 40 | BSB canvas 50 | bsb10507786_00050]

Cartwright was not at all mistaken in this. Whitgift’s objection is untrue: that
we quarrel only about accidental matters of discipline, not substantial ones,
because only the matter of discipline—namely, the Word and sacraments—is
substantial, as though these alone were precisely delivered in the Word. Who ever
dreamed of essential matter without an essential form? Certainly H. S. did not;
he too regards the form of government as essential to discipline. He says: “This
belongs to the essence of order, that there be some who command and others who
obey. No better form of government can be delivered than that which the Apostles
delivered by their example.” Cartwright’s position therefore remains sound and
firm for refuting Harrison: the form of reformed discipline is essential to
discipline, but by no means to the Church itself. It is necessary only to the
Church’s well-being, not to its existence, like a wall to a city.

2. Here Dr. Copequot meets us. In his sermon at Paul’s Cross in 1584 he proves
that we deny England to be a Church because we say our discipline is as necessary
to the Church as a wall is necessary to a city. Lous pierces this wretched
argument with one stroke by demonstrating at length that a city can exist without
a wall. Yet he is not sound when he teaches that, like ancient Sparta, a city can
not only exist without a wall but can flourish; Scripture calls the city without
a wall miserable, Proverbs 25:28. If a Church can likewise flourish without
discipline as a city can without a wall, did Cartwright truly touch its necessity
when he made it only the Church’s wall? Lous himself says, “Right discipline is
more to the Church than a wall to a city.” See, then, the Hierarchists place more
necessity in discipline than Cartwright himself and come nearer to the Brownist
dogma of its essentiality, while he declares it only the Church’s wall.

But if it is only a wall, you ask, why do you contend so fiercely? As though it
were not a great benefit of God that “he enclosed it with a wall,” Isaiah 5:2,
and therefore a great calamity, “I will tear down its wall,” Isaiah 5:5—and thus,
finally, a great sin of the English Bishops, who brought this calamity upon the
Church. Yet the crash of this wall falling in England by their fault ought not so
to terrify the Brownists that they think they must flee elsewhere and seek new
dwellings. Where will you flee, especially since England has the words of eternal
life, John 6:68? Nehemiah so mourned the destruction of his city’s wall that he
flew from distant lands to restore it, Nehemiah 2:3. This was your duty, in
accordance with Augustine’s admonition that the pious correct what they can and
patiently bear what they cannot, groaning and mourning with love until God amends
and corrects it or winnows out the tares and chaff at harvest.

[Marginal references: Whitgift, *Preface*; H. S., *Against Beza*, book 2,
chapter 20; Copequot, sermon at Paul’s Cross, page 510; Lous, *Ecclesiastical
Complaint*, page 60; Augustine, *Against the Letters of Petilian*, book 3,
chapter 2.]

Your separation is diametrically opposed to this love and patience. Separation is
lawful only when the foundation itself is demolished, not when the wall is thrown
down. Since the foundation remains in England as regards the fundamentals of
faith, its errors in discipline are only hay and stubble built on the foundation,
1 Corinthians 3:12. What foundation in Scripture, then, does your separation
have? “Come out from them and be separate, says the Lord,” 2 Corinthians 6:14?
Paul speaks of unbelievers, among whom not even ruins remained of God’s law and
worship, true faith and the Gospel, or the foundation. The priests and Levites who
feared God separated themselves, 2 Chronicles 11:14? That separation was from
idolatrous Israelites who fundamentally overthrew God’s law and worship through
Jeroboam’s idols. “Come out of her, my people,” Revelation 18:4? The angel speaks
of mystical Babylon, the Roman synagogue which has scraped away the very
foundations of faith and the Gospel. “Withdraw yourself from such,” 1 Timothy
6:5? Paul speaks of a fundamental corruption of the doctrine according to
godliness. Paul separated the disciples, Acts 19:9 and 28:28? That separation was
from Jews who blasphemed the Gospel’s very foundations.

Finally, you press the separations commanded in 1 Corinthians 5:11, Psalm 17:4,
2 Peter 2:8, and 2 Timothy 3:5. But these passages command only private
separation, which abstains from unnecessary familiarity with ill-behaved men.
There is therefore no example of pious men or command of Prophets and Apostles in
Scripture for your separation. You separate not from unbelieving, idolatrous,
Jewish, or Antichristian societies, but from the English Church, the counterpart
of Laodicea, where Christ enters, reclines, sups, and feasts with his people by his
Spirit, Word, and sacraments, Revelation 3:20.

Lous might flatter us here as he once did Cartwright when Cartwright condemned the
separatists; but he ought also to add: “Go on and retract what you rashly wrote
against the Church; bring these poor little sheep, cast out of the fold by your
fault, back to the fold.” Indeed, by stubbornly defending and retaining our
Church’s corruptions you are the men who caused these poor sheep to be exiled from
the fold. The exhortation of Dionysius to Novatus, which you undeservedly apply to
Cartwright, rebounds upon you: “It would have been better to undergo every
calamity than to rend God’s Church. If you cannot bring the dissenting brethren
back to concord, at least do not permit your own souls to perish.” Yet there is no
need of Dionysius here. Let the corruptions for which you fight as for hearth and
home be cleansed away, and I dare say calm weather will soon follow.

You ask what corruptions I describe, since I just confessed that Christ reclines
in the English Church. This does nothing to excuse its discipline. Christ indeed
enters and reclines, but he makes himself familiar with those members of the
English Church who have overcome the obstacles of corrupted discipline.

[Marginal references: Lous, *Ecclesiastical Complaint*, pages 62–63.]

With respect to discipline Christ stands partly outside, Revelation 3:20. He
enters his own, but not through an open door as in the Helvetic and other reformed
Churches which answer to Philadelphia, Revelation 3:8, where the key of David is
vigorous—that is, the discipline which throws open a door for Christ to enter.
Rather, he comes through a door almost shut, because in England that key-bearing
discipline is largely absent. Thus much for the second thesis: polity is necessary
by necessity of command and right.

CHAPTER XV.

The Third Thesis: Discipline Is Immutable in Its Substantial Elements.

From this emerges the third thesis. If ecclesiastical polity is not an indifferent
thing but is commanded and established in the Word by divine right, it follows
that it is immutable in its substantial elements. The Apostle Paul expressly
attests this: “I charge you before God and Jesus Christ to keep this commandment
without spot and blame until the appearing of Jesus Christ,” 1 Timothy 6:13.
Thomas Bilson comments upon this in a way that plainly confirms our thesis about
the immutability of polity. Timothy could not possibly have observed these things
until Christ’s coming in glory, since he had to die long before it. Therefore the
commands were delivered not to him alone but to others who would succeed him in
his place until the end of the age. Bilson also cites Ambrose: “With great
watchfulness and foresight he gives commandments to the ruler of the Church. The
welfare of the Church consists in his person. He is so circumspect not from
anxiety for Timothy alone, but for his successors, so that by Timothy’s example
they might guard the ordering of the Church.”

These words make clear not only that Christ and the Apostle sanctioned an
immutable order for governing the Church, but also the point from which the era
of its immutability is to be reckoned. Ambrose’s care here is useful especially
for refuting the Pontificians and answering the Hierarchists. Sutcliffe proves
that the present form of government is mutable because it formerly changed from
Christ’s time: it was monarchical while Christ lived on earth, and the Apostles
who immediately succeeded Christ have now ceased. Nicholas Sander, conversely,
uses this to prove that there must always be a perpetual monarch in the visible
Church, and consequently a Pope, because Christ was monarch while he lived and
the form of government in his time is immutable. Bilson and Hadrian Saravia deny
that immutability must be traced from Christ’s time lest they admit the Pope, but
say that it began with the Apostles’ government, whose authority they claim to be
perpetual in the Bishops. Whitaker, answering the same argument of Bellarmine,
strikes down both Sander and these men with one blow. Discipline was not immutable
from Christ’s time, he says, for the Church was first governed—

[Marginal references: Bilson, *On the Perpetual Government of Christ’s Church*,
chapter 14; Sutcliffe, *Treatise on Discipline*, chapter 13, pages 155–156;
Sander, *On the Visible Monarchy*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 43 | BSB canvas 53 | bsb10507786_00053]

—by extraordinary rulers, Apostles, Evangelists, and Prophets, who have now been
removed; but now it is governed only by pastors and teachers. He adds that
Stapleton himself admits that the form of government in the Apostles’ time was
different from that of the present. The Pope therefore falls, and the Bishop
falls with him. The form of government now ought to differ from that under the
Apostles, much more from that under Christ; consequently the era of immutability
must be reckoned neither from Christ’s government nor from the Apostles’.

Sutcliffe says it begins at no time whatever, because it does not begin from
these times. Ambrose, however, argues from Paul that there is a definite time
from which the form of ecclesiastical government began to be immutable: the time
when the Apostles established the Church’s ordering, that is, a definite ordinary
order for governing it. This is the order Paul delivers in his letters and Luke
records in Acts as observed by the Apostles as soon as Churches were constituted.
William Whitaker describes it more particularly by citing Jerome: “At first the
Church was governed by the common counsel of presbyters.” Here is the command
about governing the Church which Paul charges to be kept without spot or blame
until Christ’s coming. The English Bishops must therefore consider how, when
Christ comes to judgment, they will answer for their monarchical usurpation
against the aristocratic form of rule by presbyters which Christ established.

2. Those who place confidence in the mutability of the first form of government
are condemned by the verdict of their own associates, as though by a preliminary
judgment of the condemnation to come. If Christ’s saying, “Your sons shall be
your judges,” does not apply, something similar does: Your associates shall be
your judges. John Whitgift says, “The Puritans’ rotten foundation is that the same
discipline which existed in the Apostles’ times must be retained now.” Matthew
Sutcliffe says, “The example and practice of apostolic times in the government of
the Church are not now to be observed.” Against them Hadrian Saravia says: “That
form of governing the Church which the Apostles and apostolic men used has not
been abolished. The Apostles knew that men are moved more by examples than by
precepts, and that the rulers whom they left over the Churches would more quickly
imitate what they saw than what was delivered to them in words. If anything had
to be testified clearly and expressly to posterity, this above all was
necessary.”

Whitgift says, “No one definite form of governing the Church is prescribed in
the Word which must necessarily be observed forever.” Against him Saravia says:
“God never gave any nation a definite and perpetual form of civil rule which
could not be changed according to place and time. But the case of ecclesiastical
government is different: since it proceeded immediately from God, men cannot
change it at their pleasure.” Whitgift and Sutcliffe say that the difference
between the Apostles’ times and ours requires a different government of the
Church because the civil magistrate has entered.

[Marginal references: Whitgift, *Preface*; Sutcliffe, *On Discipline*; Saravia,
*Against Beza*, chapter 14, page 139; tractate 17, page 658; *Theological
Tractates*, epistle to the reader, page 147.]

Against them Saravia says: “The arrival of the magistrate does not require a
change in the form of ecclesiastical government. Divine wisdom so tempered it
that it conflicts with no form of civil government. When an entire commonwealth
has become a Church, the government indeed receives some alteration, but one
which does not change its nature.” Whitgift says that the polity of Archbishops
and Bishops may be altered at will. Saravia replies that the authority of
Archbishop and Bishop is apostolic and immutable. Lous says, “The Apostles
prescribed no form of ecclesiastical government which they commanded to be
perpetual,” and Sutcliffe says that the Apostles have ceased and apostolic usage
is no longer to be observed. G. C. answers: “It certainly follows that the
governors whom the Apostles constituted in the Church ought to remain perpetually
in the Church, because they were instituted for perpetual uses.”

John Bridges says that God either did not express the government of the Church or
expressed it so that it did not bind us perpetually. Saravia replies that no man
can consent to any change in discipline without sin. George Downham says that
only ordination and jurisdiction are called perpetual by Paul in 1 Timothy 6:13,
not the episcopal office by which Titus and Timothy exercised them. Thomas Bilson
answers with an emphatic protest: the words are effectual, the adjuration
vehement, and the commands endure until the Lord’s coming rather than ending with
the death of Titus or Timothy. The persons to whom the Apostle wrote were
therefore Bishops. “When these things are rightly weighed,” he says, “no sane man
will deny that episcopal power over presbyters in these causes was sanctioned by
divine command as the Spirit dictated. Christian reader, I earnestly ask you to
attend carefully. The establishment of the episcopal office and power depends on
this if our proofs stand firm; its overthrow and ruin follow if their answer has
sufficient weight. If this fails, Bishops may claim their authority by the
Church’s custom, but not by divine right or scriptural testimony.” Yet George
Downham says this passage does nothing for episcopal authority; the divine right
of episcopal jurisdiction is therefore overthrown at its foundation.

Sutcliffe says that the form of Church government is not always one and the same.
Saravia replies that the Apostles and Evangelists left us the example and law of
perfect ecclesiastical government, to be observed forever. Sutcliffe says the
magistrate may change the governors of the Church. Bilson replies that princes
received the administration of the Church not to change or move its discipline
at their own pleasure, but to use it diligently for the Church’s and people’s
benefit. Sutcliffe says the form of ecclesiastical government in the old and new
covenants was not established on condition that it be perpetual.

[Marginal references: Sutcliffe, *Treatise on Discipline*, chapter 13, page 155;
Saravia, *Against Beza*; Lous, *Ecclesiastical Complaint*, page 57; Bridges,
book 1, page 167; Downham, book 4; Bilson, *On Perpetual Government*, chapter
14, pages 346–347; Sutcliffe, *Against Beza*, chapter 16, page 168.]

Saravia replies: “Even if we granted that not everything God established was
intended to be perpetual, the form of government which existed in the Apostles’
age was established on that condition.” Again: “The Apostles’ institution
concerning ecclesiastical government can no more be changed than God’s decree
concerning the Aaronic priesthood could be changed.” Sutcliffe says that laws
concerning ecclesiastical government do not endure forever. Bilson replies that
the laws Paul laid down in the Epistle to Timothy concerning ecclesiastical
government are inviolable. Sutcliffe says we are bound neither to the governors
nor the laws of apostolic times. Bilson replies that Paul made laws in the
Epistle to Timothy which would remain valid for all posterity.

In this fashion our opponents agree among themselves in this one chapter, though
they so bitterly exaggerate some supposed contradictions among us. Let Sutcliffe
cease accusing us as Cadmean brothers born from serpent’s seed, and turn his eyes
upon his own men, who fight one another like the sons of Cadmus. Let this suffice
for the third thesis: ecclesiastical polity is necessary by necessity of
immutability.

CHAPTER XVI.

The Fourth Thesis: Discipline Is Necessary by Necessity of the End, Which Is
Threefold.

The fourth thesis follows. If ecclesiastical polity is not an indifferent thing
but commanded in God’s Word as perpetual and immutable, necessity of the end must
also be attributed to it. It is a necessary means instituted by God for a most
necessary end. Our opponents so cloud this end with smoke that three knots must
be untied: first, its necessity for the Church’s outward subsistence; second, its
necessity for marking and judging the Church; and third and last, polity’s
necessity for the Church’s salvation and blessedness.

As to the first, Richard Field approves Stapleton’s statement that a Christian
society may be called a true Church either because it possesses only the true
nature and essence of a Church, or because beyond this it also has everything
pertaining to the Church’s integrity and fullness. According to this distinction,
doctrine alone may be called absolutely necessary to constitute the Church’s bare
essence, while discipline is necessary in a qualified sense for its integrity
and fullness. I explain it thus: the Church’s essence is considered either
absolutely, insofar as it is a Church, and thus doctrine and faith are necessary
to its essence; or in a qualified respect, insofar as it is visible, and thus
discipline is necessary to its essence. This distinction is not contemptible.
Whitgift himself says that although government, or some kind of government, may
be part of the Church as regards its outward form and perfection, it is not such
a part of its essence that Christ’s Church cannot exist without this or that kind
of government.

[Marginal references: Saravia, *Against Beza*, chapter 13, pages 232 and 240;
Sutcliffe, page 158; Bilson, chapter 5, page 82, and chapter 7, page 131;
Sutcliffe, chapter 1, page 6; Field, *On the Church*, book 2, chapter 3;
Whitgift, tractate 2, chapter 1, division 2, page 81.]

[1638 control | printed p. 46 | BSB canvas 56 | bsb10507786_00056]

Why, then, are we so abused throughout Bancroft’s chapter 34 for saying that
discipline is necessary in this fashion for the Church’s outward subsistence? Why
is Theodore Beza scourged as though he contradicted both us and himself when he
writes: “Doctrine alone is absolutely necessary to the Church’s essence. The
whole Church in the wilderness lacked the sacrament of circumcision, yet did not
cease to be God’s people. Much more may the same be said of ecclesiastical
discipline: Churches may lack it and nevertheless be pious and Christian Churches
if they possess the pure and sincere doctrine of the chief articles.”

This is Beza’s position and that of all the rest who have written on discipline.
They have never spoken more honorably of discipline’s essentiality—to use
Bancroft’s word—than Whitgift himself just did or than the other Hierarchical
writers commonly do. Bilson says that the power to announce the Word and dispense
the sacraments, the right administration of the keys, and the laying on of hands
must remain perpetually in the Church. So long as the Church remains, these
things ought not to be lacking; if even one is absent, the name “Church” will
scarcely stand. John Bridges cites and approves Ignatius: “Without the assembly
of presbyters there is no elect Church, no assembly of saints, no holy
congregation.” Saravia says that there can be no Church where no sacrament of
Christ and no ministry of the Church is seen. Downham says that the authority
held by Titus and Timothy is ordinary and perpetually necessary not only for the
well-being but for the existence of visible Churches. Again, in every government
five things must be considered: power, order, form or manner, title or the
application of power to a person, and use. The first two—that there be power to
govern and order in it—are essential and perpetual as immutable statutes of God,
since by his institution the Church ought to be an ordered society and, as it is
often called, an army in ranks, Song of Songs 6:3, 9.

Bancroft, since these things occur among your own writers, why do you rage so
fiercely against us for judging discipline necessary to the visible Church’s
existence? Do you wish the Church to be a Cyclopean crowd, where no one listens to
another? Do you oppose political writers who hold that a society without polity
and order is a band of robbers, not a commonwealth? I see the stone at which you
stumble—or rather at which you wish to persuade the world that we stumbled. You
argue as if we thought the full and perfect discipline which we say is lacking in
the English Church necessary for a visible Church’s outward existence. This never
entered our minds. Our position is plain: the Church which has perfect doctrine
and discipline is a perfect Church; one with imperfect discipline is imperfect;
one with no discipline even in the smallest part is not to be regarded as any
Church at all.

[Marginal references: Bancroft, chapter 34; Bilson, *On Perpetual Government*,
chapter 12, page 255; Bridges, book 11, page 898; Saravia, *Against Beza*, book
2, page 32, and book 4; Downham, page 102; Keckermann, *Politics*, book 1,
chapter 1.]

[1638 control | printed p. 47 | BSB canvas 57 | bsb10507786_00057]

You yourself seem elsewhere to acknowledge this when you represent us as
condemning certain German Churches, because they lack Bishops, not as no Churches
but only as imperfect ones. Saravia likewise reports our opinion not as denying
that there is any Church where every degree described in God’s Word is absent,
but only as denying that such a Church is complete in every respect.

Thus the alleged alliance with the Brownists vanishes, as though in Donatist
fashion we acknowledged no Church where discipline is imperfect. Do I speak
falsely? Something of discipline is visible in the English Church, and we think
it sufficient for the visible Church’s subsistence. If the Separatists noticed
this, they would say that their separation is not a consequence of our teaching.
All agree, both among the English and among every people, that there must be the
preaching of the Word, administration of the sacraments, censure of the keys, and
suitable pastors to administer these. Only part of discipline, not the whole of
discipline, is in controversy—and that disputed part concerns only the particular
manner and the persons by whom these things should be administered. In this
disputed part the English Church is indeed defective, and that in necessary
matters enjoined by necessary command; nevertheless it remains a Church, however
imperfect. Thus much for the first necessity of the end, which constitutes the
outward subsistence of the visible Church.

CHAPTER XVII.

The Second Necessity of the End: Namely, for Judging the Church.

We must now consider the necessity of polity which we defend for a second end:
judging the Church. The question is whether discipline is a necessary mark of the
visible Church. George Downham says, “We have learned from you that presbyterial
discipline is an essential mark of the true Church.” Have you learned this? From
whom? The author must be named unless you wish to be regarded as an accuser of the
brethren. Whoever among us said that discipline in general, as regards some part
of it, is essential to the visible Church’s outward existence has Downham himself
as witness and supporter. But Cartwright himself expressly denied that full and
perfect presbyterial discipline is essential to the Church, teaching against
Harrison that it contributes only to the Church’s well-being and not its
existence.

Downham says Bancroft teaches that Cartwright calls discipline shaped according
to God’s Word the Church’s third mark after Word and sacraments. Yet neither Beza
nor Cartwright calls it an essential mark. I add that Bancroft did not intend to
prove the essentiality of which Downham accuses us either from Beza or from
Cartwright. His immediately following words show it: “Observe their necessity of
discipline; now notice its essentiality.” Bancroft thus distinguishes necessity
from essentiality, proving the former from Cartwright and Beza but not the latter.

[Marginal references: Bancroft, page 362; Saravia, *Against Beza*, chapter 2,
page 126; Downham, book 1, chapter 6, page 128.]

[1638 control | printed p. 48 | BSB canvas 58 | bsb10507786_00058]

Although Matthew Sutcliffe is a very bitter opponent, he does not charge us, as
Downham does, with making our discipline an essential mark of the Church, but
only with making it inseparable. He saw what Field learnedly demonstrates: the
Church’s necessary marks are not only those which constitute it *a priori*, but
also those which follow *a posteriori* from its essence as its proper accidents.
How futile, then, is Bancroft. Intending to prove the essentiality of discipline
from our authors, he produces no reason except that some of us count discipline
as a mark of the Church together with Word and sacraments—as though whatever is
counted with the Word as a mark of the Church were thereby asserted to be equally
essential with the Word.

My lord, do not you yourselves regularly name the sacraments together with the
Word when describing the Church’s marks, although the sacraments are not
essential in precisely the same way as the Word, since the Church can exist
without them? Still worse is the inference that we make discipline essential like
the Word because we call it a mark together with the Word, since we rank it after
the sacraments. Yet Bancroft dislikes the very fact that we in any fashion add
discipline to Word and sacraments as a third mark of the Church. He especially
objects because Calvin, Bertrand de Loque, Philip Mornay, Peter Martyr, Augustine
Marlorat, Nicolas Galasius, and Beza himself enumerate only Word and sacraments
among the Church’s marks and omit discipline.

The words of Beza which Bancroft cites should be given, since they contain a
sufficient answer to the other testimonies. At the Colloquy of Poissy in 1561
Beza said: “There are two marks of the Church: the pure Word of God and the
sincere administration of the sacraments. Some add ecclesiastical discipline, but
we shall be content with those two.” What follows? These theologians do not, as
Bancroft does, condemn those who make discipline the Church’s third mark. Arguing
against the Papists, they are content to mention only Word and sacraments and
include discipline in them as their appendage. Nor do they condemn us as
Bancroft and the other Hierarchists do. In this matter Dr. Copequot is wholly
ours: in his published sermon at Paul’s Cross he expressly affirms three marks of
the Church—Word, sacraments, and discipline. Our Richard Field does likewise, and
no one has written on the Church’s marks more accurately or carefully.

[Marginal references: Sutcliffe, chapter 15; Field, *On the Church*, book 2,
chapter 1; Bancroft, chapter 34, pages 438–439; *Register*; Field, *On the
Church*, book 2, chapter 2.]

[1638 control | printed p. 49 | BSB canvas 59 | bsb10507786_00059]

Field says: “The Church’s absolutely proper marks, which are inseparable and
perpetual, are three: profession of the Word; use of the sacraments; and union in
this profession and use under lawful pastors for directing men in the ways of
salvation.” You ask what this has to do with our purpose? Everything. Field does
not simply name Word and sacraments but places their administration and use among
the marks of the Church. Are these not matters of discipline? Union too is
preserved and exercised through discipline; the suitable pastors whom he requires
for marking the Church are judged and elected through discipline; finally, their
direction in the ways of salvation is outward and disciplinary. Our opponents
therefore vainly carp at our position on the necessity of polity for identifying
the Church.

CHAPTER XVIII.

The Third Necessity of the End: Discipline Is Necessary for the Church’s
Salvation.

The third necessity of the end remains: whether discipline is necessary for the
Church’s salvation. When Thomas Cartwright affirmed it, Whitgift exclaimed that
his doctrine was wonderful, new, and unheard of, as though he wished to rouse the
whole neighborhood, indeed the entire school of theologians, against him.
Whitgift asks him two questions. First, does he mean absolute necessity, without
which no one can be saved, or qualified necessity, by which discipline is only a
necessary means and ordinary aid without which the Church’s salvation cannot be
obtained so easily? This inquiry was needless, since Cartwright plainly taught
that he meant only necessity of the second kind, both in the preceding division
of this chapter and in the second division of chapter three.

Second, Whitgift asks whether government in general is necessary, or this or that
immutable kind of government. He grants the first to be necessary in the second
kind of necessity, but denies that one definite kind of government is necessary
for the Church’s salvation even in that degree. Thomas Bilson, however, affirms
it. In the question of English discipline—which in Bilson’s judgment concerns one
definite and immutable government—he grants that the whole Church’s salvation is
at stake. George Downham affirms it too: “No necessity is absolute, such that the
episcopal function must be retained forever; nevertheless it must always be
retained by necessity of expedience and fitness.” Elsewhere he interprets this as
necessity of the end for the Church’s salvation when he presses Jerome’s words,
“The Church’s salvation consists in the dignity of the Bishop.”

[Marginal references: Field, *On the Church*, book 2, chapter 2; Whitgift,
tractate 2, chapter 1, division 2, pages 79–81; Bilson, *On Perpetual
Government*, chapter 13; Downham, page 64; *Against the Luciferians*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 50 | BSB canvas 60 | bsb10507786_00060]

Thus against Whitgift Downham teaches that one definite and immutable form of
government is necessary to the Church’s salvation, with a necessity and
immutability of fitness and expedience—though he mistakenly substitutes his
episcopal form for ours, which he calls presbyterial. Jesus Christ never
instituted his as the necessary form through which men should be directed to
salvation. Jerome is no obstacle.

First, when Jerome said that the Church’s salvation consists in the episcopal
function, he did not speak of the Church’s salvation simply but only of its
outward safety, which consists in outward peace. Second, Jerome did not support
this episcopal authority by divine right or apostolic ordinance, as Downham does,
but only by human right. Jewel says he considered it a political invention
against schisms, which does nothing for the English Bishop. Episcopal government
had become prevalent in his time and was thought a good political invention for
uprooting nurseries of schism. The contrary has occurred in our time, when it has
become a nursery of schisms. Since the reason is different, every right of that
political invention ceases too.

Third, Jerome never dreamed of the English authority in the Bishop of his day. He
approved only a presidency for preserving order. This is shown when he says Peter
was placed over the Apostles so that the occasion of schisms might be removed.
John Reynolds rightly concludes from this that a presidency such as exists among
equals suffices to preserve order, keep the Church’s peace, and cut off schisms.
Daily experience proves this everywhere in the reformed Churches. Do they not
enjoy a sweeter and richer peace under their president than England does under
its Lord Bishop or Archbishop?

Conrad Vorstius’s cry that Belgium has no power to remove heresies because it
lacks Bishops amounts to nothing. What more could a Bishop in Belgium have done
against heresies, had one existed there, than the classis without a Bishop has
hitherto been able to do—especially in a commonwealth where the magistrate judges
that freedom for mixed religions should be granted? So far was the episcopate in
former times from being useful for removing heresies in Belgium that I think
nothing was more suited to fanning and propagating them. As things now stand, who
would ascend to the episcopate sooner than you, Vorstius, and your associates,
the refurbishers of your most dangerous heresy? Even today you would not think so
much about erecting an episcopate in Belgium unless you judged it advantageous to
your affairs.

Fourth, Jerome approved a presidency in one Bishop for preserving order as
perpetual according to the custom of his age, but not as necessary—only as useful
for those times.

[Marginal references: Jewel, article 4, division 32, page 309; Jerome, *Against
Jovinian*, book 1; Reynolds, *Conference with Hart*, chapter 6, section 2.]

[1638 control | printed p. 51 | BSB canvas 61 | bsb10507786_00061]

This is what William Whitaker answers to Duræus: “Jerome defends the superiority
of one pastor in the Church not as necessary but as fitting; and in the same first
book against Jovinian which you cite, he says the Church was founded upon all the
Apostles and that every Church equally received the keys of the kingdom of heaven
from them.”

Fifth, Jerome did not judge the episcopal function useful simply, but only in one
respect, namely for suppressing schisms. Yet the road must be closed against
tyranny no less than against schism, and after Jerome’s times the episcopate
degenerateed into tyranny. He did not see this as we do. Had he seen it, he would
have labored no less than we for abolishing the Bishop. Daniel Chamier made this
point when he answered Bellarmine, who argues for one Pope just as the
Hierarchists argue for one Bishop: a single city can be ruled only by one. “Then
the Apostles must be accused, since they constituted several Bishops in the same
city. Men indeed thought one Church could be governed better and more easily by
one when the custom of one Bishop was introduced; but it does not follow that
what seems better is therefore necessary. Nor did it seem better absolutely, but
only in a particular respect, namely for avoiding schisms, as Jerome testifies.
In like manner we can say that equality among pastors is better in one respect:
for avoiding the tyranny of a few over the other brethren. Experience has at
last shown how great an evil that is and how ready an open door is provided for
it by ambition for the chief seat.”

Let this be the end of the third excellence of ecclesiastical polity—its
necessity.

CHAPTER XIX.

The Use of the Foregoing: It Is Every Church’s Duty to Institute an Exact
Reformation.

We have perceived the excellence of ecclesiastical polity: we saw its dignity,
considered its utility, and finally expounded its necessity. From these emerges
this conclusion: it is every Church’s duty to institute its exact reformation so
that it may shine with its ancestral and original splendor. All the more must we
grieve that what Gerard Lorich lamented in his age now occurs in England: there
are pseudo-Catholics who do not fear to delay the Church’s reformation by every
means. Indeed, as in Hezekiah’s time, “the Levites were readier to sanctify
themselves than the priests,” 2 Chronicles 29:34; and as in Nehemiah’s time, the
great men “did not put their necks to the work,” Nehemiah 3:5. Those who ought to
go before the rest of the brethren in England as standard-bearers delay and even
persecute those brethren. They not only withdraw their necks and abandon the work
of reformation but, as though stiffening their necks, hinder it with all their
power.

[Marginal references: Whitaker, *Against Duræus*, page 741; Chamier, *On the
Ecumenical Pontiff*, book 2, conjecture 1, page 92; Lorich, *On Deferring the
Mass*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 52 | BSB canvas 62 | bsb10507786_00062]

First, some of them argue as if, once the Gospel’s doctrine is restored, we need
not labor much over discipline. They ask: Was this not well done? In Mary’s time
this liberty would have been most welcome to you. Now that Elizabeth has restored
the Gospel itself, can you not be content? We say it was indeed well done; we
rejoice in it and give the highest thanks to divine mercy. Yet something lessens
our joy: like the elders of Judah in Ezra 3:12, we so behold the foundations of
God’s house laid that we are compelled to miss the first Church’s purity and
glory in many things.

It was not without cause that Beza, writing to great Elizabeth in 1572, warned
that discipline still remained to be restored: our temples were not exactly
cleansed, and some high places which ought to be abolished still stood. I do not
see why Bancroft attacked this admonition so strongly unless he thinks the
restoration of doctrine alone sufficient or discipline already sufficiently
restored. If he dreams either, let him hear the brilliant refutation of John
Reynolds, a man as moderate as he was learned. After mentioning Hezekiah’s
reformation, Reynolds adds that in our kingdom religion is pursued with much less
zeal, especially in matters pertaining to the offices and duties of Levites.
Therefore God must be entreated with fervent prayers and men urged by every kind
of protest, exhortation, and admonition, so that in all things pertaining to the
Church’s cleansing and reformation the most gracious Queen, the senate, all the
nobles, and the whole people may perpetually remember day and night their duty
toward Jehovah with thankful testimony of heart.

One may see that Beza warned of nothing which ought not to be warned, and found
nothing lacking in the English reformation which ought not still to be desired by
everyone. Bancroft is more burned by Beza’s statement in letter 69, written in
1572 to an English nobleman, that the Churches are not in a much better condition
because doctrine is reformed unless they also aspire to join purity of discipline
to it. George Downham, who said nothing about the former letter, attacks this
statement in Beza as not only inconsiderate but ungrateful to God. Yet Beza did
not speak as though doctrinal reformation were not a wonderful benefit of God.
His point was that without discipline’s aid this benefit cannot long be
preserved. He said only in this sense that the Churches’ condition was not much
better because doctrine was reformed: while discipline is exiled, that much
better condition cannot be maintained for very long.

But leaving Beza aside, the Hierarchists now attack us thus: Do you so disfigure
the reformation of Elizabeth, heir of Israel?

[Marginal references: On the obstacles removed by the English Reformation;
*Humble Admonition*, page 24; Beza, dedicatory epistle, 1572, and *Annotations
on the New Testament*; Reynolds, *Censure of the Apocryphal Books*; Bancroft,
chapter 3, page 55; Downham, book 1, chapter 6, page 128.]

[1638 control | printed p. 53 | BSB canvas 63 | bsb10507786_00063]

As though Zephaniah disfigured King Josiah’s reformation, far more illustrious
than ours, when he said Josiah had left certain remnants of Baal which had to be
removed, Zephaniah 1:4. Nor did Jehoshaphat believe that he would undeservedly
stain Asa’s reformation when he lifted up his heart still further to Jehovah and
purged what remained to be reformed, 2 Chronicles 17:6. Baronius says Constantine
completed what Philip began; Rufinus says Theodosius completed what Constantine
left unreformed. Yet he did not dishonor Constantine’s reformation, but by his
example led every man to strive to leave a silver Church where he found one of
brick, and, like Solomon, to build a silver palace upon a wall he found, Song of
Songs 8:9.

Since these things are so, God’s servants in England are undeservedly beaten
today under the name of reformers. Following the examples of Hezekiah, Josiah,
Malachi, and finally Jesus Christ himself, they judge that labor is required not
only to reform doctrine but also to order the courses of the Levites, amend the
priests’ corruptions, and remove profanations of the temple. Nicolas de Clamanges,
as Dr. Mornay records, judged that discipline in his time should be reformed
after Christ’s example in cleansing the temple with a scourge. Charlemagne
likewise judged that he should restore collapsed discipline after Josiah’s
example, and for this purpose assembled the Council of Mainz in 813. Charles and
Louis so watched over the reform of discipline after his example, especially in
expelling and removing pastors who either would not diligently preach the Gospel
or were wholly unable to do so, that we now need to wish with Platina: “O Louis,
would that you lived now!”

John of Rupescissa, who lived until 1350, plainly foretold that the clergy would
be cleansed; that the Church would have poor and pious priests who faithfully fed
the Lord’s flock; and that superfluous ecclesiastical goods would return to the
laity. A thorough Puritan indeed! By God’s most gracious mercy this reformation
has been restored as regards discipline in each of the reformed Churches. The
Bishops now labor to deform it in England by banishing these holy pastors,
preferring the papistical ornament placed in riches and worldly pomp, and
disturbing the joy of the saints who rejoiced to be adorned with holy pastors.

I will add no more, especially since these examples make plain that an exact
reformation of discipline has always seemed necessary to the saints beyond that
which obtains in England. Thus much for the first delay of reformation.

Prateolus, *On Councils*; *Catalogue of Witnesses*, pages 1025 and 1786.]
