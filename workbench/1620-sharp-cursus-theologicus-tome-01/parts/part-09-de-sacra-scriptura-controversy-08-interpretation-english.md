Eighth Controversy: The Interpretation of Holy Scripture

The matters pertaining to the explanation of this controversy can be reduced
to three headings. The first treats the sense of Scripture; the second, the
means of discovering Scripture's true sense, or of interpreting Scripture;
and the third, the authority to interpret the Scriptures and in whom it
resides.

I. The Sense of Scripture

Scripture consists not in bare words but in the sense—the sense and intention
of the words—which is Scripture's internal form. Jerome bears witness in his
commentary on chapter 1 of the Epistle to the Galatians: “Let us not suppose
that the gospel is in the words of the Scriptures, but in their sense; not on
the surface, but in the marrow; not in the leaves of the sayings, but in the
root of the meaning.”

Bellarmine, in book 3, chapter 3 of *The Word of God*,

[1620 Tome I | col. 83 | BSB frame 00056]

says that Scripture has two senses, one literal or historical, and the other
spiritual or mystical. He defines the literal sense as the one the words
immediately present, and holds that it is twofold: either simple, consisting
in the proper meaning of the words, or figurative, in which the words are
transferred from their natural signification to another sense, with as many
kinds as there are figures. He defines the spiritual sense as occurring when
the sense is referred to something other than what the words immediately
signify, and holds that it is threefold. 1. The allegorical, when the words of
Scripture signify beyond the literal sense something in the New Testament
pertaining to Christ or the church. 2. The tropological, when words or deeds
are referred to signify something pertaining to morals. 3. The anagogical,
when words or deeds are referred to signify eternal life. He says that the
ancients call all these senses allegories; that there is a literal sense in
every statement; that there are sometimes several senses in one statement;
and that a spiritual sense is not always found in every statement of
Scripture. In the same place he admits that they and we agree that effective
arguments must be drawn from the literal sense alone.

We concede that there are allegories, anagogies, and tropologies in the
Scriptures, but deny that these produce multiple and diverse senses of
Scripture. We therefore say that Bellarmine commits seven very serious errors.
The first error is distinguishing the literal sense from the spiritual,
because, by his own testimony, the literal sense is what the words immediately
present; and, by that same testimony, the sense drawn immediately from the
words belongs to the Holy Spirit. But the sense of the Holy Spirit in the
Scriptures is the spiritual sense. The second is his contention that Scripture
has several senses, because the one true and

[1620 Tome I | col. 84 | BSB frame 00056]

genuine sense of Scripture is what arises from the words rightly understood,
which we call literal. Allegories, tropologies, and anagogies are not diverse
senses, but diverse deductions from that one true sense, or diverse
applications of that one sense; diversity of application does not destroy the
unity of the sense. We prove this to be true as follows. 1. If Scripture had
several senses, it would also have several forms, for Scripture's sense is its
form. But one thing has only one form. 2. Bellarmine concedes that effective
arguments can be drawn from the literal sense alone, because, as he says, it
is certain that the sense drawn immediately from the words belongs to the Holy
Spirit. Although the mystical senses are diverse and edifying when they are
not contrary to faith and good morals, it is not always certain whether they
proceeded from the intention of the Holy Spirit. Hence we argue: only that
sense of Holy Scripture which the Holy Spirit intended is to be acknowledged.
Therefore, only the literal sense is to be acknowledged as Scripture's true
sense; consequently, if the allegorical sense and the rest belong to the Holy
Spirit, they are not different from the literal sense. 3. Only the sense that
belongs to the Holy Spirit is the sense of Scripture (2 Pet. 1:20). But all
other senses besides the literal arise from the human brain, and it cannot be
established whether they belong to the Holy Spirit. Therefore. 4. Because of
its one simple meaning, Scripture is called pure, purified, and so forth (Ps.
12:7; 119:140; Prov. 30:5). Therefore. 5. Unless Scripture had one definite
sense, it would teach nothing but would always leave the hearer in doubt.

Bellarmine's third error lies in defining the literal sense as what the words
immediately present. In this way he confuses proper words with improper and
allegorical words, for improper words do not immediately present the literal
sense, but do so mediately insofar as they are re—

[1620 Tome I | col. 85 | BSB frame 00057]

—ferred to proper words. For example, Psalm 91:13 says, “You will walk upon the
asp and basilisk, and trample the lion and dragon.” If the literal sense is
what the words immediately present, let Bellarmine show the lion, asp, or
basilisk upon which Christ walked. Therefore, either the literal sense is not
what the words immediately present, or these words have no literal sense. The
literal sense, therefore, is what arises from words taken either properly or
figuratively, or what terms taken either properly or figuratively present.
Thus Alfonso de Castro says in book 1, chapter 3 of *Against Heresies*: “The
literal sense is what either the terms or the things expressed by the terms
signify.” Accordingly, the sense of the saying “The woman's offspring will
crush the serpent's head” is that Christ will conquer Satan and break his
power, even though the devil is neither a serpent nor has a head. Hence Thomas
rightly says: “The parabolic sense is contained under the literal, for by
words something is signified properly and something figuratively. The literal
sense is not the figure itself, but what is figured. For when Scripture speaks
of God's arm, the literal sense is not that there is such a limb in God, but
what is signified by this limb, namely power or operative strength.”

The fourth error is his saying that the allegorical sense is another and new
sense beyond the literal; it is only an application of the literal sense.
Moreover, allegory is a sustained metaphor, but metaphor is not a spiritual
sense; it is literal. The complete literal sense does not lie in the words
taken properly, but partly in the type and partly in the thing itself; only
when the two are joined is the sense complete. Although we can undoubtedly
use allegories often and fruitfully according to Scripture, we must be sparing
in devising them, since allegories do not constitute a new sense. For example,
David was a type of Christ, and Goliath

[1620 Tome I | col. 86 | BSB frame 00057]

of the devil; their battle and David's victory signify Christ's battle and
victory over the devil. Thus David overcame Goliath; therefore, the faithful
will overcome every affliction. Yet this is not a new sense, but only an
application of that history.

Bellarmine's fifth error is saying that the allegorical sense signifies
something in the New Testament, as though there were no allegories in the New
Testament but only in the Old. Yet it is evident to anyone that the New
Testament has many allegories. The sixth error is confusing Scripture's sense
with its application, not only in allegories but also in tropology. Tropology
is an effective transfer of Scripture in which we gather from the Scriptures
something useful for forming morals and ordering life, yet no new sense arises
from it. For example, Abraham overcame five kings with a very small band.
Therefore, one must not trust in a multitude of soldiers, victory does not
depend upon numbers, and one must not despair because of fewness. Likewise,
David committed adultery while idle; therefore, idleness must be avoided.
Likewise, Peter denied Christ in the high priest's courtyard and went out and
repented. Therefore, companionship with the wicked must be avoided, and
repentance must be performed for sins. See other examples in 1 Corinthians
9:9; 1 Timothy 5:18; and Matthew 12:41–42.

Bellarmine's seventh error is distinguishing the allegorical sense from the
anagogical, since anagogy is a species of allegory by which the glory of
eternal life is signified through things known and accessible to the senses.
Thus in Revelation 21:1 and following, the glory of eternal life is depicted
by the likeness of a most beautiful city. Likewise, Psalm 95:7, at the end,
has these words: “I swore in my wrath that they shall not enter into my rest.”
Here “rest” indeed signifies the land of Canaan, but anagogically eternal
life, because the land of Canaan was a type of eternal life. Yet this is not a
twofold sense, but only an anagogical application

[1620 Tome I | col. 87 | BSB frame 00058]

by which the sign is referred to the thing signified, and thus what lay hidden
in the sign is expressed more openly. When we speak only of the sign by
itself, we express only part of the sense; but since there is a mutual relation
between the sign and the thing figured, situated in their similarity and
correspondence, then the entire and complete sense is set forth. Thus the
sense of 1 Corinthians 10:11, “All these things happened to them typically,”
is that we ought to apply to our instruction what happened to the Jewish
people: they sinned and were punished; therefore, if we sin, we will suffer
punishment. Manna fed them in the wilderness; so Christ refreshes or feeds us.
Water flowing from the rock refreshed them; so Christ's blood refreshes us,
and so forth. Yet this is not a new sense. Rather, by comparing the sign with
the thing signified, we reason this way and thus bring forth the complete and
entire sense of the words.

From this we may therefore rightly conclude: 1. Scripture has one simple
sense, the literal. Although many things follow from Scripture's words rightly
understood, there are not many senses, but rather corollaries or consequences
flowing from the words rightly understood. For the sense of Scripture is
ultimately what the Holy Spirit intended, as Bellarmine himself concedes. But
the Holy Spirit intended the allegorical, tropological, and anagogical sense.
Therefore, if they are senses of Scripture, they are not different from the
literal. 2. Bellarmine agrees that firm arguments can be drawn from the literal
sense alone. But a firm argument can be drawn from Scripture's proper and
genuine sense. Therefore, tropology, allegory, and anagogy, if they are true
and genuine senses, are literal.

3. No senses of Scripture are to be asserted or admitted unless they agree
with the intention and declaration of the Holy Spirit. Since he alone is the
author of Scripture, his interpretation alone must be—

[1620 Tome I | col. 88 | BSB frame 00058]

—heard (2 Pet. 1:20).

The Means of Discovering the True Sense of Scripture, or the Means of
Interpretation

For discovering Scripture's true sense and interpreting it, rules of three
kinds are necessary. Some precede interpretation itself; some must be observed
in interpretation itself; and some follow it. Unless they are observed, we
cannot avoid error in interpretation, since a corresponding number of
impediments in us stand opposed to them.

There are six rules of the first kind. 1. Prayers are required before all
else, because of Christ's commands and promises (Matt. 7:7–8; Jas. 1:5), so
that the Holy Spirit may illuminate our minds, since without his illumination
we cannot understand the Scriptures (John 20:21–22; 1 Cor. 2:12). They are
understood through the same Spirit by whom they were dictated (2 Pet. 1:20).
Jerome therefore rightly said to Laeta, “Reading follows prayer, and prayer
follows reading.”

2. We must renounce all our own prudence and human reasonings, listen to God
alone teaching in the Scriptures, and bend every thought of ours into
obedience to Christ. Nothing hinders progress in sacred things more than a
vain opinion of one's own wisdom (Matt. 11:25; Rom. 8:6–7; 1 Cor. 3:18–19).

3. We must approach the Scriptures without prejudice conceived either from
our own brain or from the teaching of others, but with a candid, pure mind
eager to learn; prejudice is the greatest impediment to progress. Therefore,
Christ commanded his followers to beware of the leaven of the Pharisees. See
Colossians 2:7–8.

4. We must approach with a mind purified by faith and repentance, in which
there is zeal for true piety (Mark 1:15). For the Spirit of Christ, who
illuminates minds, does not dwell in the impenitent and profane (2 Tim. 3:7).

[1620 Tome I | col. 89 | BSB frame 00059]

5. We must approach with reverence and love of the truth, and with a desire to
recognize and retain it. Hence those who approach the study of Scripture only
from custom do not attain the intention of the Holy Spirit (2 Thess. 2:10–11).

6. We must approach with the intention not only of recognizing God's will but
also of doing it, because the end of Scripture is not bare knowledge, but
practice as well (Titus 2:12).

The following are eleven rules of the second kind.

1. We must understand the very words the Holy Spirit used in the Scriptures.
The original languages must therefore be known, and we must not remain at the
little streams, because from the original languages Scripture's true sense is
gathered, its greatest emphasis is perceived, without them we often go astray,
and from them many errors are refuted.

2. Each word must be carefully examined so that we distinguish figurative
words from proper ones and recognize their more customary and more proper
signification. Things stated figuratively must not be interpreted properly,
and vice versa.

3. The primary meaning, end, and purpose of an entire passage must be
considered. It must be gathered both from what precedes and from what follows.
As Chrysostom says on Psalm 2, “Just as a structure without a foundation is
weak, so Scripture is useless unless its purpose is discovered.”

4. All the circumstances of the passage at hand—such as place, time, persons,
and so forth—must be weighed, as must what precedes and follows. If a word has
many meanings,

[1620 Tome I | col. 90 | BSB frame 00059]

the circumstances of the passage must show which signification is more
suitable. Augustine therefore rightly says in sermon 2 *On the Words of the
Lord*: “The words of the gospel carry their own expositions with them.” If
these things are done, it will not be difficult to refute many errors and to
render open and clear what at first sight appears obscure.

5. One passage must be compared with another, namely the more obscure with
the clearer.

6. In comparing passages, not only similar passages must be compared with
similar ones, but dissimilar ones with dissimilar ones as well.

7. Scriptural expressions must be noticed, especially the Hebraisms which
occur everywhere in the New Testament, and their sense must be drawn from the
Hebrew sources themselves.

8. Allegories must be expounded not according to anyone's arbitrary judgment,
but from the circumstances of the passages in which they occur and from other
passages of Scripture.

9. Every interpretation of Scripture must be tested against the analogy of
faith, so that it is analogous to the context and the articles of faith (Rom.
12:6). The analogy of faith is nothing other than Scripture's constant and
perpetual meaning in its clear and least obscure passages, such as the
articles of faith contained in the creed and the things contained in the
Lord's Prayer, the Decalogue, and the whole catechism.

An exposition that conflicts with this is false. Chrysostom therefore rightly
says in homily 4 on Matthew that Scripture is like a chain, since it is wholly
consistent with itself and everything within it fits together most suitably.
Augustine, in book 15, chapter 7 of *The City of God*, teaches that Scripture
must not only be expounded according to the rule of faith, but also forbids
expounding any passage so that it conflicts with other, clearer passages.

10. In interpreting the Scriptures,

[1620 Tome I | col. 91 | BSB frame 00060]

we must keep within their own bounds, both the bounds of things and of words
(Deut. 4:5; 1 Tim. 6:3, 20). The papists sin most greatly in this, for they
borrow terms and things from the pagans themselves.

11. In interpreting the Scriptures, we should consult the better and more
sincere interpreters (Acts 8:31; Rom. 10:15). They must be read with judgment,
since they are human beings and can therefore err. Consequently, their
interpretations are to be embraced only insofar as they agree with the analogy
of faith (1 Thess. 5:20–21).

There are three rules of the third kind, which must be employed after the
sense of Scripture has been understood.

1. Thanksgiving must be offered, arising from the greatness of the benefit,
namely the knowledge of the divine will and heavenly mysteries. Because of
ingratitude, God either deprives many of the knowledge already given or at
least does not increase it.

2. The word understood must be meditated upon, ruminated upon, and fixed in
the memory (Ps. 1:2; 1 Tim. 4:15); otherwise, all that knowledge will vanish.

3. Piety must be pursued, and what we read and understand must be applied to
our use, whether they are precepts, prohibitions, exhortations, consolations,
and so forth. By them faith must be strengthened and life rightly ordered,
since this is Scripture's end (Rom. 4:23; 1 Cor. 10:11; 2 Tim. 3:16). Those
who act otherwise fall away from the truth by God's just judgment. Therefore,
Amos 8 says that God will send a famine not of bread, but of the word. See
Romans 1:18 and 1 Timothy 1:19. Aristotle also rightly says in the *Ethics*
that knowledge is destroyed by the corruption of morals.

The papists maintain that there are only four certain and authentic means of
interpreting Scripture. First, the rule

[1620 Tome I | col. 92 | BSB frame 00060]

of faith. We offer no objection if they understand this to mean Scripture or a
summary of Scripture, as was said above. But by this rule they mean written
and unwritten traditions approved by the pope. 2. The church's practice as
observed by the pope. 3. The agreeing interpretation of the fathers which the
pope follows. 4. The interpretation of councils which the pope likewise
confirms. Thus every interpretation of Scripture is ultimately lodged with
the pope.

The Interpreter of Holy Scripture and the Judge of Controversies

Bellarmine, in book 3, chapter 3 of *The Word of God*, admits that they and we
agree that the Scriptures must be understood by the Spirit through whom they
were made, that is, the Holy Spirit. He says that the entire question concerns
where that Spirit is. He says he thinks that although this Spirit is often
granted to many private persons, he is nevertheless certainly found in the
church—that is, in a council of bishops confirmed by the church's supreme
pastor, or in the supreme pastor together with a council of the other pastors.
He then asserts that the judge of Scripture's true sense and of all
controversies is the church, namely the pontiff together with a council. Thus
the Council of Trent, session 4.

He further reduces the papist position to three headings. First, he says that
Scripture's sense is found in the fathers when they agree. 2. If they do not
agree, he wants us to seek Scripture's sense in a council confirmed by the
supreme pastor, that is, the pope. 3. If doubt still remains, he wants
Scripture's sense to be sought from the supreme pastor together with his
ordinary council.

Alfonso, in book 1, chapter 4 of *Against Heresies*, says that a council alone
has this supreme authority, because a council alone is the representative
church. In chapter

[1620 Tome I | col. 93 | BSB frame 00061]

8 he says that the Apostolic See holds the next place after a general council
in making definitions. He says that the Apostolic See includes not only the
supreme pontiff but also the council he employs, and that this council assists
him in defining; he means the council of cardinals. Thus on their position the
supreme interpreter of Scripture and judge of controversies is the pope, who
is indeed assisted by a council, yet in such a way that he alone can define
every controversy.

We, however, say first that four things are required in a judge of
controversies. 1. He must be incapable of error, and it must be perfectly
clear to us that he cannot err. For all the dogmas of theology are immutably
true, but they are constructed from true interpretations. Therefore, both the
interpretations and the judge of interpretations must be immutably true.
Furthermore, the interpretation must be of the same character as the text.
But the text is immutably true. Therefore, the interpretation must be so as
well. It follows that a judge capable of presenting such an interpretation is
required. But only the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture is such a judge, not
the pope. Therefore.

2. The supreme judge must be one from whom no appeal can be made. But only
the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture is such a judge, not the pope.
Therefore.

3. This judge must not be partial, and much more importantly, he must not be a
judge in his own case. The Holy Spirit can truly be said to be such, but not
the pope, because the question concerns him and his doctrine. Therefore.

4. He must be able to compel the parties to obedience. But this obedience is
spiritual and situated in the will, and no one except God can compel the will.
Therefore.

Second, we say that the judge of controversies and interpretation is either

[1620 Tome I | col. 94 | BSB frame 00061]

supreme, who is God alone, or subordinate. There are two kinds of subordinate
judges. The first are those called immediately, such as the prophets, apostles,
and so forth, who had divine inspiration as the rule of judgment and
interpretation. They therefore did not err in judging and interpreting. Their
judgment was not true merely because they judged in that way, but because they
judged according to divine inspiration.

The second are those called mediately. They are either public, such as the
church's pastors and teachers, whether outside or in a council, or private
believers, who ought to search the Scriptures and examine interpretations and
judgments soberly and from a desire to advance, as the Bereans did in Acts 17.
They all have Scripture itself alone as their judge. Their judgment is not
true merely because they judge in that way, but because they judge according
to the written word; otherwise, they can err in judgment.

The orthodox position, therefore, is this: the supreme right, authority, and
judgment to interpret the Scriptures resides with the Holy Spirit speaking in
Scripture. The interpretation of Scripture and supreme judgment of
controversies are not bound to some particular seat and succession of human
beings, as the papists maintain, but must be ascribed to the Holy Spirit who
secretly confirms the Scriptures in believers' hearts. There is no conflict
when we say that this resides with Scripture and with the Holy Spirit. The
Holy Spirit is called the supreme interpreter because, in order for us to be
certainly persuaded of Scripture's true sense, we must be illuminated by the
Holy Spirit. Otherwise, whatever means we use, we will never attain that full
assurance which resides in believers' minds. This is the internal persuasion
which reaches only us, for in this way we do not

[1620 Tome I | col. 95 | BSB frame 00062]

compel others to receive the sense and judgment of the Holy Spirit. Scripture
itself, or the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture, is called the supreme
interpreter and judge with respect to external persuasion. Therefore, to
persuade others, we must come to this external judgment of Scripture.

The state of the controversy, therefore, is this: does the authority to
interpret the Scriptures and the supreme judgment of controversies reside
with the church—that is, the pope together with a council—or rather with the
Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture? The papists affirm the former and deny the
latter. We deny the former and affirm the latter, moved by the following
arguments.

Argument 1. If Scripture is not known to be Scripture except through itself
and through the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit in individual believers,
then the supreme judgment and supreme authority to interpret Scripture and
judge controversies reside with Scripture alone and the Holy Spirit. But the
former was proved in the sixth controversy. Therefore.

Argument 2. Whatever alone has the power to produce faith alone has the
supreme authority to interpret the Scriptures and adjudicate every controversy
of faith. But Scripture alone together with the Holy Spirit has this power.
Therefore, this supreme authority must be ascribed to them alone. The
proposition is proved from Romans 10:17, for faith arises from Scripture's
sense rightly perceived. But Scripture's sense must be drawn from Scripture
alone; indeed, it is Scripture itself, and the perception of the true sense
comes through the illumination of the Holy Spirit. The assumption is proved
because the Holy Spirit alone can infuse faith. Hence the scholastics call
faith “infused.” The church, however, cannot infuse faith; the faith we have
from the church is called “acquired,” which by the papists' own testimony does
not suffice for certain persuasion.

[1620 Tome I | col. 96 | BSB frame 00062]

Indeed, faith comes from the church only materially. Therefore.

Argument 3. If the Scriptures must be interpreted and understood by the same
Spirit through whom they were written, everyone who wishes to interpret or
understand them must consult the Holy Spirit and depend upon his persuasion.
Bellarmine concedes the former in the cited passage, although the papists
falsely maintain that this Spirit resides in the pope, when he resides in each
godly person (1 Cor. 2:15; 1 John 2:20, 27; Isa. 54:13; John 6:45; Jer.
31:33–34; Luke 10:21; 22:6). Bernard therefore rightly says in his sermon to
the Fathers of the Mountain: “You will never enter Paul's sense unless you
drink in Paul's spirit.” Jerome likewise says in his commentary on Galatians
1: “Anyone who understands Scripture otherwise than the sense of the Holy
Spirit by whom it was written demands can be called a heretic.” Therefore.

Argument 4. The rule for interpreting Scripture and judging controversies
must always be the same, certain, and firm. But the agreeing interpretation of
the fathers approved by a council and the pope—which the papists nevertheless
maintain is the rule of interpretation and of judgment concerning
controversies—was not always a firm rule for interpreting the Scriptures,
because at one time their interpretations did not exist; nor do they agree
among themselves, and some do not even agree with themselves. Therefore, this
is not the supreme rule of interpretation and judgment.

They will not say that there was no rule at that time. If, therefore, the rule
that then existed has been changed and the rule they now press has been
substituted in its place, let them teach when, by whom, how, why, and so forth.
But they cannot do so. Therefore.

Argument 5. Scripture's authority is greater than the church's. Therefore,
Scripture rather than the church must be acknowledged as supreme judge in
judgment. The consequence is proved because supreme judgment together with

[1620 Tome I | col. 97 | BSB frame 00063]

supreme authority are necessarily joined. The antecedent was proved above in
the fifth controversy. Furthermore, the present church must not be given
greater authority than the church in the apostles' time. But that church did
not have greater authority than Scripture; indeed, it could not interpret
Scripture except according to Scripture (Gal. 1:8; Rom. 12:6). Therefore.

Argument 6. If the apostles' doctrine was examined and judged from Scripture,
much more must the doctrine of the present church be examined and judged
according to Scripture. But the former occurs in Acts 17:11. Therefore.

Argument 7. If we are commanded to examine every doctrine and spirit and,
after examination, to retain true doctrine, then the interpretation and
judgment of a council or pope must be examined. If they must be examined, they
must be examined from Scripture. Therefore, they are inferior to Scripture.
But the former is taught in Matthew 7:15, 1 Thessalonians 5:21, and 1 John 4:1.
Therefore.

Argument 8. If the fathers, councils, and pope have this supreme authority,
then our faith is ultimately resolved into their judgment, for our faith
ultimately rests in what provides the most certain sense of Scripture and
judges every controversy most truly. But the latter is absurd. Indeed, these
things can properly be said only of the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scriptures.
Therefore.

Argument 9. He alone who established a law has supreme authority to interpret
it and judge according to it. But God alone established the law or rule of our
faith, that is, Scripture. Therefore. The proposition is manifest in itself.
Thus the apostles confirmed their doctrine from the authority of the Old
Testament, and Nehemiah is said in Nehemiah 8:9 to have given understanding of
Scripture through Scripture. Therefore, Scripture is its own interpreter, and
the Holy—

[1620 Tome I | col. 98 | BSB frame 00063]

—Spirit illustrates and expounds himself in Scripture.

Argument 10. If Scripture does not interpret itself or judge controversies,
this is either because it is obscure or because it is imperfect. But the sixth
controversy proved that it is not obscure in matters pertaining to judgment
concerning controversies of faith, and the next controversy will prove that
it is not imperfect. Therefore.

Testimonies

Irenaeus says in book 4, chapter 63 of *Against Heresies*: “The legitimate and
least dangerous exposition of Scripture is according to the Scriptures
themselves.”

Hilary says in book 1 of *On the Trinity*: “The best reader is one who waits
for the understanding of the statements from the statements themselves rather
than imposing it, and who takes back rather than brings forward; he does not
force the statements to appear to contain what he presumed must be understood
before reading. Therefore, when there is discourse about the things of God,
let us grant to God knowledge of himself and serve his sayings with pious
veneration. For he who is not known except through himself is a suitable
witness to himself.”

Augustine says in book 2, chapter 21 of *Against Cresconius the Grammarian*:
“The church ought not to place itself before Christ, because he always judges
truly, whereas ecclesiastical judges, being human, are often deceived.” The
same author says in book 2, chapter 33 of *Marriage and Concupiscence*: “This
controversy requires a judge; let Christ judge.” And shortly afterward: “Let
the apostle judge with him, because Christ himself speaks in the apostle.” The
same author says in sermon 29 *On the Words of the Lord*: “In this controversy
let us come to a judge. Let neither of us presume to give a sentence for his
own side. What judge will we find? After considering everything, I do not know
whether I can find a better judge than the man through whom God speaks. Let us
not go in this controversy to secular literature; let not a poet judge between
us, but a prophet.” And shortly afterward: “A voice sounded from heaven:

[1620 Tome I | col. 99 | BSB frame 00064]

‘We have the more certain prophetic word’ [1 Pet. 1:19].” And afterward: “When
a controversy has arisen, let us come to a judge; let the prophet judge—indeed,
let God judge through the prophet, and let us both be silent.”

Basil says in epistle 80: “Let us stand by the judgment of divinely inspired
Scripture, and let the truth of the position be entirely awarded to those
whose doctrines are found consonant with the divine oracles.”

OBJECTIONS

Objection 1.

Exodus 18:13, 26. When God's people began to be reduced to a certain form of
ecclesiastical commonwealth, Moses sat as the ruler and head of that church
and answered every question that arose concerning the law; he did not refer
people to a private spirit. Therefore, by like reasoning there should now be
one common tribunal in the church and some supreme judge from whom everyone
must seek interpretations of the divine law and judgment in controversies,
and in whom all must simply rest.

Response. The inference is denied, because not everything that existed in the
Old Testament must have a place in the New. Indeed, the consequence rests upon
this false proposition: whatever Moses was under the Old Testament, the pope
is under the New. 2. Moses' authority was extraordinary (Deut. 34:20).
Therefore, it cannot be applied to establish the supreme ordinary authority
the pope arrogates to himself. 3. We insist upon the supreme tribunal of the
Holy Spirit in whom all must simply rest, yet at the same time we acknowledge
other inferior judges and public interpreters. We therefore do not refer
people to a private spirit, as the papists slanderously allege. 4. This func—

[1620 Tome I | col. 100 | BSB frame 00064]

—tion must be ascribed to Aaron rather than Moses, because Aaron, as an
ordinary priest, had a successor in the priesthood, but Moses did not. After
Aaron's consecration, Moses did not exercise the priesthood, but the prophetic
office and political rule. From the cited passage, therefore, only this would
follow: supreme judgment in matters of faith belongs to political rulers. Yet
our adversaries will not say this.

First insistence. When Moses appointed lesser judges according to his
father-in-law's counsel, he always reserved doubts concerning religion to
himself. Therefore.

Response. Those judges and the cases they handled were only civil.
Furthermore, Moses did not judge doubts concerning religion as supreme judge,
but as a prophet and interpreter of the law, and as God's intermediary to the
people and the people's intermediary to God (Exod. 18:13; 19:7–8).

Second insistence. Moses was a supreme pontiff greater than Aaron. Although he
was not an ordinary pontiff to whom someone would succeed, but an extraordinary
one, so also in the New Testament all the apostles were indeed equal to Peter
in ecclesiastical power, yet with this distinction: Peter was the ordinary
pastor of the whole church, to whom alone there was to be succession; the
others were extraordinary, and there was to be no succession to them in that
power.

Response. 1. Moses may have been an extraordinary priest before Aaron's
consecration, but afterward he did not exercise the priesthood or perform the
priestly functions Bellarmine discusses. Furthermore, all the apostles were
equal in power without any distinction between Peter and the others. It is
therefore false that Peter had another office in addition to the extraordinary
apostolate, for they all had the same command and calling (Matt. 28). If Peter
had any ordinary of—

[1620 Tome I | col. 101 | BSB frame 00065]

—fice, it was to be the apostle of the Jews, not the Gentiles (Gal. 2). It
would follow that he was the supreme priest of the Jews, not of Christians
from the Gentiles.

3. The apostolate was an extraordinary office in which, properly speaking,
there is no succession. If there are successors of the apostles, they are all
bishops without distinction. Jerome bears witness in epistle 85 when he says,
“All bishops are successors of the apostles.” Cyprian teaches in epistle 65
and his book *On the Singleness of Prelates* that Peter had no such
prerogative: “The other apostles certainly were what Peter was, endowed with
an equal fellowship both of honor and of power.”

4. If the analogy between Moses and Peter holds, why were not the apostles,
who were merely extraordinary pastors, superior to Peter the ordinary pastor,
just as Moses the extraordinary priest was superior to Aaron? Finally, if
Moses was supreme priest together with Aaron, then there were two supreme
priests at the same time, and yet one supreme priest was greater than the
other. This is absurd. Therefore.

Third insistence. Moses is called a priest in Psalm 99:6. Therefore.

Response. The Hebrew term signifies leading men of the people. Thus David's
sons are so called in 2 Samuel 8:18, although they were not priests.

2. Moses is called a priest because he performed the priesthood before
Aaron's consecration.

3. As supreme priest, Moses was a type of Christ, not the pope. Therefore,
even if we conceded that Moses was the supreme judge of Scripture, it would
follow only that Christ, whom he represented, is supreme judge. This is what
we insist, since the types ceased after Christ's coming. The saying that an
argument from types is invalid is also commonplace. These things therefore do
nothing to establish the pope's authority, since there is no similarity
between Moses and the—

[1620 Tome I | col. 102 | BSB frame 00065]

—pope. Moses was a prophet immediately called by God, adorned with
extraordinary gifts, possessing express commands of God for what he did,
performing miracles, and so forth. But nothing of this kind is established
about the pope. Finally, Moses was the supreme civil judge, but they will not
say that the pope is such among Christians.

Objection 2. Deuteronomy 17:8–9 and following. Those in doubt are not referred
to the Holy Spirit, but to a living judge, that is, the supreme priest. The
same must be done in the New Testament.

Response. 1. We concede that the sentence of our supreme judge must be obeyed,
but the papists ineptly understand this judge to be the pope.

2. The inference is denied, because not everything that was valid under the
Old Testament must be done now.

3. They slander us when they say that we refer all judgments and
interpretations to private judges.

4. The cited passage does not speak of one individual or only of the supreme
priest, but of other priests and even Levites (verse 9).

5. In the same passage, supreme judgment is not ascribed to the priest, but to
God speaking in the law, for it says, “You shall do what they teach you
according to the law.” Thus also today, pastors speaking according to God's
law, whether separately or jointly, must be heard, but not otherwise. For God
did not appoint either the priests of old or pastors today as absolute judges
with power to decree anything without a foundation in Scripture (Mal. 2:7;
Matt. 13:2–3). Finally, the cited passage speaks no less about the civil judge
than the ecclesiastical.

First insistence. The words “and they shall teach you according to the law”
occur only in the Vulgate edition.

Response. Here Bellarmine accuses the Vulgate edition of error. Furthermore,
these words

[1620 Tome I | col. 103 | BSB frame 00066]

occur in the edition the papists regard as authentic. Finally, Bellarmine's
exception is false, for the Hebrew text also has “and he shall teach you
according to the law,” where the priest is established as the mouth of the
law.

Second insistence. These words express not a condition, but an assertion or
promise, namely that the judge will always judge rightly. For he did not mean,
“Obey the priest's judgment if he teaches you according to the law.” People
would then have been in greater doubt than before, and there would have been
no need to go to the priest if they could judge their case for themselves from
the law of the Lord. Indeed, the priest would not then have been judge, but
they themselves.

Response. 1. The antecedent is denied, because the words contain a condition.
Furthermore, if it were a promise, it would also pertain to the civil
magistrate, since the passage speaks about him as well. Yet the papists concede
that he can err.

2. They are referred to the priest only in doubtful cases, about which it was
not lawful to judge outside and contrary to the law or to abide by the judges'
sentences. Furthermore, history establishes that supreme priests and others
often erred in matters of faith. Yet it does not follow that they were not
judges or that people should not have rested in their sentences when they
judged according to the law.

Third insistence. That judge is only ecclesiastical, not political. Therefore.

Response. This is false, because the passage speaks of controversies between
blood and blood, and cause and cause, which are civil questions. Furthermore,
the text distinguishes civil judges from ecclesiastical ones, for it says,
“Go up to the priest and the judge.”

Objection 3. Ecclesiastes 12:8, 13 says: “The words of the wise are like goads
and nails driven high, which are given through the council of teachers by one
shepherd; beyond them, my son, seek nothing.” Here Solo—

[1620 Tome I | col. 104 | BSB frame 00066]

—mon teaches that one must go no farther but rest entirely when a sentence has
been given by the supreme pastor, especially with the counsel of the wise
joined to him. If these things are said of the priest of the Old Testament,
how much more of the priest of the New, who received far greater promises?

Response. 1. The inference is denied, because the priest of the Old Testament
did many things which ministers of the New Testament cannot and ought not do.

2. Those words must not be understood of the supreme priest of the Old
Testament. Yet even if we conceded that they mean the priest of the Old
Testament, who signified the supreme priest of the New, this one shepherd is
Christ alone, besides whom there is no other priest of the New Testament. Let
them produce documents proving that God or Christ promised anything to the
pope. Finally, the sense of those words is this: the heavenly doctrine
delivered by God, which the prophets wrote and ministers teach, is like goads
because it pricks, stimulates, urges, and thus shakes off laziness. It is also
like nails because it keeps us firm and fixed in piety, so that nothing
further is to be sought in faith. Thus this one shepherd is God, as Jerome
teaches against the Manichaeans, who posited one author of the Old Testament
and another of the New; or it is Christ or the Holy Spirit himself, as other
teachers, even papists, have asserted.

Objection 4. Haggai 2:11 says, “Ask the priests about the law.” Therefore,
private persons do not judge concerning the meaning of the law.

Response. 1. This does not establish the matter in question, for the question
concerns the supreme judge, and no one says that private persons are supreme
judges.

2. The inference is denied, because the judgment of priests does not abolish
the judgment Scripture ascribes to private persons.

3. We

[1620 Tome I | col. 105 | BSB frame 00067]

concede that just as the Old Testament priests were formerly to be questioned
about the law, so pastors and teachers today are to be questioned and must
answer what is asked. Yet they must not answer according to their own judgment,
but according to the law, since ministry belonged to the priests, while
supreme authority belonged to the law.

4. The passage speaks of priests in common, not of one supreme priest. It
therefore does not support the pope's supreme authority.

5. This is a fallacy of what is said in a qualified sense and of a false
cause. God does not send the prophet to the priests to be instructed by them,
but to convict and expose the hypocrisy of the priests and people by the
priests' own confession. Finally, the priests often erred concerning the law
under the Old Testament, as Matthew 5 and 6 and other passages teach.

Objection 5. Malachi 2:7 says, “The priest's lips shall guard knowledge, and
they shall seek the law from his mouth, because he is called the angel of
Jehovah.”

Response. 1. This does not establish the matter in question.

2. The inference is denied, because these words teach what priests ought to
be, not what they always will be. They contain a command about the duty of
priests and the faithful people, not a promise that they will not err in
judgment, for it immediately follows, “But you have turned aside from the
way,” and so forth. Thus in Hosea 4:6 and following, God accuses the priests.

3. It does not follow that because the priests must guard the law, they are
supreme judges. The contrary follows instead: the law, whose ministers and
custodians they are and which they must have in their mouth, is the supreme
judge. The priest answers not from himself or with absolute authority, but
with limited authority, as an angel—that is, a messenger and herald. Yet what
does this have to do with the pope, who wants us to depend upon his mouth?

Objection 6. In 2 Chronicles 19:10–11, King Jehoshaphat distinguishes the
pontiff's of—

[1620 Tome I | col. 106 | BSB frame 00067]

—fice from the king's and gives judgment concerning doubtful matters of the
law to the pontiff alone. Therefore.

Response. Civil and ecclesiastical offices must be distinguished, and civil
judges must decide civil matters while ecclesiastical judges decide
ecclesiastical matters—the former according to civil laws and the latter
according to the written word. Thus the supreme right resides with the Holy
Spirit speaking in Scripture. Therefore, the matter in question is not
established.

Objection 7. They argue from Matthew 16:19: the one to whom the keys and power
of binding and loosing were given is supreme judge and interpreter in the
church. The keys mean not only power to release from sins, but also from every
other bond and impediment, unless which are removed no one can enter the
kingdom of heaven. For the promise is general, and it does not say “whomever
you loose,” but “whatever,” so that we understand that every knot can be
loosed: those of laws, by dispensing with them; those of sins and penalties,
by remitting them; and those of doctrines and controversies, by explaining
them. But the keys were given to Peter and his successors. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because the keys signify the entire
ministry, which consists in preaching the gospel and administering the
sacraments and discipline. Through these the kingdom of heaven is opened to
believers and closed to unbelievers.

2. The proof of the proposition errs in several ways.

1. Bellarmine says that supreme power of judgment was given under the name of
the keys, when only the ministry of remitting sins and explaining the
doctrines and controversies of religion was given.

2. He asserts that power was given to loose not only sins, but also every
bond, unless this is skillfully explained, which Bellarmine does not do. For
we concede that Christ speaks about sins, as is evident from Matthew 18 and
John 20:23; but let him prove that Christ speaks about other

[1620 Tome I | col. 107 | BSB frame 00068]

bonds.

3. He asserts that those to whom the keys were given can dispense with laws
established by God and penalties inflicted by God.

4. He ineptly distinguishes here between “whomever” and “whatever,” because
Christ speaks only about sinners being loosed or bound on account of sins, as
is evident from the cited passages.

Response 3. Peter indeed had ministerial power to bind and loose, but not
anything in any way whatever, because he could teach only what had been
commanded and dispense it rightly (Matt. 18:20; Acts 2:4; Gal. 1:8). 4. The
power of the keys was given not only to Peter but also to the remaining
apostles. For it is not said exclusively of Peter, “I will give to you alone,”
but inclusively of the other apostles as well, who held this power in common
with Peter.

5. By Peter's successors understand either the pope alone or all bishops
together. If the former, it is denied, because the pope performs neither
Peter's teaching nor his works. If the latter, he means either each and every
bishop since Peter's time, or only those bishops who followed Peter's teaching
and works. If he means all without distinction, it is denied. Ambrose rightly
says in book 1, chapter 6 of *On Repentance*: “He who does not have Peter's
faith does not have Peter's inheritance.” Jerome says in his epistle to
Heliodorus: “Those who occupy the places of the saints are not children of the
saints, but those who perform the works of the saints.” 6. The keys were not
given in this passage, but only promised, for he says “I will give,” not “I
give.” We read that they were given to all in Matthew 28:18–19 and John 20:21
and following. 7. Peter had no successor in his extraordinary office, much
less the pope. If he did, the other apostles also had other successors who

[1620 Tome I | col. 108 | BSB frame 00068]

received the same authority, and thus there would not be one supreme judge of
controversies on earth, but several. Finally, Augustine says in tractate 124
on John that Peter signified the universal church when the keys were given to
him. Therefore, this power was given to the whole church, not to Peter alone.

Objection 8. Matthew 18:17 says, “If he does not hear the church, let him be to
you as a heathen and a tax collector.” Here, they say, he speaks of injuries
one person suffers from another, and, by an argument from the greater, even of
injuries committed against the whole church and God, such as heresy. But this
case cannot be brought before the congregation of the faithful. Therefore,
“church” must mean prelates or, as they maintain, a congregation of prelates;
for just as a human being hears and speaks through the head, so the church
does through the prelates. It follows that final judgment belongs to the
pastors.

Response. 1. The inference is denied. It would follow only that judgment
belongs to the church, which we concede, but not final judgment, because the
church's judgment is merely ministerial. 2. Christ does not speak there of the
supreme judge of every controversy or of Scripture's supreme interpreter, but
only of fraternal correction and admonition. Those who despise it are to be
brought before the church, and if they do not hear the church, they must be
excommunicated. 3. The church must always be heard, but under two conditions:
first, if it is established from Scripture that it is the true church; second,
if it prescribes what Christ approves and prescribes. Otherwise, not even an
angel is to be believed (Gal. 1:8), for many councils and bishops who refuted
heretics erred in many other things. Finally, if final judgment belongs to the
pastors in a council, it does not belong to the pope alone, as the Jesuits
maintain.

Objection 9. Matthew 23:2–3 says, “The scribes and Pharisees sit in the chair
of Mo—

[1620 Tome I | col. 109 | BSB frame 00069]

—ses; therefore, observe and do everything they tell you to observe.” They say
three things must be noted here. 1. Throughout that entire chapter, Christ
rebukes the vices of the Pharisees. Because the weak might infer from this that
prelates should not be believed because they live wickedly, he first wished
to teach that notwithstanding the prelates' wicked life, their doctrine must
be followed. 2. In Scripture God never rebuked pontiffs and priests by naming
them, but only under the name of scribes and Pharisees, lest he appear to
rebuke the chair and priesthood, which are always owed honor even if the
person is less than good. 3. What is said of Moses' chair must, by an argument
from the greater, be understood of Peter's chair. Therefore.

Response. 1. Bellarmine's first observation—that the prelates' doctrine must
be followed—must be restricted. It must not be followed absolutely and
simply, but insofar as they sit in Moses' chair, that is, teach what Moses
taught. Otherwise, this is a fallacy of equivocation, because “chair” is taken
in two ways: for doctrine, and for the office or persons. 2. His first
observation does nothing for the pope, because we acknowledge Moses' doctrine
and Scripture as supreme judge. The Pharisees—and today even prelates who live
wickedly—were to be heard when and insofar as they taught well. Yet Matthew 16
and Acts 4:19 and 5:29 teach that they are not to be heard when they teach
wickedly. 3. Moses himself warned that even those sitting in his chair must
not always be heard (Deut. 18:20). Christ commanded his followers to beware
the leaven of the Pharisees (Matt. 16:6), and refuted their errors in Matthew
15:6. 4. Bellarmine's second observation is false. Although the teaching office
itself is not to be rebuked, nor those who rightly perform it, those who abuse
the chair must be rebuked, as we see in the prophets.

[1620 Tome I | col. 110 | BSB frame 00069]

5. His third observation is likewise false, for Scripture says nothing about
Peter's chair in the papists' sense. Indeed, the pope does not sit in Peter's
chair, whether “chair” is taken for the teaching office or for the doctrine
itself. As to what Augustine asserts in epistle 165 about the succession from
Peter to Anastasius—if any such succession were granted—we concede it to be
true: namely, that in the Roman church up to Anastasius's time there had been
no traitorous or Donatist bishops. Furthermore, neither the church nor
Christians are stained even if the bishop were a traitor, since the Lord says
that even the wicked must be heard, and one must do not what they do but what
they say. But Augustine says nothing about an argument from the greater.
Finally, those who sat in Moses' chair excommunicated Christ and his hearers
(John 9:22).

Objection 10. From John, the final chapter, verse 16: “Peter, feed the sheep.”
They say three things must be noted here. First, what is said to Peter is said
also to his successors, because Christ willed to provide for the church not
for twenty-five years, but for as long as the world endured. 2. “Feed” refers
chiefly to doctrine, as in Jeremiah 3:15. 3. “Sheep” signifies all Christians.
Therefore, Peter and his successors, that is, the pope, were commissioned to
teach all Christians. But this cannot be done by sermons or commentaries and
writings, because many pious pontiffs wrote no commentaries. Therefore, this
must necessarily be understood of the unique office of teaching the whole
church by establishing and decreeing what must be believed by all.

Response. 1. Even after granting these three conditions Bellarmine notes,
nothing is established for the pope, since he is not Peter's successor. 2.
Not everyone commanded to feed the sheep receives supreme authority to
interpret and judge Scripture, because feeding signifies only min—

[1620 Tome I | col. 111 | BSB frame 00070]

—istry (Acts 20:28). 3. Peter indeed received power to teach anyone in any
place, but not to teach all Christians by himself, for Christ's other apostles
had the same power (Matt. 28:19; Mark 16:15), since, by Bellarmine's own
testimony, to feed is to teach. 4. After the apostles, no one succeeded Peter
in this function, much less was authority given to the pope to decree what
everyone must believe. We read that it was given to Christ alone (Matt. 17:5),
as Cyprian also notes in epistle 63. 5. These words were properly spoken to
Peter alone, so that by this threefold command he might be restored to the
former dignity of the apostolic office from which he had fallen by his
threefold denial. Thus a threefold confession corresponded to a threefold
denial, as the fathers interpret it. If these words are referred to successors,
however, they pertain to every true bishop, who is Peter's successor with
respect to doctrine, not office. 6. “To feed is to teach,” but the pope does
not teach; therefore, these words do not pertain to him, since Bellarmine says
they must be understood neither of commentaries nor sermons. They cannot be
understood of authority to decide matters of belief, because “feed” is never
used in Scripture in that sense, since everything that must be believed is
prescribed in God's word. Finally, Peter taught the church by living voice and
writings, but the pope does neither.

Objection 11. Those for whom Christ prays that their faith may not fail cannot
err in interpreting and judging. But he prayed for the faith of Peter and his
successors, that is, the pope. Therefore, the pope is consequently the supreme
judge and interpreter.

Response. The proposition is denied, because not falling away from saving
faith, about which Christ speaks, is one thing, while not erring in doctrine
is another.

[1620 Tome I | col. 112 | BSB frame 00070]

Saving faith is unconquerable in the heart, even in those who sometimes err
in doctrine. 2. The passage must be understood of Peter, who would be severely
tempted and would fall. In a general way it can be applied to every believer,
for whom Christ prayed that they might not fall away in temptations. See John
11:11 and Hebrews 7:25. But what does this do for the authority of Antichrist?
Indeed, if the argument were entirely valid, it would follow that no believers
err, because Christ prayed for them. 3. Christ did not pray simply that Peter
might be immune from every error; otherwise, he would not have obtained what
he requested from the Father, because Peter often erred. He thought that
Christ's kingdom could subsist and be advanced by the power of physical arms
(Luke 22:49; John 18:10), and that Christ's kingdom would be worldly (Acts
1:6). He also erred when he denied Christ, when he did not believe in the
calling of the Gentiles (Acts 10), and when Paul withstood him because he did
not walk uprightly (Gal. 2:14). Finally, if Christ prayed for the popes, it
would follow that he did not obtain what he sought, because many popes have
erred even in deciding controversies, as Alfonso asserts in book 1, chapter 1
of *Against Heresies*.

Objection 12. From Acts 15:6–7 and following. When a question of faith arose
whether the law should be observed by Gentiles converted to the faith,
individuals were not each sent to the Holy Spirit, but to a council over which
Peter presided. Peter spoke first in it, followed by James. These things show
that the sentence of a council over which Peter presides is the sentence of
the Holy Spirit, especially because Paul commended that council's sentence to
the churches.

Response. 1. This establishes nothing. We concede that a council ought to
judge controversies, though not absolutely and with supreme authority, but
only according to Scripture, as did this council of Jerusalem, which judged
according to the testimonies of the prophets that

[1620 Tome I | col. 113 | BSB frame 00071]

are found in the cited passage. 2. It is false that Peter spoke first in this
council, as is evident from verses 5 and 7. 3. It is likewise false that Peter
presided over the council, because James presided instead. The apostles and
elders framed the decree in accordance with his words (verses 13 and 22).
Finally, the Holy Spirit presided over this council, and its sentence was the
sentence of the Holy Spirit. But what does this have to do with papist
conventicles, or with the pope, whom they must still prove to be Peter's
successor?

Objection 13. From Galatians 2:2. Paul went up to Jerusalem to compare his
gospel with James, Peter, and John. According to Jerome, Tertullian, and
Augustine, the church would not have believed Paul unless his gospel had been
confirmed by Peter. Therefore, it belonged to Peter then, and consequently to
his successor now, to judge concerning the doctrine of faith.

Response. 1. This does not establish the matter in question. We say that
Peter could and did judge concerning the doctrine of faith, and that his
successors—that is, true pastors—can still judge, but not with supreme
authority. Yet what does this have to do with the pope? 2. If the reasoning of
the antecedent is understood of Peter alone, it is false, because the fathers
never asserted this. Furthermore, Paul went up, as the text says, to confer
publicly with the whole church and privately with those who were held in
esteem (verses 2–3). Bellarmine's assertion about the church can be understood
in two ways: either of what the church ought to have done, in which case the
antecedent is false, because the church ought to have recognized the truth
when Paul preached even without Peter's approval; or of what could not easily
happen, in which case we concede that the church would not so easily have
believed Paul without the approval of others, because some thought Paul
preached otherwise than the remaining apostles. He wished to remove that
suspicion through this conference.

[1620 Tome I | col. 114 | BSB frame 00071]

Finally, he did not confer with Peter alone, nor is Peter named first among
those with whom he conferred, but James. Therefore, if there was any
prerogative in that conference, it belonged to James instead.

Objection 14. From 1 Corinthians 12:8–9, where it is openly said that the
Spirit for interpreting the Scriptures is not given to all. Therefore.

Response. This does not establish the matter in question. Furthermore, this
is a fallacy from the equivocation of the word “interpretation,” for one kind
is private and another public. It is true that not everyone has the gift of
public interpretation. But all believers who are taught can privately
understand the Scriptures and recognize their true sense.

Objection 15. From 2 Peter 1:20: the interpretation of Scripture is not one's
own and private. Therefore, no private person can be a judge of Scripture's
true sense, because the Spirit is not given to all and no one is certain
whether he has the Spirit or to whom he is given. Therefore, the church alone,
which certainly has the Spirit, is judge; and the church speaks only through
the mouth of the pastors, especially those gathered in a general council.
Therefore.

Response. This does not establish the matter in question, because no one
asserts that private persons are supreme judges. 2. We concede that true
pastors lawfully summoned are judges and that Scripture's true sense is to be
sought from them, yet it does not follow that a council is supreme judge. 3.
The interpretation of the passage is false. In that passage one's own
interpretation is not contrasted with a common or public interpretation, nor
does it signify believers' private interpretation. It signifies what comes
from natural skill and the acuity of human intellect, which is contrasted
with the supervening gift of the Holy Spirit who cooperates in understanding
the Scriptures. 4. It is false that believers are not certain whether they
have the Holy Spirit,

[1620 Tome I | col. 115 | BSB frame 00072]

since Scripture asserts the contrary in Romans 8 and elsewhere.

Objection 16. From 1 John 4:1. The spirit of private persons must be tested to
see whether it is from God, because many boast that they have the Holy Spirit
who are driven by a spirit of dizziness and falsehood, as in the final chapter
of 1 Kings and in 2 Chronicles 18:14, 19–20. Therefore, a private spirit cannot
be judge, since it must itself be judged. Rather, the judges are the prelates
in a council or the pontiff from the chair, who have the Spirit.

Response. This does not establish anything against us, because we contend
that every spirit and doctrine must be examined at the judgment of the Holy
Spirit speaking in Scripture. 2. We say that not only the spirits of private
persons must be tested to see whether they are from God, but also those of
public persons, that is, those who teach publicly in the church, as the
apostle means in the cited passage and in 1 Thessalonians 5:21. Therefore,
since the spirits of everyone, including pastors whether separately or
jointly in a council, must be examined according to Scripture because of
false prophets, only the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture is supreme judge.
For if the spirit of private persons, a council, and the pope is good, it is
the same as the spirit of the apostles and the first believers. But they
judged from Scripture, or according to the voice of the Holy Spirit speaking
in Scripture, as is evident from Acts 15 and other passages.

Objection 17. From the church's custom. Augustine bears witness in epistle 118
that it is the most shameless insanity to say or write that what the universal
church has always done and still does is not rightly done. But in every age
new doubts have arisen in the church and have always been settled in the same
way, by the authority of the bishops then living, as is proved from Acts 15,
ecclesiastical history, and councils.

Response. The assumption is denied. Doubts have indeed arisen in every age
and have been settled by councils,

[1620 Tome I | col. 116 | BSB frame 00072]

but not by the supreme or absolute authority of the council or bishops, but
by Scripture's authority. Thus in Acts 15, those who defended circumcision
were refuted from the Scriptures; likewise, at the Council of Nicaea the
Arians were condemned by Scripture's authority, as is evident from
Constantine's words to the council: “There are prophetic and apostolic books
which aptly teach what must be believed. Therefore, laying aside every hostile
disagreement, let us draw from the divinely inspired Scriptures the solution
of the matters called into controversy.” If councils follow Scripture,
therefore, supreme judgment resides with Scripture. 2. It is false that the
questions in the council of Acts 15, Nicaea I, and other councils before the
tyranny of Antichrist was established were decided in part or in whole by the
judgment of the Roman bishop. 3. He falsely restricts judgment of
controversies to the pope with his councils, since it belongs to bishops and
other godly persons. 4. This is a fallacy of a false cause and of what is said
in a qualified sense, for from the Roman bishop's common agreement with others
in refuting errors, he infers that the pope has supreme authority above the
rest. Finally, it does not follow that because certain councils attended by
Roman bishops were ratified, all were ratified because he attended rather
than because others did. They were ratified only because they agreed with
God's word.

Objection 18. From the testimonies of pontiffs and emperors. Pontiffs and
emperors teach that the weightier cases, especially those of faith, belong to
the judgment of the Apostolic See. Therefore.

Response. 1. The inference is denied. The testimonies of pontiffs speak for
themselves in their own case and therefore prove nothing, for the same person
cannot be both a litigating party and judge (Deut. 17:8–9; chapter 19). 2. It
is a fallacy of a false cause, because weightier cases were formerly brought
to the Roman church by the bishops' arrangement, not because the Roman bishop

[1620 Tome I | col. 117 | BSB frame 00073]

was supreme judge, but so that the church's peace might be better served and
heretics and schismatics more easily repressed by the Roman bishop's
authority. From this he gradually arrogated greater authority to himself. 3.
It is established from the pontifical writings Bellarmine cites that some are
spurious and some exceed the bounds of Christianity. 4. Bellarmine's inference
from the emperors' statements is a fallacy of what is said in a qualified
sense. The emperors gave the bishops the right to judge controversies insofar
as they decided from God's word, not their own judgment or the pope's.

Finally, it does not follow: councils formerly judged ecclesiastical matters
without emperors and laypeople involving themselves; therefore, the pope or a
council is supreme judge of controversies.

Objection 19. Certain Latin and Greek fathers teach that controversies cannot
be settled from the Scriptures alone, because heretics expound them in diverse
ways, and that controversies must be settled from the Roman church's doctrine,
not by the authority of secular rulers. Therefore.

Response. 1. The first part of the antecedent is denied. In citing Irenaeus,
book 3, chapter 2, Bellarmine commits falsification, because Irenaeus speaks
not from his own position, but that of the heretics, whose opinion he sets
forth and rebukes—namely, that they preferred traditions to the Scriptures.
2. What he cites from book 3, chapter 3 proves nothing, because we concede
that the Roman church was formerly regarded as most illustrious and was
greatly esteemed by the fathers because it preserved the apostolic tradition
for many centuries. But this does nothing for the pope and Roman church today,
which has collapsed and degenerated into an antichristian church. 3. He cites
in vain testimonies proving that rulers and laypeople ought not judge what
must be believed, because we concede that they ought not perform the work of
priests. 4. Augustine's testimony in book 1,

[1620 Tome I | col. 118 | BSB frame 00073]

chapter 33 of *Against Cresconius*, when he says, “Let him who fears being
deceived consult the church,” proves nothing. We contend that the true church,
which is the custodian of Scripture, must be consulted and heard insofar as it
speaks from Scripture. Neither does the statement in chapter 5 of *Against the
Fundamental Epistle*, “I, however, would not believe the gospel,” and so forth,
prove anything, because there he speaks only of the assisting and instrumental
cause of faith, not the primary cause, and not about faithful converts but
those who are to be converted. With respect to them, the church's testimony
is an introduction and preparation for believing.

Finally, the fathers speak about councils and the Roman church not absolutely,
but according to the condition of their own times.

Objection 20. From reason. God was not unaware that many difficulties
concerning faith would arise in the church. Therefore, he had to provide some
judge for the church. But this judge cannot be Scripture, a private spirit, or
a secular ruler. Therefore, it is an ecclesiastical ruler either alone or
with a council, and nothing else can be imagined to which this judgment might
appear to belong.

Response. 1. We say that God provided for the church most excellently and left
both subordinate judges and the supreme judge. The latter is not a private
spirit, secular rulers, councils, and much less the pope, but only the Holy
Spirit speaking in Scripture.

Insistence. Scripture receives diverse senses and cannot itself say which
sense is true, since it does not speak. Therefore, this proves that Scripture
is not the judge.

Response. The antecedent is denied, for Scripture receives one true sense. If
people assert several senses, they are not really senses of Scripture but
human inventions.

2. It is false that the Scriptures do not speak, although they do not speak
as a human being does, because a mouth is attributed to Scripture in
Deuteronomy 11:1. It is said to—

[1620 Tome I | col. 119 | BSB frame 00074]

—condemn in John 7. It is also said to speak in John 7:42, Romans 4:3, Hebrews
12:5, and elsewhere. Hence we are also said to err when we do not hear the
Scriptures (Matt. 21:29). It is likewise called the word of God, because we
hear God speaking in Scripture no less than if he addressed us with an
articulate voice. From royal letters and letters from friends, we understand
their intention no less than if they addressed us with an articulate voice.
Thus the Scriptures are nothing other than God's letters sent to us, in which
we hear God speaking. Finally, if the heavens are said to declare God's glory
and all creatures to proclaim God's praises, much more must speaking be
ascribed to Scripture.

Second insistence. In every well-ordered commonwealth, the law and the judge
are distinct things. The law teaches what must be done, and the judge
interprets the law and directs people according to it. Therefore, since
Scripture is law in the church, it cannot be judge.

Response. 1. In every commonwealth a judge must interpret law from law, not
according to his own judgment; otherwise, he follows not the law but his own
mind. In civil matters the magistrate is therefore nothing other than the law
speaking and the law's administrator. Thus in the church, pastors must
interpret Scripture from Scripture.

2. The analogy does not hold, because no human being is to be granted as much
authority over Scripture as a civil judge has over civil laws. Furthermore,
these things are distinct in civil judgment, but not in divine judgment if
the supreme judge is considered, because the divine law is judge, interpreter,
and rule.

3. If some human being is this judge, he either sets himself a rule in
interpreting the Scriptures or does not. They will not say the latter. If he
sets himself a rule, it will be either public

[1620 Tome I | col. 120 | BSB frame 00074]

or private. If private, it must be rejected because it is doubtful and
uncertain, and can be no better than the testimony of a private spirit, which
they reject as judge. If public, it will be Scripture or something else. If
Scripture, they concede what we maintain, namely that Scripture is the supreme
judge of Scripture's true sense. If something else, what is it? Certainly none
can be given, because the rule must be more certain, or at least as certain,
as Scripture itself. But and so forth. Therefore.

Third insistence. The question concerns the interpretation of Scripture. But
Scripture cannot interpret itself. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because what is stated obscurely in one
passage is found clearly in another, and according to Tertullian, fewer
passages must be understood according to the greater number. Furthermore, the
question concerns a supreme judge who is not one of the contending parties.
Since Scripture is not led by partisan zeal, while the pope is one of the
parties, Scripture alone, not the pope, must be admitted as supreme judge.

Fourth insistence. A judge must have coercive authority; otherwise, his
judgment would not be effective and no one would rest in it. But Scripture
does not have such authority. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is distinguished, for coercion is twofold, internal
and external. External coercion does not constitute a judge in matters of
faith; otherwise, the civil magistrate could be judge, since he possesses this
power. Furthermore, no external coercion can bend the will to believe, nor
did God establish such power in the church as church (Luke 22; 2 Cor. 1:14; 1
Pet. 5:3). Internal coercion belongs only to God and the Holy Spirit speaking
in Scripture and working inwardly in us, who persuades us and bends our will
so that we believe. Therefore, he alone can be supreme judge in the church.
