# When a Godly Wife Is Treated Harshly by Her Husband Because of Religion

*A corporate French response dated 22 July 1552; a fresh translation from CO X/1 239–241.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 239]

Concerning the case put before us of a good lady who, because she wishes to follow true and pure religion, is badly treated by her husband and held in very strict and cruel bondage; and concerning the request she made to us by letter:

### [CO X/1, p. 240]

namely, whether it would be lawful for her to leave her said husband and withdraw here or to another church where she might live with her conscience at rest—we have agreed unanimously to give the following answer.

First, we consider the perplexity and anguish in which she may be, and we have such pity and compassion for her as we are bound to have, praying that God may be pleased to give her such relief that she may have reason to rejoice in him.

Nevertheless, since she asks our advice about what she is permitted to do, our duty is to answer purely and simply what God shows us about it by his Word, closing our eyes to everything else. We therefore ask her not to be offended if our advice is not altogether in accordance with her desire. For she and we must follow what the Master has ordained without mixing our affections into it.

Here we must consider the marriage bond. It is such that a believing spouse cannot of her own free will separate from an unbelieving spouse, as Saint Paul shows in chapter 7 of First Corinthians (verse 12 and following). There is no doubt that Saint Paul means this even when the believing man or woman has much to suffer. In his time the pagans and Jews were no less embittered against the Christian religion than the papists are today. Yet Saint Paul commands that the believing person, holding firm and persevering constantly in God's truth, must not leave the spouse who resists him or her.

In short, we must prefer God and Jesus Christ to the whole world so completely that fathers, children, husbands, and wives are nothing to us in comparison. Thus, if we cannot adhere to him without renouncing everything, we must do so. But this does not mean that Christianity should abolish the order of nature. When the two can live together, the Christian wife ought rather to exert herself doubly in discharging her duty toward a husband who is an enemy of the truth, in order to win him if possible, as Saint Peter discusses in chapter 3 of his First Catholic Epistle (verse 1).

Nevertheless, as things are today under the papacy, a believing person does not discharge her duty unless she seeks and attempts to bring her spouse onto the path of salvation. And even if there is great obstinacy, she must not appear to turn aside from the faith but instead testify to her firmness and constancy, whatever danger she sees in doing so.

Moreover, if persecution were raised after the said spouse had discharged her

### [CO X/1, p. 241]

duty, she would certainly be permitted to flee it. That is, when a man or woman has confessed the faith and has shown, if need be, that he or she ought not and cannot in any way consent to the abominations of the papacy, if persecution is raised against them on that account and they see themselves in imminent danger, they may justly escape when God gives them the means. For that is not to undertake a voluntary divorce but to flee persecution when one is forced and constrained by it.

Now the good lady who asks our advice is very far from having reached the point of doing her duty. According to what she says in her letters, she merely remains silent and dissembles; when pressed to pollute herself with idolatries, she gives way and consents to them. There would therefore be no excuse for leaving her husband without making a fuller declaration of her faith, being placed under greater compulsion, and resisting it. She must consequently pray that God strengthen her and then, in the power of the Holy Spirit, struggle more steadfastly than she has done: both showing her husband with all gentleness and humility what faith she holds and declaring to him that she must not offend God in order to please him.

We take full account of the husband's harshness and cruelty about which she warns us. But none of this removes her duty to take courage and entrust the outcome to God. For it is quite certain that when, because we are preoccupied by fear, we do not dare attempt what belongs to our calling, this proceeds from unbelief. It is therefore not a foundation on which we should build.

If, after she has tried the things stated above, she sees herself in imminent peril—even if her husband persecutes her to death—then she may use the liberty our Lord gives all his people to escape the rage of wolves.

As for what concerns her bodily safety, the gentleman who presently bears this message will report what we told him orally, together with the reason why we have gone no further. Done 22 July 1552.

## Translator's notes

1. The response is written throughout in a corporate “we” and says its answer was reached “unanimously.” CO recovers the French from the registers of the Genevan Company of Pastors. The edition therefore does not recast this as an ordinary private letter written by Calvin alone.
2. The p. 239/240 transition divides printed *Assa-voir*. Following repository policy, `french.txt` places the p. 240 marker before the complete word *Assavoir*, and the English marker before “namely.”
3. The biblical wording and references are translated from this historical French rather than copied from a modern English Bible. The “First Catholic Epistle” is First Peter.
4. The plural and gender shifts in the paragraph about fleeing persecution are present in the source. The translation makes the inclusive application to a man or woman readable without narrowing it to the woman whose case prompted the response.
5. “Being placed under greater compulsion, and resisting it” renders the compressed *avoir eu plus grande contraincte et y avoir resisté*. The wording appears to require an open test and resistance before the proposed separation; it should not be softened into a modern safety recommendation.
6. This is a historical translation, not contemporary pastoral, legal, or domestic-safety advice. Its demand that an abused wife take additional religious risks before leaving reflects its sixteenth-century setting. A reader facing abuse should seek qualified local help and prioritize immediate safety rather than treat this document as governing advice.
