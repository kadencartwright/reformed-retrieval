Wolzogen, a betrayer of the cause.

OR,

An Examination of

LUDWIG WOLZOGEN'S LITTLE BOOKS

ON

THE INTERPRETER OF THE SCRIPTURES.

Although, in my purpose to demonstrate by the truest arguments and most
indubitable proofs, and to make manifest to everyone, that *Wolzogen*, in his
two little books *On the Interpreter of the Scriptures* against the
*Paradoxical Essayist*, is an open deserter and betrayer of the orthodox cause,
which he ought to have defended and which he professed to champion, it is by
no means necessary to institute a painstaking examination of every clause in
those books; rather, it should suffice to summon their chief heads for
examination and weigh them carefully, while passing dry-shod over the other
matters treated there. Nevertheless, so that no occasion might be given for
the complaint that, without considering his purpose, weighing all the
circumstances, or observing the whole thread of the discourse, a reply was
fashioned to some sentences excerpted here and there, considered separately,
and torn away from what precedes and what conse-*

[Page 2 | ONB canvas 56 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000056]

*quently follows, and thus distorted perversely and contrary to the author's
meaning, and that the author himself was therefore unjustly charged with
error—I thought it advisable to examine these two little books in order and
to follow the author himself step by step. Meanwhile I have only noted in
passing those matters which contribute less to the principal cause but which
nevertheless do not please me, and have examined more closely the others which
are of greater importance. Nor have I omitted the things that he adduces in
his own defense in his *Orthodox Faith*, or *Censure of the Censure*, insofar
as they concern us. Yet our purpose is not to refute at greater length the
errors we discover Wolzogen to favor, but only, once they have been exposed,
to make plain that he has deserted and betrayed our cause.

I. He rightly warned in his dedicatory epistle *that so great a matter is at
issue here that, although it may remain doubtful whether truth is discovered
with greater benefit or error is fostered with more immediate harm, this at
least is true: nowhere does either of them have a surer and more widely
diffused succession. And that the whole of Religion is brought into danger,
which God has indeed enclosed in Scripture, but which the interpreter alone
draws out of Scripture. And that if this interpreter is untrustworthy, he
intercepts all Religion. And that no one is more untrustworthy than the
interpreter recommended by Reason; for Reason is so blind in matters of faith
that it is easily led away from the truth, and by nature people so dote upon
Reason as their own that they willingly follow both the errors of reason and
its sect. And that, since the force of nature is very great, there was reason
to fear that it would drive very many headlong into the paradoxical position.*
But if he said these things in the best faith, who can regard him as excused
when, notwithstanding all of them, he embraced that very position of the most
heterodox Paradoxical Essayist, at least for the most part, so that he appears
to differ from it little or not at all as regards the substance of the matter,
as will afterward become evident? But if he believed that so great a matter
was at issue here and that the whole of Religion was brought into dan-*

[Page 3 | ONB canvas 57 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000057]

*ger, with what good faith could he so greatly favor that untrustworthy
interpreter? With what conscience, if matters stand so, could he ascribe so
much to Reason, when no interpreter can be more untrustworthy—especially when
he himself saw that Reason is blind in matters of faith, so that it can easily
be led away from truth, and that everyone so dotes upon Reason as his own that
he willingly follows it?

Moreover, where he seems to dissent from this heterodox Essayist, why did he,
the first of all who wish to be regarded as orthodox, desert the well-worn road
which all the orthodox have entered, held until now, and demonstrated to us,
and take another? Did he intend to make a new sect, of which he would be the
first head and author? Is this to consult the peace of the Church? But he
continues at the same place: *Learned men have applied a brake*, he says, *and
there would be little need for this writing if provision had been made for our
reputation as the cause had been answered.* By whom, I ask, was that brake
applied? By those of whom he himself doubts *whether they sufficiently
understood themselves*, and who *did not conceive the matter itself with
sufficient distinctness or disclose their mind with sufficient clarity*, as
he says on page 222? By those *who in empty argumentation neither defended
their own positions nor attacked those of others*, as he says on page 247? By
those who *did not have a thorough view of the intimate nature of this
question*, page 260?

So loftily does Wolzogen think of the most learned, discerning, and faithful
doctors of the Reformed Church—of Calvin, Whitaker, Chamier, Sharp, Walaeus,
Polanus, Alting, and other Reformed theologians who rendered the best service
to Christ's Church. The brake, therefore, was not applied by them, nor was the
cause answered. By whom, then? By those who refuted this paradox before
Wolzogen? But who are they? Yet however it may be, if the brake was applied,
with what effrontery can he remove or break this brake applied by others? Who,
I ask, whether among the ancients or the more recent writers, ever entered the
road that Wolzo-*

[Page 4 | ONB canvas 58 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000058]

*gen follows? Does he not himself say on page 5 that *this question has by no
means been unfolded with sufficient clarity until now?* Therefore, either he
judges that no brake was applied, although he toys with his masters by saying
this, or that the brake was not sufficiently firm and fixed, and consequently
that the cause has not yet been answered. The occasion or cause for this
writing which follows he pursues and explains at length and with many words in
the first five or six pages of his little book. Let us therefore go there.

II. *Although*, he says, *when a tumult has been stirred up, it is lawful for
anyone to take up arms in defense of the commonwealth, and no tumult seems to
need to be fought in a Christian commonwealth from a more just fear than in
these very difficult times, when the very citadel of Religion is besieged and
in danger, so that I have nothing to fear lest it incur any criticism from men
that I have undertaken to refute the most dangerous little book which an
unspeakable Author recently published on philosophy as the Interpreter of the
Scriptures.* Reply. 1. No one who has the safety and preservation of
Christianity at heart doubts that, when a tumult has been stirred up, it is
lawful for anyone to take up arms in defense of the commonwealth and of the
Christian realm. Nor, Wolzogen, would any prudent lover and supporter of truth
and Reformed religion have faulted you for undertaking to refute that
unspeakable little book, if only in this business you had in fact proved
yourself a faithful champion of truth and friend of the Reformed commonwealth.
But since you not only failed to calm the tumult which had been stirred up,
but also aroused another tumult besides; and not only failed to extinguish the
fire which threatened the whole house with ruin, but also increased the blaze
by pouring on fresh oil, who will pronounce you innocent? Whose censure will
you escape among those touched by zeal for truth and God's house? 2. You call
these times very difficult, and rightly so. But why, then, do you make them
more difficult? Impiety, vanity, hatred of God, hatred of the godly, error,
heresy, Popery, Soci-*

[Page 5 | ONB canvas 59 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000059]

*nianism, Erastianism, Libertinism, and what else does not rage everywhere?
But what do you contribute toward averting and removing these and similar
evils? You say that you undertook to refute the *Paradoxical Essayist*. I hear
you say this, and I do not deny that you undertook it; but the outcome shows
how you emerged and how you completed this work you assumed, and we will
demonstrate it in examining this work of yours. 3. If you think yourself
immune from all censure because you girded yourself to liberate and fortify
the already besieged citadel of Religion, you cannot judge us blameworthy for
undertaking to fortify and defend, with the arms we possess, the same citadel
of Religion, which has been placed in the most certain danger no less by you,
though a friend in name only, than by other open enemies. Indeed, it ought
still less to be held against us that we defend the citadel of Religion
against your efforts, because experience has frequently shown that masked men
who profess the most intimate friendship and promise the holiest fidelity not
only fail faithfully to discharge the office they assumed, but secretly and
wickedly plan surrender and treacherously hand over to the enemy the citadel
they profess to defend, harm Religion much more than open adversaries. There
is no lack of by far the weightiest reasons moving me to number you among the
traitors; I will show them as I proceed. 4. You say that the very citadel of
Religion, now besieged, is in danger. You say this rightly, but your guilt is
thereby increased, because you not only fail to free it from this danger and
siege, but strengthen the siege, or rather flee most effeminately after
casting away your weapons, and indeed, as was said, contrive its surrender by
every art you can. But, Wolzogen, say frankly: Did zeal for God and love of
truth and Reformed Religion drive you to this contest? If matters stand so,
what do the following words mean? *Yet there is some particular reason of my
own*, you say, *by which I was chiefly in-*

[Page 6 | ONB canvas 60 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000060]

*duced to write nonetheless about a subject already occupied by the industry
of very many excellent men. It is not that I lack many other considerations I
could bring forward for this, both concerning my role, which is such as has
been laid upon me by my masters in both the academy and the Church that
nothing in theology can appear very alien to it, and concerning the pious
decree of the illustrious States of this Province. For in proscribing the
paradoxical writer, they sufficiently declared by their own example how all
their subjects ought to be disposed toward him. Yet this matter in particular,
as it especially concerned me, chiefly moved me: our Church was so confidently
marked out, even in published disputations, that scarcely anyone doubted that
that dreadful monster had burst forth from its consistory.* Reply. I now see
that the principal reason by which you were chiefly induced to treat this
subject was not a Christian love of truth, but something else peculiar to you:
namely, to free yourself, your Church, and your consistory from this charge
laid against you. 2. I also hear you pass over rhetorically certain other
considerations which could have moved you: namely, the nature of your role,
which is such as was imposed upon you by your masters in both the academy and
the Church that it could demand this. But look here! You seem to imply that you
enjoy no other calling to the Church over which you have been placed than the
one by which, furnished with an academic office, whatever it may be, you
discharge your function. Do you depend upon them in the same way insofar as
you are a pastor as you do insofar as you are a lecturer—or something similar?
Take care not to say it. 3. Do you have no role imposed upon you by the Lord
Christ? Do you acknowledge no Lord other than those masters of yours whom you
so often mention? Then you are doubtless a servant of men, not of Jesus
Christ. 4. I do not know by what decree the States of this Province proscribed
that paradoxical

[Page 7 | ONB canvas 61 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000061]

writer. They perhaps condemned the little book and forbade it to be offered
for sale by their subjects, but until now I have happened to hear nothing
about the author himself. However that may be, you ought to have learned
something far different from that pious decree than we see you learned. The
States declared, you say, how all their subjects ought to be disposed toward
him. Therefore they did not wish anyone to approve this book by his vote, or
to defend or confirm the impious dogma it championed—which nevertheless, for
the most part, both you and your elder have done, as is and will be evident.
Beyond doubt, by this pious decree the States wished to signify their will
that the orthodox doctrine received and approved until now remain safe and
intact. But you, repudiating this pious decree, reject the orthodox position
and hiss it off with contempt. Let others judge, then, whether in this you
conducted yourself as became a dutiful subject. 5. I certainly would not wish
anything false to be imputed even to the devil himself, nor would I fault you
for endeavoring to vindicate yourself, your Church, and your consistory from
any charge unjustly laid against you. But if my judgment counts for anything,
all Christians ought to consult God's glory more than their own reputation.
This chiefly ought to have moved you: that that profane author insolently
attacked God's truth with his blasphemous assertions and openly declared war
on the Holy Spirit. It also ought in some measure to have affected you that he
so wantonly insulted all the orthodox. But these things do not particularly
concern you. This is certainly no wonder, since you appear to be led by nearly
the same spirit which led that most heterodox *Essayist*.

But what was the matter? *Scarcely anyone doubted*, he says, *that that
dreadful monster had burst forth from its consistory.* And afterward, on page
4: *It remains for the Gallo-Belgic Church itself, as the vindicator of its
own reputation, to establish by deed how contrary its Doctrine is to this
paradoxical posi-*

[Page 8 | ONB canvas 62 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000062]

*tion.* Reply. I have not yet heard that anyone thought that that dreadful
monster burst forth from your consistory. Even the man who dispelled that report
never said that the consistory of the Gallo-Belgic Church at Utrecht was the
author of that book. When Maresius merely asked him to communicate anything
he knew about the author of that Infamous Book, he replied that Dr. Hoornbeeck
had informed him by letter that Velthuysen had brought forth that writing.
What? Does Wolzogen think that everything done by any member of a consistory
must be imputed to the consistory? You would perhaps say that no slight wrong
was done to you if you were regarded as the author of all those little books
which your elder Velthuysen brought forth and were summoned to court on that
account. You would complain much more if the lapses of every member of your
Church, or of any of them, were imputed to you. By what right, then, can you
say that what was imputed to Velthuysen alone was imputed also to your whole
Church and consistory? But now you teach us to learn about everyone from the
charge against one man; and hereafter, if anyone imputes the errors of
Wolzogen or Velthuysen to the consistory and Gallo-Belgic Church at Utrecht,
he will be free of every charge. Yet far be it from me to do this.

He further adds: *Nevertheless, here we do not undertake the defense of that
most illustrious man against whom suspicion was cast by a deliberate blow.
He is sufficiently protected by his own innocence and, when he must be
defended, will be defended by no stronger arms than his own. But because the
very name of the Church was attacked through his person, I judged it my duty
to afford the greatest protection to the Church.* Reply. Therefore you do not
undertake Velthuysen's defense. Whose defense, then? That of the Church, you
say, whose name was attacked through his person. But unless you undertake the
defense of Velthuysen himself, you will never defend the Church attacked
through his person. Velthuysen, you say, is sufficiently protected by his own
innocence; the Church will then also be sufficiently safe. Velthuysen must
therefore be de-*

[Page 9 | ONB canvas 63 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000063]

*fended, but with no stronger arms than his own. But what, I ask, are these
arms of Velthuysen's? Are they his own writings, by which he makes it manifest
to the whole world that he favors Pelagianism, or Semipelagianism, Popery,
Arminianism, Socinianism, Erastianism, Libertinism, Cartesianism, Hobbesianism,
and so forth? Or is it even that writing *On the Use of Reason in Theology*,
by which he not only fails to free himself from that heterodoxy which the
Essayist defends, but in many respects confirms the same position and proposes
several other paradoxical, indeed heterodox, positions? This is the man whom
you say elsewhere has *a mind tenacious of orthodox truth*. This is the man
protected sufficiently by his own innocence. This is the man through whose
person the very name of your Church was attacked. But how will you now defend
either Velthuysen or your Church? Everyone who knows Velthuysen, and does not
stick in the same mire, or who reads his little books, knows how heterodox that
innocent Velthuysen of yours is. Woe now to your Church, if all Velthuysen's
crimes, and yours as well, must be imputed to it! But how, I ask, was the very
name of your Church attacked through Velthuysen's person? Does your Church
approve everything which that Innocent man tenacious of orthodox truth—or,
rather, hater of it—asserts, affirms, and endeavors to establish in his little
books? Then it is all over with your Church's orthodoxy, and it must no longer
be regarded as an orthodox Church. Did everyone who had even heard of
Velthuysen by report regard him as a member of the consistory of the
Gallo-Belgic Church at Utrecht? Does he always distinguish himself by the
title *elder* of that Church? But perhaps you speak of those to whom this was
known. Nevertheless, I do not yet see how the name of your Church can be
attacked through Velthuysen alone. *It was commonly reported*—so you say in
your *Dedicatory Epistle*—*that the paradox had proceeded from our Church, and
the envious were already rejoicing that heterodoxy was charged against it
among the learned, just as laxity of conduct was im-*

[Page 10 | ONB canvas 64 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000064]

*puted to it among the common people.* Reply. It was indeed reported that that
paradoxical little book had proceeded from Velthuysen. But I do not see why
they should therefore say that it proceeded from your Church, unless the
Church approved it or, once the author was known, failed to mark him with its
censures. But since everyone was uncertain about the author himself, no one
could rightly impute it to your Church.

But now that the elder Velthuysen has sufficiently declared his own position
in the little book named above, and you also have proposed your position in
these little books now at hand; and since each is erroneous and paradoxical, I
do not see how that Church will be freed from guilt before God unless it warns
both its pastor and its elder of their error and, unless they come to their
senses, return to a sound mind, and openly attest their repentance, takes the
ordinary course prescribed by Christ, by which the pastor is removed from the
pastoral office and the elder from his ecclesiastical office, and they are no
longer regarded as members of the Church. 2. I certainly do not know who those
envious people were who rejoiced that heterodoxy was imputed to your Church.
Nor do I consider it Christian to envy any Church, much less to rejoice that
it is regarded as guilty of heterodoxies. I judge that a cause for sadness,
not joy. It can nevertheless sometimes be an occasion for joy when certain
people little friendly to truth appear painted in their own colors, so that
they can no longer deceive many people with their disguises. 3. You say that
*laxity of conduct was imputed to your Church among the common people.* I do
not know whether this was just or unjust. It is probable that a Church to
which the common people impute laxity of conduct is not the purest and most
chastened, since the common people for the most part greatly desire freedom
and license, long for laxity of conduct, and seek it with their whole heart.
That laxity of conduct must be very great which the common people reproach in
anyone, since this very laxity is generally dear to them. Yet I admit that
among the

[Page 11 | ONB canvas 65 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000065]

common people themselves are not a few who, although destitute of grace,
possess natural conscience and through it can condemn many things in others
which they themselves nevertheless do not hate.

But how do you remove this charge laid against you? *Its whole case is now
pleaded by us*, you say on page 4, *if we repel the accusation even by a bare
denial. The paradoxical book is not ours; the book's position is not ours. Let
the exposed offspring seek a parent elsewhere; it has none here. Let it seek
a fosterer elsewhere; it will not find one here. We do not believe in this
way; we do not teach in this way. What can even slander itself object here? We
have as many witnesses of our doctrine as we have hearers; it is necessary
that we who state our meaning be believed.* Reply. 1. Whether that paradoxical
book is yours or not is not mine to inquire anxiously. I will say only that I
do not yet see so great a discrepancy between that position of the Paradoxical
Essayist and your position, Wolzogen, and that of your elder Velthuysen, as
could compel me to admit that it is impossible or altogether improbable that
that paradoxical book proceeded from the same pastor or elder. For unless my
mind greatly deceives me, both the pastor Wolzogen and the elder Velthuysen,
no less than the author of the paradoxical book himself, conspire in attacking
the truth and tearing up the foundation of Religion. To the extent they can,
they bring all their strength to eliminating the inward, effectual, and most
necessary power of the Spirit; to driving from his throne the Holy Spirit, the
one supreme and infallible Interpreter of the Scriptures; and they all conspire
alike to displace the Scriptures, which the orthodox have until now regarded
as the one infallible standard for interpreting the Scriptures. Thus, as far
as the heart of the matter is concerned, it makes no difference whether the
position of the Paradoxical Essayist is approved or that of his refuters,
Wolzogen and Velthuysen. What? Is the book's position not yours? Why, then, do
you re-*

[Page 12 | ONB canvas 66 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000066]

*ject and trample upon the orthodox position received and defended by the
Reformed until now, no less than the *Essayist* himself does? Why do you assert
that Reason has so great a prerogative in interpreting the Scriptures that
there is little or no difference between you and the *Paradoxical Essayist*?
But we will demonstrate all these things afterward in their proper place. 3.
Will that book find no fosterer among you? Why, I ask? Will not any man free
from prejudices who reads your books judge that no one who had not expressly
wished to profess himself a proselyte and follower of the paradoxical position
could have said more to confirm it than you said in your books? Therefore you cannot
say that you do not believe in this way or teach in this way; your books
attest the contrary. And now you have as many witnesses of your doctrine as
you have, not only hearers, but readers. I will gladly judge your meaning from
your words.

What next? *We will nevertheless grant this*, he says, *to suspicious men: we
will explain clearly how far our position is removed from the new paradox. And
so that the discourse may not become too long, I confess that this entire
title displeases me: Philosophy the Interpreter of Scripture.* Reply. You will
certainly remove every suspicion at once if, by this exceedingly brief
statement that the title of the paradoxical book displeases you, you remove
it. But what if the things treated in that paradoxical book do not displease
you so much? What if the Paradoxical Essayist had prefixed the same title that
your elder Velthuysen prefixed to his little book, namely, *A Dissertation on
the Use of Reason in Theology*? Would that title please you more? But perhaps
you will say that you do not regard philosophy as the interpreter of the
Scriptures. To what extent you concede this, and to what extent you deny it,
we will see afterward. But as concerns the point that you wish to possess as
your own—that linguistic usage is the Interpreter of Scripture—I do not see
why it should not displease you just as much as it ought*

[Page 13 | ONB canvas 67 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000067]

*to, however. The Paradoxical Essayist wishes philosophy, or human reason, to
be the infallible Interpreter of Scripture. When you wish to differ from him,
you say that this prerogative must be ascribed solely to grammar, or linguistic
usage. O what a pair! Who with one grain of salt remaining will not say,
affirm, and asseverate that you run against truth no less than he does, and
that both of you oppose truth more than you oppose each other? And if this
position of yours, peculiar to you, is received, is it not all over with the
whole Christian Religion no less than if the paradoxical position were
received?

III. Our man continues on page 5: *This question about the Interpreter of the
Scriptures is indeed in itself as noble as any other, and at the same time is
especially necessary. For it contains the whole Christian Religion, which
assuredly is nothing if it does not flow in an uninterrupted channel from the
very fountain of the Scriptures.* Reply. Who could read these words without
knowing Wolzogen and not say that Wolzogen intended, beyond all doubt, to
treat with the greatest fidelity, diligence, and care this question which is
so necessary and contains the whole Christian Religion? But whoever believed
this would be disappointed in his hope. For although no one with a sound brain
can deny that it is most dangerous and a mark of a perverse disposition to
stumble in a question of such importance, to depart even a little from the
straight and safe path, and to defect to the adversaries' side, this man
nevertheless dares, after setting aside the sound position of all orthodox
theologians, to level a new road for us—but one which leads straight to the
adversaries. If, however, as Wolzogen professes both in the dedicatory epistle
and at the very threshold of the little book, he had consulted his own
reputation in undertaking this work; and, what must always be held in higher
esteem among all faithful Christians, if he had sincerely intended God's
glory and the edification of the Church, and had pur-*

[Page 14 | ONB canvas 68 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000068]

*sued this end with a mind free from every disguise and deceit, he would have
instituted a far different method of refutation and treated this cause much
more cautiously than I see him treat it. It would have been a matter of
religion for him to depart from the road of truth, defended until now with
God's abundant approval and blessing by truly heroic men and Herculean
champions. But why did our modern innovator Wolzogen desert our learned,
orthodox, faithful, godly theologians, who were most practiced in this arena
and most versed in this study? See what follows; perhaps the reason will
appear. You, reader, judge whether it is contrived or true.

*And this question was by no means unfolded with sufficient clarity until
now*, he says, *so that he who has stirred up controversies about it among the
learned seems especially to have done something worthwhile.* Is that so,
Wolzogen? Have our outstanding heroes labored in vain until now and not yet
penetrated to the kernel of this question? Then you alone must be heard? You
will be called the *Angelic, Seraphic, Irrefragable, Subtle, Most Well-Grounded,
Illuminated,* and *Resolute Doctor*. All the others wasted their labor and oil,
occupied with the bark, while you will give us the whole matter perfectly
unfolded. Blessed indeed are those to whom it is granted to be raised at your
feet and to hear you disentangle, unfold, and explain subtle, difficult, and
intricate questions! But you continue, and appear to praise the *Essayist*
himself, or at least to commend that work because he who stirred up
controversies about this subject among the learned seems to have done something
exceedingly worthwhile. That is, he deserves praise because he furnished you
an occasion to set forth your position, which was never heard until now, for
you wish to appear to have dispelled the remaining darkness by yourself. What
you afterward say on page 6 about the Paradoxical Essayist can with equal
right, changing a few things which must be changed, also be said about you:
*For he himself*, you say, *seems to me to have been struck by an unusual
glare, and then, his eyes dazzled, both to be blind and to rave with an
alienated mind. So thoroughly does he see nothing who follows Reason as his
guide in this*

[Page 15 | ONB canvas 69 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000069]

*way without reason as the Interpreter of Scripture; and he is mad who exults
over it so intemperately, as though in triumph. Indeed, unless we say that,
unconcerned for the Church, he seeks nothing but the most trivial fame from
the insolence of his opinion, or seeks the wicked pleasure of forming parties
by dissension, he foolishly persuaded himself that his position would have
followers. That position could neither please, being far too abrupt and
precipitous, nor could it lawfully be admitted by anyone bound by the laws of
any order, since it is so contrary to the received formulas of commonly
accepted Doctrine.* For you seem to no small number of people to have been
struck by an unusual glare, and then, your eyes dazzled, both to be blind and
to rave with an alienated mind. What, I ask, do you see, when without the use
of reason you follow Usage as your guide and the Interpreter of Scripture?
You too are mad, exulting over it so intemperately, as though in triumph. Does
not everyone see that you were unconcerned for the Church and sought nothing
but the most trivial fame from the insolence of your opinion? How could you
think that your position could please, when it is no less abrupt and
precipitous, and could not lawfully be admitted by anyone bound by the laws of
any order, since it is so contrary to the received formulas of commonly
accepted doctrine?

I admit that the following words cannot so conveniently be said about you:
*So that the paradoxical title appears to me hardly less paradoxical than the
question itself*, you say, *by which the unsound author seems deliberately to
have wished to ensure that perhaps no one might be deceived and fail to know
that a dangerous error was being commended by him.* For you appear not to have
acted so candidly: while professing to refute that paradox, you commend to
unwary readers a position no less paradoxical and an error no less dangerous;
nor do you take care, by giving some sign, that no one might be deceived and
