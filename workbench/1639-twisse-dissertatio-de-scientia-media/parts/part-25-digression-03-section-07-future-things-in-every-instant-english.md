[Book I, printed p. 69; BSB canvas 00095]

NAVARRETE.

You nevertheless maintain that future things exist in eternity at every instant of time,
notwithstanding the subtlety of Capreolus’s and Cajetan’s contrary distinction: namely, that the
proposition concerning the existence of future things in eternity is true for every instant of
time, although the future thing by no means exists in eternity at every instant of time.

Against this I argue as follows. It follows that future things are present not only in eternity but
also in time. For to be at an instant of time signifies a thing’s existence in time just as to be
in eternity signifies its existence in eternity. If someone said that an angel is in the *aevum* at
every instant of time, would any sober person doubt that this signifies that the angelic existence
lasts through every instant of time as well as through the *aevum*? Likewise, if someone said that
God is in eternity throughout every instant of time, would he not thereby signify that God endures
through every instant of time as well as through eternity itself?

I acknowledge that the inference is invalid if the added term diminishes the thing’s existence in
time. For example, if someone said, “At every instant of time the future thing is willed by God,” or
“At every instant of time the future thing is known by God,” it by no means follows that the future
thing therefore exists at every instant of time. But this too conflicts with Navarrete’s opinion
proposed above, when he said that future things are in eternity through a certain containment,
insofar as eternity extends itself to future things. Is that not just as if he had said that
eternity extends itself to those instants at which the individual future things respectively
exist? Thus future things should not be said to exist in eternity at every instant of time, but
only at the time when they exist. Otherwise, while eternity extends itself to this instant, future
things exist in it, provided they exist in eternity at every instant of time. Navarrete’s disputation
against Cajetan and Ferrariensis appears sound to me, but the opinion which he substitutes after
removing the former appears no healthier.
