SECTION THREE.

ARMINIUS. The appendage was the threat of bodily and spiritual death, the death of body and soul.
A spiritual penalty, opposed not only to animal good but also to spiritual good, ought not by the
rule of equity to have been added to a law performable without supernatural grace—especially since
that same law, if observed, could not confer supernatural or spiritual good, inasmuch as it could
be performed without supernatural grace. Moreover, it seems inequitable that transgression of the
law could merit eternal and spiritual death, while performance of it could not obtain eternal and
spiritual life from God by a covenant of divine goodness and justice.

JUNIUS. The second proof, from the law’s appendage, stands in exactly the same way. This seems to be
the argument: If, in the matter of election and reprobation, God had dealt with human beings
considered in their pure natural endowments, he would not have established a spiritual penalty,
opposed not only to animal good but also to spiritual good, for transgressors of a law performable
without supernatural grace. For by the rule of equity the penalty should be equal to the offence.
But God established such a penalty. Therefore. This first statement, which is in the conclusion,
is not asserted by us.

But let it be so; I shall say nothing about the antecedent of the major premise. As has often been
said already, the consequent is denied. For the proposition would be true if both offences or
crimes, and penalties, were assessed solely from the deed which the law forbids and from its kind.
But there are now many other things from which the gravity of crimes is most justly accustomed to
be assessed: the author of the law, the author of the crime, the object, the circumstance of the
end, etc.

I shall go further. Tell me, my brother: do you think this penalty is more justly imposed upon a
human being considered in corruption than upon one considered in pure natural endowments? For this
is what your argument means. But I would not hesitate to affirm the contrary. I say that
Adam sinned more gravely because he sinned while upright than he would have if situated in sin. But
as for your addition that it seems inequitable that transgression of the law could merit eternal
and spiritual death, etc., I am indeed astonished that this should be pronounced by you.

ARMINIUS. My second proof seems not to have been grasped by you in conformity with my meaning. Its
meaning is this, and its force consists in this: a spiritual penalty cannot be attached to the
transgression of a law for whose performance spiritual good has not been promised. But spiritual
good has not been promised for the performance of this law, if indeed it can be performed without
supernatural grace. For supernatural grace and supernatural happiness are analogous. Hence it
follows by reasoning backward: if a spiritual penalty has been attached to the transgression of
that law, then spiritual good has also been promised for its performance, and consequently the law
was performable only by supernatural grace; otherwise nature can by its own deed attain a
supernatural good.

As for what you say about the gravity of the sin perpetrated by our first parents, I acknowledge it
to be most true, and I do not think its heinousness can be expressed in words.

But from what do you gather that my argument intends that

a penalty is more justly imposed upon a human being who has transgressed a command when he was in
corruption and sin than when he was upright? I did not contrast those things here, but contrasted a
human being in pure natural endowments with one possessing those same supernatural endowments. A
penalty is more justly imposed upon the latter when he transgresses than upon the former; indeed,
it is imposed unjustly upon the former if the law was not performable without supernatural grace.
And if the performance of the law had no promise of spiritual good, a spiritual penalty is
unjustly imposed upon the transgressor of that law.

RESPONSE. 1. Let us consider Arminius’s reasoning. “The appendage,” he says, “was the threat of
bodily and spiritual death, the death of body and soul. A spiritual penalty, opposed not only to
animal good but also to spiritual good, ought not by the rule of equity to have been added to a law
performable without supernatural grace.” If it ought not to have been added, then either it ought
not to have been added for this reason, because the penalty seems heavier than befits the fault, or
for some other reason. It is not because it is heavier than befits the fault. For if it would not
be heavier than the transgression of a law performable only through supernatural grace, how much
less should it be reckoned heavier than the transgression of a law performable apart from
supernatural grace? The reason is that transgression of a law performable apart from supernatural
grace is graver than transgression of a law performable only through supernatural grace. For the
easier the observance of a law, the graver its transgression; but observance of a law performable
apart from supernatural grace is easier than observance of a law performable only through
supernatural grace. Again, on the contrary, I approve the opinion of Gerson, who affirms that, once
sin is posited, it lies with God to punish the sinner to whatever measure he wills, and that it
implies a contradiction to affirm that God can punish any sinner beyond what is deserved. But let
us attend to Arminius’s reasoning. “Especially,” he says, “since that same law, if observed, could
not confer supernatural or spiritual good, inasmuch as it could be performed without supernatural
grace.” Nothing solid occurs here. By the same reasoning he would say: because the creature cannot
merit eternal life, it therefore cannot merit eternal death. Again, the antecedent is false,
because God can reward in any manner whatsoever the observance of a law performable apart from
supernatural grace. For he can confer any good upon a human being even without preceding
obedience; how much more will he be able to do this when obedience of any kind whatsoever has
preceded?

2. When Junius replies that this very thing which was in the conclusion is not asserted by him—
namely, that in predestining God does not deal with human beings considered in natural endowments—
he must be understood restrictively. For he maintains that God dealt not only with human beings
considered in pure natural endowments but also with human beings not yet created and even with
corrupted human beings; that is, with the human being commonly considered. We have more than once
said enough about the construction of this opinion.

What Junius next adds, discoursing fully on the gravity of Adam’s sin, Arminius pronounces to be
true. It also seems to me that all those things are sufficiently well fitted to weaken Arminius’s
argument.

As for what he proposes in the third place, I entirely fail to grasp how it fits; indeed, it seems
to me that I see plainly that it does not fit. I do not doubt that the remaining things directed
against Arminius are sufficient.

3. Your second proof seems altogether to have been grasped sufficiently. Nor does it follow that
spiritual good was not promised for the performance of that law, if indeed it can be performed
without supernatural grace. For if God could have conferred eternal life, had he wished, with no
preceding obedience, how much more could he have conferred eternal life when some obedience,
however slight, had preceded? You say that supernatural grace and supernatural happiness are
analogous. What does this mean? I believe that the one corresponds to the other as the fitting
reward of the other. I acknowledge a proportion, but one which by no means binds God. For God can
even annihilate the holiest human being, as Arminius testifies; whence it would come about that
supernatural grace would by no means be followed by supernatural happiness. And if God wishes, he
will also be able to reward obedience which did not proceed from supernatural grace with
supernatural happiness, since, even without regard to preceding obedience, he will be able to
confer glory upon anyone if he wishes. And what wonder is it if nature could by its own deed—but by
God’s kindness—attain a supernatural good, when it could attain that very supernatural good apart
from any deed of its own? But even if supernatural happiness had by no means been promised for the
performance of a law by nature, it would by no means follow from this that God cannot inflict
eternal death upon a creature transgressing a natural law performable by its own powers. First,
because failure to perform what is more easily performable merits a greater penalty. Second,
because, once any transgression whatsoever is posited, it lies with God to inflict whatever penalty
he pleases upon the transgressor. Nor ought it seem strange that a creature can merit eternal
penalty and cannot merit eternal life. What you discourse about the threefold difference occurring
in the transgression and performance of a law is altogether irrelevant, although Arminius plays
the master while explaining it.

If you judge that the heinousness of Adam’s sin cannot be expressed in words by reason of the
circumstances mentioned by Junius—and all of them, every one, indeed apply even if the law
prescribed to Adam was performable apart from supernatural grace, while this very fact, as a
circumstance distinct from the others, contributes somewhat to amplifying its heinousness—why does
it seem to you that Adam would have been treated more severely by God than was fitting if God had
inflicted eternal death upon him because of this transgression?

In my judgment Arminius must be freed from that imputation. But he does not even attempt to
extricate himself from Junius’s following argument.
