QUESTION VI.

ON THE SACRAMENTAL WORD.

Is the sacramental word a preached and proclamatory word, or is it a consecratory
word that is operative? We affirm the former and deny the latter against the
papists.

[Marginalia: What the sacramental word is.]

I. We said before that the word of institution, which consists of a command
and a promise, is necessary for the constitution of a sacrament. We call this
the sacramental word, or the proclamatory and preached word. It is called this not
so much because it is taught and preached before the congregation to teach the
proper manner of administration, as because it is proposed clearly and aloud
in the institution of the sacrament to inform the faithful who are present.

II. The papists, however, do not wish the sacramental word to be proclamatory or
preached, but consecratory and operative. They hold that it breathes and
infuses a certain hidden power into the elements so that they become
sacraments; that it is directed to the lifeless element rather than to human
beings; and that it ought not to be pronounced aloud to instruct those
standing around, but whispered secretly. In this sense we rightly reject such
a consecratory word, since it was neither spoken by Christ nor written by the
apostles, but devised by human superstition. In another sense, however, we
readily admit that the preached sacramental word of institution is also
consecratory with respect to the signs, insofar as through it they are
transferred from common and profane use to sacred use.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

III. The state of the question is evident from this. The question is not
whether there is some consecratory sacramental word, which we do not deny, but
whether it is consecratory in such a way that it is also operative, which we
deny. Nor is the question whether some word must be pronounced over the signs,
but whether it is a word that must be preached in a loud and intelligible voice
so that it may be understood by those to whom the sacrament is to be
administered and can for that reason be called proclamatory. Or is it situated in
a certain formula that does not edify those about to partake, but consecrates
the elements by a certain power imparted to the words themselves, words that
are to be muttered over the elements in a subdued voice? The papists deny the
former and affirm the latter; we do the opposite.

[Marginalia: The sacramental word is proved not to be operative. 1. Because its
use consists in signifying.]

IV. The reasons are these. 1. The use of the sacramental word consists in
signifying; therefore it must be preached aloud. The antecedent is proved,
first, from the fact that words are signs of things, but words that signify
nothing have no use. Next, since the sacramental word is the word of the
institution of the sacrament itself, which consists of a command and a
promise, it is evident that its use is situated in signifying. The command
prescribes the administration of the sacrament, and the promise declares its
dignity and efficacy. But no one can declare anything through words that do
not signify, such as words whispered without meaning and understanding. The
consequence is clear, because the preaching of the word, or its utterance in
an intelligible voice, and its signification are so joined that one cannot
exist without the other. A word that is not preached is unknown; what is
unknown cannot signify, for what signifies is a sign of the thing signified,
but a sign cannot be unknown.

[Marginalia: 2. Because it pertains to all believers.]

V. 2. The sacramental word pertains to all believers and is proposed to all
believers. Therefore it must not be secretly whispered over the elements, but
preached for the edification of all. For God willed it to be committed to
writing so that it might pertain to all and be proposed to all, not so that it
might be muttered without meaning.

[Marginalia: 3. Because the words must determine the signs.]

VI. 3. No instituted sign, such as the sacraments, can exist without the
preached word. Instituted signs are indeterminate in themselves with respect
to signifying, but they are determined through the words by which they are
instituted, whereas words that are not understood determine nothing. Since,
therefore, the sacramental words determine, while words signifying nothing
determine nothing, the sacramental words cannot be words that are not
understood and thus not preached. This is confirmed from induction, because in
former times no sacraments had words muttered without meaning, but words
spoken in a clear and intelligible voice. This is evident from circumcision,
the Passover, and the others, which did not lack a preached word declaring the
end for which they were instituted.

[Marginalia: 4. Because Christ preached the word.]

VII. 4. The sacramental word was preached openly by Christ when he addressed
the apostles. Therefore it must be preached by them to all believers, for what
Christ did for the disciples he wills pastors to do for the faithful. Thus
Paul testifies (1 Cor. 11:23) that he delivered to the Corinthians what he had
received from the Lord.

[Marginalia: 5. Because it is the word of faith.]

VIII. 5. The sacramental word is the word of faith, which must be preached,
since faith is from hearing. The sacramental word contains the promise, which
is the word of faith, to which faith is related as one correlate to its other.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

IX. The sacramental word is necessary (1) on account of the recipient, because
without it he would not receive the sacrament, and on account of those present,
because otherwise they would not participate in that sacrament. (2) Although a
recipient is sometimes not capable of instruction through preaching in a
present act, as an infant is not, he is nevertheless capable of it by a future
faculty, without which he undoubtedly ought not to be admitted to the
sacrament. This latter kind of capacity introduces the necessity of preaching,
for if it were not preached, no one would ever be capable, since what is not
preached signifies nothing. (3) Although the recipient is exceedingly learned,
he nevertheless needs the preached word, which is necessary not only for the
recipient’s instruction but also for the institution of the sacrament.

X. Although infants do not possess understanding with respect to its act, it
is sufficient that they possess it with respect to the capacity, so that the
sacramental word may be used in baptism. Thus in infant baptism the preaching
is directed both to the present instruction of the whole church, which
actually hears and understands, and to the future instruction of the infant
who is baptized. Nor is it

absurd for something to be spoken at this or that time that is not understood
until some time later, just as the apostles profitably heard many things from
Christ that they understood some time afterward.

XI. Although the sacramental word of baptism prescribed by the Lord has the
form of invocation and blessing, it does not follow that it lacks the force of
institution, because invocation and institution are not opposed, since one and
the same word has both forces. Add that invocation does not consist in mere
muttering. Thus Paul (1 Cor. 14) wishes prayers to be composed with
understanding, that is, with edification, and consequently with institution,
without which there is no edification. Again, the adversaries gratuitously
suppose that the words of baptism regard the act of baptizing and not the act
of teaching. They regard both: namely, the act of baptizing through teaching
and the act of teaching through baptizing, so that it may be evident at once
both that the washing with water is being applied and to what end.

XII. Most of the adversaries’ arguments commit an ignorance of the refutation,
as though by *proclamatory word* we understood the minister’s public sermon
expounding some passage of Scripture in an ecclesiastical assembly, which is
commonly accustomed to precede the celebration of the sacraments. But from
what was said above, it is evident that we understand nothing other than the
word of institution, which must be pronounced aloud by the minister for the
consecration of the signs. Thus we do not oppose the proclamatory word to the
consecratory, as the papists do, but rather subordinate it to the consecratory,
because every consecratory word rightly spoken is proclamatory, but,
conversely, not every proclamatory word is consecratory.

XIII. The principle of the word is not the same as that of the sacrament or
sign in the administration of baptism. Apart from its present contact with the
body, the element of water can have no use for the remainder of life, and
therefore it must touch the infant. But the principle of the word is different,
for it has its use not only in the one by whom it is heard in the present, but
also for the future through the instruction of the baptized person.

XIV. The arguments customarily brought forward for a consecratory word in the
papal sense, that is, an operative word, will be discussed below when the
efficacy of the sacraments is treated.
