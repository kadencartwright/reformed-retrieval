QUESTION VII

ON THE CANON

Has Any Canonical Book Perished?—Negative.

[Marginal heading: “Canon” is taken either broadly or narrowly.]

I. In order that the various questions raised about the canon of Scripture
may be resolved more easily, a distinction concerning the canon must be
premised. For this word is taken either broadly or narrowly. In the former
sense it was used by the fathers to designate ecclesiastical decrees and
constitutions, by which councils and rulers of churches used to define the
things which seemed to pertain to faith, morals, or “good order” (εὐταξίαν).
To this belong the various canons both of the universal church and of the
African church, the collection of canons of Burchard, Ivo, and Gratian, and
canon law itself, which is contained in the Code of Canons and distinguished
from divine law, which is included in the codex of Sacred Scripture. In the
latter sense the word is attributed “preeminently” (κατ’ ἐξοχὴν) to Scripture
alone, because God gave it to us to be the rule of faith and morals. In this
sense it is called by Irenaeus “the unbending canon of truth” (Κανὼν τῆς
ἀληθείας ἀκλινής), book 4, chapter 69; and by Chrysostom “the exact balance,
standard, and canon of all things” (ἀπάντων ἀκριβὴς ζυγὸς, καὶ γνώμων καὶ
κανὼν)—an accurate balance, standard, and rule of all things.

[Marginal heading: The canon of doctrines and of books.]

II. Now the Word of God can be considered in two ways: either as the doctrine
divinely revealed, or as the sacred books in which that doctrine is
contained. So there can be a twofold canon: one of doctrines, which embraces
all the fundamental doctrines, and the other of books, which contains all the
“God-breathed” books (θεοπνεύστους).

[Marginal heading: Why Scripture is called canonical.]

Scripture is called canonical for two reasons. First, with respect to its
doctrines, because it is the canon and rule of faith and morals, the term
being derived from the Hebrew קנה, which signifies a reed or measuring rod
and is so used in Galatians 6:16 and Philippians 3:16. Second, with respect to
its books, because it contains all the canonical books. In this sense
Athanasius at the beginning of his *Synopsis* warns that the books of
Christians are not infinite but finite and comprehended in a definite canon.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

III. The first question concerning the canon treats of its integrity: Has any
canonical book perished? That is, is the corpus of Scripture today deprived
of any book which God placed in the canon? On this question both the papists
and the orthodox divide into various parties. Most of the papists contend
that various canonical books have perished, so that they may thereby prove
the imperfection of Scripture and the necessity of traditions, with which
they fill

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 88 | IA leaf 142 | PDF p. 142]

its gaps. Some of our men, such as Musculus and Whitaker, following
Chrysostom, assert the same, but with a twofold difference. First, they affirm
this only of certain books of the Old Testament, not of the New as the papists
do. Second, they judge that the perfection of Scripture which the papists
attack on this ground loses nothing thereby, because they measure the
integrity of the canon not by the number of sacred books, or their
quantitative perfection, but by the fullness of doctrines and the essential
perfection of all the things necessary for salvation, which are found
abundantly in the books that remain. Nevertheless, the more common and
sounder opinion of others is that no truly canonical books have perished and
that, if any books did perish, they were not marked with this character.

[Marginal heading: That no canonical books have perished is proved from the testimony of Christ.]

IV. The reasons are drawn, 1. From the testimony of Christ, Matthew 5:18 and
Luke 16:17: “It is easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one
stroke of the law to fall.” But if not even a stroke, or the smallest letter,
can fall away, how could various canonical books have been lost? Although
Christ speaks directly about the doctrine of the law, not about its books,
his words can nevertheless be applied analogically to the sacred books to
assert their immunity from destruction. This is all the more so because he
not only mentions the letters and strokes by which Scripture is constituted,
but God also willed that this doctrine be preserved in written books.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the declaration of Luke and Paul.]

2. From the declaration of Luke and Paul. For Luke 24:27 could not have made
mention of “all the prophets” and “all the Scriptures” if some of its parts
had perished. Nor could Paul have asserted in Romans 15:4 that “whatever
things were written were written for our instruction” unless he supposed all
the writings of the Old Testament to survive.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the providence of God.]

V. 3. From the providence of God, which keeps perpetual watch for the
salvation of the church and cannot be conceived to have willed to permit so
ruinous a loss. Otherwise, what becomes of the wisdom, goodness, and power of
God if he willed that a treasure of such great value be shown to his church
but then withdrawn, and that the corpus of Scripture survive today torn and
mutilated?

[Marginal heading: 4. From the office of the church.]

4. From the office of the church, which is reverently to preserve and
diligently examine the oracles of God committed to it. That the church did
not fail in this office is evident even from the fact that neither Christ nor
the apostles ever accuse the Jews on this charge. If they had been guilty in
this matter, those who do not omit lesser offenses would certainly not have
passed over such sacrilege in silence. Indeed, Paul proclaims this privilege
of the Jews, that “the oracles of God” (λόγια τοῦ Θεοῦ) were entrusted to
them, Romans 3:2 and 9:4.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the end of Scripture.]

5. From the end of Scripture, which was appointed as the canon of faith and
life until the consummation of the age. This end could not be attained if,
through the loss of certain canonical books, a mutilated and truncated canon
had been left to the church—that is, one which could no longer be a canon.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the practice of the Jews.]

6. From the practice of the Jews, because no more books of the Old Testament
than those we have were ever acknowledged as canonical, expressed in the
Targums, or translated by the Septuagint.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VI. Not everything the men of God wrote is automatically divine and
“God-breathed” (θεόπνευστα). For they could compose some things as men, with
historical diligence, for an abundance of knowledge, and other things as
prophets, by divine inspiration, for the authority of religion. The former
can be judged with freedom; the latter must be believed of necessity, as
Augustine well says in *The City of God* 18.38. Just as not everything they
said was canonical, so neither was everything they wrote. Therefore, if
Solomon wrote various books of proverbs and songs, as well as about plants
and animals, 1 Kings 4:32–33,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 89 | IA leaf 143 | PDF p. 143]

it does not follow that they were canonical. For they could have been
composed by human diligence to attest the manifold knowledge of things with
which he was furnished, not as evidence of divine and supernatural
inspiration.

VII. The books which are said to have perished either are not sacred and
canonical—such as the *Book of the Wars of Jehovah* mentioned in Numbers
21:14 and Joshua 10:15; the *Book of the Upright*, 2 Samuel 1:14; and the
*Chronicles of the Kings of Judah and Israel*, 1 Kings 14:19–20 and 15:7.
These contained not the doctrine of religion but either political annals in
which the deeds of the Israelites were recorded, or registers of public acts
and political statutes, as is evident from 1 Kings 11:41. Or the books which
are said to have perished still survive under other names. Such are the books
of Nathan and Gad mentioned in 1 Chronicles 29:29; of Jehdo, 2 Chronicles
9:29; and of Shemaiah and Iddo, chapter 12:15. That these constitute a part of
the books of Samuel and Kings is taught by the Jews, noted by certain fathers,
and acknowledged by various papists of no obscure name, such as Sixtus of
Siena, Paul of Burgos, Luis de Tena, Sanctius, and others.

VIII. The *Book of Jehovah* in Isaiah 34:16 is nothing other than the prophecy
itself which he was writing in the name of the Lord and which he therefore
calls the book of Jehovah. The *Book of the Lamentations of Jeremiah* over the
death of King Josiah, mentioned in 2 Chronicles 35:25, is still read in
Lamentations.

IX. Colossians 4:16 does not say that there was an epistle of Paul to the
Laodiceans, because it speaks of an epistle “from Laodicea” (ἐκ Λαοδικείας),
not “to Laodicea” (πρὸς Λαοδίκειαν). It could have been an epistle written by
the Laodiceans to Paul in which there were some things it was important for
the Colossians to know; Paul wishes it to be read by them with his own.
Hence it is clear how groundlessly Faber Stapulensis wished to foist an
*Epistle to the Laodiceans* upon the Christian world, as the more judicious
papists have acknowledged.

X. Jude 14 makes no mention of a book of Enoch, but only of his prophecy,
because he is said to have prophesied, not to have written. That the book, if
there was one, was never placed in the canon is evident both from the silence
of Josephus and Jerome and from the fact that Moses is reckoned the first
canonical writer, Luke 24:27. It does indeed appear from Augustine, *The City
of God* 15.13, that an apocryphal book existed in those times whose title
falsely named Enoch as its author. We also owe a fragment of it to Scaliger in
his *Annotations on Eusebius*.

XI. If the apostles refer to certain passages from the Old Testament which
are now not read expressly in any canonical book, it does not follow that a
canonical book in which they stood has perished. Rather, they are either
contained implicitly and “according to the sense” (κατὰ διάνοιαν), as what is
said of Christ in Matthew 2:23, “He shall be called a Nazarene,” is taken
either from Isaiah 11:7 according to Jerome, where Christ is called a shoot,
or from Judges 13:5, where Samson, a type of Christ, is said to be a Nazarene
of God from the womb. Or, concerning what is said in 1 Corinthians 5:9 about
the epistle which he had written to them, nothing prevents us from
understanding that very epistle which he was writing and in which a little
earlier he had ordered that the man who had defiled himself with incest be
excommunicated. So also in Colossians 4:16, “when the epistle” (ἡ ἐπιστολὴ)
“has been read” means this epistle which he was composing. Or they are merely
historical reports, such as what Jude relates in verse 9 about the dispute of
the devil with Michael over the body of Moses. He could have obtained this
either from tradition, as some maintain, or from some ecclesiastical but
noncanonical book which perished.

XII. Although the autographs of the law and the prophets which were preserved
in the ark could have burned with it when the city was destroyed and the
temple burned at the time of the Babylonian captivity, it does not follow
that all the sacred books then perished and were restored anew by Ezra, as by
a second Moses, within forty days. Various copies could have remained among

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 90 | IA leaf 144 | PDF p. 144]

the pious, from which the worship of God was afterward restored, Ezra 6:18;
Nehemiah 8:2. Nor is it likely that Ezekiel and the pious priests, as well as
Jeremiah, Gedaliah, and Baruch, who had received the liberty of remaining in
Judea, lacked them, especially since the custody and reading of the sacred
books belonged to them. Concerning Daniel this appears plain from chapter
9:2. The passage in 4 Esdras 4:23 and 14:21, from which universal destruction
is inferred, proves nothing, since it is apocryphal even to the papists; and
it is refuted by another apocryphal book which is canonical to the papists, 1
Maccabees 2:4, which says that the ark in which the book of the law had been
deposited, Deuteronomy 31:26, was preserved by Jeremiah in a cave on Mount
Nebo. Above all, this invention is refuted by the profound silence of
Scripture. Since Scripture so anxiously laments the pollution of the
sanctuary, the destruction of Jerusalem, the seizure of the sacred vessels,
the ruin of the temple, and other things, it would not have passed over so
great a loss without public mourning. Ezra, therefore, could devote his labor
to collecting and even correcting and restoring copies which had been
corrupted during the captivity—which he could do most suitably as one who was
“God-breathed” (θεόπνευστος)—but there was no need for him to hand them anew
to the church.
