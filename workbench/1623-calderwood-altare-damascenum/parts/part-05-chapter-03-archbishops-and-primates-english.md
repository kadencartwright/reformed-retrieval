Insofar as they are archbishops:

1. In receiving appeals and issuing rescripts in reply to appeals lodged from
their suffragan bishops.

Downham would persuade us that the bishops of the same province were called
“suffragans” from casting votes in the election of metropolitans and, together
with the metropolitans themselves, in provincial synods celebrated by them.
The fallacy lies in the meaning of *suffragium*, as Lyndwood frankly reveals:
“They are called suffragans because they are bound to assist and support the
archbishop. For they are called to share the archbishop’s burden of care, not
the fullness of his power.” They could not have been called suffragans from
casting votes together with the metropolitans in provincial synods,

[Marginalia: *Defense*, book 2, chapter 6, p. 113; book 1, title *On
Constitutions*, chapter *And Below*.]

[1623 printed p. 61 | IA leaf 73 | PDF p. 73]

since provincial councils had already fallen into disuse; nor from casting
votes in episcopal elections, since election had already been transferred to
the canons alone or to the prince and the canons. When a presidency of order
was changed into a degree of command and power, and bishops began to be
subjected to the metropolitan, they were then called suffragans. We see how
dangerous it is to tolerate a permanent presidency in the church’s
aristocratic government. Under English law, therefore, one may appeal from a
bishop to an archbishop. But formerly a metropolitan could do nothing in
another’s diocese without the judgment of the bishops of the province.
Anicetus writes to the bishops of Gaul: “If any of the metropolitans becomes
puffed up and, without the presence of all the bishops of the province or the
counsel of the bishops, attempts to conduct either their causes or any other
causes—except only those belonging to his own parish—or wishes to burden them,
let him be severely corrected by all, lest he presume to do such things
thereafter.” Pope Hyginus says: “The privilege of the Roman church being
preserved in all things, let no metropolitan hear anyone’s causes without the
insistence of the other bishops of the province, because proceedings will
otherwise be void unless argued in the sight of them all; and if he does so,
let him be restrained by his brothers.” The Council of Antioch says: “It was
resolved that a council of bishops be held twice a year in each province; in
it presbyters and deacons ought also to be present, and all who consider
themselves injured should await the synod’s judgment.” An aggrieved man appealed
from the bishop to the synod of bishops, not to the metropolitan. The Council
of Sardica did indeed establish: “A presbyter or deacon who has been deposed by
a bishop shall have the power to take refuge with the metropolitan bishop of
that province.” But “we rightly regard these canons as suspect,” says
Sutlivius, who, though not the most learned of the hierarchists, is nevertheless
the most venomous. Spalato likewise says: “The canons of Sardica were never
received by the universal church. Hence the ancient body of canons of the
universal church, which the East always used, contained no canon of Sardica.”
Justinian did indeed enact this law: “If the clergy or anyone else brings an
action against a bishop on any cause whatever, his case is to be judged before
the metropolitan according to the sacred rules and our laws. If anyone
contests the judgments, the case is to be referred to the archbishop and
patriarch of that diocese, who is to judge it according to the canons and
laws.” But Justinian’s laws have no weight with us insofar as they disagree
with God’s word. The First Council of Constantinople decreed that one injured
by a bishop “should first bring his accusation before all the bishops of that
province; but if they are not competent to correct the crimes charged against
the bishop, it commands them then to approach the greater

[Marginalia: Cause 9, question 3, chapter 6; Cause 9, question 3, chapter 4;
canon 19; Sardica, canon 15; *On the Pontiff*, book 3, chapter 13, p. 256;
Spalato, *On the Ecclesiastical Commonwealth*, book 4, chapter 8, section 34;
Novel 123, chapter 22; Constantinople I, canon 6.]

[1623 printed p. 62 | IA leaf 74 | PDF p. 74]

council of bishops of that diocese.” Here an appeal from a lesser to a greater
council is granted. But later, at the Council of Chalcedon, which occurred
about the year of Christ 455, when everything was gradually turning for the
worse, it was decreed: “If a cleric has business or a dispute with his own or
another bishop, let him be judged by the provincial synod. But if a bishop or
cleric has a controversy with the metropolitan of his province, let him go to
the exarch of the diocese or the throne of the imperial city of
Constantinople and litigate before him.” Here appeal to an exarch or patriarch
was granted, but not to a metropolitan apart from the provincial synod. Yet it
makes no difference whether this subordination of appeals—from bishop to
metropolitan and from metropolitan to exarch or patriarch—was introduced by
the fathers in some ecumenical council or by Justinian to exalt the
metropolitan, since it introduced a destructive change in ecclesiastical
government. Bilson asserts that this change was rightly introduced into the
church and should not be borne with displeasure. “They saw,” he says, “the
intolerable expense and trouble by which the bishops of each province were
almost exhausted while they remained at synods until they settled all the
private disputes and complaints arising in each province. They perceived no
reason why the bishops of the whole world should twice a year be troubled
about the manifold disputes of certain men. Rather, they observed that by
this means lawsuits would receive either long delay or a superficial
examination and a hasty judgment.” A little below he says: “How unjust and
intolerable it would be for all the bishops of this realm to assemble in one
place and expend not only labor but money in hearing, examining, and judging
as many controversies, injuries, and disputes as are now conducted in all the
consistories of England.” Provincial councils were celebrated annually even
after Justinian’s time, much more in Justinian’s age when that law was passed.
The ancients did not yet plead labor or expense, as Bilson imagines. When it
was decreed that they should celebrate a synod only once a year, they gave
reasons other than expense and labor. The Council in Trullo commands that,
“since the rulers of the church cannot celebrate synods twice a year because
of barbarian invasions and other intervening causes, a synod shall by all
means be held once a year in each province.” Later the bishops did indeed
suffer from idleness and sloth and alleged the length of journeys, as at the
Second Council of Nicæa: “Because of the hardship and because those compelled
to travel had insufficient means to complete the journey, it determined that
this be done once a year; and if the metropolitan does not take care that it
be done, he is to be subjected to canonical penalties.” Yet, as you see, even
the superstitious fathers of Nicæa did not entirely abolish fixed provincial
councils as the English and papist bishops have. They seek the honor and
dignity of the episcopate but flee its burden and labor. In their courts and
palaces,

[Marginalia: Chalcedon, canon 9; *On Government*, chapter 16, pp. 462–463;
canon 8.]

[1623 printed p. 63 | IA leaf 75 | PDF p. 75]

dissolved in pleasures and reclining at ease, they lazily idle amid pomp and
claim that a lion is in the path. Funds are available for display and nothing
is lacking, but when they are summoned to labor and the church’s business,
nothing is available. The province of Canterbury extends almost as widely as
the realm itself. But the bishops’ dioceses ought to be contracted, and the
metropolitans’ provinces fitted and proportioned to the convenience of
synods, if they wished to consult the church’s welfare instead of enlarging
their own dignity. Moreover, they would not have to expend labor and money in
hearing, examining, and judging as many controversies and causes as are
conducted in all the consistories of England if they refused to meddle in
civil causes. “Perhaps,” says Bilson, “you would remove from synods the
judgments concerning tithes, wills, legacies, betrothals, marriages, and
similar causes and admit only those pertaining to the restraint of offenses.”
Why would we not remove them, since our power concerns crimes and offenses,
not possessions or civil property, as Bernard speaks? The clamor of civil
causes must be kept away from the church. “The jurists contend among
themselves in innumerable subtleties about what the laws require in appeals,
how they are to be pursued, how stayed, what is to be done about attempted
acts, and similar things. We know that, according to 1 Corinthians 11, God’s
church has no custom of creating disputes, except after it became almost
entirely temporal and restless jurists ignorant of theology reached the
papacy.” Furthermore, the archbishop himself does not hear the cases brought
to the court they call the Court of Arches. Rather, a dean presides over the
Arches Court and answers all appeals in the province of Canterbury, whence he
is called the Dean of Arches. “There are sixteen or more advocates in this
court at the archbishop’s pleasure, all doctors of law, two scribes, and ten
proctors,” Camden reports in his *Britannia* where he treats the English
tribunals. “It is right,” says Bilson, “that they have assistants, unless it
pleases you that they spend more time learning human laws than examining the
Holy Scriptures. You therefore seem inept to me when you inveigh so sharply
against the Court of Arches and the other consistories of ecclesiastical
jurisdiction.” He certainly pours out absurdities when he assigns to
ecclesiastics causes which they are altogether unfit to know and handle and
whose examination they must cast upon others through ignorance or the great
mass of business, as the pope does upon his auditors. It is right that your
canon lawyers be dismissed, civil causes returned to competent judges, and
ecclesiastics handle only ecclesiastical matters in ecclesiastical order. It
should be noted in passing that this court’s president is called a dean
because he exercises jurisdiction over thirteen London parishes exempt from
the bishop of London’s jurisdiction; and he is Dean of Arches because the
tower of the church of the chief parish is built with arches.

[Marginalia: *On Government*, chapter 16, p. 463; Spalato, book 3, chapter 8,
section 13; p. 481.]

We therefore admit purely ecclesiastical consistories and no others. “Yet,”
says Bilson, “experience itself teaches how long causes remain pending in the
greatest courts of ecclesiastical jurisdiction because of their number and
magnitude, although the judges are accustomed to devote a great portion of
the whole year—the lawful days, as they call them—to rendering judgment.
Would it not be a work most worthy of praise for you to cause pastors
established throughout the province to remain perpetually in one place and
attend only to appeals, which would be frequently lodged from every quarter?”
England has thirty-nine counties, yet only one praetorium at Westminster, in
which courts both of law and equity sit and hear cases at fixed times called
lawful, judicial, or court days; these four seasons of the year scarcely make
up three months. Yet the commonwealth suffers no harm during the intervals in
which the courts are closed. In the counties there are sheriffs and barons who
hear lesser causes in their courts, and judges in the larger cities and towns
where disputes involve even greater sums of money, the freedom to appeal to
the supreme tribunals being preserved. If consistories were restored to
parishes, classical presbyteries to deaneries, synods to counties, and
national councils to all in common; if chancellors, officials, archdeacons,
and the rest of that rabble were banished; and if ecclesiastics handled only
ecclesiastical causes in ecclesiastical order, the fixed councils and the
provincial synods celebrated twice a year would rarely be troubled by appeals.
We know this from experience in our church. Appeals from parish consistories
and classis meetings held every week to the county synod were infrequent, and
from the synod to the national council celebrated once a year, very rare. The
other Reformed churches, where national councils are less frequent, have
learned the same by experience. It is no wonder that appeals are more often
lodged from your officials, chancellors, and archdeacons. But we consider it
utterly unjust to appeal from one bishop to another as an individual judge
superior in jurisdiction, much less from a classis meeting to a bishop or
archbishop. “It would be absurd to think that God wished to deny his Spirit to
many and inspire only one,” as the African fathers wrote to Pope Celestine
concerning appeals. Spalato acknowledges that appeals multiplied chiefly
because of civil causes, not offenses: “Thus the causes of the subjects of
suffragans are first transferred to the metropolitans, especially after
forensic judgments and a secular procedure were introduced into the church.”
Bilson asks why synods must be given the trouble when cases can be dispatched
with less labor by an archbishop. Synods were once needed to make
constitutions; since the canons have now been made, fixed synods are no longer
needed.

[Marginalia: *On Government*, chapter 16, p. 463; *On the Ecclesiastical
Commonwealth*, book 3, chapter 8, section 1.]

[1623 printed p. 65 | IA leaf 77 | PDF p. 77]

“Synods are necessary in the church for many causes,” says Beza, “both to
preserve agreement, to seek remedies by common counsel for evils befalling all,
and also to provide for those who do not acquiesce in the judgment of their
particular presbyteries and think themselves injured.” Saravia is not so
unjust as Bilson: “I readily agree with Dr. Beza concerning the necessity of
synods.” Synods were formerly convened not only to enact canons but also “to
correct each matter that arose,” as the Council of Chalcedon says. “For
ecclesiastical causes, to resolve controversies which arise, and that all who
consider themselves injured may await the synodical judgment,” said the
fathers of Antioch. “That all questions may be examined in common,” said the
earlier fathers of Nicæa; “that canonical inquiries may be made through
assemblies of bishops,” said the later fathers of Nicæa. The power not only to
direct by canons but also to govern according to them must not be entrusted to
one man. Otherwise we shall change the aristocratic government of the
province’s churches which we have hitherto posited and proved into a monarchy.
Monarchs too govern subjects according to enacted laws and nevertheless
remain monarchs. An archbishop can be carried away by passions and exercise
tyranny in executing canons no less than in making them. In chapter one we
showed that the English metropolitan alone with the prince can also compose
ordinances and definitions—the forms of service—and articles concerning
ecclesiastical discipline and order. It is sport and amusement to observe
Bilson’s wondrous artifice in fabricating his metropolitans and archbishops.
At the beginning of chapter 16 he discusses at length the usefulness and
necessity of synods to show that his metropolitan is useful and necessary;
but immediately he unravels the web he was weaving and removes the usefulness
and necessity of synods. Indeed, he alleges many inconveniences and
difficulties to introduce an archbishop who settles disputes and rules the
province without a synod. That is, he lowers the metropolitan to raise the
archbishop and commands the archbishop to shut out the metropolitan’s soul.
For at the beginning of the chapter he established an equal necessity for
metropolitans and synods. He began to fashion an amphora; as the wheel turns,
why does a pitcher emerge?

2. In visiting the whole province according to the laws and customs.

Although an English archbishop visits rarely through others and scarcely ever
in person, he can nevertheless visit the churches of his province as their
ordinary judge, even for correction. Whitgift of Canterbury says that when an
archbishop visits, “he cannot make new laws or prescribe new

[Marginalia: *On the Threefold Episcopate*, p. 91; Council of Chalcedon, canon
19; Antioch, canon 20; Nicæa I, canon 5; Nicæa II, canon 6; *Reply*, p. 439.]

[1623 printed p. 66 | IA leaf 78 | PDF p. 78]

rites unless commanded by a superior power. He merely inquires whether the
laws and constitutions which bishops and others are bound to obey are
observed; nor can he decree anything without them except what is granted by
their consent and the authority of the laws and the prince, or do anything in
their dioceses except what they themselves are bound to perform.” By the
bishops’ consent he means their voluntary surrender or approval in Parliament
when the archbishops’ power was sanctioned by laws. For it is sufficiently
clear that he can visit the province against a bishop’s will and without
consulting him and meddle in causes properly belonging to his suffragans. They
take refuge in the laws of the realm and the prince’s edicts or authority
when they cannot defend themselves otherwise. They are accustomed to cut
Gordian knots with the sword in this way, not untie them by arguments. We
demand the repeal of human laws opposed to divine law, and ask not what
princes can do in fact but what they can do by right. If episcopal government
is divinely instituted, as they claim and as we still suppose, they will never
show that an archbishop with power to visit the province and correct the
excesses and defects of suffragan prelates rests either upon divine law or the
church’s ancient polity. We heard Anicetus and Hyginus above; let us also hear
Calixtus: “Let no primate or metropolitan presume to excommunicate or judge a
diocesan’s church or parish, or anyone from his parish, or do anything without
his counsel and judgment. Rather, let that be observed which was established
by the apostles, fathers, and our predecessors and confirmed by us: If any
metropolitan bishop attempts to act outside his own parish—except in a matter
belonging only to it—without the counsel and will of all the bishops of the
province, he will endanger his rank, and his act shall be regarded as null and
void. Whatever must be done or arranged concerning the causes of the bishops
of the province, the needs of their churches, clergy, and laity, is to be done
by the common consent of the bishops of the province, not with any pomp of
domination but with most humble and concordant administration.” The Council
of Trent decreed: “Even after metropolitans have fully visited their own
diocese, let them not visit the cathedral churches or dioceses of their
provinces unless the cause has been examined and approved in a provincial
council.” The Tridentine fathers are therefore sounder than the English in
reducing a metropolitan’s power. Our adversaries also say: “During an
archbishop’s visitation the ordinary’s jurisdiction is suspended; he can
exercise no jurisdiction except that derived from the archbishop and
exercised as his delegate.” An archbishop can not only inquire but also
correct when he visits; indeed, he can remove the suffragan bishop himself
from office and rank, as Cartwright testifies. Whitgift answers: “Neither a
metropolitan nor all the bishops together can deprive a bishop of authority

book 2, chapter 6, pp. 112–113; *Reply*, p. 440.]

[1623 printed p. 67 | IA leaf 79 | PDF p. 79]

without the prince’s consent.” It is enough that, with the prince’s consent,
he alone can remove a fellow bishop from office without the other bishops.
Among the papists, says Sylvester, “an archbishop can take cognizance of a
bishop’s crime, although he cannot proceed to his deposition, which is
reserved to the pope alone.” The Council of Antioch decreed: “If a bishop is
judged concerning certain crimes and the bishops of the province happen to
disagree about him, some considering the accused innocent and others guilty,
the holy synod resolved, in order to settle the whole controversy, that the
metropolitan should summon other judges from a neighboring province, so that
whatever appears just may be approved by them together with the bishops of the
province.” You see that the metropolitan could not settle the disagreement by
his vote and decide about the bishop; neighboring bishops had to be summoned.
Likewise, at the synod of Constantinople celebrated under Honorius and
Arcadius: “A matter adjudged—that is, a bishop—ought not to be deposed by
three, nor by two, but by the judgment of a greater council and of those who
belong to the same province.” Field admits that the ancient canons require the
consent of at least twelve bishops for a bishop’s deprivation and degradation.
The archbishop has jurisdiction throughout the province not only during a
visitation but also outside it. Whitgift asserts that a metropolitan can
exercise jurisdiction only in exempt cases. But what he cannot do as
archbishop by ordinary power, he can do as the king’s commissioner by
extraordinary power. Spalato, excessive in exalting his lesser pope, the
archbishop, also rejects the qualification concerning exempt cases. “Thus,”
he says, “the jurists’ qualification by which they restrict this power of the
metropolitan to the matters expressed in the laws is empty.” He wishes the
metropolitan, as universal guardian of the canons and avenger of corrupt
customs, promoter of the whole province’s common good, corrector of the
suffragans, and ordinary judge, to rise against a delinquent in the name of
the bishops of the province wherever and whenever offenses occur. Whatever
the whole church can do against its bishops, he wishes the metropolitan to be
able to do against his suffragans. Spalato himself proved by many arguments
that all bishops are equal by divine law, and above we showed that in earlier
centuries the metropolitan could do nothing without the bishops of his
province. After many centuries, either the arrogance of the metropolitans or
the sloth and indolence of the bishops of the province transferred to the
metropolitan the power of appeals, visitations, and correction in certain
cases. The provincial synods at last fell into disuse while bishops indulged
in leisure in their vast dioceses and broad estates. This was the collapse of
ancient discipline, to be lamented rather than imitated. Those who thrust a
little antichrist upon us—why do they not also embrace the Roman bishop as a

book 5, chapter 30; *Reply*, p. 464; book 3, chapter 8, section 14.]

greater antichrist, if not as universal bishop, at least as patriarch of the
West, with the same power over archbishops which they grant archbishops over
suffragans, and as avenger and guardian of the canons of general councils? If
he is unequal to governing the universal church as universal bishop, he will
nevertheless not be unequal to governing the churches of the West as
patriarch. The Council of Chalcedon permitted appeal from a metropolitan to
the exarch of the larger diocese or to the patriarch of Constantinople; but
the patriarch of Constantinople was second in dignity to the Roman patriarch.
Zypaeus says that the Council of Trent altered the jurisdiction of
archbishops insofar as they were bishops’ ordinary judges, so that now the
pope is their judge in serious criminal causes and the provincial council in
lesser ones. “In these matters perhaps ancient law was departed from, so that
the matter might be examined more seriously by several bishops together. In
addition, difficulties can sometimes arise between archbishops and bishops
because of appeals and similar matters, on account of which they might perhaps
yield somewhat to passion. However it may be, the authority of archbishops is
reduced here, as also in chapter three, so that even after archbishops have
visited their whole diocese they may not visit those of their suffragans
unless the cause has been examined in a provincial council—although an
archbishop’s visitation had ceased to be practiced before the council.” You
see what Zypaeus gathers from the reform decrees in sessions 13 and 24 of the
Council of Trent. We do not approve what accrues to the pope from the
reduction of the metropolitan’s power. But insofar as it is restored to the
provincial council, we acknowledge the restoration of ancient discipline and
judge the Tridentine fathers more equitable than the English. In its
declarations the sacred congregation of cardinals who interpret the canons
and decrees of the Council of Trent explains a decree of session 13 as
follows: “An archbishop ought not to intrude himself at first instance into
causes conducted before his suffragan.” An English archbishop can intrude
himself into any cause, whether already conducted or not yet begun before his
suffragan, by ordinary or extraordinary delegated power, and exercise every
kind of ecclesiastical jurisdiction over suffragans and their subjects.
Therefore, in power and malice he surpasses any papist archbishop, whether the
old kind or the new Tridentine kind, enriched with the spoils of the Roman
pontiff and armed with the power of both swords in the legatine court.

Insofar as they are primates or lesser patriarchs:

1. By law, to receive appeals from inferior judgments even when the
intermediate court is bypassed. 2. By prescriptive custom, to have custody of
ecclesiastical jurisdiction whenever an episcopal see in their province is
vacant.

The two English primates, Canterbury and York, were equal not only in power,
dignity, and office but also in the number of suffragans until King William’s
time, as Godwin reports from Stubbs concerning the archbishops of York. He also
relates that by decree of the king and nobles, with the Roman pontiff’s
permission, the province of York was separated from Canterbury by the Humber
and confined within very narrow limits. The archbishops long disputed the
primacy until it was finally decreed that York be called Primate of England
and Canterbury Primate of All England. They still delight in these titles and
their foolish distinction. Appeals may also be made to these primates while
bypassing the intermediate court. Spalato’s words about immediate appeal to
the pope can be applied here: “Our lawyers have now made it an axiom that one
may bypass the intermediate court and fly immediately to the apostolic see,”
that is, bypassing the metropolitan. So here one may bypass the intermediate
court and appeal to them as primates; the documents make this distinction.
Matthias Stephani says immediate appeals were devised to satisfy avarice: “By
canon law an appeal may be made to the pope alone, bypassing every intermediate
court. They established this to draw every question into their own
jurisdiction and glut their avarice, because clerics above others strain to
heap up money, contrary to common honesty and their faith pledged to God.” So
the lesser popes—the primates called lesser patriarchs—arrange by several
routes, mediately or immediately, to transfer into their courts disputes and
causes argued in inferior courts. Even Innocent IV, in the Sext, title *On
Appeals*, disapproves appeals from archdeacons and other inferior prelates and
their officials to the archbishop while bypassing the suffragans. What we said
above about appeals can be pressed especially against immediate ones. A
primate’s custody of spiritual jurisdiction in any diocese of his province
during a vacancy diminishes the rights of the chapter and the authority of the
churches in the diocese, for the chapter succeeds the bishop by ordinary
right. “Immediately upon the bishop’s death,

1, chapter 3.]

until another is substituted, the whole, full, and complete ordinary
government of that church—except ordination and acts proper to the pontifical
order—belongs to the whole college of presbyters, that is, the chapter, not of
canons but of presbyters,” says Spalato. In book three he likewise asserts
that the pope can by right do nothing more in vacant churches of his province:
he cannot take administration from the chapter and entrust it to another
except in a case of negligence. That qualification was not an ancient
institution but an addition of discipline corrupted under Antichrist. We
shall discuss the chapter’s power in its place.

According to the position peculiar to Canterbury in the ecclesiastical
estate: 1. Every bishop of his province whom he confirms must furnish him one
chaplain until he provides the chaplain a sufficient benefice. 2. As Primate
of All England he can grant letters of protection by which an appellant may
pursue his appeal without meanwhile being molested.

The archbishop of Canterbury’s dignity in the English hierarchy is very
great. He excels the other bishops and can no less now than when Urban II
called him patriarch of another world—or more truly, pope. Although he has
laid aside the title of legate by birth which he received from the pontiff and
enjoys only the designation of primate and metropolitan of all England, his
dignity and power are no less than in earlier centuries. In the prince’s
legatine court he can exercise another legatine jurisdiction with the
sanction of both swords, as we said. Only two peculiar prerogatives are listed
here. Having chaplains is common to him and other bishops, but it is peculiar
to him that a suffragan whom he confirms must furnish a chaplain until he
provides him a benefice. Bleynianus says: “Christians allowed altars only in
churches until, by a special privilege, princes were permitted oratories at
home with altars and petty sacrifices. At last this permission was extended
even to private men, provided episcopal authority intervened.” “From all this
it is clear,” says Filesac, “how poorly they care for themselves and their
souls who, through some pride, disdain, or supine negligence, spurn the public
assembly of the parish mass. Some allege a solicited—or more truly, unjustly
extorted—episcopal indulgence or dispensation by which they contend that they
may hear the rite in private oratories.”

Many bishops of Gaul rightly condemned this corruption in their diocesan
synods. Filesac adds a diocesan decree of William, bishop of Angers, from 1314.
What they say about the mass is rightly affirmed of the true worship of God.
If nobles are members of a parish church, they ought to assemble for public
worship with the other members. But bishops’ possession of crowds of
chaplains to preach in their chapels or elsewhere at their pleasure shows
that they indulge excessively not only the pride and leisure of others but
also their own, and contend with magnates in every way lest anything appear
lacking from their display. The chaplains of bishops and magnates either hold
benefices or hold none but seek them. They attach themselves to a bishop or
noble chiefly to obtain a benefice through his work. Once they acquire a
benefice with cure of souls they do not care for the flock, but remain in the
nobles’ courts, while no one breaks bread to the hungry in the parish. A
bishop is as guilty of this crime as anyone else. The crowd of chaplains seems
to have arisen because strict canon law bound a holder of a benefice with cure
to reside, leaving no one to conduct household rites. It was devised that
they should have several chaplains who ministered by annual, half-yearly, or
quarterly turns in the chapels and oratories of nobles, while the flocks had
to feed upon their own sap. Those turns were not always observed, and the
matter ended in an open profession of perpetual nonresidence. We shall soon
say more about chaplains’ nonresidence and plural benefices. Canterbury’s
other peculiar right, granting letters of protection to an appellant, would
not be objectionable if it were not peculiar to him and if mediate and
immediate appeals to an archbishop had been lawfully introduced. For although
an appellant’s malice must be restrained, one who appeals lawfully ought to
be protected against an inferior’s insolence in defense of innocence.

According to the place they hold in the civil estate, either common to both
or peculiar to one. The place common to both is either by common municipal law
or by grant of princes; by common municipal law, either in ecclesiastical or
civil matters.

In ecclesiastical matters they have this prerogative: to approve and register
wills and grant administration of intestate estates to successors whenever
the deceased had notable goods in different

[1623 printed p. 72 | IA leaf 84 | PDF p. 84]

dioceses or jurisdictions of their province.

The recent canons of the provincial council of 1603 define notable goods as
five English pounds. The archbishop has a tribunal called the Prerogative
Court, “in which,” says Camden, “a commissioner renders judgment concerning
inheritances transferred by intestacy or will.” If the deceased had no
notable goods outside the diocese where he died, the proof of the will—that
is, the reception of evidence by witnesses or instruments—its approval or
decree of validity, its registration or publication and copying into the
judge’s acts, the commission to administer an intestate’s goods, and the bond
for faithful administration belong to the diocesan bishop’s chancellor,
commissioner, or official, not the archbishop. But if he had notable goods in
different dioceses, the chancellor, commissioner, official, or anyone else
exercising ecclesiastical jurisdiction dismisses those cited to his court
concerning proof of the will or administration of the goods, or those who
come voluntarily, after first administering an oath. He “shall openly and
expressly profess that this matter belongs to the archiepiscopal prerogative
of the same province, and shall warn and exhort the party to prove the will or
seek administration of the goods before the judge of the said Prerogative,
and within forty consecutive days exhibit before him such proof or
administration authenticated with the seal of the Prerogative Court. The
registrar of every inferior judge shall also, without difficulty, inform the
apparitor of the Prerogative Court—coming to him only once each month—which
executors or applicants for administration his judge dismissed to the
Prerogative Court in the preceding month because his jurisdiction was
incompetent in this respect.” These are the words of constitution 93 in their
translation. From them the reader can discern that the Prerogative Court is
not properly ecclesiastical but civil. Titles concerning wills, legacies,
trusts, and successions in civil law show that emperors formerly entrusted
these purely civil causes to their prefects and governors. Lyndwood says
concerning registration of wills: “Registration or publication of wills is
forbidden to clerics by law, and the cited laws give the reason: it is a
disgrace for clerics to wish to show themselves skilled in forensic business.”
In the same place: “By English custom this publication belongs to
ecclesiastical judges, but it is otherwise at common law.” Again: “The liberty
to approve such matters rests upon royal consent granted from antiquity by the
king and nobles.” Secular judges, then, supposedly cannot determine whether
an earlier will is invalidated

[Marginalia: canon 93; book 3, title *On Wills*, chapter *Likewise, Because of
the Laity*; following chapter.]

[1623 printed p. 73 | IA leaf 85 | PDF p. 85]

by a later one, void, broken, or null; whether a legacy is perpetual or for a
term, absolute or conditional; whether a condition is honest or possible;
whether the deceased died incapable of making a will or intestate, an adult
or a minor. Can those who judge immovable property not judge movable property?
Can those who discern lunatics and madmen in other causes not determine
whether the deceased was insane or deranged at the end? Can they not discover
by evidence and witnesses whether he was deaf? What prevents a legatee from
pursuing legacies by a writ from Chancery any more than a creditor pursues a
trustee or testamentary executor by such a writ in the testator’s or
deceased’s name? If testamentary causes were transferred to episcopal hearing
because of bishops’ piety, the same reason requires that all civil and
criminal causes be committed to them and that they be entangled in secular
business. If the person’s piety and virtue are chosen, moreover, why do they
appoint deputy judges and cast the burden on others? Can they communicate
piety and integrity to officials, commissioners, and chancellors? Finally,
those holy by office are not always godly; and who is more wicked than a
degenerate cleric wandering outside his proper sphere?

In civil matters:

1. To bear the title “Your Grace,” which we call *Grace* in English.

Each archbishop is adorned with “My Lord’s Grace,” while a bishop bears “My
Lord.” In England the title *Grace* is communicated only to dukes, not to
earls, marquesses, or others of inferior noble rank. Nor does *My Lord* denote
any except nobles or those distinguished by some secular dignity; it has a
different meaning from the Latin word in mutual salutations among learned
men. By the reason they are distinguished with these titles, they might also
be adorned with those of duke, marquess, earl, and viscount, since these are
now designations not of magistracies as formerly but of dignities. Indeed,
the bishop of Durham is regarded as a count palatine. Camden says that from
William the Norman’s time his bishopric was considered a county palatine and
the bishop possessed royal rights, so that the goods of outlaws fell to him,
not the king. “Recently,” he says, “James Pilkington the bishop summoned Queen
Elizabeth to law over the goods of Charles Neville, earl of Westmorland, and
others outlawed in that region who had raised an impious war against their
country; and he would have pursued the cause had not the authority of
Parliament intervened

[Marginalia: Camden, *Britannia*, Frankfurt edition 1616, p. 600.]

[1623 printed p. 74 | IA leaf 86 | PDF p. 86]

and awarded the matter for that time to the queen, because at very great
expense she had rescued both bishop and bishopric from the rebels’ jaws.”
Another bishop is called prelate of the most noble Order of the Garter, as
Travers reports. Edward III enrolled this most noble fellowship of knights to
adorn military valor with honors, rewards, and splendor. From the garter given
as their token in a battle that ended happily he named them Knights of the
Garter. They fasten around the left leg a blue garter inscribed in gold, as
Camden reports. The most powerful Christian princes have been admitted to
this fellowship, and in our age certain English bishops were enrolled in it.
This Prelate of the Garter bears great responsibility at the feasts of St.
George, when Bibles are carried before the knights in place of the cross, as
the authors of the admonition to Parliament say. What we said above about
seats and ranks in the civil empire is also true of titles of dignity. Some
Roman imperial magistrates were adorned with the title “illustrious”—the
praetorian prefect, city prefect, count of the sacred largesses, and so forth;
others with “respectable”—the proconsul, count of the East, and Augustal
prefect; others with “most distinguished”—the praetor and others. Bishops
either sought or eagerly accepted the same epithets and began to be styled and
greeted “illustrious,” like senators of the first order. From the
*Clementines*, Chassanaeus gathers in part 4, consideration 25, of his
*Catalogue of the Glory of the World* that they were to be called “most
serene.” Even before this they addressed their letters “to the most honored
lord,” “to the most blessed lord,” and “to the most reverend brother.”
Socrates censures this custom in the preface to book six: “Those who hold
ecclesiastical persons in the highest honor will accuse us because we do not
call bishops ‘most dear to God,’ ‘most holy,’ or similar names. Others who are
somewhat more curious will sometimes fault us because we do not call
emperors ‘most divine,’ ‘lords,’ or other titles men customarily give them.
But since I can clearly prove from ancient writers that in their books a
servant addressed his lord by his proper name, I shall serve the truth as I
ought and utterly despise those titles of dignity.” Christ prohibited his
disciples worldly titles of dignity, saying in Luke 22:25: “The kings of the
Gentiles lord it over them, and those who exercise authority over them are
called Benefactors.” He does not wish the apostles adorned with the splendid
titles borne by the kings and rulers of nations and kingdoms. Nor does Christ
prohibit only the title *Benefactor*, with which two

[Marginalia: *On the Discipline of the Church*, fol. 59; Clementines, *On
Election*, chapter 1, *In Most Cases*.]

[1623 printed p. 75 | IA leaf 87 | PDF p. 87]

kings of Egypt were honored, but by synecdoche every such title used by kings
who rule and inferior magistrates who exercise power over nations. The title
*Grace*, proper only to princes and dukes, vies in magnificence with
*Benefactor*: the latter displays munificence, the former clemency and favor.
It is therefore ridiculous that some claim Christ forbade them to be greeted
and addressed as Benefactors but not as Gracious and Clement Lords. Bilson
says Christ “forbade them ambitiously to seek or greedily receive honor
otherwise due to them.” But earlier he said Christ commanded that “his
disciples neither seek nor expect the splendid titles of princes, which by no
means suit the calling of prophets and apostles, but leave to the powers of
this age magnificence both of words and things.” When we press further that if
the less pompous title *Benefactor* is denied to apostles, much more the
inflated titles Gracious or Clement Lord, he answers: “The name Benefactor
ought not to be denied to priest or anyone among the people, provided he has
in fact been liberal to the commonwealth or private persons. But for anyone to
seek it when he is far removed from the praise of beneficence and kindness,
since that reeks of desire for empty glory, is equally unlawful for princes
and pastors.” He cannot remain in one place but speaks contradictions. Christ
forbids using the very titles used by gentile kings and princes, whether or
not they have actually fulfilled what the titles imply. Otherwise the title
“Most Serene” must be denied princes who are anything but serene, and no
archbishop who is a harsh and cruel tyrant—as the English archbishops are—may
be adorned with “Gracious” or “Clement,” although all receive *Grace*. No one
among the people or ecclesiastical order may assume or accept these titles,
even if truly munificent or gracious. Princes are called munificent and
benefactors because they can exercise such bounty toward others. Hence the
Hebrews call princes *Nedibim*, “munificent and liberal men,” by antonomasia,
as Whitgift admits, corresponding to *Benefactors*. Titles are not always
given because of virtues but because of secular dignity and rank. Names of
virtues are often used where no true virtue exists, yet titles displaying
power and majesty are more fitting than names derived from moral virtues.
When Christ prohibited lordship and power, he condemned under the single name
*Benefactor* all titles which princes rightly or wrongly assume to display
that rule and power. The same judgment applies to *My Lord*. The objection
from Elijah, 1 Kings 18, and Elisha, 2 Kings 2, does not help. Rebekah calls
Abraham’s servant lord in Genesis 24:18;

[Marginalia: *On Government*, chapter 6, p. 98; p. 94; *Reply*, p. 63.]

Sarah calls her husband lord in Genesis 18:12; the Greeks call Philip lord in
John 12:21; and Mary Magdalene calls the man she thought the gardener lord in
John 20:15. Hebrew *adonai* and Greek *kyrios* were not titles of secular
dignity among eastern peoples but respectful addresses by which persons even
of modest fortune courteously honored one another. Hence *kyrios* was often
used in eastern bishops’ letters, with the honorific epithets “most honored,”
“most reverend,” and so forth—and sometimes, though foolishly, “most blessed”
and “most holy.” So learned men in our age address letters with “lord.” Its use
was so widespread that bishops even called presbyters lords. Alexander,
bishop of Alexandria, addressed Athanasius while he was a presbyter: “To
Athanasius, lord and beloved son.” But their later wish to be distinguished by
“illustrious,” the title adorning first-order senators and distinguishing them
from “respectable” and “most distinguished” men, was altogether different and
the very thing the Lord forbade to apostles. “Lord” crept into the western
church, but first in the same sense as the Greek and Hebrew words in the East,
as in the Third Council of Arles, Third of Orléans, First of Tours, Third of
Toledo, and Ambrose’s letter 81, where Aper the presbyter subscribes a synodical
letter “by command of Lord Bishop Geminianus.” At the Council of Mérida the
parish presbyter is called lord: “These clerics shall receive food and clothing
through the presbyter’s distribution and ought to be faithful to their lord
and presbyter and to the church’s welfare.” Filesac says “the word lord was
attributed to a parish priest not to express authority over clerics, but only
for respect.” In English the title *Lord* is not so ordinary that it is shared
with pastors, but has a more august meaning and belongs only to nobles or
eminent magistrates. We must ask why they are called *My Lords*. Downham says
it is because of jurisdiction and authority. Although shared with nobles and
political magistrates, he says bishops are not civil lords, proving this by
the common distinction between spiritual and temporal lords. If, as Downham
wishes, they are called lords because of ecclesiastical jurisdiction, then
bishops are lords in the church. But there is only one Lord in the church,
the Lord Christ, and the title does not belong to ministers and servants. He
says the relation to the church is understood in the word “bishop” when one is
called Lord of Rochester or Bath and Wells, as if “Lord Bishop of Rochester”
were said. How frivolous these subtleties are. If he bears this title insofar
as he is bishop, it is because of ecclesiastical government, which we have
said does not suit the church’s pastors. Yet, they say, Christ himself adorns
them with the more magnificent title of angels.

[Marginalia: chapter 18; *On the Origin of Parishes*, p. 609; *Defense*, book
3, chapter 6, p. 150; p. 155.]

First, I deny that “angel” is more magnificent than “spiritual lord”: the
former signifies ministry, the latter dominion. Second, no argument follows
from the improper and allegorical names of prophetic visions to daily and
ordinary titles. Magistrates are called gods; but anyone designating the king
as God of England would be absurd and even impious toward God. Moreover, all
ministers are also honored with the title of angels, yet they are not adorned
with titles of secular dignity. The documents say bishops receive the title
lord because of the baronies annexed to their bishoprics. Mocket says the
same. The examples of Elijah and Elisha were therefore adduced in vain, for
they did not possess baronies and broad estates on account of which they were
greeted as lords. Thus the distinction between temporal and spiritual lords
is taken not from a difference of dominion but from the character of the
persons, the latter being ecclesiastical and the former secular. If it is
taken from a difference of dominion, one must say that bishops are called
spiritual lords because, besides being barons and therefore lords like other
secular holders of baronies, they also possess dominion in spiritual and
ecclesiastical causes, as a mixed race and two-formed offspring. But since the
apostles are wholly forbidden these titles as well as the civil powers and
dignities whose designations they are, bishops are not to be adorned with
*Lords* either separately or jointly, nor archbishops with *Grace*. At the
Third Council of Arles a subscribing presbyter does not write, “Desiderius,
presbyter directed by my lord, the most honored Lord N. of this or that see,”
but “by my lord John, bishop.” Likewise Aper writes “by command of Lord Bishop
Geminianus,” that is, by command of my lord Geminianus, who is Bishop N. The
address “lord” was placed before personal names for honor, not before sees as
in England for the display of dignity.
