Leading the line are those passages of Scripture which affirm that Christ took our place and was delivered up and died for us: “for the ungodly,” Romans 5:6; “he died for us,” verse 8; “a ransom in place of many,” Matthew 20:28; and “a counter-ransom for all,” 1 Timothy 2:6. This is in precisely the same manner in which a guilty person of old presented an expiatory victim before God and confessed his sins with his hands laid upon its head, Leviticus 1:4; 16:21. As Eusebius most correctly says, it was “as though he were offering the sacrificial victim in place of his own head,” so that it might undergo the punishment in his place, stead, and name. To this refer the statements about Christ that he bore and carried our griefs and iniquities, Isaiah 53:4, 11; that he is that Lamb of God “who bears away the sin of the world,” John 1:29; and that “he bore our sins in his body upon the tree,” 1 Peter 2:24. By a vicarious work, as Theophylact excellently says, he himself sustained for us what we ought to have suffered. This is the meaning of Athanasius’s “a body given in exchange for the soul” and the saying we read in Eusebius about “having undertaken death for us and transferred it to himself.” He assumed, undertook, transferred to himself, and gave an exchange. These are the Fathers’ ancient statements on this matter and their interpretations of those passages which proclaim that Christ died for us. But the Socinians wish “for us”

to be understood not as “in our place” but as “for our benefit.” They will never persuade grammarians of this; they will never find it when sufferings are under discussion, nor when someone is said to suffer for a criminal, either anywhere in Scripture or in common usage.

Second, the same point is proved by passages which say that Christ “makes expiation” for his people’s sins, Hebrews 2:17, and call him the “propitiation,” 1 John 2:2 and 4:10, and the “mercy seat,” Romans 3:25. The latter name also designates the cover placed over the ark of the covenant, Hebrews 9:5. Just as that cover hid from us those tablets of the Law which we had broken, so Christ as it were covered our sins from God’s sight. He thus pacified God’s wrath, which had been most deservedly kindled against us, by bearing it himself, and was therefore a truly and properly expiatory sacrifice for sins.

[Marginalia: Argument 4.]

They say that God could never have been angry with Christ, since he was his Son, always uniquely beloved and especially most beloved when he was obedient even unto death;

nor, indeed, with us, who were eternally elect in Christ.

They add that God displayed his antecedent and supreme love toward us by sending his most beloved Son to do and suffer so many and such great things for us.

Therefore they would have it that Christ reconciled not God to us but us to God, and that he did not render God propitious but only showed him to be so. Behold the heart of Zenodotus, behold the liver of Crates, behold the pure, unadulterated, putrid Socinian theology! Our replies are these:

1. “To propitiate” signifies not to show someone propitious but to render him so. Thus under the Mosaic law, when a priest who had offered a sacrifice is said to propitiate someone, he did not show that the guilty person had already been freed from guilt; rather, by duly performing the rite he freed the man who would otherwise have remained guilty. God indeed displays the surpassing riches of his grace toward us in Christ, as the apostle says in Ephesians 2:7. Yet he does this especially by willing his beloved Son to suffer such things so that, through that propitiatory sacrifice of himself, he might render God propitious to us.

2. The loving-kindness by which he sent Jesus Christ to save us was a love of benevolence, which can coexist with actual wrath, as God himself openly indicates in Job 42:7–8. As for the love of delight, it shows him to be placable, not actually placated.

3. As regards Christ, should Socinus or rather Paul be believed? Paul clearly declares in Galatians 3:13 that Christ was made “a curse” for us, and a curse cries out wrath. We acknowledge that God was most properly angry with us as regards the affection of wrath, if it is permissible to speak of God in this way. Yet the effects of this wrath struck Christ our surety from God as just Judge. This is more than sufficiently evident from his being grieved, distressed, and greatly amazed; from his bloody sweat; from the angel strengthening him; and from that most sorrowful cry uttered on the cross, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” This is what we certainly believe and earnestly maintain, and it suffices for our case. Nor does anything prevent the most loving Father, at the very moment Christ endured these things—indeed precisely because he endured them so obediently—from regarding him as his most beloved Son.

4. As regards us, the apostle explicitly says in Ephesians 2:3 that, though elect, we were nevertheless by nature children of wrath. Some, I acknowledge, wish to explain “children of wrath” by a Hebraism

[1679 new-series fol. 49 | IA leaf 369]

as “worthy of wrath.” But in that sense believers even now are still sometimes children under wrath, for because they remain sinners as long as they live here, they are always worthy of wrath. In the apostle’s meaning, however, “children of wrath” are those who remain in a state of wrath; and the apostle’s express assertion there is that actual believers have now been transferred out of that state.

Nor does their being elect any more prevent them from being children of wrath while unregenerate than it prevents the Jews from being enemies for our sake with respect to the gospel while beloved for the fathers’ sake with respect to election, Romans 11:28. It is also like a king who as a father can love a rebellious son insofar as he is a son, yet as a just judge pursues him with wrath insofar as he is guilty, until he grants him pardon.

As for the final claim about reconciliation—that we are reconciled to God, not God to us—we say that this reconciliation is mutual, and that God lays aside his wrath no less than we lay aside our enmity. This can be established even from common usage. When we say that the prodigal is reconciled to his father and Absalom to David, we also understand the father to be reconciled to the prodigal and David to Absalom. Especially pertinent is the apostle’s definition of reconciliation through free access to God in Ephesians 2:16–18. This indicates God’s favor more than ours. Moreover, in 2 Corinthians 5:19 God is said to have reconciled the world to himself by not imputing their sins to them, just as “to be justified” and “to be reconciled” sound alike in Romans 5. Yet justification and remission of sins properly belong to God. He himself, therefore, is reconciled and rendered propitious. Since all this happens through Christ’s death, Christ must be an expiatory sacrifice for sins.
