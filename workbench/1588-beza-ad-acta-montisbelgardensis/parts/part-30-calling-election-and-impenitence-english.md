## Part XXX: Calling, Election, and Impenitence

[Part II | PDF page 191 | logical source scan 190 | printed page 189 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

We endeavored shortly above, in response to annotation 8, to declare most fully from both the surest Scripture testimonies and the firmest arguments proved elsewhere how far our Lord God, who is truth itself, is removed from all hypocrisy, although he does not draw everyone whom he calls and does not always declare through his servants’ outward voice what he has decreed to do; and how the distinction between the will of sign and of good pleasure must be understood. Consequently, the conflict between the hidden decree and outward calling which Andreae dreams up here is vain and foolish.

But how could what he says afterward—that only those who oppose election revealed in Christ are condemned—proceed from a person of reasonably sound judgment? These people certainly oppose either their own election or that of others. If their own, they too were elect or divinely destined for election. How can that be truly said without entailing one of the following: the gross absurdity that everyone is elect before God, although no one can be said to choose without rejecting some; that God, changing his mind, casts out by far the greatest part of those whom he formerly destined to salvation; or that through either nature or grace commonly bestowed upon all, it lies within each person’s discretion to will and be able to receive the offered gospel? The first is utter insanity, the second manifest impiety, the third rank Pelagianism, and the fourth belongs to one who manifestly opposes all God’s Word, especially these two testimonies: “No one comes to me unless my Father draws—

—him,” and “Faith does not belong to everyone.”

Finally, who can patiently endure this man’s ignorance or shamelessness in corrupting the testimonies of God’s Word? In Matthew 11:25 and following, Christ prepares his disciples—and in their person all the godly—against that dreadful stumbling block by which the Son would not only go unrecognized by the evil tenants themselves but even be killed, as the church still experiences daily. After he adores the Father’s just good pleasure in this matter and warns the disciples that the grace in which the Father made them participants was not common to everyone, he finally passes over the rest and leaves them to the Father’s just judgment. Calling to himself all who labor—that is, those who do not delight in their utterly false righteousness but, like that publican, grow faint under the sense of their sins and gasp beneath so great a burden for God’s mercy alone—to himself as the one fountain of that grace, he promises them certain relief. Even if everyone came to Christ when he calls, they would nevertheless be few in comparison with the rest. Yet Andreae is not ashamed to distort this beautiful passage to prove his fiction of universal election.

We have answered so often concerning this fictitious absolute will, upon which this man has already so often suffered shipwreck as upon a reef, that we are ourselves ashamed of the repetition.

Indeed, this slander is full of extreme shamelessness. I did not say the reason impenitent people neither will nor can be converted flows from God’s abandoning them in impenitence. What could be more foolish than making the thing in which they are abandoned the cause of the abandonment itself? I said that the impenitent persevere in their impenitence not because God prevents them when they wish to repent—otherwise their impenitence would be coerced rather than voluntary—but because by their corrupted nature they do not will to be converted. Moreover, unless God dignifies them with the grace of conversion, they cannot even will to be converted, much less actually be converted. What does Andreae have to censure here, unless perhaps with manifest Pelagians he denies either that the grace of conversion proceeds wholly from God or that the willing of which Paul speaks in Philippians 2:13 pertains only to the elect? In Romans 8 Paul not only denies that the mind of the flesh is subject to God’s law but affirms that it cannot be. John 12:39 says that those resisting Christ not only did not will to believe but could not believe, as Isaiah had foretold. And this, supposedly, is to remove the whole doctrine of the gospel, the use of the sacraments, and all consolation from troubled consciences.
