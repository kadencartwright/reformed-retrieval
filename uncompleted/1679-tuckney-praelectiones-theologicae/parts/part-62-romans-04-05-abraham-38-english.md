Next Castellio proceeds to prove that the same thing cannot be accomplished
through the power of death, “because that is not for a person to be healed,
but to die. Otherwise robbers would heal a person just as much by killing
him as Christ himself does by curing him; the souls of the wicked no less
than those of the godly would be cleansed from every sin in death itself;
thus no one would perish, and Christ would contribute nothing to a person's
salvation.”

1. As for the claim that this is not for a person to be healed, but to die:
Aeschylus does not speak this way, for, as we said before, he calls death a
physician. But, you will say, he speaks poetically. Yet if we are healed
through death, this is not only to die but also to be healed—truly enough,
although the language is figurative.

2. Castellio says that if we are healed through death: 1. “Killers would be
physicians just as much as Christ; 2. the wicked as well as the godly would
be healed by death; 3. indeed, no one would perish; and 4. Christ would
contribute nothing to our salvation.” How splendidly, and more than
athletically, he fights in an empty field, fiercely arranging four argu-

[1679 printed p. 286 | IA leaf 294]

ments like ranks of artillery, but without an adversary! For who among us,
or anyone else—unless perhaps some Jew Apella who believes that all his sins
are expiated by death—ever said or dreamed that death considered nakedly in
itself, the enemy of nature and wages of sin, had by its own character
become medicinal and saving, so that people were immediately cleansed from
sin by the mere fact that they die? On that supposition killers would
indeed be physicians, the wicked as well as the godly would be healed by
death, and no one who dies would perish eternally. We freely surrender to
his first three arguments. But we do not do the same with the fourth, that
Christ would thus contribute nothing to our salvation. On the contrary,
Christ contributes especially in this very respect: as though transforming
death, he leads captivity captive; having removed its sting, he turns the
viper into an antidote. Through the merit of his death and the efficacy of
his Spirit, death—which in itself was the prelude and vestibule of
hell—becomes the gate of heaven, the departure from sin and entrance into
glory. We owe this not to Death, but to Christ, the Lord of life. Just as he
entered his glory through the intervention of his own death, Luke 24:26, so
through his merit, with our death intervening, we are made entirely free
from sin and participants in glory.
