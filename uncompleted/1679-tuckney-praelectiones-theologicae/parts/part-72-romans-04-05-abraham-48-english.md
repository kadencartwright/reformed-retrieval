We have already disposed of his fifth argument, as also his seventh. I
therefore pass them over now.

His sixth argument runs as follows: “Christ restored to us what we had lost
in Adam. But in Adam we did not lose imputed righteousness, but true,
inherent righteousness. Therefore through Christ we receive true
righteousness, not righteousness that is merely extrinsic and imputed.”

Some of our theologians maintain that this argument errs in form, and they
set it out in this way: “What was not lost in Adam is not given in Christ.
But imputed righteousness was not lost in Adam. Therefore imputed
righteousness is not given in Christ.” They say that it errs in form because
the minor premise is negative in the first figure. I do not agree with this
objection, for two reasons.

1. In the syllogism as thus formulated, the minor premise is not negative.
Although the proposition “Imputed righteousness was not lost in Adam,” taken
separately in itself, is a negative proposition, yet when taken together
with the major premise, in whose subject the negation was contained—“what
was not lost in Adam”—it must be taken as affirmative. So it is in this
syllogism: “What does not perceive is not an animal; a stone does not
perceive; therefore it is not an animal,” and in other similar cases.

2. Although the argument as they formulate it is in the first figure, it can
nevertheless be in the second as Bellarmine proposes it, and in that figure
the minor premise can be negative. It would run thus: “We receive in Christ
what we lost in Adam; but we did not lose imputed righteousness in Adam;
therefore we do not receive imputed righteousness in Christ.” This syllogism
does not err in form, except that its major proposition is not universal, as
it must be. It would have to be stated or understood in this way: “We receive
in Christ only what we lost in Adam”—which expresses a restriction contrary
to universality. But leaving the form of this syllogism aside, let us answer
each of its propositions separately.

[1679 printed p. 297 | IA leaf 305]

First, we give two answers to the major premise.

1. If it is taken with the restriction and stated as the force of the
argument requires—“We receive in Christ only what we lost in Adam”—it errs
in matter just as it does in form. For in Christ we receive not only what we
lost in Adam, but also more and greater things.

We receive more things from Christ: faith in the Mediator, repentance for
our offenses, and conversion. We did not lose these in Adam because we never
possessed them in him; nor did we possess the willing of what we are able to
do, or perseverance in good.

We also receive greater things: Christ's grace, which is far greater than
Adam's; and indefectible grace, which Adam's was not, as we have learned by
wretched experience. Through Christ we will also finally attain in heaven
even greater inherent grace than what we lost in Paradise through Adam's
fall.

2. The answer our theologians commonly give here is also entirely true:
this is the fallacy of accident, an inference from the thing itself to its
mode. In this argument *what* a thing is is changed into *what kind* it is,
and the thing is confused with its mode. The thing we lost in Adam is the
same, namely true righteousness, but the mode of attaining it is different.
In Adam it came through infusion alone; in Christ it comes partly through
infusion as well, but primarily and radically through imputation, from which
the subsequent infusion flows. Thus in Adam we lost original righteousness,
while in Christ we receive true righteousness, and indeed righteousness
identical in essence, but not original righteousness—that is, righteousness
implanted in us from our origin.

To the minor premise, “But in Adam we did not lose imputed righteousness,” I
also answer by conceding that Adam's primal righteousness was not imputed to
him from his first origin, but infused and conferred. I nevertheless say,

First, if he had persevered in it, it would have been imputed to him as
righteousness under the covenant of works, according to the apostle's words
in Romans 4:4: “To the one who works, the reward is not imputed by grace but
as a debt.” We therefore lost in him what, though not actually imputed
because of his fall, was to be imputed under the covenant.

Nor was it to be imputed to Adam alone, but also to us. Indeed, insofar as it
could be called ours, it was ours only by imputation, because it was never
personally inherent in us who fell before we were born, though we nevertheless
lost it. Just as we lost it in Adam insofar as we were in him as in our root
and principle, so Christ's righteousness, though it does not inhere in us,
can nevertheless be counted ours insofar as we are in him as members in
their Head.

I add this too: the question in this controversy is not whether
righteousness is restored in Christ, but whether it is imputed. We are not
therefore concerned here with whether it was imputed or conferred upon Adam.
Our point is that we lost it, whatever its character was, and do not recover
it unless the imputation of Christ's righteousness precedes and effects this
recovery.

Finally, to the conclusion, “Therefore through Christ we receive true
righteousness, not righteousness that is merely extrinsic and imputed”: once
we reject his utterly false opposition between true and imputed
righteousness—which he seeks to insinuate and thrust upon us both here and
throughout, as if imputed righteousness were not true—and once this single
point has been observed and rejected, we concede that the conclusion is
true, namely that

[1679 printed p. 298 | IA leaf 306]

we receive inherent righteousness from Christ and not only imputed
righteousness. We have always gratefully confessed that we receive both from
Christ, the former in sanctification just as the latter in justification. I
mean that we receive inherent righteousness, though not formally through
justification, yet concomitantly with justification in sanctification,
which is joined to justification by an indissoluble bond. Is Bellarmine,
then, such a fearsome enemy, when we receive his entire weapon against our
body and suffer no harm from it? May their arguments always conclude in this
fashion: concluding nothing against Christ's truth or our doctrine founded
upon it.
