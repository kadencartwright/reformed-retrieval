### QUESTION X

#### Do Daniel’s seventy weeks signify a fixed and definite period, or an uncertain and indefinite one?

Answer. In the whole range of chronology, no question treated by everyone is
either more distinguished or more difficult than that concerning Daniel’s
seventy weeks. Its distinction consists in the fact that it concerns the
exceedingly illustrious prophecy about the death of the Messiah and its
various benefits. Its difficulty is evident from the fact that, although all
chronologists differ greatly among themselves on many matters, nowhere is
their disagreement more conspicuous than on this subject. Indeed, they all
complain of the great obscurity here, except for a few who boast that they
have discovered an easy way of untangling all the knots. This boasting is
certainly vain, for on the road they wish to be so easy and downward-sloping,
we find obstacles as rough as on any road belonging to the others. Augustine,
as may be seen from letter 80 to Hesychius, certainly never allowed anyone’s
entreaties to persuade him to undertake a treatment of these weeks. In his
commentary on Daniel 9, Jerome reviews the opinions of others at length and
with precision, but altogether avoids expressing his own judgment or even his
assessment of the opinions of others. The English annotators, especially in
their later edition, tread Jerome’s path: they recount the positions of
others and leave the reader free to choose, but conceal their own minds. In
this indifference or doubt, this ἀδιακρισίᾳ, “indecision,” or ἀπορίᾳ,
“perplexity,” they have as predecessors the Genevan annotators in the French
Bible, as cited by Rainolds.

> Marginalia, printed p. 162: *The question about Daniel’s weeks is at once
> most distinguished and most difficult*; *The silence of modesty is useless
> and unwelcome to the church*.

We do not dare condemn this silence, which arose not from any envy or
negligence, but from sheer modesty and fear of error. Yet we think that the
church owes more to those who, in their desire to help others advance,
honestly set out their opinions even when mistaken than to those who remain
silent for fear of error and permit the mass of mankind to be tossed on a
stormy sea. For many of them, through their varied learning and strength of
intellect—even when they think they are moving in darkness—would undoubtedly
offer far more light and comfort to those following them through guidance, or
even an effort at guidance, of whatever quality. They would also deserve
better of learned people than when, by obstinate silence and refusal to lead,
they abandon individuals to their own ignorance and, as far as it lies with
them, allow them to wander endlessly without any guide.

In keeping with our plan of brevity, we shall raise only six questions about
these weeks. We expressed the first in the present title. The second will
concern their beginning, and the third their end. The fourth will concern the
first section, namely, the seven weeks named by the angel. The fifth will
concern their other portion, the sixty-two weeks. The sixth will concern the
last section—or at least the one named last by the angel—that one week in
which the Messiah died.

> Marginalia, printed p. 162: *The author’s plan here*.

Anyone who properly resolves these six questions will leave little or nothing
unresolved in this truly Gordian knot. But there is far too much disagreement
about all of them among chronologists and interpreters of Scripture, both
ancient and modern. It is far beyond our powers to settle these disputes so
that we can promise peace of mind either to ourselves or to others. I shall
only indicate which view in each case is more pleasing to my mind and the
reasons why, so that at some point you yourselves may be stirred to a more
exact investigation of these matters in your private studies.

I admit that some of the questions named concern later epochs rather than the
present one. But the sections of Daniel’s prophecy before us are so connected
that, if I had chosen to separate them and defer the last of them to their own
periods, I would have impeded the explanation of the others and withheld for
far too long a great portion of the light altogether necessary to illuminate
this rather obscure text.

On the first question, I find only Rainolds and Broughton on the other side,
together with Nisbet, who follows Broughton here. Rainolds, beginning the
weeks with Cyrus’s first year and closing them at Christ’s passion, agreed
with Calvin and many others.

[Printed p. 163 | IA leaf 193 | access PDF 193 | OCR object 192]

But when he discovered that in that interval far more than the precise span
of 490 years represented by the weeks had elapsed, he entered a new path
trodden by himself alone. He interpreted the weeks as no fixed and definite
period, but as whatever time might finally have elapsed from Cyrus to the
Messiah. He devotes much serious labor to proving this in six entire lectures.
I find no one who later contradicted him except our own Nisbet, who does so
briefly but, as it seems to me, correctly and soundly—if anything in his
entire work does. My only regret is that, passing over Rainolds himself, Nisbet
makes a show of disputing with Rainolds’s pupil Pemmell, who had excerpted a
short compendium from his teacher’s lectures. I do not know whether Broughton
wrote anything after Rainolds’s lectures were published.

> Marginalia, printed p. 163: *Rainolds takes the seventy weeks as an
> uncertain and indefinite period*.

I argue first as follows against Rainolds’s opinion. A number is fixed and
definite when the Holy Spirit himself distributes it into different sections
and assigns various events to each section. We admit that a definite sum of
years must sometimes be taken as indefinite, but when a definite number is
divided into parts and each part is applied to different events within those
parts of the time, that total cannot be taken as uncertain and indefinite.
That is the condition of the present total. At God’s command the angel divides
the seventy weeks into seven, sixty-two, and one, and teaches at length what
would happen in each—or at least in the second and last parts.

> Marginalia, printed p. 163: *Against Rainolds, Argument 1*.

Second. A period whose beginning, middle, and end are defined by God is fixed
and definite. But the beginning and end of the seventy weeks are defined by
God. For everyone, including Rainolds himself, admits that this period begins
with the issuing of the word about building Jerusalem. There are indeed great
disputes over the true meaning of those words, but no one has ever doubted
that God marked a fixed beginning of the weeks with this feature. Indeed,
Rainolds, taking that word as Cyrus’s decree, believed the beginning of the
weeks should be fixed in Cyrus’s first or second year. As for their end,
although there is considerable difference of opinion over the years of
Christ’s birth and death, and over the destruction of Jerusalem first by
Titus and afterward by Hadrian, everyone nevertheless admits that Daniel
impresses definite marks on the end of these weeks. Rainolds himself fixes
their precise and definite end at Christ’s death. As for the middle, the angel
expressly numbers seventy weeks from the issuing of the word to the events
mentioned by Daniel—that is, seven, sixty-two, and one, neither more nor
fewer.

> Marginalia, printed p. 163: *2*.

Third. A period is fixed and definite when the angel expressly calls it
precisely delimited and defined. But the seventy weeks are such a period:
verse 24 says, “Seventy weeks are cut off.” We do not insist on the first
meaning of the word נחתך—*to cut off, curtail, and shorten*—from which
Africanus, followed by Pererius and many others, concludes that solar years
are truly shortened and reduced to lunar years eleven solar days shorter.
Accordingly, fifteen years are cut from the total of 490 years, and 490 is
changed to 475. Indeed, from the same meaning Pareus shaves half a week away,
whereas his opponent Scaliger thinks half a week—three and a half years—must
be added to the total of seventy weeks. We insist only on the second meaning
of the word, one no less proper and more common than the first: *to define,
appoint, determine*. Since God calls these weeks defined and determined, no
one ought to make them indefinite and undetermined.

> Marginalia, printed p. 163: *3*.

Rainolds’s distinction here between defining the thing itself and defining
its circumstances or time is not satisfactory. For the thing God determines
and defines here is the time and circumstances of the weeks.

This argument is confirmed by the unusual construction, correctly noted in
the latest edition of the English annotations. The angel does not say “weeks
are determined,” in the plural, but “weeks is determined,” in the singular.
Now when plural number-words are construed with a singular verb or noun, it
signifies the particular application and distribution of that verb or noun
through each of its parts. The construction therefore conveys a particular
determination not only of the entire sum of seventy weeks but of the
individual parts within that sum, as if all these weeks and every one of their
days or years were said to be determined with the greatest precision. See
Buxtorf’s *Thesaurus*, p. 342, and also p. 425.

Fourth. All the earlier periods of time expressed in God’s Word, from the
beginning of the world to Cyrus’s first year, are fixed and definite.
Therefore this period now before us and expressly stated is not uncertain and
indefinite either. Broughton draws this argument from God’s example in
presenting the other periods of time from the creation of the world in fixed
and

> defended*.

[Printed p. 164 | IA leaf 194 | access PDF 194 | OCR object 193]

definite numbers. Rainolds ridicules this argument at length, but with a
slight change of form it will readily stand and, unmoved, repel all of
Rainolds’s battering rams. It rests upon the common maxim *Like things agree
with like*. If definite determination suits the earlier periods of time, then
the most certain determination ought much more to suit this final period,
which extends to Christ’s death. For the certainty of all the earlier times
was fixed by the Holy Spirit with this purpose especially: that the faithful
might be led by a clear and certain thread from the beginning of the world to
Christ’s passion. As for the later times, from Christ’s passion, fulfillment
of his promises, and completion of the work of redemption until the end of
the world, the Holy Spirit deliberately leaves them altogether undetermined,
so that no delay might be cast upon the godly person’s daily longing and
expectation for Christ’s return. But the period immediately preceding the
passion, if any did, required definite certainty. The argument does not
proceed absolutely, as Rainolds claims Broughton childishly argues, but upon
the supposition that God expressed this final περιοχήν, or “period,” by some
number. Given that supposition, the argument firmly proves that this number
was definite.

Rainolds’s distinction between periods set out in historical narrative and
periods mentioned in prophetic prediction does nothing to relieve him. To
God, future things are as present as both present and past things, and he is
as fixed and definite in his predictions of the future as in his narratives
of the past.

Of the counterexamples with which Rainolds tries to weaken the argument, none
is relevant to the present matter except one concerning the four hundred
years during which Abraham’s descendants are said to have sojourned in Egypt,
although thirty years above four hundred certainly have to be understood
within that total. Answer. We responded to this counterexample in its proper
place with a twofold distinction. First, rounding numbers by small excesses or
deficiencies does not make a number uncertain and indefinite. Second, the
years stated are precisely four hundred or 430 according to different
starting points for the calculation. Add a third point: the period from Adam
to the people’s exodus from Egypt is defined from other passages of Scripture,
not solely from the passage where that round number of four hundred years is
expressed. But the περιοχή, or “period,” now before us that extends to
Christ’s death is expressed nowhere but in Daniel’s weeks under discussion.
These numbers, therefore, are not altogether alike.

Fifth. This invention of Rainolds must by no means be accepted by Christians,
for it makes the time of the Messiah’s advent altogether uncertain. In spite
of Daniel’s prophecy, Rainolds postpones Christ’s death a full seventy years
beyond the seventy weeks. Why, then, could not the end of that prophecy be
extended with the Jews to another seventy years, when Hadrian all but
destroyed the Jewish nation? Indeed, with Apollinaris, why could we not
postpone the end of this uncertain period all the way to the end of the world,
and with Origen trace its beginning back to Adam himself? Rainolds’s supreme
piety and learning were far removed from those most dreadful consequences.
Nevertheless, extending the weeks to indefinite limits, with an uncertain
beginning and an uncertain end, opens far too wide a door for foolish people
to quibble.

Finally, all the testimony of all scholars stands against Rainolds. However
great the disagreement among many scholars on the various matters concerning
these weeks, they nevertheless affirm with one voice that the weeks signify
some fixed period. Indeed, not even those who place their beginning at the
world’s origin and their end at the world’s conclusion deny their certainty.

There is no reason to except Pemmell, a genuinely learned and erudite man,
from this catalog of scholars. For the writing under Pemmell’s name that
Nisbet refutes can properly be attributed to Rainolds himself and to no one
else. It was not published by Pemmell himself, but merely found among papers
in which he had, as a young man, excerpted a compendium from Rainolds’s
lectures without any contribution of his own intellect, apart from a single
abridgment.

> Marginalia, printed p. 164: *Pemmell agrees with Rainolds here*.

These are the principal arguments Rainolds advances for his opinion. First,
in various passages of Scripture definite numbers of days and years are taken
as indefinite. Therefore Daniel’s seventy weeks can be interpreted in this
way.

> Marginalia, printed p. 164: *Response to Rainolds’s arguments*.

Answer. Rainolds acknowledges that this argument is only probable. Certainly,
an argument from particular instances is a fault that excludes probability
itself. We do indeed admit that a definite period must sometimes be taken as
indefinite, but this is rare, and numbers of days and years are for the most
part—indeed, almost always—taken in their most proper sense, without any trope
or synecdoche. Moreover, Scripture usually indicates when a synecdoche is to
be employed, at least by a clear inference from some passage; but that does
not occur in Daniel’s weeks.

> Marginalia, printed p. 164: *To 1*.

Rainolds draws his second argument from Daniel’s text itself and wishes it to
be necessary and demonstrative. Its entire force comes down to this. The last
of the seventy weeks is to be taken by synecdoche

[Printed p. 165 | IA leaf 195 | access PDF 195 | OCR object 194]

as only half a week, namely, as three and a half years. Therefore the seventy
weeks cannot be taken in their proper sense as 490 whole years, but as 486
and a half.

Answer. This proof of Rainolds works against his own purpose. For the aim of
his discussion is to extend the seventy weeks as far as seventy years beyond
the common total, but by this argument he restricts them to less than the
common total. Yet let us grant Rainolds the marvelous art of deriving one
contrary from another, so that he can forge expansion out of contraction—and
a great expansion of seventy years from a slight contraction of three and a
half years. Nevertheless, as he himself admits, this whole argument falls if
its foundation falls: that the weeks end at Christ’s death and that Christ
died in the middle, not at the end, of the last week. How fragile and
unstable are both parts of this foundation! In Rainolds’s estimation no
chronologist is greater than Scaliger, but Scaliger, together with Junius and
very many others, denies that the weeks end at Christ’s death. He ends them
thirty-six or forty years afterward, at the second destruction of Jerusalem
by Titus. Even if we grant Rainolds that the weeks close with Christ’s death,
does it follow that the final week must be shortened by three and a half
years? Certainly, even if Christ had died at the beginning of the seventieth
week, the seventy weeks would rightly be said to close with his death because
he would have died in the last of them. Whatever occurs at the beginning of
any day, month, year, or week of years is said just as properly to occur in
that day, month, year, or week of years as if it had occurred in the middle or
at the end of that time.

> Marginalia, printed p. 165: *On the limits of the one week, against the
> extravagances of Pareus, Rainolds, and Scaliger*.

But let this pass. What prevents Christ from having been crucified at the
very end of the final week? Daniel does not say that Christ died in the middle
of the week. Read the text: these words appear nowhere. But if Rainolds argues
by inference, the best theologians will deny this consequence: “The offering
and sacrifice ceased at the middle of the week; therefore Christ died at that
same moment.”

Let us go on being generous to Rainolds. Suppose that Christ died at the same
moment when sacrifice ceased, and that the middle of the week—a full six
months, although no one would grant this—is compressed into the single moment
of Christ’s death. Does it necessarily follow from this that Christ died
three and a half years before the end of the week? On that reasoning he would
have died not at the middle but before the middle of the week, namely, in its
first half.

Both Scaliger and Rainolds argue at length about the words *middle* and
*half*, as if there were a real difference between them. Yet they are forced
to concede that these words are often used interchangeably; however things
may stand elsewhere, interpreters in this passage certainly use the word חצי
indifferently for *middle* and *half*. Since the prophet wishes to be precise
about Christ’s death, an event supremely memorable since the creation of the
world, he identifies not only its week but a half of that week. Whether he
means its first or second half, or the very midpoint between the two, is not
so plain. Great authorities support all three opinions.

> Marginalia, printed p. 165: *Here “middle” is not distinguished from
> “half”*.

Here we wish to avoid only three reefs. The first is that of Pareus, who wants
Daniel’s final, seventieth week to consist of only three and a half years,
shaving the entire latter half from that seven-year period. The second is
Scaliger’s, for he wishes that final week to consist not only of seven whole
years but of an additional three and a half, adding half of another week to
it. The third, finally, is Rainolds’s: he confines Christ’s death to the
middle of this final week, wishing not only to cast away the latter half from
that week and make the first half bear the name of the whole week by
synecdoche, but also thinking that from this synecdochical contraction of one
week it can be proved that all the other weeks may be extended by a similar
synecdoche to any greater number, at least by seventy years. These three
consequences are so difficult to digest that these exceedingly learned men
have so far found very few willing to follow them on this pathless road. But
more about that final week in its proper place.

By another proof from Daniel’s words, Rainolds thinks he can establish that
the weeks are to be interpreted synecdochically. Their first part, he says,
namely the seven weeks, must be extended to many more. For he takes Daniel to
mean that Jerusalem’s restoration was impeded during those seven weeks, but
it is agreed that Jerusalem’s wall was built by Nehemiah no earlier than
Mnemon’s twentieth year, more than one hundred years after Cyrus’s first. The
seven weeks in which Daniel affirms that the city would be built must extend
through all those years. And since the first portion of the weeks is thus
extended by synecdoche, why could not the other portions and the whole total
be extended by a similar trope to a much greater number of years?

[Printed p. 166 | IA leaf 196 | access PDF 196 | OCR object 195]

Answer. This proof, like the earlier one, is a false argument that errs in
many ways. Daniel does not say either that the wall was built during the first
seven weeks or that its construction was impeded during them. He assigns the
wall’s construction to both portions of the seventy—the seven weeks and the
sixty-two weeks—but does not say in which part of these portions the wall was
built or its construction impeded. Scaliger indeed admits that he has no idea
why these seven weeks are separated from the rest. But more about this in its
proper place.

Next, even if Daniel had expressly affirmed, without a long circuit of
inferences, that the city’s construction was impeded for seven weeks, and if
that obstruction had in fact persisted beyond seven to twenty weeks, would
the seven weeks therefore have continued by synecdoche for all twenty? What
an astonishing novelty of tropes this would be!

Finally, this entire argument rests on Rainolds’s false premises: namely, that
the weeks began in Cyrus’s first year and that the wall was built in the
twentieth year of Artaxerxes Mnemon, not Longimanus. We do not admit these
things.

Rainolds’s final and truly principal argument—the one that seems above all
others, if not alone, to have misled him onto his pathless road—is this. From
the beginning of the weeks to their end, from Cyrus’s decree to Christ’s
death, at least seventy years beyond 490 elapsed, the latter being the first
and proper total of the seventy weeks. Therefore the seventy weeks must be
extended by synecdoche to a greater number.

> Marginalia, printed p. 166: *To 3*.

Answer. Unlike Rainolds’s opponents Beroaldus, Broughton, Pareus, Howe, Nisbet,
and others, we are unwilling to deny that so many years elapsed from Cyrus to
Christ. That would certainly be cutting knots rather than untying them. We do
indeed think that Rainolds, most learnedly after Scaliger and before Ubo
Emmius, demonstrated that the authors named wrongfully and without any reason
accuse all the ancient Chaldean, Greek, and Latin writers of falsehood, as if
they had assigned one hundred years too many to the Persian kingdom. But our
reply to Rainolds is that he wrongly placed the beginning of the weeks in
Cyrus’s first year. If, with Scaliger—whom he almost always, and indeed often
in this very argument, follows—he had begun the weeks in the second year of
Darius Nothus, or if, with the company of both ancient and modern scholars, he
had begun them in either the seventh or twentieth year of Artaxerxes
Longimanus, he could easily have freed himself from his own snares and would
have had no need of any synecdoche or any extension of precise numbers. But
let us now see what the true beginning of those weeks was.
