# Confession concerning the Trinity in Response to the Calumnies of Peter Caroli (1537)

*A fresh translation from the Latin main text at CO IX 703–710. The title,
section labels, page notices, and explanatory notes are editorial rather than
words silently attributed to the three subscribers.*

## The confession

[CO IX, p. 703]

When the human mind is of itself completely blind in contemplating God's
majesty, and, if it tries to imagine God according to the narrowness of its
own capacity, can do nothing except envelop itself in endless errors,
entangle itself in strange perplexities, and finally plunge itself into the
deepest darkness, we trust that we shall have the leave and indulgence of all
good people in doing this: seeking God nowhere but in his word, thinking
nothing about him except in company with his word, and saying nothing about
him except through his word. If this sobriety and reverence is commended even
in familiar conversation, where many things are commonly blurted out without
prior thought, then, when someone undertakes to publish a public confession of
his faith on this subject, no diligence that can be applied is sufficient,
whether in forming propositions or in choosing words, so that nothing may be
found in it except the very truth of Scripture itself, reverently comprised
in carefully chosen and solemn words. Let no one, then, be angry with us if we
so frame the confession which we wish all godly people to approve that it is
not stitched together from various human opinions, but is carefully tested
against the straight rule of Scripture. Here we must call to mind what the
apostle teaches: faith is from hearing, and hearing through the word of God
(Romans 10:17). Now, if a confession of religion is nothing but testimony to
faith conceived within, then, for it to be sound and sincere, it must be drawn
from the pure springs of Scripture. Nor indeed do we accept only a confession
that has been superstitiously woven and stitched together from the bare words
of Scripture. Rather, we insist that it be written in words
which possess a meaning

[CO IX, p. 704]

bounded by the truth of Scripture, contain as little harshness as possible by
which godly ears might be offended, and attribute nothing to God unworthy of
his majesty. Because we have endeavored to maintain this manner in our
confession, we do not doubt that every good and judicious man will approve our
effort.

We believe and worship the one God whom the Scriptures proclaim to us. We
also conceive him as he is described to us there: namely, as being of an
eternal, infinite, and spiritual essence, who alone has in himself and from
himself the power to subsist and bestows it upon every creature. We repudiate
the Anthropomorphites with their corporeal God, and likewise the Manichaeans
with their two principles. Yet in the one essence of God we acknowledge the
Father with his eternal Word and Spirit. In using this distinction of names,
we neither imagine three Gods, as though the Father were something other than
the Word, nor, on the other hand, understand them to be bare epithets by which
God is designated in various ways from his works. Instead, with the
ecclesiastical writers, we hold that in the utterly simple unity of God there
are these three hypostases, that is, subsistences. Although they consist in
one essence, they are nevertheless not confused with one another. Thus,
although there is one God—the Father with his Word and Spirit—the Father is
not the Word, nor is the Word himself the Spirit. There are firm testimonies
of Scripture to confirm this position. For the Son is called both Jehovah and
Mighty God, and God blessed forever; all the angels of God worship him, and
his throne is established forever and ever. Moreover, all things are
attributed to him

[CO IX, p. 705]

which can in no way belong to anyone except the one true and eternal God. For
just as he is called our salvation, righteousness, and sanctification, so we
are taught to repose all confidence and hope in him and to invoke his name.
This practical knowledge is undoubtedly surer than any idle speculation. For
there the godly mind beholds God as immediately present and almost touches
him, where it perceives itself being given life, enlightened, saved,
justified, and sanctified.

Proof confirming the divinity of the Holy Spirit must therefore be sought
from the same springs. What Scripture attributes to him is far removed from
creatures, and we ourselves learn it by the sure experience of piety. For he
is the one who, diffused everywhere, sustains, quickens, and gives life to all
things; who, dwelling in believers, leads them into all truth, regenerates
and sanctifies them, and will one day give them life in full. Nor indeed does
Scripture refrain from calling him God when it speaks of him. Paul infers
that we are God's temple from the fact that his Spirit dwells in us. And when
Peter reproved Ananias for lying to the Holy Spirit, he said that Ananias had
lied not to men but to God.

Now, since it remains settled that God is one, not many, we maintain that the
Word and the Spirit are nothing other than the very essence of God. And
indeed, if we are baptized into the faith and religion of one God, we profess
that the one into whose name we are baptized is our God. From this it is
plain that the Father, Son, and Spirit are contained in the one essence of
God, since we are baptized into the name of the Father and of the Son and of
the Holy Spirit.

Scripture also demonstrates a certain distinction of the Father from the
Word and of the Word from the Spirit. For the meaning of the statement is
plain: the Father created all things through the Son, sent that same Son into
this world, exposed him to death, raised him from the dead, and gave him the
name which is above every name. Unless someone intends openly to resist God's
word, he cannot evade this. For the Father did not descend to earth, assume
our flesh, undergo death, or rise again. Christ also signified that he was
distinct from the Spirit when he promised that he would send another
Comforter. This distinction is so far from impeding the utterly simple unity
of God that it is possible from it to prove that the Son is one God with the
Father, because he shares one and the same Spirit with him. The Spirit,
moreover, is not something different and diverse from the Father and the Son,
because he is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. Let this, then, be the
summary: in that eternal and spiritual

[CO IX, p. 706]

and infinite essence of God which we confessed above, we do not confuse the
Father with the Word or the Word with the Spirit. We reject and execrate the
madness of Arius, who stripped the Son of eternal divinity, and at the same
time that of Macedonius, who understood by the Spirit merely the gifts of
grace poured out upon human beings. No more do we assent to the error of
Sabellius, who admitted no distinction between Father, Son, and Spirit.

As for Christ in particular, we affirm that he consists of two natures.
Before he assumed flesh, that eternal Word was begotten from the Father
before the ages, true God, of one essence, power, and majesty with the Father,
and therefore Jehovah, who always had existence from himself and breathed
into others the power to subsist. For he himself testified that he possessed
with the Father from the beginning, before the foundations of the world were
laid, the glory with which he was glorified in the flesh. Paul likewise
teaches this when he writes that Christ was in the form of God, understanding
majesty by the word *form*. Christ himself asserts his power in these words:
“I and the Father are working.” To the same effect, Scripture also repeatedly
teaches that the world was created through him, that in him was life, and
many things of this sort. There is no value in dwelling on the matter at
greater length here. For if he is God, he contains in himself everything that
belongs to God. The Arians therefore lied most foolishly when, while
confessing him to be God, they took divine subsistence away from him.

Nor by the designation *Word* do we understand a fleeting and vanishing
sound emitted into the air, such as the oracles delivered to the fathers and
the prophecies were. We mean the perpetual Wisdom abiding with God, from whom
all oracles and prophecies proceeded. For the ancient prophets spoke by the
Spirit of Christ no less than did the apostles and all who administered God's
truth to human beings.

Then, at the time appointed for the manifestation of our redemption, the
Word became flesh—not because he was changed into flesh, but because he
assumed flesh from the virgin—so that he was the true and genuine seed of
David, and the one who was the Son of God became the Son of Man. Yet we assert
that divinity was so joined to humanity that each nature retains its own
unimpaired property, while from those two there is constituted one Christ.
Thus, although Scripture sets the properties before us to be considered
distinctly, attributing to Christ at one time what belongs to God alone, and
at another time

[CO IX, p. 707]

what belongs to man, it nevertheless expresses with such religious care the
union between them that subsists in Christ that it sometimes communicates
them to one another. Thus it says that the church was acquired by the blood
of God (Acts 20:28), and that the Son of Man was in heaven (John 3:13) at the
time when he was still living on earth—a figure which the ancients called
*ἰδιωμάτων κοινωνία*, “communion of properties.” But clearest of all
are those passages which set forth Christ's true substance while embracing
both natures together, of which there are very many in John's Gospel.

We therefore assert that Christ, true God and true man, is the Son of God
even according to his humanity, though not by reason of his humanity. This is
both expressly attested in the Scriptures and received by the agreement of
the ancient church. We detest as sacrilegious all who have attempted to
overturn, diminish, or obscure the truth of the divine majesty in Christ. We
utterly reject the Marcionites, who attributed to him an apparition in place
of a body, and likewise the Manichaeans, who dreamed that he was furnished
with heavenly flesh. Nor do we assent to Apollinaris, who took away half of
his humanity. Finally, we condemn the error of Nestorius, who separated the
divinity from the humanity and invented a double Christ.

### First appended clarification: the terms “Trinity” and “person”

Because we understand that the terms *Trinity* and *persons* contribute
greatly to Christ's church, both so that the true distinction of Father, Son,
and Holy Spirit may be expressed more clearly and so that contentious
controversies may be met more effectively, we are so far from shrinking from
these terms that we gladly embrace them, whether we hear them used by others
or use them ourselves. We undertake that, as we have done this before, so in
future, as far as we are permitted, we will take care that their use is not
abolished in our churches. For neither when writing or expounding Scripture,
nor in sermons to the people,

[CO IX, p. 708]

will we ourselves abstain from them, and we will teach others not to shun
them superstitiously. But if someone is restrained by a misguided religious
scruple from venturing to use them freely, although we testify that we do not
approve such superstition and that our effort to correct it will not be
lacking, it nevertheless does not appear to us a sufficiently firm reason
for rejecting a man who is otherwise godly and agrees with us in his
understanding of the same religion. We will tolerate his lack of learning in
this matter to the extent that we do not cast him out of the church or brand
him as unsound in the faith. Meanwhile, neither will we interpret it
maliciously if the pastors of the church at Bern are unwilling to admit to
the ministry of the word those whom they have found to spurn these terms.

### Second appended clarification: Christ as Jehovah

Because we assert that Christ is Jehovah, who always had existence from
himself, and because this clause has seemed somewhat obscure to some, we will
indicate briefly the sense in which we used it, lest anyone be offended. When
Christ's divinity is under discussion, the things proper to God are rightly
attributed to him also, because attention is then directed to the one essence
of God, passing over mention of the distinction between Father and Son. In
this respect Christ is truly said to be the one and eternal God, existing
from himself. Nor is this position contradicted by what the ecclesiastical
writers also truly handed down: that the Word, or Son of God, is from the
Father even with respect to his eternal essence. Indeed, this designation of
persons has its place where the distinction of Father and Son is mentioned.
The name *Jehovah*, however, is a title of divinity which includes the Father
and the Spirit no less than the Son. Thus Cyril, who often calls the Father
the principle of the Son, nevertheless regards it as an immense absurdity in
the third dialogue *On the Trinity* if the Son is not believed to have life
and immortality from himself. He likewise teaches that, if it is proper to
the ineffable nature to be from itself, this properly belongs to the Son.

[CO IX, p. 709]

Indeed, in book 10 of the *Thesaurus* he also reasons that the Father has
nothing from himself unless the Son also has it from himself. Nor should
anyone be troubled because, a little earlier, when explaining the doctrine of
the persons, we said that we would set forth there the matters which pertain
particularly to Christ. It is sufficiently clear that the distinction in
question looks to nothing other than the mystery of the incarnation, whose
particular treatment is distinct from the differentiation of the Trinity.
Otherwise Christ ought not

[CO IX, p. 710]

to have been called Jehovah there, since he does not possess divinity
separately from the Father.

G. Farel, J. Calvin, and P. Viret subscribed.

## Endorsement

We judge that this profession and explanation of our respected brothers and
fellow ministers G. Farel, J. Calvin, and P. Viret ought to satisfy the
churches of Christ.

W. Capito. M. Bucer. O. Myconius. S. Grynaeus.

## Translator's notes

1. CO's title and both internal headings are supplied paratext. The
   manuscripts have no headings for the appended clarifications, while the
   Bern witness calls the first part simply *Confessio Genevensium
   praedicatorum*, “Confession of the Genevan preachers.” The labels above
   therefore identify document layers without claiming authorial wording.
2. The confession itself speaks throughout in the first-person plural. CO's
   historical introduction says Calvin first produced it in the name of the
   Genevan ministers; its surviving subscription names Farel, Calvin, and
   Viret. The wording does not identify a sole drafter.
3. *Hypostasis*, *subsistentia*, and *persona* are retained or translated
   closely because the document distinguishes the one divine essence from
   three non-confused subsistences. The first clarification simultaneously
   defends this vocabulary and refuses to excommunicate an otherwise orthodox
   person merely for hesitating to use it.
4. *Practica notitia* is “practical knowledge”: experiential recognition of
   divine action, contrasted with *otiosa speculatio*, idle speculation.
5. The Greek *ἰδιωμάτων κοινωνία* names the communication or communion
   of properties, illustrated here by predicating what belongs to one nature
   of the single person of Christ.
6. CO's main text has the difficult *qua ratione vera dicitur* on p. 708;
   its Neuchâtel and Amsterdam witnesses read the adverb *vere*. The English
   gives the evident sense, “in this respect ... is truly said,” without
   silently changing `latin.txt`.
7. CO notes divergent punctuation around *Hucusque* on p. 706. The
   translation takes it with the following summary of scriptural testimony;
   this does not alter the substantive claim.
8. The second clarification uses “from himself” while also insisting that
   the Son is from the Father according to eternal essence and possesses no
   divinity separately from him. Its immediate purpose is to explain the
   confession's use of *Iehova*, here rendered “Jehovah” to preserve the
   historical terminology rather than modernize the divine name.
