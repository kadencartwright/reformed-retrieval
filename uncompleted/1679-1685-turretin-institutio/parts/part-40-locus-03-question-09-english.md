QUESTION IX

ON THE IMMENSITY OF GOD

Is God immense and omnipresent according to essence? Affirmative against
Socinus and Vorstius.

I. After God's infinity with respect to essence, that same infinity must be
considered with respect to place and time. Through it God is conceived as
uncircumscribed (ἀπερίγραπτος), bounded by no limits either of time or of
place. The former is called immensity and the latter eternity. They must be
treated distinctly, and first immensity.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question does not concern the presence of power and operation, which
is called operative (ἐνεργητικὴ), in the way the sun is said to be present to
us by the power of its rays although its body is very far distant. The
adversaries do not deny this presence. Rather, the question concerns the
presence of nature, which is called substantial (οὐσιώδης), or essential, and
which they refuse to admit.

III. The question does not concern the various modes of God's special
presence according to which he is said to be in Christ through the hypostatic
union and the fullness of deity, Col. 2:9; in heaven through glory, 1 Kings
8:27; and in the church through grace, Ezek. 48:35. This special presence in
turn is either symbolic presence, when under some visible symbol he reveals
himself to the faithful, as to Moses in the bush, to the people in the pillar
of cloud,
and to the fathers under the form of angels and human beings; or sacramental
and mystical presence, when under outward signs and elements he seals and
presents his grace to us; or spiritual and life-giving presence, when he
dwells through the Spirit in the hearts of the faithful. For no one denies
that these various kinds of God's presence are particular and proper to
definite places. Rather, the subject is God's general presence through
essence, which abstracts from all these singular modes.

IV. God can be said to be present to all things in three ways: 1. through
power and operation; 2. through cognition; and 3. through essence. The
Scholastics customarily express this in the following little verse:

God is everywhere essentially, presently, and powerfully.

He is said to be everywhere through power because he produces and governs all
things and works all things in all. In this sense Paul says in Acts 17, “We
live, move, and exist in him.” Through knowledge he is present to all things
because he sees and beholds all things that exist or happen everywhere as
most present to himself and set before him. Hence in Hebrews 4:13 all things
are said to be naked and open before him, and no creature can be hidden in his
sight. Finally, he is present through essence because his essence penetrates
all things and is, through itself and in its entirety, intimately present to
each and every thing. The present question properly concerns this presence.
For the adversaries do not deny that God is present everywhere through power
and knowledge, as has already been said.

V. Three modes of existing in a place are commonly posited: 1.
circumscriptively, which is attributed to bodies, because they are in place
and space so as to be commensurate with the parts of space; 2. definitively,
which is said of created spirits and incorporeal substances, which are bounded
by definite places and are here in such a way that they are not elsewhere;
and 3. repletively, which is ascribed to God, because his immense essence is
present to all things and, as it were, fills all places. Although this
distinction can be subject

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 179 | IA leaf 233 | PDF p. 233]

to objections when it is cut to the quick, because the expression *to fill*
(τὸ *replere*) seems properly to pertain to a body that occupies a place by
its extension, nevertheless it should not be rashly rejected. It has been
received through long use among philosophers and theologians and can obtain a
suitable sense from the mind of the Holy Spirit himself, who testifies that
God fills heaven and earth, Jer. 23:24, provided that it is skillfully
explained and understood in a sound sense. This is especially so since we
lack proper and precise terms by which to explain God's ubiquity and are
compelled here to use expressions taken from finite and corporeal things.

VI. God is therefore said to be repletively everywhere because of the
immensity of his essence, with this understood in a manner utterly different
from the mode in which bodies exist in a place: that is, without occupying
space and without multiplying, extending, or dividing himself or being mixed
with other things, but unlocally and indivisibly. For wherever he is, he is
whole—whole in all things and whole outside all things, included in no place
and also excluded from none. Nor is he so much in a place, since the finite
does not contain the infinite, as in himself. To this pertains the fact that
the rabbis call God מקום, “place,” because God is not so much contained by a
place as he contains all things in himself. It also pertains to what
Theophilus says in book 1 of *To Autolycus*: “God is the place of all things
and is his own place” (ὁ Θεὸς τόπος ἐστὶ τῶν ὅλων, καὶ ἑαυτῷ τόπος ἐστὶ)—God
is the place of all things and is his own place.

VII. The Socinians together with Vorstius raise a dispute with us about this
immensity and omnipresence of God. They admit presence with respect to power
and operation but deny it with respect to essence, maintaining that God is
contained by heaven, as Socinus in his treatise on Francis Dávid and Crellius
in book 1, chapter 127 of *On True Religion* assert more than once and try to
prove with various arguments. Although Vorstius seems to hold back (ἐπέχειν)
on this question, he nevertheless reveals his opinion clearly enough in his
*Treatise on God* and his *Apology*, when he ranks substantial omnipresence
among things that are incoherent (ἀσύστατα) and impossible (ἀδύνατα). He
expressly teaches that God according to essence is in heaven, but according
to power and efficacy is on earth and present to all creatures.

VIII. The orthodox, however, believe and confess God's immensity and
omnipresence not only with respect to power and operation but especially with
respect to essence. They judge that its mode should neither be investigated
too curiously nor rashly defined. They hold only that this must be maintained
with certainty: it consists neither in the multiplication of the divine
essence, which is numerically one and undivided, nor in the extension and
diffusion of some corporeal mass, since he is the uncreated Spirit, nor in
physical contact, which belongs only to bodies. Rather, it consists in the
simple and to us incomprehensible infinity of the divine essence, which is so
intimately present to all things that it is everywhere in the world and yet
is not included by the world.

[Marginal heading: That God is immense is proved from Scripture.]

IX. The arguments are as follows. 1. Scripture attributes this omnipresence to
God, Psalm 139:7–9: “Where could I go from your Spirit, where could I flee from
your face? If I ascended to the heavens, you are there; or if I made my bed in
the grave, you are there. If I took the wings of the dawn to dwell at the
extremity of the sea, even there your hand would lead me and your right hand
would grasp me.” So also Isaiah 66:1: “Thus says the Lord: Heaven is my seat,
but the earth is my footstool. What is this house that you will build for me?”
There he proves that he cannot be enclosed in handmade temples, not by an
argument drawn from the remoteness of the place in which his proper seat is,
as Vorstius imagines, but from the greatness of his substance, which fills
the whole universe. Indeed, elsewhere, in Jeremiah 23:24, he declares that he
fills heaven and earth: “Do I not fill heaven and earth?” And lest anyone
think that he is contained within heaven or the world, elsewhere the Holy
Spirit testifies in 1 Kings 8:27 that the heavens of heavens cannot contain
him.

X. Crellius objects in vain that these passages concern the omnipresence of
power

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 180 | IA leaf 234 | PDF p. 234]

and efficacy, not of essence. For 1. the words themselves teach the contrary,
since they do not speak only of operation but of essence itself. For what
else do these words mean: “You are there”; “I fill heaven and earth”; “The
heavens of heavens do not contain you”? 2. By parity of reasoning, God must be
in the grave or hell in the same mode in which he is said to be in heaven,
because God's presence is asserted equally in both. Therefore he is either
everywhere with respect to essence or nowhere with respect to essence. 3. The
purpose of the psalm is to show that no one can be hidden from God so as not
to be seen by him, because no one can be where God is not and no one can hide
himself without being easily uncovered by God, since darkness is the same as
light to him. 4. Things set in opposition must be reconciled: the presence of
power does not exclude but necessarily presupposes the presence of essence.
For where God is not, he cannot operate, because he operates immediately
through his essence. Thus if he operates everywhere, he must be everywhere.

XI. 2. The same thing is demonstrated by the passages which teach that God
works all things in all and upholds all things by his powerful Word, Heb. 1:3,
and that he is not far from each one of us, Acts 17:27–28, because in him we
exist, live, and move. For the presence of a spirit can be known in no more
certain way than from its operations, and it is no less impossible for
someone to effect something immediately where he is not than when he does not
exist. Nor should it be said that God works all things in all but does so
through secondary causes, whether physical or hyperphysical, and that
therefore, once that operation is posited, the presence of his essence is not
immediately inferred. For besides the fact that God does not always act
through secondary causes but often immediately through himself, as in the
creation and sustaining of the world, in miracles, and in works of grace in
hearts, when he uses secondary causes he is most immediately present to them
so that he may operate through them.

XII. 3. If God is not immense in essence but finite, it will not involve a
contradiction for a creature greater than God to exist. For just as nothing
can be added to the infinite, so it belongs to the nature of the finite that
something can always be added to it. Indeed, if God's essence is nowhere but
in heaven, the world will be greater than God. Next, if he is finite in
essence, he must also be finite in power, because infinite power cannot be in
a finite subject. Third, if God is not immediately present to creatures by
his essence but only by operation, faith in the incarnation of the Son of God
cannot be placed on safe ground. For what hypostatic union could have taken
place with human nature if the divine essence remained enclosed in heaven and
was not present on earth?

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XIII. God is said to be in heaven, not to the exclusion of the earth, as if he
were enclosed in heaven with respect to essence, but because in heaven, as in
a royal palace, he reveals his glory in a singular mode. And if those who pray
raise their eyes to heaven, whether from the instinct of nature or from the
prescription of the Word, they do this not because God is enclosed in heaven,
which the apostle disputes in Acts 17:24, but 1. so that, as we prepare to
approach God, we may remove all our thoughts from these perishable and earthly
things and think nothing of God except what is great and exalted; 2. so that
we may know that heaven is the seat and throne of God, where both the
revelation is more illustrious and the communication of glory and grace more
abundant, and from which all blessings are to be expected and sought. Thus
under the Old Testament those about to worship turned their faces toward the
ark and the temple, not because God was enclosed in the ark or the temple,
but because of the singular manifestation of grace that was customarily made
there. 3. It is so that we may remember that heaven is also the dwelling
prepared for us by God, toward which we must continually strive. Just as the
Israelites driven into exile looked back to the temple, yearning in their
prayers for their homeland and freedom, so may we be reminded of exile and
pilgrimage, sigh for freedom, and seek our homeland with unwearied strength.
4. It is so that we may show that our prayers are no longer to be

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 181 | IA leaf 235 | PDF p. 235]

directed to a definite place established on earth, but are to be raised into
heaven so that they may be placed upon our altar, who is Christ in heaven, to
be sanctified by him.

XIV. It is not unworthy of the divine majesty to be everywhere on earth, even
in the filthiest places, because he is not there through physical contact or
through any mixture or composition, but as the efficient and preserving cause
of things. 2. If it was not unworthy of God to create all things, even the
most worthless, why would it be unworthy of him to be perpetually present to
them? The sun passes through filthy places with its rays and yet is not
defiled by them; how much less is God?

XV. God is far from the impious with respect to the special presence of favor
and grace, but he is always present to them through the general presence of
essence. Where God is, there his grace indeed is originally and subjectively,
but not always effectively, because its exercise is entirely free.

XVI. It is absurd to say that God is in devils and the impious in the mode in
which he is in the faithful, as a gracious God. But it is not absurd for him
to be in them as God the judge and avenger of crimes, not as an approver of
wicked deeds but as the creator and preserver of nature. Although he is in
hell and in heaven in different modes—in heaven through grace and in hell
through justice, in heaven by blessing and in hell by punishing—he can
nevertheless be in both with respect to the immensity of his essence.

XVII. Although God's essence is abstracted from all created beings, it does
not follow that he cannot be omnipresent with respect to essence. For it is
called abstracted insofar as it is separate from them and is not a part of
them. But it is called omnipresent not because it enters into composition
with them as a part or is contained by them, but insofar as it is bounded by
the essence of none and excluded from none.

XVIII. When God is called immense, so that he is everywhere in the world and
yet is not included by the world, which is finite, but is also conceived as
existing outside the world, this should not be understood positively, as if
some spaces outside the world were to be conceived which God fills with his
presence. Rather, it should be understood negatively, insofar as all the
spaces of the world do not exhaust God's immensity so that he is contained
and circumscribed by them. Therefore he is said to be outside the world
because he is not enclosed by it, not because he is in certain imaginary
places, but because he is in himself in the infinity of his essence. Thus
before the world was created, he was in himself; he himself was place and
world and all things to himself, as Tertullian says in chapter 5 of *Against
Praxeas*.

XIX. One who conceives God as present everywhere by his essence does not by
that very fact conceive God as extended throughout the whole world in the
manner of bodies. Rather, he conceives him by the most simple infinity of his
essence as containing the whole world or as indivisibly coexisting with all
created things, in the way eternity embraces all time as a point in its bosom
and indivisibly coexists with it.

XX. So far is the doctrine of God's essential omnipresence from inducing
atheism, as the Remonstrants maintain in their *Apology*, that on the contrary
its denial favors atheism considerably. For it removes reverence and fear of
God by imagining him to be absent, and therefore either not to see human sins
or to be unable to punish them. But the pious who fear God are said to walk
before God because they are stirred by his presence to worship and obey him.

XXI. It is one thing to declare God's presence through outward operation and
demonstrate it *a posteriori*. It is another to define God's presence *a
priori* by the operation itself, or to posit that God is present only through
power and operation. We grant that the former can rightly be done, but we
deny the latter, because God's operation presupposes his presence: he must be
conceived first as being and existing before he is conceived as acting. If,
therefore, there are some among the more recent writers

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 182 | IA leaf 236 | PDF p. 236]

who define God's omnipresence by his operation, their opinion can be admitted
if they understand this in the former sense, as his manifestation *a
posteriori*. But if they refer this to its constitution *a priori*, it is
rightly rejected as contrary to Scripture and approaching too closely the
error of the Socinians.

XXII. Although God's immensity and omnipresence are always joined, they must
nevertheless be distinguished. Immensity denotes an absolute property in God
which belongs to him from eternity. Omnipresence, founded upon immensity,
denotes a relation to place existing in time. They stand as first and second
act, or as principle and what proceeds from the principle. For omnipresence
arises from immensity and presupposes immensity as its foundation. God is
therefore omnipresent because he is immense.

XXIII. When God is said to ascend or descend, depart or come, this does not
take away omnipresence, because it is said not with respect to essence but
only with respect to the absence or presence of various operations. God
therefore comes when he operates somewhere in a special manner, whether in
grace or in justice. He departs when he withdraws the symbols of his grace and
denies human beings the testimonies of his saving presence. In other respects
we always live, move, and exist in him, as Paul says in Acts 17.

XXIV. The Gentiles themselves were not ignorant of this attribute of God.
Hence Trismegistus says that God is a circle whose center is everywhere and
whose circumference is nowhere. And Aratus says:

All things are full of Jove.

And Virgil:

God goes through all things—
The lands, the tracts of sea, and the deep heaven.

To the same point belong these words:

Where do you flee, Enceladus? Whatever shores you reach,
You will always be under Jove.

Seneca, book 4 of *On Benefits*, chapter 8: “Wherever you turn, there you will
see him meeting you; nothing is empty of him; he fills his work.”
