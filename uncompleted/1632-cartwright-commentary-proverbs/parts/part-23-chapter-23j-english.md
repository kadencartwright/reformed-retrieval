Verse 19. Hear, my son, and be wise, and direct your heart in this way.

Verse 20. Do not be among those drunk with wine, nor among those who gorge themselves on flesh;

Verse 21. For the drunkard and the glutton will become poor, and drowsiness will clothe a man in rags.

In יין, “wine,” and בשר, “flesh,” there is a synecdoche of the part standing for any food and drink.

The purpose is to draw the godly away from intemperance and excess and exhort them to moderation.

There are two parts:
- First he prepares the minds of the godly to listen, verse 19.
- Then he sets forth the command itself and confirms it.

But both may appear unnecessary, since, as has often been said, he addresses the godly. For very many people are found everywhere who are strangers to all godliness and even its appearance, yet do not overstep the bounds of moderation in food and drink. But it must be understood that no one is so devoted to temperance that his mind, infected and corrupted by the common stain of sin, cannot be bent toward this sin by a slight impulse. Or even if someone never departs utterly from the path of temperance, it can still happen that he either does not keep to the right way or does not walk with a sufficiently firm step. Accordingly, it is not unnecessary, but exceedingly useful and necessary, to exhort those free from this sin to adopt minds that abhor it still more; those who aim at moderation to draw closer to it daily; and those who best preserve a due measure to be established in it more and more. Moreover, Satan lies in wait with the greatest intensity for the godly, as is evident from the sad fall of those most excellent men, Noah, Lot, and Uriah. Drunkards and gluttons also make it their chief business to drag godly and sober men into the same excess in which they themselves wallow. Next, since two kinds of intemperance are set forth here, one in drinking and the other in food, it is not so easy to guard against both that there is no danger of falling into one while fleeing the other.

But it must be noted that the prophet, seeking to draw the godly away from drunkenness and gluttony, forbids not merely being drunkards or gluttons, but even associating with those who are such. For some people's disposition is quite remote from all excess and intemperance; yet through the contagion of bad association with strenuous drinkers, after crossing the bounds of sobriety several times, they are finally carried away with such eagerness for drinking and gorging that they closely resemble in character those from whom they formerly differed greatly, Proverbs 13:20. Noah himself, living in an age given over and devoted to drinking parties and revelry, Matthew 24:38, and righteous Lot, dwelling among Sodomites devoted to gluttony, Ezekiel 16:49, appear to have been somewhat spattered and polluted with the stain of intemperance by those among whom they lived. Uriah too, although an outstanding man, affected with grief by awareness of the hardships suffered by the Lord's army, was nevertheless worn down by repeated invitations to drink and finally gave way to drunkenness, 2 Samuel 11.

The dissuasion rests on a reason amplified from the accompanying punishment: namely, poverty in the first half-verse, and beggary in the latter, which is indicated by its adjunct, ragged clothing. Poverty and beggary are brought upon them partly because they squander in gluttony the goods they possess, partly because, languishing in idleness, they abstain from labor by which more might be acquired. This inactivity arises not only because, in eating and drinking, they

[1632 witness | PDF p. 522 | printed columns 1028-1029]

consume much time that ought to have been given to work, but because intemperance makes them sleepy and drowsy, while a great quantity of vapors, exhaled from an overstuffed stomach and transmitted to the brain, burdens the head with heaviness and induces sleep. Since, then, someone who indulges in sleep, whatever its origin, does not escape poverty, Proverbs 6:11, what shall we think will happen to someone who, to say nothing of rash agreements and ruinous contracts, buys sleep at the price needed to satisfy gluttony and drunkenness?

It is asked why he mentions this lighter punishment, omitting the more severe punishment of eternal death, which the apostle pronounces against all drunkards in 1 Corinthians 6:10. Answer, first: because this threat is most suited to the teaching of the law, which formerly invited the Jews, like children, to obedience to God through outward promises, or deterred them from rebellion through threats. Next, because most people are more moved by the calamities of this life than by eternal torments, and therefore refrain from wrongdoing in order to live comfortably in this world, although they are guided by no awareness of future life or death. Such were the Sadducees of old. Furthermore, this punishment, which follows drunkards not always but ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ, “for the most part,” appears more suited to proverbial teaching. Moreover, Solomon particularly pursues that teaching which concerns the preservation or destruction not only of the church but also of the commonwealth. Finally, by setting forth this one punishment he does not exclude the other. Indeed, it is useful for both punishments to remain before our eyes, so that if, taken separately, they are insufficient in view of our inclination toward intemperance, yet together they may avail to tear us away from it.

### Translator's notes

The source's two-branched analytical diagram is represented as a two-item list. The Latin retains *querentē*, *nun*, and *morem*; their contextual senses are respectively “seeking,” “not,” and “of character,” corresponding to expected *quaerentem*, *non*, and *morum*. The printed *reddūt* is also retained despite its imperfect grammatical agreement with *intemperantia*. The medical account of vapors rising from the stomach belongs to Cartwright's historical explanation.
