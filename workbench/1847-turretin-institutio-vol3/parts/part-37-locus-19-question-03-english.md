QUESTION III.

ON THE SACRAMENTAL SIGN.

What is the nature of the sign required in a sacrament?

I. Since everyone agrees that the sacraments are certain signs and that, with
respect to them, *sign* has the character of a genus, it is rightly asked what
kind of signs they are, for theologians do not agree about this.

[Marginalia: What a sign is.]

II. A sign in general, as Augustine well observes in book 2 of *On Christian
Doctrine*, chapter 1, is “a thing which, besides the appearance it presents to
the senses, causes something else to come from itself into thought.” Thus in a
sign attention is directed not so much to what it is as to what it signifies
and shows, as Augustine adds in the same place. And in book 3 *To Maximinus*,
chapter 22: “In the sacraments attention is always directed not to what they
are but to what they show, since they are signs of things, existing as one
thing and signifying another.” Although signs do not exist without some
material subject, no account is taken of that subject here; attention is
directed only to their signification. Thus in matrimony attention is directed
not to the price of the ring or the worth of its material, but to its use for
sealing.

[Marginalia: And of what kind is it in the sacraments?]

[Marginalia: Not natural,]

III. Since signs are manifold, we must see to what genus of signs sacramental
signs belong. Here we say first, (1) they are not natural signs, which are such
by nature and have the power of signifying from themselves, such as smoke
signifying fire, dawn the rising sun, and pallor disease. For things that
signify naturally

[1847 vol. III printed p. 295 | IA leaf 329 | PDF p. 329]

signify both all such things and always, which cannot be said of the
sacraments.

[Marginalia: but voluntary.]

Rather, they are voluntary signs, which are such by imposition from the
institution of God or human beings and which Augustine calls *given signs*.

[Marginalia: Not by human but by divine institution, with an analogy.]

IV. (2) They are not signs by human but by divine institution, because the
right to signify and seal grace belongs to the same one to whom it belongs to
promise and give it. (3) They are not signs by imposition alone, depending
solely on the decision of the one instituting them, such as the rainbow and
the scarlet cord. Rather, they are also such signs according to analogy,
whether the character of signifying arises from a property or from a likeness.
They have their imposition from God’s institution but their analogy from
likeness. Augustine says in letter 23: “If the sacraments did not possess some
likeness, they would not be sacraments.” (4) They are not merely theoretical
signs, which do nothing other than represent and signify the thing of which
they are signs, but practical signs, which not only signify but also seal and
actually exhibit. For although the signs theoretically signify our mystical
profession, the efficacy of Christ’s grace in us, and his passion for us, for
whose remembrance they were instituted, they do not end with this theoretical
signification. In addition, they have a practical signification that both
seals and, in its own manner and sense, exhibits the thing signified. Thus the
handing over of a key has the practical signification of putting someone into
possession and both seals and exhibits that possession.

V. I acknowledge that an external thing cannot exhibit grace physically,
substantially, and immediately, but nothing prevents it from doing so
instrumentally and mediately, with the Holy Spirit principally performing
this work. Nor, if it belongs to the Holy Spirit alone to confer grace upon us
effectively, does it follow that the sacraments cannot seal and exhibit it
morally, for subordinate things do not conflict. Although the sacraments in
many cases are without effect accidentally because of the recipients’ fault,
they do not cease to be practical signs in themselves and by God’s
institution—not in opposition to faith, but in composition with it. This will
be seen more fully below when their efficacy is treated.

VI. (5) They are not intelligible signs, such as images in the mind, the sign
of Jonah, and the like, but sensible signs that affect the senses. This is
evident both from a survey of the sacraments themselves, all of which are
constituted in a sensible thing, and from human nature. We have been so formed
that nothing passes into our minds except by the aid and service of the senses.
(6) They are not audible but visible signs. We maintain this against the
papists, who hold that “it is enough for a sacrament to be perceived by some
sense, and chiefly by hearing,” in order to favor their fictitious sacraments.
The reasons are these. 1. Every sacrament must consist of things as its matter
and the word as its form: let the word be added to the element, and it will
become a sacrament. But no audible object consists of things and words. 2.
Otherwise there would be no distinction between the Word and a sacrament,
which is absurd. 3. If words were sacramental signs, they would be signs either
insofar as they sound or insofar as they signify. Not insofar as they sound,
because in that respect they would have no likeness; not insofar as they
signify, because signification affects the mind, not the sense. 4. The Fathers
throughout call the sacraments “visible things,” “visible symbols of things
understood,” and, as Augustine speaks, “a visible word.” Furthermore, that
visible sign ought to be a certain substance and thing, not an accident of a
thing, because the analogy is taken from the very nature of the thing and from
its properties. This is evident in all the sacraments and is the point of
Augustine’s rule cited above: “Let the word be added to the element, and it
will become a sacrament.”

VII. (7) They are not only signs that commemorate past things, such as the
heap of stones in the Jordan and Aaron’s rod in the tabernacle. They are also
signs that signify future things, such as the rainbow, Gideon’s fleece, and
the burning bush; and signs that seal or exhibit present things, which
demonstrate and seal that the thing they indicate is actually

[1847 vol. III printed p. 296 | IA leaf 330 | PDF p. 330]

given, insofar as this can be done. Thus the Supper takes place in
commemoration of Christ’s past death, in the
sealing of present grace or communion in his flesh and blood, and in the
signification of future glory and the wedding feast that is to be celebrated
in heaven.

[Marginalia: Not formal but instrumental.]

VIII. (8) They are not formal signs, which denote the very intelligible
species received by the cognitive faculty to which the sign is proposed.
Rather, they are instrumental signs, which represent themselves to the senses
and a thing different from themselves to the intellect, making it present in
their own manner. For God uses them as organs and instruments to offer and
confer grace upon us in his own manner. The formal aspect of a sign, however,
must not be confused with a formal sign. The former is opposed to a sign
considered materially. Thus in the Supper the bread is the material aspect of
the sacramental sign, but if it is considered as a sign, it is a sign of
Christ’s body. This pertains to its formal aspect, for it is considered
reduplicatively as the sign of Christ’s body. A formal sign, by contrast, is
distinguished not from a material sign but from an instrumental sign. It is
the intelligible species itself, aroused in the mind through the sign in its
formal aspect.

IX. This bears upon the question raised here by the Lutherans, who insist that
the genus of a sacrament is an action rather than a sign, because sacraments
outside their use are not sacraments and because the effects of the sacraments
are the effects of actions, not signs. But it is far more correct to say that
a sacrament is a sign than an action. Scripture speaks in this way throughout
(Gen. 17; Rom. 4:11; 1 Pet. 3:21), and all antiquity agrees, among whom the
sacraments are everywhere called signs, symbols, types, antitypes, and images.

X. Although the sacraments are not signs outside their use and do not signify
except by means of an action, it does not follow that they are actions rather
than signs. This is common to all signs by institution, which do not thereby
cease to be signs. In the same way, spoken words do not cease to be signs of
things first and in themselves because they do not signify unless someone is
acting and speaking.

XI. The sacraments can be considered either *in actu signato*, precisely in
themselves, or *in actu exercito*, as they are conferred by a minister and
received by a believer. Considered in the first manner, the elements in their
mystical and signifying existence have the character of sacramental signs
apart from the circumstance of any action, such as bread and wine in the
Eucharist and water in baptism. But in the second manner certain actions
intervene, such as the breaking of the bread to be distributed and its
distribution for communion in the body of Christ. They do not accomplish this
through their natural and physical existence, but through their mystical and
signifying existence.

XII. The question can therefore be resolved easily if we say that the
sacraments are signs, but signs clothed and administered with certain rites,
ceremonies, and actions. Thus a sign considered materially, or in its material
existence, is not a sacrament; it is a sacrament formally and reduplicatively
as a sign. It cannot be such in an exercised act except with certain actions,
for substance simply in itself is not the foundation of the relation, but
substance dispensed with certain rites is.

XIII. Furthermore, it must be observed concerning this sign, which is the
material aspect of the sacrament and the foundation of the relation, first,
that it is common to all who partake; second, that it is something ordinary
and taken from common use, so that the rites also may be exceedingly simple
and easy. In this way, the whole dignity and efficacy of the sacraments is
from God.
