# Preface to Nicolas Duchemin's *Antapologia*

Paris, 6 March 1531

This is a fresh English translation of John Calvin's complete dedicatory
preface printed in *Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. IX
(Corpus Reformatorum XXXVII), pp. 785–786. It belongs to Calvin's early
humanist and legal milieu rather than his later theological writing. The CO
title is editorial; Calvin's own text opens as a letter to François Connan.

## Translation

### [CO IX, p. 785]

John Calvin to François Connan, most devoted student of law, greetings.

At last our Duchemin's *Apology* is coming out and submitting to the common
hazard faced by authors, although it was not written with the intention that
it should ever appear in public. Yet it was fair that these things too, which
that man had composed for the sake of stimulating and exercising his talent,
should be published, lest L'Estoile lack his own defender and some Albucius or
other sing paeans of victory to himself without blood or sweat. If anyone
prefers Alciati in place of Albucius, I would neither dare affirm it nor wish
to refute it. But certain shrewder people scent that Alciati wished to be
disguised under a borrowed name, lest the eloquence of his excessively biting
and impatient tongue be repaid by the law of retaliation.

No one should suppose, however, that L'Estoile lacked resources, or interpret
as a confession his silence, by which until now he has allowed Albucius the
victory. Instead, let him understand that a man occupied with serious business
and supported in this matter too by confidence in the truth did not wish to
waste effort on things scarcely necessary, since the matter spoke sufficiently
for itself. Otherwise, with the sharpness of intellect, industry, and finally
legal expertise with which he is endowed, he would already have aimed his pen
at a thousand men like Albucius. In legal expertise he holds first place in
our time, without controversy, alongside only one or two others.

Duchemin too had adopted this conviction and could not bring himself to
publish this little work unless he was overcome by the solid arguments of
certain people who showed that his patience was harming L'Estoile. For certain
cavillers argued that L'Estoile would not have failed his own cause if he had
been able to construct a defense by any means, since he had resisted Zasius so
steadfastly. Driven by this apparent necessity, our Duchemin changed his mind.
What he had wished to keep suppressed for almost two years he now sets forth
publicly to crush their slanders. Above all, however, he wished to ensure that
it did not come into people's hands mutilated and corrupted. Accordingly, when
he heard that I was preparing a journey to Paris, in view of the friendship
and familiarity that

### [CO IX, p. 786]

exists between us, he entrusted me with this role: to apply myself diligently
to the task of preventing any errors from creeping in. I willingly accepted
the assignment, but on the condition that I should be compelled to answer for
no fault except negligence. For I shall appear to have discharged my duty
admirably here if I exercise the diligence he desired.

Alciati ought not to take it badly when he considers, first, that he is being
attacked justly, and then that it is done modestly and respectfully, without
omitting the due preliminary acknowledgment of his distinction. He favors the
public good so greatly, I think, and yields so much to truth that he would not
wish to dictate to it under the pretext of his own authority. Since he
understands, therefore, that truth lies submerged in the depths, let him also
allow it to be dug out by disputations—provided care is taken that the very
truth being sought is not lost through excessive wrangling.

I wished to say this in passing so that at the same time I might clear myself
both to Alciati and to you. I fear that affection for the man may incline you
toward the opposing cause, and that you may turn into an accusation the very
fact that I do not stand on Alciati's side. For I know how enthusiastically
you have been accustomed to praise him: a most grateful pupil, naturally, of
an excellent teacher. But I have also learned repeatedly—both recently in
familiar conversation and earlier from your many letters—how favorably you
regard L'Estoile, whom you also heard, and how honorably you speak of him. I
should therefore have no reason at all to doubt that you will bring no
preconceived opinion to this dispute, especially since you know well the man
who undertook to defend the whole matter by his judgment: our Duchemin.

As you know, Duchemin is a man most capable of sustained study, of penetrating
intellect, and—what is especially excellent—of most exact judgment. Perfectly
polished in humane letters, he is now successfully engaged in legal studies
and was engaged in them for a very long time before.

As far as the present case is concerned, let readers have freedom of judgment:
not uninitiated readers, to be sure, but those who have penetrated somewhat
more deeply into the mysteries of law. Indeed, most learned Connan, judge for
yourself. You are not one of the crowd, but are truly exempted from that class
by the merit of your deeper learning. For my part, I think that, as it now
stands, this disputation is of such a kind that it can readily earn your
judgment and that of any candid reader—and certainly ought to do so.

Farewell. Paris, the day before the Nones of March—that is, 6 March.

## Translator's notes

1. The preface's legal controversy involved the jurists Andrea Alciati and
   Pierre de L'Estoile (*Stella*), with Aurelio Albuzio (*Albucius*) writing a
   defense of Alciati and Nicolas Duchemin (*Cheminus*) answering it. Calvin
   reports but does not certify the suggestion that “Albucius” concealed
   Alciati himself.
2. “The law of retaliation” renders *ius talionis*: repayment in kind for the
   attack of a biting tongue.
3. CO prints the unusual *vitiligatores* for “cavillers.” Its apparatus notes
   that Herminjard emended the word from Pliny to *vitilitigatores*. The
   normalized Latin follows the printed main text and the English gives its
   evident polemical sense.
4. Ulrich Zasius was a leading jurist with whom L'Estoile had engaged in
   controversy. Calvin invokes that earlier resistance as the cavillers'
   argument that silence toward Albucius showed inability rather than
   restraint.
5. *Provinciam* is an assigned sphere of duty or commission. Calvin sharply
   limits his responsibility to editorial diligence, not Duchemin's legal
   argument.
6. “Most capable of sustained study” renders *lucubrationum patientissimus*.
   *Lucubratio* evokes study or composition by lamplight; the phrase praises
   Duchemin's endurance in learned work.
7. *Ad unguem expolitus*—literally “polished to the fingernail”—is an idiom
   for perfect finish or exact refinement.
8. “Uninitiated” renders *profani* in a metaphor that treats jurisprudence as
   possessing inner mysteries; it does not mean religiously impious readers.
9. The closing *pridie Nonas Martias* is 6 March in the Roman calendar. The
   work's 1531 publication supplies the year; the Latin closing itself does
   not repeat it.
