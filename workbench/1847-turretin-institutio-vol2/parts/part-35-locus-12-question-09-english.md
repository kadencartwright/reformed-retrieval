QUESTION IX.

THE SURETY OF THE COVENANT OF GRACE.

*Did Christ under the Old Testament have only the character of a Surety as
guarantor, or also that of an expromissor? The former is denied; the latter is
affirmed.*

I. For the *nature of the covenant of grace* to be known more fully, the nature
and mode of the *suretyship* upon which it rests must be examined more closely.
The purpose is not to inquire into Christ’s mediatorial office, which will be
treated afterward in its proper place, but only to examine more thoroughly the
mode of the suretyship undertaken by him under the Old Testament, concerning
which a question can be raised.

II. Here we must first recall what we observed before. This covenant of grace,
which was instituted between an offended God and guilty, sinful man, could not
have been ratified except through the intervention of a Mediator who would
reconcile the dissenting parties to each other and discharge the role of
Surety, not

[Printed p. 213 | Internet Archive PDF 241 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

only by pledging to us *in God’s name* that he would fulfill the promises of
the covenant if we fulfilled its conditions, as Curcellaeus, following
Socinus, perversely quibbles, but also by pledging himself *to God on our
behalf* that he would fulfill for us all those conditions whose performance
would cause the testamentary inheritance to come to us. This principally
concerned rendering the fullest satisfaction to divine justice for our sins.
No controversy occurs here among the orthodox, but only with the Socinians
and their followers, against whom we will have to argue when satisfaction is
treated.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

III. Therefore, the question raised here properly concerns the mode of the
suretyship undertaken by Christ under the Old Testament: whether it has only
the character of a guaranty or also that of an expromission. Some number this
among the other differences between the Old and New Testaments: that under the
former Christ was indeed a guarantor but not an expromissor. That is, he
indeed pledged himself for the elect, but in such a way that no absolute
transfer of the debt to him was made and the fathers were not freed from the
guilt of sin. Instead, God retained a right against them to inflict punishment
if Christ had abandoned his suretyship and had not satisfied him.

In this manner civil law customarily distinguishes a guarantor from an
expromissor: “A guarantor is one who is obligated for another in such a way
that the other nevertheless remains obligated until the guarantor pays.”
Hence Justinian observes in *Institutes* 3, title 21, that “the obligation of
guarantors is an accession to the principal obligation.” But an expromissor
is said to be one “who takes another’s obligation upon himself, the defendant
being released or not obligated under the name by which he otherwise ought to
be obligated.”

IV. But the common opinion of the orthodox, with whom we agree, is that from
the time Christ’s suretyship was undertaken, it had not only the character of
a guaranty but also of an expromission, through which the ancients were by
that very fact truly freed from the penalties they owed. Nor do we think it
necessary here to distinguish anxiously between a guarantor and an
expromissor. Apart from the fact that the usage of these terms is not so
different that jurists do not often employ them interchangeably, guaranty can
have the force of expromission. As Cujas testifies (*Observations* 13.35), “An
expromissor,” he says, “is called as it were a guarantor,” law *in persona*,
§1, *de pactis*. “And in the Velleian senatus consultum expromission is
included under the word guaranty”; and law 10, *de in rem*: “These are
equivalent—to give a guaranty, to undertake a judgment for another, and to
expromise.” Thus it should not seem strange if, in describing Christ’s
suretyship, the terms guaranty and expromission are used interchangeably.

Although, strictly speaking, this distinction may have a place in civil law,
we do not think it fitting in this argument to appeal to political tribunals
in order to explain the nature of Christ’s suretyship from legal formulas. It
is evident how immensely distant the court of earth is from the court of
heaven. Here the matter concerns not human but divine judgment; not a
monetary debt and a mortal creditor, but a penal debt and God as Judge and
Ruler of the world; not an ordinary surety and mere man, but an extraordinary
θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man*, who as ἀντίψυχος, *a substitute for another’s life*,
places himself in our stead and takes upon himself the penalties owed by us.
The civil laws describe no such Surety. Therefore, we judge that Christ’s
suretyship cannot be sufficiently explained either by the guaranty or by the
expromission found in a human court, since it is far more perfect than

[Printed p. 214 | Internet Archive PDF 242 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

both. Nevertheless, if we must speak according to their usage and employ
their terms because no others are available, we say that it comes closer to
expromission than to guaranty, since it contains eminently and most perfectly
whatever perfection and efficacy an expromission possesses. Various reasons
are not lacking.

V. First, from *the nature of Christ’s suretyship*. Since this suretyship is
nothing other than Christ’s most absolute and immutable will to substitute
himself, according to the Father’s eternal counsel, in the place of the elect
and to satisfy for their sins, by that very fact it necessarily entails a true
transfer of their debts to him and the full liberation and immunity of those
for whom he pledged himself. Thus guaranty, insofar as it is distinguished
from expromission, cannot simply have a place here; expromission must.

For a guarantor is appointed only to make confidence about payment more
certain. He does not necessarily promise absolutely that the payment will be
his own, but ensures that the creditor is certain of payment, in a case where
the principal debtor can be supposed still solvent or at least remains
obligated and can and ought to be approached first before recourse is had to
the guarantor. But the case here is altogether different. Since the elect
were absolutely insolvent in this way, satisfaction could never be expected
from them. Through Christ’s suretyship and substitution in their place, they
were so freed by God’s counsel that payment should no longer have been
demanded from them, for Christ had taken it upon himself by his suretyship.

In this he truly fulfilled the role of an expromissor, who takes another’s
obligation upon himself so that payment is demanded and expected from him
alone, while the principal debtor no longer remains obligated. For he
obligated himself by his suretyship to payment in such a way that it could be
sought and exacted from him alone. The elect, who were the principal debtors,
were freed from every personal obligation and were so entirely no longer
obligated that if they themselves had wished to pay, they would deservedly
have incurred the guilt of those who despise the Messiah and, disregarding
God’s righteousness, attempt to establish their own. Such was the guilt of
the Jews (Rom. 9:31, 32; 10:3).

VI. Scripture itself demonstrates not obscurely that Christ’s suretyship is
of this kind when it testifies that God “laid upon him the iniquities of us
all, and the chastisement of our peace was placed upon him, so that by his
wounds healing might be ours” (Isa. 53:5, 6). How can human sins be said to
have been laid upon Christ if his suretyship was not absolute, involving a
true transfer of debt to himself and the release of the guilty? Nor should it
be said that “Jesus took up sins only when he prepared himself for sacrifice,
and that God laid sins upon him because they were then actually exacted from
him.” For it is one thing to demand the debt from Christ for present payment;
it is another to lay sins upon him and impute them to him. The Surety’s debt
can have been imputed long before it is exacted for present payment. Isaiah
does not teach that sins were laid upon Christ when they were actually
exacted from him, but that sins were exacted from Christ through his endurance
of punishments because they had already been imputed to him as Surety in God’s
counsel.

VII. To this pertains Christ’s declaration in Ps. 40:7, 8 that he came to do
the Father’s will, that is, to offer the sacrifice of his body in place of the
legal victims which God rejected as useless for this purpose. Why would he
display so great an eagerness and readiness to fulfill this office unless his
suretyship was already absolute and unconditional, entailing the true
transfer of the penalties owed by us? It does not help to reply that “Paul
refers these words to Christ’s entrance into the world” (Heb. 10:5). Although
Christ could ascribe these words to himself as he entered the world, nothing
prevents them from already applying to him at the time of which the psalmist
speaks.

Indeed, this is necessarily presupposed, because the subject is God’s eternal
and immutable will, by which, all the legal sacrifices having been rejected,
Christ was appointed Surety and his satisfaction alone was decreed and
promised from the very beginning. Thus it is written of him *at the head of
the book*, that is, Scripture, and Christ’s own response is added, by which he
accepts God’s call and declares himself

[Printed p. 215 | Internet Archive PDF 243 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

ready to carry it out. Nor does the statement that he is “the Lamb slain from
the foundation of the world” (Rev. 13:8) pertain to anything else than to
designate the efficacy and certainty of this suretyship. By it the sacrifice
that was to occur only after many ages was already regarded as offered.

VIII. Second, *the nature of the thing itself* requires this, whether we
consider *God*, to whom the suretyship had to be made; the *Surety*, who was
bound to make it; the *elect themselves*, for whom he pledged himself; or the
*nature of the suretyship itself* together with its effects. With respect to
God, his justice demanded a true expromissor, not a mere guarantor. Just as it
could not expect payment from the first debtors, so it could not acquiesce in
anything except the Son’s absolute suretyship. Nor was it fitting for justice
that Christ and the elect should remain obligated at the same time by the
same decree, since he was given to them as Surety precisely so that they would
thereby be set free.

Nor could God’s wisdom, foreknowing the certain payment to be rendered by
Christ alone, keep under obligation as debtors those from whom payment was
never to be sought, and still regard them as guilty. Indeed, since God’s works
are known to him from eternity (Acts 15:18), by the light of omniscience this
suretyship was regarded by God not only as future but as already present—indeed,
as completed. Through its efficacy it gave the fathers the same benefits that
we enjoy, which a mere guaranty could not have furnished.

IX. *The character of the Surety* also entails this. Since he is infinite and
most perfect God, his suretyship had to be most perfect and to embrace
whatever perfection can be conceived as contained in an expromissor for the
liberation of debtors. Further, he is a Surety who was neither found nor given
by men, but by God himself; indeed, he offered himself to the Father from
eternity and substituted himself in the place of sinners without their
knowledge or desire. He alone was to render payment by God’s immutable decree,
whose certainty was confirmed by various promises and sealed by many types.
He could not have accomplished this unless he had already then fulfilled the
role of an expromissor, and not only that of a guarantor.

Thus the efficacy and effect of this suretyship was such that by its power the
fathers were completely freed from all penalties deserved by them, so that
payment could no longer be exacted from them. The learned men with whom we
are now concerned could not deny this. Hence the celebrated Cocceius says in
*Summary of Theology* 35.2: “The effect of the suretyship was that after sin
had been committed, it was not imputed to the heirs inscribed in the testament;
that is, that there could not be in God a will to punish sin in them, since it
had been driven upon the Surety and had to fall upon him and be exacted from
him” (Isa. 53:6, 7). And in §134: “For he was substituted for them, became
their Surety, and their sins were laid upon him.”

X. Third, the *elect themselves*, for whom the suretyship was made, required
an expromissor, not merely a guarantor. Their Surety had to be ἀντίψυχος, *a
substitute for another’s life*, as was said before, who would substitute
himself for the guilty in order to suffer the penalties they owed and thereby
free them. A mere guarantor cannot furnish this, for he pledges himself only
to make the creditor’s confidence of receiving payment more certain, but not
necessarily of receiving his own payment. Further, just as the elect were no
longer solvent, so payment could never be expected from them; indeed, by
God’s decree it was never to be demanded. Therefore, the Surety had to take
payment absolutely upon himself and thus possess in reality the character of
an expromissor.

Finally, since they were not only to be saved through Christ but were in fact
saved and received into heaven by the power of Christ’s suretyship, it is not
enough that Christ was a guarantor, which does not entail the personal
liberation of the principal debtors. He necessarily had to possess the
character of an expromissor, who acquires complete liberation for those for
whom he pledges himself.

[Printed p. 216 | Internet Archive PDF 244 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Otherwise, it would have to be said that they were freed and yet remained
obligated, were regarded as righteous before God and yet were always under
guilt, which is ἀσύστατον, *inconsistent*.

XI. Fourth, *the nature of this suretyship* indicates it. Whatever is absolute
and immutable in an expromission is found far more perfectly and eminently in
Christ’s suretyship. In an expromission, the expromissor alone is obligated
to payment, while the principal debtor no longer remains obligated, and the
debt is demanded and payment expected from the expromissor alone. Likewise
here, Christ’s suretyship excludes the elect’s own payment, because he
obligated himself to payment so absolutely that by that very fact they were
freed from every personal obligation and payment could be sought and expected
only from him. This is further confirmed by the end of the suretyship itself,
which was nothing other than the absolute liberation of the elect. Since that
end could not fail, the suretyship necessarily had to take on the nature of
expromission, not only of guaranty.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XII. Nor can it be said that *God always reserved to himself the liberty or
right to exact payment from the elect, or principal debtors*. Christ’s
suretyship was absolute by the immutable decree of God, by which he was
substituted in the place of sinners and alone had to bear the penalties owed
by them. Given that decree that the penalties were to be demanded only from
the Surety, it cannot be conceived how human beings still remained obligated
and God reserved to himself the liberty to exact payment from them.

It is indeed true that in a civil court a creditor has the right to demand a
debt from either of two co-obligors, whether the principal debtor or the
guarantor. But after a debt has been contracted and the debtor has become
insolvent, when a Surety intervenes who takes upon himself the whole burden of
discharging the debt and whose credit the creditor follows, by that very fact
the first debtor is withdrawn from every exaction. Otherwise the creditor
would act against his admission of the suretyship and would seem to call the
Surety’s credit into doubt.

Thus here, because Christ by his suretyship took upon himself the burden of
paying for sinners, by that very fact God laid aside the will to exact the debt
from them. *Divine equity* could not allow two to remain obligated when God,
content with the satisfaction of one and his absolute obligation to it, had
fully acquiesced in it. Nor could *wisdom and truth* permit the debt to be
exacted from those for whom a Surety was appointed by an absolute decree and
in whom they were justified and exempted from every penalty. Nor could
*goodness and mercy* permit God to regard as guilty and indebted those for
whose liberation the Surety presented himself at God’s judgment.

XIII. It is objected in vain that *payment could always have been exacted from
the elect while satisfaction had not yet been rendered, on the supposition
that Christ had not stood by his suretyship and promises but had abandoned
his undertaking*. This is to imagine what is plainly ἀσύστατον,
*inconsistent*. Certainly, when a suretyship or undertaking by a deceitful and
inconstant man is in question, his abandonment of it is reasonably feared.
Hence creditors admit a guaranty in such a way that they do not relinquish
their right against the debtors, but retain it and exercise it if the Surety’s
promised payment fails.

But what danger of abandoning the undertaking can be conceived in Christ,
who is most truthful, powerful, and good? Indeed, what possibility of it? How
could his absolute promise, made in God’s eternal decree and from that decree
in the oracles of the Old Testament—“Behold, I come to do your will,” and so
forth, that is, to satisfy for those whom you have given me—have been revoked
without thereby rescinding God’s immutable decree and changing election to
salvation, or making it coexist with the punishment of the elect? Since this
is plainly ἀδύνατον, *impossible*, the supposition from which it is deduced
must also be absurd.

XIV. Although payment had not yet been rendered under the Old Testament, it
does not

[Printed p. 217 | Internet Archive PDF 245 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

follow that the fathers were still obligated to it in such a way that it
could have been exacted from them. Payment had to be rendered only by the
Surety, from whom alone it had to be demanded and expected according to God’s
eternal decree and covenant. It is true that on account of the union between
us and Christ, when Christ satisfied for us it is the same as if we ourselves
had satisfied through him (2 Cor. 5:15, 21). Nevertheless, it would be less
accurate to say that the believers of the Old Testament were obligated to
payment to be rendered through the Mediator. On the contrary, Christ, as the
Surety of sinners appointed from eternity, was obligated by the power of his
suretyship to satisfy for them.

Certainly, what someone does through another is to be regarded as done by
himself if he substitutes in his place the person through whom he is said to
act and gives him the authority and powers to do something on his own. But
the case here is altogether different. We neither substituted Christ in our
place nor even thought of him. He was freely given to us, and we could give
him no power or capacity to do what he performed for us.

XV. Just as it is no injury to Christ the θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man*, to be called
*sin and curse* in time, so it is not inconsistent for him to be obligated from
eternity to the actual endurance of that curse in the nature he was to assume,
which is to take upon himself the elect’s vicarious guilt. If it was not
unbecoming for Christ to become our Mediator, neither could it be unbecoming
for him to accomplish all that the nature of his office and the salvation of
the human beings in whose place he acted required. Here Christ must not be
regarded simply as λόγος, *the Word*, but economically as the future
θεάνθρωπος, *God-man*.

XVI. The transfer of a monetary debt to a Surety is not of the same nature as
the transfer of a penal debt. The former can be absolute and complete with
respect to every aspect of the debt, because attention is paid not to who
pays but only to what is paid—not to the person, but to the money. In criminal
matters, however, where the person and not only the punishment is considered,
the obligation cannot be transferred in all its parts to a Surety. It can be
transferred only in one respect, namely, with respect to a particular penalty
to be inflicted on the Surety in place of the guilty person, but not with
respect to the personal obligation, or the penalty to be borne in one’s own
person.

Our sins are said to be transferred to Christ in the former respect, not the
latter. Our personal obligation was not transferred to Christ but graciously
dissolved. Hence, although our sins can be called Christ’s with respect to
the imputation of guilt, they do not cease to be ours with respect to
ownership and commission. Just as he substituted himself in our place, so
whatever he paid is rightly said to have been paid for us and in our name.
For when someone obligates himself as ἀντίψυχος, *a substitute for another’s
life*, to suffer for another’s offenses, he must always suffer in the other’s
place unto satisfaction. No other payment can be conceived here than a
vicarious payment arising from gracious acceptance.

XVII. Although we call Christ an expromissor, we do not thereby mean that he
is a mere expromissor or that a juridical *novation* occurred here. A novation
differs in various ways from Christ’s suretyship, but especially because
through novation an obligation can become another’s in all its parts.
Monetary debts can be fully transferred to another, and their payment need
not be said to occur in another’s place. As has just been said, this does not
apply in criminal matters.

A novation also differs in that when a delegated debtor is obligated to
payment through novation, no remission occurs, since the obligation to pay
the same thing that is owed remains. That does not occur in this matter,
where only a vicarious penalty is paid for us, not our own and personal
penalty. We press only this point in order to explain the perfection and
efficacy of Christ’s suretyship, which cannot be sufficiently understood
through guaranty alone. Thus Christ’s suretyship must always be called far
more perfect and excellent than any civil expromission.

XVIII. When Christ is called the ἔγγυος, *Surety*, of the New Covenant (Heb.
7:22), his suretyship must not be restricted to guaranty alone. Besides the
fact that Paul’s words refer no less to the time of the New Testament, in
which no one denies that he was an expromissor, the term itself in accepted
usage denotes not only a mere guarantor but also an expromissor. Hesychius
gives “Ἔγγυος, ἀνάδοχος,” *a surety, one who undertakes*, while ἀναδέχεσθαι,
*to undertake*, is the proper act of an expromissor, who takes the debt upon
himself and acknowledges it as his own. Budaeus is a witness, and Martinius in
his philosophical lexicon at the entry ἐγγύη, *suretyship*: “Sponsio,
expromissio, ἀναδοχή,” *suretyship, expromission, undertaking*.

XIX. The legal and ἀλόγων, *irrational*, victims do not stand in the same
relation as the heavenly and divine Victim. Guilt could not be imposed on the
former before they were offered, because the transfer was made to them by
sinners at God’s ordinance, with no consent on the animals’ part, for the
expiation of their sin. But nothing prevents the transfer from having been
made to Christ before he was actually offered, since he willingly undertook
that office by God’s decree. Indeed, his voluntary promise and suretyship
necessarily had to precede his oblation, so that its fruit might flow to the
believers of the Old Testament, who through faith had to apply to themselves
the power of Christ’s merit, which was already then efficacious for their
liberation.

XX. Although the ceremonial law with its sacrifices, in its legal σχέσει,
*relation*, was like a promissory note which pressed the right arising from
the violated covenant of works, this did not entail that Christ’s suretyship
was a mere guaranty and that believers still remained obligated under guilt.
For in its evangelical σχέσει, *relation*, insofar as it was a type of Christ,
it foreshadowed liberation from guilt and the justification given in Christ
the Surety. Hence circumcision is called the seal of the righteousness of
faith (Rom. 4:11), and the Rock is Christ (1 Cor. 10:4). Whoever desires more
should consult the celebrated *Leydekker*, theologian and professor at Utrecht,
who treats this argument fully and solidly in book 2 of *The Force of Truth*,
controversies 1 and 2.
