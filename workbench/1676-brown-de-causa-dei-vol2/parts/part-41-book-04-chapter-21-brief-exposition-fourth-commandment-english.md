# CHAPTER XXI

## A brief exposition of the fourth precept

In explaining the Decalogue, orthodox commentators customarily distinguish
the worship of God into mediate and immediate worship, according to the two
tables of the Decalogue into which the Lord himself divided this entire law
when he reduced the whole of it to two heads in the New Testament.

This mediate worship, which is more properly called *service*, renders honor
to God, but through the mediation of the human beings whom it immediately
concerns. It is commanded in the second table of the Decalogue, where all the
heads of that duty are comprehended.

Immediate worship, which is rendered immediately to God by people who render
the honor due to him alone, is commanded here in the four precepts of the
first table. These four precepts therefore contain all the rules of the whole
of religion, or of immediate worship. For the Decalogue prescribes all the
duties of piety and justice: the first table, which comprises four precepts,
prescribes the duties of piety, just as the second table, containing six
precepts, expresses the duties of justice. Thus these first four precepts are
so many categories of true piety, or of immediate worship, and all moral
duties of piety are referred to them. Therefore, although in different
respects certain duties of piety can be referred to different heads, those
distinct heads have their own distinct relations and considerations.

[Printed p. 208 | IA PDF 216 | IA leaf 216 | IA decausadei02brow]

The duties of piety, when formally considered under those relations and
considerations, are referred to one head or another.

It follows that these precepts are formally distinguished from one another,
or that they command different and formally distinct things. The *first*
commandment therefore prescribes the legitimate *object* of worship: namely,
that we worship the true God alone. The *second* commands the legitimate and
sole *matter* of worship: that is, that we worship God not with the kind of
worship he forbids, but with the kind he prescribes and enjoins. The *third*
prescribes the legitimate and sole *manner* of worship: namely, that the true
God be worshiped *reverently* in the manner prescribed by himself. The
*fourth* designates the legitimate and solemn *time* of divine worship: that
solemn time at which the one true God ought to be worshiped, with the kind of
worship he saw fit to determine, and reverently and in the proper manner.

Or, as the *Shorter British Catechism* has it: [In the *first* commandment it
is required that we both know and acknowledge Jehovah to be that one and true
God and our God (1 Chronicles 28:9; Deuteronomy 26:17), and that we worship
and glorify him as such (Matthew 4:10; Psalm 29:2). It prohibits denial of the
true God (Psalm 14:1), neglecting to worship and glorify him as God (Romans
1:21) and as our God (Psalm 81:10–11), and also assigning or rendering to any
other the worship and glory due to him alone (Romans 1:25–26). The *second*
commandment requires us to receive, observe, and finally preserve pure and
entire all worship and every ordinance of religion which God has established
in his Word (Deuteronomy 32:46; Matthew 28:20; Acts 2:42). It forbids the
worship of God through images (Deuteronomy 4:15–19; Exodus 32:5, 8) or in any
other manner which God has not prescribed in his Word (Deuteronomy 12:31–32).
The *third* requires us to employ in a holy manner and with the highest
reverence God's names (Matthew 6:9; Deuteronomy 28:58), titles (Psalm 68:4),
attributes (Revelation 15:3–4), ordinances (Malachi 1:11, 14), words (Psalm
138:1–2), and works (Job 36:24). Conversely, it prohibits every profanation
and abuse of anything by which God makes himself known (Malachi 1:6–7, 12;
2:2; 3:14). Finally, the *fourth* requires us to keep holy to God that stated
or fixed time which he has designated in his Word—namely, one whole day out
of seven, to be celebrated as a holy Sabbath to him (Deuteronomy 5:12–14). It
prohibits both the omission and the negligent performance of the duties
required in it (Ezekiel 22:26; Amos 8:5; Malachi 1:13), as well as any
profanation of that day, whether by spending it idly (Acts 20:7, 9), by doing
what is sinful in itself (Ezekiel 23:38), or finally, with unnecessary
thoughts, words, or deeds concerning worldly affairs or recreations (Jeremiah
17:24–26; Isaiah 58:13).]

Thus the later precepts add something to the earlier and presuppose them. No
obedience can be rendered to the *second* unless the *first* is observed. The
*second* requires something beyond the *first*: namely, that we not only have
the true God as the sole object of our worship but also worship this true and
legitimate object with the kind of worship prescribed by himself. The
*third* presupposes the first two and adds something: namely, that we not only
render the prescribed worship exclusively to the true God but also render
this worship in the proper manner—that is, *reverently*. Finally, the
*fourth* presupposes all the preceding precepts,

[Printed p. 209 | IA PDF 217 | IA leaf 217 | IA decausadei02brow]

commanding us to render the prescribed worship to the true God in the proper
manner. This further requires the observance of solemn times in which this
prescribed worship can be rendered to the true God in the proper manner.

What is specially commanded in the fourth precept, therefore, is the
observance of those solemn times prescribed by God himself. For the opponents
themselves concede that what is *moral* in the fourth precept is that some
sufficient time be consecrated to be spent upon God and divine worship. Above,
in book 2, chapter 14, we showed that it belongs to God alone to determine
this sufficient and necessary time. The determination of this solemn,
necessary, and sufficient time, therefore, is contained either in this fourth
precept or nowhere, for the preceding commandments, as is clear from what has
been said, require something distinct from this. The opponents themselves
cannot deny this, for they refer all feast days to this precept as appendages.
Such a reference could not be rational unless this were the general aim of
the fourth precept. Thus this precept identifies that solemn time which is to
be dedicated to God and his worship: namely, what that time or portion of
time is which God wished to be reserved particularly for himself.

This commandment is placed, as it were, in the middle of the Decalogue,
because it is a kind of transition from one table to the other and joins the
second to the first with a firm bond, so that the harmony of the whole
Decalogue may appear. Indeed, some things which properly concern love toward
one's neighbor are enjoined by this commandment. It thus fastens the two
tables together.

This precept is set forth in more words than any other. It is therefore all
the more astonishing that there are people who oppose it and attempt wholly
to remove its binding force. Astonishing indeed is the audacity of those who
have dared to abolish a commandment fortified with such a multiplicity of
words, enjoined so solemnly, and explained so clearly.

This precept consists of a command and certain reasons attached to it, as do
all the other precepts of the first table (for *before me* in the first
precept also has the force of an argument). It further has something peculiar
to itself: [*Remember* (the Lord says) *the Sabbath day*], or, remember by
remembering. For that word is in the infinitive form. It thereby denotes that
the observance of the Sabbath was not first instituted at this time, for we
properly remember things known before but now consigned to oblivion. See
Zanchi on the fourth precept, page 850 in my copy. At the same time it
indicates that the observance of this precept is a matter of great importance,
which we ought always to remember. We are to complete our business throughout
the entire week in such a way that it presents us no impediment in sanctifying
the Sabbath. We are bound to compose and prepare ourselves in good time for
the celebration of the approaching day, lest we be found ill-disposed and
insufficiently prepared when the Sabbath begins. We must further devote every
effort to sanctifying it, lest anything be omitted or negligently performed.
Finally, we must not quickly forget it once past, lest we deprive ourselves of
the fruits of a Sabbath rightly sanctified.

[The expression *remember* (as the *Larger British Catechism*, question 120,
has it) is placed at the beginning of the fourth commandment partly because of
the supreme benefit from remembering it, since we are thereby greatly aided
in our preparation to observe it (Exodus 16:23; Luke 23:54, 56, together with
Mark 15:42; Nehemiah 13:19), and by its observance we are aided toward the
better observance of all the remaining commandments

[Printed p. 210 | IA PDF 218 | IA leaf 218 | IA decausadei02brow]

(Psalm 92, title, together with verses 13–14; Ezekiel 20:12, 19–20), and at
the same time toward preserving in grateful memory those supreme benefits of
creation and redemption which comprise the whole of religion in a kind of
summary (Genesis 2:2–3; Psalm 118:22, 24, together with Acts 4:10–11;
Revelation 1:10). It is placed there partly also because we are prone to
forget it (Ezekiel 22:26), both because the light of nature requires this
less clearly from us (Nehemiah 9:14), and because it diminishes natural
liberty in matters otherwise not unlawful (Exodus 34:21); because it recurs
only once in seven days, while many worldly affairs intervene which all too
often distract our minds from thinking either about our preparation for it or
about its celebration (Deuteronomy 5:14–15; Amos 8:5); and finally, because
Satan and his agents strive very greatly to erase the glory and even the
memory of this day, so that they may introduce every irreligion and impiety
(Lamentations 1:7; Jeremiah 17:21–23; Nehemiah 13:15–23).]

The time whose holy observance is enjoined here is called a *day*: [*Remember
the Sabbath day*]. The duration of that time—its *how long*—is thus determined:
namely, a day. A day is either *artificial and unequal*, because it consists
of unequal hours—namely, twelve hours which are longer or shorter according
to the sun's course toward the equinox or solstice—or *natural*, consisting
of twenty-four hours; and this day exhausts one-seventh of the week. A natural
day is meant here, because what follows proves that this day is the seventh
part of a week. This does not mean that all that time must be devoted to
sacred things, for time is allowed to refresh our bodies with food, sleep,
and other necessities. What is required here is therefore a whole day of the
kind we are accustomed to devote to our secular affairs, not some part,
whether before or after midday, or only some hours of a day, as we shall show
more fully later. It is further evident that some indefinite or undetermined
time is not required here, as if nothing else were commanded by this precept
than that some time, as seems convenient to us, be dedicated and set apart
for divine worship. This will be proved later.

This day is called the *Sabbath day*. In the Scriptures *Sabbath* is used
either *definitely*, when it denotes a particular Sabbath or a certain
designated day of rest, or *indefinitely*, when it designates in general any
Sabbath day. A determined Sabbath was either ordinary or extraordinary. The
*ordinary* was the Sabbath of the seventh day and is more often called the
*Sabbath day* by way of preeminence, because it was that necessary,
sufficient, and solemn time set apart by God for divine exercises. Hence the
week itself sometimes takes its name from it and is called the Sabbath, as in
Luke 18:12, *I fast twice in the Sabbath*. There were various *extraordinary*
Sabbaths: namely, the first and last days of solemn feasts; the Day of
Atonement, or that solemn fast described in Leviticus 23; and the rest of the
land every seventh and fiftieth year, which is also called a Sabbath.
*Sabbath day* is used here indefinitely and generally, as will be evident
later. For it does not say, [remember to sanctify the seventh day], but [to
sanctify the Sabbath day]. See Zanchi on the fourth precept, page 854 in my
copy.

This day is to be sanctified: [*that you sanctify it*]. *To sanctify* commonly
means the same as *to set apart*. This is done either by *designation*, the
sense in which the word is used at the end of this commandment when it is said
of God that he *sanctified the Sabbath day*, or by *application*,

[Printed p. 211 | IA PDF 219 | IA leaf 219 | IA decausadei02brow]

when the day designated by God is applied to sacred uses, devoted to sacred
exercises, and acknowledged and celebrated as a day sanctified by God, set
apart from the others, and consecrated to divine worship. The manner of
sanctifying the Sabbath is not prescribed here, nor are all the duties to be
performed on the Sabbath day prescribed. This is partly because those duties
are enjoined in the first three precepts; partly because the external manner
of worshiping God was mutable and had to be changed, for it is now different
under the New Testament from what it was under the Old, yet both formerly and
always the Sabbath was sanctified and must still now be sanctified by the
true worship due to God and prescribed by himself; and finally, partly because
nature itself dictated that a day cannot be sanctified to God by sheer
idleness.

This commandment further requires cessation from our labors: [*You shall do no
work on it*]. This necessarily follows from setting the day apart for sacred
uses, and it is further required here expressly. Therefore, although cessation
considered in itself and absolutely is not and never was a part of worship,
nevertheless, since rest is commanded here, failure to cease is a part of the
violation of the Sabbath. And since cessation itself is a necessary means of
sanctifying this day and is commanded here as such a means, it becomes a part
of the religious observance owed to God. More will be said about this
cessation later.

The Lord further says, [*Six days you shall labor and do all your work, but
on the seventh day is the Sabbath of Jehovah*]. Some hold that a command
properly and directly to avoid idleness, or to work, is contained here. But
this properly pertains to the second table of the Decalogue, specifically to
the eighth precept. The present precept directly regards the time of rest and
only indirectly the time of labor. Nor does it determine that time of rest
exclusively, as if it were unlawful to devote any time besides the seventh
day to divine exercises. Others hold that these words contain only a
*permission*. But to what purpose would it then be added here, since it would
have been implied even if he had said only, [you shall do no work on the
seventh day]? Rivet holds that they include a reason from the commandment's
equity and an argument urging the observance of the day. Since six days are
granted by God's liberal indulgence for people to complete their business, it
is fair that the seventh be sanctified to God. And since God, according to his
dominion, could have commanded that six days be sanctified to him and that
only one be devoted to worldly affairs, no one can reasonably claim that
consecrating the seventh day to God is an unbearable burden. Rivet's words are
these: [*By these words God wished to demonstrate the supreme equity and ease
of his command, so that he might meet in advance the unjust complaints of a
stubborn people and spur them toward a readier observance of the Sabbath. For
if he had wished to employ absolutely the dominion by which he had acquired
that people for himself, by right both of creation and of their recent
deliverance, he could have commanded them to devote more days necessarily to
his worship. Yet he was content with the seventh, so that they could desire
nothing more equitable or easier.*]

It is evident, however, that these words contain a *limitation*, and therefore
something greater than a *permission*, because they contain an implicit
prohibition and are thus some part of the precept. For when he says, [*Six
days you shall labor and do all your work*], he limits and definitely
determines the days granted for our labors. The sense is this: ordinarily you
shall not work more than six days together. You may indeed work for six days
(and thus it is a permission), but ordinarily you shall neither exceed nor
diminish this number (and thus a limitation is included). That is, ordinarily
you shall work neither more nor fewer days together, but after six working
days you shall consecrate the seventh to God.

[Printed p. 212 | IA PDF 220 | IA leaf 220 | IA decausadei02brow]

It thus commands that after six days the seventh be holy to God, and it
forbids us ordinarily to have more sacred days in a week. God therefore makes
here a distribution of time, granting us six of its parts
to accomplish our work and reserving the seventh for himself. No one will
deny that in this commandment the ordinarily sufficient and solemn time to be
dedicated to God was limited for the Jews, and also the time granted for human
affairs, although God, to whom alone this power belongs, established
additional sacred days outside the ordinary order. This, then, must be the
meaning of these words. God wished there to be no conflict between the
service due to him and our daily duties, but that they should agree perfectly.
It was therefore necessary for him to limit the time to be devoted to each,
lest there be confusion or the total or partial omission of either. Yet this
was done nowhere except in this precept. Above, in book 2, chapter 14, we
proved that to God alone belongs the power to establish sacred days and to
prescribe as many as he wishes to be sanctified to himself, which is done
most suitably in this precept. It is therefore necessary that the working
days be limited and their number designated here, for without this restriction
of the number of working days no fixed determination of feast days could have
been made.

We nevertheless concede that daily works are also commanded here, not
directly and simply, but indirectly and only in a certain respect. That is,
God here commands us to labor for six days, not as a means of guarding against
theft and lawfully acquiring external goods to support ourselves and others,
and so forth, but so that we complete our work within the space of six days
in such a way that it presents no impediment to sanctifying the Sabbath, and
so that ordinarily we do not devote more days together to those labors. For
God here prescribes not how many days we are to be idle, but rather how many
we are to work, with a view to sanctifying the Sabbath. The example of God
set before us here also presses this point: he had worked for six days
together and after six working days sanctified the seventh. Thus it is not
fitting for us to be idle either, but to work diligently for six days so that
we may be free for God on the seventh. We are to work for six days in our own
affairs and on the seventh in divine affairs. Diligence in our works is
therefore indirectly enjoined insofar as it advances or can advance the
sanctification of the seventh day. For it often happens that by carelessness,
idleness, or negligence in administering our affairs, we are made less fit to
sanctify the Sabbath to God.

The following words must next be explained: [*But the seventh day is the
Sabbath of your God*]. The question is what is meant by the *seventh day*.
Some hold that *seventh* is understood here in order, as though precisely the
seventh or last day of the week were enjoined here. But only what portion of
time is to be consecrated to God is described here. Thus *the seventh day*
designates not the *order* but only the *number*: that is, God here enjoins us
to consecrate the seventh to him after six working days, or to dedicate to
God one day out of a group of seven. This is to be done in such an order that
the six working days are joined together. It is as though he had said: You
shall ordinarily work six days in a week, neither more nor fewer together,
and on one day of the week, ordinarily not more, you shall be free for God
and divine exercises.

That God designates here not the *order* but the *number* of the days will be
proved later. Indeed, the preceding words designated not the order of the
days granted for conducting worldly work but their number. Since those six
days are therefore to be understood *of number*, not *of order*, why not the
seventh also? And since *Sabbath day* is used indefinitely at the beginning

[Printed p. 213 | IA PDF 221 | IA leaf 221 | IA decausadei02brow]

of the precept, it is fitting that *seventh* here denote number, not order.
Nowhere could the number of days to be consecrated to God be determined more
appropriately than here. Every
reason requires that this power be ascribed to God alone, since this principal
and solemn time is, as we proved elsewhere, a part of divine worship. Indeed,
if these words determined principally and specially the *order* of the days,
and not their *number*, this precept would have been violated by all Christian
churches which do not observe that day. Otherwise it would have to be said
that this precept has been wholly abolished. But we shall speak more fully on
this matter later.

The Hebrew words read as follows: `שבת ליהוה אלהיך`. They can be rendered,
[*The Sabbath of Jehovah your God*]. They will thereby denote the separation
of this day from all others and indicate that this day belongs to God and is
to be devoted to divine exercises, and that God claims a peculiar property
in that day for himself. It follows that the person who violates this Sabbath
commits the crime of sacrilege, because God wished this day to be reserved
for himself and devoted to sacred exercises. Or they can be explained thus:
[*A Sabbath to Jehovah your God*]. In this way they prove that the Sabbath, or
day of rest, must be dedicated to God alone, not to angels or human beings,
and consequently that the power to determine these sacred days, which are a
part of worship, belongs to God alone.

What works may or may not lawfully be done on the Sabbath day we shall
indicate more fully in the proper place later, when the sanctification of the
Sabbath is treated. It is therefore unnecessary to say anything about that
matter here.

The Lord does not merely say here, [*You shall do no work on it*], but further
adds, [*neither your son nor your daughter, your male servant nor your female
servant, your beast nor any stranger who dwells within your gates*]. From
this it is evident that this precept extends both to Gentiles and to servants.
The opponents deny both, but we shall prove them later. Since this precept is
addressed in the same way as the others—[*you shall remember*]—it is clear
that it is no less moral than the others and pertains to everyone. And when
it is later added, [*You shall do no work on it, neither your son*, and so
forth], it is evident that inferiors no less than superiors are bound by this
precept. Parents, heads of households, and by synecdoche magistrates are
further bound to devote every effort to ensuring that others rightly sanctify
the Sabbath. Zanchi says in the place cited above, page 858: [*We also have
here the duty of parents and magistrates in religion. Every head of a
household can and ought to compel his domestic servants to external worship;
why may not the magistrate also compel his subjects? For it does not say, You
remember to sanctify it, but you shall admonish your son or daughter to
sanctify it also. It does not speak this way, but: Remember that you sanctify
it, and that others who belong to you also sanctify it. Parents and
magistrates must therefore devote their efforts to ensuring that absolutely
all their subjects assemble for the public gathering and sanctify the Sabbath
day together with others.——He wishes no one to be excluded from sanctifying
the Sabbath, because servants as well as masters, children as well as parents,
strangers as well as natives, are bound to God and born for his worship.*]

He further says: [*As for the beasts, there is another reason why he commands
them also to be idle and rest. They are not commanded to rest from their
labors so that they may be free for divine worship as human beings are, but
partly so that we may not be impeded from worshiping God on their account,

[Printed p. 214 | IA PDF 222 | IA leaf 222 | IA decausadei02brow]

since they cannot be employed in labor without some human driver, and partly
also to care for the animals themselves, lest they become utterly exhausted
and perish. For what lacks alternate rest is not
durable, and so forth.*]

Other reasons are next appended to this commandment by which we are urged
more forcefully to observe it, and there are two. One is drawn from God's
example: [*For in six days God completed heaven and earth, the sea, and
whatever is contained in them, but on the seventh he rested*]. The force of
the argument consists in this: because God had worked for six days, neither
more nor fewer, and rested from his works on the seventh alone, and did this
to furnish us an example, it is therefore fair—indeed necessary—that we
imitate this example particularly set before us. That is, we are ordinarily
to observe the same distribution of time, work for six days, and dedicate the
seventh to God.

The other is expressed in these words: [*Therefore the Lord blessed the
Sabbath day and sanctified it*]. This reason is drawn (as the *Larger British
Catechism*, question 119, has it) from the blessing which God bestowed on that
day, not only by consecrating it to be a day peculiar to his worship but by
ordaining it to be a means of blessing us who sanctify it. When it says
[*therefore*—`על־כן`—*the Lord blessed the Sabbath day*], it indicates clearly
enough that God's cessation from his works was also the reason for this
sanctification. God rested on the seventh day; hence, as a conclusion or
consequence, it follows that he therefore blessed the Sabbath day.

Some hold that these two words, *blessed* and *sanctified*, signify different
things, so that *sanctification* denotes setting the day apart for sacred
uses, while *blessing* denotes some good redounding to those who observe the
day in holiness. The sense would thus be: God set apart the Sabbath day from
the other working days to be observed for himself and established it as a
day of *spiritual* blessing for those who rightly observe it, and indeed also
of *temporal* blessing as regards our daily labors, which will suffer no loss
from the observance of this day. Others hold that the terms are used
exegetically, as though it had been said, [he blessed it by sanctifying it] or
[he sanctified it by blessing it]. See Tremellius on Genesis 2:3 and Rivet on
the passage. It makes no difference in which way these words are taken, for
although God's *blessing* can be distinguished from his *sanctification*,
nevertheless, whenever God by sanctifying sets anything apart for pious uses,
he joins his blessing to it, and his sanctification has blessing as its
consequence.

It must further be observed that he does not say that he blessed the *seventh
day*, but the *Sabbath day*. Although that seventh day was indeed the Sabbath
day, he did not bless it insofar as it was the seventh but insofar as it was
the day of God's rest. Consequently, the blessing directly pertains to the
Sabbath, not to the seventh day.

From this it can also be inferred that we ought to sanctify the Sabbath day.
For God sanctified this day so that it would be a Sabbath to be celebrated by
us. He sanctified it by dedication and separation, so that we might sanctify
it by application and observance.

Finally, from this we can gather that God sanctified the Sabbath from the
beginning. For he sanctified it when he rested: because he rested, he
therefore sanctified it. This,

[Printed p. 215 | IA PDF 223 | IA leaf 223 | IA decausadei02brow]

however, we abundantly proved above in book 3, chapter 9, where we showed that
there is no prolepsis in the words of Moses in Genesis 2. Indeed, these words
look to the past: *He rested, blessed, sanctified*.

In this precept, therefore, we have a *command* and attached *reasons*. The
command states two things: first, the *time to be observed*, and then the
*observance of that time*. The time itself is first set forth *indefinitely*
and is called the *Sabbath day*. Then it is set forth more *definitely*, both
with respect to its *duration*, or *how long*, when it is called a *day*, and
with respect to its *frequency*, or *how often*, when it is called the
*seventh*, not in order but in number: that is, one out of a group of seven.
As to its *observance*, a twofold duty is enjoined, either with respect to
ourselves or with respect to others. In the *first manner*, everyone is
commanded to remember this day, diligently prepare themselves for its holy
observance, and observe it in holiness both by resting from daily works and
by performing sacred duties. In the *second manner*, superiors are commanded
to compel their respective inferiors to observe this day. The reasons are
either presented *implicitly* or more *explicitly*. The *first two* are drawn
both from the *grant* of six days for conducting our affairs and from the
*appropriation* of the seventh to sacred uses, for [*the seventh is the
Sabbath of God or to God*]. The *later two* are likewise drawn from God's
*example* and from the *blessing* and *sanctification* of this Sabbath.

The following matters must further be observed concerning this precept.

1. While all the remaining precepts are presented either only
*affirmatively*, as the fifth is, or only *negatively*, as the others are,
this one is presented in both ways. For it not only says, [*You shall sanctify
the Sabbath*], but further adds, [*You shall do no work on it*]. This shows
beyond doubt how strict this obligation is, however greatly people may for
the most part scorn it. At the same time it implies that corrupt humanity is
now so blind that it can scarcely gather the positive requirement here from
the negative, or conversely the negative from the positive. Therefore, in
consideration of our ignorance and depravity, God himself set forth his mind
in both ways, so that there would remain nothing for us to object and nothing
with which to cover the wickedness of those of us who violate this
commandment, whether by doing our own works or by not sanctifying the day.

2. While not a few other precepts have no attached reason to move us to
render obedience, and some have only one, this one has several attached
reasons, both explicit and implicit, as we have seen. This is so that those
who dare to violate this commandment and teach others to do the same may be
less excusable, and so that the mouths of all who by practice or teaching
dare to remove the obligation of this commandment may be all the more firmly
stopped.

3. Many things are added for the sake of explanation, so that we may know
what is meant by the Sabbath day: namely, the seventh after six working days.
God wishes not two or three Sabbaths to be celebrated in one week, but only
one. He wishes us to do our work for six days together and then, on the
seventh—not in order but in number, after those six—to be free for God and
divine exercises. And so that we may know what sort of Sabbath day this ought
to be, he calls it the Sabbath *to the Lord* or *of the Lord*: that is, it
ought to be a day belonging to God and consecrated to God alone.

4. To make its obligation firmer, he not only enjoins upon every individual
the sanctification of this Sabbath but further commands parents, heads of
households, and magistrates in particular to devote their efforts, according
to their calling, to ensuring that this Sabbath of God is observed by their
inferiors and subjects no less than by themselves.
