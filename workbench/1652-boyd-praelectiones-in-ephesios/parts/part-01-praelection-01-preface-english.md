PREFACE TO PAUL’S EPISTLE TO THE EPHESIANS, set out in the customary manner.

[Marginalia: Lecture I. Preparation.]

Although in reality there is only one doctrine of the truth that accords with
godliness, which we have been accustomed to call by the single word
*theology*, and although it is fully and perfectly contained in the books of
divinely inspired Scripture—holy Scripture, I say, which alone is the rule of
Christian faith and life and from this office alone is deservedly distinguished
by the name *canon* or *canonical*—yet just as all Scripture is God-breathed
and useful for doctrine, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness,
so that through consolation and patience we may have hope, indeed so that the
man of God, that is, the minister of the gospel and interpreter of the divine
will, may be perfect and thoroughly equipped for every good work, so too it
alone can make one wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.

[Marginalia: 2 Timothy 3:15, 17; Romans 15:4.]

Nevertheless there is a twofold manner of learning this same saving doctrine
and, as it were, drawing it from the pure springs of Scripture, customarily
employed both in rightly constituted churches and in academies. One is by the
sincere interpretation of the divine word contained in Scripture. The other is
by an ordered treatment of commonplaces—that is, the heads and articles, as it
were, of true religion that concern faith and life. When the latter is shorter,
plainer, easier, and adapted to the understanding of beginners, novices, and
children, it is in reality the same as catechetical instruction. Both are
especially necessary for students of true theology.

[Marginalia: The twofold office of theology.]

First is the right and faithful exposition of Scripture. For otherwise, from
where is that heavenly truth to be sought, if not from the springs themselves?
From where faith, hope, and consolation, if not from God’s word? Those who hear
and keep it are pronounced blessed by him in whom the fullness of all our
blessedness resides. From where do we have either a foundation, a rule of
faith and life, or a criterion of true and false, apart from a right
understanding of holy Scripture?

[Marginalia: Amplification from adjuncts. Luke 11:28. Utility.]

The teaching of the commonplaces and articles of doctrine gathered from it is,
in turn, very useful for an easier, fuller, firmer, and more distinct knowledge
of the same things. Students of this divine science thus possess in their mind
a sort of body and system of it, arranged in a distinct sequence into its
parts, heads, and articles. From those first principles of holy Scripture very
many corollaries and appendices—that is, theological conclusions—are actually
and distinctly deduced, each is placed in its own location, and each is
referred and applied to its proper uses, whether for confirming truths or
refuting falsehoods. From this the students themselves become better fitted
both to interpret holy Scripture according to the analogy of faith (since they
grasp those heads of Christian faith and therefore know what differs from
them), to convey the sum of that doctrine suitably to others, and to judge so
many varied and complex controversies skillfully and without risking error.

Each is bound to the other by the closest bond and highest mutual affinity, so
that one can scarcely, if at all, be separated and disjoined from the other, as
I recall observing above from this very passage. For how could holy Scripture
be faithfully expounded without any mention of the theological commonplaces,
or the articles and heads of Christian faith (which occur in nearly every
verse or certainly every section, not merely in whole chapters or books)? Or
how could the true heads of faith be treated, proved, confirmed, and
illustrated methodically and suitably, and contrary errors and dogmas refuted,
overthrown, and destroyed, except from those first principles and foundations
of true theology—the divine word, I mean, sincerely expounded according to the
analogy of faith?

[Marginalia: Affinity.]

Thus these two offices and functions of teaching true theology—or parts, or
methods, if you prefer—render mutual service to one another. As the most
graceful poet says of those who climb beneath dark night, or a steep mountain,
or a slippery upward road, joining hands and strength to help one another:
“they hold hands with one another, and as they are drawn they draw, a mutual
relief from toil.” So too these two, like most skillful hands applied to grasp
and receive the saving doctrine of godliness, even if they do not “bear equal
measures of toil,” yet certainly “have become defenders of one another in
reciprocal affection.” Even if, I say, they do not each bear an equal weight of
labor, they nevertheless converge on one and the same end and conspire
amicably with one another. The former lays a firm and immovable foundation;
the latter builds upon it gems, gold, and silver wholly consonant with the
foundation. The former supplies self-authenticating principles that are truly
first, immediate, and indemonstrable, and thus possess supreme authority and
force among us. The latter draws conclusions conformable to them and brings
into act what lay enclosed in them potentially. The former presents, as if
scattered limb by limb, the parts of that divine doctrine according to the
Holy Spirit’s varied purpose in teaching his Church. The latter compacts those
same limbs into one body, employing the extrinsic light of method and a fitting
order, like so many bonds and ligaments especially adapted to strengthen the
memory of teachers and learners.

[Marginalia: Oppian. Comparison concerning order.]

Finally, each puts to its true and chief practical use that instrumental habit
which directs our understanding in knowing things—logic, I mean, the science
of teaching and learning. Neither disdains the ministry of so noble, useful,
convenient, and familiar a handmaid, granted by God most good and great to the
Church chiefly for this very use and end; neither is without its use in
performing its office.

There is, however, this noteworthy difference. When logic is put into practice,
two offices are principally in view. The first is analysis, by which, once the
purpose proposed by an author has been shown, his works are resolved into their
parts and members according to the rules of analytic doctrine. The second is
synthesis, or generation if you prefer, by which we ourselves proceed
according to the prescribed law of didactic order and compose a body in an
ordered fashion, in the manner most agreeable to our purpose and design and to
the nature of the subject under treatment. In the exposition of holy Scripture
we chiefly exercise analysis, as is self-evident. In the shorter and
catechetical, or the fuller and more exact academic, treatment of commonplaces,
we chiefly employ the synthetic method: gathering into one and arranging in
order everything that pertains to the same doctrinal commonplace, head, or
article of faith and has been sought and drawn from various parts and books of
holy Scripture.

[Marginalia: Difference.]

Now since all theology—the saving doctrine of faith and godliness—however it
is handed down and learned, whether from the analysis and faithful
interpretation of holy Scripture or from the orderly connected synthesis of
commonplaces, always rests on the same foundations and must always be drawn
from the same spring, not from inventions of the human brain or principles and
arguments of human wisdom but solely from God’s word, sealed in the authentic
and self-authenticating monuments of holy Scripture; and since it always
remains the same in reality and leads us to the same end and goal, Jesus Christ
our Lord, in whom all treasures of grace, wisdom, and knowledge are hidden;
far be it from me to institute between these two parts of the theologian’s
office a comparison as unjust as it is inept in dignity, use, or necessity.

[Marginalia: Rejection.]

Perhaps only concerning order it could not unreasonably be asked which of these
methods or ways of learning sacred theology is prior in the order of nature or
better adapted to students of this discipline from the very threshold. On each
side reasons not to be despised present themselves. One side advances the
springs, foundations, principles, and other things naturally prior, from holy
Scripture itself. The other advances a clear and exact method, with everything
arranged into its orders and classes, along with ease, brevity, and other such
supports for memory and aids to a less trained capacity. We shall touch this
knot and difficulty only in passing, first laying down these two necessary
foundations of our answer. First, a less exact acquaintance with histories or
even with certain principal parts and sayings of holy Scripture—such as may be
acquired from attentive and devout reading without the care and precision of
academic instruction—is one thing; the exact analysis and exposition of the
same customarily instituted in Christian schools is another. For—

[Marginalia: Two foundations of the answer. First.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 36 | Præfatio p. 2]

—the former belongs to all members of the Church, even the less trained and
uneducated, while the latter chiefly requires a knowledge of good literature
and of those instrumental habits. Likewise, catechetical instruction, which is
like a synopsis of the commonplaces and an easier introduction, is one thing;
a full treatment of them, which carefully pursues all the questions and
controversies customarily and profitably raised about them, with all their
arguments, reasons, and proofs, is another. Further, the individual disciplines
were not first established by their inventors or authors in the same order and
manner in which, once published, discovered, and established, they are later
learned by other students. To come closer to the present point, perhaps the
study of sacred theology should not be undertaken in the same order necessarily
followed by many faithful interpreters of Scripture and writers of
commonplaces. By diligently turning over the sacred volumes and studiously
comparing them both with one another and with the labors of others, those men
had doubtless attained no small understanding of what is called the sacred
context before putting their hands to gathering and arranging theological
commonplaces from it.

[Marginalia: Second. Note well.]

But the same necessity does not lie upon us, who by God’s boundless generosity
may enjoy the labors of godly men both in Church and academy. For us setting
out upon this theological journey, besides the torch and lamp of his own most
holy word—which the royal Psalmist declares to be a lamp to our feet and a
light to our paths, whose very entrance and threshold illuminates and instructs
the simple—God has also granted so many perfect monuments of the most faithful
teachers and pastors of the Church. Whether you consider the chief heads of
Christian godliness and religion, treated both at length and methodically and
also succinctly comprised in epitomes, tables, synopses, catechisms,
confessions, and other such outlines and systems of commonplaces; or the sacred
word of God accurately translated into various languages and illustrated by
various observations, analyses, commentaries, and other helps to its clearer
understanding, almost nothing more could be desired by a godly student of
theological truth. Almost nothing remains for us but to follow their footsteps
and to use and enjoy those heavenly treasures gathered with such care, labor,
and skill for every future generation of the Church.

[Marginalia: Psalm 119:105.]

With these two things laid down as foundations, let us say briefly and without
controversy that catechetical instruction, embracing the first foundations,
elements, or rudiments of Christian faith, must be placed before everyone, for
it is necessary to the learned, as they say, as well as the unlearned and those
without liberal education. It must be supported and confirmed by clear
testimonies of Scripture itself, from which each head of Christian religion is
to be drawn and deduced.

[Marginalia: Answer.]

For those especially who have wholly devoted themselves to the study of sacred
theology—with a view chiefly to whom we undertook to resolve this question
about the order of these parts—a more exact comprehension of doctrinal
commonplaces should next follow, together with their principal arguments,
divisions, parts, theorems, and at least the discussion and resolution of the
more notable controversies. Thus they may comprehend the whole body distinctly
and in order, and may refer everything encountered in reading or hearing to its
proper classes and series no less skillfully and distinctly than apothecaries
arrange that varied and manifold mass and medley of drugs into their boxes and
little cases, or—to use an example closer to literary work—than printers
dispose the matrices of their letters, each in its proper cell and compartment.

[Marginalia: Similes.]

Meanwhile the study of commonplaces ought to be accompanied by diligent and
constant reading of the sacred context itself, especially in the original and
authentic languages. It should be joined to it with such care that young men
given to God and dedicated to divine studies may early recognize, love,
admire, and adore the peculiar idiom of holy Scripture and the character of
the Holy Spirit speaking in it through instruments chosen for himself—a
character conspicuous for holiness, simplicity, depth, and unaffected majesty,
and separated by an immeasurable interval from every human discourse. Let them
become accustomed early to reading it, select from it its more illustrious
sentences, like especially notable gems or rather stars gleaming in this truly
heavenly expanse or firmament, drink them in, and carefully store them in the
treasury of their memory.

From there let them at last proceed, in a certain order and with discernment,
to the particular exposition of Scripture itself and the exact analysis of its
individual books, employing for this purpose the never sufficiently praised
labors of the most approved interpreters and comparing them with the sacred
page itself by their own talent and industry. Let them finally rest in this
study of holy Scripture; let them grow old in its meditation, exposition,
investigation, application, use, and practice. For all the commonplaces of
truly theological doctrine lead us to it as if by the hand, just as they first
flowed from it. As rivers draw their origin from the ocean, though by channels
hidden from us, and return and roll back into it as into their own spring, so
let this be both goal and boundary of your theological study, just as it was
its threshold. You will never wholly exhaust its depths, however wholly you
apply yourself to it with study, labor, and constancy, and whatever leisure,
talent, skill, and powers of mind and body you bring.

It will always open to you new secrets of saving truth, altogether marvelous
mysteries of the divine will and of your salvation not perceived or noticed at
first glance. It will repeatedly furnish new matter for consolation, joy,
hope, patience, and endurance; new supports of truth, new engines against the
impious dogmas and deceits of heretics, new defenses against all the clients
of Satan and Antichrist, and new weapons, counsels, examples, and ramparts for
your faith against every blow, snare, and fear of temptation. It will never
allow any distaste, nausea, or weariness of itself to creep over godly and
religious minds. As Augustine, that eminent Father and truly Christian heart,
says, the Holy Spirit has so tempered it that in clear passages he meets our
hunger, while by obscure ones he wipes away weariness, so that we are fed by
the clear and exercised by the obscure.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, book 2.]

Indeed it supplies us with an occasion for true humility, modesty of soul, and
constant prayer from the recognized blindness of our nature, especially in
attaining and learning divine and saving things. Finally, it lifts our hearts
to conceive thanks to his goodness more readily and wholeheartedly when we are
given the key of right understanding and at last introduced by our supreme
Prophet and Initiator into the inner sanctuaries of the heavenly sanctuary.
These things are valued the more by us, and received with the greater reverence
and esteem, the longer, more submissively, more vehemently, and more constantly
we have knocked at the doors of his grace.

There you have, dearest hearers, the order I have observed among those parts
of theological doctrine, or rather the diverse ways of learning and teaching
it which I proposed to myself from the beginning. We have already shown above
their close bond with one another and, God favoring, shall show it by
experience itself in the performance of the office entrusted to me, so that
nothing prevents what is joined in nature from being joined also in use and
work. Nothing prevents young men devoted to this discipline and consecrated to
God’s Church, after being sufficiently imbued with that introductory
instruction—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 37 | Præfatio p. 3]

—and not wholly destitute of the provision and aid of languages and the arts
that serve it, from applying themselves vigorously to both parts at once,
embracing both a methodical outline of the commonplaces and an exposition of
the sacred page at one and the same period of study and with one and the same
care, and contributing each to the progress and increase of the other, so far
as the nature of their respective subjects permits, by prudent counsel and
praiseworthy discernment.

[Marginalia: Exhortation.]

And do not, I ask, candid and kindly hearers, be troubled because, although I
have already asserted that all our study must come to rest in the orthodox
interpretation, godly meditation, right use, practice, and application of
Scripture itself as in theology’s highest degree, and that nothing beyond it
must be sought, expected, or even desired for holding and completing the surest
way of salvation in a straight course, I myself seem to have inaugurated this
theological undertaking in the opposite order, or at least am henceforth to
begin it under the guidance and auspices of God most good and great. For I have
undertaken to expound to you this Epistle of the apostle Paul to the Ephesians
according to my small measure and weakness, or rather according to the measure
of the gift freely bestowed upon me by God’s kindness. I do not know whether
any part of God-breathed Scripture more plainly and fully reveals to us the
unsearchable riches of Christ, those treasures of heavenly blessings, those
mysteries, foundations, and principles of our salvation, and those secret
counsels of divine wisdom, or commends Christ’s incomprehensible grace with a
greater fervor and efficacy of spirit and a heart overflowing into such force
of words and thoughts. Let no such difficulty, I say, disturb you by
interrupting your silent thoughts at the very threshold of our labors.

First, it is a far more arduous work and no little heavier burden to compact
the whole body of sacred theology and reduce into a methodical system so many
parts, members, theorems, questions, controversies, decisions, answers, and
opinions scattered so far and wide, with supreme skill and care, than to
interpret one part of holy Scripture and observe from it, in the course of
explanation, its more notable heads of doctrine. Anyone, unless I am mistaken,
will grant this of his own accord. Know also that in this matter excellent
provision has been made both for you and your advancement and for my weakness:
the heavier province rightly fell to the one whom divine Providence granted us
to govern, establish, and support this school as on his shoulders, while the
far smaller and lighter load was placed upon me, though even it, I confess,
seems like an Etna to my weakness.

[Marginalia: Reason 1. The venerable old man Dr. Béraud was meant.]

Therefore, since in this theological course the commonplaces must be first in
your heart and care, and you must chiefly labor and sweat over them before you
are yourselves summoned and admitted to advance the Lord’s work—since later
you will be able to spend your whole life in the exposition of holy Scripture
and daily advance both by teaching and learning—how should not the principal
parts be assigned to the man whom the wisdom of God most good and great chose
as the chief instrument of this work? They should not be cast upon this slender
shoot, growing only from that tree’s huge and age-old trunk.

Next recall the marvelous tempering of the Scriptures. Their meanings are so
deep that even elephants may swim in this ocean and cannot reach its bottom by
their sagacity, yet they also display the ease of a ford so passable and clear
that lambs or infants can cross it. For Scripture accommodates itself suitably
to each person’s capacity and stature of faith and gives each the measure of
spiritual light that, in proportion to the divinely bestowed gift and measure
of progress, each can draw and receive from it.

[Marginalia: Reason 2.]

If, then, we cannot yet unlock the inner riches and hidden treasures of this
chamber at the first attempt, let us not therefore grow faint, nor let our zeal
in asking, seeking, and knocking grow slack or our diligence abate. Let us
instead cherish and stir even a tiny seed of this gospel doctrine, received
with a humble and reverent mind, striving to feel some daily increase, always
remembering the gospel parable in which our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ
beautifully compares the kingdom of heaven to a mustard seed. Although it was
the smallest of all seeds when a person sowed it in his field, once grown it is
the greatest of garden plants, becoming a tree so that the birds of heaven come
and nest in its branches. In this way the Lord richly blesses even the slender
beginnings of his elect, so that neither they nor others conscious of such
smallness could ever have expected, from so tiny a seed like the least mustard
grain, so abundant and splendid a harvest; from so slight a seed of a fruitful
plant, so excellent a tree; from so small an ember, so great a fire of faith;
or from so little a spark, so great a radiance of light.

[Marginalia: Matthew 13:31.]

Trusting and supported by this hope that our God in his time will add
abundantly from his rich grace, and according to measure for his glory and our
good and that of his Church, what we now cannot attain and unfold from his most
clear word because of the slowness of our immature infancy, let us undertake
the task selected for us: the analysis, I say, of this Epistle.

[Marginalia: Approach to the subject itself.]

In it, if anywhere, we shall find most true what our apostle wrote in the
second letter to Timothy: although he was afflicted and bound as a criminal
for the gospel, God’s word was not bound. For while captive at Rome, the
apostle not only won many for Christ by preaching the gospel; from that prison
he also sent so many noble and divine letters to various churches and church
leaders—to the Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, and the second to
Timothy. In them some ancient teachers of the Church observed some special
grace and affection breathing marvelous faith and charity, as though through
the very constraints of chains and prison the saving voice of gospel truth
burst with greater force and impetus from our apostle’s divinely inspired
breast. For, as Cyprian once told the martyrs, Christ himself not only watches
his servants contend and fight; he himself struggles, crowns and is crowned in
the contest, and gives relief in chains, solace under pressure, and protection
in need.

[Marginalia: 2 Timothy 2:9. A noteworthy reason. Chrysostom. Cyprian,
*Address to the Martyrs*.]

Since, I say, throughout all those letters sent from Rome we have in our
apostle a noble—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 38 | Præfatio p. 4]

—and luminous example of a most faithful and vigilant pastor, one whom concern
for all the churches bound far more than those chains, and who, not only while
absent but even when soon to depart this mortal life for the Lord through the
glorious end of impending martyrdom, strove nevertheless to remain both present
and surviving for Christ’s churches by every care, counsel, prayer, warning,
doctrine, and monument of sacred writing. In this letter in particular,
directed from Rome to the Ephesians, one may observe an overflowing freedom
and boldness not only of gospel speech but also the Holy Spirit’s force,
feeling, and efficacy; a flame of Christian love flashing from that chosen
breast; a fervor of zeal; a marvelous radiance of divine light shining from
it; and a spring of living water—that is, every blessing and spiritual grace—
gushing, or rather boiling, from it. Such is the abundance that the eminent
feeling of his heart presses upon the soul’s own perceptions and inmost
conceptions; the conceptions press upon the uttered words; and every preceding
word presses, urges, and overwhelms what follows, while in the most emphatic
words and thoughts he presents clearly and succinctly to us today, just as to
the Ephesians of old, each principal head of Christian doctrine concerning
faith, morals, and life, and especially the benefits of Christ the Redeemer.

[Marginalia: Amplification.]

Therefore, most courteous hearers, as you look more closely at this Epistle’s
great dignity and singular majesty, I need not, unless I am mistaken, give a
longer reason why at the very beginning of this professorship I undertook to
expound it above the others. Rather I must lament that God has not granted me a
measure of grace and gift proportionate in some degree both to so splendid an
occasion—the opening for the first time of this theological school—and to so
choice, divine, and sublime a subject.

[Marginalia: Corollary.]

Consider the apostle’s purpose in sending this letter: to urge and encourage
the Ephesian church, which he had already planted, watered, and cultivated with
such care, faithfulness, and skill for three whole years, to persevere in the
true doctrine of faith and godliness, and therefore in a life corresponding to
its gracious calling; indeed, to make progress in both, namely in faith and
holiness of life. He did not wish them to fall from gospel truth and the former
purity of their faith or permit themselves to be led away, whether through
fear of persecution, a perverse love of the world, shame at Paul’s chains and
afflictions borne for the gospel, or the pernicious cunning and frauds of
ravenous wolves and deceitful foxes—that is, pseudo-apostles and false
teachers—whom he had warned would both creep and burst into their flock.
Instead, they were not only to persevere constantly in that purity but also
press eagerly toward the heavenly goals of their calling.

[Marginalia: Reason 1, from the purpose. Acts 20:29.]

If, I say, we attend to this purpose of our apostle, will it not suit all who
in our age have been called out from Antichrist’s darkness and tyranny into the
light of the gospel and the freedom of God’s children, and especially those
whose labor God may one day deign to use either to summon others from the mire
of that same filth or to confirm them in their calling and advance them along
the way of salvation they have begun? While the cause remains, the effect
remains, as logicians say; and when the same diseases and evils
either rage or threaten, the use of the same remedies and precautions ought to
remain in force. Since the same world still tries to turn us away from
Christian truth, true godliness, and seeking the kingdom of heaven either
exclusively or earnestly; the same flesh still tempts us; the same crosses and
swords still surround us, although the latter are sheathed for a time and the
former lie hidden in the hatred of an obstinate heart; the same storms
threaten; the same roaring lion prowls around the fold; the same ungodliness
and credulous acceptance of corrupt error still reign in unbelieving hearts;
and the same teachers and champions of ungodliness and error, architects of
every fraud and fallacy but covered with sheepskins, clamor and roar on every
side around the Lord’s little flock, intent on every opportunity both to
overthrow us and exalt themselves—what is more fitting for us, dearest hearers,
what belongs to us sooner and more urgently, than to fortify ourselves with
this prophylactic of our apostle and arm ourselves with these saving remedies
against every stain and ruin of ungodliness and apostasy?

[Marginalia: Application.]

From it, as from Eden’s garden planted and cultivated by God’s own Spirit, let
us diligently pluck and gather those divine plants of promises, precepts,
exhortations, consolations, correction, rebuke, and instruction—in a word, of
Christian life and doctrine. From them let us prepare and keep ready a saving
antidote, first for ourselves and then for everyone whom our godly care can
reach, against every poison of sin and Satan, whether it strikes the hearts of
careless people with lethargic stupor or torments, constricts, and racks their
wretched and restless consciences with hellish torches and furies.

Or consider the subject treated in this Epistle: an epitome and compendium of
almost the whole of Christian doctrine and of the principal commonplaces
usually treated in theology. In what manner, I ask, could students of true
theology apply their study and labor more pleasantly, fruitfully, and happily
than in a diligent and accurate reading, meditation, and exposition of this
Epistle?

[Marginalia: Reason 2.]

Although absent and captive, our apostle was pressed by godly concern for the
church he had earlier instructed by his living voice. To exhort the Ephesians
more effectively to the constancy in faith learned from him and in the
Christian life befitting true members of Christ Jesus, we see him recall to
their mind and memory the sum of all doctrine, especially the surpassing
benefits of the most merciful God in Christ. In this Epistle he proclaims them
most magnificently, especially toward Gentiles alienated from God and Christ,
opening the primal spring itself and drawing and deriving from this source of
every good every spiritual blessing: the free favor and mercy of God the
Father toward humanity, from which every benefit flows to us through Christ.

[Marginalia: Summary of this Epistle.]

First of all come our eternal and free election and predestination in Christ,
then the marvelous redemption accomplished for us through the same incarnate
Christ. He teaches that we truly become participants in these and receive them
through faith’s firm embrace of the preached gospel and steadfast perseverance
to the end in that faith. All the elect are necessarily endowed with this—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 39 | Præfatio p. 5]

—firmness of faith, since they are sealed by the promised Holy Spirit, who
remains in us as earnest and pledge of our heavenly inheritance until the
fulfillment of the divine promise on that last and glorious day of our full
and perfect redemption.

Meanwhile, however, we must strive with every effort to make constant progress
in faith and holiness, applying ourselves to prayer (the pursuit of which he
commends by his example), the fruits of new life, and every exercise of
godliness and love. We must no longer walk like the other nations, alienated
from God and his kingdom, in the vanity of our senses and the stains and
corruptions of the flesh. As befits so holy, heavenly, and gracious a calling,
we must daily put off the old person more and more and with all care and zeal
put on the new person, renewed in true holiness, righteousness, and purity of
spirit after the image of Christ himself, our head. We must do this with
greater eagerness, readiness of mind, constancy, and vigilance, whether we
consider our wretched condition outside Christ—for in ourselves by our nature
we are only children of wrath, alienated from God and his covenant, life,
grace, and glory, and dead in sins and offenses—or the dignity and excellence
of that life by which he deigned to make us alive in Christ; or that blessed
communion and fellowship of the Church into which, after demolishing the wall
between Jews and Gentiles, he gathered and summoned us beneath the same head,
Christ, by the bond of the same faith and his Spirit, so that from him, as true
and living members of his body, we may draw all power, sense, motion,
spiritual life, and its increase;

or that fatherly care, providence, and ineffable love of Christ with which,
received into heaven and raised to the glorious right hand of his Father, he
has attended his Church. Sending the Holy Spirit, he established this public
ministry in it so that this mystical building might be founded in himself,
erected, and continually advanced until at last brought to its summit; so that
the saints might be gathered and joined, united ever more both with their head
and with one another through faith, the Holy Spirit, and true love; and so that
they might not be carried about by every wind of doctrine but be strengthened
daily in truth and advance in saving knowledge of Christ, until at last they
grow into a perfect person, to the measure of Christ’s perfect stature.

Whether, I say, we consider any one or all of these things, clearly and
emphatically proposed to us in this Epistle, we see nothing but the supreme
riches of divine grace in and through Christ toward us who are utterly
wretched—though not deserving pity, since we most justly became so by our own
fault. That is, we see as many arguments supremely binding, urging, and
compelling us both to perpetual thanksgiving and the praise and glory of our
God in Christ with the highest exertion of mind, and to testify our gratitude
not merely by voice and public profession but far more by every obedience,
duty, godliness, and newness of life.

[Marginalia: Argument.]

Thus, if we read this Epistle attentively, we may observe in it all the chief
theological commonplaces, either touched upon briefly in passing or treated
more fully and deliberately, as will be clear from this enumeration:

[Marginalia: Commonplaces contained here.]

1. The one God subsisting in three persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit; some
divine attributes, namely efficacious power, manifold wisdom, goodness, mercy,
grace, and ineffable loving-kindness, which shine most splendidly in the
marvelous mystery of our redemption and therefore in the gospel, its divine
revelation; also the order of action of the divine persons in accomplishing our
salvation, and especially the offices of the Holy Spirit toward the elect and
believers.

2. God’s eternal and free predestination and election of believers in Christ,
explained and illustrated by its efficient and final causes, means, and
effects.

3. The dispensation of our redemption and communication of salvation in time:
it is offered through the preaching of the gospel, received through faith, and
sealed in the hearts of the elect by the Spirit of adoption. Hence effectual
calling, preaching the gospel, faith and ingrafting into Christ through faith,
adoption, and related things, illustrated by various circumstances of causes,
effects, adjuncts, and objects.

4. Christ the head of the Church and his glorious state, exalted above all
heavenly angels and all their ranks and orders; his resurrection from the dead,
glorious local ascension into heaven, and triumph over all the defeated and
subjugated powers of darkness.

5. Original sin and the most wretched condition of those left in natural
death, outside and apart from Christ.

6. Our reconciliation with God through Christ, vivification, ascension,
resurrection, and heavenly glorification, all of which belong to us through
and because of Christ.

7. The calling of the Gentiles into the fellowship of the Church; the one
Church consisting of all the elect, Jews and Gentiles; and the very close union
of the Church’s members both with Christ the head and with one another through
the intervention of his Spirit and the bond of brotherly love.

8. Ecclesiastical ministries, illustrated by their end and use and also by
their author, Christ himself.

9. Marriage and the mutual love of spouses, illustrated and confirmed by the
mystical and spiritual union of Christ and the Church.

10. Christian conduct, that is, the various good works and duties to be
performed in the various stations of life according to each person’s
particular calling, rank, sex, and condition.

11. The Christian struggle and warfare against those capital enemies of
divine glory and our salvation, and the spiritual panoply or armor by which we
must be protected against every snare and attack of the devil.

12. The perseverance of the saints in Christ’s faith and grace through
prayers, vigilance, and every exercise of godliness, by which these saving
benefits are not only cherished in us but daily grow and increase until at
last they are consummated in heaven.

See with what theological commonplaces and heads of Christian doctrine this
Epistle is filled and adorned, like a necklace with so many notable gems or
the sky with stars! This was the second reason for our design in deciding to
begin in this academy with this Epistle the office of interpreting holy
Scripture.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the second argument.]

Or consider, third, the various testimonies and strongest arguments with which
this Epistle abounds against many heresies, both ancient ones expelled and
driven out by orthodox Fathers long ago and those now revived in the Christian
world and summoned, as it were, from hell—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 40 | Præfatio p. 6]

—against the enemies of God’s grace and champions of free choice and human
merits; and also for repelling and refuting those who, by the most absurd
monsters of “metousia,” “synousia,” and “pantachousia,” attempt to overthrow
and undermine the truth of Christ’s human nature and his real and local ascent
into heaven, or the definite certainty of eternal election, the first and
firmest foundation of our entire salvation.

[Marginalia: Reason 3. Its first part.]

It is also fitted to awaken from their stupor those whose whole religion is
placed in a bare profession of Christian faith and religion, in a certain
external form and order and assent rendered to divine truth; whose bow and
stern consist in a wholly dead and fruitless faith and a vain and wavering
hope. It seems plainly enough to them for salvation to profess Christ’s name in
the Church’s assembly and pattern themselves after other people—that is, be
conformed to the form of this age. Meanwhile they give no thought to newness of
life, the transformation of the mind into Christ’s image, new obedience,
seeking the things of the coming age and God’s kingdom before, above, and apart
from everything—that is, even at the loss of everything else—testifying their
gratitude to God the Father and Christ the Redeemer, the Christian struggle
exercising Christ’s true members throughout life, the perpetual increase of
faith, godliness, and love and continual decrease and mortification of sin, or
sealing their free election and adoption in their hearts by the promised Holy
Spirit through that arduous and divine work of sanctification. Or at least,
because of this world’s fleeting and frivolous things—indeed, shadows and
dreams of things—they attend very little to these heavenly realities.

Or if you desire fortifications and defenses of consolation and faith, like
the provision and equipment necessary for a faithful soul in every day, mode,
and kind of trial, they may surely be drawn abundantly from here against every
kind of temptation. We must now especially prepare ourselves to bear these
with an unbroken and steadfast soul while this calm lasts, scarcely ever
trustworthy or firm for God’s true Church. We must not delay until winds rush
in, waves press upon us, and storms mass together; rather, our breasts must be
fortified early, and every kind of spiritual weapon carefully stored in their
inner chambers for every use in life. From this best-equipped armory our
commander Jesus Christ graciously supplies an abundance of such weapons
against every assault of an enemy powerful in both force and fraud.

[Marginalia: Its latter part.]

Finally, consider the style and character of the Holy Spirit speaking through
the mouth of this chosen instrument, expressed in this Epistle as he sets
before us the mysteries of eternal salvation introduced above. Nothing could
be said or imagined more fitted and effective to inflame souls that love God
and Christ with vehement desire for these things. The apostle does not recount
and explain these eternal and indescribable benefits to the Ephesians dryly,
sparingly, meagerly, perfunctorily, with no or only slight feeling for them.
Good God! With what fullness of heart and mouth, what feeling of soul, what
energy and efficacy of Spirit, what weight of thoughts, and what elevated,
intensified, magnificent speech he pursues them all and pours them from his
pregnant breast like a bubbling spring!

[Marginalia: Reason 4.]

Whether he speaks of the praise of God’s glorious grace; the overflowing
abundance of all wisdom and understanding; the riches of the glory of his
inheritance in the saints; the surpassing greatness of his power exercised in
believers according to the working of his might and strength; God’s rich mercy
and the surpassing riches of his grace and kindness toward us in Christ Jesus;
the unsearchable riches of Christ; God’s manifold wisdom; Christ’s love,
infinite in every dimension, in which he wills us to be rooted and founded;
all God’s fullness with which he wishes us to be filled; the whole panoply or
complete armor of the faithful; the perseverance he requires of the saints,
keeping watch through the Spirit in every prayer, petition, and supplication
for all saints at all times; the opening of his mouth with boldness to make
known Christ’s gospel, indeed the mystery of the gospel; or other such things
occurring throughout this Epistle—who does not see that this breast full of the
Spirit of God and Christ, even with so great an exertion of mind and so splendid
and excellent an array of the weightiest words, was still quite unable to
equal the greatness, height, and excellence of the realities he discussed?

[Marginalia: Ephesians 1:6, 8, 18–19; 2:4, 7; 3:8, 10, 18; 6:11, 18, etc.]

Lest anyone think those words, phrases, and epithets hyperbolic and exceeding
the reality’s measure because they display nothing slight, moderate, or finite,
far, far from us be that impious thought! Although the apostle spoke from
extraordinary feeling and marvelous abundance of heart, he nevertheless
discussed those heavenly realities more according to the measure of grace
given him than according to their immeasurable sublimity and extent. So far
was the abundance and fullness of his speech from exceeding their weight and
majesty.

[Marginalia: By anticipation.]

If this is so, as no true Christian will deny, and we descend into ourselves
and examine ourselves as in a mirror of this apostolic breast, alas, what
stupor, what insensibility, what slight feeling, and what little advancement
in God’s kingdom and progress in spiritual life we shall perceive if we compare
our dry, meager, earthly—indeed still stony—hearts with this fullness! What,
then? Have the springs of grace long since dried up? Has that abyss of kindness
become a ford? Are those treasures of blessings exhausted? Have those
unsearchable riches failed? Has Christ’s love, beyond every dimension, ceased
or cooled? Has the Lord’s arm been shortened, or has the efficacy of his mighty
strength changed—the strength he once exercised first in Christ himself the
head, and then in giving life, enlightening, renewing, possessing, governing,
and filling his living members together with the head so powerfully with a
certain fullness of his Spirit?

[Marginalia: A noteworthy corollary.]

Certainly not, dearest friends. Rather, everlasting thanks and blessing be to
him who, after the gospel’s radiance was restored to the earth, clearly
exercised in many thousands this same marvelous power which the apostle both
felt and proclaimed so greatly. Not only did their most holy lives and most
blessed departure from the body savor of nothing but the divine and heavenly;
the highly useful labors and writings of some still abundantly breathe and
attest that same fullness of God. Since—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 41 | Præfatio p. 7]

—these things are so, hearers, either our souls must be wholly atheistic and
profane, or our hearts stony and harder than any flint, if they do not excite,
move, and stir us to seek, desire, and thirst for some sense and experience in
ourselves of this great grace and life and this great efficacy of Christ’s
Spirit. We must never cease or rest until we too become sharers with Paul and
all the saints in these riches and blessings and in this spiritual power. For
otherwise, alas, why have we been preserved for this favorable time? Why have
we ever seen this gracious light of the gospel? Why have we come or assembled
here? What benefit is there for us in chattering about these things like
parrots, without feeling, motion, God’s life, or the efficacy of his Spirit,
like blind men debating colors? What use in wrangling without end, after the
manner of sophists, about grace, righteousness, holiness, faith, the life of
God, and other such things, if meanwhile we ourselves spend a miserable life
without and untasting all of them? Just how much did it once benefit the
followers of falsely named wisdom to dispute all their lives about humanity’s
highest good or blessedness without Christ the Mediator, true faith, remission
of sins, repentance, living hope, righteousness, peace, and spiritual joy?

[Marginalia: Application.]

See, therefore, dearest friends, what fruit above all I wish you to carry away
from so excellent a treatment of this Epistle, which by God’s guidance and
favor I undertake for you. By comparing Paul’s marvelous abundance with our
condition, I want you to recognize our own meagerness, dryness, barrenness, and
stupor; and from this to gain an insatiable and unquenchable hunger, thirst,
and ardor for life and grace, until we too are at last filled and satisfied
with that fullness of God. From this it is clear to everyone, unless I am
mistaken, how fruitful the explanation of this Epistle will be for us if we
approach, study, and treat it with this mind, and what weighty reasons led me
to select this Epistle above the others, embracing the counsel of that lyric
poet: “When a work is begun, set a far-shining face upon it”—that is, place a
most radiant façade upon the work begun.

[Marginalia: Ode 6.]

It remains now, after giving the reason for our design, to preface this Epistle
in the customary manner with these particular matters: 1. Ephesus itself, or
the Ephesians to whom this Epistle was directed; 2. the apostle’s purpose and
thus his occasion for writing it; 3. its subject or summary; 4. the principal
commonplaces of theological doctrine to be observed and explained in it; and,
finally, its division into primary parts. It would be superfluous to say much
about the author himself, Paul the apostle, or rather the Holy Spirit’s
instrument and secretary in composing it. Once these matters were completed,
we would proceed to expound the text itself. But since some of these prefatory
articles have already been sufficiently clarified above, we shall pass over
the remaining preliminaries with a drier foot.

[Marginalia: Transition. The heads of this preface.]

First, concerning the city Ephesus and its Ephesian inhabitants, it is too
well known to require us to linger over its explanation. It was a most famous
city of Asia Minor. Although Herodotus in *Clio* assigns it to Lydia, the
consensus of other historians reckons it in Ionia. This matters little, since
Lydia borders Ionia and Herodotus in the same place numbers the Ephesians among
the Ionians because they used the Ionian dialect, although he places Ephesus
itself in Lydia. The city was anciently famous on many accounts: for a naval
harbor so convenient and opportune for Greeks crossing into Asia or from Asia
into Greece that it became the most celebrated commercial center of nearer
Asia; and for several famous natives, such as Heraclitus, a man of no obscure
reputation though his obscure writings earned him the surname “the Dark”;
Hermodorus the philosopher; the painters Apelles and Parrhasius; Alexander the
orator; and Theodotion the Jew, who translated the sacred Bible into Greek.
Above all it was famous for the worship of the idol which superstitious and
blind antiquity called Diana. Many kings and peoples built her at Ephesus a
most magnificent temple distinguished by 127 columns, and thus the name Diana
of the Ephesians spread through the whole ancient world, as is evident even
from Acts 19.

[Marginalia: Section 1. On Ephesus.]

For these and similar matters concerning Ephesus, consult ancient records:
Herodotus in *Clio*, Pliny book 36, Pausanias in *Achaica*, and others. For the
apostle Paul’s first arrival at Ephesus, his return and stay there through all
three years in which he diligently instructed them in gospel doctrine by his
living voice; the things that befell him while at Ephesus through the labor and
counsels of that idol-mad Demetrius and his Diana—that is, the Ephesian
devil’s ringleader—and finally his last address at Miletus to elders summoned
from the Ephesian church while Paul was passing through and going up to
Jerusalem, see Acts 18, 19, and 20. Concerning the apostle’s statement in 1
Corinthians 15 that he fought beasts at Ephesus, I do not find agreement among
the most learned interpreters whether it is to be understood literally or
metaphorically of those stubborn and obstinate Jews from the Ephesian
synagogue whom Luke mentions in Acts 19:9.

[Marginalia: Passing over the details. 1 Corinthians 15.]

The apostle appears further to have placed Timothy the evangelist over this
church, or at least to have left him at Ephesus for a time when departing for
Macedonia, as he expressly writes in 1 Timothy 1. Eusebius also reports in book
3 of his *Ecclesiastical History* that John the evangelist governed this
church for a time. Here too, in the year of Christ 450, or according to others
437, under Theodosius the Younger, the third ecumenical synod against Nestorius
was gathered and held, presided over by Cyril, bishop of Alexandria. So much
for the place or city.

[Marginalia: 1 Timothy 1:3.]

Concerning the purpose and occasion, enough has already been said above. We
cannot gather from either this Epistle or Luke’s history in Acts that the
occasion for writing was schism and dissension, as in the first Epistle to the
Corinthians, or defection from gospel purity, as in the preceding Epistle to
the Galatians. It was rather godly care and concern for this church, intensified
in the apostolic breast by the danger which the prophetic Spirit foresaw
threatening the Ephesians from false teachers. He openly warns in Acts 20 that
he knows grievous wolves will arise after his departure, not sparing the flock
but attacking it with every cunning and fraud to draw away and seduce disciples
with their perverse doctrine. Another occasion was joined to this and is
expressly mentioned in the Epistle: lest they be ashamed of his chains, in
which he was held captive at Rome for the gospel—

[Marginalia: Section 2. On the purpose and occasion. Acts 20.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 42 | Præfatio p. 8]

—and lest the Ephesians’ courage fail or grow weak because of the affliction
their most faithful apostle and teacher endured in Christ’s name, or turn aside
from the right course of truth and godliness through fear or shame. His
proposed purpose, then, was the same one that ought to be the purpose of all
our life and study, and the end and purpose of the entire gospel ministry
established in the Christian Church: not only our confirmation—just as that of
the Ephesians then—in faith, truth, purity of life, and holiness corresponding
to Christian profession, but also perpetual increase and progress until at
last we reach the goal of our heavenly calling.

For this end—to touch now in third place the third of these prolegomena, which
was to concern the subject or sum of the Epistle—after greeting the Ephesians
in the customary manner, Paul first proposes those immense benefits of God in
Christ generally and then introduces them specifically, by a method especially
agreeable to the nature of the things themselves. He applies the same
benefits—particularly calling and vivification—to Jews and Ephesians, and thus
to the remaining nations called to communion with Christ. The Ephesians’
calling, first proposed generally, he later explains more particularly and
distinctly, both comparatively to illustrate it and by other arguments to
confirm it.

[Marginalia: Section 3. On the summary and subject.]

And lest the teacher’s condition offend their minds and so impede the work of
edification which the apostle kept before his eyes while writing, he carefully
addresses the scandal of the cross. After explaining the cause of his chains,
he exhorts them not to grow sluggish because of his afflictions. Then, as he
had done above, he digresses into a place suitable for winning goodwill,
namely fervent prayers conceived for the Ephesians, so that the conclusion of
the preceding exhortation might move their minds with greater force and leave
sharper goads fixed in them. Once the doctrine is finished, he most suitably
attaches exhortations, both a general one to every duty worthy of a Christian
person and particular ones concerning a definite way of life, sex, or
condition, in which no one should demand a precisely exact order. To these
particular duties he adds, as a conclusion, one common general exhortation for
all about putting on the spiritual panoply. Leaving his private affairs to his
brother and fellow worker Tychicus, who would explain them to the Ephesians in
person, he concludes this divine Epistle with almost the same greeting with
which he began it. He executes all these things, I say, to exhort the
Ephesians to constancy in faith and godliness and urge continual progress on
that way of salvation, and to arm and fortify them against every force, fear,
shame, and fraud of temptations, persecutions, his chains, and false teachers—
especially Jews striving to mix law with gospel, faith with works, truth with
figures, body with shadows, that is, fire with water.

Concerning the fourth head, we have already sufficiently and accurately
enumerated the commonplaces of Christian doctrine treated in this Epistle.
Among them these rise above the rest like the summits of lofty peaks: the
election and predestination of the saints; the Holy Spirit’s offices toward the
elect and believers; Christ the head of the Church, his glorious state and
ascension on high; original sin, or the wretched condition outside Christ; the
catholic Church’s union both with Christ its head and within itself;
ecclesiastical ministries; marriage; the conduct of Christian life and the
contrary vices to flee; and spiritual struggle and armor.

[Marginalia: Section 4. On the principal commonplaces.]

There remains only the last portion of our preface: the division of the
Epistle into principal parts, which is sufficiently clear by itself to an
attentive reader. Leaving aside two less principal portions—the greeting
prefixed at the start of chapter 1, and the notice attached near the end about
Tychicus’s coming with its prayerful closing—we see the remaining body divided
into two principal parts. The former is largely didactic, concerning God’s
benefits toward believers in Christ or the causes of salvation, contained in
the first three chapters. The latter is more exhortatory, concerning the
duties of believers toward God for testifying their gratitude to him for such
immense benefits, contained in the final three chapters. Thus, just as we may
say the purpose of the former part is the increase of faith, so we may assert
the purpose of the latter is the increase of repentance.

[Marginalia: Section 5. On the division.]

In the first part he discusses the mystery of salvation not only of the
Ephesians but of all believers. By expounding the causes of salvation and
placing their former misery before their eyes, he intends to confirm their
faith, in which true and certain salvation consists. In the latter part he
hands down the various precepts of Christian life, to excite and encourage
them to testify a grateful heart toward God the Savior by duties of godliness
and the pursuit of a new and holy life. We may therefore conclude in one
sentence: in the former part he confirms faith; in the latter he kindles
repentance, or the pursuit of godliness.

[Marginalia: Two things to note. Consequence 1.]

We shall draw two things in passing before proceeding to the exposition of the
words themselves. First, this apostolic custom of discussing Christian
doctrine first and then conduct, the fruits of faith—an order Paul keeps
almost everywhere—observes an especially natural and fitting order, since good
works and the Christian life are effects of faith, not the reverse; good fruit
springs from faith no differently than from a living root; and we are justified
not by works but by faith apprehending Christ and his free benefits. It cannot
be doubted that the apostle intended to show this to us almost with his finger
by this method of teaching.

[Marginalia: Consequence 2.]

Second, since we see the apostle draw from that teaching of faith various
exhortations and consolations adapted to the use and times of the churches to
which he writes, it ought immediately to occur to us that the doctrine of
grace is so far from making truly faithful souls sluggish in good works that
nothing equally sharpens, inflames, and stirs them to live well and holily as
the remembrance of God’s supreme loving-kindness and goodness toward us in
Jesus Christ. The greater its sense and apprehension in our hearts, the greater
and more ardent the pursuit of godliness and good works necessarily thrives
and grows warm in them. This is especially so when we consider the end of our
calling: not that we should roll back into the same mud from which we have
already been washed and cleansed by so precious a bath—the blood of Christ
himself, God most good and great—but that, walking continually according to the
holiness of our calling, we should serve our God and seriously compose and
conform ourselves and our whole life to the most perfect and pure example of
Christ our head. May the heavenly Father’s boundless and inexhaustible goodness
accomplish this through Christ’s life-giving death. AMEN.
