ON CHAPTER ONE:

And first,

ON THE SALUTATION.

[Marginalia: Lecture II. Transition.]

Now that these preliminary matters and prerequisites have been set out, it is
time for us to turn to considering attentively the Apostle’s own words. We
shall begin with the salutation or inscription contained in the first two
verses. Although it is common to nearly all our Apostle’s epistles and thus
more familiar and obvious, it has not yet been treated by us as we begin this
work and therefore must by no means be passed over lightly. Once we have
performed this task in a single epistle, we may be relieved of it in expounding
the rest. Three members, then, occur in this inscription—or salutation, if you
prefer: the person greeting, who wrote this epistle; the persons greeted, to
whom this epistle is directed; and the salutation itself, properly and
strictly taken, that is, the good things for which the person greeting prays
and which he desires for those greeted. The first two are contained in verse
one, the last in verse two.

[Marginalia: Division of the salutation.]

As to the first, the person greeting is Paul the Apostle. He is designated
both by name and described from the accompanying office of apostleship and
from his divine calling to that office, when he is said to be “an Apostle of
Jesus Christ, by the will of God.” Here the Apostle illustrates that office
from its authors: Christ, whose embassy he discharged and whose Apostle he
was, and God the Father himself, by whose will he discharged that embassy—or
apostleship, if you prefer.

[Marginalia: The person greeting.]

Concerning the name Paul, we note only this: Acts 13 records that he was called
both Saul and Paul. Interpreters do not agree on the reason. Some hold that he
had two names and was called by the one Jewish name Saul because he was Jewish
by nation, but by the other, Paul in Latin, because he had been born a Roman
citizen at Tarsus in Cilicia, whose citizens had been granted the right of
Roman citizenship, as he himself testifies in Acts 22. Others say that with
his religion he also changed his name because, as Augustine says more piously
and cleverly than solidly, from a proud Pharisee he had become a little
disciple of Christ, just as new names were given by the Lord to some of the
Apostles. Others think that the Apostle acquired the name from the proconsul
Sergius Paulus when the latter was converted to the faith.

We cannot agree with any of them. If he had changed his name when he changed
his religion, Luke ought to have used this name immediately from his
conversion, which he nowhere did before Acts 13. Nor from that passage onward
does Luke ever use the name Saul, while he calls him Paul even before the
proconsul was brought to faith. Neither was it customary for a person’s name
to be changed by God without some mystery, as with Abraham, Jacob, Peter, and
others. And if he had possessed two names, Luke would not have concealed this
from us from the very beginning of the history and delayed it until this
passage.

[Marginalia: Paul by removal and by addition.]

We therefore rather embrace the opinion of Beza, who judges that the same name
is designated by the forms Saul and Paul, but was altered a little by Greeks
and Latins to the form and shape of their own languages. Thus Jochanas,
Joannes, Jean, Hans, John, Joan, and Giovanni are the same name; so too Ludwik
or Dietrich is the same as Louys or Thierry, that is, Lodovicus or Theodoricus,
but varied according to the languages. Thus the man whom the Hebrews and
Syrians called Shaul was called Paul by the Greeks, who were then accustomed
to Roman names, because of the great affinity of these names. Nor is it
without reason that Luke uses the name Paul from the time when, after having
previously lived among Jews and Syrians, he turned to the Gentiles, to whom he
thereafter never ceased to preach the gospel. Luke therefore rightly retained
the name that was continually used among the Gentiles for naming our Apostle.
It is indeed not improbable that he was first called this by the household of
the Roman proconsul.

As for Paul’s own person—who and what sort of man he was both before and after
his conversion—it is plain not only from the history of Acts but also from
various epistles of Paul himself, particularly Philippians, Galatians, 1
Timothy, 1 Corinthians 15, 2 Corinthians 11, and elsewhere. All these matters
are so well known to every Christian from sacred history that by rehearsing
them I think I would wrong the ears of those to whom, by God’s grace, they have
long been known and familiar: his tribe and homeland; his Pharisaic sect; his
teacher Gamaliel; his blameless life according to that sect; his zeal for the
law and ancestral traditions, from which the persecution of the Church arose;
his authority among the Jews, such that he obtained power from the chief
priests to search out Christians and take them bound to prison; his conversion
to Christ, its manner, time, circumstances, and the events that followed it;
and other things of that kind. As for what Nicephorus writes about the form and
stature of his body in book 2, chapter 37, since that author tells so many
other fabulous and incredible things, he weakens his own credibility even in
matters where he might otherwise tell the truth.

[Marginalia: What sort of person Paul was.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 44 | Caput I p. 2]

Paul himself elsewhere discusses his apostleship abundantly, especially in
Romans 1, Galatians 1, and chapter 3 of this epistle. From the latter it will
be plain in its place, as also from Galatians 2, that he was particularly
appointed Apostle of the Gentiles. Three things are required for this office.
First, an immediate calling by Christ himself and a sending to preach the
gospel. Second, an extraordinary illumination of the Holy Spirit, by whose
inspiration the Apostles were taught evangelical doctrine immediately and not
by ordinary study. From this also followed that testimony of the Holy Spirit
concerning their immunity from error in teaching, whether in writing or by the
living voice: because they were governed immediately by the Holy Spirit, their
doctrine was self-authenticating and their writings God-breathed. Third, they
were not bound to fixed seats but possessed authority to plant, found, and
water churches everywhere.

Thus an Apostle is Christ’s ambassador, called immediately by Christ himself
to preach the gospel and furnished with an extraordinary light of knowledge
or doctrine, a testimony of freedom from error, and the authority of the
highest ministry in the Church—that is, of planting churches everywhere. He
therefore holds the first rank and order among all other ecclesiastical
ministries. Histories and experience testify that Paul possessed all these,
and that in a singular manner as a chosen vessel to preach Christ to the
Gentiles, because he both labored more than all and suffered more things for
Christ’s name.

[Marginalia: His apostleship.]

He calls himself an APOSTLE OF JESUS CHRIST with regard both to the author of
his calling and to the subject of his preaching. He professed that he knew
nothing but Jesus Christ and him crucified—Jesus Christ, I say, the wisdom of
God and the power of God (1 Corinthians 2). No one asserted more powerfully,
fully, and constantly than this chosen instrument the grace of Christ, indeed
the fullness of grace and its sufficiency for eternal life and salvation,
against false apostles who pressed the law of Moses and the righteousness of
works. In the inscriptions of his epistles he makes perpetual mention of the
author of his calling, namely Jesus Christ, by styling himself an Apostle of
Jesus Christ. He does so chiefly for two apparent reasons. First, he would
defend his apostleship against the slanders and barking of the false apostles,
who tried by hidden tunnels to undermine the authority of this eminent servant
of Christ because he had not lived with Christ on earth as the others had,
although it was established that he had been called by Christ himself from
heaven. Second, by this name he would commend his doctrine to the faithful as
having proceeded from Christ himself, whose embassy to them he discharged. He
taught the churches nothing other than what he had received from the Lord
himself, as he expressly testifies in 1 Corinthians 11.

[Marginalia: Of Jesus Christ.]

Moreover, in describing his apostleship he adds another phrase that must by no
means be passed over in silence, when he affirms that he is an Apostle of Jesus
Christ BY THE WILL OF GOD. By the name God he indeed understands the first
person of the Deity, namely God the Father *by preeminence*, as some orthodox
Doctors speak, that is, because of that prerogative of order and source. Yet we
do not thereby exclude the will of the Son and Holy Spirit, which is one and
the same with the will of God the Father, just as there is the same nature,
Deity, majesty, essence, power, operation, or action toward creatures, while
the inviolable and immutable order of the Persons is preserved.

[Marginalia: By the will of God.]

The name *Father* is sometimes taken personally—namely, when he is compared
with the Son or Holy Spirit—for the Father’s own person as distinct from the
other two. At other times it is taken essentially for the whole Deity common
to the three Persons, when God is opposed to creatures, as when the Father is
said to be “the only true God,” to “alone possess immortality,” and so forth,
to the exclusion of creatures and idols or false gods. So too the very name
*God* is sometimes taken properly and essentially as equally common to all
three Persons when there is no mention of the Persons distinctly and
individually. At other times it is taken personally and less properly to
denote the Father’s own person by preeminence as the fountain and origin of
Deity. Even then the other two Persons are not excluded but rather included in
the Father as the first source and fountain. This occurs whenever God is
mentioned separately together with Christ the Mediator, as here and in 1
Timothy 1, where Paul mentions “God our Savior and the Lord Jesus Christ our
hope.”

[Marginalia: Note well. 1 Timothy 6:16; 1 Timothy 1:1.]

That the will of the Son and Holy Spirit is not excluded is plain not only
from their consubstantiality—that is, from the same nature and will of all
three Persons—but also from the rich testimonies of holy Scripture. Galatians
1 says, “not from men nor through a man, but through Jesus Christ.” Acts 13
says, “And the Holy Spirit said, Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the
work to which I have called them.” Acts 20 says, “Take heed to yourselves and
to the whole flock, in which the Holy Spirit has appointed you bishops.”
Ephesians 4 says, “Christ gave some to be Apostles,” and so forth. Paul
therefore affirms that he was called to the apostleship immediately by Christ
as Mediator, through whom God the Father governs all things and especially his
Church; yet he was called by the will of God the Father, because Christ’s call
to this office came from the will of God the Father, with which the will of
the Son and Holy Spirit is also the same, just as their nature, Deity, essence,
and majesty is one and the same.

This phrase is also added to confirm the dignity of his office and ministry,
and not so much to obtain as to uphold and preserve among the Ephesians, and
indeed all the faithful, the authority of his doctrine. In the
exercise of his whole ministry he regarded solely the glory of the one God and
the salvation and edification of the churches, not himself or his own dignity,
honor, or gain. He thereby leaves us to gather this most beautiful use: only
when Christ’s glory and the Church’s salvation and edification require it may
we freely and steadfastly assert our calling to the office we discharge in
God’s Church and vindicate it from the slanders and lies of the enemies of
truth. We must set that glory, salvation, and edification before ourselves
throughout our life as we perform the office entrusted to us by God.

[Marginalia: Reason and use.]

As for the second part of this salutation, the persons greeted, namely the
Ephesians, were discussed above in the preface. Only this must be observed in
passing. First, it certainly seems remarkable that Basil, in book 2 against
Eunomius, omits the phrase “in Ephesus” (ἐν Ἐφέσῳ) from this inscription,
without which the inscription would be altogether deficient and imperfect,
since the people to whom the epistle was written would not be named, contrary
to our Apostle’s solemn custom in his other epistles. Indeed, he not only
omits it, but—what may seem still more remarkable in so great a man, so
practiced and experienced in handling holy Scripture—he manifestly stumbled
in so obvious a matter.

[Marginalia: The persons greeted. Second part. I.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 45 | Caput I p. 3]

From this Basil even seizes an occasion to argue that only the faithful truly
*are* or exist, because they are joined by faith to God, who is truth and life
itself. But the other nations, destitute of the knowledge of God and joined to
a lie, “that which is not,” are not properly said to be or exist, according to
the Apostle’s statement in 1 Corinthians 1 that God chose “the things that are
not,” and so forth. These are plainly sheer trifles and empty subtleties. Let
us learn even from this that the holiest Fathers, though most practiced in
holy Scripture, were nevertheless men, could err in the human manner, and
quite often did err in searching out Scripture’s true sense. We therefore
must not seize without examination and inquiry whatever we find in their
writings.

Second, not all the Ephesians indiscriminately are addressed by this Apostolic
salutation, but only the Ephesian Church. Here we should note its description
or circumscription: TO THE SAINTS WHO ARE AT EPHESUS, AND TO THE FAITHFUL IN
CHRIST JESUS. What else is the Church but the communion of saints, that is, the
assembly of the faithful in Christ Jesus? They are called saints for three
reasons. First, by separation or segregation from the remaining crowd of
profane people: by God’s effectual call they were chosen and separated from
the world, that is, from the remaining corrupt mass of the human race. This is
the proper and peculiar sense of the word *Kodesh* in holy Scripture. Not only
are people, or that holy nation and people whom God chose as his peculiar
possession, called holy; all other things transferred, dedicated, and
consecrated from profane and common uses to the worship of God and to sacred
and religious use are also called holy: the temple, vessels, victims,
offerings, tithes, and so forth.

Second, they are saints by imputation: Christ’s innocence, holiness, and
righteousness is imputed by faith to the faithful who are in Jesus Christ and
accepted by God the Father, just as if they appeared holy and righteous in
their own persons before his tribunal. Finally, they are saints by the
beginning of holiness in them through the Holy Spirit, who sanctifies them and
renews them after Christ’s image. This holiness is begun here, continued
throughout life, and advances as if through its degrees, but will finally be
perfected in the life to come.

[Marginalia: Of the saints and faithful. 1. 2. 3.]

Paul further explains “saints” by “faithful in Christ Jesus,” showing both the
fountain and origin of all this holiness of the faithful, namely Christ Jesus,
and also the means and manner by which we are sanctified in him, namely faith:
for by faith we are justified, and by faith our hearts are purified. I am not
unaware that some hold the word *saints* to be more common and general,
embracing all who have given their name to Christ and profess the Christian
faith, whether they are truly faithful or only hypocrites. They have at least
been sacramentally sanctified through baptism and cleansed from sins, and by
the rule of charity are to be regarded as truly holy and clean so long as they
do not betray themselves. On this view, the word *faithful* is narrower and
belongs only to those who possess true faith and therefore are also truly holy
and washed from sins in Christ’s blood. The Apostle properly had regard only
for them and properly wrote this epistle to them so as to confirm them more
and more in the faith they possessed. But the former exposition pleases me
more as the simpler one, especially since all who do not show the contrary by
corruption of life and morals are to be regarded as such by charity.

[Marginalia: Acts 15:9.]

As for the good things themselves that the Apostle prays for them—the third
part of this salutation—in his customary manner he does not wish them health,
joy, success, or other such things current among the Gentiles themselves. He
wishes them GRACE AND PEACE FROM GOD THE FATHER as the fountain, first cause,
and source of every good, and FROM OUR LORD JESUS CHRIST as Mediator, through
whom the Father gives the elect all things, both heavenly and spiritual, and
earthly and bodily.

[Marginalia: Third part. The good things desired.]

When Jesus Christ is called Lord, the Father and Holy Spirit are not thereby
excluded as if they too did not possess, together with the Son, supreme and
absolute dominion over creatures. Rather, the Son is regarded as Mediator of
God and men and according to that royal office by which he was given to us by
the Father as King and head of the Church and constituted Lord of all
creatures, since all power in heaven and earth was given to him. Conversely,
when the Father alone is called God, the Son and Holy Spirit, who are one and
the same God with the Father, are not thereby excluded. But because an evident
distinction between the Son and Father must be made—one adapted to teaching us
and training us for salvation—Deity is rightly ascribed to the Father as its
fountain and origin, in and with whom the two remaining Persons are understood
insofar as they are the same God with the Father. Others express this by
saying that the Father is called God by preeminence, as we noted above, on
account of the prerogative of order and source; but Lordship is ascribed to
Christ by reason of the office that he exercises from the Father as King and
Lord over every creature, and especially over his Church.

[Marginalia: Note carefully.]

As for the good things Paul desires, by *grace*—without any controversy among
interpreters that I know of—we must understand God’s gratuitous favor and free
benevolence, dependent on his mere good pleasure, kindness, mercy, and love
for humanity. With this he pursued the elect in Christ from eternity and
manifests the same in time by effectually calling and saving them.

[Marginalia: Grace.]

By *peace*, however, some understand a prosperous condition and happy success
in everything in this life, according to Hebrew usage and the customary form
of greeting. They judge that in one expression Paul signified and jointly
embraced eternal and spiritual goods, and in the other earthly and temporal
goods. But others, with whom I rather agree, understand the spiritual peace of
which the Apostle speaks in Romans 5:1 and Philippians 4:7: peace of
conscience, which arises in our hearts through the Holy Spirit with
inexpressible joy. It springs from confidence in the remission of sins through
Christ’s cross and blood, from a sense of God’s love poured into our hearts by
the Spirit of adoption, and from that full assurance that God in Jesus Christ
is not merely appeased and reconciled to us but is also our gracious and most
merciful Father. It therefore necessarily presupposes faith, confidence,
justification, effectual calling, solid hope, and supreme and glorious joy,
either as antecedents or inseparable companions. Thus under the name *grace* I
think I may properly comprehend the fountain and origin of every saving
blessing and benefit, namely the free benevolence of the merciful God; and
under *peace*, all those saving effects of grace that follow it in time. From
this we may see not only the supreme majesty and gravity of this Apostolic
salutation, which bears a splendid testimony and, as it were—

[Marginalia: Peace. A twofold inference.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 46 | Caput I p. 4 (through Lecture II close)]

—a summary both of faith in God and in Christ the Mediator and of Christian
love, but also the truly excellent condition of the faithful. They are not
called saints as the papists call only those entered in their calendar and
marked with red ink or rubric; rather, they are truly saints in Christ Jesus,
whatever their lot, sex, or condition in this life. Let us now pass to
explaining the body of the epistle itself.
