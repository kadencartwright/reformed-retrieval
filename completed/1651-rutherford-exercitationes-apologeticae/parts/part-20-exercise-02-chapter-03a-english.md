CHAPTER III

*Whether God's good pleasure is the first rule of all moral justice in God
toward creatures, and also of his goodness and mercy.*

The question presupposes that justice is in God essentially and prior to the
act of divine good pleasure. For who would say that God freely decrees that he
himself should be just and infinitely good, as though he were freely just and
freely good, and thus could be unjust and not good? But the question concerns
God's justice toward creatures. The same debate can be undertaken concerning
God's mercy, which regards creatures. For the Arminians maintain that this

[1651 logical printed p. 288 | IA leaf 306 | PDF p. 306]

relational mercy is in God a natural desire which, prior to a creature's merit or demerit,
desires the salvation of all men and angels by necessity of nature, yet in
such a way that by a fixed and peremptory will he has mercy on one no more
than another. But God has mercy on whom he wills, and freely; therefore not by
an impulse of nature. 2. Such a desire devoid of cognition is altogether
unworthy of God. 3. An inclination in God, or a natural tendency toward
something which most certainly will never occur, is a frustrated eternal
volition of God, which conflicts with God's infinity. 4. The Remonstrants in
their Apology deny that God's will is determined by any external agent. We ask,
then, how it is determined by the creature's misery, through a natural
impulse, to give aid to them.

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, ch. 2, § 44.]

Others maintain that mercy is indeed essential to God, yet that with respect
to its secondary acts it receives moderation and determination from God's
wise good pleasure. Hence the remote cause of an act of divine mercy toward
creatures is God's mercy, while its proximate cause is God's good pleasure.
Thus God's mercy is the cause why he has mercy indefinitely, while God's good
pleasure is the cause why he has mercy on this person rather than that one.
But allow me to argue against this as follows. 1. God's mercy is either a
remote natural cause or a free cause. If the former, by necessity of nature
God indefinitely has mercy on at least some. But this is false, for

[1651 logical printed p. 289 | IA leaf 307 | PDF p. 307]

he could have rejected everyone in order to manifest the glory of his justice. If the latter, the
same consequence follows: namely, that God can have mercy either on everyone
or on many. But mercy cannot be called formally a free cause. Add that if
mercy were a free cause, there would be no need for the free good pleasure by
which it is determined. 2. What God does from natural mercy, he does not freely
but necessarily. For mercy is not an entirely free principle of action in
God. I therefore say with the most learned William Twisse that God's mercy is
considered in two ways. 1. As it is a native and essential inclination in God
to communicate his goodness to the miserable. 2. As mercy is to be manifested
to creatures.

[Marginalia: Twisse, *Defense of Grace*, book 1, part 2, digression 5, section
4, ch. 1.]

In the first sense I confess that I cannot see—perhaps this is my
immaturity—how God can freely have mercy upon a creature so that acts of mercy
flow from God's mercy as from a vital and formal principle. For if acts of
mercy flow from God's natural inclination, which is disposed to aid the
miserable, he cannot but have mercy upon all men and angels. What flows from a
natural agent as such flows from it not by a free but by a necessary outflow.
For example, there is no reason why fire should burn this dry wood brought
near to it but not that one, because a natural agent acts equally upon all
subjects equally near and disposed. Likewise, there is no reason why God
should have mercy upon this person

[1651 logical printed p. 290 | IA leaf 308 | PDF p. 308]

rather than that one. But the case of mercy as it is to be manifested to creatures is
far different. This mercy appears to me not to be that native and essential
mercy which is in God, but relational and σχετική, which is in God from the
free determination of his divine good pleasure. I define this mercy as God's
benevolent affection, which is in God insofar as he aids miserable creatures
from his free good pleasure. But this benevolent affection presupposes a decree
and free intention to show mercy. Hence, if God had mercy from natural mercy,
he could not fail to have mercy upon this person while having mercy upon that
one, unless he transgressed against his own merciful nature. But without doubt
God can no more sin against his mercy than against his justice. Yet through
this relational mercy, which presupposes God's free decree regarding this
person and not that one, the freedom to have mercy is left to God. This mercy,
however, is not a weak wish or natural will to aid every miserable creature as
far as possible while justice and wisdom remain intact. For God cannot will or
do anything contrary to what justice and wisdom allow. Otherwise there would
be in God a power released from justice and a power to sin. They might say
that theologians consider God's omnipotence absolutely insofar as it precedes
the act of divine good pleasure, which freely prescribes for God a rule of
willing and acting, and that in this order of reason God can do nothing

[1651 logical printed p. 291 | IA leaf 309 | PDF p. 309]

by omnipotence which he cannot do in the order of reason following the free
decree. Thus God absolutely could have delivered Christ by more than twelve
legions of angels, but after the decree he could not do so. Reply. This power
in God is only natural, not moral. Indeed, not even omnipotence naturally does
anything apart from God's good pleasure, for it would produce an effect apart
from God's wise intention, which would be blasphemous. Hence there is no power
of acting in God except that which he can bring into act, so to speak, after
calling upon his most wise good pleasure. Nor does it follow that God could
not have delivered Christ from death, but that he could not do so simply
except on the hypothesis that he had so decreed. For a new act of complacency
apart from a preceding eternal decree of God establishing that such an act
would occur is simply impossible. Hence omnipotence will necessarily always be
limited by some free good pleasure of God, although it need not be limited by
this or that act of good pleasure. From this it follows: 1. God's power and
God's good pleasure extend equally far. God has no power to do more things
than he can determine by his good pleasure; simple omnipotence and the power
to will and act wisely are entirely equal in scope. 2. Hence, considered
simply with respect to natural mercy, God could have had mercy upon those who
are now reprobate and could have withheld mercy from those who are now

[1651 logical printed p. 292 | IA leaf 310 | PDF p. 310]

elect. God's σχετικὴ, or relational, mercy would have been just as wise in that
case as it is now.
But with respect to relational mercy after God's free determination, God
cannot have mercy and withhold mercy upon any others than those upon whom he
now in fact has mercy and withholds mercy. Thus mercy is owed to the elect,
owed according to the rule of God's good pleasure which prescribes this manner
of acting to God; and the withholding of mercy is owed to the reprobate
according to the order of divine consistency and constancy. For without the
stain of inconstancy God cannot fail to act and have mercy when he has once
decreed to act and have mercy. Thus God's good pleasure is always his rule for
willing and acting, just as the law of nature and the written Word are the
rule for angels and men in willing and acting. 3. Relational justice is also
God's constant will to reward the obedient with life and damn the disobedient.
This volition is God's free constitution. And—what might seem surprising—the
justice of God revealed in Scripture is not God's essential will to give the
creature what is its own. Indeed, it is in God not necessarily but freely, not
essentially but contingently. The same is true of God's relational mercy and
goodness. For if by necessity of nature God gave the creature what is its own,
he would give everyone the eternal destruction which they have deserved,
would leave everyone in sin, and would

[1651 logical printed p. 293 | IA leaf 311 | PDF p. 311]

damn everyone. For this is properly ours. And God could not fail to give it to
us if these acts flowed formally from justice as heating flows from fire.
Therefore God's abandoning and hardening sinners are not acts of God's
justice. If they were, the act of not hardening sinners and the act of
forgiving sinners their sins would be an act of injustice; yet such acts are
in God. The distinction between mercy and justice is rather this: those in
whom God declares rewarding and avenging justice are identified in Scripture
by a definite quality, namely those who are finally obedient and those who are
finally disobedient. But although the vessels of at least the first mercy—which
I believe to be effectual calling, not election—are signified and determined
by name and most particularly in God's secret decree, there is nevertheless
no particular determination in Scripture. For nowhere is it written, “Do this
and you will be effectually called.” God's unrevealed good pleasure identifies
the recipients of the first mercy.

2. It is from God's absolute will that the second death is owed to final
impenitence. For although God's threats are made according to the order of
justice, that order of justice is from God's absolute will. Hence it is clear
that sin's demerit is according to the order of justice. And since justice is
from God's good pleasure, it is clear that it is not true that the damned
deserve punishments more severe than those which they actually incur.

[1651 logical printed p. 294 | IA leaf 312 | PDF p. 312]

For demerit is properly what is owed from the order of justice, and mediately
what is owed from the order of God's good pleasure. But no one ought to incur
punishments more severe than God has seen fit to inflict according to his wise
good pleasure. Yet this is also most true: if God inflicted punishments six
times more severe upon the damned, that would indeed be just, for he would
inflict them according to the wise good pleasure which is the first rule of
justice. Hence it is just that all Adam's descendants share in the guilt and
first sin of Adam because it so seemed good to God. And if everyone had been
born from the sinner Adam free from sin, that would have been equally just.
Thus both of these are true: “We mortals deserve far more grievous troubles
and miseries than we suffer,” and, “We mortals do not deserve far more
grievous troubles and miseries than we suffer.” The first statement is true
on the hypothesis of God's good pleasure. For it would not have been unjust if
God had inflicted more grievous troubles upon us sinners, provided that it had
seemed good to God. The latter statement is true because it is neither fair
nor a just demerit that we suffer more grievous miseries than those to which,
according to God's actual good pleasure, we are now in fact subjected and now
suffer. Thus, without a distinction, it is not true that sinners deserve more
grievous things than they suffer, although that manner of speaking commonly
prevails from a certain devout submission to God's most just will.

3. But perhaps it seems harsh that the justice

[1651 logical printed p. 295 | IA leaf 313 | PDF p. 313]

which is essentially in God is not revealed to us in Scripture. I reply that
it is revealed. For Scripture teaches that God would not be God if he were not
just. But the justice toward his creatures which is discussed everywhere in
Scripture is in God not essentially but according to his free good pleasure.
Yet in this relational justice there is something which is essentially in
God. For it is just that the finally disobedient be damned because God freely
so decreed, although God could absolutely have had mercy upon the finally
disobedient without loss or expense to justice, if he had so willed. But once
it has been established that the finally disobedient are to be damned, God
cannot fail to damn them because of the evenness, constancy, and truthfulness
of his most simple nature. For God's most constant evenness and truthfulness
require, prior to God's decree, that he execute and not annul his decrees and
steadfastly keep his promises. This constancy of God is not in him according
to free good pleasure but essentially. Indeed, he would not be God if he were
not truthful and constant. Hence these sayings: “Would Jehovah speak and not
act?” and, “Would the judge of the world not act rightly and justly?” Sin in
its formal character is something entirely contrary to the approving divine
will, and therefore sin cannot fail to displease God. For he would not be God
if sin did not displease him, since rectitude and constancy are essential to
God. But the punishment of sin is not formally included in the

[1651 logical printed p. 296 | IA leaf 314 | PDF p. 314]

essence of sin; it is something naturally subsequent to sin already
constituted in its integral essence. Hence God punishes sins by no necessity
of nature and indeed could absolutely refrain from punishing. For he punishes
only according to free good pleasure, and freely forgives many in Christ, the
Son of his love.

4. Thus it is one thing for God to do something from an intention to reveal
and manifest his justice, and an altogether different thing for him to do
something from justice. For what God does from an intention to manifest
justice, he does most freely, since he reprobates from an intention to manifest
justice and most freely could refrain from reprobating. But what he does from
justice itself he cannot fail to do unless he sins against justice, because
justice presupposes God's free decree. Let those who maintain that Esau's
reprobation was an act of justice toward Esau, therefore, say what free decree
was presupposed by that act: did God decree to reprobate Esau before he
reprobated him? Is that so? Then it was before God decreed, and so on to
infinity. For God's reprobation is the decree. I therefore say that these
claims are false: that God reprobates from justice as from a formal principle;
that from justice God abandons many in native corruption; that from justice he
denies them a Savior who would die for them; and that from justice he wills
not to call them effectually. Proof. 1. Justice is God's constant will to give
a creature what is owed to it, and that according to God's will restricted
and determined to a definite rule. But

[1651 logical printed p. 297 | IA leaf 315 | PDF p. 315]

reprobation is not owed to Esau. For God would not act contrary to what is owed
if he elected Esau, as no sane person says. Further, when someone asks for the
rule according to which God would reprobate Esau, abandon him in native sin,
and deny him Christ and effectual calling, no other answer having the force of
a rule could be devised except that Esau is a sinner. But this is no rule, for
then God would have reprobated everyone. 2. What God does from justice as from
a formal principle he cannot fail to do unless he acts against justice and
thus acts unjustly. But he reprobates and leaves men in sin in such a way that
he could, without any injustice, refrain from leaving and reprobating them,
etc. They say here that God is free either to elect and effectually call
according to mercy, etc., or to reprobate and deny Christ and effectual
calling. Reply. 1. By this answer they concede that God reprobates and leaves
in sin from a free choice not restricted to justice or mercy, which is to
concede the entire argument. It is the same as if they said that God does
these things from absolute good pleasure. Their error is that they do not
distinguish carefully enough between God's free will and God's just or
merciful will. For justice adds something to God's will. 2. Properly speaking,
God is not free to act according to justice with men, for that is a
contradiction in terms. For that is free to God which he can do or not do; but
that is just which

[1651 logical printed p. 298 | IA leaf 316 | PDF p. 316]

he cannot fail to do. Thus it is just, not free to God, to fulfill promises
and carry out threats, since not doing so is contrary to God's nature.

5. Hence these corollaries. 1. Vain is the fear of those who make the corrupt
mass the object of predestination, lest God's justice be endangered. The
manifestation of justice is the final cause of reprobation, but justice itself
is not the principle or cause from which reprobation formally takes its origin
without manifest prejudice to God's absolute Dominion, such as the potter has
over clay, Romans 9. For there is no rule determining the potter's will to make
from the same lump a vessel for honor and a dishonorable vessel; nor is there
any rule of justice for God by which he reprobates some and elects others. Let
them produce the rule by which they would absolve Jehovah from the charge of injustice in
the wholly gratuitous election of angels, without regard to their good works,
and in the reprobation of devils I have never been able to see. Nor do they
preserve justice by saying that man is considered by God as corrupt. I ask:
as actually existing or not? If as actually existing, then he is also burdened
with actual sins. But then both reprobation and election will be from foresight
of works, which is Pelagian. If as not yet existing, then he is not a sinner.
For actually to exist and be actually a sinner, just as simultaneously to
exist in the mind and be a sinner in mental consideration, is simply not to
exist and not to be a sinner.

[1651 logical printed p. 299 | IA leaf 317 | PDF p. 317]

That is to say, a mental notion preserves God's justice. But God does not
exercise justice without injustice unless a man exists as a sinner not only
in mental and objective consideration but actually and really. 2. It follows
that God's good pleasure alone is the cause why Adam's sin should be ours and
ours should be laid upon Christ. 3. It follows that the threat, “On the day
you eat, you shall die,” was not, in the divine intention, purely legal or
pronounced simply according to the order of justice, because very many have
not died for sin, but Christ died for them. Hence the covenant of works, which
was formed according to God's most wise will, with respect to the elect and
especially Adam himself—who did not fall from the grace of election by that
first transgression—included God's gracious evangelical will, if we regard
God's intention. Only with respect to the reprobate was it purely legal,
according to the strict justice by which God had determined to punish such
sinners. Thus if that threat is considered in common insofar as it concerned
the elect and reprobate indefinitely, it was pronounced from God's pure good
pleasure, not yet restricted to a definite and determined rule of justice. For
from God's absolute liberty, God legally executed that threat in some persons,
but evangelically in others, in Christ their surety. Thus if the hypothesis of
God's decree were set aside, mercy would not have been harmed if everyone had
paid in their own persons

[1651 logical printed p. 300 | IA leaf 318 | PDF p. 318]

the eternal punishments for that transgression, nor would justice have been
harmed if everyone had paid those punishments in Christ. 4. Hence the covenant
of works does not essentially demand personal passive obedience, as many
think. Rather, from his absolute good pleasure God so formed the covenant that
its transgression might be expiated indifferently either by personal suffering
or by surety-suffering, as it pleased God. Yet I do not mean here that
surety-obedience is not passive. I deny that the law as such essentially
demands the suffering of the transgressor himself. For if that covenant had
necessarily and from the order of justice demanded personal suffering, the
whole gospel, which presents to God's justice not personal but surety-suffering,
would plainly be contrary to God's justice, which is absurd. Thus Christ died
for us according to the covenant of works as well as the covenant of grace.
That he suffered belongs to justice, for God's mercy does not formally demand
death from anyone, much less from Christ, but justice does. That he suffered
for us belongs to mercy. These statements, therefore, must be carefully
distinguished here: “Christ suffered,” and, “Christ suffered for us.” Thus
Arminius has no grounds for saying that our predestination conflicts with
God's justice, which, it seems, does not allow God to will life for someone
unless Christ has first acquired it by his death. Rather, we say that God by
his absolute will willed life for the elect and that predestination is not an
act of justice

[1651 logical printed p. 301 | IA leaf 319 | PDF p. 319]

or mercy, but the freely willing absolute choice of God to manifest justice
and mercy. That great theologian would indeed philosophize splendidly who said
that the act of demonstrating divine omnipotence is formally an act of
omnipotence, and that the act of demonstrating justice is an act of justice!
5. Thus these statements differ greatly: “Man did not merit that God should
send a Savior into the world,” and, “Man merited that God should not send a
Savior.” The former is true and the latter false. For merit is what is owed by
justice, a promise, or a divine covenant. But if it had been owed by justice
that God should not send a Savior, then God would have been entirely prevented
by the debt of justice, and would plainly have violated justice in sending the
Savior. What could be more absurd? Nor is there any promise or covenant in
which God said or determined that he did not will to send a Savior. Hence
everyone sees that, because God sends the Savior from an absolute will, he
does not send him from natural love or mercy, and that God's will never was,
nor had justice established, that the violated law could be expiated only by
the personal punishment of sinners. Rather, the covenant of works was only a
decree concerning punishing sinners in whatever manner seemed good to God,
whether by personal or surety-punishment.

6. Durand therefore rightly says: “It is clear, then, that justice in God,
both communicative and distributive, is nothing other than”

[Marginalia: Durand, book 4, distinction 46, question 1, § 9.]

[1651 logical printed p. 302 | IA leaf 320 | PDF p. 320]

“divine fittingness in rendering rewards and punishments and in distributing properties and
principles of action as befits the substance of each thing.” And rightly so,
for there is properly no commutative justice in God, since there is no
proportion between God's benefits conferred and our services accepted by him.
For, as Durand notes there, a son can never compensate his fleshly father,
from whom he has his existence and nourishment, although improperly he
compensates him as far as he can. Nor can there properly be distributive
justice in God, by which each receives according to the dignity of persons.
For all human dignity here is from the gracious esteem of God who freely
accepts. The dignity of a created person before God is not intrinsic.
Otherwise God would be unjust unless he acted according to the person's
intrinsic dignity, and he would be a debtor to his creature not only from a
free covenant and decree but even prior to God's decree, contrary to Romans
11: “Who has first given to him, that it should be repaid to him?” I therefore
believe that the sounder and more profound Jesuits cannot, on their own
principles, avoid denying every properly so-called merit of good works. I only
wish they had delivered the doctrine of merits to us more plainly. Suárez
reasons rightly in the same way concerning goodness, by which he maintains
that creatures are fitting and good for God as works becoming him and agreeable
to his goodness and wisdom. Certainly, by the same reasoning, God will be good
toward creatures not essentially, because then by necessity of nature

[Marginalia: *Metaphysics*, vol. 1, disputation 10, *On Goodness*, section 3,
figure 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 303 | IA leaf 321 | PDF p. 321]

God would necessarily have to be good to creatures and communicate goodness to
them, and thus would
necessarily have created the world, which is absurd. Nor is there any doubt
that if creatures had possessed a suitability to God's fittingness very
different from the one they have, they would have been no less good than they
now are. Therefore God's goodness toward creatures depends upon his wise good
pleasure.

7. God's will is not simply the rule of justice as distinguished from wisdom,
but insofar as it is determined by God's wise intellect, as Aquinas and Henry
teach.

[Marginalia: Aquinas, part 1, question 21, article 2, reply 1; Henry,
*Quodlibet* 8, question 1.]

2. God's will is not the internal and essential cause of things. Nor does it
arise from God's decreeing will that man is an animal or that obedience is
conformity to the divine law. For essence and the things belonging to essences
cohere with one another necessarily, not from a free decree. But man would not
be man—which is absurd—if he were not an animal. 3. Anything whatever is
considered either materially, denominatively, or according to possible being
(for these are the same here), or formally and according to actual being. In
the former way God's good pleasure, whose office it is to fashion and
constitute the essences of things and the degrees of essences, is the cause
why such a thing has such an essence and degrees of being such as five, six,
or seven, neither more nor fewer. It is likewise the cause why the sacrifice
of Isaac is obedience; why homicide and eating from the tree of knowledge are
sin, but eating from the tree of life is not sin; and why a thing that will be
a man—that is, why a man—has such an essence from God's

[1651 logical printed p. 304 | IA leaf 322 | PDF p. 322]

free decree. But after a thing formally exists, it has such an essence not
from God's good pleasure but essentially. After God has willed this to be the
nature of sin—namely, that it is what displeases God's approving will—then, if
one asks why God wills not to have sin, the answer is not because he so wills,
but because it is essentially evil. But if one further asks why the nature of
that thing which is sin, and of that thing which is man, is such, one must
always return to the highest source, God's eternal good pleasure. Some things
are good and just prior to God's decree, but how? Possibly, that is: they can
be made good and just if God should will it. But nothing is actually good and
according to moral obligation, so that it ought to be done, except because God
willed and decreed that it should be done. These statements, therefore, are
not to be admitted without distinction: 1. God cannot fail to will the good.
2. God's will cannot fail to will what his wisdom declares to be good. There
are two things in the divine volition of a good: 1. the thing willed; and 2.
the manner of willing. With respect to the thing willed, it is false that God
cannot fail to will a good, including one which reason declares to him to be
good. For reason and infinite wisdom declared the creation of the world and
Christ's mission in the flesh to be good, yet God could have willed not to
create the world or send the Savior. But with respect to the manner of willing
and the necessity of specification, whatever God wills,

[1651 logical printed p. 305 | IA leaf 323 | PDF p. 323]

he must will wisely, and wisdom must declare that it is to be willed. “A preponderant reason,” says
Bradwardine, “is one which declares that it is better to do this than that, or
to do what is better rather than omit it. Such a reason neither moves nor
constrains the divine will, nor determines it to act, for God could do better
things than he does and many good things which he does not do.” That man of
most acute intellect sees this, and not without reason. For in this matter a
preponderant reason is twofold: either a preponderant reason derived from the
created things themselves or a preponderant reason with respect to divine
wisdom. These two preponderant reasons are often opposed. A better world and
creatures more excellent than those which exist are in themselves more
excellent than this world and the things now created. But according to God's
wisdom it seemed better that the world should be less excellent. Obedience in
itself is better than disobedience if we regard the preponderant reason derived
from the things themselves. But according to the preponderant reason of divine
wisdom it seemed better to God that disobedience should exist as well as
obedience.
