THEODORE BEZA’S RESPONSE TO THE THESES ON PREDESTINATION

Antithesis I, Theodore Beza

We affirm generally that our first parents did not fall with God unwilling, ignorant, or idly foreseeing, and that nothing in the world has happened, happens, or ever would have happened in that manner; not even sparrows are excepted from his will (Matthew 10:29).

ANNOTATIONS OF JAKOB ANDREAE

1. That our first parents sinned with God willing—that is, that God willed them to transgress the command given by God in paradise concerning the fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil—the Holy Spirit nowhere teaches this in the whole of Holy Scripture.

Theodore Beza’s response to Dr. Jakob Andreae’s annotation

God’s foreknowledge—even in Adam’s fall—must not be separated from God’s will and eternal decree.

In this first thesis Dr. Andreae follows the opinion of those who make foreknowledge common to the elect and reprobate but consider God’s purpose and decree only in the elect, fearing that otherwise God may be judged the author of evil and those who sin may be able to excuse themselves. He acknowledges that the fall of Adam and of all people in Adam was foreknown, but by no means that it was decreed and ordained. Yet what is feebler, and even more unworthy, than that separation of divine foreknowledge and counsel? Nothing future can be truly foreknown unless it will truly be; and scarcely anything more impious can be said of God than that something will be future without his having decreed it future, or that what he decreed would be future will not be future. Again, nothing more absurd can be said of God than that what he does not will to happen will happen, or that what he wills to happen will not happen. The matter itself cries out that, just as counsel can in no way be torn from the divine will, so foreknowledge cannot be torn from predestination. And since both good and evil things exist in the nature of things, and both evil and good events occur, these three must necessarily be joined in both: God’s eternal foreknowledge with his immutable will and decree.

How it by no means follows from this either that God is the author of sins or that sinners can cast the blame upon God we will say in its place. For now our adversary compels us to discuss Adam’s fall. “For nowhere,” he says, “does the Holy Spirit teach in the whole of sacred Scripture that God willed our first parents to transgress that command not to touch the fruit of that tree.” I answer first concerning the verb *to will*, although because of its ambiguity I abstained from it in my antithesis. Sometimes it is said only of those things which God simply approves, as things good in their own nature and agreeable to God’s nature. In this sense he is said to will good things alone—

[Part II | PDF page 155 | logical source scan 154 | printed page 153]

—but not to will evil things: that is, to delight in the good things he approves and to be offended by the evils displeasing to him. At other times it is taken more broadly, generally for *willing*, and therefore for decreeing and determining that whatever will be future should happen. In this sense he also wills certain things—that is, decrees and ordains them as future—which he nevertheless does not will, that is, does not approve in themselves. Nor does he properly decree them insofar as they are evil, but insofar as, in Augustine’s elegant words, “although things that are evil are not good insofar as they are evil, nevertheless it is good that not only good things but also evil things should exist; so that, in a wonderful and ineffable manner, what occurs even against his will does not occur apart from his will”—that is, his decree—“because it would not happen if he did not permit it; nor does he permit it unwilling, but willing.” By these last words of Augustine the opinion of those who separate permission from God’s purpose and definite counsel is refuted.

I am not unaware how cautiously Augustine had to speak about this question, which was not yet much agitated before the Pelagians; what Prosper writes about it; or what Fulgentius, following Prosper, writes to Monimus. Nor am I unaware how reverently one must speak—much less make determinations—about so grave a matter. But I think no one speaks more reverently than those who, following the Apostle’s footsteps, adore what surpasses our understanding. Those certainly do not do this who, in order to escape inferences dictated by human sense, suffer shipwreck upon the very rocks they sought to avoid, as we will declare in its place.

I ask only whether Adam’s sin in eating the fruit of the forbidden tree was graver than the sin of Herod, Pilate, and the rest, by whom the Lord was accused and condemned against conscience. Theirs certainly was, in my judgment. But let us grant that it was lighter. Nevertheless the Holy Spirit, through the apostles’ mouth, says that they did what God’s hand had predestined to be done. I now repeat what I said cannot be denied without impiety: our first parents fell neither with God unwilling or ignorant nor with him idly foreseeing. I demonstrate it first by arguments from the true and natural knowledge of God, which Paul honors with the name “truth” in Romans 1:18; next by Scripture’s testimonies; and finally from the agreement of Augustine—or rather of the orthodox church speaking through Augustine’s mouth.

Whoever attributes either ignorance or weakness to God certainly denies that God exists, or substitutes an idol for God. To take away from God the government of the things he created—whether highest, intermediate, or lowest—and a government which is sovereign, is nothing other than, as Cicero himself testifies against Epicurus, to confess God in word but deny him in fact. But if something comes into existence without God’s knowledge, or happens to any created thing, and likewise if anything occurs against his will, this God will be such that ignorance falls upon him in the former case and weakness in the latter. If someone objects that certain things happen against God’s will in the sense that he does not approve them—not because he cannot prevent them but because he does not will to prevent them—I answer that this is the same as saying they happen with him willing. For things which he certainly could prevent if he willed—

[Part II | PDF page 156 | logical source scan 155 | printed page 154]

—must happen because by not resisting he wills them to happen. It follows that this objection does not remove but establishes what I affirmed in my antithesis. The third notion, that God’s providence is idle, is so impious and Epicurean that it needs no refutation. If any event ever possessed such importance that in it God must be considered by us as all-knowing, omnipotent, and governing all things, it is the matter we are treating, since he founded the whole universe for the sake of man—not as its last and principal cause, but as a proximate cause, and one of the greatest importance. Therefore so great a change in man, and through man’s fall a transformation of all created things, could not occur with God ignorant or unwilling or as though an idle spectator.

As for Scripture’s testimonies, Dr. Andreae is gravely mistaken if he thinks nothing can be proved from Scripture unless it is distinctly and individually written there. Teaching must therefore be distinguished from examples, which must be judged from its prescription when the question is whether to approve and imitate them or to disapprove and avoid them. Thus, for example, neither Noah’s nor Lot’s drunkenness, the latter’s incest, Isaac’s lie in Gerar, Judah’s malice and fornication, the fathers’ polygamy, nor almost countless similar things are found expressly condemned. Yet from the rule of the dishonorable and honorable—both that which remained imprinted upon human hearts and that which God declared in his divinely revealed law—they are understood in themselves to have been divinely condemned.

The same must be determined concerning the knowledge of God himself as he reveals himself to us in his Word, so that from it we may judge his works. I therefore say that every passage of Scripture affirming that rashness does not fall upon God, that God is omnipotent, that he not only made all things but also rules them—especially human beings—and that he never changes, says just as often that Adam fell not only with God foreknowing but with God ordaining it in this manner. If before making man he did not deliberate to what end he would establish man, he acted rashly and imprudently. But if he decreed something other than what happened, either he was not omnipotent, since Satan shattered his counsels, or he was inconstant and changed his counsel.

If you object that a condition was attached to God’s counsel—if Adam remained in integrity—although I do not deny that, I will nevertheless ask whether the foundation of the divine counsel depended upon Adam. If Dr. Andreae dares affirm it, judgment, as Augustine elegantly says, will have rested not with the potter but with the clay. And what kind of God will this be, who makes the foundation of his counsels depend upon the power of a created thing? If these things do not suffice, Scripture cries aloud that not even sparrows fall to the ground without the heavenly Father’s will and that all the hairs of our head are numbered. Does it not testify plainly enough that our first parent did not fall without the Father’s will—unless perhaps sparrows or our hairs are of more value to him than the whole—

—human race? And if Satan could attack neither Job nor even the pigs unless the Lord granted it to him, will he have overthrown the whole human race in one man with God ignorant, unwilling, or unconcerned? And why should Paul’s statement in Ephesians 1:11, that God does all things according to the counsel of his will, not apply especially here? Surely we must confess that God has always been the same.

But Dr. Andreae requires something more explicit. When the same Apostle therefore testifies that God, like a potter, made from the same mass some vessels for honor and some for dishonor, how can this statement be truly and suitably explained without ascending all the way to Adam’s creation? For the name *mass* suits the human race not as already established but as to be established; much less should the corrupt human race be understood by the term *mass*. For if that were so, the Apostle would by no means have said that God makes vessels of wrath from that mass, but that he leaves those which were already vessels of wrath in that miserable mass; nor would the Apostle have had to labor to assert God’s justice in the destruction of the reprobate.

God is therefore compared there to a potter. If a potter is granted the right to assign vessels at his own discretion for both honorable and dishonorable uses, and no injury is therefore thought to be done to the clay, was it not much more lawful for God to dispose in this way the clay from which he would establish the human race in one man, so that from it he might bring forth some upon whom he would exercise his just wrath, and others whom he would bless through his remarkable goodness? But how did he establish them? Certainly in Adam, to whose creation the Apostle undoubtedly looked when he mentions the potter and clay. How, then, in Adam? Certainly only through the intervention of Adam’s voluntary fall. For unless Adam fell of his own accord and—although not apart from God’s ordination—nevertheless without any fault in God, no place would have remained for either mercy or severity, and consequently God’s counsel would have been void.

Finally, when Paul says in Galatians 3:22 that God enclosed all things under sin so that the promise from faith in Jesus Christ might be given to believers, does he not manifestly ascend all the way to the source of stubbornness itself, that is, to Adam’s fall, so that the distinction of vessels begins from there? How, nevertheless, the whole blame for this fall resides entirely in the will of man sinning voluntarily, which is excused neither by the will of God, who wills what he permits and not only what he himself does, nor by the necessity that God’s will be fulfilled, I will declare below.

Now let us hear Augustine speaking in *On Rebuke and Grace*: “We most wholesomely confess what we most rightly believe: that God, the Lord of all things, who created all things very good, foreknew that evils would arise from good things”—and Augustine never separates foreknowledge from God’s purpose—“and knew that it pertained more to his almighty goodness to do good even from evils than not to permit evils to exist; and so ordained the life of angels and human beings that in it he would first show what free choice could do, and then what the benefit of his grace and the judgment of his justice—”

“—could do.” The same author says in *Enchiridion to Laurence*, chapter 100: “These are the Lord’s great works, sought out according to all his wills: when the angelic and human creature had sinned—that is, had done not what he but what it itself willed—even through that same creature’s will by which what the Creator did not will was done, he fulfilled what he did will. Being supremely good, he used even evils well for the condemnation of those whom he justly predestined to punishment and for the salvation of those whom he graciously predestined to grace. For so far as concerned them, they did what God did not will; but so far as concerned God’s omnipotence, they were by no means able to accomplish it. For by the very fact that they acted against God’s will, God’s will was done upon them. Therefore the Lord’s every work is sought out according to all his wills, so that in a wonderful and ineffable manner what is done even against his will is not done apart from his will, because it would not be done if he did not permit it; nor does he permit it unwilling.” I explained a little earlier the sense in which those first human beings are said, in sinning, to have done not what God willed but what they themselves willed.
