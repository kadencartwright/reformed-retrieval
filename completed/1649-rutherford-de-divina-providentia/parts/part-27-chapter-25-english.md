CHAPTER XXV

The position of Durandus and Lodovicus à Dola, who deny GOD’s immediate
concurrence with all secondary causes, and, second, of those who maintain that
GOD’s general concurrence is determinable by the secondary cause, is refuted.

I.

The nineteenth argument is this. Whoever concurs with a failing will that
produces the entitative act of sin, with which malice is inseparably connected,
and does so by a truly causal action, cannot but will that this malice exist
while he permits it. But GOD concurs in such a manner. Therefore GOD cannot but
will that the malice exist while he permits it. The major premise is certain,
because if GOD altogether willed that sin not occur, he would effectually
prevent its existence and would not, by any causality, bring about its material
foundation. The minor premise is the adversaries’ confession. There are two
answers to this argument. The first is that of Durandus and Lodovicus à Dola,
of the Franciscan order of Capuchins. À Dola says that *“for GOD to concur is
either to create free choice, as in natural acts, or, in supernatural acts, to
give a power or habit of acting or not acting, through which the will itself
does the whole in every supernatural act, without any other immediate
concurrence of GOD actually advancing the action.”*

[Marginal argument XIX.]

2. But this empty opinion conflicts with the sacred Oracles. Job 26:13: *“By
his hand acting as a midwife the long serpent was formed.”* Job 36:27: *“For he
draws off the drops of water which poured out rain for calamity.”* Verse 32:
*“He covers the light with his hands and gives command to it.”* Chapter 37:5:
*“He says to the snow, ‘Be on the earth.’”* Job 9:7: *“He forbids the sun and it
does not rise; he seals up the way before the stars.”* When GOD’s active command
forbids, not even those things which act by nature’s impulse perform any
actions. But if the sun rises or does not rise at GOD’s nod and command, GOD
concurs in the rising of the sun—and likewise of the stars, whose way he seals
up so that they do not shine—with a particular command. This is something other
than the bare power of rising and shining which is present in the sun when it
does not rise

[Marginal note: GOD’s immediate concurrence in the operations of all secondary
causes is proved against Lodovicus à Dola.]

[Chapter XXV; printed page 372; IA PDF page 431; BSB canvas 424]

and in the stars when they do not twinkle for us during the day. Raynaud presses
this text, Psalm 139:15: *“My strength was not hidden from you, and רֻקַּמְתִּי
בְּתַחְתִּיּוֹת אָרֶץ—I was embroidered with a needle in the lower parts of the
earth.”* Here David proves GOD’s inscrutable knowledge from the artful and
wonderful formation of his body in his mother’s womb. This argument would prove
that David’s father—who was truly an efficient cause in that formation by
imparting the generative power along with the seed—possessed such knowledge,
unless GOD acted here by a particular, immediate action of his concurrence.
Matthew 6:30: *“He clothes the grain of the field.”* 1 Corinthians 15:38: *“GOD
gives it a body as he wills.”* Bodies would be formed, not as GOD wills, but as
nature wills, unless a particular action of GOD intervened in forming bodies.
John 5:17: *“My Father works until now, and I work.”* This reasoning would have
no force unless GOD acted daily in creatures by an action as particular as that
by which CHRIST healed the lame man on the Sabbath. But the action of GOD by
which, at creation, he implanted in every creature the power to act is not such
a daily action of divine care as that healing by CHRIST was. Philippians 2:13:
*“For it is GOD who brings about in you both to will and to accomplish.”* But if
GOD did not act here by an immediate action, he would no more bring about
willing in the Saints than not willing, and accomplishing good than not
accomplishing it. Proverbs 21:1: *“The king’s heart is in the hand of the LORD;
he inclines it wherever he wills.”* Yet no habits of grace predetermine the will
to one of two opposites. Therefore, if GOD acted with the will merely by giving
it a habit or a power of acting, GOD would never incline the will where he
wills. Rather, the will itself would incline itself wherever it wills. Nor, by
the mere communication of a power or habit—which always leaves the will to its
own indifferent power of not willing, willing, acting, and not acting—would GOD
have any dominion over the acts of the will or be able effectually to bring
about the will’s assent rather than its dissent. Hebrews 13:21: *“May the GOD of
peace fit you for every good work”*; that is, as Cumel rightly says, may he apply
and move you to doing it. 2. GOD predetermines the will to all acts both of
nature and of grace, as I proved elsewhere in my *Apologetic Exercise for
Divine Grace*, exercise 3, section 1. Therefore he furnishes immediate
concurrence to every action. 3. There would be no reason for us to pray that GOD
would bend our heart to actual obedience, for GOD could not accomplish this
effectually if he gives the will

[Marginal reference: Cumel, volume 2, *On Grace and Free Choice*, discourse 5,
section 2, page 142.]

only the power of bending itself or not bending itself. 4. The faith by which we
hope that GOD, according to his grace, will grant us actually to cling to him
and to persevere in grace would perish. 5. We would believe in vain that GOD
would procure favor with enemies and turn their wills to actual benevolence
toward us. 6. No account could be sought from GOD’s providence of why the race
does not belong to the swift and victory to the strong. For GOD gave both the
power to run and to conquer; on this opinion, the race and the palm and glory of
victory would come from themselves. Solomon openly denies this in Ecclesiastes
9:13. 7. Suppose GOD is the cause of generations, snow, rain, winds, and all
natural and free effects merely because he gave nature and the will the power
to act—and it is certain that the sacred Oracles ascribe all positive effects
to GOD as their cause. GOD would then be the cause of Adam’s sin in exactly the
same manner in which he is the cause of begetting children, because he gave Adam
the power to sin, namely free choice. Lodovicus à Dola answers: *“The reasoning
is not parallel, because GOD gives the power to beget with the intention that
generations occur, but he did not give free choice with the intention that Adam
sin.”*

[Marginal reference: Lodovicus à Dola, *On Concurrence*, part 4, chapter 17,
section 6.]

I answer: 1. Either GOD intends lustful generations polluted by adultery and
incest, in which case Lodovicus says nothing relevant; or he intends the
generations but not the disorders attached to them as such, in which case he
returns to our position. To what purpose, then, is this novel and profane
opinion? 2. GOD’s bare intention, distinguished from Omnipotence, which applies
causes to acting, contributes no causality to producing an effect. Otherwise,
because the intention and volition by which GOD wills things to occur existed
from eternity, all the things willed would also have actually existed from
eternity. Therefore it follows soundly from this answer that GOD contributes as
much physical, though not moral, causality to the production of sin as he
contributes to the production of all positive effects. Consequently sins ought
to be ascribed to GOD as a physical cause just as snow, hail, rains, summer, and
winter are customarily ascribed to GOD in the Scriptures. Lodovicus falls into
Scylla while trying to avoid Charybdis. 8. Hurtado de Mendoza argues in this
way: *“GOD could not provide for all things, especially by miraculous
providence, unless things, and therefore actions, depended immediately upon
him; for without a physical action GOD could neither restrain the actions of a
secondary cause nor command them.”*

[Marginal reference: Hurtado de Mendoza, *Physical Disputations*, disputation
10, section 2, figures 12, 13, 14.]

Nor is the command of the divine will sufficient by which all actions exist or
do not exist according to GOD’s pleasure. First, if that command moved Peter to
will well, and Peter, though moved in this way, willed badly, GOD’s command
would be violated. 2. It is just as contrary to GOD’s holiness to exercise this
command for producing sins as to furnish his immediate concurrence to the
entitative act of sin. But Lodovicus says: *“Even though my action,”* he says,
*“is not also the proximate and immediate action of GOD, nevertheless I do not
act unless GOD immediately and directly wills or at least permits it and
furnishes the power to act.”* But this is trifling. For if GOD immediately wills
the entitative act, why does this innovator deny that GOD immediately concurs
in that act? Finally, this opinion is Pelagian. In Jerome, *Critobolus denies
that GOD’s help is needed for writing, trimming a pen, speaking, being silent,*
and so forth. Nor do Lodovicus and Vásquez answer correctly here that *“Jerome
speaks of these acts insofar as they are referred to GOD’s glory.”* For in that
case Atticus says nothing relevant in answer to Critobolus, who raised the
question about actions considered according to their natural being. But the
arguments against general concurrence also refute this opinion. Therefore I
will spend no more time refuting this empty position.

3. There is another answer, or rather the celebrated opinion of the Jesuits and
Arminians. It says that GOD’s concurring with a sinning creature in the
entitative act of sin does not mean in any way that he wills sin to occur,
because GOD’s own *“concurrence is of itself general and indifferent and is
determined by the concurrence of the secondary cause.”* So say Molina,
Bellarmine, Gregory of Valencia, and Vásquez. Jacob Arminius likewise argues as
follows: *“That the act exists depends upon the will’s freedom with respect to
exercise; that it has this essence rather than that one—or that this act exists
rather than another, a forbidden rather than an unforbidden act, against this
precept rather than that one—depends upon freedom with respect to the species
of the act.”* So also Grevinchovius and Corvinus, although Molina says that
*“GOD intends only to produce the being of the effect, while the will produces
its being of this or that sort, good or evil.”* Suarez and Bellarmine, however,
say that *“GOD intends the goodness of the act.”* In his *Against Perkins*,
Arminius says that *“this indifferent concurrence of GOD contributes nothing to
the created will by which it is inclined, helped, or strengthened for acting,
and that the created will, before it acts, has GOD’s concurrence within its own
power, ready and laid open to it.”*

than an act of stubbornness and impenitence by which someone stubbornly resists
GOD’s call. Consequently, the Jesuits and Arminians imagine GOD, when graciously
concurring in acts of conversion, faith, hope, and so forth, to be an idle,
cold, and ineffectual agent—indeed, a non-agent.

4. Against this fictitious and impious concurrence I argue first as follows. 1.
No concurrence should be imagined in GOD which posits imperfection in GOD’s
will. But this concurrence does so. Therefore such a concurrence should not be
imagined in GOD. The minor premise is proved. GOD would have to say: *“I decree
that I will furnish creatures with general concurrence, but I have not yet
determined what sort it will be specifically, until I consult the created
will.”* 2. GOD says, *“I must investigate the created will so that I may know
whether my concurrence is to be identified with volition or with non-volition,
with an act of love for GOD or with an act of hatred for GOD.”* For GOD must
know this before he actually concurs if he is to know his own concurrence
properly. 3. GOD says: *“Now that the determination of the created will is
foreknown, and now that the fulfilled condition under which I concur with the
will is foreknown—namely, that the will is now placed in actual influx toward
this side of the opposites—I now determine absolutely, rather than
conditionally as in the prior moment, to furnish my concurrence.”* Here are
many things utterly unworthy of GOD’s will. 1. Paul says from Isaiah: *“Who has
been GOD’s counselor?”* Yet in this case GOD can no more determinately decree or
know anything about his determinate concurrence until he consults the
determining nod of the created will than a man hesitating at a fork in the road
can establish anything certain until a new light shines upon him. 2. GOD
proceeds in a plainly imperfect manner from genus to species, from concurrence
in general to this determinate concurrence. 3. GOD decrees all human acts
twice: first confusedly and in general, then specifically. 4. If GOD were
consulted in the first logical moment about the futurity of free acts, he would
answer, “I do not know until I consult the created will.” 5. GOD sees the
futurity of free acts not in himself but in creatures. 6. Thus GOD’s eternal
counsel would properly be determined by the human will. Besides taking away
GOD’s freedom in decreeing—for they teach that an agent which is determined by
something other than itself is not free—this entails GOD’s shameful dependence
upon his creature’s-

[Marginal references: Romans 11:34; Isaiah 40:13.]

[Chapter XXV; printed page 376; IA PDF page 435; BSB canvas 428]

-slippery nod. Bellarmine and Arminius do indeed say that *“GOD does not depend
here upon the
created will, but upon his own free decree by which he determined to concur with
free causes.”* But no one is so dull that he does not see that the decree by
which GOD determined to furnish this determinate concurrence depends altogether
upon the nod of the created will. It lies within that will’s power whether any
such decree exists in GOD at all—for if the will, as it can, never acted, there
would never have been such a decree in GOD—or whether there exists in GOD a
decree concerning concurrence identified with this free volition rather than a
decree concerning some other concurrence identified with a free non-volition
or another impious volition. Someone sells himself to a Tyrant to torture the
guilty, grind, dig, and so forth. Although he does this freely, once he has sold
himself, he is compelled to serve the Tyrant’s pleasure by grinding, digging,
or doing anything else night and day. So perhaps GOD freely made a decree to
furnish his concurrence with free causes. But once this decree is posited, GOD
is necessitated to make another, servile and dependent decree, by which he
determines—dependently upon the nod of the created will—to concur or not to
concur.

Argument II. This fictitious concurrence removes the necessity of prayer.
Therefore it must be rejected. The antecedent is proved. It is futile to pray
that GOD would reconcile Joseph’s favor to the Patriarchs and Ahasuerus’s favor
to Esther. For it does not come from GOD moving the heart by a concurrence
determinable and indifferent toward acts either of favor or of hatred that
Joseph smiles graciously upon the men rather than burning with hatred. Rather,
it comes from the will of Joseph and Ahasuerus determining GOD’s concurrence
toward an act of favor rather than an act of hatred. Prayers, therefore, must
be addressed either to fortune or to the men themselves, in whose power it
absolutely lies to hold us dear, but in no way to GOD. For GOD can neither
kindle men’s hatred nor procure their favor except as it pleases the men who
determine GOD’s own motion. Indeed, in this case the Saints would pray, as the
most distinguished and learned Antonius Walaeus observes, for GOD’s general
influx. Nothing could be more barren than such prayers.

Argument III. If this concurrence stood, GOD would promise in vain in Isaiah 44
the liberation of his people through Cyrus, in Jeremiah 50 the devastation of
the Chaldeans through the Medes, and the conversion of the Gentiles through the
free ministry of the Apostles and Pastors. For it does not depend upon GOD, but

[Chapter XXV; printed page 377; IA PDF page 436; BSB canvas 429]

upon free agents determining GOD’s will and his general concurrence, which is
of itself indifferent toward the people’s liberation or non-liberation, whether
liberation rather than non-liberation and the devastation rather than the
non-devastation of the Chaldeans exists. For in this way GOD could not
effectually fulfill his promises, and GOD would not be truthful either in
promising or in threatening things that occur through the ministry of free
agents, unless he complied with men. But GOD is truthful, though all mortals are
liars.

Argument IV. If general concurrence stands, we give thanks to GOD in vain for
benefits procured through free agents. In vain we trust in GOD, hope, fear, and
patiently submit our wills to him so that the good which came or will come to us
through the ministry of free agents may occur, that the contrary evil may be
turned away, or that it may be borne with a calm and patient spirit. For our
confidence, hope, fear, and calm submission of spirit would be owed not to GOD,
through whose concurrence these things effectually occur for us, but to the
free causes which determine GOD’s efficacy in them. For when GOD concurs, these
good or evil things just as much fail to occur as occur.

Argument V. Suppose all effects occur through such an indifferent concurrence
of GOD, through which they just as much fail to occur as occur and contrary
effects just as much occur as these. It then follows that the abundance or
scarcity of crops, sheep, lions, and fish, and also of gold, silver, and riches,
which come to men through free acts of trading, are not from GOD. For, 1. if it
does not come from GOD’s indifferent concurrence, but from free causes which
determine that concurrence, that the effects are of such a kind, then it will
not come from GOD’s concurrence that the multitude of effects is of such a kind
and such a number. The number of free actions will not lie within GOD’s power.
2. If the determination of the divine concurrence comes from the secondary
cause and not from GOD, neither will the numerical determination of the effects
come from GOD, but from a free cause. For it does not lie with GOD that there
are so many free acts of trading or so many successful acts of trading, because
the success of a commercial transaction is a qualification and determination
of the transaction. But no determination of an effect comes from an indifferent
and determinable concurrence. Hence what Scripture says, that *“wealth and
poverty are from GOD,”* would be false. Deuteronomy 15:5; 28:4, 17, 18; 1 Samuel
2:7.

Argument VI. If GOD’s concurrence is altogether indifferent, in many ways

GOD’s particular providence is overturned. 1. *“The disposition of lots is not
from GOD.”* For when GOD’s concurrence is entirely indifferent toward this lot or
that one, the drawing of this lot rather than that does not come from it, but
either from fortune or from the hand which takes up the lot. 2. Nor is it from
GOD that a sparrow falls to the earth, because flight rather than non-flight is
not determined by GOD. 3. *“When everyone seeks the face of a judge, each one’s
sentence is nevertheless not from the Lord.”* For when GOD concurs with a judge
who pronounces a judicial sentence, he is indifferent toward a just and an
unjust sentence. 4. Nor is it from GOD that a man is killed by the axe of a man
cutting wood who intends nothing of the kind. For GOD’s concurrence with the
woodcutter’s hand and the axe’s motion is as indifferent toward the traveler’s
not being killed as toward his being killed. 5. All the following statements
would be false: in Job 37, that *“GOD gives snow, showers, rains, ice, clouds,
and winds”*; that he *“gives food to ravens, wings to the stork, strength to the
horse”*; and that he *“gives food to the hungry, frees prisoners, raises the
bent down, opens the eyes of the blind, preserves strangers, supports orphans
and widows, consigns to death, brings out of the grave, raises beggars to a
throne, and casts Kings down from their seat.”* All these are determinate
effects, yet on this Epicurean opinion they cannot be attributed to GOD. 1. For
these effects can no more be attributed to GOD, who concurs by a concurrence
indifferent toward these effects or their non-effects, than can their
contraries—or, indeed, the complete absence of all such effects. 2. These are
determinate effects, but a determinate effect as such does not come from GOD’s
indeterminate and indifferent concurrence. Bellarmine answers that *“the whole
human action is entirely from GOD and has from him not only its generic being,
but also its specific and singular being. Yet it has this through GOD’s
concurrence as determined to such an action by the human will, or the creature;
and therefore it does not have its specific being from GOD’s mode of acting,
but from the mode of acting of the human will, or creature.”*

watchful care.]

[Marginal references: Psalms 104; 146:7; 1 Samuel 2:7–9; Deuteronomy 32:39.]

I answer: 1. GOD, then, does not act in such a way that there are such
determinate acts and effects, but commits to the choice of the creature and the
secondary cause whether there are snow, rain showers, the feeding of ravens,
food for the hungry, release of prisoners, death, life, poverty, riches, honor,
and disgrace. For grant that GOD’s concurrence determines these effects
specifically. Nevertheless, because the action of the secondary cause
determines GOD’s concurrence, which is indifferent toward

[Chapter XXV; printed page 379; IA PDF page 438; BSB canvas 431]

these effects or their non-effects, it follows from first to last that poverty
rather than wealth, death rather than life, and disgrace rather than honor come
from the secondary cause. Therefore, if the secondary cause determines every
effect specifically by the help of the divine concurrence, which it also
determines because that concurrence is indifferent, it follows that determinate
effects as such come from the secondary cause alone, not from GOD. O providence
destitute and empty of all faith, hope, love, and consolation! 2. *“If specific
and singular being comes from GOD’s concurrence as determined by the action of
the secondary cause,”* then an action insofar as it is evil has its specific
being from GOD’s concurrence, which the Jesuit strives to avoid; or an action
insofar as it is good does not have its specific being from GOD’s concurrence,
which is absurd. 3. GOD’s concurrence is the action of the first cause and is
more principal than that of the secondary cause. Therefore specific being will
come prior and more principally from GOD than from the secondary cause. But
this is contrary to a determinable concurrence. And an action’s evil being will
come prior and more principally from GOD than from the creature which goes
astray.

Argument VII. No concurrence should be imagined in GOD which releases free acts
from GOD’s Dominion, necessitates GOD to act, and makes it impossible for him
not to act. But GOD’s indifferent concurrence does this. Therefore such a
concurrence should not be imagined. The major premise is certain. The Minor is
proved in parts. If by his concurrence GOD produces an act no more than a
non-act, then he does not have, through his concurrence and consequently
through no other act of causing, the act rather than the non-act within his
power. This concurrence therefore overturns GOD’s Dominion. 2. If the secondary
cause acts, GOD must co-act and concur, because the secondary cause’s action is
impossible without the first cause’s action. By the same reasoning, GOD is
rendered unable not to concur if the secondary cause has first acted in the
order of nature. If someone says that *“GOD is not necessitated to concur with
the will,”* I then say with Bradwardine: *“GOD can freely prevent the will from
acting. Therefore he freely wills not to prevent it, because by proposition 22
of book 1 GOD has some act of will concerning not preventing and
non-volition—for by proposition 10 of book 1 the non-prevention would not then
exist, and consequently the prevention would exist.”* But it is unthinkably
impossible and contradictory that GOD should prevent a secondary cause which
has already acted before GOD concurs, if it acts freely and is not annihilated.
I add: 1. If GOD’s concurrence is within my power, then GOD’s

Argument VIII. This indeterminate concurrence makes GOD wait for the cooperation
of the created will before GOD furnishes his determinate concurrence. Therefore
such a concurrence is empty. The reason for the consequence is that GOD cannot
wait for what is impossible. For the created will lacks a co-principle
essentially required for bringing about its cooperation: namely, it lacks GOD’s
determinate concurrence, which is essentially required for this cooperation,
because the concurrence is still indifferent. This is just as if a judge waited
for a bound prisoner freely to walk through the fields before he arranged
either for him to be released from his chains or for the doors to be opened.

Argument IX. Every determining cause, because it is more actual than what is
determinable, is also more perfect. Therefore, if GOD’s concurrence is
determined by the secondary cause, GOD will be a less perfect cause than the
secondary cause. So say Bonaventure and Báñez. Grevinchovius answers that *“this
is not universally true, because the visible species is less noble than the eye
and determines it to seeing.”* I answer that the species determines the eye to
seeing only instrumentally, but the soul accomplishes this principally.

Argument X. If GOD concurs in every good, evil, natural, and supernatural act by
an indifferent concurrence, then by his concurrence GOD would no more cause
good than evil acts, and supernatural than natural acts. For his concurrence is
equally indifferent toward all these acts. But it is absurd that GOD should be
the cause of good and evil things in no different manner. For it takes away the
glory of actual grace, weakens zeal for prayer, and offers man material for
empty boasting. They give two answers here. 1. Bellarmine and Suarez say that
*“this does not follow, because although GOD’s concurrence is equal and
identical,*

*GOD nevertheless commands and persuades good actions, intends good actions,
and neither commands nor intends evil ones.”* I answer that GOD’s command and
persuasion are GOD’s moral actions. But it still follows that in the order of a
Physical cause, though not in the order of a moral cause, GOD when concurring
exercises no different or greater causality in acts of faith, hope, and love
than in acts of unbelief, blasphemy, and malicious hatred of the most holy
Deity. For GOD’s persuasion and intention posit nothing real in the will. GOD’s
intention is a merely immanent action in GOD which, as such, exercises no
causality outwardly. 2. [They say] that GOD, when concurring with Devils,
intends the Devils to perform good and holy actions and to obey. I find it
difficult to be brought to believe this. Let the adversaries believe it; but
let them see by what light they do so. 3. No one with a grain of sense would
believe that GOD intends what it is not within his power effectually to
accomplish, or that GOD intends something for whose production he employs a
causality as indifferent toward its non-production as toward its production.
Soldiers do not intend to attack this city rather than that one when they
depend altogether upon their commanders’ direction as to which cities they
will attack and which they will not. 2. They answer: *“Good actions are from GOD
through the help of grace, but evil actions are not.”* But I answer: 1. GOD can
concur by general concurrence in two persons who possess habitual grace, as in
“4,” and nevertheless an act of faith can follow from that same concurrence in
one and an act of unbelief in the other. It follows here that GOD in concurring
contributes no causality to the act of faith which he did not contribute to the
act of unbelief. 2. For the Jesuits, this grace is often mere persuasion, which
contributes no real causality to the action. 3. Yet it still follows from this
that GOD’s actual grace is altogether equal in supernatural acts and in the
most wicked acts.

[Marginal reference: Suarez, part 1 of *On Divine Grace*, chapter 22.]

Argument XI. There is no reason why they imagine this general concurrence.
Therefore there is no such concurrence. The antecedent is proved. Their reason
is to free GOD from the causality of the act insofar as it is evil. But then, 1.
GOD would also have to be freed from the causality of the act insofar as it is
good, because general concurrence as such is the cause of no determinate
effect. 2. A general cause produces both a being and this being. Thus the sun
and the horse generate both a horse and this horse, Bucephalus. Therefore,
notwithstanding the generality of the concurrence, GOD and man will produce
both an act and this act of killing the Savior.

[Marginal argument XI.]

Argument XII. If this concurrence is posterior to the influx of the secondary
cause, then it cannot be a cause of the secondary cause’s action, which is
absurd. For a cause is not posterior to its effect. If it is prior, it cannot be
determined by the influx of the secondary cause, because everything that
determines is prior, not posterior, to what is determined. If it is simultaneous
with the influx of the secondary cause, then the following statements are
false: *“Because GOD concurs, therefore the secondary cause acts”;* and likewise,
*“Because the secondary cause acts, therefore GOD concurs.”* For when causes act
collaterally and by simultaneous motion, one does not act because the other
acts. 2. For, as Bradwardine says, *“if GOD and the will are posited as equal in
the order of action and naturally coeval, both are reduced to some third cause
prior to them.”* 3. Nor will GOD be the first cause if his concurrence is
determined by the secondary cause, because the act of determining will be prior
to the first cause’s action.

order, number, and quantity.”* Báñez says that *“this opinion inclines toward
the Pelagian heresy.”* Álvarez says that *“it posits a great imperfection in
GOD.”*

Molina objects as follows. *“Through one and the same influx by which one
numerically identical act is produced according to its natural being, it is
also produced according to its moral being. Therefore through the same influx
of GOD there is produced the act, and the act insofar as it is good and evil.
The reason for the antecedent is that a foundation and a relation are produced
through the same influx, as are a white thing and a similar thing, or a number
and parity; but goodness and malice are certain relations.”*

[Marginal reference: Molina at the place cited.]

I answer: 1. It is true that the act and its goodness are produced through the
same influx of GOD, but that influx is a special act of grace. For GOD’s
influxes need not be multiplied without necessity. But it follows by no means
from this that an evil act is produced through the same influx by which a good
act is produced. Because a number and an even number are produced by the same
act, by what logic, I ask, does it follow that both an even number and an odd
number are produced by the same act? This is precisely the question. I grant
that by the same generation both a man and a son can be born. But I do not think
it follows at all from this that a man, a son, and a father are born in the same
man. 2. Goodness and malice are not relations according to *being* (*esse*),
but only according to *being said* (*dici*), because relations according to
*being* have no activ-

-ity. 3. They are not intended in themselves by the agent. But the adversaries
will not deny that goodness and malice possess the moral force of meriting and
demeriting, and that goodness is intended in itself by the agent. 4. It is most
absurd that Peter’s faith should be produced by no more powerful and gracious
an act of GOD than Judas’s betrayal. Yet this follows necessarily from this
opinion.

Finally, it can be asked whether it is permissible in no sense to concede a
general concurrence of GOD. I answer: concurrence is called general in the
following senses. 1. “General” is distinguished from “special.” In this way,
GOD’s concurrence in agents in the sphere of nature is general, while that in
the sphere of grace is special. In this sense I concede that there is a general
concurrence of GOD. 2. We can also concede a general concurrence of GOD which
is determined by the secondary cause in the order of a material cause, *“by
which it is thus modified insofar as,”* says Cumel, *“GOD’s concurrence is
received by the secondary cause according to the nature, mode, and requirement
of the receiving nature.”* In this sense Thomas—although the Jesuits corrupt
his meaning into another sense—says that *“secondary causes as it were
particularize the action of the first cause.”* But we judge impious and
blasphemous any such concurrence of GOD which is efficiently and specifically
contracted and determined to the goodness and malice of an act by the action of
the secondary cause, as though of itself it regarded either this or that act
indifferently, confusedly, and without any order of providence, according as it
pleased the secondary cause.
