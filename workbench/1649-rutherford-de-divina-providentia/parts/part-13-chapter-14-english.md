# CHAPTER XIV

## The Jesuit Francis Annat’s arguments as an opponent of William Twisse for
middle knowledge, drawn from ten misunderstood Scripture passages, are
unraveled. Things passed solely through GOD’s will from eternity out of the
state of possibles into the state of futures; the created will is not the
cause; and the rationale of the most profound mystery of election and
reprobation is not unfolded through middle knowledge.

[Marginal reference: Annat, *On Middle Knowledge*, disputation 2, chapter 2.]

The basis on which Annat denies that possibles passed out of the state of
possibility into the state of futurition is this. He says: “The Adversary must
be allowed to recognize a transition where one cannot exist. For since there
is no time and no instant in the whole of eternity in which what is called
conditionally future was not future, it cannot be said to have passed from the
state of something merely possible into the state of a future. For if
something truly passes from one state into another, it must first have existed
under the term from which before existing under the term to which. This is
just as if one were to ask why David should be delivered into Saul’s hands by
the Keilites, if he remained among them, rather than why he should not be
delivered when that condition was posited.”

[Chapter XIV; printed page 165; PDF page 216 — the catchword “beyond” is
excluded]

I answer: 1. One must not dispute against those who deny first principles. I
do not deny that there is no time from eternity, before the creation of time,
“in which the possible could have passed to the state of a future.” Nor is
there “any instant in the whole of eternity,” if the discussion concerns a
successive instant or an instant of duration in which such a transition would
occur. This indeed is true. And the Jesuit speaks about a transition of the
kind which would occur in time, or about a “transition of motion” in which what
passes is first, in duration, “in the term from which,” and then “in the term
to which.” We do not imagine such a transition either in God or in eternity;
we are not so obtuse. If the thing is agreed, there is no need to quarrel about
the word “transition.” But if the discussion concerns (as it undoubtedly does)
an “instant of reason” which has a place in eternity, a Jesuit who denies that
all future things—much less conditionally future things—are first possible in
an instant of reason and pass from this state into futures, solely by the
determination of the divine will, scarcely deserves to be called a theologian.

[Marginal summary: The transition from the state of possibles to the state of
a future from eternity is demonstrated to occur solely by God’s will, against
Francis Annat the Jesuit.]

I demonstrate it as follows. 1. Just as everything which exists in time was
future before time and from eternity, so whatever began to be future, or as it
were began from eternity (for there is no time here), was, in an instant of
reason before it acquired futurition, either simply possible or not simply
possible. The Jesuit will not deny that these are contradictories in relation
to GOD’s creative power. If it had first been impossible, it would have implied
a contradiction for it to acquire futurition, for what is simply impossible
cannot be produced by God. Therefore it was never to be produced; therefore it
was not future. It was therefore possible, in a sign of reason, before it was
future. Indeed, everything feasible is prior in an instant of reason to what
is to be done (if one may coin the term); everything creatable is prior to
what is to be created, and everything producible to what is to be produced.
For nothing is to be created and nothing is to be produced even by
omnipotence unless it is first creatable and producible.

And let the Jesuit minds give some cause of this transition besides GOD’s will:
why, out of a thousand worlds and a thousand other creatures creatable and
producible in relation to GOD’s creative power (if, with the grammarians’ kind
permission, we must speak this way), was this one and only world to be created
in GOD’s decree from eternity, rather than others which were equally creatable
and producible by God? For since nothing was created in time which, before
time, was not to be created in GOD’s decree and wise intention (unless we say
that things were created

[Chapter XIV; printed page 166; PDF page 217 — the catchword “which” is
excluded]

beyond God’s decree and intention—that is, fortuitously, which is godless),
everything to be created acquired futurition from GOD’s free decree. Otherwise,
if God did not freely decree to create this one world and to produce this free
act of believing, rather than the contrary act which lies under the
lawlessness of unbelief, or to produce this world rather than a thousand other
worlds equally creatable in relation to creative Omnipotence, then he created
this world and decreed to create it under compulsion by some necessity
proceeding extrinsically from the object. Sound theology does not admit this;
it comes closer to Manichaeism than is proper.

But that GOD freely decreed to create this world and not another, and to
produce this free act and not its contrary, when other worlds and other acts
are equally producible by God, means nothing other than that, although this
world was only creatable and producible, it acquired its status as something
to be created and its future creation from God’s free decree. But this is the
transition from the possible to the state of a future through GOD’s decree.
For nothing besides GOD’s decree made this world, out of the many creatable
worlds, the one to be created and the future one, separated it from all the
others, and raised it to actual creation. We challenge the Jesuits to declare
some other cause which existed from eternity.

2. Prophecies and predictions by GOD and the prophets present many future
things to us, and future contingently—things which occur freely in time—such as
CHRIST’s generation from David’s seed, the gracious calling of the Gentiles
through the apostles and other pastors, the entirely free obedience of those
called, Josiah’s acts of justice before the king was born, the invocation and
prayers of the saints, the faith and obedience of those who in the world’s old
age will be θεοδίδακτοι, taught by God, and the patience, constancy, and
faith of God’s martyrs. All these works of GOD’s grace either were merely
possible from eternity and never future, or were possible in an instant of
reason before they were future. If they were merely possible from eternity
and never future, then they could neither be foreknown by God nor predicted by
the prophets. For whatever is foreknown and whatever is predicted must be
determinate and of determinate truth, as is known by the light of nature. How
could the obedience of the Gentiles be infallibly predicted as future if it
were only possible and disobedience were just as determinate and certain as
obedience? Moreover, all these things certainly happen. Therefore they were
future, because whatever exists as present in time was future from eternity,
just as everything temporal

[Chapter XIV; printed page 167; PDF page 218]

which is now past was once present. And since they were future, they must also
have been simply possible, because what is simply impossible is never future
and never present. Therefore, if these things were possible and future at the
same time, who from eternity carried those possibles forward to the state of
futures except the one who also makes futures present and “works all things
according to the counsel of his will”? Let the Jesuits hear it.

3. For us, this transition of possibles to the state of futures is nothing
other than GOD’s decree, by which, out of many possibles, he determined within
himself from eternity that this would be future, not that; this world, not
another equally possible world; this act, not that; this contingent event, not
another. For example, he determined that CHRIST’s bones would not be broken,
rather than that they would be broken—which would have been just as possible
as the breaking of the robbers’ bones if we consider the nature of contingent
causes.

[Marginal summary: What the transition from the state of possibles to the
state of a future properly signifies.]

Those, therefore, who deny such a transition deny GOD’s eternal decrees. And
the Jesuits and Arminians in reality incline this way: they deny all the
statutes and decrees by which God determined all things to one outcome, leave
everything from eternity in the state of possibility, and hold that all things
were possible but none determinately future. They define a free cause as one
which, when all the things required for acting have been posited (whether
those requirements are God, GOD’s knowledge, a decree, the strongest motion of
grace, or anything else), can absolutely act or not act. This is to commit
everything to chance and fortune and utterly overthrow GOD’s providence, the
governor of all things. Hence Annat says that “the question here is why it is
true rather than not true that the Keilites will deliver David into Saul’s
hands if David remains and Saul approaches.” He says that the cause is the
Keilites’ free will separated from GOD’s decree—indeed, prior to and apart from
every decree of GOD or determination of the divine will.

He therefore says: “Concerning these contingent futures, one should say not so
much that they *are going to happen* as that they *would happen,* and not so
much that they *pass* from the condition of a possible into the condition of a
future as that they *would pass.*”

“If the present free will of the Keilites, aided by God’s concurrence and
tested by Saul’s approach, whether by threats, entreaties, or promises, were the
cause why David would be delivered into Saul’s hands, then it is true from
eternity that such a hypothesis would be such a cause. And just as the present
hypothesis causes the present determination of a free and contingent effect,
and, when absolutely future, will cause that same determination, so too, when
conditionally future, it will cause that same determination. Likewise, just
as it is now tr-*

as the cause of the eternal futurition of contingent futures.]

it—indeed, throughout all subsequent eternity—so it is true that a similar
hypothesis will cause it absolutely; and before the age and from eternity that
very thing was true. Equally, it is true that the same hypothesis would cause
that very thing throughout all past, present, and future time. The Adversary
will never find anything to oppose to this doctrine, no matter how much he
shakes himself.”

I answer: 1. Annat raises a mere verbal quarrel when he distinguishes between
“they are future” and “they would be future,” between “they pass” and “they
would pass.” For someone who knows grammar can easily turn verbs in the
subjunctive mood into the indicative. I posit the present tense (which can
easily be posited for the sake of argument, and which the Jesuit posits in the
present argument): “If Saul comes here, the Keilites are now delivering David
into his hands.” It will therefore be futile to distinguish between “they
deliver” and “they would deliver,” because even if the Jesuit “shook himself,”
he would not find more differences of time than present, past, and future. The
moods of tenses, or of the verb—indicative, imperative, optative, subjunctive,
or infinitive—are only moods, not differences of time. It was therefore true
from eternity “in an instant of reason.”

God decrees this possible thing—namely, a possible world—as future, so that this
world which is to be created is not merely possible but genuinely future. The
same applies to this: “The Keilites are delivering David if Saul is now coming
to the Keilites and now soliciting them to deliver him.” Thus Annat answers
nothing beyond little letters and syllables. Our argument stands unconquered
and unshaken: “The Keilites were going from eternity to deliver David into
Saul’s hands if Saul came to them and David remained.” But there was no
connection and no determinate truth in the object except from GOD’s decree.
There was no determinate truth from the terms themselves and none from the
force of the terms; the proposition could of itself have been true or false.
There was no determinate connection from the terms and no determinate truth
from GOD’s foreknowledge, because the Jesuits teach that such propositions
have determinate truth antecedently to GOD’s foreknowledge. There was no
determinate truth from GOD’s essence and none from GOD’s omnipotence, because
GOD’s essence is not the cause of things or of truth except insofar as GOD’s
free and most wise will accedes to his essence or omnipotence, freely creating
all things outside God. By the same reasoning, the Keilites’ present free will
is not the cause determining David’s delivery, but the one by which it is
deter-*

[Chapter XIV; printed page 169; PDF page 220 — the catchword “operations” is
excluded]

*mined toward its entitative act by GOD and by his eternal decree. Therefore
(to turn this weapon back upon the Jesuit), such a delivery was not future
from eternity except by GOD’s decree causing that futurition. Thus if Annat
understands Twisse’s “aid of physical predetermination,” which supposes GOD’s
absolute and antecedent decree, and sets it down as the cause determining the
free will of the Keilites to deliver David, he confirms our argument rather
than dissolving it.

Nothing, then, is more false than what Annat adds: “I say likewise that, just
as the possibility of a necessary cause which exists from eternity is the
sufficient ground and cause on account of which a necessary effect is
determinately future, so the possibility of a free cause and of everything
necessary for it to act freely, which also exists from eternity, is the
sufficient ground and cause on account of which its free effect is
determinately future.” And later: “In this also [Twisse] continually seems to
apply force: that a supposition from which the effect can as readily follow as
not follow—which is the supposition of a complete free cause—cannot be the
ground and cause why the effect will follow rather than not follow. But I deny
that a supposition from which an effect can as readily follow as not follow
cannot be the ground or cause why one will be future rather than the other,
provided that it involves a force which determines and is determinative of one
of them. Let it be so if it involves such a force. This is clear in GOD’s
freedom, which is a suitable cause or ground why there is said to be a volition
to produce the world, although, when that same freedom was posited, there could
just as readily have been a nolition of that thing.”

I answer: Although there is nothing new here which has not been hammered home
a hundred times by Suárez and the other Jesuits, nevertheless elsewhere and
later I prove that every cause, both necessary and contingent, is determined
as to exercise by God alone. This is evident from Job, chapters 38 and 39;
Psalm 104; Psalm 107; Psalm 75:8–10. For he who appoints that the sun and stars
not rise, commands the winds to blow or not blow, and commands the rains, hail,
snow, clouds, sky, birds, fish, human beings, and every kind of creature to
operate or not operate, to operate in this way or that, and who has supreme
dominion over all the operations of creatures so that, at the command and nod
of his ordinary providence, they happen or do not happen—he undoubtedly
determines all necessary and contingent causes. For only the one who determines
causes to operate or not operate is the supreme LORD of all

[Marginal summary: All causes are determined by God.]

excluded]

the operations of creatures. But according to the Scriptures, God alone does
the latter.

2. The possibility of no thing can be the cause, much less the sufficient
ground and cause, on account of which a thing is determinately future. 1. A
thousand other worlds, trees, suns, stars, brute animals, fish, birds, angels,
and human beings are possible, and other species of things could have been
created; but these are never future, never determinately future. For on this
account there would be as sufficient a ground and cause on account of which
the things that happen in time were future as for the things which will never
happen and are never future.

[Marginal summary: The possibility of no cause can be the cause of a thing’s
futurition.]

2. If the formal ground of all futures is given, then all things are future.
For futures are future in their formal causes. But if possibles are future
because they are possible, everything which involves no contradiction, since
it is possible in relation to GOD’s creative power, will be determinately
future. Who revealed such a philosophy to the Jesuits: that a thousand other
worlds are determinately future, thousands of myriads more species of animals,
fish, birds, reptiles, angels, and human beings; a world eternal toward the
future; CHRIST’s deliverance from death on the cross by the sending of more
than twelve legions of angels from God the Father; and the creation of children
for Abraham out of stones in place of those who heard John the Baptist
preaching? For both reason and sacred Scripture establish that all these
things, and infinitely many others of this kind, are possible. Therefore they
are determinately future. But that conclusion is utterly absurd.

3. Things which can never happen are not future; most possibles can never
happen. Therefore, and so forth. The ground of the major premise is that
futures would not be future if they were not going to happen. But if they can
fail to happen, in many cases they would never happen, and thus would be both
future and not future.

4. We would have to believe GOD’s promises, threats, and predictions not
because the things God promises and threatens are determinately future, but
because they are possible. But then we would have to believe very many
promises made by the Devil and false people which are nothing other than
splendid lies, even though very many such things are possible: “Adam will not
die”; “Ahab will obtain victory over the Syrians and return alive from the
battle at Ramoth-gilead.” These things are possible but never future.

5. Nothing certain is to be believed concerning possibles, for they can happen
and can fail to happen; but contingent futures which God

[Chapter XIV; printed page 171; PDF page 222 — the catchword “obedience” is
excluded]

predicts must be believed with the certainty of faith. Therefore those futures
predicted by God are something very different from possibles. But on this
opinion, the things which were future from eternity were nothing other than
things possible from eternity.

6. GOD freely created for himself and for his own will and glory whatever
things are future. Proverbs 16:4; Romans 11:36–37; Revelation 4:11. And the one
who “works all things according to the counsel of his will,” as Paul says in
Ephesians 1:11, decreed from eternity that all things which are present and
past would be future. Accordingly, futures are not future antecedently to and
apart from every decree which causes futures. But all possibles have being
before every free decree or free efficiency of GOD. For formally, the possible
is that which does not conflict with being, or that which can be. But every
possible is such that it can exist apart from every decree of GOD. Nor does God
properly bring the possible about, because the possible cannot fail to be such
in relation to GOD’s creative and productive power. God cannot freely decree
that a world, an angel, a human being, a rose, a plant, or creatures are
possible, because they cannot fail to be producible. But whatever God freely
decrees to be future, God can freely decree not to be future. Thus the Jesuit
and his fellows betray supine ignorance and overthrow the immovable principles
of sounder philosophy when they defend this new middle knowledge, unheard of
before these times.

The Jesuit does nothing but beg the question when he contends that “a free
cause is the cause why a free effect is future rather than why it is not
future,” because it has the power to determine itself and mastery over its own
acts, as he said a little earlier. For 1. according to Annat, even all causes
which lack such a determinative power and mastery “are, because they are
possible, the causes why things are determinately future.” Annat taught us
that not long ago.

2. It is false that a created free cause determines itself to act or possesses
such mastery over its own acts. God alone bends and determines free wills to
act. Otherwise, I pray in vain that he bend my will to render free obedience
to God, as Psalm 119 does a hundred times and nearly everywhere in Scripture,
if it depends not on God but upon my free will that I be heard in this prayer.

3. In vain do I render GOD thanks and the glory for obedience rather than
disobedience.

4. In vain would I owe God the honor of confidence and hope that he graciously
works in me

5. If the little human being possesses such mastery to refuse obedience rather

Scripture says that God can present human beings blameless before CHRIST (which
is undoubtedly the very powerful potency of saving grace: Jude 24; Romans

stripped of all faithfulness and truthfulness in the promises of the gospel and
the new covenant, and of all dominion over free acts. For on Jesuit middle
knowledge God cannot, by employing all his power or command, bend the free will
of any human being or angel to free obedience. Behold the fragile and
worm-eaten prop on which the fiction of this new knowledge rests: unless GOD’s
providence collapses, it cannot stand.

3. There is no equal account of divine and uncreated freedom and the freedom
of a created, mutable, and slippery creature. For no potentiality is mingled
with GOD’s freedom; it is pure act determining itself from the intrinsic wisdom
which it essentially involves. But potentiality is conjoined to created
freedom and every second cause. It cannot obtain such mastery over its own acts
(which is the creature’s weakness) by which it can fully actualize itself, much
less determine itself. This consequence is therefore invalid: GOD’s free will
is the cause determining a possible effect to futurition; therefore, the
created will causes futurition from eternity.

Annat, with many futile arguments secretly purloined from Suárez, chapter 3,
disputation 1, numbers 22–25, contends that GOD’s decree by which he determined
to permit David’s delivery into Saul’s hands if David remained in the city of
Keilah and Saul approached was a properly so-called conditional decree,
because its object was conditional. But we deny the inference. In GOD there is
no decree which, as to the act of willing or decreeing, is suspended rather
than complete and fixed. Nor is any decree conditional on account of an
uncertain condition to be posited by the creature. Such a decree belongs to an
ignorant human being, not to the omniscient GOD. God has in himself an absolute
decree concerning an object which is indeed conditional. I say again: all
GOD’s decrees are formally and in themselves absolute, even those which are
conditional on the part of the object, because both the condition and the
conditioned thing depend imm-*

Annat responds after his master Suárez over many pages: “It follows from this

GOD’s predefinition overthrows created freedom (which is what the present

independent freedom toward the Tyrians and others. Augustine says: “This

2. This argument censures GOD’s wisdom, who decreed to give eternal life to
everyone fulfilling the law. Yet neither fulfillment of the law nor the life
given to everyone ever exists. But we say that such a decree could have been
absolute in GOD.

“I absolutely will and decree repentance for the Tyrians, as people more
pliable toward the gospel and less obstinate than the people of Capernaum, if
indeed they saw the signs.” Yet God did not even foreknow this repentance
except in the decree to give repentance. For repentance is GOD’s gift.

[Marginal summary: Our response to the passage about the Tyrians, Matthew

Annat answers first: “The Tyrians’ repentance could not have been future except
from GOD’s gift and a decree presently and absolutely existing in act.” I deny
this. “It could not have been future except from a possible gift and decree to
be posited according to GOD’s freedom.” I grant this. “For it has often now
been said that an absolute future requires a cause either absolutely existing
or future; but a conditional future requires only a possible or conditionally

I answer: 1. That which is determined—and is future not in a possible mode
absolutely indifferent to either alternative, but is determinately future and
a fixed and unavoidable foundation of infallible divine foreknowledge—has a
cause which is not merely possible and indifferent to either alternative but
actual. Such is the Tyrians’ repentance, presented and predicted by CHRIST,
although it is conditionally future. And in the mode in which it is foreknown,
it has a cause in which it is foreknown. But the Tyrians’ repentance is
foreknown as future and as having an actual connection and linkage with the
signs seen and the gospel heard. Therefore, the actual cause of that
infallibly foreknown connection was posited, and it requires a cause in act.

For Annat supposes that the object of middle knowledge is a being which is
merely possibly future because it is conditionally future. But this is no
reason. The Tyrians’ repentance is conditionally future, but not merely
possibly future such that it may be future or not future, for then it would not
terminate GOD’s infallible knowledge. Rather, it is determinately future—that
is, it has a determinate connection with the seeing of the signs, for what
CHRIST predicts is infallibly, actually, and determinately true. But the
Tyrians’ repentance coheres with the signs seen determinately and infallibly,
either from the nature of the thing or from some actual cause connecting those
extre-*

*mes. They do not cohere determinately from the nature of the thing, because
the seeing of the signs does not determine or efficaciously persuade
repentance. But for an actual connection there is an actual and posited cause,
not a possible cause yet to be posited; and no cause besides GOD’s decree can
be imagined. Here I am arguing concerning the Tyrians’ repentance according to
the Jesuits’ gloss, from which I declare that I dissent.

2. Annat adds: “Yet I do not understand this in such a way that I think the
hypothesis from which something is called conditionally future involves a
decree predefining that which is said to follow. This conditional was as true
from eternity: ‘If the gospel confirmed by miracles is presented to the Jews
with the predefinition of their repentance, the Jews’ repentance will be
future,’ as this other: ‘If the same gospel similarly confirmed by signs is
presented to the Tyrians with a similar predefinition of repentance, the
Tyrians’ repentance will be future.’ Hence, in order that what is said to be
future in the Tyrians not be future in the Jews, it is necessary that the
hypothesis from whose condition something is said to be future not involve any
decree predefining what is future.”

I answer: I grant the entire antecedent. For when a gracious predefining decree
and efficacious grace were posited together with the gospel at the same hour,
both the Jews and the Tyrians would have repented. Nor can the Jesuits escape
this if we suppose both Jews and Tyrians to be subject to the same congruous
decree and the same congruous motion, just as they suppose against us that
both are subject to the same absolute predefinition. The Jews’ repentance is
as certainly future as the Tyrians’. They therefore bear the burden of solving
this argument, if it has any force, no less than we do.
