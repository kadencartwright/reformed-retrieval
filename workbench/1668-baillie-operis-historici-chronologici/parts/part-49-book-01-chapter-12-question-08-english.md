### QUESTION VIII

#### To What Times Should the Construction of the Temple, the Division of the Land, and the Other Things in Ezekiel 40 and the Following Chapters Be Referred?

The things Ezekiel has in his last nine chapters, exceedingly well suited to
the church’s consolation but exceedingly difficult and obscure, would receive
light especially from their time, if it were established at last to what
precise time they should be referred. Time here would indeed be a golden key
for opening the sweetest mysteries that are most deeply hidden. But the same
inextricable difficulty which interpreters have always acknowledged in
Ezekiel’s chorography, if not a greater one, will appear in his chronology to
those who examine it seriously. The Jews formerly commanded everyone to
refrain from this part of Ezekiel before reaching the priestly age of thirty,
as Galatinus, Sanctius, and others report from the rabbis. Certainly Deodati
and many others wholly abstained from interpreting it, while most interpreters,
both ancient and more recent, assert things that do little to illuminate the
passage.

> Marginalia, printed p. 276: *The question’s difficulty*.

When Jerome, overcome by the virgin Eustochium’s entreaties, had broken his
fixed purpose and persistent resolve to remain silent, he professes his own
trepidation in explaining Ezekiel’s temple and his conjectures in this most
difficult work, warning his reader that, if he desired

> Marginalia, printed p. 276: *Jerome’s modesty*.

[Printed p. 277 | IA leaf 307 | access PDF 307 | OCR object 306]

the truth, he should seek it there from others rather than from him. To his
commentary here he applies both the fear and the darkness which, as a boy at
Rome, he had experienced in underground crypts where apostles and martyrs
were buried, employing this line of Virgil:

> Horror strikes every heart, and the very silence terrifies;

and this saying of the prophet: “Clouds and thick darkness are beneath his
feet; darkness is his hiding place.” He frankly confesses that he is compelled
to say, “Oh, the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How
unsearchable are his judgments and how untraceable his ways? The Lord’s
judgments are a great abyss.” Accordingly, he does not wish rashly to discourse
upon Ezekiel’s temple, about which all ages have been silent, but in faith and
the fear of God merely to set his conjecture before his readers, adducing
these lines of the poet:

> Here was that labor of the house and the inextricable maze,
> Such as the Labyrinth once was said to be in lofty Crete,
> An intricate path woven through blind walls, and with a thousand
> Ways possessing a bewildering deception, by which it might frustrate
> Unmistakable signs for following and become an irretraceable maze.

And at the beginning of chapter 42 he says: “Because of my despair and the
greatness of the subject, I had wished to pass over the present testimony in
silence, but I thought it better to say whatever I could than to say nothing,
employing that Socratic saying, ‘I know that I do not know,’ for it is part of
knowledge to know what you do not know.”

I approve this great modesty in so great a man more than the latest English
Annotators’ rather confident promise concerning the light they would
contribute for posterity’s benefit, although we embrace their deserved censure
of the Jesuit Sanctius, who dared once and again to blaspheme the holy
prophet’s chorography—the most exact, if any ever was—as senseless, and once
again as senseless. If so impious a statement had escaped from any of our
men, what tragedies, do you suppose, those clamorous men would have raised?
We have said these things by way of preface so that an excuse for our ignorance,
or even error, in this question might be readier at hand.

> Marginalia, printed p. 277: *The blasphemy of the Jesuit Sanctius*.

There are four opinions here, to which we shall add a fifth. The first is that
of Theodoret, Sanctius, and others. Although they expound certain portions of
the prophet mystically of Christ’s church under the gospel, they nevertheless
interpret the house and city themselves as the second temple built by
Zerubbabel and the city Jerusalem built by Nehemiah. We do not deny that the
angel describes an exact image for the consolation of the Jews exiled in
Babylon after the ruin of temple and city; but we prove as follows that this
image is not of the temple, city, or condition of Israel immediately after the
Babylonian captivity.

> Marginalia, printed p. 277: *Argument 1 against Theodoret’s opinion*.

First. The enclosure, or outermost wall, surrounding Ezekiel’s temple and all
its courts was a quadrangle consisting of four equal sides. On each were five
hundred, and so in all two thousand, reeds, each of which consisted of six
cubits and six handbreadths, chapter 42:16 and following. This is eight
Italian miles, or at least four; for an ordinary cubit consists of a foot and
a half, so that six cubits make nine feet, and six handbreadths, each
consisting of four fingers or three inches, make a foot and a half. Ezekiel’s
reed was therefore ten feet, with something more which we omit. Five feet,
however, make the greater geometric pace, of which a thousand make an Italian
mile. Thus two thousand reeds, each consisting of ten feet, or two paces, or
six cubits and six handbreadths, make four Italian miles; but whether they
amount to eight can rightly be doubted.

For in his commentary on Ezekiel 40 Junius, and others after him, make all
sacred measures twice their ordinary counterparts. Thus the sacred reed of
two paces, employed by the angel to measure God’s most holy buildings, contains
four ordinary paces. I admit that Junius counts no more than two paces in
Ezekiel’s reed, but this is certainly less consistent with his own calculation;
for if sacred measures are double the ordinary, and the angel’s reed, employed
solely to measure sacred things, is most sacred, why should its numbers, if
any, not be doubled? Nevertheless, all the writers on this matter who are at
hand, whether they call Ezekiel’s reed ordinary, royal, or sacred, do not extend
it beyond six ordinary cubits and six ordinary handbreadths.

> Marginalia, printed p. 277: *Junius wrongly makes sacred measures twice the
> ordinary*.

And certainly Junius’s reasons by which he proves sacred measures double the
ordinary do not seem sound. The first comes from 2 Chronicles 3:3, where the
sixty cubits of the temple’s length and twenty of its breadth are called the
original measure. No word about doubling appears here, for this original
measure can be referred to the pattern earlier shown by God to David, or even
to the measures employed by Moses in the tabernacle, in which the named cubits
cannot be understood as double. I recall no one who stretches the holy place’s
length and breadth, and the Holy of Holies’ height, from twenty cubits to forty.

Junius’s other reason is that Solomon’s palace, which he erected for his own
residence, and very many sacred buildings among Christians today would be far
narrower than the Jerusalem temple unless we doubled the temple’s measures.
Response. Even if we double those measures, both Solomon’s palaces and many
Christian basilicas will still be larger than the temple, for

[Printed p. 278 | IA leaf 308 | access PDF 308 | OCR object 307]

this obvious reason. The temple sanctuary lay open to only one man, the high
priest, and the outer house to the few priests serving at the altar of incense,
the table, and the lampstands. But Solomon’s palace and Christian basilicas
were intended for the assembly of the entire people. If a comparison were to
be made, Solomon’s court and Christians’ sacred buildings should be compared
not with the temple but with the people’s courts; these greatly surpassed the
palaces of any kings in both length and breadth.

Villalpandus, the most exact among learned architects, contends that only one
handbreadth should be added to the six cubits. But he admits that in this even
the most learned men of his own party—Vatablus, Pintus, and others—oppose him,
rightly asserting from chapter 43:13 that every cubit requires the addition of
a handbreadth, and therefore that the six cubits in the reed rightly require
six handbreadths, or a seventh ordinary cubit.

> Marginalia, printed p. 278: *Villalpandus’s error*.

Certainly Villalpandus himself and all learned men after Herodotus compose a
royal cubit, much more a sacred one, of a foot and a half plus a handbreadth.
Why, therefore, should the angel’s reed, consisting of six cubits, not have a
handbreadth added to each cubit? In his calculation Villalpandus is forced to
extend the handbreadth which he adds to the six cubits beyond either three
inches, four fingers, or any measure of a handbreadth named by anyone.

Whatever the case, let the temple’s measures be as small as you please; our
argument will stand. An enclosure of two thousand reeds, consisting in any
fashion of six cubits and a handbreadth, makes four Italian miles. But neither
Solomon’s nor even Zerubbabel’s temple ever reached this circuit. Indeed, the
whole city Jerusalem in its most flourishing times does not seem to have
occupied so much space.

Three responses are made to this argument. 1. Junius wishes each side to have
cubits, not reeds, thus making the space six times smaller. I reply: 1. Even
if so great a diminution is granted, the enclosure of Solomon’s temple will
still be far from the space now before us. 2. Junius and those who follow him
openly corrupt the text here, inserting cubits for reeds not only against our
Piscator, Deodati, all three sets of English Annotators, and other Protestants
generally, but also against every Roman Catholic. It is true that in verse 20
both the Greek and Latin versions read “cubits” for “reeds,” but the Jesuits
themselves, Maldonatus and à Lapide, acknowledge the versions’ manifest error,
which they correct by deleting “cubits” and restoring “reeds.” They do so on
Jerome’s express authority; he admits his own inadvertence here along with that
of the Septuagint interpreters, having in his haste added the word “cubit” to
the Hebrew text.

> Marginalia, printed p. 278: *Junius corrupts the sacred text*.

The Jesuits Sanctius and Villalpandus profess to defend this acknowledged error
of Jerome against the Hebrew text: the former on the ground of the Latin
version’s infallibility, the latter as though the prophet in the final verse
spoke of an inner wall other than the one discussed in the four preceding
verses. Both reasons are bad; yet not even they affirm that “cubits” should be
read for “reeds” in the preceding verses. It is therefore surprising that
Junius embraced this reading in all five of these verses. I thought at first
that the most learned man had been deceived by the Hebrew word in verse 16,
which through a transposition of letters is read אמות, *Emoth*, “cubit,”
instead of מאות, *Meoth*. But the scribe’s marginal slip, the *qere*,
immediately corrects this by writing מאות, *meoth*, and every one of the
following four verses manifestly approves this correction, all expressly
reading מאות, *meoth*, not אמות, *emoth*. All the versions—Greek,
Chaldean, Latin, and vernacular—confirm this reading, so that the source of
Junius’s error does not seem to lie here.

> Marginalia, printed p. 278: *Villalpandus’s other error*.

The second response is Villalpandus’s: namely, that the prophet applies reeds
to one wall and cubits to another, the former to the outermost enclosure
surrounding the court of the gentiles, the latter to the inner wall surrounding
the people’s court. I reply: This response confirms, and does not weaken, the
argument, which was drawn from the outermost enclosure in verse 16 and the
others before verse 20. This distinction of Villalpandus is moreover rightly
rejected even by his companions, à Lapide and others, who correctly teach that
in verse 20 the prophet speaks of no wall other than the one discussed in the
four preceding verses, and therefore that this verse must be interpreted of
reeds no less than they are.

> Marginalia, printed p. 278: *Likewise a third*.

The third response is also Villalpandus’s. He does not wish the measure of five
hundred reeds in verse 26 to be understood of any one side separately, but of
all four sides around the circuit, so that the separate sides each had only
125 reeds, and all together 500, by no means 2,000. Reply. Sanctius at first
rejects this invention of Villalpandus as something which he neither likes nor
understands. Yet afterward the inconsistent man, retracting these things,
inclines to embrace it, though reluctantly. But à Lapide, Maldonatus, and others
openly reject it as a bold and false invention. Certainly, since the prophet
most expressly attributes five hundred reeds, not once but five times, to each
side separately—namely, east, west, north, and south—and collectively to all in
both breadth and length, it belongs to abandoned audacity for any mortal to

> Marginalia, printed p. 278: *A fourth as well*.

[Printed p. 279 | IA leaf 309 | access PDF 309 | OCR object 308]

wish to attribute only 125 to them. So gross an error by Villalpandus in the
measurements compels me to suspect
that this man’s immense and very learned labors on the prophets’ sacred
architecture were not so successful as I once thought. It is not without
reason that Sanctius, à Lapide, and other Jesuits openly profess that they do
not comprehend Villalpandus’s mathematics.

> Marginalia, printed p. 279: *The second argument against Theodoret*.

The second argument by which we prove that Ezekiel’s temple was not an image of
Solomon’s temple, nor a model set before Zerubbabel after which he should fashion
his second temple, is this: Both Solomon’s and Zerubbabel’s temples were inside
the walls and gates of the city Jerusalem. But Ezekiel’s temple was many
thousands of paces outside the walls of Jerusalem, or of whatever city has
been or will be holy to Israel. No one disputes the major premise, for although
both the earlier and later Jerusalem was double, upper and lower, indeed
triple, the whole city, and especially the temple, was nevertheless contained
within a single outer wall. Chapters 45 and 48 prove the minor premise. There,
by divine command, a firstfruits offering in the land is to be separated,
25,000 reeds in both length and breadth. Of these, 25,000 in length and 10,000
in breadth fell to the priests; as much in both length and breadth to the
Levites; and 25,000 in length and 5,000 in breadth to the holy city, *Jehovah
Shammah*.

The length and breadth of these portions must be reckoned differently from
those of the whole land. In the days of Joshua, Zerubbabel, and Ezekiel the
length of Canaan was measured from north to south, because this dimension was
four or six times the breadth from west to east—that is, from the Mediterranean
Sea to the Jordan or to the Sea of Galilee or the Dead Sea. But the dimensions
of the firstfruits and the sacred portion must be measured in the opposite way:
the priests’ 25,000 reeds in length proceed from west to east, while their
smaller measure, the breadth of 10,000 reeds, proceeds from north to south,
the direction in which not the breadth but the length of the whole land ran.
The same applies to the portion of the Levites and of the city.

At any rate, the temple stood in the middle of the priestly allotment. Between
it and the city’s outermost walls lay the whole portion of the Levites, ten
thousand reeds wide, and almost half the priestly portion as well, at least
more than four thousand reeds. Now fourteen thousand reeds make twenty-eight
Italian miles, the distance by which Ezekiel’s temple had to be separated from
the holy city. Nevertheless the Jesuit Sanctius dares to affirm, without any
argument, that this temple stood below that city. But if you inspect the plans
of Fuller, à Lapide, or Lyranus, or Ezekiel’s own text, you will pronounce this
statement of Sanctius a senseless absurdity.

I argue third. The holy city has a circuit of 18,000 reeds, that is, thirty-six
Italian miles, when two paces or ten feet are assigned to each reed. But
Jerusalem on earth never attained a quarter of that space. I certainly do not
agree with the middle English Annotators, who, on Ezekiel 43:18, specifically
affirm that the holy city’s circuit equaled and far surpassed the boundaries
of all Canaan. For even if you extend those 18,000 reeds in circumference to
25,000 in length and 10,000 in breadth, which is a much larger space, these
authors still do not make this interval exceed ninety-seven miles in length
and thirty-nine in breadth. These are far short of the 160 miles which all
learned men after Jerome ascribe to the length of Canaan, and the sixty
assigned to its breadth.

> Marginalia, printed p. 279: *The English Annotators are censured*.

The latest Annotators do indeed correct the middle ones here. On the first and
sixteenth verses of chapter 45, they ascribe only eighty-one miles to those
25,000 reeds, although I do not see how this calculation accords with the
truth, nor how the three others which they name there very confusedly and
indeterminately—namely, seventy-seven, 154, or 162 miles—do so. Whatever the
case, our argument stands: Jerusalem on earth never reached 18,000 reeds,
however they are taken.

To escape this argument, Sanctius affirms that those 18,000 are to be
understood not as reeds but as cubits. Reply: 1. Although the sacred text in
the whole of chapter 48 does not express the word “reed,” all interpreters,
ancient and modern, understand reeds. 2. The measure in chapter 48 is to be
understood as the one by which the angel measured the temple and its courts in
the preceding chapters; but that measure was a reed, not a cubit, as can be
seen at 48:16 and elsewhere. 3. Ezekiel’s city is parallel to John’s city and
has some proportion to it. Although the latter did not have twelve thousand
stadia on each side, it had that many around its circuit—that is, fifteen
hundred Italian miles, and by the calculation of some, as many German miles,
as Paraeus notes on Revelation 21:16.

> Marginalia, printed p. 279: *Sanctius’s error*.

[Printed p. 280 | IA leaf 310 | access PDF 310 | OCR object 309]

Indeed, the walls of the new Jerusalem could not have been fifteen hundred
Italian miles high, an elevation which the latest English Annotators rightly
call
prodigious and therefore necessarily mystical. Accordingly, lest all
proportion between those two cities be destroyed, the measure of the former
must be the larger reed, not the smaller cubit, since the latter’s measure is
not the reed itself but the stadium.

> Marginalia, printed p. 280: *The prodigious height of the new Jerusalem*.

The fourth argument. In Ezekiel’s division, one equal part of Canaan is
assigned to each of the twelve tribes. But in Zerubbabel’s time and that of the
second temple absolutely no portion was assigned to the ten tribes, for they
had not yet returned; and the few who were mingled with the Jews obtained no
distinct dwelling place, either from the others or among themselves. Indeed,
the portions of Judah and Benjamin were intermingled, and not even the
allotments of these two tribes were entirely distinct.

I state this argument in another way. Ezekiel’s division was wholly different
from every division made either in Joshua’s days or at any later time. For in
Ezekiel 48 every tribe obtains an equal and distinct allotment, but in the
periods of both the first and second temple the tribes’ allotments were very
unequal and mingled with one another. The allotments of Judah and Ephraim were
larger than those of almost all the other tribes, and Judah and Benjamin were
so intermingled that one part of Jerusalem belonged to the former and another
to the latter. In Joshua’s allotment, Gad lay beyond the Jordan in the furthest
eastern and northern region of Canaan, but in Ezekiel’s division it lay in the
furthest south and west. In Joshua’s division, Dan lay amid the Philistines,
but in Ezekiel’s it lay very near Damascus, where part of that tribe had once
occupied Laish at the source of the Jordan. Joshua placed Naphtali, Issachar,
and Zebulun in Galilee, north of Jerusalem, but Ezekiel places them south of
the holy city. In the first division, although the Levites and priests had
cities and suburbs in every tribe, they received no peculiar and distinct
allotment. But in the later division the firstfruits field of the priests,
Levites, and city, fifty miles wide, separated Judah from Benjamin and placed
Judah to the north and Benjamin to the south.

The fifth argument. From Ezekiel’s temple flowed a fountain of living water
which soon became a great river, whose banks were clothed with an abundance of
trees that flourished with perpetual foliage and monthly fruit. The waters of
this stream flowed into the Dead Sea and healed the fetid Asphaltite lake, so
that afterward fish lived there in abundance. But nothing of the kind appeared
in either the first or the second temple. There was no fountain at all there,
nor any stream at Jerusalem. Through hidden channels of aqueducts, as Josephus
relates, subterranean waters did indeed flow beneath the altar of burnt
offering to carry the filth of the sacrifices away into the brook Kidron. This
brook passed from the Mount of Olives through the Hinnom Valley along
Jerusalem’s eastern side and flowed through the wilderness of Judea into Lake
Asphaltites. But this brook was far from a river, and its banks were generally
bare of any shrubbery. To this day the Dead Sea remains most salty and foul,
killing every creature that enters it.

Finally, I argue as follows. In the firstfruits field a large portion
surrounding the portion of the priests, Levites, and city is assigned to the
prince. But nothing of the kind occurred in the time of the second temple. The
princes of the Jews after the Babylonian captivity who lived before the
Maccabees possessed very little majesty, very meager means, and few fields—in
fact, none distinct from those of the other tribes. And on the sides of the
holy portion no space at all remained for the princes in the land of Canaan.
For this square portion had on each side 25,000 reeds, that is, approximately
fifty Italian miles. Yet the land from Dan to Beersheba extended only 160
Italian miles, and where it was widest, even including the regions beyond the
Jordan, no more than sixty. From the Mediterranean Sea to the Jordan you will
scarcely find fifty miles, as will be clear to anyone who examines the maps of
Adrichomius, Fuller, or any other mapmaker of the Holy Land and compares with
them either the plans of Cornelius à Lapide or those of Richardson in Fuller,
drawn from Ezekiel. The latest English Annotators did indeed promise at the end
of Ezekiel 48 that a chorographical map would follow, but they did not fulfill
this promise. From such maps you will readily perceive that, along the length
of Canaan, between the sacred portion and Judah’s allotment to the north, and
the same portion and Benjamin’s allotment to the south, no interval was left
for the prince. For even in Ezekiel the limits of the land’s length are nearly
the same as in Joshua’s and Solomon’s chorography: Ezekiel does not proceed
beyond Damascus to the north, nor beyond the city Tamar and Kadesh-barnea to
the south. These, however, were the ancient boundaries of the Holy Land under
the first temple. For David, Solomon, and Jeroboam II extended their northern
and eastern boundary beyond Lebanon and Damascus as far as the Euphrates

[Printed p. 281 | IA leaf 311 | access PDF 311 | OCR object 310]

was extended, and the southern boundary not merely to the city Tamar—which
Sanctius wrongly wishes to be Jericho, the city of palms that was in the tribe
of Benjamin and
was further north than the whole tribe of Judah and even Jerusalem. À Lapide
and others also wrongly wish it to be Palmyra, which was near the Euphrates.
Rather, they extended it to the brook of Egypt: not the Nile, indeed, nor any
of its mouths, but the Shihor, which flowed into the Mediterranean Sea at
Rhinocolura, the Egyptian boundary of the tribe of Simeon. Now, if you give to
each of the twelve tribes a day’s journey or half of one, indeed even a sixth,
or almost any proportional share alongside the fifty-mile sacred portion, not
a foot will remain for the prince along the land’s length. For the whole land
was only 150 miles long, of which fifty were allotted to the priests, Levites,
and city. If you divide the remaining hundred among the twelve tribes, no more
than nine miles will remain for each. Nor does any more seem likely to remain
for the prince along the land’s breadth, for at its widest, even including the
regions beyond Jordan, it did not extend beyond sixty miles. And within the
Jordan and the Great Sea, outside of which boundaries Ezekiel does not place
his restored people, it never exceeded fifty, if you trust any maps we possess
today. Given these facts, not one palm or even a finger’s breadth of land will
remain for the prince along the width. The 25,000 reeds of the sacred allotment
completely consume those fifty miles. À Lapide’s claim that these reeds contain
no more than forty-five miles, so that something may remain for the prince by
this reckoning, arises from a computational error, for he assigns only nine
feet to Ezekiel’s reed when more than ten must be assigned to it.

So much for the first opinion; we shall touch briefly on the rest. The second
is that of the Jews. Although formerly, as Jerome says on the passage, they
held the first opinion, for some centuries now, as Lyranus, himself a Jew by
birth, teaches, they interpret these chapters of Ezekiel of a third temple and
a new earthly happiness which the Messiah will bring to the people of Israel.
We shall not linger in refuting this most depraved invention, for everything
adduced for the truth of the Christian religion bears upon it. Lyranus also,
on the passage, and à Lapide following him, briefly and forcefully stop the
Jews’ mouths more than sufficiently on this argument; we shall add only a few
*ad hominem* points.

> Marginalia, printed p. 281: *Argument 1 against the Jews’ opinion*.

First. The most learned rabbis deny that the temple is to be erected on earth,
and this, according to them, from an entirely divine tradition, although to us
it is a most false fable. For as Galatinus says in book 5, chapter 14, they
teach that on the day before his death Moses was taken up into heaven and
spoke with the coming Messiah, whom he saw standing with Aaron in the third
temple, which God was then constructing wholly from gems, not on earth but in
the highest heaven.

Second. Ezekiel’s prince was not the Messiah, for according to the rabbis and
the truth of the matter, the Messiah has neither children nor successors. But
Ezekiel assumes that his prince has both children and successors: rules are
prescribed to this prince concerning his sons’ inheritances, and provision is
made lest this prince, to the prejudice of his successors, alienate royal lands
in favor of his servants.

Third. The Jews imagine that a pseudo-Messiah will build the third temple, and
the Roman Catholics that Antichrist will; they place it within the holy city.
But Ezekiel’s temple was situated at least twenty-eight miles outside the city.

Fourth. The Jews and Roman Catholics affirm that the third temple will be much
more magnificent than both Solomon’s first and Zerubbabel’s second. But Ezekiel’s
temple was not smaller even than Zerubbabel’s second, much less Solomon’s first.
What Jerome says about the magnificence of Ezekiel’s temple beyond Solomon’s
must be understood of its courts and circuit, for the holy place itself did
not exceed forty cubits in length, twenty in breadth, or thirty in height. The
Holy of Holies in Solomon’s days did not exceed twenty cubits in length,
breadth, or height, 1 Kings 6:3. As for what we read in Ezra 6:3 about sixty
cubits in length and breadth, it must either be understood with Junius of
ordinary cubits, compared with which the sacred were double, or rather, with
the latest English Annotators, of the temple together with the buildings
immediately adjoining. In any event, Ezekiel’s temple was in no respect larger
than Solomon’s temple, either in length or breadth, as can be seen in Ezekiel
41:2 and there in Villalpandus, Maldonatus, à Lapide, Junius, the latest English
Annotators, and others. Consequently Ezekiel’s house was much smaller than
Herod’s temple if we believe Josephus, *Antiquities* 15:14, for there he asserts
that Herod constructed a new temple from the foundations, a hundred cubits
long, the same in breadth, and 120 in height.

It is true that the vestibule of Solomon’s temple, twenty cubits long and ten
wide, was 120 high, but the entire remainder of the house did not exceed
thirty cubits in height, while the wholly square Holy of Holies did not exceed
twenty.

Therefore I cannot put faith in Josephus, though an eyewitness, when he says
that Herod’s temple was so vast that in it the Holy of Holies itself, much less
the holy place, was

[Printed p. 282 | IA leaf 312 | access PDF 312 | OCR object 311]

120 cubits high and a hundred wide, especially since he asserts in the same
place that in his own day a full twenty cubits of the temple’s height had
subsided—a manifest fable. Who could believe that in the few years which
elapsed from Herod the Great to Josephus’s youth this house, founded upon solid
rock, subsided so many cubits, when we do not read that Solomon’s temple
subsided even one handbreadth throughout all the ages in which it stood?

> Marginalia, printed p. 282: *Josephus’s fable*.

Fifth. The Jews say that when the Messiah comes, all the Israelites throughout
the whole world will flock into the Holy Land and occupy the allotments
described by Ezekiel. But with Ezekiel’s measurements standing, this accords
with neither reason nor faith. For, as said above, the outer boundaries of
Canaan of which Ezekiel speaks do not exceed the ancient limits in David’s
time: Damascus to the north, Rhinocolura to the south, the Jordan to the east,
and the Mediterranean Sea to the west. Yet these boundaries would not provide
a dwelling place for even a tenth of the Jews known on earth today. Therefore
Ezekiel does not speak of this supposed condition of the Jews.

Finally, the pseudo-Messiah whom the Jews expect is assumed to be an extremely
wealthy and flourishing prince, who will possess very extensive lands of his
own without any plunder from another. But the estates of the prince of whom
Ezekiel speaks are very narrow, as we proved above. Nothing remains for him to
either the south or north, and very little to the east and west, unless you
pass far beyond the land’s boundaries described by Ezekiel.

The third opinion is the common one of Christians—Jerome, Gregory, Lyranus,
Junius, and almost all Protestants and Roman Catholics—who judge that these
chapters of Ezekiel should be understood mystically of the church’s times from
Christ’s birth to the present day. The substance of this opinion does not
displease us, but we find various things wanting in it as most explain it.

> Marginalia, printed p. 282: *What we find wanting in the common opinion*.

First. Where so long, exact, and detailed a historical account is set forth by
Ezekiel, it seems rather harsh to transform it into pure allegory and merely
mystical senses.

Second. The mystical senses here are so numerous and diverse that almost every
interpreter—ancient, modern, and intermediate—has his own peculiar applications,
in which he differs from everyone else.

Third. The mystical senses of many appear so foreign to reason, or at least to
Ezekiel’s intention, that they can win the approval of few sensible people.
Jerome wishes the gates’ five cubits to denote the five external senses, and
the reeds’ twenty-five cubits the spiritual senses, which are five times
superior to the bodily. Gregory proves that all these things of Ezekiel must
necessarily be understood mystically and spiritually because the first
measurements in chapter 40:5 are simply impossible. For there, he says, the
breadth and height of the whole building are said to be only one reed, while
the dimensions of the gates and courts are said to be many reeds, as though
the whole building containing these parts were much narrower than several of
its own parts. Certainly, with reverence for Gregory, this is a very poor
sophism. The building measured there by the angel is the enclosure or outermost
wall, which, although only one reed in breadth and height, could still readily
surround courts and buildings containing many thousands of reeds in their
various dimensions. Since Gregory began so absurdly at the threshold, we are
not at all surprised at his very many highly contorted inventions of mysteries
as he proceeds. Nevertheless, the ordinary gloss transcribes many things word
for word from Jerome’s and Gregory’s mystical senses. And in his *Postilla*
Lyranus devises other mysteries from his own brain, all of them as the very
sense intended by the prophet. This is surely great boldness, if not the
insolence of human ingenuity, and it makes most of the mystical senses here
utterly tasteless to my palate.

> Marginalia, printed p. 282: *Jerome’s and Gregory’s empty mysteries*.

À Lapide philosophizes here at great length about the works and power of
clerics over the people and even the highest princes by divine right. But in
this kind of thing Alcasar surpasses everyone. On Revelation 3:12 he wishes
the prophet, by the holy city *Jehovah Shammah*, to mean the city Rome, where
Jehovah dwells in the heart of the pontiff, his vicar on earth; and by the
temple separated from the city, he wishes the prophet to indicate the Roman
monks. Though these men have their palaces in the city’s central and loveliest
places, by their holy vows, institutions, and entire life they are far
separated from the city and the world. Indeed, Bibliander, Polanus, and others
of our men who descend in great detail to particular mystical senses often
offer things which, though most true in themselves and analogous to the faith,
would still scarcely escape censure for rashness by experts in these matters
if anyone affirmed them to be the Holy Spirit’s intention in that passage.

> Marginalia, printed p. 282: *Alcasar’s ridiculous mysteries*.

Christian times, but very late and final ones, when after the Jews’ return to
the faith, the church’s reformation on earth will reach its maturity. I do not
number here the millenarians or the millenarianizing, but other learned and
godly men who think that the Jews, converted to faith in Christ, are to be

there and enjoy their ancient habitations, where in great peace and even

second coming. These passages of Ezekiel, if any, seem to tend strongly toward

come into the full light, it will prove to have plowed with the millenarians’

removed.

The fifth opinion is that of those who refer Ezekiel’s temple, city, and
division of the land, together with John’s new Jerusalem in Revelation 21 and
22, to the church triumphant in heaven. We do not wish to descend here into an
examination of this opinion either, since a more proper and suitable place for
it remains in the questions on Revelation.

> Marginalia, printed p. 283: *Nor is the fifth*.

We have stated the view of others; our own, if you exclude the Jews, is
composed from the rest. This final vision of Ezekiel, sent by God to console
the Jews exiled in Babylon, appears to be referred to four times. First, it
contains much concerning the restoration of the second temple and the people’s
return to their deserted and desolate homeland. Second, among the many things
which cannot be applied to this time, a large part concerns the earlier times
of the Christian church. There God’s people, composed of Jews and gentiles,
possessed a much more august temple and greater gifts from God, both spiritual
and temporal, than it had attained in earlier ages. Third, many parts of the
vision seem to be referred to the church’s last times on earth, when the whole
house of Jacob and the entire body of Israelites, today buried in unbelief and
dense darkness, will rise from the dead and, together with the fullness of the
gentiles, enjoy those promises of God which appear not yet to have been
fulfilled. Fourth, those things in this vision and the other prophets which do
not fit the church on earth, whether of Jews, gentiles, or a mixture of both,
may safely and without danger of error be referred to the Jerusalem above.
But distinguishing the vision’s parts and adapting each to its own time belongs
to the interpreters of the passage, at least before it belongs to chronology;
in any event, we have neither the leisure nor the inclination for this work at
present.
