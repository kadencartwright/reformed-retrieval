## CHAPTER IX

### On the Judicial or Forensic Law

THE nature of our purpose does not permit us to discuss all the controversies
raised about this kind of divine laws. It will be enough for our purpose if we
show briefly how these laws are distinguished from the Moral ones.

*Judicial laws* were those which properly pertained to the Jewish commonwealth
as such and were enacted for the preservation of political order among them.
Accordingly, just as the Ceremonial laws were, as it were, determinations of
Moral laws in matters by which a human being is ordered toward God, so these
Forensic laws were, as it were, determinations of Moral laws in matters by which
human beings are ordered toward one another, as Thomas speaks in part 1 of the
second part, question 104, article 1.

In Tilenus, the division of these *Forensic* laws is drawn from the various
political orders enacted by this law. That Political Order is considered either
in the *bond of right*, or in the *reciprocal communication of benefit* among
those who are bound together by this bond. One use of this bond is Private, the
other Public. The former consists either in domestic discipline, which exists
between Husband and Wife, Parents and Children, and Master and Servant, or in
the Mutual association and daily intercourse of citizens. The Public use is
considered either in the bond between the Magistrate and his subjects, or in
the commercial relations of the Citizens and the entire people with
foreigners. According to these various uses of the bond, they divide these laws
into laws concerning *domestic discipline*; those concerning *civil society*;
those concerning *Magistrates and Subjects*; and finally those concerning *the
commercial relations of the Israelites with foreigners*, whether these were
peaceable—such as *travelers* passing through Canaan, *newcomers, resident
aliens, and citizens*, or *proselytes of righteousness*—or *enemies*. Thomas
also follows this account in part 1 of the second part, question 104, article 4.

They are also divided in various ways by reason of the benefit of association.
For this communication of benefit takes its origin either from *Public
Authority* or from the *will of private persons*. Judgments, penalties, and
whatever must duly be done by the Magistrate rest upon the former. Lawfully
acquired things, concerning which each person determines as he pleases, are
subject to the latter; hence arises the varied and manifold utility of
contracts.

Others reduce all these forensic laws to three headings, namely, *persons,
things, and actions*, since every right is said to consist in them.

Others divide all these laws into *Domestic* laws, such as the laws concerning
Spouses, Parents and Children, and Masters and Servants; and *Political* laws,
such as the laws concerning the Mutual Association of citizens, Magistrates and
Subjects, the order of Judicial Proceedings, Contracts, the Distinction of
ownerships, the Distribution of inheritances, Penalties, Military Service, and
Foreigners, and so forth.

But *Apollonius* judges it most appropriate “*to enumerate the political laws
according to the order of the precepts of the Decalogue, since they are
appendices of those same precepts and pertain to the observance of the moral
law*.” See which laws he assigns to each commandment of the Decalogue in his *On
the Law of God*, last chapter, thesis 2.

But it is of greater importance to investigate how and to what extent they are
distinguished from the Moral and—

[1674 logical printed p. 104 | IA leaf 122 | PDF p. 122]

—Ceremonial laws, and how or to what extent they are abolished or still remain
in force.

In this *Forensic* law one must distinguish between *Precepts* and
*Permissions*. Theologians affirm that none of its precepts was contrary to
*natural right*, because natural right is perpetual and immutable. But this
must be understood of *Right Natural to God*, not of *Right Natural to us*. For
the precept by which a person was bound to marry his brother's widow was
contrary to Right Natural to us, though not contrary to Right Natural to God.
In the place cited above, thesis 4, Apollonius distinguishes *Permission* into
*Full Permission* and *Less than full Permission*. The *former* gives a right
to do something altogether lawfully. The *latter* grants only impunity among
human beings and the right that no one else may lawfully impede what the law
permits. “From permission of the former kind,” he says, “no less than from a
precept, it follows that the matter with which the law deals is not contrary to
the law of nature.” But this right is natural only to God, not to us, as was
just said concerning the precept.

Although, as Apollonius has it in the place cited above, the laws of *Moses*, if
compared with the laws of other nations, possessed divine Perfection and were
beyond every exception—since they were enacted by the wisest, most just, and
most incorruptible Judge of the universe itself and of every individual who
dwells in this universe—and were most perfectly adapted to the Jewish polity,
it nevertheless does not follow from this that all of them would suit every
Commonwealth in the best way. The circumstances of the Jews are one thing, and
those of other Nations another. The Israelite People was σκληροτράχηλος,
“stiff-necked,” Acts 7:51. God often complains about this through Moses in
Exodus 32:9 and Deuteronomy 9:13 and 33:3. Hence, because of this
σκληροκαρδίαν, “hardness of heart,” certain things were permitted to them by
those laws, as Christ testifies in Matthew 19:8, which are not permitted to
others.

Furthermore, certain Judicial provisions were so connected and intertwined
with Ceremonial ones that, together with the Typical ceremonies themselves—as
Zepperus speaks in the place cited above, page 23—they are not only dead, but
would be deadly if they were resumed or restored to their former right.
Therefore, once these and similar provisions which properly pertained to the
time when Christ was awaited and the Gospel was not yet revealed have been
removed, no one will deny that it is lawful for any Commonwealth now to retain
the same laws or to enact others having the same political sense.

The laws enacted for the Israelite people purely insofar as they possessed the
land of Canaan and insofar as the *Messiah* was to arise from them cannot bind
other Nations. For the promises upon which certain political laws particularly
rested had not been given to other Nations. Indeed, some of those laws, such as
the permission to give a certificate of divorce and the command to marry the
widow of a brother who died without offspring in order to raise up offspring
for the brother, are now altogether unlawful, as may be seen from Matthew 5 and
19; Luke 16; and Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21. And the rationale of certain laws
for keeping the distinction of the tribes inviolably now has no place once the
Messiah has been exhibited.

Even those who contend rather vigorously for the non-abolition of these laws
distinguish *between those laws* which prescribe mutable things peculiar to the
Israelite nation for that time when their polity was still standing, and Those
which prescribe immutable things common to all Nations. They concede that the
*former* are abolished, but by no means the *latter*.

And it is indeed most true that some *Forensic laws* (so called) have a—

[1674 logical printed p. 105 | IA leaf 123 | PDF p. 123]

—Natural and Moral rationale, while others have a Special rationale and are,
namely, adapted and accommodated to the polity and the Jewish people. For
through *Moses* God prescribed something to them in common, insofar as they were
human beings subject to the common Law of Nature and to the Reason and Right
common to rational creatures. He also prescribed something peculiar to them,
just as individual Nations, Peoples, and even certain Cities have particular
and peculiar dispositions which make it necessary for them to have certain
peculiar laws that would be useless and perhaps even harmful to other societies
and polities.

Tilenus distinctly explains this in his *Syntagma*, part 1, disputation 45,
thesis 29 and following, in these words: “*The Jewish people is considered in
two ways: first, as a People; second, as Jewish—that is, as having been admitted
into a singular covenant with God and distinguished from the other Nations by
the singular signs of that covenant. The first consideration regards the
common foundation, that is, the very nature of humanity, according to which all
human beings, as human beings, are bound by a common law. The latter regards a
singular mode, not of nature but of grace, to which God calls the Church,
whether it consists of Jews, as formerly, or of Christians, as today. Just as
human beings, so things also are considered in two ways. Their use is either
common, pertaining indiscriminately to everyone by nature, or singular: by a
peculiar institution of God they are employed to seal the covenant of grace
for the sacred uses of human beings sanctified through the covenant. Thus the
one and same region of Canaan offered both uses to the Jews: first, the Natural
use which, as human beings, they received from its cultivation and habitation;
then the Supernatural or Sacramental use, insofar as they, as God's peculiar
emphyteutic tenants, used the produce of that soil as symbols and tokens of
spiritual promises. From this twofold consideration both of Things and of
Persons arises a twofold consideration of circumstances. For as the former are
occupied with works either of nature or of grace, so the latter too assume
either a common or a singular mode and rationale. From the diverse condition
and relation of all these things taken together there therefore arises a
twofold relation and mode of the right pertaining to all of them. Hence comes
the distinction between common and singular right. Of these, the former
pertains to the Jews as human beings, and the latter to the same people as
covenanters. Hence it is clear that whatever is common right in Moses ought to
be right of the most certain and constant kind among everyone, just as the
common rationale, which is always just and right and from which common right
proceeds, is certain and constant among everyone. But matters of singular right
in Moses no more bind us than the laws of the ancient Assyrians, Persians,
Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans, or the municipal laws of foreign cities.*”

Now because various Mosaic laws have something Moral, something Ceremonial, and
something Judicial in them, no one will reasonably say that they all, just as
they were enacted, bind us now. Everyone will admit that precise distinctions
must be made here. But to make this distinction clearly apparent by giving the
characteristic signs of these diverse rationales—here is Rhodes, here is the
leap. For various κριτήρια, “criteria,” are assigned by different people, and it
is rather difficult to cut correctly here.

As for the difference between those things in the forensic laws which are of
*moral and natural equity* and those which are of *particular right* binding the
Jews alone, some assert that the whole Forensic law is of common right if,
first, prudent Men even among other Nations have judged it equitable, just, and
necessary by natural reason and conscience; and then if it directly serves to
explain and confirm—

[1674 logical printed p. 106 | IA leaf 124 | PDF p. 124]

—some precept of the Decalogue or to defend or preserve either the Family, the
Commonwealth, or even the Church. Others hold that the Forensic laws are of
natural right even with respect to the very kinds of penalties which God
enacted to uphold the authority of the Decalogue. Accordingly, they hold that
Christian Magistrates are no less bound than the Jewish Magistrates were to
inflict upon transgressors those penalties which were inflicted formerly,
because offenses do not change with the times but retain one and the same
nature in every age. But this does not wholly please others. See Voetius in the
corollaries to the disputation *On Right and Justice*.

It appears to us that this should be said: Whatever in those laws was not
peculiar and proper to the Jews is of common right. And everything prescribed
to them was peculiar which was prescribed either insofar as they were the
Lord's Emphyteutic Tenants, or possessors of the land of Canaan, or insofar as
the Messiah was to arise from them. It follows from this that almost all the
properly Jewish Forensic laws had something ceremonial mixed into them. For the
land of Canaan itself was a typical land, and their National constitution was
in some manner typical. Thus not only their Ecclesiastical but also their
Political state had a special relation to the Christ who was to come, or was
ordered toward Christ, as Thomas speaks in part 1 of the second part, question
104, article 3, in the body.

As for the abrogation of these Forensic laws, Theologians hold various
Opinions. Some assert that they have been entirely abrogated; see their reasons
in Zepperus, in the place cited above, book 1, chapter 3. Others say that they
have for the most part been abrogated; Zepperus presents their reasons in the
same place, chapter 4. See also Maccovius, who defends this opinion in his
*Common Places*, chapter 12. Others distinguish between the *Forensic* precepts
which are of *common right* and those which are of *peculiar right*. They assert
that the *latter* have been abrogated while the *former* still bind. This is
indeed the more common and sounder opinion, which Zepperus presents in the
place cited above, chapter 5, in these words: “*Whatever in the Mosaic laws has
an immutable and perpetual rationale and nature binds all human beings by
common right, immutably and always, as if by an adamantine bond, in every time
and place. But whatever has a rationale and condition involving change no
longer holds the consciences of Christians constrained.*” Polyander also
follows this opinion in *Synopsis*, disputation 18, thesis 51. Calvin, Beza,
Zanchi, Apollonius, Maresius, Tilenus, Ames, and very many others also follow
it. Our British Confession as well, drawn up by the assembly of Theologians at
Westminster, says in chapter 19, section 4, that the Forensic laws expired
together with the political state of the Jews and bind no one now except to
the extent that their general equity requires. This is confirmed there by the
following testimonies of Scripture: Genesis 49:10; 1 Peter 2:13–14; Matthew
5:17, 38–39; and 1 Corinthians 9:8–10.

Apollonius, in the place cited above, thesis 8, proves that the Mosaic Forensic
laws are now abrogated as follows. 1. God had fixed a certain endpoint for the
observance of this law, namely, the coming of the Messiah, Genesis 49:10, where
the scepter and the legislator are joined and a definite time is fixed for the
duration of each. 2. Even under the Old Testament it did not bind foreign
Nations converted to God, as the examples of Jethro, the Queen of Sheba, Cyrus,
Darius, and others teach. 3. That polity has now been destroyed; therefore the
laws of its polity are removed. 4. We are bound to render obedience even to
pagan Magistrates in their kingdoms, Romans 13:5 and 1 Peter 2:13–14. 5. These
laws were not imposed upon Nations converted to the Christian faith in the
time of the Apostles, as is proved by the—

[1674 logical printed p. 107 | IA leaf 125 | PDF p. 125]

—examples of the *Centurion*, Matthew 8:13; the *Roman Soldiers*, Luke 3:14;
*Cornelius*, Acts 10:1; and *Sergius the proconsul*, Acts 13:12. They all
administered magistracy according to the prescription of Roman law and yet
were not reproved. 6. Paul subjected himself to Roman laws when he appealed to
Caesar, Acts 25:25. 7. The Gospel and Christ's kingdom neither abolish polities,
free citizens from obedience to magistrates, nor alter civil laws, but take
them up.

This rationale may be added to these: In the form of that Mosaic Polity, many
things had been adapted to the character and circumstances of that Nation,
land, and time which cannot now be observed, such as rash Divorces, Marriage to
a sister-in-law, the Draught of Jealousy, the Sale of children, Bondservants,
and similar things. And, as was said before, many of the *forensic* provisions
were typical; many were permitted by God's forbearance in the pedagogy of the
Jewish polity and people and in the infancy and childhood of that Church. They
cannot now be restored without scandal.

These rationales indeed prove abundantly that none of the *Judicial provisions*
binds us now. Yet it must not be said that they must all be discarded so as to
be of no use among us now. For then, as Zepperus says in the place cited above,
chapter 5, page 31, the constitution of divine worship and of the Church,
decorum and order in the external worship of God, the duties of love, the
administration of justice, punishments for crimes, and judicial proceedings
will all perish. Nor must it be said that these are sufficiently supplied from
the law of nature and from human laws and constitutions. For, as he says in the
same place, the notions and principles of Natural law have been greatly
corrupted by our evil and fault, and there is now such imperfection in us that
these principles too have been greatly weakened. The more we descend from them
by reasoning to particular or singular conclusions, the more we slip into vile
and shameful excesses and defects contrary to the very law of nature and wander
off the path. Hence Aristotle ranked private vengeance among works of
magnanimity and εὐτραπελίαν, “wit,” or pleasantries among the virtues, contrary
to Ephesians 5:4. And Zeno placed mercy among the vices. Therefore, as Zepperus
says in the same place, page 24: Who does not see that it was necessary for
certain hedges and barriers, as it were, to be set around us, lest we
thoughtlessly wander from that natural law and, while professing ourselves
wise, become fools and exchange God's truth for a lie? For this reason God
brought a law higher than nature near to us and by his grace raised us above
nature, giving the moral law and transferring its precepts, judgments, and
general maxims into certain, familiar, and accessible use by determinations,
expositions, amplifications, and applications to various cases and causes in
human life, affairs, and actions.

By this middle opinion the arguments of those who hold that the *Forensic laws*
remain sound and intact are abundantly answered. For no window is opened to
Heresies, Idolatry, Blasphemies, or the Destruction of the Church and Ministry.
Rather, the general equity of the laws concerning the punishment of False
Prophets, Blasphemers, Idolaters, and so forth will prepare the way and lay the
foundations for punishing such persons now under the Gospel. By this rationale
a solid foundation is laid for exterminating Barbarity, cultivating Justice,
and so forth. Nor are punishments for crimes removed, although the same
punishments are not retained in every circumstance.

As for the objection that, once those laws are abolished, a magistrate cannot
be certain in his conscience that he pleases God in his political government—

[1674 logical printed p. 108 | IA leaf 126 | PDF p. 126]

—in enacting laws and imposing penalties, Zepperus and Apollonius answer that
the magistrate pleases God when he establishes all these things from the Moral
law, the Law of Nature, and the fundamental Laws of the Commonwealth. For God
directs the magistrate in all his duties through the general rules of equity,
justice, love, and prudence. And since the forensic laws of every nation are
appendices of the moral law, agree with uprightness, and are adapted to the
safety and well-being of each Commonwealth, the Magistrate who directs his
government in every particular according to those rules acts not with a
wavering conscience but with an assured mind and according to the will of God,
who directs the magistrate's power by those rules.

The words of Calvin in *Institutes*, book 4, chapter 20, section 16, also pertain
here: “*What I have said will become plain if, as is fitting, we consider two
things in all laws: the law's constitution and the equity upon which the
constitution itself is founded and rests. Because equity is natural, it can
only be one and the same for everyone; therefore, the same equity, according to
the kind of matter, ought to be set forth by every law. Because constitutions
depend in part upon certain circumstances, it does no harm for them to differ,
provided that they all alike aim at the same goal of equity. Now since the law
of God which we call moral is clearly nothing other than a testimony of
natural law and of that conscience which God has inscribed upon human minds,
the entire rationale of the equity of which we now speak is prescribed in it.
Therefore it alone ought also to be the goal, rule, and boundary of all laws.
Whatever laws are framed according to that rule, directed toward that goal,
and limited by that boundary, there is no reason for us to disapprove of them,
however they may differ either from the Jewish law or among themselves.*”

To the objection that an insult is thus done to the law of God enacted through
Moses, Calvin answers in the same place that it is “*utterly vain. For other
laws are not preferred to it when they are approved as more suitable, not by a
simple comparison, but in relation to the condition of time, place, and nation.
Nor is a law abrogated which was never enacted for us. The Lord did not deliver
it through Moses to be promulgated among all Nations and to flourish
everywhere. Rather, when he took the Jewish nation into his trust, protection,
and guardianship, he also willed to be its particular legislator. And, as was
fitting for a wise legislator, in enacting laws he took account of its singular
circumstances.*”

But all these things will be clearer if by abolished political laws we
understand only those which were peculiar and proper to the Jews—that is, those
prescribed from a peculiar regard to their singular condition, insofar as they
possessed that typical land and Christ was to arise from them. Anyone can see
that the arguments of Maccovius now have no force. For there will be certain
judicial laws delivered in the word which are, namely, moral and of common
right. A definite rule will be given even for imposing penalties.

From what has been said it is abundantly clear how this kind of law agrees with
the Ceremonial law and how it differs from it and from the Moral law.

The Judicial and Ceremonial law agree: 1. in their *efficient cause*, because
each has God as its author; 2. in their *ultimate end*, because each was enacted
for God's glory; 3. in their *common matter*, because each is mutable; and 4.
in their *external form*, because each has its binding force from the mere
institution of the legislator.

They differ: 1. in their *proximate end*, for the Ceremonial law regards God
immediately, while the—

[1674 logical printed p. 109 | IA leaf 127 | PDF p. 127 | ends before CAP. X]

—Judicial law regards one's neighbor; 2. in their *proper matter*, for the
latter concerns common affairs, while the former concerns sacred ones; 3. in
their *internal form* and manner of signifying, for the former was instituted
primarily and in itself to signify something, while the latter does this
secondarily and by consequence, insofar as “all things happened typically” to
the Jews, 1 Corinthians 10:11; see Thomas, part 1 of the second part, question
104, article 2, in the body; 4. in their Mode of Abrogation: the former became
deadly after the Gospel was promulgated, while the latter merely became dead
after the polity of Moses was destroyed, insofar as it was free from
Ceremonies.

This *Forensic law* also differs from the *Moral law*: 1. The former prescribed
particular things peculiar to one nation, but the latter prescribes things
common to all nations and every time. 2. The former was enacted concerning
Judgments and Political affairs, but the latter concerning Morals. 3. The
former does not bind now, but the latter holds everyone bound. 4. The former's
rationale, cause, and foundation lay in some peculiar condition of the Jews;
this cannot be said of the latter. 5. The former serves and ministers as a
handmaid to the latter insofar as it regards the uprightness, society, and
tranquility of the human race. 6. The latter is perfect, requiring not only
external obedience but also the sanctity of the whole human nature and its
conformity to the rule of Justice and Holiness; the former, like the Ceremonial
law, speaks only of external obedience. 7. The latter is written in the book of
nature and can still be read there now, at least in some measure; the former
cannot. 8. Right Reason aided by God's word can perceive the latter, but not
the former, because it depends purely upon the legislator's institution. 9. The
former contains certain exceptions from the latter, such as the law concerning
a man's marriage to his widowed sister-in-law.

The *Socinians* hold that this law too has been abrogated because it not only
accords poorly with Christ's Kingdom but in part conflicts with it. In that
Kingdom the highest love, patience, gentleness, modesty, and mercy are
commanded, to such an extent that it is unlawful to demand even an eye for an
eye and so forth, or to avenge an injury inflicted upon us in any way, Matthew
5:20, 38–40. But our theologians answer that the Forensic laws, which were just
and equitable, do not conflict with those virtues and formerly accorded
perfectly with them when those virtues were also required. In Matthew 5 Christ
forbids only what the law itself forbade, namely, that a private person should
avenge himself, which the Pharisees judged lawful. See Romans 12:19–21;
Deuteronomy 32:35; Proverbs 25:22; and Leviticus 19:18. See more on this matter
in Apollonius, *On the Law of God*, pages 226–227 and following. On page 449 he
says: “*Therefore, the cause which the adversaries put forward—that the laws
accorded poorly with Christ's kingdom—is false. For since Christ's kingdom is
not of this world, no forensic laws, as such, accord with it. Nor can
it be imagined that Roman or Saxon laws accord with Christ's kingdom better
than Mosaic ones. Only because of their connection with the legal ceremonies
can they not, once those ceremonies are abrogated, remain in force among
Christians under this Evangelical economy of Christ's kingdom. But once they
ceased to be practicable because the Jewish polity was abolished and the
ceremonial worship removed, they can no more be judged to have conflicted in
themselves with Christ's kingdom than can the Jewish polity itself and the
ceremonies themselves, in whose abolition the laws too obtained their own
abrogation.*” See also what he says on the following page in response to the
other rationales of the Socinians.
