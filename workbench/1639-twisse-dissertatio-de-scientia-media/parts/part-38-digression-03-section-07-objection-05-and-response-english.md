NAVARRETE.

Fifthly, God now knows Antichrist as he is. But Antichrist is future; therefore God knows Antichrist
as future. Therefore he does not know him through intuitive knowledge.

RESPONSE.

God knows Antichrist intuitively for the time at which he is to exist, on the side of the thing
known; but he knows Antichrist as present throughout all eternity.

RESPONSE.

That response is by no means suitable. The argument concludes that God knows Antichrist as future,
because it is assumed that Antichrist truly is future. Without doubt God knows things as they are;
otherwise he would err rather than know. You also appear to concede that God knows Antichrist as
future, yet you do not willingly go so far as to profess this openly. You do not say precisely that
God intuitively knows Antichrist as future for the time, as you say that God knows Antichrist as
present in eternity. Yet when you say that God knows Antichrist intuitively for the time when he is
to exist, and that time is future, it is just as if you had said in so many words that God knows
Antichrist intuitively for a future time—that is, knows Antichrist as future. For this is to know
Antichrist as he is.

Yet lest some storm excessively harmful to your opinion arise from this, as though fearing some
evil, you add—so as to blunt the reader’s acuity—that this occurs “on the side of the thing known.”
It is as if Antichrist could be known by God in two ways: either on the side of the thing known or
in some other manner about which you judge it best to remain silent. When you add that God knows
Antichrist as present throughout all eternity, you mention no other member of the distinction
properly opposed to the former—say, on the side of the thing not known, or on the side of anything
else. Yet from what you dictated in response to the immediately preceding argument, one may infer
that the other member of the distinction is “on God’s side.” There you had said that God’s
production of things, if considered as it stands on God’s side, is measured by eternity. In
considering that answer of yours, I was wretchedly tormented as I looked about for what other member
of the distinction could finally be invented and properly opposed to this member “on God’s side.”
Perhaps it occurs here. With your characteristic prudence, you scatter the mangled members of your
distinctions separately, just as Medea once treated her son. Unless I am mistaken, you do this to
keep the reader’s mind suspended, divided, and astonished. Come then, let us fit these pieces
together into one integral distinction and see how beautifully it coheres. Your full opinion
therefore proceeds as follows: God intuitively knows Antichrist as future for the time, on the side
of the thing known; but he knows Antichrist as present throughout all eternity, on the side of God
who knows.

But in this manner the side of the thing known and the side of God who knows are most unsuccessfully
separated. If God knows Antichrist as future for the time, both God who knows and the thing known
must play their parts in this cognition. If he knows Antichrist as present throughout eternity,
here too God must act as knower and Antichrist must play his part as the thing known. Neither
cognition takes greater account of God as knower than of Antichrist as the thing known.

Moreover, the distinction is universally absurd by which, when God is said to know something, he is
said to do so either on the side of the knower or on the side of the thing known. I can scarcely
believe that such a careless manner of distinguishing ever became established anywhere in any
author before Navarrete introduced it—not to illuminate the truth but rather to blunt the reader’s
judgment lest he perceive the truth. Perhaps the qualifications “on the side of God who knows” and
“on the side of the thing known” are to be referred not to God’s cognition of the thing but to the
thing’s existence, insofar as it is said either to be future in time or present in eternity. Or
perhaps the thing is said, on its own side, to be known by God as future in time, while God is said,
on God’s side, to know the same thing as present in eternity. Neither interpretation proceeds
successfully; all are equally absurd. If a thing’s existence is said to be future in time, it is no
more future in that manner on the thing’s side than on God’s side: it is to be produced on the
thing’s side and God is to produce it on God’s side. If it is present in eternity, it is no more
present in that manner on God’s side than on the thing’s side. These qualifications are universally
absurd and introduced beyond every reason and example. Nor is a thing known by God as present in
eternity more on God’s side than on the thing’s side. Nor is the thing’s existence future in time
more on the thing’s side than on God’s side. Considered universally, these qualifications savor of
no sobriety and cannot be defended by the authority of any

argument. Let us therefore now examine it.
