ROMANS 4:3

Τί γὰρ ἡ γραφὴ λέγει; Ἐπίστευσεν δὲ Ἀβραὰμ τῷ Θεῷ, καὶ ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς
δικαιοσύνην.

For what does Scripture say? Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to
him as righteousness. — Vulgate

For what does Scripture say? Abraham believed God, and it was credited to
him as righteousness. — Beza

We previously discussed this clause's connection with what precedes and
follows in our general analysis of the passage. Only these further points
need now be noted.

First, the verse can refer directly to verse 1 and provide a new
confirmation of its proposition: Abraham did not find righteousness or
justification by works. Verse 2 proves this because he lacked boasting
before God, which he could have possessed if justified by works. The present
verse proves it by a second argument from Scripture's testimony: faith was
credited to him as righteousness. Faith and works stand opposed in
justification in Romans 11:6, because crediting faith speaks only of grace,
whereas works demand wages from debt. Bucer and Beza seem to follow this
analysis.

Second, it may fit Paul's thought and the order of his words even better to
join the verse with verse 2's immediately preceding words. Abraham had
nothing about which to boast before God because his faith was credited to
him as righteousness. Since that crediting was wholly gracious, it cut off
every occasion for boasting. Calvin therefore calls the verse the proof of
the minor: “If Abraham is justified because he embraces God's goodness by
faith, it follows that he has no glory, for he brings nothing of his own
except acknowledgment of misery, which seeks mercy,” and so forth.

In this way the chapter's first five verses form a chain in their textual
order, each later link grasping and drawing the former. The whole argument
proceeds as follows:

Abraham did not find righteousness and was not justified by works. This was
the first proposition in verse 1.

That he was not justified by works is proved by his lacking boasting before
God in verse 2.

That he lacked boasting or any matter for it is proved from Scripture's
testimony in verse 3: his faith was credited to him as righteousness from
pure grace.

[1679 printed p. 197 | IA leaf 205]

Verses 4 and 5 prove by an evident reason that faith was credited to him from
grace alone. Works claim wages from debt and demanding justice in verse 4,
whereas faith, believing in the one who justifies the ungodly, is credited
as righteousness to the nonworker wholly from grace in verse 5.

Let us now explain the present verse in itself. Certain smaller matters may
advance like light-armed soldiers in the first line; after this lighter
skirmish we will proceed to weightier, pitched battle.

First, as we saw similarly in verse 1, the words take interrogative form:
“For what does Scripture say?” As some have noted, and I do not deny, this
conveys confidence in the truth. I would add that Paul speaks as though
deliberating and consulting about the matter he is to prove and appeals to
Scripture's authority and testimony. In matters of faith resting on divine
revelation, this must always be done reverently: “To the law and the
testimony” (Isaiah 8:20); “These things are written that you may believe”
(John 20:31).

Here one must always hear and be heard—not what enthusiasm dreams, raves,
and madly imagines or what human reason devises, but, as here, “What does
Scripture say?” Faith must rest upon Scripture because it is the word of
faith (Romans 10:8). Or as was formerly asked, “What has the Lord answered?
What has the Lord spoken?” (Jeremiah 23:35). If people come to us in the
Lord's name, let them produce sealed tablets and display these credentials,
so that while professing to be ambassadors they may be recognized as acting
according to their commission. Haggai 1:13 expressly calls Haggai “the
Lord's ambassador in the Lord's commission”—according to the terms of his
commission. If they do not speak according to this word, no morning light is
in them.

The second observation concerns “What does Scripture say?” Through
personification, Paul attributes personhood and speech to Scripture. This is
common both among secular authors and in sacred Scripture: John 7:38, 42;
19:37; Romans 9:17; 10:11; 11:2; Galatians 3:8; 4:30; 1 Timothy 5:18; and
James 4:5 all speak of what Scripture says. Although Grotius sometimes
explains this as “it is said in Scripture,” elsewhere he admits that Paul
speaks of Scripture as a person—indeed, as God himself.

In such expressions “Scripture” stands for God, and “Scripture says” and
“God says” amount to the same. “Scripture says” means God speaks in
Scripture, because Scripture is God's word. Scripture consequently not only
speaks but foresees, as in Galatians 3:8: “Scripture, foreseeing, proclaimed
beforehand.” Properly this belongs not to Scripture but to God's
foreknowledge. What belongs to God speaking in Scripture is attributed to
Scripture, and Scripture is understood to say what God says in it.

[1679 printed p. 198 | IA leaf 206]

This speaks clearly about the faith and reverence owed sacred Scripture—the
same as is owed God speaking in it. We give a king's written diploma the
same credit as the king. A person and the letters written by his hand possess
the same truthfulness and should receive the same trust. Love, fear, faith,
and reliance, which properly belong to God as parts of worship and our due
obedience, are consequently also attributed to Scripture or God's word
contained in it: when Scripture speaks, God speaks.

This strips away the deception of enthusiasts who are always talking of the
Spirit speaking to and within them. After Scripture was written, God's
Spirit speaks in Scripture. If he inspires something more immediately, it
is always according to Scripture. When they market something outside or
contrary to Scripture as proceeding from the Spirit or spoken to them, we
may certainly know it comes not from the Spirit of faith (2 Corinthians
4:13), holiness (Romans 1:4), and truth (John 14:19), but from a Pythonic
spirit (Acts 16:16), as we must fear often occurs today, or from the spirit
of the world (1 Corinthians 2:12) and error (1 John 4:6). “To the law and
the testimony; if they do not speak according to this word,” and so forth
(Isaiah 8:20).

This can also silence the blasphemous Jesuits who argued at the Regensburg
Colloquy: “The judge of controversies must speak. Scripture does not speak
but is a mute letter. Therefore it cannot be the judge of controversies.”
They consequently cry that an external judge, the pope, must suffice. Anyone
who calls Scripture mute is utterly deaf, since he does not hear it here and
elsewhere saying, speaking, and even crying aloud. Unless he repents, he will
hear it pronounce judgment at the last judgment. Thus Scripture spoke to
Pharaoh in Romans 9:17 and will one day speak similarly to other unbelievers.
Human laws are vocal enough; will divine laws be silent? They certainly
speak clearly, if not to ears, then to consciences.

But let us proceed: “For what does Scripture say? Abraham believed.”

Our third observation is that Paul says Abraham, not Abram, although the
Genesis passage from which he draws his testimony does not. Genesis 15:6
reads, “Abram believed the Lord, and it was reckoned to him as
righteousness.” He had not yet been called Abraham. The additional letter
was inserted into his name later, in Genesis 17:5.

Long before Paul wrote, however, the new and enlarged name Abraham had
become renowned, together with its enlarged glory. Paul therefore calls him
by this more magnificent and universally current name. I think Scripture
never again calls him Abram after God says in Genesis 17:5, “Your name shall
no longer be called Abram, but you shall be called Abraham.”

Jerome takes the inserted letter he to be the chief letter occurring twice
in the divine Tetragrammaton, implying that Jesus the Messiah, who truly is
Jehovah, would be born from Abraham.

[1679 printed p. 199 | IA leaf 207]

More likely, however, the he is inserted from *hamon*, “multitude,” to
signify that Abraham, formerly Abram or “exalted father,” would now be father
of a multitude of nations. God himself explains the changed name in the
immediately following words: “Your name shall no longer be called Abram,
but your name shall be Abraham, because I have made you father of a
multitude of nations.” Scripture always uses this name afterward, lending
greater credit to the divine prediction and command.

Jacob similarly hears, “Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but
Israel” (Genesis 32:28), repeated in Genesis 35:10. Yet Scripture often
afterward calls both him and his descendants Jacob. Junius therefore renders
the words, “Your name shall no longer be called Jacob alone,” supplying a
common ellipsis. Other interpreters do not supply it this way but admit it
is present. They understand the words comparatively: he would not so much
be called Jacob as be distinguished by the more illustrious name and title
Israel. This also concerns the new name's greater frequency, since at least
his descendants are called Israel much more often than Jacob.

After his name's change, however, Scripture never again calls Abraham Abram.
Thus Paul, even while citing a passage where he was called Abram, identifies
him by the name commonly used when Paul wrote.

This may seem slight and scarcely worth explaining, but it helps elucidate
many passages where biblical writers narrate events long before their own
time and give things and places the names current when they wrote, not when
the narrated events occurred. Interpreters commonly call this prolepsis. I
would rather, if the term may be stretched, call it metalepsis, since they
use present words for ancient things.

Genesis 21:14 calls a wilderness Beersheba by the name current when Moses
wrote, though it had not received that name when the narrated events
occurred, as verse 31 shows. Joshua 15 calls cities Hebron and Debir by names
current when it was written, though during the narrated events they were
Kirjath-arba and Kirjath-sepher. Second Kings 16:6 uses the name Jews, which
was not known when the events happened but was when the book was written.

Many similar examples could be accumulated, but excessive proof of an
unnecessary point would be vain. The learned Masius's general assertion may
stand for particular examples: “The almost perpetual custom of sacred
historians is to use all the most recent names and pass over the more ancient
in silence, except those of celebrated places.”

[1679 printed p. 200 | IA leaf 208]

Thus the person called Abram in the cited passage is called Abraham by Paul,
who cites him by the name commonly known when Paul wrote.

The fourth expression concerning this Abraham is “he believed God.” Its
explanation would cause us little difficulty. Peter Lombard famously draws
from several Augustinian passages a distinction between believing in God,
believing God, and believing God to exist.

To believe God is to believe that what he says is true. Evil people do this,
and we believe a person without believing in that person. To believe God to
exist is to believe he is God; evil people and even demons do this. To
believe in God is to love by believing, go to him by believing, and adhere
to him by believing.

The scholastics follow Lombard as master. Aquinas holds these to be a single
act of one habit diversified with respect to the material object—believing
God; the formal object—believing God who speaks; and the intellect's motion
by the will—believing in God. The equally subtle Durandus rejects Thomas's
subtlety and contends they are several acts, not one. Let them quarrel among
themselves; we need not be so fine-spun.

It is enough to say that Augustine's threefold distinction became peculiar
to the Latin church. Latins say “we believe God,” with “to exist” understood,
as James 2:19 explicitly says, “You believe that God exists.” Greek
theologians, so far as I know, do not mention this third form but sometimes
distinguish the remaining two: believing God, or believing about God, and
believing in God.

Augustine says: “To believe in God is more than believing God, for one
should often believe another person without believing in him. To believe in
God is to adhere by believing so as to cooperate well with God who works
good.” Eucherius similarly says: “Believing God and believing in God differ.
We rightly believe Paul and Peter, but must not believe in Peter or Paul and
transfer the Lord's honor to servants. Anyone can believe another person,
but know that you owe believing in someone only to the Majesty. Even the
devil is found believing that God exists; only the one who devoutly hopes in
God is proved to believe in him.” Many of our common and most learned
writers, including Chamier, follow them.

In explaining the matter, we must consider either the realities contained
under these phrases or the phraseology itself. As realities, they differ in
subject, object, and principle. Believing in God is an act of will directed
to the good. Believing God and believing that God exists are acts of
intellect directed to truth. Believing God has God as the formal object on
account of which I believe. Believing that God exists has God as the
material object or thing believed, not as the motive of believing.

Although believing in God virtually contains the other two, the realities
are distinct. Someone can be thoroughly persuaded that God exists without
faith properly so called, merely from natural light and demonstration.
Someone can also believe that what God says is true by merely historical
faith. Truly believing in God belongs only to saving faith and, as part of
divine worship, is owed to God alone.

[1679 printed p. 201 | IA leaf 209]

Our writers truly affirm this with Augustine, Eucherius, and Rufinus, who
writes on the Creed: “The Creator is separated from creatures and divine
things from human. Thus when faith is ordered toward deity, the Creed says
‘in God the Father,’ ‘in Jesus Christ his Son,’ and ‘in the Holy Spirit.’ In
the other articles, which treat not deity but creatures and mysteries, the
preposition ‘in’ is not added. We are not said to believe ‘in the holy
church,’ but to believe the holy church.” This careful distinction of speech
religiously observed in the Creed should not be despised or carelessly
neglected as some recent writers do.

If we consider phraseology alone, I readily grant that Scripture sometimes
uses the Greek “believe God” and “believe in God,” and the corresponding
Hebrew phrases, interchangeably. Drusius and others repeatedly observe this,
and the matter itself proves it. Two points should nevertheless be noted
from Scriptural usage.

First, the Hebrew phrase with the preposition beth is used not only of God
but also creatures. Exodus 14:31 says, “They believed in the Lord and in
Moses his servant”; 2 Chronicles 20:20 says, “Believe or trust in the Lord
your God, and believe or trust his prophets.” The same expression is used of
believing in God, Moses, and the prophets, though in far different senses,
so Roman Catholics must not seize from Exodus an occasion to establish
idolatry. Piscator rightly says they trusted principally and essentially in
Jehovah as almighty God and favorable Father, but secondarily and
incidentally in Moses as God's faithful servant. Paul supplies several
examples of such trust in people (2 Corinthians 2:3; 7:16; Galatians 5:10; 2
Thessalonians 3:4). Thus we believe in prophets as God's ambassadors, in
miracles as motives of faith (Psalm 78:32), and in God's precepts as the
object of faith (Psalm 119:66).

Second, and especially important here, Genesis says Abraham “believed in the
Lord.”

[1679 printed p. 202 | IA leaf 210]

Yet Paul, following the Septuagint, renders this “he believed God.” Believing
God and believing in God can therefore have the same force. Here “believing
God” must mean believing in God, since Paul treats not merely historical
faith but saving and justifying faith. That is the subject of his present
argument.
