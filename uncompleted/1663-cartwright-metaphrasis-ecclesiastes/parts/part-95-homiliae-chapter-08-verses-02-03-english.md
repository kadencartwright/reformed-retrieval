VERSE 2. Do not depart from his presence in agitation; do not persist in an
evil matter, for he would do whatever he wished.

This is another command: if someone, overcome by agitation, has strayed from
the path or from his purpose, he should nevertheless take care not to harden
his mind in defection, but should immediately retrace his step so that he
returns to his former obedience. Solomon gives the reason from the attendant
danger, which is confirmed by the cause of that danger, namely the power to do
whatever he wills. “Fury,” he says, “provides arms.” Kings incensed with anger
are therefore equipped with an abundance of arms, and they will not lack the
means with which to punish such people. He says, however, “according as they
will,” rather than “according as guilt and violated loyalty deserve.” For just
as subjects, stirred by an agitation of mind, withdraw from duty, so Kings,
made more violently angry by this affront, often rush headlong upon offenders.

VERSE 3. Wherever the king’s word is, there is dominion; and who may say to
him, “What are you doing?”

He proves from the efficient cause that the King will act according to his
will—namely, from his power, which is joined to his word, the indication and
messenger of his will. This is also amplified from the territory subject to
him: wherever in his entire empire and dominion the King’s word or command is,
there are power and forces to execute what is commanded. This is so not only
when you are at court or near the royal residence, but even when you are
situated far from the King’s presence. Nothing contrary to obedience to the
King should be admitted in the hope that you will do it with impunity because
you are separated from him by a long interval of distance.

Daniel describes this power of a King in the person of Nebuchadnezzar, Daniel
5:19, where he says that Nebuchadnezzar was a most mighty King: he put to death
whomever he wished, kept alive whomever he saw fit, raised up whomever he
wished, and likewise put down whomever he wished. Even in a foreign dominion,
such is often a King’s influence among foreigners that he can call people back
even from another ruler’s territory. Thus it may truly be said of Kings that
they are all long-armed and also endowed with long ears, and that the name and
appellation of gods are not attributed to them without reason, 1 Kings 18:10;
Jeremiah 26:20–23.

But if departing from loyalty to princes is not free from grave danger,
because they are equipped with great power by which to put their will into
effect, how great will be the danger in which we are placed if we defect from
loyalty and obedience to God, who is armed with such great and immeasurable
power? What madness, then, what stupor of mind, what lethargy is this: for you
to be afraid of the power of princes yet not dread the fear of God, whose word
alone is able to lay low this entire world that he made, even if he should
employ no other instruments of his power? What insanity is this, as though
human beings could punish more harshly than God or reward our obedience more
abundantly and generously?

The reason is that we do not see God with the bodily eyes with which we look
upon a king. We apprehend the King’s sword and rods with our bodily

senses, with which we cannot see God’s weapons and the agents of his
vengeance.

He amplifies this power of the King from its opposite, the powerlessness of
his subjects. They do not dare even to mutter against the King, lest he inflict
some more refined and fearsome punishment or kind of torment. Or if they do
speak against him, as sometimes happens, they nevertheless beat the air, for
the King does not allow himself to be called to account. This can be affirmed
with infinitely greater truth about our powerlessness if a contest should be
made with God’s omnipotence.

Hence what Kings—and especially the Pope and Roman Pontiff—unjustly claim for
themselves, namely that they are unaccountable, the Holy Spirit attributes to
God by the very best right, Job 9:12. Solomon says this of the King, that he is
unaccountable, not because it is lawful for him to act in this way by right,
but in order to curb his subjects’ audacity in resisting and to teach them to
yield even what is theirs by right rather than litigate with the king, lest in
shaking off a wooden yoke they bring an iron one upon themselves.

*Translator’s notes:*

The Greek `ἀνυπεύθυνοι` and `ἀνυπεύθυνον` mean “not liable to answer” or “not
accountable.” The translation uses “unaccountable” in this juridical sense,
not in the modern sense of “inexplicable.”

The Latin’s `metuere vobis à principum potentia` is irregular. The translation
renders its evident direct-address sense, “for you to be afraid of the power
of princes,” without silently altering the transcription.
