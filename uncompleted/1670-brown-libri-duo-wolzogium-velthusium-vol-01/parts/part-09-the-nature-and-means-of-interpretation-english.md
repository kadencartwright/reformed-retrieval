XVIII. Since, therefore, the principal question concerns that thing or norm
which guides in interpretation and by which an interpretation must be
examined, it will be worth knowing the general nature of that thing. To define
it for us, *Wolzogen* says on pages 51–52: *The whole question must be carefully
distinguished from two others which, because they are very closely related,
are not always distinguished equally well by everyone. For when Scripture is
under discussion, it can first be asked why we should give assent to those
things contained in Scripture; second, why we believe these writings to be
Scripture; and third, why we determine this and no other to be the meaning of
Scripture.* Answer: I freely concede that these questions must be
distinguished, and I know no Reformed writer who would not also concede it.
Indeed, all our writers, in treating this controversy concerning Scripture
against the Pontificians, distinguish these questions and examine them
separately. For they treat the first when the question is raised concerning
the authority of Scripture; the *se-*

[Page 95 | ONB canvas 149 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000149]

*-cond* when they dispute concerning the canon or the canonical books; and the
third when they speak concerning the Interpreter. Who does not see these
questions distinctly explained and treated in their writings? What is it,
then, that *Wolzogen*, who wishes to appear as the sole most perspicacious
examiner, most solid investigator, and most distinct disputant concerning this
whole question, continually brands the Orthodox at every seized opportunity
and accuses them of ignorance and lack of skill, as if they did not know how
properly to distinguish these questions? But afterward he says that the last
two questions are of a nature far different from the first. *For*, he says,
*they do not touch God himself so closely. They relate to a human being and to
human matters, as the third does; or they depend upon the very constitution of
the subject matter being treated and upon other signs, as the second does.* We
can concede this too: namely, that the latter two questions do not touch God
as properly as the first does. Yet *Wolzogen* cannot deny that they also touch
God in this way. For just as the one who errs in the first question denies
God's authority itself, the one who errs in the second calls God's voice
itself into question and rejects it, and the one who hesitates in the third
doubts the true meaning of God's word. But these things contribute little to
the substance of the matter unless he wills the answer to the second question
to depend solely upon those marks of divinity which appear in those books,
and the answer to the third upon usage of speech. He insinuates this not
obscurely when he speaks as follows on page 54: *Therefore the command of a
Prince must be obeyed because he is a Prince; likewise, the divine Scriptures
must be believed because they are divine.—We judge that this command belongs
to the Prince from the seal affixed to it and from other signs. In the same
way, from the image of God impressed upon the writings we judge them to be
Scripture, and we employ other circumstances which confirm this. Finally, the
words and the context of the Discourse must prove that this, and no other, is
the meaning of the edict. The like obtains in Scripture.* It follows that the
answer to the second question depends primarily and properly upon those marks,
not

[Page 96 | ONB canvas 150 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000150]

upon the inward operation of the Holy Spirit bearing witness and persuading
in our hearts, through and with the word, that these are the Scriptures of
God. And it follows that the sole and primary answer to the last question
depends upon usage of the language, not upon any operation of the Spirit
instilling that truth, inscribing it, or impressing it sweetly and
efficaciously. Yet he adds that he is not unaware that *the Holy Spirit also
has certain parts in both of these questions, for the mind is illumined by a
certain divine light so that it can embrace the divinity of the Scriptures
and discern their true meaning.* Answer: Nor indeed am I unaware that he says
these things. But, 1., on page 85 of the *Censure of the Censure* he adds: *Yet
I affirm that this does not prevent the nature of the second question from
being such that, when it is asked why I believe these writings to be
Scripture, the answer should be that I do so because I discover marks of
divinity in them; nor the nature of the third from being such that, when it is
asked what the meaning of the Scriptures is, the answer should be that it is
the meaning which each of the sacred languages exhibits.* And thus the answer
to these questions will not depend upon the Spirit or his operation. Next,
according to him, the entire operation consists in the mind being made fit to
perceive those marks of divinity, the force of the words or usage of the
language, and similar things. For although he here mentions a certain divine
light by which the mind is illumined, elsewhere he asserts clearly enough
that no new light is infused by God into our minds. And indeed, if the Spirit
of God only makes our minds fit to perceive these objects, I do not see how
*Wolzogen* could speak otherwise. Yet neither do I see in what the singular
feature consists. For to understand the nature of other Writings and grasp
their meaning, that work of the Spirit by which we are made fit, and so forth,
is required. But he says on page 55 that *it is the singular character of
Scripture that by the power of the Spirit we are also confirmed concerning
those things which regard the last two ques-*

[Page 97 | ONB canvas 151 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000151]

*-tions.* Answer: If the Spirit merely confirms those signs, he does not work
in human minds that persuasion concerning the divinity of the Scriptures or
the true meaning of the text. But, you will say, he speaks still differently
on page 85 of the *Censure of the Censure*: *That singular feature*, he says,
*does not depend upon the nature of these questions but upon the constitution
of divine things, such that we can neither discern those marks of divinity
with the assent of faith nor explain the sacred text unto salvation unless we
are assisted by the Holy Spirit.* I therefore see that we can perceive those
marks of divinity, though not with the assent of faith, and can explain the
sacred text, though not unto salvation, even without being assisted by the
Holy Spirit. Then he must admit that, to that extent, the second question must
be answered as follows: I believe with the assent of faith that these writings
are Scripture because the Holy Spirit causes me to perceive and embrace the
marks of divinity in them with the assent of faith. And the third must
similarly be answered in this fashion: I perceive this to be the true meaning
of this text because the Holy Spirit causes me to perceive and explain it unto
salvation. But all this does nothing for the matter at hand unless he numbers
this aid of the Spirit among those things which guide reason so that it does
not err in forming its judgment and place solid supports beneath it so that,
when shaken, it does not totter. And if he does this, there is no reason for
him to charge the Orthodox with Enthusiasm. For he will fasten this charge, at
least in part, upon himself. If he says that he does not hold this view, and
that here he speaks only of the Spirit's aid in and about the object, by which
aid he renders the object fit for perceiving that norm, or that which
certainly guides reason, then all these things are outside the target and
uttered to no purpose at all. Yet his words sound very different, whatever he
himself may say. For on page 53 he acknowledged that the divine Spirit has
certain parts in the resolution of each question. The second question was:
Why do I believe these writings to be Scripture? And the third: Why do I
determine this and no other to be *Scrip-*

[Page 98 | ONB canvas 152 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000152]

*-ture's* meaning? That is, What is it which guides me so that I do not err
when I judge these writings to be God's Scripture and this to be their
meaning? Now if the Holy Spirit has certain parts in these questions, whether
he wills it or not, *Wolzogen will be an Enthusiast*, at least in part.
Moreover, when he says that the Holy Spirit's having certain parts in the
resolution of the last question depends upon a certain singular nature of the
Scriptures, he agrees badly with himself. For above he said that *the
Scriptures were composed in an entirely human manner and must be handled no
differently in their interpretation than other human writings.* For if the
answer to the question why we determine this and no other to be the meaning of
the Scriptures depends in part upon the Spirit's illumining our minds by a
certain divine light so that we can discern their meaning, then the
interpretation of the Scriptures must be handled far differently from the
explanation of other books.

XIX. But to what end does he discuss all these things? To this end, he says on
page 55: *that the nature of our question may not be believed to be a
controversy which, like that first one, belongs to faith alone and rests
solely upon God's testimony. For because it concerns human matters—words,
terms, and discourse—it must also be resolved by human means.* Answer:
*Wolzogen* is like himself. He speaks hesitantly or doubtfully because he does
not will to
explain his mind to us clearly and distinctly. He does not speak openly here
when he says that it *belongs to faith alone and rests solely upon God's
testimony*. For he appears to insinuate that something must be attributed to
God's testimony even in this question; and yet he denies this and leaves no
place here for faith, because the question concerns merely human matters and
must also be resolved by human means. But do our writers teach in this way? O
*Wolzogen!* What sort of faith is yours? What sort of Religion is yours? Upon
what foundation, I ask, does it rest, if not upon divine testimony or
authority? Is your religion contained in Scripture? Do you believe with
divine faith that the things pertaining to religion are re-*

[Page 99 | ONB canvas 153 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000153]

*-vealed* there? Yet how can this happen unless you also believe, on account
of God's authority, that this is the meaning of this passage? Does your entire
faith rest upon human things? 2. If this whole question must be resolved by
*human means*, there is no need of the Holy Spirit, for he must not be numbered
among human means. 3. Are the words, terms, and discourse of Scripture merely
human things? Elsewhere we hear you speak otherwise, as on pages 262–263. If
so, why would the work of the Holy Spirit be any more necessary for properly
employing these merely human things in the interpretation of the Scriptures
than in the interpretation of other books? And in what, then, consists that
singular nature of the Scriptures about which we recently heard? Here we have
sand without lime.

XX. We have now had a description of the general nature of that thing which,
namely, guides reason so that it does not err in forming this judgment. And yet
we have seen nothing besides the distinction between this question and the
two others very closely related to it. From what has been said *Wolzogen*
permits us to conjecture what the general nature of that thing is, for he does
not tell us. But at any rate we have heard that it is the thing which guides
reason so that it does not err in this judgment, placing solid supports
beneath reason so that, when shaken, it does not totter; or that by which the
interpreter, moved by way of an object, can determine with certainty in his
own mind that this meaning must be joined with this discourse. Next, on page
55 and following, he treats those who are to interpret the Scriptures. He
says that *all Interpreters must be endowed with reason,* which we readily
concede. But then on page 56 he says: *There is a great difference between a
believer and an unbeliever. The latter, infected with the stain of sin, is
said to be blind in divine things; the former, cleansed by regenerating grace,
is now judged to see. Let each read and interpret the Scriptures: each will do
so with a different outcome, because they are placed in very different
conditions.* Who disputes this? But, 1., the question does not concern the
outcome of the interpretation, but the inter-*

[Page 100 | ONB canvas 154 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000154]

*-pretation* itself. 2. Although an unbeliever is said to be blind in divine
things, he can nevertheless see human things. But you were just saying that
interpretation concerns human matters—words, terms, and discourse—and must be
resolved by human means. Therefore an unbeliever can also understand these
things and consequently Interpret the Scriptures, even if he is blind in
divine things. 3. If an Unbeliever cannot Interpret the Scriptures as well as
a believer, or perceive equally well the thing which guides reason in forming
its judgment, that thing must be something other than usage of the Language,
because usage of the language can be no less known to unbelievers than to
believers. By this reasoning, therefore, you weaken your own assertion, if you
do not destroy it. But he promises that he will soon explain these things more
openly.

XXI. He goes on in the same place to distinguish between the Interpreter who
is to explain the Scriptures to himself and the one who undertakes to do so
for another. We too distinguish, as was said above, between the subordinate
public and private Interpreter: that is, between the one who interprets the
Scriptures publicly and authoritatively, though always ministerially, whether
alone or joined with others in a Synod; and the one who privately searches and
meditates upon the Scriptures and seeks out their meaning by his own diligence
and the aid of the Holy Spirit. But let us hear what difference he places
between them. *For*, he says, *there are two matters of the greatest importance,
each of which makes an immense difference between the two. I can exhibit to
another everything which makes for persuasion, and these things can be of
such a kind that the one who turns his attention to them without being
hindered by any Impediment ought to be satisfied. Yet I cannot extort assent;
thus there will be no Interpretation of mine. But I will easily obtain from
myself that I attach faith to the things which I see to be firm and
indubitable.* Answer: Concerning what Interpreter, I ask, is the dispute
instituted? We saw above that he says nothing about the supreme Interpreter,
although from what is said here it would very well appear to follow that the
supreme Interpreter is the one and only Interpreter properly so *cal-*

[Page 101 | ONB canvas 155 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000155]

*-led*, because he can extort assent, which it lies in no one else's power to
extort. And here Wolzogen excludes every public ministerial interpreter,
asserting that their interpretation is absolutely nothing because they cannot
extort assent. Alas for orthodox *Wolzogen*, the defender of the Orthodox!
What? Does a pastor not Interpret the Scriptures in a sermon? Does a Classis
or Synod not Interpret the Scriptures when they join the meaning with the
words and authoritatively exhibit and propose that same meaning? I would like
to know whether the only person to be called an interpreter is the one who can
penetrate another person's mind. Or is the person who undertakes to Interpret
the Scriptures for another to be called an interpreter provided he happens to
extort assent, even if it is not in his power to penetrate the other person's
mind? Or ought no one be said properly to Interpret for another unless he has
actually extorted assent? If this last is said, then Christ himself and the
Spirit, with respect to the many who resisted and did not actually give—and
still do not give—assent, will not be called Interpreters, and their
interpretation will be nothing. Then the interpretation of the Apostles and
Prophets was also frequently, if not always, nothing with respect to certain
people. Indeed, the interpretation of the Old Testament through the New will
be nothing, because the Jews do not give assent to that interpretation. If the
first is said, then no mortal can be said to Interpret the Scriptures for
another. To what end, therefore, were pastors instituted by Christ? To what
end are public sermons, instructions, private admonitions, and similar things
instituted which pertain to the office of Pastors and private Christians and
cannot be performed without the Scriptures and some Interpretation of them?
Does Scripture not mention certain Interpreters? 1 Corinthians 14:5, 13, 27;
Job 33:33; Acts 18:26. See also Acts 8:32, 35 and following; 17:2; and 18:24,
28.

[Page 102 | ONB canvas 156 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000156]

If the second is said, then the same interpretation will be an interpretation
with respect to certain people, namely those who give assent, but no
interpretation with respect to others. How ridiculous this is! For if they do
not give assent, that interpretation will be useless; nevertheless, it will
be a true Interpretation, because one person can exhibit to another
everything which can objectively convince and satisfy him, and this is enough
to make an Interpretation for another. Let Wolzogen himself be heard on page
92: *For both the one who is his own interpreter is moved by some certain
reason, and the one who serves others can employ the same reason—not, indeed,
so that they are actually convinced. For who can bend to his own pleasure the
unyielding and untamed mind, the mind occupied by preconceived opinions, or
the foolish mind of another which is devoid of reason? But he can employ it so
that it may be known that they ought to have been convinced by the force of
the reason brought forward, if they themselves had contracted no defect.* But
what is the other difference? *To this*, he says, *a second difference must be
added: I cannot bring to bear in persuading another everything which can move
me to believe. For that power of the Spirit which bathes my mind in his light
is not in my power, so that I can share it with another. Thus it can happen
that I draw out the meaning of the Scriptures because, under the Spirit's
guidance, I recall to mind all those things which are fitted for explaining
that meaning, while another remains stuck, to whom the Spirit has not granted
this grace.* Answer: What are those things which can move you or anyone to
believe? Are they not the things which move and touch your mind by way of an
object? Are they not the things which guide reason, or can guide it, so that
it does not err in drawing out the meaning? That is, are they not the
arguments on account of which you determine this to be the meaning of these
words? I ask, therefore, why you cannot bring all these things to bear?
*Those dwell in the vicinity of Enthusiasm*—your *Velthuysen* says on page 60
of *On the Use of Reason*—*who frequently say from the pulpit that they speak
things certain and established, that the rationale for interpreting some
passage of Scripture is established for them, that they perceive*

[Page 103 | ONB canvas 157 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000157]

*this or that argument to be contained in some passage of Scripture, but that
these things are clear only to themselves and the regenerate and imperceptible
to others, and so forth.* Must you too be numbered among the Enthusiasts? It
is indeed true that the power of the Spirit is not in our control, so that we
can share it with others. But must no one be said to interpret for others
unless he can do this? You then exclude every authoritative ministerial
interpretation. Indeed, no one can be said to interpret for himself, because
no one has this power of the Spirit in his own control; and so, in this
respect, there is no difference between those interpreters. Finally, the
discussion here concerns only the object, or that which can move us by way of
an object to make such an Interpretation; that is, it concerns the reason by
which I am moved to determine with certainty in my mind that this meaning must
be joined with this discourse. To what end, then, do you mention the Spirit?

XXII. Finally, having made all these observations, on page 58 he brings forth
his position concerning the Interpreter of the Scriptures. *And I state it*,
he says, *in three words as follows: all the things which appear capable of
contributing something to Interpretation must be joined together. And since
all these can be reduced chiefly to three heads—the Holy Spirit, reason, and
usage of speech—I would not have any of these regarded as foreign to our
position.* Answer: It is not worth inquiring whether everything which appears
capable of contributing something to Interpretation can be reduced to these
three heads. In this, the cunning of the evasive *Wolzogen* is sufficiently
plain, and it is evident that he betrays the cause. Although the question
already under debate concerns the absolute and independent interpreter, and,
with respect to us, the infallible norm of interpretation, he invents a
question about the means which we employ to discover the true meaning of
Scripture, as if this were the only question: What means must be employed so
that we may finally arrive at the true and genuine meaning of the Scriptures?
Who, I ask, raised a controversy about this matter? Is this

[Page 104 | ONB canvas 158 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000158]

the principal question when our writers and the Pontificians dispute
concerning the Interpreter of the Scriptures? Or does the controversy between
our writers and the Socinians concern this question? What sort of disputant,
then, is *Wolzogen*? Is he an excellent disputant who thus perverts the
question and fashions a question about an uncontested matter, but does not
take up or join issue in the controversy actually raised, instead deserting
and betraying it like a betrayer of the cause? The question was, by his own
testimony, what it is on account of which we determine this and no other to be
the meaning of the Scriptures; or what is that reason by which I am moved to
determine with certainty in my mind that this meaning must be joined with this
discourse; or what is that thing by which the one who uses reason can and
ought to be led to allow himself to be persuaded of the truth of some
Interpretation? Hence it is evident that the only thing asked in this question
is what guides us by way of an object in making an Interpretation and can
persuade us that an Interpretation has been properly constructed. Yet here he
joins together everything which appears capable of contributing something to
Interpretation, whether it pertains to the object or to the subject. Here is
the man who dispels the remaining night and treats and presents this entire
matter more explicitly than has been done before. Here is the resolver of the
question and defender of truth. Shall I call him a defender or a colluder,
when he says nothing which the *Paradoxical Essayist* does not likewise say?
The Paradoxical writer wills Reason or Philosophy to be that which moves us by
way of an object in making a legitimate interpretation. *Wolzogen* concedes
this, but meanwhile adds usage of speech, which the Paradoxical writer will
readily concede also has its own use here. He also adds the Holy Spirit, who
in some way disposes and moves the subject, which the *Paradoxical writer* will
not deny. Finally, *Wolzogen* had said above that the words and the context of
the discourse must prove that this, and no other, is the meaning of these
words, and he inculcates the same thing throughout his little book. Yet here
he also joins Reason to them.
