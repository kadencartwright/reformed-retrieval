Chapter XIV

The Manner in Which Original Sin Is Propagated

Thus far we have discussed the nature and subject of sin. We must now discuss
its propagation. Although many shrink from this question, relying on the
authority of Augustine in book 2, chapter 36, *On the Merits of Sins*, who
advised that nothing should be rashly asserted in such a question, we will
nevertheless state briefly and soberly—with their kind permission—what must be
held from the Scriptures concerning the manner of propagation.

There are various opinions about the manner in which sin is propagated.

The first opinion is that of the Pelagians, who said that sin is propagated
only by imitation. Their opinion is refuted by the following arguments.

Argument 1. If we are all dead in Adam, then we sin not only by imitation. But
the former is true, Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:22. Therefore.

Argument 2. All are born in Adam's likeness—not the likeness in which he was
created, but the one that had been corrupted by sin—Genesis 5:3; Job 14:5.
Therefore.

[1610 printed p. 83 | Google Books PA83]

Argument 3. David acknowledges that certain sins belong to conception, Psalm
51:7. Therefore not all sins arise by imitation.

Argument 4. As righteousness is propagated from Christ, so sin is propagated
from Adam, and conversely, Romans 5:12. But righteousness from Christ is
propagated not by imitation alone but by communication. Therefore.

Argument 5. By nature we are children of wrath—not insofar as we were created
by God, but insofar as our nature was corrupted in Adam—Ephesians 2:3.
Therefore.

Argument 6. That which is born of flesh is flesh, John 3:6. Therefore we are
stained with sin in the very act of generation, and thus not only by
imitation.

Argument 7. Many who cannot imitate the sins of another nevertheless die and
are judged, Romans 5:14. Therefore.

See the remaining arguments under the question, “Is there original sin?”

Objections

Objection 1. Nahum 1:9 says that God does not punish the same sin twice. But he
punished Adam's sin in Adam. Therefore he does not punish it in us, and
consequently it is not propagated into us, but we sin only by imitation.

Response 1. Adam's sin was not one sin numerically but one in species, and a
sin of the whole species. Therefore, just as he sinned, so we sinned in him,
and God justly punishes us for it.

Response 2. They interpret the passage badly. Its meaning is that God will
punish the people so severely that there will be no need to repeat the
judgment. It does not follow that we sin only by imitation.

[1610 printed p. 84 | Google Books PA84]

Objection 2. If Adam's sin harms those who do not sin, then Christ's
righteousness will benefit those who do not believe. But the latter is absurd.
Therefore so is the former.

Response 1. I deny the assumption, because Christ's righteousness benefits
those who do not actually believe, as in the case of infants. See Augustine,
volume 7, book 1, *On the Merits of Sins*.

Response 2. It is false that Adam's sin harms people who have not sinned,
because all sinned in him, Romans 5:12.

Objection 3. Whoever does not have sin cannot transfer it to others. But
believing parents who have been baptized and justified do not have original
sin, because it is removed in baptism.

Response 1. Augustine says in volume 7, book 3, chapter 8, *On the Merits and
Remission of Sins*: “In turn, I ask how the foreskin removed by circumcision
remains in the children begotten by the circumcised, and how the chaff
separated with such diligence by human labor remains in the fruit born from
purified wheat.” Therefore the proposition must be distinguished. “Those who
do not have sin cannot transmit it” is false with respect to grace but true
with respect to nature. With respect to nature, however, believers have sin,
and generation belongs to nature, not grace.

Response 2. Pelagius admits that infants are baptized so that they may become
Christians. Augustine therefore responds as follows in chapter 9 of the work
just cited: “Just as you say that none but righteous people are born from
righteous people, since no one gives what he does not have, why do you not
also say that Christians are born from Christians? Just as you say that
non-Christians are born from Christians and are baptized so that they may
become Christians, so we say that unrighteous people are born from righteous
people and are therefore baptized so that they may be born again.”

[1610 printed p. 85 | Google Books PA85]

Response 3. I deny the assumption. Although the guilt of sin is removed in
believers, sin itself truly remains, though it is not imputed. For as long as
they live here, believers are sinners by nature but righteous by grace. They
beget children not insofar as they are in grace, but insofar as they are human
beings by nature, and thus insofar as they are corrupt.

Objection 4. Adam's sin was a single transitory action. Therefore it could not
pass into others.

Response 1. The apostle says that it passed into others, Romans 5:12.

Response 2. It is true that a personal action does not pass into others. But
Adam's sin belonged to the whole nature. Finally, an action does not pass into
others insofar as it is an action, but the *lawlessness* in the action does.

Objection V. Sin has its seat not in the flesh but in the soul. But the soul is
not transmitted from Adam. Therefore neither is sin.

Response 1. Augustine says in book 3, chapter 10, *On the Merits of Sins*: “If
it is unjust for a soul which does not descend from Adam's soul by propagation
to undergo sin, then it will likewise be unjust for it to pay the penalties of
sin. But it suffers the penalties, and this is not called unjust. Therefore it
is not unjust for the soul to undergo original sin.”

Response 2. I deny the proposition, because the whole human being is the seat
of sin, as was proved above. Finally, although a soul is not transmitted from
Adam's soul, the whole human being begets a human being, because the end of
production and generation is not the production of one part but of the whole.
Consequently, nothing follows from the objection.

The second opinion is that of those who maintain that original sin is
transmitted through the soul because the soul itself is propagated. Their
opinion is refuted by the following arguments.

Argument 1. If Scripture testifies that God alone is

[1610 printed p. 86 | Google Books PA86]

the author and father of souls, while parents are only the authors of bodies,
then souls are not propagated. But the former is true, Hebrews 12:9.

Argument 2. If Scripture teaches in various places that souls are daily
infused by God, then they are not propagated. But the former is true, Numbers
16:22; Ecclesiastes 12:9; Isaiah 57:20; Zechariah 12:1; Psalm 33:15; Acts
17:24. Here the present-tense word “giving” must be noted.

Argument 3. If souls are propagated from parents through generation, then
souls are liable to corruption and death, for that which is properly generated
is also corrupted. But the latter is absurd. Therefore so is the former.

Argument 4. God created nothing from nothing to which he imparted the power of
producing something like itself. This is proved by induction: the elements,
stars, heaven, angels, and so forth do not produce things like themselves,
because they were created from nothing. But the soul is created from nothing.
Therefore, if bodies created from nothing cannot produce things like
themselves, much less can souls do so.

Objections

Objection 1. A human being begets a human being. Therefore he also begets the
soul.

Response. The inference is denied. He does indeed beget the human being, but
not the whole of what belongs to a human being, just as the whole Christ is
said to be Mary's son, but not the whole of Christ is from her.

Objection 2. Scripture attributes this corruption to the soul. Therefore it is
propagated through the soul.

Response 1. The inference is denied, because Scripture also attributes it to
the body. Yet it does not follow that it is propagated through the body.

Response 2. It is attributed to the soul not because of propagation but
because of inherence and imputation. This is therefore a fallacy of treating
what is not the cause as the cause.

[1610 printed p. 87 | Google Books PA87]

The third opinion is that of those who maintain that original sin is
propagated through the body. They do not agree among themselves. Some hold
that the soul is corrupted by the body as pure oil is corrupted by a corrupt
vessel. Others hold that the soul is potentially in the seed, that the seed is
infected with original sin, and that sin is propagated in this way. Still
others hold that sin properly and in itself is not propagated by parents, but
that a certain principle of sin from which it may arise is propagated. They
say that this principle is a disposition to the depraved temperament that
accompanies original sin and that it is propagated through the seed. Thus,
when an infant is formed in the womb by means of the vital and animal spirits,
a certain impression from the mother is made upon the whole infant, from
which this sin springs as fire from tinder. The whole infant draws this
impression from the mother in no other way than it draws the impressions of
diseases such as gout and stone, which are not actually diseases but
dispositions and principles of those diseases. Stapleton says that seed is the
instrument by which sin is transmitted and that sin is naturally transmitted
in the seed. Bellarmine, however, says to the contrary in book 4, chapter 12,
*On the Loss of Grace* that he cannot see how the body could infect the soul,
and that nothing else is required for the propagation of sin except that a
human being descend from Adam by true generation. By the very fact that a
person was in Adam's loins when Adam transgressed the commandment in paradise,
that person participated in his sin, Romans 5:12.

We, however, prove by the following arguments that it is propagated neither
through the body, nor through the seed, nor through anything in the seed.

Argument I. If the soul draws corruption because it is joined

[1610 printed p. 88 | Google Books PA88]

to an impure body, then God cannot be excused from being the author of sin,
because he joins a pure soul to an impure body. But the latter is absurd.
Therefore so is the former.

Argument II. If the soul draws corruption from the body, then what is immortal
and spiritual can be corrupted by what is mortal and bodily. But the latter is
absurd. Therefore so is the former.

Argument III. If original sin is transmitted through a body or seed infected
with original sin, so that the soul is infected through the body or seed, then
original sin will exist in the body alone or in the seed alone, for no one
gives what he does not have. But the latter is absurd. Therefore so is the
former.

The assumption—that original sin does not exist in the body alone or the seed
alone—is proved as follows.

Argument 1. If original sin were in the body alone or in the seed, then God's
image could exist in the body and seed alone, because a habit and its
privation belong to the same subject. But the latter is absurd. Therefore.

Argument 2. If sin existed in the seed or body alone, then the body or seed
could rightly be called a sinner, and the law could be said to have been given
to the seed or body, because the law was given to sinners, 1 Timothy 1:9. But
these things are absurd. Therefore.

Argument 3. If sin existed in the seed or body alone, then only the body or
seed would be liable to condemnation because it sins, and it could believe,
repent, be redeemed, be saved, and so forth, because that which sins can be
saved. But these things are absurd. Therefore.

Argument 4. Sin and death are said to be transmitted only into human beings,
Romans 5:12. But neither seed nor body is a human being; rather, a human being
is a composite of soul and

[1610 printed p. 89 | Google Books PA89]

body. Therefore before the soul's conjunction with the body, sin exists
neither in the seed nor in the body.

Objections

Objection 1. Sin is propagated through that alone which is capable of being
propagated and actually is propagated. But only the body is such a thing.
Therefore.

Response. Properly speaking, sin is not propagated because it is not capable
of being generated; the expression is metaphorical. Further, what is
propagated is not the body but the human being, because a human being begets a
human being, although not according to the whole of himself.

Objection 2. The soul is affected by the body because, as Galen testifies, the
soul's affections naturally follow the body's temperament, and philosophers
posit a *sympatheia*, or sympathy, between body and soul.

Response. The inference is denied, because before the soul's conjunction with
the body, neither the soul nor the body can be the subject of sin, as was
proved above.

Thus far it has been shown in what ways original sin is not propagated. We
must now show briefly how it is propagated. For our position to be understood
better, the following six points must be held as foundations.

Foundation 1. Properly speaking, neither body nor soul is said to have sinned
in Adam, but the whole human being.

Foundation 2. All human beings were in Adam as in the root of the human race,
as if all human beings had been one human being and Adam had been all human
beings. A human being, however, consists of both soul and body. Therefore both
soul and body were in Adam.

Foundation 3. Neither body nor soul was actually in

[1610 printed p. 90 | Google Books PA90]

Adam, but potentially, and in different ways. The body was in him with respect
to the substance or matter from which it arises in the seed, though this was
not actually the body of Adam's son. The soul was in him with respect to its
first subsistence, insofar as it is joined with bodies. For although souls are
created immediately by God, as was proved above, they do not first exist
outside the body and afterward become enclosed within it. Rather, they are
created in the body, or infused by being created.

Foundation 4. Souls or bodies do not have original sin before the conjunction
of soul and body has taken place, because before that conjunction they are not
human beings, while Adam's sin is transferred only into human beings, Romans
5:12.

Foundation 5. Therefore, although the seed from which the body is made is in
some manner unclean—in its beginning or preparation, Job 14:4—and thus a
certain uncleanness begins in us, in the opposite manner from Adam, in whom it
began from the soul, the soul is nevertheless not infected with original sin
by an action of the body upon the soul. Rather, it occurs through God's
ordinance, who said to everyone in Adam, “On the day you eat from this fruit,
you shall surely die.” This threat was common to Adam and the descendants who
were in his loins.

Foundation 6. At the very moment the soul is created in the body, a son of
Adam exists. Hence he is liable to sin and to threefold death: 1. spiritual
death, which is the loss of God's image; 2. bodily death, which is the
separation of the soul from the body; 3. eternal death.

These points having been established, we conclude that original sin, whether
imputed or inhering, is propagated in the following manner.

[1610 printed p. 91 | Google Books PA91]

The soul is infused into the body by being created. Once it has been infused,
there is a human being and therefore, at the first moment, a son of Adam liable
to sin and punishment. Existing in Adam's loins, he committed that sin when
Adam sinned as the stock and root of the human race. For that sin he is justly
abandoned by God and deprived of original righteousness, in accordance with
the words, “On the day you eat, you shall surely die.” For Adam lost God's
image both for himself and for his descendants.

Scotus appears to favor this position in book 2, distinction 32, where he
considers how original sin is contracted. “From what has been said,” he says,
“it appears one may conclude concerning infants that, because Adam received
righteousness for himself and for the whole nature then in him, God justly
requires from the whole nature, wherever it exists, the righteousness he gave
to the nature. By sin Adam stripped the nature of the righteousness it owed,
and a nature stripped of such owed righteousness, wherever it exists, makes
that nature naked and indebted.” And a little later: “It is contracted through
the mediation of the flesh, not in such a way that my flesh causes this
original sin, but because flesh is sown with concupiscence, and from it an
organic body is formed into which a soul is infused, constituting a person who
is a son of Adam. Therefore this person who is Adam's natural son is for that
reason a debtor for the original righteousness given by God to Adam himself
for all his children, and he lacks it,” and so forth.

Question I. Why does God abandon the pure soul he created and withdraw
original righteousness from it?

Response. He does not simply abandon the soul but a human being who is Adam's
son and who, in Adam's loins, lost God's image together with Adam and all the
rest. Further, this

[1610 printed p. 92 | Google Books PA92]

abandonment and withdrawal of original grace is the punishment of Adam's first
sin insofar as it was the sin of the whole species.

Question 2. God infuses a pure soul into a body so that the resulting composite
becomes liable to sin, and does so necessarily on account of God's ordinance,
since the soul cannot avoid corruption from that conjunction. How can it be
that God is not the author of that corruption?

Response 1. It can very well be so. 1. God is the author only of the
conjunction, not of the corruption.

Response 2. When God joins the soul to the body, he does not join it so that
the soul may be infected with sin, but so that a human being may exist. Sin
comes in accidentally because Adam sinned and such a human being was in Adam
as in the root. Nor is it right for God to refrain from creating souls and
uniting them to bodies because of the evil that accidentally follows.

Question 3. The scholastics and papists ask whether those who die with original
sin alone are punished with the punishment of sense.

Response. The Master of the *Sentences*, book 2, distinction 33, says that they
are punished only with the punishment of loss, not of sense. Augustine,
however, says in chapter 27 *On Faith to Peter* that one must hold most firmly
that little children who die either in their mother's womb or after being born
but without baptism are punished by eternal fire. Understood universally,
this opinion is no less false than the other, because it is not the mere
absence or lack of baptism that condemns, but contempt for it. It must
therefore be said that those who die with original sin alone, unremitted, are
condemned. Yet it is remitted to all elect infants, whether dying in the womb

[1610 printed p. 93 | Google Books PA93]

or outside it. Further, the papists concede that baptism with one of the three
kinds of baptism is sufficient for salvation. But an elect infant who dies is
baptized with the baptism of the Spirit. Therefore.

Objection. Revelation 18:6 says that punishment is inflicted in proportion to
actual delight. Therefore where there is no actual delight in sin, there is no
punishment of sense.

Response. I deny the inference. Further, the punishment of sense is inflicted
not only in proportion to actual delight but also on account of original sin,
as Gregory of Rimini proves from Augustine in the place cited.

Question 4. Do the sins of immediate parents pass originally into their
descendants?

Response. Augustine raises this question in the *Enchiridion* but leaves it
unresolved. With Thomas, question 4, article 8, *On Original Sin*, we say that
it is impossible for the actual sins of immediate parents to pass originally
into their descendants, because a son does not bear his father's iniquity,
Ezekiel 18. Further, sins are no more propagated than the faith, holiness, and
so forth of the parents. The actual sins of parents can indeed be and are
propagated by imitation, but not by way of origin. Finally, Thomas argues as
follows: a begetter of the same kind communicates the nature of its own
species to what it begets, and consequently all accidents that follow the
species. Thus a human being begets a human being, and a being capable of
laughter one capable of laughter, and so forth. This is also true of
individual accidents that in some way belong to the body, but not only of
accidents that belong solely to the soul. Thus a white father generally begets
a white son, but a grammarian never begets a grammarian. Since sin is the
privation of the gift of original grace, the same

[1610 printed p. 94 | Google Books PA94]

must be said about sin as about the gift of grace. But the gift of grace was
given not to the person but to the nature, and this gift resided not only in
the intellect or in the intellect and will but also extended to the body,
which is propagated from parents. All sins other than the first sin, whether
Adam's or those of others, are opposed only to God's grace given properly to
the person, not to the nature alone. Many sins of parents reside either in the
intellect alone or in the intellect and will, and do not extend to the body
because they are not carried into execution through the body's members.
Therefore neither the grace nor the actual sins of any parents—indeed, not
even those of Adam himself, except for that first sin—are propagated into
their descendants.

Chapter XV

In What Respect Original Sin Remains in the Regenerate

Bellarmine asserts in book 5, chapter 6, *On the Loss of Grace* that the
corruption of nature, or concupiscence, that remains in the regenerate after
baptism is not original sin. This is not only because it is not imputed, but
also because it cannot be imputed, since in itself or by its own nature it is
not sin at all. In chapter 5 he says the question between us is not whether
human nature was gravely corrupted through Adam's sin, nor even whether this
corruption pertains in some way to original sin so that it may be called its
material element, following Thomas, *First Part of the Second Part*, question
82, articles 1 and 3. Rather, he says, “the whole controversy is whether the
corruption of nature, and

[1610 printed p. 95 | Google Books PA95]

especially concupiscence in itself and by its own nature as it is found in the
baptized and justified, is properly original sin. Catholics deny it, since
they teach that after the will has been healed by justifying grace, the
remaining diseases not only do not make human beings guilty but cannot do so,
because they do not have the true character of sin.” In chapter 4 he says that
ignorance, concupiscence, and so forth are wounds, weaknesses, and diseases of
nature left by original sin. The Council of Trent in session 5 says that
original sin is truly removed in baptism and does not exist in the regenerate,
but is remitted and not imputed, and that it is called sin improperly. It is
called sin in this way because it is the cause and punishment of sin.

We, however, assert that in the regenerate concupiscence, or original sin, is
truly and properly sin, so that by its own nature it renders us liable to
death. Nevertheless, it does not have the power to condemn because of Christ,
who by his blood prevents its power from condemning us and by his Spirit
restrains it from ruling. Yet this sin retains and exercises some power, so
that it fights against the Spirit and prevents us from fulfilling the law in
this life. This power is struck with a mortal wound at the first moment of
conversion, so that it never reigns in us in its full vigor. In sum, the
formal element of this sin—namely, liability and its consequent
punishment—is entirely removed in the regenerate, Romans 8:1. Yet the
corruption itself truly and properly remains in the regenerate as sin. We and
the papists therefore agree that this sin is removed in the regenerate; the
question concerns the measure

[1610 printed p. 96 | Google Books PA96]

of its abolition. They maintain that it is wholly removed insofar as it is
sin, but remains only as a defect and weakness that renders the heart fit and
ready to receive sin. We, however, say that it remains as something truly and
properly called sin.

The state of the controversy is therefore this: does original sin, or
concupiscence, remain after baptism in the regenerate as something truly and
properly called sin? They deny it; we affirm it, relying on the following
arguments.

Argument 1. If this sin existed in the regenerate and baptized apostle so that
he could not be freed from it as long as he lived here, then this sin exists
in believers after baptism so that they cannot be freed from it in this life.
But the former is true, Romans 7:24.

Argument 2. That which renders the regenerate wretched and liable to death is
truly and properly sin. But original sin is such a thing in the passage cited.
Therefore.

Argument 3. That which Scripture calls sin in the regenerate is sin properly
so called in them. But it calls concupiscence in the regenerate sin properly
so called. Therefore. The assumption is proved from Romans 7:7: what it first
called sin it then calls concupiscence. Further, what is prohibited by God's
law is sin properly so called. But concupiscence is prohibited by the tenth
commandment.

Argument 4. If original sin and concupiscence did not exist in the regenerate,
then the apostle would have said in Romans 6:12, “Do not let sin exist in you
so that you obey its lusts.” But he said only, “Do not let it reign.” Therefore
it is removed only with respect to its reign, that is, its guilt and
punishment. Augustine explains it this way in tractate 41 on John: “The
apostle,” he says, “does not say, ‘Do not let it exist,’ but ‘Do not let it
reign.’ As long as you live, sin must exist in your members; only let its reign
be taken away.” Bellarmine, however, in book 5, chapter 10, *On the Loss of
Grace*

[1610 printed p. 97 | Google Books PA97]

concedes that the apostle calls concupiscence sin, but says he does so
improperly. This is refuted as follows.

Proof 1. “Sin” taken improperly—that is, not for fault—signifies either
punishment, a sacrifice for sin, or the cause of sin. But Bellarmine maintains
that it is taken in that passage only for the cause of sin. Augustine says to
the contrary in book 5, chapter 3, *Against Julian*: “The concupiscence of the
flesh against which the good Spirit desires is sin, the punishment of sin, and
the cause of sin.” It does not follow that because it signifies the cause of
sin it is therefore not sin, since one and the same thing can be both sin and
the cause of another sin.

Proof 2. The apostle condemns sin and all its lusts, and therefore speaks of
“lusts.” But the sin of concupiscence is threefold. 1. It is actual when we
actually obey the inward motions of concupiscence. 2. It is partly actual and
partly original when it arises from nature's natural corruption and we who
desire it actually feel it, though without consent. 3. It is original when it is the
fountain of all concupiscences and sins. Therefore the apostle condemns
original concupiscence.

Proof 3. The words of Scripture are to be taken figuratively and improperly
only when the sense they have according to their proper signification does not
agree with Scripture and the analogy of faith, but conflicts with them. Yet if
“sin” is taken properly here, it will agree perfectly with Scripture and the
analogy of faith. In Romans 7 it is called the thing that is not good, the evil
the apostle hates, and something to be crucified, destroyed, and mortified,
which opposes God's law, and so forth. Therefore, properly speaking, it is sin.

Argument 5. If baptized and regenerate infants

[1610 printed p. 98 | Google Books PA98]

die though they do not actually sin, then original sin in them is sin properly
so called, because they die only on account of sin properly so called, Romans
6:23. But the former is true, Romans 5:14, for they cannot use reason or their
affections well or badly. Therefore.

Argument 6. That which opposes the Holy Spirit working in us, tempts us, and by
tempting makes us sin is by its own nature and properly sin. But concupiscence,
or original sin, is such a thing, Romans 7:17–19; Galatians 5:17; James 1:14;
Hebrews 12:1. Therefore.

Argument 7. That which does not conform to God's law is sin, 1 John 3:4. But
concupiscence, according to Bellarmine in book 5, chapter 14, *On the Loss of
Grace*, does not conform to God's law, but is a weakness arising from original
sin and materially *anomia*, lawlessness, and sin. Therefore.

Argument 8. If Christ was made like us in all things except sin, then either
concupiscence existed in Christ or concupiscence is sin. But Bellarmine denies
the former in the place cited. Therefore it is sin.

Argument 9. If concupiscence is called sin not properly but insofar as it is
the punishment of sin, then one may rightly say that concupiscence is from God
insofar as it is concupiscence, because every punishment, insofar as it is
punishment, is from God. But concupiscence insofar as it is concupiscence is
not from God, 1 John 2:16. Therefore.

Testimonies of the Fathers

Cyprian, quoted by Augustine in book 2 *Against Julian the Pelagian*, says:
“Let no one flatter himself that he has a pure and spotless heart, and since
no one

[1610 printed p. 99 | Google Books PA99]

can be without sin, whoever says he is blameless is either proud or foolish.”

Hilary on Psalm 58 says: “Who will boast that he has a pure heart before God,
not even if he is an infant of one day, since both the origin and the law of
sin remain in us according to the apostle?”

Jerome on Amos 1 says: “The first sin is to think things that are evil; the
second, to acquiesce in the thoughts; the third, to complete them in deed; the
fourth, not to repent after the sin.”

Augustine says in book 2 *Against Julian*: “Baptism heals the corrupted person
from guilt immediately, but from weakness gradually.” And in the same place:
“The law of sin is remitted, not ended.”

In book 3, chapter 21, of the same work he uses this argument: “The saints
fight against concupiscence, for they would not fight against it unless it
fought against them and were sin.”

In book 5, chapter 3, of the same work he says: “The concupiscence of the flesh
against which the good spirit desires is sin, the punishment of sin, and the
cause of sin.”

In book 6, chapter 8, of the same work he uses this argument: “Concupiscence in
the regenerate is iniquity, for it is unjust that a human being should be
depraved from the righteousness in which he was created. For it is not no
iniquity,” he says, “when in one human being either the higher things
shamefully serve the lower or the lower things stubbornly resist the higher,
even if they are not permitted to prevail.”

In book 1, chapter 21, *On Marriage and Concupiscence*, he says:
“Concupiscence is remitted in baptism, not so that it does not exist, but so
that it is not imputed as sin.”

In book 13 *The City of God* he says: “Whoever thinks that he

[1610 printed p. 100 | Google Books PA100]

lives without sin does not thereby bring it about that he has no sin, but that
he receives no pardon.”

In letter 105 he says: “The guilt of concupiscence is immediately abolished in
baptism, but its weakness is not immediately healed.”

Gregory of Rimini also says in distinction 30, question 2, article 1: “It is
evident that original sin is removed in one respect and remains in another
after baptism. It is removed with respect to guilt, but it is not removed with
respect to its essence,” and so forth.
