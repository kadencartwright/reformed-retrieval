### QUESTION XIV

#### What are the boundaries of the sixty-two weeks?

Answer. We approve the common opinion of ancient and recent Roman Catholics
and Protestants: that they begin after the end of the seven weeks discussed in
the preceding question and end before Christ’s death. Thus their first week is
the eighth in the complete sum, and their final week is the sixty-ninth. No
arguments other than the prophet’s own testimony are needed for this opinion.
He expressly uses this method in dividing the total: he assigns first place to
the seven weeks, second to the sixty-two, and third to the one. These partial
sums make the total without any break or gap. Therefore, those who wish to
place the seven after the sixty-two and the one, or to throw the seven into the
middle of the sixty-two, plainly disrupt Daniel’s method.

> Marginalia, printed p. 177: *Where the sixty-two weeks begin and end*.

Second, not only is the order of Daniel’s words plain here, but the events he
assigns to the portions of the seventy prove the same point. Although the
building of the walls and the street may not distinguish the first portion
from the second—the seven weeks from the sixty-two, as many indeed
believe—it is nevertheless certain that Christ’s death distinguishes the
second from the third. For the prophet says that the Messiah is killed in that
one final week after the sixty-two.

Third, this truth is established by the difficulties that follow every
dissenting opinion. I observe four byways into which certain great men strayed
after leaving the royal road.

The first is Joseph Scaliger’s. He ends the sixty-two weeks quite correctly at
Christ’s death, but begins them wrongly. In his first edition he began the
seventy weeks—and their first portion, the seven weeks—in the second year of
Darius Nothus, and ended the seven in the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes
Mnemon. He then began the sixty-two in Artaxerxes’s thirty-second and closed
them seven years before Jerusalem’s destruction. In his later edition,
however, since he closes the sixty-two weeks before Christ’s death, he is
compelled to begin them in the fifth year of Artaxerxes Mnemon.

Petavius and Nisbet argue extensively against this opinion, though often from
their own false principles, which we reject. In any event, the opinion
sufficiently refutes itself. For when Scaliger places the beginning of the
sixty-two in Artaxerxes’s fifth year and their end in the week before Christ’s
death, he leaves either no place or a confused place for the first seven
weeks. From the second year of Darius Nothus—the beginning of the earlier
sevenfold period according to Scaliger—to Artaxerxes’s fifth, not forty-nine
years, or seven weeks, elapsed, but only twenty-three, or three weeks and two
years. Indeed, this ἀταξία, “disorder,” reduced Scaliger to professing that he
did not know where the seven weeks should be placed: whether they ought to
precede or follow the sixty-two,

or instead fall within their limits. What remarkable confusion.

He entangles the one final week in the same confusion. For although this one
plainly follows the sixty-two in Daniel, Scaliger closes the entire seventy at
the destruction of the city. He is therefore compelled to mingle the one week
in which the Messiah died either with the seven, with the sixty-two, or with
both.

Because of this intolerable confusion, Scaliger’s followers do not follow
their master here. Junius and Diodati withdraw into a second byway in their
commentaries on Daniel 9:26. Following Scaliger’s earlier view, they begin the
sixty-two after the end of the seven weeks, in Artaxerxes’s thirty-second
year, and close them seven years before the city’s destruction. But their
distribution cannot be accepted either. Under it, the one week, the third
portion of the seventy, is either mingled with the preceding portions by
falling within the limits of the seven weeks or within those of the sixty-two,
which we rejected above; or else it is that seven-year period before the
city’s destruction, which is also absurd. For Christ was killed in that one
week, and no one has ever placed Christ’s death in the week immediately before
the city’s destruction.

> Marginalia, printed p. 178: *The error of Junius and Diodati*.

To avoid this exceedingly gross absurdity, these most learned men devised a
new escape for themselves, one that is no more tolerable. Each corrupted the
text of Daniel 9:26 by translating it badly. Junius renders it, *after the
sixty-two weeks, Christ having been cut off*; Diodati follows him with
*Sterminato il Messia*, “the Messiah having been exterminated.” Each goes
against Daniel’s explicit words twice over. For the Hebrew text is יכרת, the
third-person future of the *niphal*, “he will be cut off,” which they render
with the past participles *having been cut off* and *having been exterminated*.
Then, although Daniel affirms that Christ’s death occurred after the end of
the sixty-two weeks, they contradict him and affirm that it occurred before
the sixty-two weeks were completed and within their limits. See where a small
ἀναχρονισμός, “anachronism,” carried these most judicious men.

> Marginalia, printed p. 178: *They corrupt the sacred text*.

Theodoret strayed into a third opinion in his commentary on Daniel 9, as
Sanctius correctly observes on the same passage. Following Africanus and
coming very near the truth, he begins the seventy weeks with the twentieth
year of Artaxerxes Longimanus and ends them at Christ’s death. Yet he makes the
sixty-two weeks the first portion of the whole seventy and the seven weeks the
second, thus inserting the seven between the sixty-two and the one. Besides
reversing the angel’s and Daniel’s method, as we said just above, this opinion
does not fit the chronological sequence. From Longimanus’s twentieth year to
Hyrcanus, the last of the Maccabees, who was killed by Herod, he counts 434
years, or sixty-two weeks, and from there to Christ forty-nine years, or seven
weeks. This calculation agrees with the reckoning of no chronologist and no
historian.

> Marginalia, printed p. 178: *Theodoret’s error*.

As the fourth error we note the opinion of Francisco Suarez—or at least of
Chrysostom, to whom Suarez attributes it here without expressing much
disapproval of his own. They begin the sixty-two weeks in the thirty-second
year of Artaxerxes Mnemon and close them before Christ’s death. To those they
append Daniel’s one week, assigning the Messiah’s passion to the sixty-third
week. They count the six following weeks from Christ’s death to the city’s
destruction as forty-two years, and think that the final, seventieth week is
either left vacant or overlaps the preceding one at its beginning.

> Marginalia, printed p. 178: *The error of Suarez and Chrysostom*.

This opinion is thoroughly unsound. Besides reversing the prophetic order by
making the first last, the last middle, and the middle first, contrary to all
authors they begin the weeks with Artaxerxes’s thirty-second year and close
them a full nine years after the city’s destruction. This is to open and close
this great period contrary not only to every human authority but to Daniel’s
divine authority: they establish its beginning a full decade after the last
decree anyone issued for Jerusalem’s restoration, and its end one whole week
and two years after the city was destroyed.
