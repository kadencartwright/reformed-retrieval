# CHAPTER XVII

## Dr. Burmann's contrary arguments are examined

In the two preceding chapters we proved that our opponents' assertions are
false—namely, *The Decalogue is the formula of the covenant of grace*; *it
contains the pure and simple covenant of grace*; and *it contains nothing but
the covenant of grace*. It remains for us now to examine our opponents'
arguments. First, Dr. Burmann's statements both in his *Apology* and in his
*Synopsis* must be considered.

In *Apology*, chapter 2, page 58, he attempts to show that the argument *There
are ten precepts, or words, of the law; therefore there are ten moral
precepts* proves nothing. He says: [1. *The Decalogue was not given as the law
of works; therefore there is no basis for establishing that it is the ten
precepts of the law of works.*] I reply: 1. Although the Decalogue had not
been given as the law of works, it can very soundly be established that the
Decalogue contains ten moral precepts, as was seen above in Chapters VII and
VIII of this book. 2. We concede that the Decalogue was not given so that the
Israelites might be justified by works of the law or ultimately expect
eternal life from works of the law. We say, however, that the legal covenant
which the Lord had made with Adam, the first parent of all, was set before
them so that, despairing of obtaining salvation through works of the law,
they might flee to the Messiah and be driven to him—or, to use Burmann's own
words in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 6, section 9, so that the Israelites
might thus recognize their sin and curse

[Printed p. 172 | IA PDF 180 | IA leaf 180 | IA decausadei02brow]

and the impossibility of legal righteousness and, driven from that hope,
might thirst for the Redeemer's righteousness and be led by the hand to
Christ.

[2. He says: *The Decalogue as the Decalogue is the formula of the covenant
which God made with the Israelites. It therefore does not follow that the
words of the covenant are ten, and consequently that the words of the law of
works (that is, the moral words) are ten, since the Decalogue must be
considered not as the formula of the law of works—that is, the moral law—but
as the formula of the covenant. God, however, never made, or could have made,
a covenant with the Israelites concerning righteousness to be sought from
works without the Decalogue formally being, by God's own intention, the
legislation of the covenant of grace. It is therefore the legislation of
conversion to God in faith and love, or the formula of gratitude and
repentance.*]

I reply: 1. I would like these words explained to me: *The Decalogue as the
Decalogue is the formula*, and so forth. They imply that the Decalogue, under
another consideration, was not the formula of that covenant. If only he had
shown this. 2. We do not argue in this way: the words of the covenant are ten;
therefore the words of the law of works, or the moral words, are ten. 3. Yet
he cannot judge that inference worthy of rejection, since he believes that
the Decalogue afterward passed over for the Israelites into a certain form of
the covenant of works, as we saw above. 4. We know that God did not make a
covenant with the Israelites concerning righteousness to be sought from
works of the law. This notwithstanding, the Decalogue can be considered as
the formula of the law of works, or of the covenant of works, as we have just
learned from Burmann himself. 5. We have proved that the Decalogue is not the
formula of the covenant of grace. 6. Although the law of the Decalogue was
promulgated for an evangelical end—that is, so that they might be driven to
Christ—we deny that its promulgation was the legislation of the covenant of
grace. For the legislation of the covenant of grace sounds like this:
*Believe in Christ Jesus and you will be saved.* The Decalogue did not first
make this known or first command it. Yet it would have done so if its
promulgation had been the legislation of the covenant of grace. 7. That which
is the formula of gratitude, or contains the matter and rule of gratitude, is
not for that reason the formula of the covenant of grace. For gratitude
presupposes that the covenant of grace has been made and that its condition
has already been fulfilled. That condition is distinct from the formula of
gratitude. Although the law does not express the condition of the covenant of
grace, it can very well express the condition of those in covenant—that is,
the duties required of the covenanted—and the matter of gratitude. 8. What,
I ask, is this inference: the Decalogue does not contain moral precepts
because it is the legislation of conversion to God in faith and love and the
formula of gratitude and repentance? Does he not himself acknowledge in
chapter 1 of his *Apology* that faith and repentance are moral duties? No one
will deny that love is something moral.

[*For nowhere* (so he says) *is that promise of the covenant of works added
in the Decalogue: “The one who does those things will live in them,” or “Do
those things so that you may live in them.”*] I reply: 1. Nor is this promise
of the covenant of grace added anywhere in the Decalogue: *Believe in Christ
so that you may live.* Nevertheless, he wishes the Decalogue to be the formula
of the covenant of grace. Why, then, can it not be the formula of the covenant
of works even though that promise of the covenant of works is not added in
it? 2. Even when, by his own admission, the Decalogue had a certain form of
the covenant of works, no such promise appeared in the Decalogue. It is
therefore not required that such a promise be expressly inserted in the
Decalogue for it to be the formula of the covenant of works. 3. Nevertheless,
let him read Ezekiel 20:11, 13, and there he will see that promise contained
virtually in the Decalogue. Let us grant that the Decalogue does not

[Printed p. 173 | IA PDF 181 | IA leaf 181 | IA decausadei02brow]

express the words which formally constitute the character of the legal
covenant; they nevertheless occur elsewhere.

He goes on: [*It is indeed true that all the precepts of works are contained
in the Decalogue, because love cannot be described except through those
precepts. Yet they are not proposed in the Decalogue for that end, but rather
for the confession of faith and of God's grace and for the glorification of
God in the Mediator.*] I reply: 1. That which is proposed for the confession
of faith and God's grace and for the glorification of God in the Mediator is
not immediately proposed as the formula or stipulation of the covenant of
grace. These things designate the duties of covenanted people who have
already made the covenant, not the condition of the covenant or the duty of
those entering it. 2. The Decalogue could be proposed as the stipulation of
the covenant of works so that they might be compelled to flee to Christ or
the Messiah. Once this had been done, it could also be proposed to them for
the confession of faith and God's grace and for the glorification of God in
the Mediator—namely, through the required gratitude. Thus the Decalogue is now
the rule of conduct and the matter of gratitude, and yet it can be pressed as
the covenant of works in order to humble the unregenerate and impenitent more
deeply and constrain them so that they may at last embrace Christ offered in
the gospel. 3. Was this moral law proposed to Adam in innocence for the
glorification of God in the Mediator? Let us now hear the arguments.

[He says: *This is evident: 1. from its preface and the title of Jehovah in
it, taken from the promise made to Abraham in Genesis 17:7: “I am Jehovah your
God.”*] I reply: 1. From that preface and that title of Jehovah it is rightly
inferred that God made the covenant of grace with the Israelites. But it does
not follow from this that the precepts of the Decalogue are the formula of
the covenant of grace or contain the covenant of grace and nothing besides
the covenant of grace. We are speaking about the commands themselves, not
the preface. Shall we affirm that wherever God declares himself to be Jehovah
and the God of certain people, the formula of the covenant of grace is
immediately expressed there? God proposes many duties to be performed on this
ground. Must all those duties therefore be said to contain the formula of the
covenant of grace and nothing besides the covenant of grace? 2. I also grant
that it can follow from this that the Decalogue itself was proposed for an
evangelical end—namely, so that the Israelites might at last be compelled to
embrace the Messiah and seek salvation and eternal life in him alone.
Nevertheless, it does not follow that the Decalogue contains nothing but the
covenant of grace or does not contain in itself the formula of the covenant
of works. Why could Jehovah, as their God, not propose the covenant of works
to them in order to break those who trusted in their own strength, reduce
them to extremity, and compel them to flee to the Messiah and embrace the
covenant of grace while setting aside their own works? 3. It rightly follows
from that title that God did not propose this formula of the covenant of
works to them so that they might pursue righteousness from works of the law.
Yet it does not conflict with that title to propose the formula of the
covenant of works so that, perceiving the impossibility of legal
righteousness, they might seek the righteousness of the Mediator. For
afterward, by our opponents' own admission, the Decalogue was proposed in the
form of the covenant of works for this end, and yet Jehovah was their God.
Nothing can therefore be inferred against us from this title or from the
preface.

[2. He says: *From the commemoration of the benefit already bestowed—namely,
the exodus from Egypt, so that they might be led into the land sworn to the
fathers as an inheritance, that is, a pledge of the heavenly inheritance.
This exodus was also accomplished through the intervening blood*

[Printed p. 174 | IA PDF 182 | IA leaf 182 | IA decausadei02brow]

*of the lamb, which was a figure of the Mediator's blood.*] I reply: 1. This
commemoration of the exodus from Egypt, in order that they might be led into
the land which the Lord had promised under oath to give to the fathers, and
which was accomplished through the intervening blood of the mediator—of
which the blood of the lamb was a type—shows clearly enough that God dealt
with them in the manner of the covenant of grace. But this in no way proves
that God did not propose the formula of the covenant of works so that they
might be more tightly constrained and more forcefully compelled to flee to
Christ. 2. This commemoration of a benefit does not transform everything
pressed on that occasion into the formula of the covenant of grace. Nor does
it follow from such a commemoration that all the precepts reinforced by this
ground contain nothing but the covenant of grace.

3. He says: *Because the Decalogue itself speaks expressly of grace promised
to faithful parents unto the thousandth generation of their offspring.* I
reply: 1. The same answer can satisfy here also—namely, that this appended
promise does not change the nature of the precept or declare that this
precept contains nothing besides the pure and simple covenant of grace or is
the formula of the covenant of grace. God indeed made the covenant of grace
with this people, and in order that the end of this covenant might be attained
more easily among this stiff-necked people, he pressed the works of the law,
not so that they might be justified through works, but so that, perceiving the
impossibility of salvation from works, they might flee to the Mediator of the
covenant of grace. What, then? Does such an appended promise turn a legal
precept into the mere covenant of grace? In that case it would have to be
said that the precept *Go and sell everything* in Matthew 19:21 contains
nothing besides the covenant of grace and is the formula of the covenant of
grace, because the promise is added, *And you will have treasure in heaven.*
2. Moreover, this promise stood in the Decalogue even after, by Burmann's own
admission, it had the form of the covenant of works—that is, when these
declarations were finally heard: *The one who does them will live in them*,
and *Cursed is everyone who does not remain in all things.* Therefore, because
of this appended promise it should by no means have been said that the
Decalogue contains nothing besides the pure and simple covenant of grace;
otherwise it would not even have had the form of the covenant of works, and
those added declarations would have accomplished nothing. We must distinguish
between the commands themselves and the appended promises. We know that the
latter relate to the covenant of grace, but we are speaking of the former,
not the latter.

[4. He says: *God inserts into it the sign of promised sanctification—the
Sabbath—and the pledge of the heavenly inheritance—the earthly inheritance.
For he promises long life in the land which the Lord gives them. The law was
therefore the law of the covenant with the people, so that they might possess
in holiness and honor the land which God was prepared to give them as an
inheritance, as a figure and pledge of the true inheritance.*] I reply: 1. We
denied above that the Sabbath is merely the sign of promised sanctification
and established the contrary with arguments; see Book III, Chapters XXV and
XXVI. 2. The earthly inheritance mentioned in the fifth precept is not called
in the Decalogue a pledge of the heavenly inheritance, nor is it expressly
promised there as a pledge or figure of the true inheritance. 3. Shall we say
because of the appended promise of long life that the fifth precept contains
nothing besides the covenant of grace and is the formula of the covenant of
grace? Long life in paradise was promised to Adam. Was that covenant therefore
the covenant of grace? 4. This same promise always stood in the Decalogue,
even when those two declarations indicating the covenant of works had been
heard. Yet notwithstanding this promise, the Decalogue had a certain form of
the covenant of works.

[Printed p. 175 | IA PDF 183 | IA leaf 183 | IA decausadei02brow]

And the law was not a covenant with the people so that they might possess in
holiness and honor the land which God was prepared to give them as an
inheritance, as a figure and pledge of the true inheritance.

[5. *Hence the Decalogue is also called a covenant in Deuteronomy 4:13, and
thus it is “the legislation of the covenant.” Therefore, no command can be
given for having the covenant except the command of faith in God and of
gratitude for glorifying God as he makes his covenant known.*] See also Dr. de
Raadt's *Apology*, page 135, where he further cites Exodus 34:27–28;
Deuteronomy 9:9–11; and 1 Kings 8:9, 21. I reply: In Scripture the word
*covenant* is applied by synecdoche to various parts of God's covenant: to the
gracious promise in Genesis 17:7 and Galatians 3:17, 22; to the stipulated
condition in Psalm 50:5; to the appended seal in Genesis 17:10; to the
Mediator himself in Isaiah 42:6 and 49:8; and to the promised efficacious
grace in Jeremiah 31:33 and Hebrews 8:8. Here it is applied to the condition
of the covenanted, or to those duties which covenanted people are bound to
perform, as it is elsewhere. Indeed, God speaks through Jeremiah in 34:13–14
of this covenant as though through it he required nothing other than the
release of servants every seventh year according to the law of Exodus 21:2
and Deuteronomy 15:12. It therefore no more follows that the Decalogue is the
formula of the covenant of grace, or *the legislation of the covenant*, than
that circumcision is, and so forth. 2. The Decalogue is called a covenant even
when, by Burmann's own admission, it had the form of the covenant of works,
namely after the first tablets had been broken, as can be seen in Exodus
34:24. The tablets, even when they had been deposited in the ark, were called
the tablets of the covenant in Hebrews 9:4; and that ark was called the ark of
the covenant in the same place, Joshua 4:7, Judges 20:27, 1 Samuel 4:3, 1
Chronicles 17:1, and Jeremiah 3:16. Was it then *the legislation of the
covenant* when it had the form of the covenant of works? 3. The command of
faith in God is a command of the law of works and is distinguished from the
command of faith in Christ the Mediator. Why could a command other than faith
in Christ not be given remotely, though not proximately, for having the
covenant? What absurdity is there in saying that God gave the law of works so
that they might at last be compelled to believe in the Messiah and become
spiritually covenanted?

[He continues: *Far be it, therefore, from us to consider the Decalogue with
the Jews as the formula of the covenant of works. Their fall came from this,
for God cannot make such a covenant with a sinful person whom he calls to
himself.*] I reply: 1. The Jews' fall did not arise from their considering the
Decalogue as the formula of the covenant of works. It arose from their
thinking that God had proposed this formula of the covenant of works for the
end that they might be justified from works and seek salvation, rather than
that they might be driven more strongly to Christ and to seeking salvation
through faith in him. It arose because they did not know that Christ was the
end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes; thus, ignorant of
God's righteousness and not subject to it, they attempted to establish their
own (Romans 10:3–4). They stumbled at that stone (Romans 9:32) and did not use
the law as a pedagogue unto Christ so that they might be justified by faith
(Galatians 3:24). 2. We know that God does not now make such a covenant with
a sinful person whom he calls to himself, so that the person would pursue
righteousness by that method alone. Yet he can propose the covenant of works
to a sinner so that the sinner, recognizing his sin and misery as well as the
impossibility of that legal righteousness, may long for the Redeemer's
righteousness and be driven to Christ.

[He says: *So that I do not know what is meant by the statement on page 360:
“God's covenant with spiritual Israel was in truth the covenant of grace; yet
at the same time the covenant of works with its curses was repeated and
pressed upon them”*

[Printed p. 176 | IA PDF 184 | IA leaf 184 | IA decausadei02brow]

*(Leviticus 18:5; 26:14–39; Deuteronomy 27:14–26; 28:15, and so forth), “for
that did not take place in the Decalogue and therefore has no bearing on the
matter.”*] I reply: Those were indeed the words of the very distinguished Dr.
Essenius in his *Vindications* on the page cited. He went on to add: [*so that
carnal and reprobate people might be convicted all the more, while the elect
might be humbled in themselves and compelled to seek and embrace grace in
Christ.*] There is no reason for Burmann not to know what these words mean,
since they agree with his own statements in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 6,
section 9, cited above. 2. This was indeed not done in so many words in the
Decalogue, just as it was not done afterward when Burmann concedes that the
Decalogue had the form of the covenant of works. Yet it was done virtually,
since the precepts of the Decalogue considered in themselves are the formula
of the covenant of works made with Adam.

At last he concludes: [*But although the covenant of works with its curses,
under which the ungodly stood, was repeated with respect to the ungodly, it
was nevertheless never made with respect to spiritual Israel as though they
were under the covenant of works, although a reminder of the covenant of
works was present in those clauses in Leviticus 18:5 and Deuteronomy 28:15.
Likewise, on account of them the Decalogue is called by Paul the law in
contradistinction to the promise. This was done because of the precepts, “The
one who does them will live in them,” insofar as they had a legal effect in
Israel on account of sin by exacting the handwriting and recalling sin, as
though demanding a sacrifice for sin. Yet the covenant of works was never
restored or repeated.*]

I reply: 1. If the covenant of works with its curses was repeated with
respect to the ungodly, he is wrong to say that it was never restored or
repeated. 2. Dr. Essenius did not say that this was done with respect to
spiritual Israel, whom he said were under the covenant of grace. 3. From those
clauses Burmann himself acknowledges in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 6,
section 9, that the Decalogue exhibited a certain form of the covenant of
works and, indeed, that it was repeated in book 4, chapter 25, section 2. 4.
If it follows from this that the Decalogue is called by the Apostle the *law*
in contradistinction to the *promise*, the Decalogue was the formula of the
covenant of works and the law in contradistinction to the promise, even if
those clauses appear nowhere in the Decalogue itself. Thus what the very
distinguished Dr. Essenius said does bear on the matter, although Dr. Burmann
just denied it. 5. If the Decalogue is called by Paul the *law* in
contradistinction to the *promise* because of the precepts, *The one who does
them will live in them*, it is evident that the Decalogue is the formula or
stipulation of the covenant of works and for that reason is called by Paul
the *law* in contradistinction to the *promise*. 6. If the precepts *The one
who does them will live in them* are precepts of the Decalogue and had a legal
effect in Israel on account of sin by exacting the handwriting and recalling
sin, it is evident that the Decalogue is not the formula of the covenant of
grace but contains something besides the covenant of grace. Indeed, it is the
stipulation of the covenant of works, and the covenant of works was then
repeated—not so that they might pursue righteousness from works of the law,
but so that, despairing of obtaining salvation from works of the law, they
might flee to the righteousness of the Mediator. Who does not now see that
Dr. Burmann yields the point?

These are the things he expounds on this matter in his *Apology*. We must now
see what he says in his *Theological Synopsis*.

In book 4, chapter 6, section 6, he has this: [*And the very summary of the
Decalogue testifies to this, for it contains nothing but the commands of faith
and repentance, like those we read were given to Abraham: “I am the
sufficient God. Walk before me and be upright” (Genesis 17:1).*

[Printed p. 177 | IA PDF 185 | IA leaf 185 | IA decausadei02brow]

] I reply: 1. If the Decalogue contains nothing but the precepts of faith and
repentance, there are no such precepts in the Decalogue as could, if God had
pleased, have been converted into the covenant of works. For the precepts of
faith and repentance cannot be converted into the covenant of works. Yet he
says this openly in section 7 of the same chapter. Nor would the Decalogue
actually have been converted into a certain form of the covenant of works,
nor would it have been a reminder of the covenant of works and a daily
reproach of their faithlessness. Yet he expressly says this in section 9 of
the same chapter. Moreover, if the matter stood as he says, the Apostle would
never have said that the law exhibits the formula of the covenant of works
and is opposed to the promise and to faith, as Burmann himself acknowledges
that he did in section 8 of the same chapter, from Romans 10:5 compared with
Galatians 3:10, 12; Romans 4:14; and Galatians 3:12. For how can the precepts
of faith and repentance exhibit to anyone the formula of the covenant of
works or be opposed to the promise and faith? But this was sufficiently
refuted above. 2. The command *Walk before me and be upright* indeed contains
the duty of a covenanted person, but it is not the same as the command of
faith in Christ, nor faith insofar as it is the special instrument for laying
hold of Christ offered in the covenant of grace for justification, nor
repentance insofar as it is the amendment of a life wrongly lived under the
hope of salvation. It therefore does not contain the formal condition of the
covenant of grace. What, shall we say that the condition of the covenant of
grace is perfection or integrity in holiness? In that case we would be
justified through works of the law, and Abraham would have been justified
from works, contrary to the Apostle. He denies this in Romans 4:2 and proves
from the Scriptures that Abraham was justified by faith in verse 3 of the
same chapter and Galatians 3:6.

[He continues: *It no more prescribes the formula of the covenant of works to
the Jews than it does to us under the New Testament.*] I reply: God indeed no
more prescribed the formula of the covenant of works to the Jews than to us
so that they might seek righteousness from works of the law. Yet he
prescribed it so that they might be led by the hand to Christ, as Burmann
himself testifies in Romans 10:4, and as a pedagogue to Christ in Galatians
3:24. What more is needed?

[He says: *For the words “I am Jehovah your God,” and so forth, with which God
prefaces this covenant, are words of grace and cannot be pronounced to a
sinner in the covenant of works.*] I reply: They are indeed words of grace,
but it does not follow from this that the precepts of the Decalogue contain
nothing but the covenant of grace. For they had the same preface when they
passed over into the form of the covenant of works and those declarations
were heard which exhibited in themselves the contract and stipulation of the
covenant of works and were a reminder of the covenant of works. Does the
precept in Leviticus 11:43, *Do not make yourselves abominable with any
creeping thing that creeps*; or that in Leviticus 18:30, *You shall therefore
keep my ordinance, so that you do not practice any of those abominable
customs which were practiced among you; nor shall you defile yourselves by
them*; or that in Leviticus 23:22, *When you reap the harvest of your land,
you shall not reap the edge of your field completely, nor gather the gleaning
of your harvest; leave those things to the poor person and the sojourner*; or
that in Judges 6:10, *Do not fear the gods of the Amorites*—does any such
precept contain anything besides the pure and simple covenant of grace
because God presses those commands on this ground: *I am Jehovah your God*?
It would indeed be strange if it did. Or does even Leviticus 20:7, *Be holy,
because I am Jehovah your God*, do so? For if our holiness is the condition of
the covenant of grace, the Roman Catholics, who wish us to be justified from
works of the law, have won.

What comes next? He says that the first precept flows from that preface, and
in it [*God, all other gods being removed, enters this covenant on his part.*]
I reply: Did it flow from that preface when it was given to Adam in innocence?
Then, it would seem,

[Printed p. 178 | IA PDF 186 | IA leaf 186 | IA decausadei02brow]

God first made the covenant of grace with Adam and afterward implanted this
law in his heart. Or must it be said that this precept was not given to Adam?
2. If the Lord enters that covenant on his part in the first precept, he had
not yet entered this covenant on his part when he said, *I am Jehovah your
God.* How, then, was he their God? Further, if God enters that covenant on his
part in the first precept, it does not contain the stipulated part and,
consequently, is not a precept but belongs rather to the preface. Dr. Burmann
will not assert that.

This concerns the first. What does he say of the second? [He says: *In the
second he declares his loving care and his grace unto thousands. These things
are foreign to the covenant of works.*] I reply: They are nevertheless not
foreign to a precept of the covenant of works when it is pressed so that the
covenant of grace may be received, as has often been said.

What does he say of the third? [He says: *In the third he commands that this
covenant be held sacred on man's part; and since the man is in covenant with
God, he forbids this sacred name to be taken in vain and requires confession
of the covenant and of grace.*] I reply: 1. When this precept forbids
covenanted people to take that sacred name in vain and commands that the
covenant be held sacred on man's part, it proposes the condition of the
covenanted rather than the condition of the covenant itself. That is, it
presents the duty of those who are already in the covenant, not the condition
by whose eventual fulfillment people become covenanted or are admitted into
the covenant. 2. I had thought that the *first* precept commands the
confession of God and his grace and that the *third* commands the proper manner
of that confession—namely, that it be made in a holy and reverent manner.

He says that the *fourth* [*was typical, contained a sign of justification
through Christ, and foreshadowed the blessed times of the New Testament.*] I
reply: All these things are inventions, refuted in their place and to be
refuted further. I add only this: this is the first time I have seen the
Sabbath called a sign of our justification through Christ. Where, I ask, is
this insinuated even obscurely in the whole Old and New Testament? And if it
is a typical command, it is not a command of faith or repentance.
Consequently, the Decalogue contains precepts other than those of faith.

What does he say of the fifth? He says that the *fifth* [*meanwhile delivered
the pledge of the covenant and sealed a portion of the goods promised in it
by the inheritance of Canaan.*] I reply: The land which God was going to give
them is indeed mentioned in the fifth precept, but there is absolutely no
mention there of a pledge, much less a pledge of the covenant. But we spoke of
this promise above.

What does he say of the others? [He says: *After these commands of faith
toward God, the remaining precepts together with the earlier ones contained
the norm of repentance, gratitude, and holiness.*] I reply: We grant that
repentance is exercised in obedience to these precepts, but it is not evident
that any of them properly and directly commands evangelical repentance. 2. We
concede that the Decalogue contains the norm of gratitude and holiness. Yet it
does not follow from this that nothing besides the covenant of grace is
contained in it or that it is the formula of the covenant of grace. Otherwise
we must yield to the Roman Catholics in the dispute about justification, as
has already been said.

At last he concludes this paragraph in the following way: [*The covenant of
works consists no more in these things than in all Christ's and the Apostles'
exhortations to love and good works. And therefore, since no formula of the
law of works, no*

[Printed p. 179 | IA PDF 187 | IA leaf 187 | IA decausadei02brow]

*stipulation, and no contract of that kind occurs in the Decalogue, but rather
the stipulation of faith and the requirement of the confession of grace and
of God's name, it is plain that it belongs not to the covenant of works but to
the covenant of grace. Nor does it deliver any formula other than that of the
covenant formerly made with the patriarchs, which had its whole faith in the
ancient promise and the stipulation of repentance (Genesis 17:1).*
] I reply: 1. Were the Jews under the pedagogy of the law in a different way
than we are under the New Testament? Has he not himself acknowledged that the
Decalogue eventually had the form of the covenant of works so that by it they
might be led to Christ, who was the end of the law for justification to the
believer (Romans 10:4)? Will he say that all Christ's and the Apostles'
exhortations to love and good works have the form of the covenant of works?
Yet it cannot be denied that those exhortations can sometimes drive an
ungodly person to seek Christ's righteousness when he sees his inability to
perform those duties and that he is consequently subject to divine wrath.
But who will say that those moral precepts contain nothing besides the
covenant of grace? 2. We saw above how the formula and stipulation of the
covenant of works occur in the Decalogue. 3. The stipulation of faith insofar
as faith is a general virtue, and the requirement of the confession of God's
name, are also required in the law of works. It was shown above that in the
Decalogue God does not directly stipulate justifying faith and evangelical
repentance. 4. The words of Genesis 17:1 do not present to us faith in the
ancient promise and the stipulation of evangelical repentance. 5. It is false
that the Decalogue delivers to us the formula of that covenant which was
formerly made with the patriarchs.

Enough on these matters. Let us proceed to examine the arguments of others.
