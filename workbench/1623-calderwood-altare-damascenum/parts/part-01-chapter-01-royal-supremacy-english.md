CHAPTER I

On royal supremacy and the jurisdiction of royal commissioners in
ecclesiastical causes.

In considering the ecclesiastical polity of England generally, there come
under consideration: 1. persons and their adjuncts; 2. possessions; and 3.
constitutions concerning both. Persons either have an administration or have
none. Persons having an administration have either the supreme and more
absolute one, which is called the Royal Supremacy, or a less extensive one.

The Royal Supremacy is to be considered either generally or particularly.
Generally, it is that authority by which the prince, as supreme governor under
God, can decree in the ecclesiastical causes of his dominions whatever is not
contrary to the divine Word.

With respect to causes, the royal majesty has supreme authority over persons,
even ecclesiastical persons. But because authority over ecclesiastical persons
and causes is various and manifold, one must first set out the division of this
authority into its various kinds, as Mucket distributes it. *Authority about
ecclesiastical persons is occupied either in electing and nominating them to
ecclesiastical dignities and benefices; in consecrating them by the laying on
of hands; or in establishing and inflicting penalties. This punitive power, in
turn, either chastises them with civil penalties, such as prison, monetary
fines, and death, or with ecclesiastical penalties, such as excommunication
and anathema. Finally, authority coercing by civil penalties either assembles
ecclesiastical persons for national and provincial councils, or brings before
a civil judge and condemns those who offend against the peace of the realm and
the royal dignity. Similarly, authority in ecclesiastical causes varies
according to their variety. “Cause” has the broadest signification, so that
imperial law has used it for “matter.” Ecclesiastical matters are either
contentious and judicial or noncontentious and extrajudicial. Contentious and
judicial matters are those which, through joinder of issue between plaintiff
and defendant, come into dispute and are determined by a lawful judge with the
aid of witnesses, instruments, and proofs—for example, suits concerning
tithes, offerings, the dissolution of a marriage because of a precontract,
and alleged crimes of simony, blasphemy, heresy, idolatry, adultery, incest,
and so forth. Noncontentious and extrajudicial ecclesiastical matters, although
they are deliberative, never properly undergo judicial examination. Nor are
they of one order: they are primary matters or appendages of primary matters.
Primary ecclesiastical matters are those which God teaches and commands in his
Word as necessary for the inward sanctification of our soul, such as the
doctrines of faith, by an effectual knowledge of which, and the sacraments, by
a faithful use of which, we attain salvation. Their appendages are external
rites and ceremonies for the sake of order and decorum, such as appointed
places for hearing the Word, appointed hours, kneeling when pouring out prayers
before God, and receiving the Eucharist, and so forth.*

[Marginalia: Mucket, *On the Polity of the English Church*, chapter 3.]

[1623 printed p. 2 | IA leaf 14 | PDF p. 14]

We shall later teach that kneeling in receiving the Eucharist is not to be
numbered among rites of order and decorum. Mucket reduces the manifold
authority over causes or ecclesiastical matters to the following heads.
*Concerning noncontentious and extrajudicial ecclesiastical matters, there is
authority either to teach them publicly in preaching and to apply them to
people's consciences through exhortations and reproofs, if they are doctrines
of faith, or to administer them, if they are sacraments; or a power to
deliberate about them, if they are doctrines of faith or rites and ceremonies,
and to investigate their truth and equity according to the standard of the
divine Word; or, finally, an authority to promulgate and establish them by
public laws and edicts, so that the faithful people may religiously observe
and embrace them. Likewise, concerning contentious and judicial ecclesiastical
matters there is authority either to decide them when they have been brought
to the supreme court through several subordinate appeals, or to determine them
without any process of appeal, and to punish by imprisonment and monetary fine
the accused and other disobedient persons who are bound to appear before this
tribunal.*

The extent of royal power over ecclesiastical persons and causes is comprised
under the title “Supreme Governor.” By the term “governor” the prince is
understood to be not merely the guardian and avenger of both tables, but also a
judge and one who delegates jurisdiction to others. For although they removed
the metaphorical title “Head,” which Henry VIII first assumed, lest it cause
offense, and substituted “Supreme Governor,” the sense nevertheless remains
the same. Henry of Saltzburg renders the words of the first Elizabethan
statute as follows, except that—whether prudently or imprudently I do not
know—he omitted the words enclosed in parentheses: *That ecclesiastical
jurisdiction which formerly was exercised by some spiritual or ecclesiastical
power, or could lawfully be exercised, for visiting the ecclesiastical state
and order, and likewise for reforming, setting in order, and correcting
ecclesiastical persons and all kinds of errors, heresies, schisms, abuses,
offenses, and enormities within this realm, is perpetually united and annexed
to the imperial crown of this realm. The king of England may appoint and
authorize—whenever, as often as, and as long as he pleases—those of his
native-born subjects whom he deems suitable to execute and exercise under his
highness every kind of spiritual or ecclesiastical jurisdiction within this
realm, and every privilege and preeminence in any way pertaining to it: to
visit, reform, compose, correct, and amend all such errors, heresies, schisms,
abuses, offenses, contempts, and enormities whatsoever as may lawfully be
reformed, composed, corrected, restrained, or amended by any spiritual or
ecclesiastical power, authority, or jurisdiction, according to the good
pleasure of almighty God, the advancement of virtue, and the preservation of
the peace and unity of this realm.*

[Marginalia: *Becanus' Staff*, pp. 140–141.]

[1623 printed p. 3 | IA leaf 15 | PDF p. 15]

Immediately after these words of the statute comes the formula of the oath,
which is rendered as follows in the *Apology for the Oath of Allegiance*: *I,
A. B., openly testify and declare from my conscience that the royal majesty is
the one supreme governor of this realm and of all the other dominions and
territories of his majesty, in all spiritual or ecclesiastical matters or
causes as well as temporal ones; and that no foreign prince, person, prelate,
state, or potentate has or ought to have any ecclesiastical or spiritual
jurisdiction, power, superiority, preeminence, or authority within this realm.
I therefore wholly renounce and reject all foreign jurisdictions, powers,
superiorities, and authorities; and I promise henceforth to render faith and
true obedience to the royal majesty and his heirs and lawful successors, and
to the best of my ability to assist and defend all jurisdictions, privileges,
preeminences, and authorities granted or owed to the royal majesty, his heirs,
and successors, or united and annexed to the imperial crown of this realm. So
help me God, and by the contents of this book.*

[Marginalia: p. 66.]

The final words were added because the oath they call corporal is sworn by
touching the Gospels. This oath of supremacy is different from the oath of
allegiance with which the papists are pressed. The preceding statutory words
are therefore an authoritative explanation of the words of the oath. In her
injunctions Queen Elizabeth declares that the administration of divine things
in the church is foreign to her and that she claims nothing beyond what Henry
VIII and Edward VI claimed and exercised. Not even Henry VIII usurped the
administration of sacred rites. Indeed, I think kings would be both ashamed
and weary of performing sacred rites in the church. But apart from the
administration of sacred rites there is, as we saw above, various and manifold
power in ecclesiastical matters. Who doubts that the statute's words about
jurisdiction are to be understood not of the simple administration of sacred
functions? Moreover, in 1534 Henry VIII, with the consent of the estates in
Parliament, obtained the title Supreme Head of the Church of England and all
honors, dignities, preeminences, jurisdictions, authorities, immunities,
revenues, and privileges in any way pertaining to that title, to be united and
annexed to the imperial crown. He also obtained power to correct and reform
abuses, enormities, offenses, and other things formerly restrained or reformed
by some ecclesiastical jurisdiction, as Foxe relates in his *Acts and
Monuments*. What does Calvin say to this on Amos 7? *Those who so greatly
exalted Henry, king of England, were certainly inconsiderate men. For they
gave him supreme power over all things, and this always wounded me deeply.
They were blasphemers when they called him the supreme head of the church
under Christ.*

[Marginalia: p. 1029, edition of 1576.]

[1623 printed p. 4 | IA leaf 16 | PDF p. 16]

Sanders supplies the following writ of Edward VI: *Edward, by the grace of God
king of England, France, and Ireland, supreme head on earth of the English and
Irish church in spiritual as well as temporal causes, to the reverend Thomas,
archbishop of Canterbury, greeting. Since all authority to pronounce law, and
indeed every kind of jurisdiction, both what is called ecclesiastical and what
is secular, flows from the royal power as from the supreme head, and so forth,
we grant you by these presents, to continue during our pleasure, the power to
ordain any persons within your diocese of Canterbury and to promote them to
all sacred and priestly orders.*

[Marginalia: *On the English Schism*, book 2, p. 227.]

In Edward's first year it was enacted that bishops exercising jurisdiction
should be furnished with summonses and other documents in the royal name, not
their own, and that bishops should be created by royal patent alone without
the consent of dean and chapter. This statute was repealed in Mary's first
year and restored in James's first. Doctor Bancroft, who was eventually raised
to the archbishopric of Canterbury, in a sermon preached at Paul's Cross in
1589 and afterward printed, attributes to the royal majesty not merely part of
the pope's power, but all the honors, dignities, preeminences, jurisdictions,
privileges, and revenues formerly thought due to the pope; he calls the queen
a certain lesser goddess.

[Marginalia: pp. 70, 68.]

Our Bancroft, John Spottiswood, pseudo-archbishop of St Andrews, asserted the
same thing when about to pronounce a sentence of deposition against a minister
because he had celebrated the Lord's Supper without kneeling to receive the
Eucharist: “I tell you, N., that the king is now pope and will continue to be.”
Royal power in the English church is commonly called ecclesiastical power.
But power may be called ecclesiastical either by reason of its object, because
it is exercised upon sacred persons and things, or by reason of the manner in
which it deals with sacred persons and things, because it operates upon them
in an ecclesiastical, not a civil, manner. We can easily discern from the
foregoing whether royal power is ecclesiastical only objectively or also
formally, but still more easily from what follows. Let us therefore first see
what the particular rights of the Royal Supremacy are.

Particularly, it consists in those things which are either first granted by
statute or restored by statute as due by right to the royal crown. The things
first granted by statute are:

[1623 printed p. 5 | IA leaf 17 | PDF p. 17]

1. To receive first fruits or annates after every vacancy; 2. to receive
annually the tenths of all ecclesiastical benefices.

The exaction of annates, first imposed by the pope as an extraordinary levy
and afterward converted into an ordinary tax, was finally condemned at the
Council of Basel. Nevertheless, in disregard of the council's decree it
prevailed widely, even in France itself despite the Pragmatic Sanction, at
least in respect of the greater benefices. First fruits or annates are the
income of one year exacted from a new beneficiary. Papal tenths were first
instituted for holy war and expeditions against Saladin, but were afterward
converted into annual charges imposed upon beneficiaries for extirpating
heretics and other more weighty business of the church. Annates had the same
origin and purpose as papal tenths: namely, that with them as the sinews of war
the Christian battle line might defend the orthodox faith throughout the
East. Rebuffus, as reported by Bleynianus, asserts that the pope, after the
example of Aaron, may as supreme high priest and head of the whole
ecclesiastical body exact monetary tenths from beneficiaries in proportion to
the assessed—or, as they say, taxed—value of their benefices, just as they
exact agricultural tithes from others.

[Marginalia: *Introduction to the Practice and Theory of Benefices*, p. 70;
Rastell, title “First Fruits and Tenths,” no. 6.]

These papal tenths were united and perpetually annexed to the imperial crown
under Henry VIII “for the increase and maintenance of the imperial crown and
of the dignity of supreme head of the English church,” as is expressly stated
in the statute of the thirty-fourth year of Henry VIII, chapter 17. Thus, as
they were formerly paid to the Roman pontiff as supreme head of the universal
church, they began to be paid under the same title to the king of England,
namely as supreme head of the English church. Under Henry VIII the Court of
First Fruits and Tenths—formerly papal, now royal—was first established.

Other rights are restored by statute as due by right to the royal crown. They
are either rights customarily exercised by a prince in his dominions, or
rights not customarily exercised until the usurped papal authority had been
entirely driven from the bounds of the English empire. Those always exercised
by a prince in his dominions include:

1. The supreme right of patronage, called *patronage paramount*, so that when
time lapses the collation of benefices devolves to the prince and never beyond
him.

[1623 printed p. 6 | IA leaf 18 | PDF p. 18]

We cannot approve ordinary presentations and collations and their devolutions;
we demand revolutions instead—that is, that the church's right be restored to
her. For the venerable Beza does not hesitate to affirm that the whole market
in presentations, plenary rights of patronage, collations, resignations, and
other crimes of this kind proceeded from Satan. In his separate book on the
marks of the true church he again rises against these crimes: *Where, I ask,
were the rights of ordinary or so-called devolved collation and resignation
ever devised, ground up, strained, and finally served for high and low alike
to taste, except in Satan's kitchen?*

[Marginalia: *Confession of Faith*, chapter 35, p. 136.]

A patron's presentation takes away the church's freedom to choose her own
pastors. When the same right, after the lapse of four or six months, devolves
step by step to superiors by right of lapse and is not restored to the church
as though by postliminy, her bondage is increased. The same must be said of
collation. A man consecrated to the ministry after the church's free election
has by that very act the right to receive the fruits of the benefice. For the
benefice—the right to receive temporal revenues—is given because of the
office—the right to administer spiritual things—and temporal things are
annexed to spiritual ones.

Just as Pliny complained that in his age vessels were made from jewels in
which gold was only an accessory, whereas jewels ordinarily adorn golden
vessels, so Duarenus complains: *What was once deservedly regarded as primary
now scarcely occupies the place of a mere accessory.* Temporal revenues are
annexed to spiritual things not antecedently, but consequently, as an effect
following its cause, not conversely. They are therefore most properly joined
to and compared with spiritual things as accessory to principal, and imitate
their nature all the more. For that reason the whole which results from both
rights—the right to administer spiritual things and to receive temporal
things—and is, as it were, morally one, is called an ecclesiastical benefice
in current usage. To confer a benefice, therefore, is to confer both rights:
to administer spiritual things and to receive temporal ones. In the collation
of a benefice the exercise of orders and of the keys in the internal forum is
given. A royal grant bestowing a benefice confers upon a presbyter the power of
a parish priest, which he did not have before the grant.

[Marginalia: *On the Sacred Ministries and Benefices of the Church*, book 8,
chapter 3.]

Henry of Saltzburg gathers from English laws that “not only do collations of
benefices belong to the kings of England, but the kings themselves act as
primates or supreme ordinaries of the English church.”

[Marginalia: *Becanus' Staff*, p. 107.]

By long-standing custom the kings of France also confer, during a vacancy of
the episcopal see, benefices which the living and surviving bishop ought to
have conferred. They barbarously call this inveterate custom the law of the
regalia, or the right of regalia. We do not ask what practice arose—whether
during a vacant see, as in France, or by devolution after a lapse of time, as
in England—from the negligence, connivance, flattery, or covenants with kings
of Roman pontiffs or bishops;

[1623 printed p. 7 | IA leaf 19 | PDF p. 19]

we ask what ought to be done by right.

2. To receive the revenues of vacant bishoprics for his own use.

This second head belongs to the right which in France they call the right of
regalia. It arose from the old law of clientship and fiefs and from the
affinity which the estates and possessions of prelates have with military and
secular fiefs. For just as military benefices become escheats at the death of
the last tenant, so when a see becomes vacant through the bishop's death,
translation, or in another way, kings appoint stewards through whose service
they receive the escheated revenues of the vacant bishopric.

3. To grant permission to elect a bishop. 4. To nominate to the chapter a fit
man for the bishopric whom they must elect. 5. To give royal assent to the
elect.

The power to elect pastors was entrusted to the church; and if the power was
entrusted, so was its exercise, which does not depend absolutely upon the will
of princes. Otherwise a power which could never be brought into act without
an additional power would have been given in vain. Whether the gospel should
be preached would thus depend upon the will of princes. During the first three
hundred years, when the supreme Caesars were Christianity's bitterest enemies,
the churches elected their pastors. Court parasites may say that the case of a
heathen magistrate differs from that of a Christian. But what if a magistrate
who is Christian in name refuses to grant permission? “The law regards as
nonexistent what does not appear.” The learned and great Junius rightly says
in his *Ecclesiastes*: *A third kind of change has arisen in our time, when
those who undertake the restoration of the churches after overthrowing what
they call papal tyranny have not cared to restore to the churches that
ecclesiastical right of appointment and ordination. Instead, they entrust
everything to the magistrate's faith and think that he must control both pages
in the affairs of the church, merely because he is a Christian magistrate.*

[Marginalia: book 3, chapter 1.]

Moreover, this power does not flow from the prince insofar as he is Christian,
for it is called a power due by right to the royal crown. Whether the
crown-holder is heathen or Christian, male or female, man or boy, the power is
therefore the same.

When permission to elect has been granted by royal patent, a letter naming the
person to be elected is sent with it to the dean and chapter. Under the
statutes they must elect him before the twentieth day, or the election
devolves to the metropolitan.

[1623 printed p. 8 | IA leaf 20 | PDF p. 20]

Piacesius describes this imaginary election and royal nomination: *Kings and
princes, under the pretended right of patronage, nominate at pleasure the man
to be elected; and the chapter, with scarcely a vestige of free election
remaining anywhere, feigns the process of its own election concerning the
person thus nominated.*

[Marginalia: *Episcopal Practice*, preface.]

The election of pastors ought not to be constrained but free, not positive
but comparative. “He is to be chosen for the priesthood,” says Origen, “who is
more excellent than the whole people, more learned, more holy, and more
eminent in every virtue.”

[Marginalia: *Homilies on Leviticus*, homily 6.]

Gregory says in his bull of 1 May 1591: *If in every rank and ministry of the
church care must be taken to choose men who build up the faithful by religion
and holiness, certainly far greater care must be taken not to err in electing
the man set above every rank. The care of the whole household is entrusted to
him as a faithful and prudent servant. He ought to be an example to others in
every virtue and in observing God's commandments, since the salvation of the
flock certainly depends greatly upon the excellent life and teaching of a good
pastor.*

[Marginalia: See Piacesius, *Episcopal Practice*, p. 3.]

In the first primitive—that is, apostolic—church, the election of pastors and
other ministers of the church belonged to the church (Acts 1:23; 6:5; 14:23).
By votes gathered individually, delegates were chosen to carry alms to foreign
churches (2 Corinthians 8:19), much more those appointed to weightier and more
sacred offices. In the church following the apostolic age, election belonged
to clergy and people. Cyprian says: *The people themselves chiefly possess the
power either to elect worthy priests or to refuse the unworthy.* Pope Leo
says: *Let the subscription of the clergy, the testimony of the honorable,
and the consent of the order and the people be retained. He who will preside
over all should be elected by all.* Charlemagne and his son Louis testify in
their capitularies that this was the primitive form of elections: *Not being
ignorant of the sacred canons, so that the holy church might more freely enjoy
her honor in God's name, we have given our assent to the ecclesiastical order,
so that bishops may be elected from their own diocese by the clergy and the
people according to the statutes of the canons, without partiality for persons
or receipt of gifts, on account of the merit of their life and the gift of
wisdom, so that by example and words they may benefit their subjects in every
way.*

[Marginalia: Cyprian, Letter 4 and Letter 68; Distinction 63, canon 34,
*Sacrorum Canonum*; *On the Ecclesiastical Commonwealth*, book 3, chapter 3.]

The words occur in Gratian's *Decree*. Whoever desires a collection of canons
and examples concerning the people's votes, election, rejection, decree, and
consent should consult Spalatensis. Bilson acknowledges this, but at the same
time asserts that this form of election was rightly changed, setting the skill
of one prince against an unskilled people. What prince, I ask, will they give
us who is more skilled than all the citizens in discerning the gifts of
pastors? Or who is more learned or more discerning of spirits than all the
honorable and leading inhabitants of a diocese—the nobles or knights, judges,
magistrates, lawyers, and other learned men? The citizens of one city ought
not to attend to this business alone, but all the diocesan people, personally
or through their delegates. What touches all ought to be attended to by all.

[Marginalia: *On the Government of the Church*, chapter 15.]

[1623 printed p. 9 | IA leaf 21 | PDF p. 21]

Next I ask whether the prince is more skilled even than the clergy of the
city. The clergy too had their part, and indeed the principal part, in
elections. Why do I say the city's clergy? I would say the clergy of the whole
diocese. “Divine law has not determined that these rather than those among the
clergy should elect,” says Bellarmine.

[Marginalia: *On Clerics*, chapter 8.]

Third, I ask whether the prince is more skilled than all the bishops of the
province, for they too took part in episcopal elections. Finally, this power
is united to the royal crown and is on that account owed even to a heathen
prince. Bilson next objects that popular tumults and schisms were frequently
stirred up by the people in elections, and that the people's right was
therefore rightly transferred to the prince. By that reasoning there will be
no free election of magistrates in cities, since killings and popular tumults
have often arisen in electing them. A godly prince ought to restrain tumults,
not abolish liberties; to ensure that all things are done orderly and
decently, not to force pastors upon an unwilling flock. “No order so prudent
and advantageous is instituted among people that inconveniences do not soon
grow up around it. Wise men ought next to consider how inconveniences are
prudently to be treated, not how a sacred institution is rashly changed,” says
Junius.

[Marginalia: *On Clerics*, chapter 7, note 23.]

Pastors can direct the people and recall them to peace with gentle warnings;
the magistrate can bring them to order. On that occasion the people can be
deprived of their freedom by devolving the election to a greater
ecclesiastical assembly. Other remedies for this evil can also be devised.
But entirely to abolish the freedom which Christ the bridegroom gave to the
church his bride is sacrilege and robbery. Moreover, it was not the people but
the bishops themselves who stirred up these factions and schisms. Nazianzen
indicates that the bishops' perpetual pursuit of the presidency and rule was
the occasion of schisms and tumults: *On account of this all our affairs are
tossed and shaken; on account of this the ends of the earth are embroiled in
suspicion and in a war so muffled that it does not even have a name; on
account of this we risk losing that great and new name. Would that there were
no presidency, precedence of place, or violent preeminence, so that men were
known by virtue alone. But now this right side and left, higher and lower
seat, and the introduced custom of walking in front or at the same rank have
brought upon us many disasters to no purpose.*

[Marginalia: Oration 1 after his return to the city.]

Ammianus Marcellinus observed the same: *Damasus and Ursinus, burning beyond
measure to seize the episcopal see, split into parties and fought most bitterly,
both sides advancing with supporters to the point of mortal wounds. Nor,
considering the display of urban wealth, do I deny that men coveting this
office should contend with every exertion to obtain what they desire, since
once they obtain it they will be so secure that they grow rich from matrons'
offerings, ride in carriages carefully dressed, and provide banquets so lavish
that their feasts surpass royal tables, and so forth.*

[Marginalia: book 27.]

“With what faith, I ask,” says Junius,

[1623 printed p. 10 | IA leaf 22 | PDF p. 22]

“can these incendiaries hurl at the wretched people the charge of their own
arson and treachery?” “It is indeed true,” says Spalatensis, *that the people
are a many-headed beast and extremely ready for sedition. But this fault in
these elections is not innate in the faithful people. They would be no less
grave in moderation and zealous for the public welfare in elections now than
formerly if bishops were such as they were in the pure church, and if the
episcopate were not an honor or occasion of pride, and haughty pomp were as
far removed from our bishops as formerly. For then perhaps worthy men would
often be drawn and compelled by the people to the episcopate, even by force
and against their will, as of old. But after the situation was reversed, and
the episcopate in many places—and especially in our churches—began to be
worldly pomp, powerful men, spurred by ambition, set their hearts upon it in
competition, corrupted the people by evil arts, and inflamed them to sedition.
Let the episcopate be restored to its true condition, and we shall find the
people as moderate in elections as before.*

[Marginalia: *On Clerics*, chapter 7, no. 28; *On the Ecclesiastical
Commonwealth*, book 3, chapter 3, no. 53.]

If patriarchs and metropolitans, disregarding the greatness of cities, had
lived in imitation of certain provincial bishops, as Marcellinus says, with
pastors teaching and leading the people, schisms would rarely—indeed scarcely
ever—have arisen. Bilson praises the Church of God's exceptional prudence in
not only admitting but requesting the people's consent in electing her
bishops: *I find so many and such great benefits from it attested in church
histories. It resulted in the people's obtaining their desire, peacefully
receiving their pastors, willingly supporting them, diligently hearing them,
loving them from the heart, and indeed exposing their life and every fortune
to danger rather than let danger come upon them.*

[Marginalia: *On the Government of the Church*, chapter 15, p. 417.]

Are not these enumerated advantages matters of perpetual equity? Why, then,
are men who act as rulers rather than perform a ministry forced upon the
churches? When free election ceased, golden priests ceased too; pseudo-bishops
introduced either popery or Epicureanism. Whence came so many mushrooms,
asses, blocks, gamblers, pigs, and even wild boars sent into the Lord's
vineyard, if not from the courts of kings and Roman pontiffs? Why did I say
episcopal election depends on the will of kings? I would have spoken more
truly if I had said it depends on courtiers. Those who surpass others in
corrupting some chief courtier with gifts, flattery, compliance, and every
service of adulation customarily win the palm and prize in this contest of
ambition. These are the men to be appointed commanders and camp prefects! But
why do I linger over royal nomination and the chapter's imaginary election,
when the prince can personally nominate and elect a bishop without even the
shadow of an election, as we said above concerning Edward VI's statute,
restored under James I, and will do so whenever he pleases? “Where is that
canon,” asks Athanasius, “that the future bishop should be sent from the
palace?”

[Marginalia: Letter to those living the solitary life.]

[1623 printed p. 11 | IA leaf 23 | PDF p. 23]

As for ratification of an election, if it is necessary, it comes to the same
thing as the power to elect which precedes the election. What if the prince,
asked at both convenient and inconvenient times, remains inexorable—will the
election always be void? The condition of churches in choosing pastors seems
to me far worse than that of cities in choosing magistrates, and I do not see
why the law should be unequal in equal cases. Emperors sometimes involved
themselves in patriarchal elections and, contrary to the canons, sometimes
changed the lawful form of election against much resistance. Yet they did not
make the elections of inferior bishops or presentations to bishoprics subject
to themselves until everything declined. We both grant and approve that
lesser magistrates, such as city magistrates, participated in elections with
the people. We saw above what Charlemagne and his son Louis decreed. When
bishops became royal feudatories and vassals, they sold the freedom of
election. “Just as, upon the vacancy of a fief, the liege lord upon whom it
depends supplies a vassal at his own discretion and choice, just as lords
customarily choose servants for themselves,” says Gentiletus.

[Marginalia: *Examination of the Council of Trent*, p. 125.]

6. To receive the bishop's oath of homage.

This oath too arose from feudal law. By reason of their fiefs, bishops, like
other feudatories, acknowledged the emperor as lord and bound themselves to
him by an oath of fealty and homage—or, to use a more fitting term, “homage.”
Frederick I writes thus to Pope Adrian: *Why should we not require homage and
oaths concerning our regalia from bishops who hold our regalia? Let them
either surrender our regalia to us, or render to God what is God's and to
Caesar what is Caesar's.* The same emperor stripped the bishops of Bremen and
Halberstadt of their fiefs because they had not accompanied him into Italy.
Thus ecclesiastics involved themselves in secular business in order to obtain
estates and possessions, regalia, cities, and towns with temporal jurisdiction.
“The clergy's avarice,” says Gentiletus, “made them prefer to grow rich by
subjecting themselves to compliance and service, rather than to retain modest
fortunes, the freedom of ecclesiastical elections, and immunity from feudal
servitude.”

[Marginalia: *Examination*, p. 125.]

We therefore see bishops serving not so much the church as ministers as kings
as clients and vassals—servants of kings and lords of churches—both parties
profaning sacred offices by bargain and removing the churches' freedom.
Gratian says in the *Decree* that some bishops, content with only the Levitical
portion, live from first fruits and tithes and wholly reject temporal goods,
lest on their account they be bound to the occupation of secular warfare and
other services. “There are others,” he says, “who, not content with tithes and
first fruits, possess estates, villages, castles, and cities.”

[Marginalia: Cause 23, question 8.]

[1623 printed p. 12 | IA leaf 24 | PDF p. 24]

The gloss on this passage says: *Bishops who do not hold a fief from princes,
but live only from first fruits, tithes, and offerings, ought not to be
occupied in secular warfare. But those who hold fiefs from princes are bound
to serve princes, unless they have obtained immunity in this matter from those
from whom they hold the fiefs.* From this passage of Gratian, Spalatensis shows
that ecclesiastical prelates are bound by reason of their regalia to fight for
the king and pay him tribute. He must therefore preach the gospel in the
midst of the church and with another trumpet declare war to the soldiers!

[Marginalia: *On the Ecclesiastical Commonwealth*, book 6, chapter 7, no. 90.]

We say nothing about the real burdens of fiefs; but a bishop, no more than a
presbyter, ought not to bind himself to personal burdens, especially armed
warfare. He is bound rather to surrender the regalia than enslave himself to
services which call him away from exercising the pastoral office. Nor is it
piety but profanity in princes to give temporalities and regalia to the church
on this condition, or to turn gifts already made to this use, so as to remove
the church's freedom in elections and make pastors vassals. Meanwhile, we do
not condemn the general oath of allegiance which all subjects must swear. But
prelates swear this oath not as citizens of the realm to their king, but as
vassals or clients to their liege lord. The condition of a greater benefice
ought not to differ from that of a lesser. Investitures by staff or rod and
ring arose by reason of regalia and temporal possessions; by them emperors
declared their assent and consigned temporalities to the elect. But all these
things arose in later centuries.

7. To bring any ecclesiastical persons before a civil judge and condemn them
for crimes committed against the peace of the realm and the royal dignity. 8.
To command ecclesiastical judges to cease trying matters of the civil forum
before themselves.

The following rights were not customarily exercised until usurped papal
authority had been wholly driven from the bounds of the English empire. They
concern: 1. appeals; 2. canons; and 3. benefices.

1. They concern appeals interposed at the instance of a party, in that the
final appeal is to the prince and not beyond the realm. 2. Through the
chancellor of England, under the Great Seal, the prince appoints delegated
judges to decide the cause.

[1623 printed p. 13 | IA leaf 25 | PDF p. 25]

If litigation in some cause began before an archdeacon or his official, the
appeal goes to the bishop of the diocese; if before the bishop or his
commissioner, to the archbishop; if before the archbishop's archdeacon, to the
court called the Arches, where the Dean of the Arches answers all appeals in
the province. From that court the cause is brought to the archbishop by the
statute of the twenty-fourth year of Henry VIII, and from the archbishop to the
Court of Chancery by the statute of the twenty-fifth year of Henry VIII. When
the cause has reached Chancery, delegated judges are appointed under the Great
Seal to determine the suit and cause, and no further appeal lies from these
commissioners.

[Marginalia: Rastell, title “Appeal,” 1; Rastell, title “Rome,” 1.]

The causes which can be brought through subordinate appeals are absolutely
all ecclesiastical causes: not only those improperly so called, such as
matrimonial or testamentary causes, but also those properly so called—not
only an assault upon the altar, breaking the mystical cup, overturning the
sacred table, use of a dead hand for magic, and similar things Mucket
enumerates, but also heresy, simony, idolatry, and error in articles of
religion. In short, every cause and suit in which an appeal formerly went to
the pope is brought by appeal to the prince and no farther. In exempt places,
where an appeal formerly went immediately to the pope, it goes immediately to
the prince in Chancery by the same statute of the twenty-fifth year of Henry
VIII and the first of Elizabeth.

[Marginalia: Rastell, ibid.]

It is therefore impermissible to appeal to an ecumenical council or a general
council of the Reformed churches, because one may not appeal beyond the realm.
Nor is there any intermediate appeal here to a diocesan synod or provincial
council; the progress is always from one individual to another.

2. These concern ecclesiastical canons or laws, whether to be enacted,
administered, or relaxed. They concern laws to be enacted in that:

1. The prince can establish new laws about ceremonies and rites with the
advice in this matter either of his commissioners for ecclesiastical causes or
of the metropolitan.

David the prophetic king, instructed only by the Spirit and by the hand of
Jehovah resting upon him, delivered to Solomon the structure of the temple and
the divisions of priests and Levites (1 Chronicles 24:12). “Hezekiah restored
the Levites in the house of Jehovah with cymbals, lyres, and harps according
to the command of David, Gad the king's seer, and Nathan the prophet; for that
command came from Jehovah through the prophets” (2 Chronicles 29:25). King
Hezekiah did not institute it,

[1623 printed p. 14 | IA leaf 26 | PDF p. 26]

but merely restored what David, both king and prophet, had instituted. And
David, although a prophet, instituted nothing new unless Jehovah commanded it
through the prophets. We have no Davidic kings and no seeing metropolitans.
Would that they were not blind, or were not blinded by avarice, ambition, and
courtly splendor! Let us therefore return to the church's ordinary assemblies
and to Holy Scripture, from whose pattern one may not depart even a
fingernail's breadth—not even in making curtain straps, pegs, or bronze rings.
General laws have been delivered to the church in the sacred books; by their
standard the church herself is directed to adapt things indifferent in their
own nature to use according to varying circumstances, so that all things may
be done orderly and decently, to God's glory and the church's edification.
Things indifferent by nature, unless suitably applied to use, hinder
edification, disturb divine order, and become unlawful. Things indifferent as
such—that is, with their indifference preserved and remaining—are useless and
idle; adapted to some use, they become useful or useless. Before laws are made
about things, the question whether they are indifferent must precede the
question of their use. Then their use must be directed according to the
standard of Holy Scripture's general canons. Let statesmen judge political
use and ecclesiastical men in ecclesiastical assemblies judge ecclesiastical
use. Moreover, not all rites and ceremonies are indifferent by nature, and
laws can be made about rites and ceremonies that are in no respect
indifferent. When Barrow was examined, the English bishops said that the royal
majesty could devise and institute any form of ecclesiastical polity by virtue
of his supremacy in ecclesiastical causes, according to the author of the
petition to the queen.

[Marginalia: p. 76.]

Finally, Beza says in his letter to Grindal, bishop of London: *Whether one
regards God's Word or all the ancient canons, by what right is a civil
magistrate personally permitted to introduce new rites or abolish old ones in
already constituted churches, or bishops to establish anything new without
the judgment and consent of their presbytery? I have not yet learned. But
because I see that these two plagues—the shameful and ambitious flatteries of
superior bishops, some abusing their princes' virtues and others serving their
vices—destroyed the Christian church, until matters came to such a point that
the most powerful metropolitan of the West, by God's just judgment punishing
both magistrates and bishops, seized divine and human things for himself, I
confess that I shudder completely whenever I think of them. I foresee the same
or still harsher punishments threatening most peoples who embraced the gospel
at first and from which they now gradually fall away.* The prince, therefore,
with a very few commissioners or with the metropolitan, can introduce
superstitious and ridiculous rites and deform the external worship of God and
the outward face of ecclesiastical polity.

[1623 printed p. 15 | IA leaf 27 | PDF p. 27]

2. A provincial or national synod ought not to be convened without a writ from
the prince directed to the metropolitans.

Far be it from us to deny that the prince can convene synods. The church also
has the same power. “If two of you agree,” and again, “where two or three are
gathered in my name,” says Christ, “there I shall be in their midst” (Matthew
18:19–20). From these words even the hierarchists infer the authority of
synods, arguing from the lesser to the greater. Someone will say that to
summon and assemble in one gathering is a human act. To assemble in one
gathering is indeed human: if for civil affairs, it is political; if for godly
exercises and in Christ's name, it is human but ecclesiastical and holy. Thus
in the Old Testament an assembly of the godly is often called a holy
convocation. The commission of power is from Christ; permission is from the
prince. The bestowal of power handed down by Christ is not taken away by the
advent of a Christian magistrate. The exercise of the bestowed power ought not
to be rashly attempted without consulting the magistrate or without the
authority of laws. But if heresies rage, disputes multiply, schisms and
factions tear the Christian people apart, and the church seems nearly
overwhelmed by waves of schisms, errors, and crimes, while the magistrate
obstinately refuses consent, we must not delay or cease from our duty. We must
risk danger to seek a remedy through a synod. This was not Constantine's
method of fostering the churches, but the method by which Licinius and others
tried to overthrow them. The church's condition under a Christian magistrate
ought not to be worse than under a heathen; ecclesiastical freedom ought to be
increased, not diminished. What is conferred upon the church is not
transferred to a Christian prince but only strengthened. The prince's power is
cumulative, not privative. He can therefore convene a synod and approve the
power conferred upon the church, but he cannot deprive her of the power or its
exercise. The powers are distinct but not divided, unless the magistrate
divides himself from the church. Doctor Field, the most learned of the
hierarchists, asserts that the church has power to gather councils and
deliberate about things concerning the church's welfare and salvation even if
worldly princes do not favor them or lend a helping hand. He likewise says
that bishops, primates, and metropolitans have authority to gather synods, and
that this authority does not so depend upon the power of princes that they
cannot exercise it without their consent and knowledge when the princes are
enemies of the faith.

[Marginalia: *On the Church*, book 5, p. 407.]

But in the statutes this power is called united to the royal crown and thus
dependent on the prince even if he is a heathen. Moreover, we have shown that
it does not depend absolutely and simply upon a Christian magistrate. Third,
if primates and metropolitans

[1623 printed p. 16 | IA leaf 28 | PDF p. 28]

have no power to convene national and provincial councils except one
absolutely dependent upon a Christian magistrate, they have the principal part
of their office in dependence upon him. Their work is consequently unnecessary
in the church, as we shall show later in its proper place. A prince intending
to convene a council can direct his writ to someone other than the
metropolitan. Spalatensis says: *We do not maintain that the right of convening
councils belongs to princes in such a way that ecclesiastical prelates cannot
assemble by mutual consent or be summoned by the authority of a metropolitan,
primate, or patriarch.*

[Marginalia: *On the Ecclesiastical Commonwealth*, book 5, chapter 5, no. 89.]

We admit what he adds: for a cause of suspicion princes can prevent them from
assembling without consultation. Let those disturbed by suspicion and
suspended in fear be consulted; let every hiding place of suspicion be
cleared; let delegates or spies be sent. Otherwise it will always be very easy
to invent causes. “Reason itself certainly indicates,” Spalatensis says in the
same place, *that calling and convening adds nothing to or takes nothing from
the authority of councils. For even if many bishops happened to assemble on
some occasion without intending a council, what would prevent them from also
handling and deciding ecclesiastical matters? For wherever two or three are
gathered—not “summoned”—in Christ's name, Christ himself is in their midst.
Those who have assembled to handle the things of Christ are said to be
gathered in Christ's name.*

3. Nothing may be handled or determined in a synod unless the prince has first
been consulted and gives assent.

What if, after being asked, he remains obstinate and hardens his mind? Will
they cease in a case of necessity? The church has been given power not only to
convene but also to define and determine, and she cannot be deprived of that
power. The same arguments apply here as in the preceding article. Yet this
power too is said to be united to the royal crown and consequently to belong
even to a heathen prince, without whose permission and assent nothing can be
decreed.

4. Nothing has the force of law until royal assent has been given to the
things the synod determined should be decreed.

If the meaning is that subjects cannot be compelled to obedience by temporal
penalties without the sanction of civil laws, we embrace it. But if the
meaning is that nothing will have the force of a canon without the prince's
assent, and that the church cannot exercise spiritual jurisdiction over
offenders through ecclesiastical censures, it appears to rest on no equity.
The arguments already given oppose this meaning. Yet this seems to be the
hierarchists' meaning.

[1623 printed p. 17 | IA leaf 29 | PDF p. 29]

“For who is it,” asks Bilson, “who restricts and restrains the frequency of
synods, or takes away their power to make canons and decide ecclesiastical
causes—the metropolitan or the prince?” In the same place: *Otherwise, where a
Christian governs, synods possess no power in themselves to make decrees, nor
can they claim for themselves the right to hear and settle controversies in
the church when the prince is unwilling or does not permit it.*

[Marginalia: *On the Government of the Church*, chapter 16, p. 459.]
