MATTHEW 6:10

Thy will be done on earth, as it is in heaven.

Our prayers are means for the fulfillment of God's will.

It is asked whether the distinction between the will of good pleasure and the
will of sign is to be admitted as the Arminians transmit it. The answer is no.
For according to them, by the will of good pleasure God wills the very same
things to occur which he wills to occur by the will of sign; and they maintain
that the will of sign is the same will of good pleasure, but revealed to us
rather than hidden. Thus Arminius, disputation 4, thesis 58.

But we teach that the will of good pleasure is God's decree, both concerning
the good things which he himself will give and effect with a necessary result,
and concerning the evil things which he himself permits to be done by others.
And the will of sign is God's

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 182 | PDF p. 233]

simple delight in and approval of a thing as morally lawful and honorable,
although the futurition of that good thing was never decreed by God.

God therefore wills and approves many things by his approving will which he
never decreed to occur by the will of good pleasure. Thus he wills the
obedience of Judas, Herod, and Pilate; yet he decreed that they should crucify
the Lord of glory, while he permitted it.

2. For God approves by the will of sign perfect obedience to his law. But by
the will of good pleasure he decreed from eternity that no one except Christ
would perfectly fulfill the law.

3. For God preaches the gospel to many obstinate people and approves their
repentance; yet from eternity he decreed not to give them the efficacious grace
without which they cannot repent.

4. What God has decreed by the will of good pleasure to occur or not to occur
will necessarily occur or not occur in its time. This is God's will simply
speaking, which God cannot fail to fulfill, because he willed and decreed it to
occur. Matthew 27:35; John 19:36; Matthew 2:15, 17–18; John 2:22. But God
approves as good many things which he never decreed to occur.

Hence it is false that we posit contradictory wills in God. For we do not
teach that God's approving something means that he decreed from eternity that
what he commands should occur or that what he prohibits should not occur. 2.
Nor do we teach that God's approving will implies that God decreed from
eternity to effect the things he approves, unless we place an obstacle.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 183 | PDF p. 234]

Allied to this is the question whether, in the calling of everyone in the
visible church, God's intention is that each and every person should obey and
be saved. The Remonstrants affirm it, articles 2 and 3, page 10.

We deny it. 1. For God does not fail in his intention, as has been proved. But
not all obey and are saved.

2. For one who is infinitely wise does not intend what he foreknows he will
never obtain.

3. For the law says to everyone, “Do this and thou shalt live.” But God does
not intend perfect obedience in all mortals, for in that case he would intend
to render Christ's death frustrated.

4. God preaches the Word to many for the end that they should be hardened, as
Scripture says, Isaiah 6:9; Matthew 13:14–15. The adversaries also concede it.

5. For God does not elect to glory everyone who is in the visible church.
Therefore, and so forth.

Allied to this is the question whether, because God not only approves the
obedience of the reprobate as something fitting which they are obligated to
perform, but also sweetly invites them, solicits them with entreaties, prays,
adjures, and grieves, is saddened, and complains concerning those who do not
obey, he therefore intends their obedience. The Remonstrants affirm it,
article 1, page 57.

We deny it. 1. For from the sweet invitation only a gentle obligation of the
creature to obedience and a serious approval are concluded, as is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 184 | PDF p. 235]

God's delight in obedience, since it is a holy thing owed to him,
morally pleasing, fitting for a human being, and salutary. These affections in
God therefore add nothing to God's simple delight in obedience except, as it
were, a certain intensity and vehemence of the divine obligation to it. By
this he testifies that the obedience of all is seriously commanded by him,
that human beings are obligated to it, and thus that it would be uniquely and
vehemently pleasing to him and salutary for human beings if it were performed,
although he has decreed that it will not be performed through human fault.

2. For from God's grief over the disobedience of the reprobate, it can no more
be concluded that there was in him an intention that they should actually
perform obedience than it can be concluded that there are in God truly human
passions, vain wishes, ineffectual prayers, and the unhappiness of a God who
grieves because he cannot bring his desires to fulfillment.

3. With the freedom of the creature preserved, God can infallibly procure
their obedience through motives which he has hidden in the treasuries of his
wisdom, as Arminius testifies. He could do so if he had decreed, intended, and
desired it from eternity as earnestly as human beings desire those things to
occur over whose nonperformance they are saddened and grieve.

It is asked what should be thought about the distinction of the will into an
efficient and a permitting will. We acknowledge it; the Arminians here

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 185 | PDF p. 236]

disagree with us. For the efficient will is that will which is the first and
highest cause of all positive beings. The permitting will, however, affirms
one thing and denies two. It affirms a positive act of the will of good
pleasure. For it is not a bare negation of will, as the Arminians maintain,
because by the permitting will God wills that sins occur for the sake of God's
glory. The Arminians maintain that by the permitting will God wills nothing,
but merely does not will that the persons permitted should sin. It is
therefore, according to them, an idle and inactive will, or a negation of
volition rather than a true will or volition. But for us the permitting will
denies two things. 1. It denies moral efficiency, for God is not the cause of
the things which he permits. 2. It denies the approving will, for God by no
means approves the things he permits or prescribes that they should be done by
the creature.

EPHESIANS 1:11

Who effects all things according to the counsel of his will.

It is asked whether God absolutely decreed all things, even contingents, from
eternity. Arminius's mind is expressed in these words, on article 7, page 143:
“The term *determination* is ambiguous. For it signifies either such a
determination of God”—by which he establishes that something should
occur—“that, when any action, motion, or impulse of God whatever has been
posited, the second cause remains free with respect to the power and use of
the power to act or not to act, so that it can suspend its action; or

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 186 | PDF p. 237]

such a determination that, when it has been posited, the second cause does not
remain free, at least with respect to the use of its power to suspend its
action, once God's action, motion, and impulse have been posited. Rather, from
it the cause is necessarily bent toward one of the two alternatives before the
determinate act itself has been produced by the free creature. In the former
mode, God by his eternal decree determined contingents toward one of the two
alternatives; but not in the latter mode, because it is a contradiction for
something to be contingent and necessary.” The Remonstrants speak likewise in
their *Apology*. We judge these words of Arminius to be false and blasphemous,
and fabricated fables contrived to please the people.

1. For the determination of contingent acts toward one of the two
alternatives in such a way that either this or that alternative, either the
free act or the non-act, follows, is a determination toward neither
alternative. And a determination toward neither alternative is
non-determination and indifference, which is openly contradictory.

2. A determination of contingent acts of faith—for example, Peter's
faith—upon which Peter's nonfaith can follow, is the same as if Arminius said
that Peter's faith is indeterminate as much after God's determination as
before it. But to say that God determines toward one the things which, after
the determination, are not determined toward one is to make sport and a joke
of the reader.

3. The other member of this distinction is non-determination. No one could
imagine what is divided in such an unhappy division, unless someone were to

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 187 | PDF p. 238]

say: “A human being is rightly divided into a human being and a nonhuman being;
stones are divided into two parts, some which are stones and others which are
nonstones; and fish, some which are fish and others which are nonfish.”

4. This determination of God leaves all contingent acts in no state of
futurition. Rather, after every volition, action, and impulse of God has been
posited, all free acts can as much occur as not occur. Consequently, no free
act is future until it actually exists. And God's decree is one by which he
establishes that all things are possible but none are future. Thus God decreed
free acts in the same manner in which he decreed Christ's deliverance from
death through more than twelve legions of angels; and that he decreed three or
four worlds and the repentance of the Tyrians if they saw signs. And
thus, when God decreed that Peter should believe, it is the same as if I said,
“God decreed that Peter should be able to believe through God's grace.” And
God's decreeing that the soldiers should not break Christ's bones is the same
as his decreeing that they should be able to break Christ's bones. He decreed
that Peter should dispute tomorrow—that is, he decreed that Peter should be
able to dispute or be able not to dispute tomorrow. But no sane person would
say that the ability to act or not to act is decreed and determined by God,
because before every decree of God Peter can, through God's grace, believe or
not believe and can dispute or not dispute. Indeed, the potency for acts or
non-acts and the potency for being or nonbeing no more fall under

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 188 | PDF p. 239]

God's free decree than God can decree that God should be infinite. Nor do
possible things fall under God's free decree, because before and apart from
God's decree all possible things are possible. Future things are decreed by
God, not possible things.

5. According to this opinion, through his decree God contributes no more to
the futurition of contingent acts than Peter's volition or Peter's walking
while the sun is shining contributes to it. God's will is no cause of the
futurition of things but leaves future contingents at τὸ posse esse (“being
able to be”) or τὸ posse non esse (“being able not to be”). Therefore, the
futurition of things is from themselves from eternity, which is absurd. For if
a thing does not exist without God's will as a cause, neither is it future
without the same will. And if things exist through God's providence, things
are future through God's providence. For there are as many future things and
as many futuritions of things as there are present things. More absurd than
absurdity itself is that the determinate number of futuritions does not depend
upon God's will, because it is impossible that there should be any present
created thing which was not future from eternity.

6. If God did not determine future contingents from eternity and did not will
them from eternity, neither does his will determine them in time. Therefore,
God begins both to will and determine them in time, and he wills in time
something which he did not will from eternity. This is so because he does not
absolutely will or determine them in time. And if the free creature

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 189 | PDF p. 240]

did not determine itself, God would neither will nor determine that act.
Therefore, there is in God a present volition which would not have been future
unless we had willed.

7. But Scripture and arguments from Scripture refute Arminius here. Isaiah
14:27: God immutably decreed the devastation of the Chaldeans through the
Medes, and the deliverance of his people through Cyrus, the executor of his
counsel, Isaiah 46:10–11. He decreed the division of Christ's garments, the
piercing of his hands and feet, and the crucifixion, Matthew 27:35; John 19:24;
Psalm 22:18; John 19:18; and the nonbreaking of his legs, John 19:33–36. He
decreed the calling of the nations, free both on the part of the preachers and
on the part of the called who respond to God calling, Isaiah 11:10; 49:1–2,
21–22; 54:1–3; 60:4–5; 66:12; Acts 2:8–11; 12:24; 13:46–48; Romans 9:24–26.

8. For he predetermined the appointed times and the boundaries of human
habitation, Acts 17:26.

9. For he decreed—2 Samuel 17:14, in Hebrew, “he commanded”—to frustrate the
counsel of Ahithophel.

10. For from eternity he decreed to give the land of Canaan to his people, who
nevertheless fought most freely, made their journey to possess it, and freely
used stratagems and military equipment. Psalm 78:69: “He built his sanctuary
like the heights, as in the land which he founded”—established—“forever.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 190 | PDF p. 241]

11. For God works all things according to the counsel of his will, Ephesians
1:11. Therefore, from eternity he decreed all those things, whether contingent
or necessary, because God does nothing in time which he did not decree from
eternity to do.

12. Lots, and the things which are most fortuitous, depend upon God's
providence as their first and certain cause. And if someone accidentally kills
a traveler with an iron blade fallen from its handle, Jehovah says that he
killed him, Deuteronomy 19:5; Exodus 21:13; Proverbs 16:33. Therefore, the most
contingent and fortuitous things are decreed by God from eternity.

13. For it is foreign to God's wisdom that there should be contingents which
are not predefined by his wise will.

14. For it is contrary to God's justice that there should be free acts which
are penal to his servants and to the impious and which are not absolutely
willed by God's will. Such are the acts of despoiling Job; defiling David's
bed on account of adultery and murder; killing impious Ahab in battle; and
killing Sennacherib, the blasphemous king of Assyria, in his idol-temple by his
own sons, Isaiah 37:37–38.

15. For we pray in vain and trust in God in vain to avert evils arising from
contingent causes unless they occur by God's absolute will.

16. For in such calamities we fear God in vain and most humbly subject
ourselves to his will in vain; likewise, we receive with a grateful heart in
vain

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 191 | PDF p. 242]

good things arising from contingent causes unless they occur by God's absolute
will.

It is asked whether God decreed all future contingents by a conditional decree
and whether this suffices to preserve God's providence, as the Arminians teach.

We maintain that their conditional decree is blasphemous and unworthy of God.
To understand it better, according to their opinion the conditional decree is
that by which God decreed contingents to be future on the hypothesis that he
foreknows—whether by middle knowledge or by some other unimaginable
mode—that by nature they exist before he decreed them to be future. It is as
though God said, “I decree that Peter should believe after I have foreknown
that he will believe, or that he will determine his own will to believe”—which
comes to the same thing—“but I decree nothing concerning the free acts of
creatures until I have foreknown that they will be future, or that a contingent
cause will produce those acts, before the determination of my will. That is, I
decree that contingent effects will be future if they will be future; and if
they will not be future, I decree that they will not be future.” But this
decree is blasphemous.

1. For in this way all future contingents are prior, with respect to their
futurition, to every decree of God. Thus the effects of second causes are prior
to the first cause and his will.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 192 | PDF p. 243]

2. It conflicts with God's freedom that free acts should be future before
God's free decree.

3. It conflicts with God's wisdom that effects should be future before an act
of the wise will of God.

4. It is contrary to justice that Absalom's incest should possess futurition
before the act of God's will which punishes David's adultery by that act, and
that Job's despoiling should precede the will of God who chastises Job and
tests his faith and patience.

5. It entangles God in fate and compels his omnipotence, which operates freely
externally, to contribute concurrence to acts possessing futurition before
every decree of his own, lest there should be an effect without any
influence of the first cause.

6. There is no place for holy fear, submissive patience, due gratitude, hope
and confidence, humility, prayers, ardor in praying, holy care, and solicitude
in using the means, either when human beings are oppressed by calamities which
come from free and contingent causes, or when they obtain good things from
contingent causes—namely, from kings, great men, friends, enemies, the industry
of farmers, and the care and labor of artisans. For they have no reason to
look to God's will in all these things, which possess futurition before every
act of the divine will.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 193 | PDF p. 244]

7. For God would do nothing from his sheer grace in free acts if all these
acts possess futurition before an act of his gracious will.

8. For all contingent effects occur by Fortune before, and therefore apart
from, an act of the divine will. And God's providence—and therefore God—in
procuring or not procuring free acts and contingent effects will be nothing
but a non-agent, a bare title, and a name without a reality.

9. For it conflicts with the dominion of providence over all beings that all
contingents should be future before an act of God's will.

LUKE 21:18

But a hair of your head shall not perish.

It is asked whether Fortune reigns according to the Arminians. They deny it;
we prove that, according to them, Fortune rules in everything.

1. For contingent things occur outside the intention of God who knows and
orders them by his providence. Therefore, they occur fortuitously with respect
to God. The consequence belongs to Aristotle, whom the Coimbra College follows
in book 2 of Aristotle's *Physics*, chapter 7, article 1; Francisco Murcia de
la Llana, *Physics* 2.6, question 1, section 10; and Julius Sirenius, *On
Providence*, book 3, who likewise says that sins occur fortuitously and
rashly.

2. For the will to permit sin is subsequent to the inclination and direction
of the created will. Therefore, everyone who wills to sin is permitted by God
to

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 194 | PDF p. 245]

do so. Therefore, all evil free acts do not depend absolutely upon God and
occur outside God's intention. Therefore, they occur by Fortune.

3. For God established nothing more from eternity than Fortune did concerning
the determination of contingent events toward this alternative rather than
that one, since God established absolutely nothing concerning them but leaves
them all at being able to be or being able not to be.

4. God, as the primary, infallibilizing, and determining cause, contributes no
more than Fortune toward persecutions rather than nonpersecutions; toward our
enduring rather than not enduring calamities and miseries which we suffer from
contingent causes; toward obtaining or not obtaining a good thing from
contingent second causes; toward prosperity or adversity; toward riches or
poverty; toward a good reputation or infamy; toward honor or ignominy; or
toward life or death which befall us from contingent causes. Therefore,
Fortune reigns.

5. For Proverbs 16:33 says, “Lots are cast into the lap, and every judgment of
it is from the Lord.” But that statement is false. For the judgment of the lot
and its determination that it should be white rather than black comes either
from the hand grasping the lot, from the lots themselves, from God, or from
Fortune. It does not come from the hand, because bare contact is blind in
itself and is not directed by any

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 195 | PDF p. 246]

light or impulse to grasp this lot rather than that one. It does not come from
the lots themselves, because the whole question concerns the lots, which in
themselves are determinable and indifferent. Nor does the judgment come from
God, because the adversaries deny that God determines contingent second
causes, and they teach that he concurs with them by a universal, indifferent,
and determinable concurrence. From this concurrence there is no grasping of
the white rather than the black lot. Therefore, it comes from Fortune alone,
or from sheer and absolute contingency, by which contingent causes, released
from every predetermining action of God and notwithstanding every action of
God, can act or not act. This is certainly a cause superior to God, which is
not governed by God, and is itself blind Fortune. For if there is Fortune,
there is no providence; and if there is providence, there is no Fortune.

6. For the certain and determinate number of contingent and free actions does
not rest with God's providence. Therefore, it rests with blind Fortune. The
antecedent is Arminius's doctrine. They condemn in our theologians the teaching
that, with respect to God's active providence and decree, contingent and free
causes cannot produce more contingent actions than they do produce.

7. We have no reason to give God thanks for an evil avoided which would have
befallen through contingent causes. For they teach that God cannot determine
a contingent cause to act if it is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 196 | PDF p. 247]

indifferent to not acting, and that God cannot determine it not to act if it is
of itself indifferent to acting. Therefore, we have reason to give thanks to
Fortune.

ROMANS 11:23

—God is able to graft them in again.

It is asked whether God retains the dominion of his providence over free acts
according to the Jesuits and Arminians. They affirm that he does; we deny it.

1. For given middle knowledge, all contingent acts possess futurition prior
to God's free decree. But God cannot possess dominion to procure their
existence rather than prevent it when they are future before every will of
God.

2. Whoever possesses true dominion over a free act, so that it should occur
rather than not occur, can effect that, when everything required for acting
has been posited, the free act should occur rather than not occur. But
according to the adversaries God cannot do this, for in this way he would
destroy freedom, as they teach.

3. The jurists rightly define dominion as each person's own faculty of using a
thing for every use permitted by law. But according to the definition of
freedom which they posit, God cannot use Peter's will for willing rather than
not willing.

4. God does not possess dominion over the determinate number of free acts of
angels and human beings. Therefore, neither does he possess it over the acts,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 197 | PDF p. 248]

because how many acts the free will elicits—no more and no fewer—is determined
neither by God's decree nor by God's action but by created free choice alone,
according to the adversaries.

5. The jurisconsults rightly deny that dominion over the same thing can belong
fully to two persons, because the will of one person to use the same thing can
conflict with the will of the other to use it. But the Arminians teach that
dominion to refuse rather than to will rests with the created will, even when
everything required for acting on God's part has been posited. Therefore, it
cannot rest with God, who often wills that a human being should refuse when he
wills, and wills that he should will when he does not will, as the adversaries
teach.

6. One who cannot dispose of a thing as he wills, but only as another who
resists him wills to dispose of it, does not possess dominion to dispose of
that thing. Thus an heir and a wife, as the jurisconsults say, do not possess
perfect dominion because they cannot dispose of money without the consent of a
guardian and husband who perhaps resist them. Therefore, God does not possess
dominion to dispose of the acts of the will unless the created will, which
often resists God, consents.

It is asked whether, according to Scripture, God retains dominion over free
acts, and indeed such dominion that he can effect that a free act should occur
rather than not occur. The Remonstrants and Jesuits attribute such dominion to
free will but do not attribute it to God. We, however, ascribe such dominion
fully to God.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 198 | PDF p. 249]

1. For Ephesians 3:20 says, “God is able with superabundance to do all
things”—and therefore to give faith, charity, repentance, and perseverance,
and the acts of those virtues which we daily seek—“above the things which we
ask or think, according to that power which works in us.” Romans 11:23: “God
is able to graft them in again.” Therefore, he can procure the free conversion
of the Jews. 2 Corinthians 9:8: “But God is able to effect that every grace
abounds in you.” Romans 16:25: “Now to him who is able to confirm you according
to my gospel,” and so forth, “be glory.” Romans 14:4: “For God is able to
establish him.” Jude 24: “Now God is able to preserve you immune from falling.”
1 John 4:4: “You are from God, little children, and have overcome them, because
he who is in you is more powerful than he who is in the world.” 1 Peter 1:5:
“You who are guarded by the protection of God's power.” John 10:29: “My Father,
who gave them”—the sheep—“to me is greater than all, and no one shall snatch
them from my hand.” Matthew 19:26: “With human beings this is impossible, but
with God all things are possible.” Romans 15:13: “That you may abound in hope
through the power of the Holy Spirit.” Nehemiah 1:10: “This is the people whom
thou hast redeemed by thy great power and thy strong hand.” But this power
exercised itself through free acts in Pharaoh, who freely let the people go;
through Moses and Aaron, who freely preached and performed miracles; and
through the people, who freely went out.

2. For God can convert and save more people, and can leave unconverted and damn
more people,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 199 | PDF p. 250]

than he converts or saves, or leaves unconverted or damns, if it had so seemed
good to him. Acts 26:29; Romans 11:23.

3. For God possesses a greater and more absolute dominion over created wills
than the created will possesses over itself.

4. For God possesses the faculty of using every created thing for every lawful
use—for dominion is so defined. Therefore, God can use the created will and
free acts so that they should occur or not occur, as he knows will conduce to
his glory.

5. For the determinate number of the volitions of human beings and angels,
with respect to number and kind, and consequently also of the elect and the
reprobate, would not rest with God if God's providence did not possess dominion
over as many volitions of human beings and angels as it seemed good to God
should be future or exist.

6. For the created will would be the first ruler, not God, and thus would be
the first agent.

7. Prayer and entreaties for the will's unalterable subjection to filial
obedience could have no foundation unless God, by his invincible dominion,
could procure those acts rather than non-acts.

8. Faith, repentance, and gratitude to God for the free good or evil acts of
human beings and angels, both good and evil, would utterly collapse unless they
rested upon God's dominion, by which he can procure

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 200 | PDF p. 251 | stop before next question]

a free act rather than a non-act.

9. The praise and glory of conversion rather than nonconversion would not be
owed to God but to the created will if the created will, not God, possessed
dominion over acts of conversion rather than nonconversion.
