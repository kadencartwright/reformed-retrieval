SECTION SIX.

JUNIUS. But if the statement is made about happiness (as you think), that assertion is not
universally true either. For the denial of happiness on account of sin was indeed considered as the
punishment of sin; but the denial of happiness on account of a voluntary order, or of mere will, is
not punishment. God denied supernatural happiness to Adam in his integrity until he had completed
the appointed order of the way. This was not a punishment to Adam, nor is it a punishment to a
private person that he is not Caesar. Therefore the denial of happiness is not punishment in
itself, but only accidentally, insofar as it follows from the creature’s sin (as they say) finally.

The same axe also cuts down what you say: that sin in actuality necessarily precedes that denial of
happiness. For this is indeed true of the denial of final happiness, as they call it; but we are
speaking of the denial of the principle from which happiness arises, that is, of grace and of
gratuitous adoption in Christ Jesus. Therefore, even if we grant you that sin in actuality precedes
this denial, it would nevertheless also have to be added that a particular abandonment by God in
the beginning and progress of sin precedes sin; and that the foundation of this particular
abandonment is non-election or preterition and reprobation, which we acknowledge to be not the
cause of sin but its antecedent. Nor is what you say universally true: that the foreseeing of future
sin precedes the determination of that act. For the foreseeing of future sin is both antecedent and
consequent to that divine denial, because that divine act (as they call it), the negative act,
precedes sin in its commission, but follows when final happiness is to be barred on account of
human sins, as was shown before. And these solutions can very well be adapted to what follows.

ARMINIUS. But I was speaking of the denial of happiness. You say that, considered universally, it
is not punishment, because the denial which occurs on account of God’s voluntary order is not
punishment. Yet I should like to be shown that there is some denial of supernatural happiness on
account of God’s voluntary order apart from the consideration of sin. You prove it: “God denied
supernatural happiness to Adam until he had completed the appointed order of the way. And this was
not a punishment to Adam.” I reply that “denial of supernatural happiness” is spoken equivocally.
One denial is of the final grace of happiness, another is temporal. The one is peremptory, the other
is under a condition. The denial of which we are speaking is final and peremptory. For the decree
of predestination and preterition is peremptory, and whatever is prepared or denied to anyone by
that decree he will finally enjoy or lack. But you speak about a temporal denial until the person
completes the order of the way according to the norm of divine justice, and about a denial under
the condition that he does not live according to God’s appointment. That denial belongs to just
divine providence, as distinguished from predestination and preterition. Indeed, your denial can be
so called only catachrestically. For how will someone be said to deny happiness to a person when he
has promised it to him under a condition? You grant, however, that sin precedes the denial of final
happiness. I add the minor premise: But preterition or non-election is the denial of final
happiness. Therefore sin precedes preterition. You say it must be added that a particular
abandonment by God in the beginning and progress of sin precedes sin, and that the foundation of
this abandonment is non-election, preterition, and reprobation. I grant that sin is preceded by the
abandonment of God which leaves a human being in the hand of his own counsel. But this abandonment
is not particular with respect to the beginning, for by it God abandoned Adam and in him all human
beings. Therefore preterition cannot be its foundation. For all were abandoned at the beginning of
sin. With respect to the progress of sin, however, it can be called particular, for God rescues
some from it and leaves others in sin. Its foundation can be called non-election or preterition, by
which some are left in the progress of sin while others are delivered from sin through God’s
gratuitous election, which is directly opposed to that preterition. Hence it follows that it is not
even rightly said that preterition or non-election is antecedent to sin, since it is antecedent only
to the progress of sin already committed—indeed, it is its cause through the denial of grace which
prevents the progress of sin. But it is universally true that the foreseeing of sin precedes the
determination of the negative act by which God does not will supernatural happiness for someone.
For the act of preterition does not precede sin in its commission, as has now often been shown. The
sin which is common to all human beings does not arise from a negative act which distinguishes
among human beings, but from a common negative act. Preterition is a negative act which is not
common, but distinguishes among human beings. Therefore preterition is not an act antecedent to
sin. And thus my proofs against your response have been strengthened; consequently they can also
serve to decide the remaining questions.

RESPONSE. “I should like to be shown that there is some denial of supernatural happiness on account
of God’s voluntary order apart from the consideration of sin.” Although thus far I have thought it
should be granted that the denial of supernatural happiness has the character of punishment—because
from this there follows only that the denial of supernatural happiness presupposes sin, and by no
means that the decree to deny it does—nevertheless, after I fixed the eyes of my consideration more
closely upon this point, occasioned, of course, by the present section, Junius’s reflections in it,
and the force (*energeia*) of Junius’s argument, which Arminius concealed and passed over in silence
but by no means refuted, I seem to myself to see a manifest demonstration of this truth which
Arminius denies and whose denial he vehemently presses as exceedingly serviceable to his arguments.
And, as if no reason for it could possibly be offered, he professes that he wishes to be taught how
such a conclusion can be reached, although meanwhile he has passed dry-shod over Junius’s argument,
which is very illuminating on this point. First, however, I shall explain Junius’s earlier argument,
which is not so illuminating. The argument is this: “God denied supernatural happiness to Adam
until he had completed the appointed order of the way.” Who doubts that God could have conferred
supernatural happiness on Adam before he had completed the appointed order of the way? What,
therefore, God did not give before that time he did not give by the mere good pleasure of his will,
and not under the character of punishment. That argument, I say, is not so illuminating. Yet let us
see how absurdly Arminius refutes it, albeit with sufficient labor. “I reply,” he says, “that ‘denial
of supernatural happiness’ is spoken equivocally.” On the contrary, I say, no equivocation occurs
here except one contrived at Arminius’s pleasure. But let us await the explanation of the
equivocation. “One denial,” he says, “is of the final grace of happiness, another is temporal.” On
the contrary, I say, in each case the same grace of happiness is understood—not temporal, but one
that will last forever. For God could, by his mere good pleasure, either have granted sooner or have
granted without a condition the same happiness that would last forever, which he was not going to
grant unless Adam had completed the appointed order of the way. At this point, then, Arminius will
locate the equivocation: “The one,” he says, “is peremptory, the other is under a condition.” Yes,
he does locate it in this way,

quite absurdly—as though the nature of happiness were diversified in this manner. It rests with God
in what order he wills to confer that happiness; hence it is absurd to invent an equivocation of
happiness. Junius had already said that this denial does not have the character of punishment
whenever it occurs on account of a voluntary order. But, setting that distinction aside, God could
have conferred this happiness after obedience had first been rendered for one day, two days, or
some years, or only after the completion of eight hundred years of life; and a denial of happiness
lasting seven hundred years and more would by no means assume the character of punishment. Will
Arminius invent an equivocation here too—that is, between happiness conferred earlier and happiness
conferred later according to God’s appointment? But if an equivocation is granted, let him show how
Junius’s proposition is true in one sense and false in another, as normally happens whenever a
proposition suffers from equivocation. Arminius did not even attempt to show this. What he adds is
false: that Junius is speaking of a temporal denial. The same happiness, which will last forever
even if it is conferred, could nevertheless have been conferred earlier; yet its delay, and
consequently its denial for a time, can be ascribed to nothing—not to anything—except God’s mere
good pleasure [the Latin prints *non* twice]. For although God will not deny him happiness forever,
he nevertheless denies it for a time, and does so by his mere good pleasure.

But there remains a far more illuminating argument, according to which it becomes manifest that the
denial of supernatural happiness does not have the character of punishment under any ordering. It
is the following argument proposed by Junius: “It is not a punishment to a private person that he
is not Caesar.” Arminius concealed this argument; he judged it wiser to pass it by without a
greeting than to make an unsuccessful attempt at solving it. Now see how far I shall proceed: if it
is not a punishment to a private person that he is not greater than Caesar, it certainly will not
be a punishment that someone is not incomparably greater than Caesar. But to attain supernatural
happiness is to be incomparably greater than Caesar. To this I add a second argument: according to
Arminius, every punishment is involuntary. But to lack supernatural happiness is not involuntary.
For how few natural human beings there are who would not rather live forever upon earth, content
even with a moderate lot, than enjoy heaven, by desire for which they are by no means captivated.
As for Junius’s statement that punishment is such only accidentally insofar as it follows finally
from the creature’s sin (as they say), this does not seem to me to be true in every respect, for the
lack of supernatural happiness did not follow sin but rather preceded it.

You will say: Damnation is punishment; but damnation includes the denial of supernatural happiness;
therefore that denial too is punishment. I deny the syllogism. For this would be the legitimately
drawn conclusion: Therefore what includes the denial of supernatural happiness is punishment. But
it does not follow from this that whatever is included in damnation is also punishment. The
infliction of death, for example, is sometimes punishment; but it does not follow that whatever is
included in death is likewise punishment. For death includes the lack of all earthly things, yet
every lack of this kind is not immediately punishment. Indeed, for any human being to lack the
riches of Croesus, the beauty of Helen, the strength of Samson, the wisdom of Solomon, or imperial
power must not be regarded as punishment. As for the final point, although the denial of
supernatural happiness by way of privation may have sin antecedent to it, it is nevertheless
falsely inferred from this: Therefore preterition or non-election—that is, the decree to deny
supernatural happiness—presupposes sin. Good works also precede the conferral of eternal salvation,
but they do not precede the decree to confer it. Yet conclusions of this kind which do not follow,
set down so often by Arminius as though they were supremely legitimate consequences and clear by
their own light, are like old grandmothers to him, whom one could not easily tear away from his
lungs.

2. Arminius grants that sin is preceded by the abandonment of God which leaves a human being in the
hand of his own counsel. “But this abandonment,” he says, “is not particular with respect to the
beginning.” And why so, pray? Because by it, of course, God abandoned not only Adam but in him all
human beings. By the same reasoning, Abraham’s payment of tithes to Melchizedek was not particular,
because not only Abraham but also Levi in his loins was paying tithes. One and the same fire might
burn various pieces of wood—various, I say, in a far different manner from that in which different
human beings are said to have been in Adam. Therefore, if we believe Arminius, this operation of
fire is not particular. Hence he concludes: “Therefore preterition cannot be the foundation of that
abandonment, for all,” he says, “were abandoned at the beginning of sin.” Certainly all were
abandoned by this abandonment; nor were they abandoned by any other abandonment than one of this
kind which was decreed by God. This decree we call intentional preterition, namely the decree to
deny the efficacious grace by which Adam and all in Adam would be kept from sin. But we deny that a
decree to pass over of this kind is the decree of reprobation, nor do we say that the aforesaid
abandonment has its foundation in reprobation any more than in predestination. Junius, however,
places the foundation of this abandonment in reprobation—indeed, not merely of this abandonment at
the beginning, but he places in reprobation the foundation of abandonment both in the beginning
and in the progress of sin. Yet the abandonment at the beginning of sin, namely Adam’s abandonment,
is equally realized in executing the counsel of predestination as in executing that of reprobation,
insofar as predestination signifies the purpose of manifesting God’s glory by way of sparing mercy.
It follows that preterition is antecedent to sin not only insofar as it signifies the decree to
abandon Adam, but also insofar as it signifies reprobation.

That the foreseeing of sin precedes the negative act by which God does not will supernatural
happiness for someone is universally false. Nor has the contrary ever been shown by Arminius with
any argument other than that sin precedes the denial of supernatural happiness. From this he infers
that sin also precedes the decree to deny supernatural happiness. But by the same reasoning,
because the conferral of salvation presupposes good works, the decree to confer salvation would
also presuppose good works, and thus election would have been made not only on the basis of faith
but also of works. Preterition, insofar as it is the same as reprobation, is a discriminating act.
Yet there is also a preterition common to all, preparing the way both for the execution of the
decree of predestination and for that of reprobation. It is the denial of the efficacious grace by
which Adam would be kept from sin. Meanwhile, not even the preterition which belongs to reprobation
follows Adam’s sin or the foreseeing of it. For then the decree to permit sin would be prior to
reprobation; consequently, the decree to permit sin would concern the end, while reprobation would
concern the means to the end, since there is no other basis on which the order of prior and
posterior is established in intention. But it is impossible that either damnation or the denial of
supernatural happiness should occur for the purpose that Adam’s sin might be permitted. Again, on
this account human beings would, in the order of execution, fall from supernatural happiness
before Adam was permitted to sin. For what is first in intention ought to be last in execution.
Thus Arminius’s proofs against Junius’s response have been weakened and consequently are of no
value for deciding the remaining questions!
