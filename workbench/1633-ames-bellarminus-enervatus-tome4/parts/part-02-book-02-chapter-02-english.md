## Chapter II. On the Cause of Sin

### The state of the question

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *If the controversy is posed in these words: Whether God is the
author of evils, and so forth, there will be no question. For the adversaries
deny this together with the Catholics. We would therefore labor in vain unless
we first showed that in reality they hold what they do not dare profess in
words.*

Protestant reply. I. Here Bellarmine professes the art of slandering rather
than disputing, since he endeavors to locate the controversy in a proposition
which he admits is denied by both sides. There is no controversy without a
contradiction in which the same thing is affirmed and denied. II. It also
arises from an unrestrained zeal for slandering that he does not state the
controversy in the things taught by our theologians, but in their
consequences, whether fictitious or true. It is as if we were to frame the
question, *Whether idolatry is lawful and praiseworthy,* and attribute the
affirmative answer to the Roman Catholics because it necessarily follows from
what they teach about religious worship being lawfully and laudably offered
to images and other creatures. III. If Bellarmine's mind was borne with such
vehemence against those who make God the author of sin, he had no need with
great labor and a circuit of consequences to trans-*

[Printed p. 239 | Google Books PA239 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*form our theologians into such men. He had the Dominicans, whom the Jesuits
accuse of this crime. He had certain Scholastics who are said to have professed
this in explicit words. Thus Medina, on 1.2, question 79, article 2: “Ockham and
Gabriel affirm that God, in strict and proper speech, is the cause of sin.” IV.
We deny that this follows from the things taught by our theologians.

2. Bellarmine. *The opinion of Zwingli, Calvin, and Beza concerning the cause
of sin on God's part can be comprised in five propositions.*

Protestant reply. I. The adversary ought therefore to have disputed about
these propositions, not about a consequence which none of them acknowledges.
II. He ought also to have explained the Roman Catholic position by opposing
propositions, so that the two positions might be compared and certain truth
thereby illustrated. But here he was at a loss. “Concerning the question
whether every action, insofar as it is an action, is from God,” Bonaventure
observes on book 2, distinction 37, “the positions of very great men have been
so diverse, and so reasonable, that not even the Master dared define which of
them contains more truth.” After those times, however, especially from the
rise of the Jesuits, such was the power of the factions holding contrary
opinions on questions of this kind that not even the Master of the faith
himself, the Roman Pontiff, could be induced by any appeals or supplications to
define anything certain about them. Bellarmine therefore, as befitted a
cunning sophist, says nothing about the common Roman Catholic position, but
incidentally intimates his own, plainly different from the more received one,
as will appear below. III. These very propositions, in the sense in which our
theologians assert them, are defended by many Roman Catholics against the
Jesuits, as will be made manifest in the individual cases.

3. Bellarmine. 1. *They teach that God not only permits but also wills that all
the sins committed by men should occur.*

Protestant reply. That is, God is occupied with the existence of sin not by an
idle or merely negative permission, but by a permission of the will which
predefines the event itself. This is what Bradwardine, the Profound Doctor,
wrote several centuries ago on behalf of the cause of God against the
Pelagians, book 1, chapter 33: “With respect to whatever there is God's
permission, there is also his actual willing of it.”

4. Bellarmine. 2. *They teach that God not only wills sins to occur, but also
decreed from eternity that all the sins committed by men should occur; and
this without foreseeing the determination of the human will. For they say that
God does not discern anything because he foreknows that it will occur, but
foreknows that it will occur because he himself decreed that it should occur.*

Protestant reply. That is, whatever God wills in time, insofar as he wills it,
he willed and decreed from eternity; and the firmness of this decree does not
properly depend upon man's contingent and mutable will. Did Bellarmine never
read this any-*

[Printed p. 240 | Google Books PA240 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*where except in Zwingli and Calvin? Alvarez, among others, testifies as
follows about this position in disputation 22, section 7: “By his eternal
decree and absolute and efficacious will, God predetermined all our particular
acts before foreseeing them and independently of all middle knowledge of
future free cooperation on a hypothesis.” “This is the position of Thomas, all
the Thomists, Scotus, Vega, and the holy Fathers.”

5. Bellarmine. 3. *They also teach that God commands Satan and wicked men to
conceive evil deeds, and that he inclines, impels, and even compels them to do
this.*

Protestant reply. These phrases of commanding, impelling, and compelling to
evil deeds are not commonly used by our theologians, nor are they approved as
proper expressions. Yet Bellarmine was not unaware that phrases of this kind
can be appropriately explained. Thus he himself says in chapter 13: “By a
certain trope God is said to command and to stir up to evil. He presides over
the evil wills themselves and rules, governs, turns, and bends them,” and so
forth. As far as the matter itself is concerned, our theologians intend
nothing other than what the Thomist Scholastics teach: “By a previous motion,
God efficaciously applies the created will so that it acts freely and
infallibly, just as he also applies the other secondary causes so that they act
naturally.” Alvarez, disputation 23, section 9.

6. Bellarmine. 4. *They also teach that God works in the minds of wicked men
and, through them as through his instruments, does all the things which with
respect to men are said to be, and truly are, sins.*

Protestant reply. As far as these phrases are concerned, they are drawn from
Scripture and approved by Bellarmine in chapter 13: “God is said to use the
evil wills of the wicked as instruments; he works invisibly in them.” The
meaning of our theologians is no different from what the Thomists approve:
“The human will is in a certain manner God's instrument—not a pure and mere
instrument, but a free one.” Alvarez, disputation 68, section 5.

7. Bellarmine. 5. *They teach that the things which with respect to men are
truly sins, although they are done with God willing, determining, commanding,
and working, nevertheless with respect to God are not sins but good and just
works, because men do all those things with an evil intention, but God with a
good purpose.*

Protestant reply. I. The antecedent part of this discrete proposition has
already been sufficiently explained, and the consequent is clear from it. Yet
we do not admit those two assertions: namely, that God commits sin as such,
and that God's and man's concurrence concerning the act of sin differs only
in intention and purpose. II. Three hundred years ago Bradwardine taught as
follows in book 1, chapter 34: “Every act is just with respect to God its
author; therefore so is every necessary consequence of any act. For if the
antecedent is just, every one of its consequences will be just, just as it will
also be true. Some such act, however, and something necessarily consequent
upon it, is sin. It therefore appears that there is no disorder, deformity, or
sin absolutely in the whole universe, but only relatively—namely, with respect
to inferior causes which will, though they are unable, to disturb the ordering
of the superior cause.” Attention must now be paid to the con-*

[Printed p. 241 | Google Books PA241 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*clusions which Bellarmine draws from those propositions.

8. Bellarmine. *Whoever impels and compels another to perform any work is the
primary author of that work. But according to the adversaries' position, God
does this in sins. Therefore, according to the adversaries' position, God is
the primary author of those sins which are perpetrated by men.*

Protestant reply. I. God does not compel the will, which, if it were compelled,
would cease to be a will. II. One who impels or moves another—not by morally
persuading him, but by inwardly directing him—freely to do that in which he
sins, and who does this by right of supreme dominion toward a good end which
he will certainly attain from it, is not the author but the ordainer of sin.
This, however, is the whole of what God does. III. Either Bellarmine confuses a
work, or an act as such, with sin, or he propounds a sophism of four terms. But
the common position of the Thomists and even of the rest of the Scholastics is
that “the act of sin, insofar as it is an act, is from God as from a cause, but
the deficiency and disorder of the act is from a deficient cause, not from
God.” Alvarez, disputation 24.

9. Bellarmine. *Any work belongs more to the one commanding it than to the one
executing it. But according to the adversaries' position, God orders Satan and
the wicked to conceive evil deeds,* and so forth. Therefore.

Protestant reply. I. If God were to command someone to perform an act which
otherwise, under the common law, is a sin, it would not be a sin for the person
to whom it was extraordinarily commanded, much less for God. For it is a
contradiction for the same act in the same respect to be both according to and
against God's revealed will. II. An answer was given above from Bellarmine
himself in chapter 13: in the Scriptures, and so by our theologians, God is
said by a trope to command sins, in view of his efficacious providence occupied
with sin after the manner of a command.

10. Bellarmine. *One who uses an instrument is the primary author of the work
which he does through instruments. But according to the adversaries' position,
the wicked are God's instruments when they sin. Therefore, God is the author
of sins.*

Protestant reply. I. There are four terms, for the work which God does through
the wicked, as such, is not sin but a just judgment. II. One who uses as a kind
of instrument a man who freely falls short, in order to effect what is good,
is not the author of the failure. III. When the wicked sin, they are not
properly God's instruments—that is, moved without an internal principle of
motion—but metaphorically: they are so moved that they freely move themselves.

11. Bellarmine. *Whoever efficaciously brings it about that someone should
sin must most truly be called the author of his sin. But according to the
adversaries' position, God efficaciously brought about the first man's sin.
Therefore.*

[Printed p. 242 | Google Books PA242 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. An efficacious procuring is either only an ordering and
governing which brings about the existence of the thing, or it is also an
effecting which produces the thing by its own power. In the former sense the
proposition is false, and in the latter the assumption is false.

Leaving aside the remaining blasphemies which in Bellarmine rest upon this
already refuted slander, we shall briefly examine Bellarmine's replies by which
he thinks that our theologians' arguments for God's efficacious providence in
the ordering of sin can be answered.

12. Protestant. There are various testimonies of Scripture which signify that
God willed and decreed the things which men do with sin: Genesis 45:1; Acts
2:23 and 4:28.

Bellarmine. 1. *In evil works it often happens that the action is evil and the
suffering good. God therefore willed and predefined these works of virtue,
namely, the supreme patience which consists in suffering, when he willed and
predefined that Joseph should be sold and Christ killed. But he neither willed
nor predefined the evil works of those selling and killing them.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine most sophistically and absurdly locates works
of virtue or patience in mere suffering. A work or action of virtue is not
properly a suffering, but an action. II. If, however, God predefined works of
virtue from eternity, it follows from this that God's antecedent decree does
not remove liberty and praise, and consequently neither does it remove the
viciousness of any action. III. Action and suffering are one and the same
motion. Suffering therefore cannot be predefined without action. IV. Suffering
without action cannot even be considered except generally, confusedly, and
indeterminately. Predefinitions of this kind are altogether foreign to God's
perfect providence. “Far be it,” says Bradwardine in book 1, chapter 34, “that
God should will anything universally and confusedly and not will it
particularly and distinctly.” “Christ's suffering,” Alvarez adds in
disputation 22, section 19, “was individually predefined by God, with a perfect
definition. Therefore, all the circumstances which concur in its
individuation were predetermined by God's eternal decree. But the intensity of
the act and its extension to those particular persons also concur in such
individuation.”

Bellarmine. 2. *For that which God had willed and predefined—namely, that
Joseph should be sold and Christ killed—to occur infallibly, it was enough for
God to have foreseen the evil wills of Joseph's brothers, the Jews, and Pilate,
and at the same time to have willed to permit those evil wills to perpetrate in
their own time what they had wickedly conceived.*

Protestant reply. By this reasoning, I. The infallibility of the divine decree
would depend upon the fallible inclination of the human will. II. The cer-*

[Printed p. 243 | Google Books PA243 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tainty of God's foreknowledge would have its first cause and rationale
outside God. III. Christ's death would not have been distinctly predefined
except on the occasion of the malice of those by whom he was crucified. Let
Bellarmine persuade his Dominicans of these things. IV. At the end of book 1,
question 1, conclusion 1, Matteo Rispoli argues from this reply as follows:
“God sees that Peter will sin infallibly if he is placed in such occasions;
and God places him in such occasions. Therefore, God is the cause of Peter's
sin.” Nor will this argument easily be answered if Bellarmine's principle is
conceded: “Whoever in any way brings sin about is the author of sin.” For when
God places a man in those occasions from which he knows that the man will sin,
and not in those from which he sees that the man would have willed to resist
sin, he does all that, according to Bellarmine's position, he can do to bring
about that man's sin. V. If from eternity God distinctly and certainly saw the
evil wills of men, then he saw them insofar as they had some entity from
eternity, and consequently as they depended in some manner upon God's own
will.

13. Protestant. In the Scriptures God is said to have created the wicked for
the evil day, Proverbs 16; to have made vessels for dishonor, Romans 9; and to
have raised up Pharaoh so that he might show his power in him, in the same
place.

Bellarmine. *God creates the wicked materially; that is, he creates those whom
he sees will be wicked, so that he may use their wickedness to manifest his
justice and power.*

Protestant reply. This is the whole of what we set out to prove in this
argument: God creates those whom he sees will become wicked by his permission,
so that he may use their wickedness for his glory. He creates them with a will
to permit, in order to attain some certain end through sins thus permitted.
The disagreement concerns only the manner of foreseeing, about which as much
has already been said as this passage permits.

14. Protestant. In the Scriptures God is said to impel men to things which
are sins for them, and to use them as instruments for works which cannot be
completed without sin: 1 Samuel 24:1; 2 Samuel 16:10; 1 Kings 11:37; 12:15, 24;
22:20; 2 Kings 10:30; Job 1:12; Isaiah 10:5, 15; 13:17; Jeremiah 51:1; Ezekiel
12:13; Psalms 44:19; 105:25; 119:36; 141:4; Matthew 6:13.

Bellarmine. 1. *God not only permits the wicked to do many evil things, nor
does he only forsake the godly so that they are compelled to suffer the things
inflicted by the wicked. He also presides over the evil wills themselves and
rules, governs, turns, and bends them, working invisibly in them, so that,
although they are evil through their own fault, they are nevertheless directed
by divine providence toward one evil rather than another—not positively, but
permissively.*

[Printed p. 244 | Google Books PA244 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. I. That final qualification, *permissively,* cannot be
referred to the whole providence of *presiding, ruling, governing, turning, and
bending* without manifest contradiction. II. Those words convey something
more than we demand or even prove. III. If God directs men to perpetrate one
evil rather than another, then he directs them to sin. For there is something
of sin in one evil—namely, in Absalom's incest and in the hatred and killing of
Christ—which is not in the other.

Bellarmine. 2. *God inclines evil wills toward one thing rather than another,
even positively, but by way of occasion and morally, by introducing some good
thought, from which the evil person takes occasion to judge that it is better
to harm this person than that one, or to serve this iniquity rather than that
one.*

Protestant reply. One who provides someone an occasion for sinning, to the end
that he should sin, also knowing that from that occasion, and not otherwise,
he will sin in that manner, is certainly rightly and truly said to bring that
sin about.

15. Protestant. God is said to blind and harden the hearts of men.

Bellarmine. 1. *God is not the cause of blinding and hardening positively, but
negatively—namely, by permitting, forsaking, and not showing mercy.*

Protestant reply. I. To say, “He is not the cause positively, but negatively,”
is nothing other than to say, “He is not the cause, but he is the cause without
the power of causing.” For every power of a cause is positive, not negative.
II. Bonaventure explains the matter much more accurately on book 1,
distinction 41: “Hardening, insofar as it is a punishment, signifies an
unfitness of the soul, and thus signifies a penal defect. Every punishment,
insofar as it is such, is just and ordered. Therefore, insofar as it is
ordered, it has an ordering cause—namely, the divine justice which
recompenses.” Thomas has similar things in 1.2, question 79, article 3.

Bellarmine. 2. *Blindness of mind, as also hardness of heart, is sin, the cause
of sin, and the punishment of sin.*

Protestant reply. If it is a punishment, then it depends upon God the judge,
not negatively, but positively willing and decreeing it from justice.

Bellarmine. 3. *That bare permission and forsaking is called blinding and
hardening on God's part, by which it comes about that, by God's just judgment
punishing past sins, man is so deprived of divine aid that it is morally
impossible for him not to fall.*

Protestant reply. Both the blinding and hardening, therefore, and the sin which
follows from them occur by God's just judgment; but a judgment is not a bare
negation.

Bellarmine. 4. *According to Scripture's manner of speaking, this forsaking is
rightly signified by words which express action.*

Protestant reply. Why, then, does he torment us with slanders when we rightly
observe this manner of Scripture?

16. Protestant. If God in no way decreed and preor-*

[Printed p. 245 | Google Books PA245 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*dained the fall, then he created man for an uncertain end. But the latter is
false; therefore, so is the former.

Bellarmine. 1. *Although God neither decreed nor willed that the first man
should sin, he nevertheless foreknew that he would sin if he were created as
he was created,* and so forth, *and from that foreknowledge he created man for
a certain end.*

Protestant reply. If the foreknowledge of a future thing is certain, it
penetrates and comprehends some certain and immutable cause by which that thing
is future. But from eternity there was no such cause besides God's will and
decree. This foreknowledge therefore presupposes God's will and decree.

Bellarmine. 2. *By the knowledge of simple intelligence, God knew that man
would fall if he were created, even before he decreed to create him.*

Protestant reply. I. According to Thomas and all the Thomists, together with
all the Scholastics, the knowledge of simple intelligence concerns the things
which can occur as such, not the things which do occur, the knowledge of which
is rightly said to be the knowledge of vision. According to them, therefore,
this is a contradiction: God knows future things by the knowledge of simple
intelligence. 2. This proposition, “If man is created, he will fall,” is
absolutely false if the connection or consequence of its parts is considered.
In that respect it therefore cannot be a suitable object of divine knowledge.
But if it is taken as a simple proposition, then it was in no respect more
knowable before God's decree than the statements contained in it: “Man will be
created” and “Man will fall.” The thing itself cries out that their truth, and
consequently the knowledge of them, is subsequent to God's decree.

Bellarmine. 3. *The things in God which are necessary are prior to the things
which are free. It is necessary, however, for God to know absolutely all things
which can be known. But it was not necessary, but free, for God to decree to
create man. Therefore, the knowledge of that conditional proposition—namely,
“If man is created, he will sin”—is prior to the future decree to create man.*

Protestant reply. This inept paralogism limps badly on all its feet. For 1.
The things which follow from God's natural and necessary perfection through
the intervention of his free will are not prior to his free will. It is
necessary for God to preserve what he wills to endure; yet the preservation is
not prior to the free will to preserve the thing which will endure. 2. The
knowledge of a future thing is rightly called free knowledge by all the
Scholastics and is opposed to natural, necessary knowledge, which concerns
possible things alone. 3. If this were necessary—“If man is created, he will
sin”—then it was not within God's power to create man in such a way that he
would not sin. 4. Nei-*

[Printed p. 246 | Google Books PA246 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ther that proposition, “If man is created, he will fall,” nor any other about
a future contingent can be known before the decree of the divine will, because
at that moment of reason it has neither truth nor any determinate entity. This
Alvarez proves soundly in disputation 13.

Bellarmine. 4. *If we were to grant that God did not foresee, before he decreed
to create man, what man would do if he were created, it still would not follow
that man was created for an uncertain end. For God created him for his own
glory. Whether, however, man would experience God's mercy or his justice would
in some manner have been uncertain, but without detriment to God's glory.*

Protestant reply. The sum of this reply is that every end of the creature
other than the ultimate end can be uncertain to God, and even that ultimate
end itself can be uncertain with respect to its distinct manner, so that in
creating, God proposed no end to himself besides his own glory considered
generally and confusedly, if this does not result in detriment to God's glory.
Any sophist can be confident about his own utterly false dogma as far as God's
glory is concerned.

17. Protestant. It arises from God's decree, not from nature, that all men
sinned in Adam.

Bellarmine. *It came about altogether naturally that when the first man fell,
the entire human race fell. For the first man vitiated nature in himself when
he lost that original righteousness which God had given him for himself and
for the whole human race.*

Protestant reply. I. Here Bellarmine plays with the ambiguous meaning of the
word *naturally.* For he himself observes in book 1, *On the Grace of the First
Man*, chapter 5, that “everything possessed from birth is sometimes called
natural; in this sense,” he adds, “we are said to be born children of wrath.”
But in this sense, what is said to occur naturally is not opposed to an order
decreed by God beyond the course of nature. II. Adam vitiated nature in
himself because by God's singular will he had been constituted the principle
and head, containing and representing the whole human race. III. The
propagation of original righteousness could not occur except by God's will;
therefore, neither could the propagation of its deprivation, original sin.
IV. It arose from God's will that such was the covenant; that, according to
the condition of the violated covenant, this punishment was inflicted; and
that each and every descendant of Adam is born mortal.

18. Protestant. Man cannot fall with God unwilling, or ignorant, or idly
looking on. Therefore, the fall occurred with God willing it.

Bellarmine. *Man fell with God permitting it.*

Protestant reply. That permission was either according to or contrary to God's
will, or with God ignorant, or idly looking upon the future fall. The argument
therefore remains unmoved.

[Printed p. 247 | Google Books PA247 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

19. Protestant. From eternity God decreed to manifest his justice and mercy.
Therefore, he willed in some manner that sin should exist, without which he
would not have attained those ends.

Bellarmine. 1. *It was not absolutely necessary that God should reveal his
mercy and justice to the world.*

Protestant reply. But it was absolutely necessary that what he had decreed
concerning the manifestation of justice and mercy should certainly be
accomplished.

Bellarmine. 2. *God could have shown his mercy and justice even if sin had
never existed.*

Protestant reply. Not such justice and mercy as the event shows to have been
proposed in God's eternal decree.

20. Protestant. God concurs in effecting that action which is sin for man.

Bellarmine. *God does not effect that action as a particular cause, but as a
universal cause, furnishing a certain power and influence indifferent and
common to that action and to its contrary, which is limited by the concurrence
of the secondary cause.*

Protestant reply. This new invention of the Jesuits is soundly refuted by the
Thomists, as may be seen in Alvarez, disputation 21, because it makes God's
providence confused, imperfect, and dependent upon the creature so that it may
be perfected by the creature. But Alvarez offers one argument which properly
pertains to this question about the cause of sin and, on that account, ought
above all others to be recalled in this place: “If God's general concurrence
is indifferent toward an act of virtue and an act of vice opposed to the
virtue, it follows that, absolutely speaking, God concurs in sin itself with
the created will as it determines itself to sin. For one who voluntarily
furnishes another with an indifferent concurrence, available for producing a
virtuous act or a sin as he pleases, truly concurs in the sin if he is
determined by the will of the other to effect that same sin,” and so forth.
