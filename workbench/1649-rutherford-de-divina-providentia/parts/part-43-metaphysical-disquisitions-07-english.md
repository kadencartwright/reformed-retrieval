### QUESTION VII

By what knowledge and will does GOD comprehend possible things?

The answer is given in the following theses.

1. By the knowledge of simple intelligence, in the mirror of omnipotence, God
knows what a creature can do in this or that order of things. But that God will
attain an end through these means rather than those, and that these causes will
produce these effects rather than those, he sees only by free knowledge, and
this in the light of his decree laid down beforehand.

[Marginal thesis: How GOD knows possible things.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question VII; printed page 560; IA PDF page 634;
BSB canvas 610]

2. By his practical knowledge God forms the ideas of possible and future
things.

3. That speculative knowledge which presupposes its object is not GOD's first
knowledge, the knowledge that forms ideas, but presupposes the ideas already
formed.

4. Nothing intelligible properly precedes GOD's intellect or offers itself to
GOD's intellect as an object prior to him in nature. First, because it is an
imperfection of a created intellect to hunt from an object outside itself for
the cause of its own understanding. Second, the infinite intellect either
makes a thing or permissively wills it to be made, whereas a finite intellect
presupposes its object as already made. There is therefore no purely
speculative knowledge in God separated from every practice. *Jerome on
Jeremiah, at chapter 26; Origen, book 7 on the Epistle to the Romans, chapter
8, verse 30; Augustine, On the Trinity, book 15, chapter 13; Anselm, On the
Harmony of Foreknowledge and Predestination, chapter 1.*

[Marginal thesis: GOD's practical and speculative knowledge, and how it differs
from our knowledge.]

Possible things appear to be of two kinds. God commands human beings to do
some things which he never decreed would be done, such as the obedience of the
reprobate. He wills these by a will of approval which is not merely practical,
because he does not effect such things, nor merely speculative, because he
employs moral means suited to procuring such obedience. The will of approval
concerning such things is therefore mixed. Other possibles are not moral
things, but things which God alone can do, such as creating more worlds, other
choirs of angels, and infinitely many species. O most fecund fruitfulness of
omnipotence! O depth without bottom or limit!

[Marginal thesis: Possible things are of two kinds.]

[Marginal thesis: GOD's approving will is twofold.]

The ideas of all possible things must be in God. Within God they provide an
infinite number of possibles coeternal with him. But who would dare affirm that
coeternal and numerically infinite objective non-repugnances exist outside
God? Concerning these non-moral possibles, God has an approving will of simple
complacency.

6. In loving his omnipotence, God necessarily loves along with it infinitely
many inward possibles. Yet I would not rashly affirm that Suárez is correct in
saying that *GOD cannot be omnipotent unless creatures are possible,* unless
the statement concerns inward possibles. Cajetan may be right in saying (and
Suárez undeservedly refutes him) that *GOD necessarily loves possible
creatures as they exist in GOD himself, not as they exist in themselves.*

[Marginal reference: Suárez, *Metaphysics*, disputation 30, section 16, number
42.]

[Marginal reference: Cajetan on Thomas, part 1, question 34, article 3.]

7. Although creatures capable of being created but never to be created are
known terminatively by God, let others consider how God could will or love so
many non-beings which will never be—as outwardly concatenated with inward
omnipotence. It appears difficult to me, unless God by a positive decree

[Marginal thesis: In what respect God necessarily loves possible things.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question VII; printed page 561; IA PDF page 635;
BSB canvas 611]

determines their non-futurition. For there is also a negative will: in rational
creatures it is the will by which they negatively do not will possible things
and many individual things unknown to them, because they exercise neither an
act of willing nor an act of not willing concerning them. But there is no such
will in God. His will is most fully actual, for providence would not be active
but idle and supine if God did not negatively will many things that will never
be, or if he did not positively will their non-futuritions just as he wills the
futuritions of other things.
