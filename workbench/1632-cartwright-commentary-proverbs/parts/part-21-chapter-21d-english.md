Verse 10. The soul of the wicked desires evil; his neighbor does not obtain favor in his eyes.

In נפש (*anima*, soul) there is a synecdoche of the part.

The purpose is, by setting forth the eager zeal of the wicked for evildoing and their mind’s aversion to doing good to others, to strike into everyone a horror of their so ruined condition.

When the Prophet says that the soul of the wicked desires evil, he says it ἐμφατικωτέρως, more emphatically,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 465 | printed columns 914-915]

than if he had said that the wicked man does this. For by this form of speech it is taught that the wicked are borne toward evil with burning zeal and the most vehement impulse, as water rushes headlong down a slope and a precipitous rock, or as an eagle swoops from a lofty place upon its prey.

Here, therefore, a distinction is established between the upright and the wicked. For they are distinguished not only in this, that the wicked exercise their wickedness as though it were an art, while they are assiduous in perpetrating it, whereas the godly, after interrupting for a time the pursuit of uprightness and justice, soon return to duty; but further in this, that even when the godly depart from duty, they approach that which is evil with a more hesitant step. For the will to do good lies near at hand for the good, but they do not find how to accomplish it; hence it comes about that they commit the evil which they do not will and which they hate, Rom. 7.18, 19. For, with nature wounded in them by depravity, they approach evil deeds like those lame in one foot, whereas the wicked man is borne toward crimes with horses and sails, as they say.

In the latter hemistich, when he says that he does not show favor to his neighbor, he teaches that the wicked man only does evil and never accomplishes any good, cares only for the things that are his own, and serves his own advantages while despising his neighbor. For their charity does not go out from the doors of their own houses, not even toward friends, since they cultivate them only for the sake of their own advantage. For if they ever confer a benefit upon another, they do it only because they think that he will be brought into their accounts, as the Pontificians who distribute alms because they persuade themselves that from them they will merit life. This, however, is not to confer a benefit freely, as the force of this word which the Prophet employs demands. Therefore, in order to vindicate ourselves from the number and register of the wicked, we must take care to be beneficent toward our neighbors and to prove by deeds that the things which are theirs are a concern to us.

Next, the fact that the eye of the wicked is not moved to show favor to his neighbor proceeds from the inhumanity and mercilessness of the soul, which he mentioned in the former hemistich, and this draws the whole body and all its members into the same fellowship of cruelty. Let us measure ourselves by the standard of this Proverb, so that, if we find in ourselves anything similar to this cruelty of the wicked, we may grieve over it and turn away from it; but if we find something dissimilar, let us give thanks to God, who has distinguished between us and them, and let us strive with prayers that he may further confirm us in the good we have undertaken.

Verse 11. By punishing the scoffer a fool becomes wise; and when a wise man is imbued with understanding, he receives knowledge.

The purpose is to teach how, from advantages or disadvantages that are our own or others’, we may advance either toward repentance or toward greater godliness. See 19.25.

Who the scoffer is, or also the fool, see above, as concerning the former hemistich of this Proverb. Only this must be added: since this passage is understood not only of civil punishments but also of divine ones, God often enters upon a different method in executing his judgments than is lawful for men. For he, passing over offenders guilty of more atrocious sins, punishes those who transgress more lightly, so that he may invite others to repentance. See Luke 13.

In the latter part, because of the double meaning of the word משכיל, a double interpretation arises. I would think that both interpretations should be embraced—especially in Proverbs, where neither the preceding nor the following material restricts the meaning of the words to one or the other—particularly since the chief excellence of a Proverb is to encompass the broadest and most capacious teachings in the fewest possible words.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 466 | printed columns 916-917]

The first sense is that a wise man is recalled to duty, or advanced toward greater godliness, by information and instruction alone, without any punishment being inflicted. By this we are taught that in corrections, especially domestic ones, great discrimination must be exercised when someone determines to correct what is depraved, so that it may be considered whether the offender’s disposition is pliable or stubborn and refractory. This also ought not to be neglected in civil corrections, insofar as it can be done through laws and the avoidance of προσωποληψίας, partiality: what can be amended and corrected by a word should not be attempted by blows. Thus we see that when Josiah was dissolved into tears at the reading and exposition of the law, God gave him through the Prophet a promise that the evil threatened in the law would not be sent while he was alive. Thus it also happened among the Ninevites, who, because they repented at Jonah’s preaching, were not brought to destruction.

The other sense is that a fool is corrected both by the punishments of the scoffer and by the prosperity and rewards of the godly and wise. For when he sees that those things which are contrary to his pursuits and manner of life are valued and honored, it comes about that, through desire to attain those good things with which he sees others adorned who follow pursuits contrary to his own, he abandons his wicked custom of living and passes over to the contrary manner. Thus at the end of Esther 8 we read that many from the nations then joined themselves to the Jews, when the Jews were honored before the King through the favor of Esther and Mordecai.

Verse 12. He imbues the righteous man with understanding concerning the house of the wicked—he who overturns the wicked because of evil.

In בית (*domum*, house) there is a synecdoche of the part for the household and those who are contained in it.

The purpose is to admonish people that, from judgments and punishments inflicted upon others, whether human or divine, they themselves should be rendered more cautious and slower to perpetrate evil deeds.

In the preceding Proverb it was shown that the just punishment of the wicked serves to restrain other wicked people from evil; but in this one it is taught that the righteous and godly also advance toward greater justice and innocence of life from the punishment of the wicked. If there were no other fruit from the punishment of the wicked than that others wandering from the way are brought back to good conduct, this alone should suffice to add spurs to those who preside over families and wield power in the Commonwealth and the Church, so that they exact the due punishments from the wicked; see Ps. 58.10, 11. But now, since not only are sinners withdrawn from evildoing, but the godly are further confirmed and even advanced in uprightness, fathers of households in their homes, Magistrates in the Commonwealth, and Ecclesiastical Ministers in the Church cannot but sin grievously when the reins are loosened to vices by impunity. For by this connivance at sins, or indulgence, what else do they do than strengthen the hands of transgressors and cause them to congeal in their own dregs; than make the hands of the godly slack for doing right and their knees tottering; finally, than cause the righteous to recoil from their uprightness? For when fear of punishment, like a bridle or halter, has once been shaken off, people of their own accord inclined toward evil deeds rush headlong into every kind of crime, as though a goad had been applied.

It has almost passed into a proverb that the righteous man is a law to himself; namely, that though no exactor and ἐργοδιώκτης, taskmaster, is present, he nevertheless applies himself to duty. But by this Proverb we are taught that the use of laws and punishments furnishes all the most righteous with great aid toward godliness. For exceedingly few are those who, while leading their life among people who have broken and shattered the barriers of godliness and uprightness and cast themselves into every license of life, preserve their integrity.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 467 | printed columns 918-919]

It is indeed true that by the singular grace of God this sometimes happens, as we read that it befell Daniel and his companions; but this is as extraordinary as preserving chastity in a brothel. Therefore, just as riders put on spurs for exceedingly spirited and very swift horses, not in order to prick a horse running of its own accord, but to urge it on by introducing the fear of being pricked, so the righteous need scourges to be placed before their eyes, so that they may be kept in duty both more easily and more surely.

Hence it is said at Deut. 17.13, 14 that every Israelite without exception, after hearing of the punishment of the stubborn man, will be struck with fear; and at chapter 19, verse 20, it is said that the rest of the people, who heard that the false witness had been severely punished, would be seized with fear. And certainly, as each person is most observant of justice and equity, so from zeal for justice no less than from consciousness of his own weakness he desires most strongly that wholesome laws and the punishments imposed when they are violated should remain in force.

Next it is further taught that we ought to advance in godliness and justice not only from our own evils but also from those of others. Hence the Jews are gravely censured at Ezek. 16 because they did not repent at the punishments inflicted upon the Israelites and Samaria, as upon an elder sister. This is especially so when those who are punished touch us by some bond. This is also confirmed from the teaching of the preceding Proverb; see Luke 13. We must advance not only from the scourges of the wicked but also from the judgments which God exercises upon their posterity or buildings.

Finally, it must be noted that the wicked are to be punished on account of their wickedness; otherwise the punishments imposed upon them are not so beneficial. For it often happens that those who preside, through error, malevolence, or envy of prosperity, establish something more severe against the wicked. Such a punishment is not so powerful and effective for restraining wickedness. For let it enter each person’s mind that he could fall in a good and honorable cause just as that wicked man fell, who was condemned unjustly.
