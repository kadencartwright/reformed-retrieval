RESPONSE.

Thomas expressly says that the future is present to God according as it actually has being in
itself. *Against the Gentiles* 1, p. 66. Again, he says that God's foreknowledge is borne upon things
insofar as they are present and already determined to one outcome, 2. 2. q. 17, art. 6, reply to the
first. Again, he illustrates the whole matter in this way:

[Marginalia: 1. q. 14. art. 13. ad 3.]

Just as one who travels along a road does not see those who come after him on it, but one who looks
down upon the whole road from some height sees all those passing along the road at once. From this he
seems to acknowledge the actual existence of all things before God, which is set before God's vision;
not indeed in time, because then all things would be eternal absolutely, and that before they had any
being at all; therefore in eternity. He explains this more fully in *On Truth*, q. 2, art. 12, in the
body: “The relation of divine cognition to any thing is like the relation of present to present. This
is to be understood in the following way. If someone saw many people passing successively along one
road, and did so over some span of time, in each part of the time he would see some of them passing
presently, so that throughout the whole time of his vision he would see all of them passing presently,
but not all presently at once, because the time of his vision is not all at once. But if his vision
could exist all at once, he would see them all presently at once, although they would not all be
passing presently at once. Hence, since the vision of divine knowledge is measured by eternity,
which is all at once and yet includes the whole of time and is absent from no part of time, it follows
that whatever is transacted in time is seen by him not as future but as present. For what is seen by
God is future to another thing which it succeeds in time; but to the divine vision itself, which is
not in time but outside time, it is not future but present.”

For our part, we would gladly receive from any theologian a sound interpretation of these passages
in Aquinas, lest men be carried off by the Doctor's authority into an exceedingly senseless position,
such as we judge the Dominicans' position to be concerning the existence of all things in eternity
according to their real being, and that from eternity. Penottus undertakes this task; but we have
partly seen how handsomely he extricates himself, and it remains for us partly to investigate it. “He
always said,” Penottus says, “that creatures are present to God from eternity because the divine gaze
is borne over all the differences of time and attains everything which occurs in time.” But here, I
say, let anyone judge between us whether this acknowledges any presence of creatures before God
other than presence according to objective being, or known being: all things are present to God
because God beholds—or knows—all things. These words savor of nothing whatever besides the objective
presence of things before God in known being. Thomas certainly has nothing about the existence of
things from eternity in eternity. He does, however, acknowledge the presence of things before God,
and that according as they actually exist in themselves, and from eternity; but this statement of
Thomas is interpreted as referring to the real being of things. Since it is most certain that things
do not have real being before God from eternity in time, the Dominicans infer from this that a real
coexistence of things before God from eternity, at least in eternity, must be acknowledged. It seems
to me, however, that Thomas is to be interpreted as speaking of the presence of things before God in
themselves indeed, but in willed being: this is how God beholds them when they are either future or
past, and he beholds them no differently when they become present in temporal terms. For God sees
everything outside God in himself and in his essence, whether considered simply or considered as
willing whatever comes to pass to come to pass, and at the time when it does. For it is as willing
that he is the principle of all actions. The claims you set out certainly differ, but it would be
difficult to judge which position is more absurd, yours or the one you attack.

You say that God sees from eternity the present being of the actual existence of contingent things
because the divine gaze or knowledge is measured by eternity and, anticipating the times, sees things
before they occur. First, I find restraint wanting in these words, for it is just as if you had said:
GOD sees the present being of the actual existence of contingent things before they occur because he
sees the present being of their actual existence before they occur; or this: God sees things before
they occur because he sees things before they occur. You may perhaps reply that I have stated your
reason incorrectly, since it was rather that God's knowledge is measured by eternity. But, I say,
you coupled that reason with the divine vision of things before they occur as though the two were
equivalent. Had the proposition been set out plainly, it would itself have exposed its own shameful
nakedness. You therefore inserted the measurement of divine knowledge by eternity, not to make your
position more reasonable, but rather to unsettle the reader's judgment so that he would not
immediately stand aghast at the construction of so ill-formed a proposition. Yet by this insertion
you do nothing to temper the absurdity of your position, but instead pile one absurdity upon another.
For this too is absurd: God sees the present being of the actual existence of things because his
vision is measured by eternity. A thing exists before it is measured, especially with respect to
duration, because it exists before it endures, just as a thing exists before it is preserved; and,
again, something endures before its duration is measured. Therefore this position of yours proceeds
according to a *hysteron proteron*, putting the later before the earlier. The argument is framed more
accurately thus: because this vision is from eternity, it is therefore measured by eternity, rather
than: because it is measured by eternity, it is therefore from eternity. For the existence of a
thing does not presuppose its measurement; rather, the measurement of a thing presupposes its
existence. From GOD's eternity it is instead inferred that whatever is in GOD has been from eternity,
and consequently that no gaze or knowledge of any contingent thing arises anew in GOD. But I dismiss
the idle talk of your argument and turn to the proposition itself, examining whether

[Book I, printed p. 89; BSB canvas 00115]

our gaze has the character it has because it is measured by time—or rather, is measured by time
because it exists. Thus, in my judgment, this phraseology of yours bodes no good. Earlier you spoke
more simply of the present being of things; here you speak more lavishly of the present being of the
actual existence of things. If you had added “of things existing in time, outside their causes,” you
would have looked after the number of your words excellently, so that nothing would remain but to
satisfy yourself and us about the weight of the proposition. As for the phrase “vision,” when it is
attributed to us it is distinguished from knowledge, but not when it is attributed to God; for you
yourself take the divine gaze and divine knowledge as the same. And so, my dear Penottus, I would like
to know whether, when God knows the present being of the actual existence of things, he knows that
actual existence to be present, and that from eternity. For God knows from eternity whatever he
knows. I readily believe that you would not wish your reader to cause you too much trouble at this
point, but rather to rest content with your abundance of words. Yet I see that it is worth the effort
for you to satisfy us at this fine point. If God knows from eternity that it is present, then it is
present either only according to intentional being—say, in known being or in willed being, which is
our position, not yours—or according to real being. And if according to real being, then either in
eternity, which is the Dominicans' position, attacked by you as far too monstrous with more exertion
than judgment; or in time, in which case you will throw yourself from the frying pan into the fire,
for it will follow that all things have existed absolutely from eternity. For contingent things to
exist from eternity according to real being in eternity is only to exist from eternity in a
qualified sense. But to exist from eternity according to real being in time is, in everyone's
judgment, to exist absolutely.

Again, I should like to scrape down this position of yours so that it may come out neater. “God sees
from eternity,” you say, “the present being of the actual existence of contingent things.” I ask
whether he sees as present the being of things which they had from eternity or the being which they
were going to have from eternity. If it is the being which they had from eternity, it follows from
your position that the actual existence of things has had present being before God from eternity;
therefore either according to real being or only according to intentional being, and so forth as
above. But if it is the being which they were going to have, then in seeing the present being of
things he saw only their future being. For the being they were going to have is future being, not
present being. Moreover, you say that from eternity he sees the present being of things. Did he not
also see from eternity the future being of those same things? Indeed, did he not see from eternity
the past being of those same things, of which you nevertheless seem to have taken no account? Yet
these points cannot be denied, for since every vision of God is eternal, it must never arise in him
anew. See, then, how I think the account should be framed at this point. From eternity God sees both
the future being of all contingent things and, at the same time, the present and past being of those
same things. But from eternity he had seen the future being of things to be present; conversely, he
had seen the present and past being of the same things to be future. Yet we say that all these things
are seen not by one vision and another, but by one and altogether the same vision. For just as to
decree that a thing will be produced at a definite time is to decree both that it will be future
until that time arrives, that it will be present as soon as the time arrives, and that it will be
past after that time has ceased to be; so too to know that a thing will be produced at a definite
time is both to know that it will be future before that time arrives, present as soon as it arrives,
and past after that time has ceased to be. Thus the cognition is altogether the same on the side of
the knower, although it is different on the side of the things known. And if you wish to hear the
truth: those whom you censure do not so much confuse things which should be distinguished as you
distinguish things which should by no means be distinguished. For while you put forward a
distinction between God's knowledge and the ground of his knowledge, you fall back into the same
thing; and while you distinguish by repeating the same thing, you bring it about that you distinguish
nothing; and by understanding in this way, you bring it about that you understand nothing. But the
Dominicans whom you attack hold that all things are known by God as present in themselves, and
accordingly place the real existence of things in themselves before divine knowledge, and that from
eternity. And since it is impossible for created things to have existed in time from eternity, by
the necessity of the consequence they are driven to place the existence of those things in eternity.
