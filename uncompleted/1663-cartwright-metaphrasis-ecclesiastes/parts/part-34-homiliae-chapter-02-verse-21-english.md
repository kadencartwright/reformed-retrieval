VERSE 25. [sic; verse 21] Therefore I turned myself away, depriving my mind of
hope concerning all this labor which I performed under the Sun.

By saying that he turned himself, he thereby declares that he devoted himself
entirely to that matter and directed all the sinews of his industry and
diligence toward it—not casually or perfunctorily, like a person who, while
keeping to a journey he has undertaken, looks back or turns his eyes to the
side. For the metaphor is transferred from a movement of the body to the most
intense exertion of the mind. This is indeed worthy of praise, provided that
the things toward which we direct the mind so intently and earnestly are
excellent; the contrary is true when we direct the mind's powers toward
useless or harmful things. We must therefore consider the nature of the thing
upon which Solomon labored so intently.

He therefore says that he did this so that he might deprive his mind of every
hope of receiving the fruit of his labors. But whether this emanated from the
judgment of the flesh or from the Spirit of God must be considered. First, to
lose heart concerning the receipt of fruit from labor appears equivalent to
breaking and enervating one's zeal and endeavor to labor, although this duty
lies upon us by the supreme God's command and decree. Furthermore, in this way
he appears to withdraw faith from the divine promise by which God promised
that a man would eat his bread from the sweat of his own face. Psalm 128. For
it is not only a threat but also contains an implied and tacit promise.

Next, if we consider the causes by which he was moved to cast himself into
this despair of mind, we shall understand that he appears to have departed
very far from his duty. The causes assigned are that he cannot himself enjoy
the things acquired by his labor or leave them to those whom he most wished
to succeed him. And what else is this than to intrude into God's secret
counsels and make himself anxious about matters which God has enclosed within
his sole power? He could take counsel with himself about the expedient by
which he might either enjoy the things acquired by labor himself or transmit
them to those most closely joined to him. Once he had done these things, it
was foolish to torment himself over his possessions and crucify his mind.

For it is just as if a servant should completely abandon the labor imposed
upon him by his master because the master applies what the servant has
produced to a use other than the one the servant desires. And that former
conduct is all the more unworthy, inasmuch as mortals generally dissipate
foolishly and unjustly what their servants have acquired, whereas God always
disposes of these things with the utmost wisdom and justice, even when they
come into the hands of the most senseless or most wicked men. Finally, even if
we receive no other benefit from labor, the fruit of duty is duty itself. It
must therefore be understood that Solomon reasoned with himself and with

his own mind, and strove with the utmost exertion to withdraw his mind from
that hope to which he had risen when he wholly devoted his mind to pursuing
these things—and not without reason. For it is plainly difficult to alienate
from the love of earthly things a mind once fixed upon them. It is therefore
necessary for a person to exert his sinews so that he may tear his mind away
when it has been united to these things.

There is also another class of men completely opposed to this one. They are
worthy of the highest blame, as Solomon is of praise. These are men who are
full of hope that they will abundantly enjoy the things they have acquired;
and when afterward they have no opportunity to enjoy them because death has
intervened, they trust that they can transfer them at their own discretion to
whomever they wish. Hence it is that, in acquiring the things by which this
life is sustained, they set no limit or measure for themselves. This proud and
arrogant class of men sins through presumption.

In the former case, this is credited to wisdom: they foresee the adversities
which enter into the account of human life. These men, however, are like the
eyeless: they look only at the things placed before their feet, remain
ignorant of the adversities that may occur, and slumber in the pleasures which
things acquired by labor supply. We must therefore keep to the middle way, so
that neither disgust with the advantages of the present life seizes us and
causes us to cast aside zeal and care for labor, nor do we pursue those
advantages greedily and thirstily in such a way as to inflame our minds and
cause us to set no limit or rule for ourselves in laboring and seeking the
advantages of this life.
