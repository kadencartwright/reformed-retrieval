# CHAPTER XXIII

## The fourth precept is proved to be moral from its own words

Finally, that the fourth precept of the Decalogue is moral can be proved from
a special consideration of the very words in which it is framed. Various
things must be considered here, but we do not wish them to be weighed
separately, but together.

*Consideration 1.* This precept is expressed in more words than any other
command of the Decalogue except the second, and there are no others besides
these two which people wish to strike from the roll of moral precepts. Without
doubt the Lord foresaw that some would come to such a point of audacity that
they would dare to mutilate this divine law and reject especially these two
precepts. He therefore wished to fortify these two with a greater apparatus
of words, and either to deter people from this crime or at least to render
more inexcusable those who would wish not only to violate these precepts but
also to dissolve them and teach people accordingly.

*Consideration 2.* This precept is urged and set forth in a singular manner.
Whereas all the others are set forth either positively or negatively, this
one is urged in both ways: *Remember the Sabbath day, to sanctify it*, and,
*the seventh day is the Sabbath of the Lord your God; you shall do no work in
it*. This was so that its obligation might appear more manifestly and
demonstratively, and so that less might remain for detractors to object. It is
certain that God does nothing in vain. He therefore wished, not without the
best reason, that this command should be distinguished from all the other
precepts of the Decalogue in its manner of presentation. Since, then, the
others, which are set forth in only one manner, are moral by the opponents'
own admission, why will this one, which is set forth in both manners, not also
be moral? That singular manner, which serves so greatly to urge and inculcate
its observance, ought not to lessen its obligation.

*Consideration 3.* To this precept alone is prefixed *remember*. In the
earlier precepts he simply said, *you shall not have, you shall not make, you
shall not take up*. In the later ones he says, *honor, you shall not kill, you
shall not commit adultery, you shall not steal*, and so forth. But here he
says, *remember*, and so forth. [*From this it is evident*, says Zanchi in the
place cited above, page 848, *how important this fourth precept is and how far
it excels the others.*] In the same place he says: [*Through all these things
God taught that it was especially close to his heart that this command be
kept, inasmuch as the sum of each kind of worship and of religion is contained
in it.*] [*Remember*, he says further on page 854, *that is, call this command
of mine to mind with an attentive and faithful mind, so that it never falls
from memory.*] The celebrated Rivet comments on this word as follows:
[*זכור, zakor, is literally “in remembering,” and זכר, zekor, “remember,” is
understood, so that when the ellipsis is supplied it is “remember in
remembering.” By this repetition the Hebrews indicate intensity, so that it
means, “remember altogether or diligently, so that you never forget.”
Scripture customarily uses this exhortation where the matter is important yet
of such a kind that it could be neglected unless it were frequently recalled,
account being taken of human weakness. In Deuteronomy 5:12, where the command
is repeated, Moses uses another word, namely שמור, shamor,*

[Printed p. 241 | IA PDF 249 | IA leaf 249 | IA decausadei02brow]

*shamor, “keep, observe,” from שמר, shamar, which is “to keep” or “preserve,”
also “to hold, retain,” which belongs to memory. This word indicates care,
solicitude, and diligence, lest what must be observed be let go, flow away, or
be shaken out. The Septuagint has φυλάξαι. The Lord used the word for keeping
or preserving in this sense: “Blessed are those who hear God's word and keep
it,” Luke 11:28, φυλάσσοντες αὐτόν. The reasons why special attention and
greater solicitude are required here are that human works had to be
interrupted and God's work undertaken. Human beings are so constituted that,
in the heat of their own works in which they are occupied for the sake of
their own advantage or pleasure, they are accustomed eagerly to linger in
them while neglecting all things, even the more important. Moreover, because
this precept is, as it were, intermediate between the other precepts of the
first and second tables, and the sanctification of the Sabbath is like the
sinew of understanding and obeying the other precepts—for the law had to be
read and expounded on the Sabbath, and the teaching of God's Word had to be
learned from its public proclamation, according to which the whole course of
life in the calling of believers had to be ordered, so that if this precept
were neglected all the others too would fall away—this special exhortation to
refresh the memory was suitably added to it. Since among the Hebrews (as also
happens in other languages) this connotes affections that either precede or
follow memory, and duties to be performed from them, nothing more apt to the
matter could have been said.*]

Because a new institution is not usually set forth in this way, one may infer
that the Sabbath was not then first instituted, as we proved above in book 3,
chapter 9. One may further infer from this that God earnestly wills this
command to be rightly observed. He therefore wills us diligently to prepare
for celebrating this Sabbath of God, laying aside our earthly cares and
business, which commonly distract a person and render him unfit to perform
this duty rightly. The law of nature itself therefore requires this, since it
is impossible for a person both to pursue his own affairs with an intent mind
and immediately worship God. Consequently, it is impossible for him rightly
to sanctify solemn time unless, by remembering, he recalls this time and
diligently applies himself so that he may sanctify it with his whole soul and
all his powers. Primerose himself admits this in part 2, chapter 7, section 2,
when he says: [*One who commands another to perform something of greater
importance at a certain ordinary and fixed future time can well use these
terms: remember such a work and the time in which it is to be performed before
it comes, so that you may be more prepared to do it rightly. And this is all
the Lord says here: namely, that they should remember that day before it comes
and seasonably dispose themselves to sanctify it.*] What then? Shall we not
say that this precept is moral, which God wished in no way to be consigned to
oblivion and for whose observance so much preparation, care, and solicitude
are required? Is this not a moral duty which concerns us no less than the
Jews? Who can rightly dissolve and render void that command which God orders
us to remember? Is it not evident from this that it is a moral duty to
sanctify the Sabbath ordained and appointed by God? Who will say that this is
something purely ceremonial? Primerose himself, in the place cited above,
does not dare say this. On the contrary, he admits that, *speaking absolutely
and universally, it cannot be called a ceremony because*, he says, *the
Sabbath day was to be sanctified by moral duties.* He does add [*that the
sanctification of the Sabbath was ceremonial insofar as the Sabbath was
limited*

[Printed p. 242 | IA PDF 250 | IA leaf 250 | IA decausadei02brow]

*to precisely the seventh day, and insofar as it was sanctified with
sacrifices, offerings, and ceremonies of that kind, and with rigorous rest.*]
But these things do not pertain to the substance of the precept about which we
are speaking. We know that the moral element of this precept does not consist
in observing the seventh day in order, in that ceremonial worship, or in that
rigorous rest, as we shall shortly make clear.

Nor does what he has in the same place, pages 145–46, harm the moral character
of the precept: [*There are some who think*, he says, *that God prefixed the
word Remember to this command because it was not natural like the others and
the Jews could therefore more easily consign it to oblivion.*] He knows well
enough that some affirm that not everything moral is natural, and that there
is such a thing as positive morality. We not only affirm this but confirmed it
above in book 1, chapter 15.

Another objects as follows: [*This word was prefixed because it was dangerous
for the Jews to forget that day, since it was consecrated to the reading of
the law, which they could not be ignorant of without harm. So great a danger
does not threaten us, for we have other days for publicly reading and
preaching God's Word.*] But 1. did the Jews themselves not have other days
besides the weekly Sabbath which God himself instituted? 2. Do we Christians
have other ordinary and fixed days instituted by God? Hence, as it seems, this
injunction of solicitude concerns us more. Thus this word is evidence of the
moral character of this precept, since it did not concern the Jews alone.

*Consideration 4.* *Remember the Sabbath day*—so the precept has it, זכור את
יום. Nothing appears here which can weaken the moral character of this
precept. For it can by no reason be denied that it is moral to consecrate a
whole day to God and divine exercises. No one who weighs how greatly God's
glory and the business of our eternal salvation are to be preferred to
earthly and perishable things which concern our body will fail to say that
right reason teaches it to be most equitable that a *whole day* be dedicated
to God and spiritual concerns, while the time necessarily required to refresh
our perishable bodies with food, sleep, and other necessities is reserved.
This determination of time with respect also to its duration therefore cannot
render this precept ceremonial; rather, it further confirms its moral
character. Nothing appears here except what breathes the highest morality, as
we showed in passing above in book 3, chapter 28.

*Consideration 5.* *Remember the Sabbath day*, זכור את יום השבת. By *Sabbath*
here extraordinary festivals are not meant, nor the Sabbath of the seventh
day in order. For, as we recently heard from Zanchi, these differ greatly:
remember to sanctify the *seventh day*, and remember to sanctify the *Sabbath
day*; and God said *this*, not *that*, for good reason. The word *Sabbath* does
not signify the seventh day, but the day of sacred rest, or a day appointed by
God for sacred rest. Heylin himself admits in his *History of the Sabbath*
that in the Scriptures Sabbath denotes a time selected by God himself and
assigned to rest and holiness. Hence Chrysostom's phrase, σάββατον ἐκάλεσεν
ἀργίαν καλῆν, and Isidore's, σάββατον πᾶσαν ἑορτὴν καλεῖται. What then? Is it
not moral to observe a day appointed by God for sacred uses? Some opponents
say that the essence, life, and spirit—as they speak—of the fourth precept
consists in sanctifying to God Sabbaths instituted by him. They hold this to
be perpetually moral. Others say that the moral character of the fourth
precept requires *some sufficient time*.

[Printed p. 243 | IA PDF 251 | IA leaf 251 | IA decausadei02brow]

But this sufficient time is time determined by God, for he alone knows what
that time is. They also say that the Jewish festivals are referred to this
precept. But this cannot be so unless *Sabbath day* here means indefinite
time, or any time determined by God. Nothing therefore appears here except
what confirms the moral character of this precept. For it is moral that all
the time God has determined and consecrated to pious exercises be observed in
holiness.

*Consideration 6.* This Sabbath day is to be sanctified, that is, dedicated
and applied to sacred uses and exercises. No particular manner of sanctifying
this Sabbath day is determined here. It is only enjoined generally that we
regard as such the day already sanctified by God—set apart from the others and
consecrated to divine worship, as Zanchi speaks in the place cited above, page
853. He therefore says that it is to be acknowledged and celebrated as holy;
that after the labors of six days rest is to be taken on it while the exercise
of religion is to be pursued; and consequently that God's sanctification, by
which he sanctified this day, is not to be profaned but preserved unimpaired
in both soul and body. A little later he says: *When, therefore, he says,
Remember to sanctify the Sabbath day, the sense is not, Consecrate this day to
God and divine worship, for God had done this from the beginning of the world;
but it is, Acknowledge in your mind this day consecrated by me to divine
worship as such, and confirm it in practice by ceasing from your works and
devoting yourself to my worship.* Nothing appears here that is not moral. The
precept says nothing about doubling sacrifices or rendering ceremonial
worship on that day, for these things are commanded elsewhere. It only
enjoins generally that we sanctify the Sabbath day, consecrated by God and set
apart from all the others, with sacred exercises instituted by God. That is,
it enjoins the application of religious exercises instituted by God to that
time determined by God, or the application of time to those exercises. It is
therefore moral to expend solemn time instituted by God upon religious
exercises, and this concerns us no less than the Jews. It is true that the
ceremonial worship with which the Sabbath day was formerly sanctified has now
been abolished. Nevertheless, the moral character of this precept remains
intact and unimpaired because it does not determine that particular worship,
but only commands us to spend the Sabbath day in worship instituted by God.

*Consideration 7.* Rest, or cessation from works, is indeed required in this
precept, but not as though it were the whole thing in which sanctification of
the Sabbath consisted, nor as though it were the principal element of this
precept. It is required as a necessary consequence of divine dedication and a
necessary means of our sanctification. For when God sanctifies a day and
consecrates it to sacred exercises, he separates and sequesters it from
secular works. God's sanctification is therefore profaned when a day
sanctified by God is expended on common labors which pertain to secular life.
Is this too not moral: that on the day which God reserved for himself and
religious exercises and separated from all the others, we abstain from
unnecessary works? This is especially so since, while intent upon our own
business, we cannot worship spiritually the God who is spirit and wills to be
worshiped in spirit, nor can we rightly perform the exercises of piety to be
rendered on that day. For *when the sense is intent on many things, it attends
less to each*. Whatever may be said of rest considered in itself and
absolutely, this is nevertheless most certain: it is a moral duty then to
cease from our daily works,

[Printed p. 244 | IA PDF 252 | IA leaf 252 | IA decausadei02brow]

when God commands us to devote ourselves to divine worship. Indeed, nature
itself teaches this: rest, or cessation from our works, is an inseparable
adjunct of a festival or consecrated day, because devoting oneself to God's
worship on a sanctified day and expending that same day on our business are
incompatible. Hence, notwithstanding this injunction of rest, the precept is
moral. Indeed, nothing is required here with respect to rest except what
nature itself teaches and requires.

*Consideration 8.* When it says, *Six days you shall labor and do all your
work, but the seventh day*, and so forth, a singular equity is manifest in
this proportional division of time between God and us. Right reason cannot
fail to recognize the equity of this precept even at first sight. Therefore a
precept resting on such a reason, which is most equitable, cannot fail to be
moral. When God, who is the absolute Lord of all time, divided time so that he
granted six days to us and reserved the seventh to himself, who will deny that
it is moral for this seventh day to be consecrated to God in holiness? Will
this be less equitable now than it was formerly? Who will say so? Primerose
himself does not deny it in the place cited above, section 4. Indeed, he openly
admits that manifest justice and equity shine here. Yet he adds [*that the
equity consists in this: if a person has several days granted to him, it is
equitable that he consecrate one to God.*] In this way he virtually denies the
equity of the precept. For the precept says that six ordinary days are granted
to human beings and that one after the six is to be consecrated to God. But if
his account of its equity is true, it will be satisfied if a person expends
one day on divine exercises in the whole course of his life, because after
expending many days on his own business he thus consecrates one to God.
Whether this is equitable I doubt; indeed, I do not hesitate to call it
supremely inequitable. [*Since God*, says Bucer, *in his singular love toward
us sanctified only one of seven days for the restoration of our faith and
therefore of eternal life, anyone who does not take care to sanctify that very
day by glorifying his Lord plainly shows himself a wretched despiser both of
his own salvation and of divine beneficence.*]

Primerose then appears to correct this when he says: [*It would be more just
if we expended more days on divine things than on our own, indeed if we
expended all of them.*] But I cannot approve this either, given things as they
now stand. God knows perfectly well what is just and what unjust. Yet this
further confirms the equity of the precept. For if it were just to give God
more days than ourselves, it will be much more just, after six days have been
spent on our business, to expend one on divine exercises. What else does he
have? [*Consequently, under the New Testament, when Christians ought to be
more generous, all days, as far as possible, should be holy to God. But
because they cannot celebrate solemn assemblies and gatherings every day,
they ought to determine a time for this business, and no fewer than one in a
cycle of seven days.*] *Response.* Since people cannot expend every day on
divine exercises, it is necessary that a principal, sufficient, and solemn
time be determined. This belongs not to any person or human society, but to
God alone, as was seen above in book 2, chapters 11, 12, and 14. It therefore
follows that God has determined this time. But where does such a determination
exist if not in this fourth precept of the Decalogue? And if such a
determination and proportional division of time has been made under both the
Old and New Testaments, it is manifest that this proportion is equitable and
just, and that its observance is moral.

[Printed p. 245 | IA PDF 253 | IA leaf 253 | IA decausadei02brow]

He later says that consecrating the seventh day to God after six working days
cannot fail to be most equitable. Yet he adds: [*This division is nevertheless
no more just, better proportioned, or more obligatory in itself and its own
nature, especially for us Christians, than one by which one day out of six,
five, or four is consecrated to God. Indeed, it is not equally just.*] I
certainly cannot agree. Whatever God does is just—indeed, most just—and I must
regard it as most just because it seemed good to him to do it. But does equity
and justice not shine in the determination and proportion made, satisfying
right reason and enabling it to conclude that the precept which enjoins the
sanctification of the seventh day after six working days is moral?

Nor can I approve what follows: [*The more days we sanctify to God, the more
we do what is just, equitable, and well ordered, and the more we discharge our
duty to which we are naturally obligated.*] Justice and equity consist in the
conformity of our actions to God's expressed law. [*God*, he says further,
*determined one of seven, not because that proportion was naturally just or
most fitting, but because at that time it seemed good to him not to require a
day in a shorter cycle from a people already sufficiently burdened with
ceremonies.*] *Response.* I admit that God made this determination from his
bare good pleasure. Whether it was *naturally just* in the logical moment
before the decree is not for me to inquire, since it is certain that it is now
the most just and equitable of all. Let that suffice. 2. That people was not
yet burdened with ceremonies when, even on his view, this determination was
first instituted.

He further says: [*Since no particular justice is attached to the number
seven more than to another number, this reason is not moral and perpetual but
only positive. What is moral is only the substance and foundation: namely,
that one of many days be dedicated to God. It is also perpetual that we
Christians determine one day in no larger a cycle.*] *Response.* Whatever may
be said about this proportion before the determination was made, it is now
certain that this proportion possesses particular justice and equity. Right
reason cannot fail to acknowledge the equity of this determination made by
God under both the New and Old Testaments. It will therefore be moral and
perpetual. 2. That general equity is far too loose, as said above. It appears
more equitable to dedicate to God one day in the weekly cycle than one in the
course of our life. 3. If it is now unlawful to determine a day in some larger
cycle, there is a certain peculiar and perpetual equity in the determination
already made. He therefore errs when he concludes: [*God made no determination
under the New Testament, and we are no more obligated to observe one of seven
than one of six.*] For the particular equity which formerly shone in selecting
one day from the number seven has not now vanished. That proportion is no less
equitable and just now than it ever was, and it is no less equitable now than
formerly that the power to determine it should belong to God alone. If we are
more obligated to observe one day in seven than one in twenty, a hundred, or
a thousand, we are also more obligated ordinarily and constantly to observe
one in seven than one in six, because of the determination already made by the
most wise Lawgiver.

*Consideration 9.* The words *six days you shall labor* contain something
moral. Therefore so do the words

[Printed p. 246 | IA PDF 254 | IA leaf 254 | IA decausadei02brow]

*on the seventh you shall do no work*. This is manifest if *six days you shall
labor* includes a command of diligent labor and a prohibition of idleness, as
some opponents judge. It is also manifest if, as is true, they contain a
precept not concerning work considered in itself, for that is enjoined in the
second table, but concerning work with respect to this limitation of time.
Thus they contain an implicit prohibition, not a bare permission. For if it is
moral that we ordinarily work no more than six days together, but complete
all our work within six days so that, freed from hindrances, we may expend the
seventh on sacred things, it will also be moral to consecrate the seventh to
God. If the means is moral, why not the end?

Primerose replies briefly in the place cited above, section 3. First, [*the
contrary is inferred from this because it is neither necessary nor just that
we work for six days together, although it is just and equitable that we work
in our present condition.*] *Response.* Since it is moral, just, and equitable
that in this mortal state we labor with our hands to obtain food and clothing,
why will it not also be moral, just, and equitable that we ordinarily work no
more than six days together, once God has made this determination and
established this division of time between our works and religious exercises?
It is moral that we worship God solemnly and perform our daily works. We
cannot perform both at one and the same time; reason therefore requires a
proportional division. Right reason teaches that it does not belong to a human
being to determine the proportion of time necessary for each. Therefore, when
this proportion is determined by God, to whom alone this power belongs, its
observance will be moral, because that determination is always the same, does
not concern one people more than another, and suits one condition of the
church no more than another.

He then replies: [*Precisely this alone is commanded here: the observance and
sanctification of the Sabbath day by cessation from ordinary works. But “six
days you shall labor” is no part of divine worship and therefore no part of
this command, but only a permission.*] *Response.* 1. Not only is observance
of the Sabbath day commanded here by cessation from ordinary works, but also
by positive sanctification. 2. Although *six days you shall labor* does not
contain a part of immediate worship, it contains a moral and necessary means
for rightly rendering worship. 3. The words do not contain a bare permission,
because that bare permission would be understood even if they did not exist.
If he had said only, *on the seventh day you shall do no work*, it would plainly
have been understood that working for six days was permitted. These words are
therefore not idle in this brief compendium, but contain something which could
not otherwise easily be understood and which is necessary for rightly
celebrating the Sabbath day. Thus they expressly determine and limit the
number of working days to be spent together on our earthly business, and do
so with a view to sanctifying the Sabbath day.

Third, he replies: [*The labor of six days can be called a type and figure of
the labors and afflictions by which believers are tormented in this life.*]
*Response.* 1. Scripture does not explain such a type to us, and types must
not be admitted without Scripture. 2. Believers in every age have had
afflictions and hard labors. But a thing prefigured by types is peculiar to
the church under the New Testament, for types are types of future things.

[Printed p. 247 | IA PDF 255 | IA leaf 255 | IA decausadei02brow]

3. Something moral can foreshadow our heavenly rest, since that rest can be
foreshadowed even under the New Testament—indeed, in the state of innocence.
Thus, although Sabbath rest were a certain foreshadowing (it is nowhere called
a type) of heavenly rest, it can nevertheless be something moral.
Consequently, the labor of six days can be something moral even if it is
constituted a certain foreshadowing of the afflictions and persecutions of the
godly in this life.

Some reply as follows: [*To argue this way is the same as saying that, because
exercising virtue is a praiseworthy work, interrupting that same work for a
time will be praiseworthy, although the contrary would rather follow.*]
*Response.* This evasion is utterly futile. We do not say that doing this or
that particular work is moral, nor that bare cessation from work is moral. We
say that it is moral to seek food by lawful labor and provide clothing for
ourselves and our families; that it is likewise moral to worship God
solemnly; that we certainly cannot rightly do both together; and that a
division of time and determination of a proportion suitable for each must
therefore be made. Finally, since this determination has been made and the
proportion established, if it is moral that we ordinarily work for precisely
six days together, it is also moral that the seventh be consecrated to God
after six working days and that on it we abstain from our work. We are now
held bound to the latter under the gospel no less than to the former.

*Consideration 10.* *The seventh day is the Sabbath of the Lord your God.*
This seventh day is not the seventh *in order*, or the last day in the weekly
cycle, whatever the opponents say, as will afterward be made clear. It is the
seventh *in number*, or one of seven, just as the six working days are not the
first six in the weekly cycle, but simply six together, whether earlier or
later. All reason plainly requires God to determine sufficient time for his
worship, or the portion of time to be expended in religious exercises. Since
this precept does not command precisely the seventh *in order* to be observed,
as we shall afterward make clear, but prescribes a proportion to be observed;
and since this proportion is not peculiar to the Old Testament church but
common to the church in every age, it is manifest that this precept cannot be
called ceremonial, but moral. We know that that particular seventh day has
been changed. But it is not established that the proportion, or seventh
portion of time, has been removed. For if the determination of this portion
has been removed, every determination of a portion has been removed.
Consequently, one who consecrates to God only one day in the cycle of a year
or twenty years—indeed, in the whole course of his life—will not sin. If
someone says that he must dedicate at least one of seven to God, we ask by
what law this must be done. If some divine law requires it, that portion will
be moral. If no divine law requires it, by what right must it be done?

*Consideration 11.* This Sabbath is *God's Sabbath*, or proper to God and
consecrated by him to his worship. Although in some manner this Sabbath could
have been called the Sabbath of human beings because it was prescribed to
them, it is expressly called *God's Sabbath* here. This is so that people may
thereby be deterred all the more from violating this precept, because he
reserved this day, or portion of time, to himself, and it cannot be applied to
other unnecessary uses without heinous sacrilege. It is impious

[Printed p. 248 | IA PDF 256 | IA leaf 256 | IA decausadei02brow]

to alienate anything reserved and appropriated by God to himself. Nature
itself recoils from this. Since, therefore, God claims this portion of time as
his own and we nowhere read that he has divested himself of that ownership,
it follows that this precept, which enjoins the holy observance of this
portion, or its actual consecration to God, is moral. For it is moral to render
to God what is God's, and to consecrate to God that day or time which he willed
to be consecrated to himself perpetually.

*Primerose objects*, in the place cited above, section 7: [*First, it follows
from this that the seventh in order must be perpetually observed, because that
is the particular seventh meant in the preceding words.*] *Response.* It is
evident from what has been said, and will be clearer from what follows, that
the seventh mentioned here is the seventh *in number*, not in order. Thus the
consequence is null.

*Objection 2.* [*He also calls the offerings and sacrifices his, as well as
the entire Jewish worship, yet it does not follow that all these things are
moral and still to be observed.*] *Response.* We do not argue for the moral
character of the Sabbath solely because of this, but because the Lord has
nowhere divested himself of this ownership. No one should reply that God
divested himself of it when he abrogated the seventh day in order with the
other ceremonies. It does not follow from this that God divested himself of
his ownership with respect to the portion of time. The seventh day in order
can be abolished or abrogated while this portion, or the seventh part of time,
remains unchanged. This precept speaks of that portion, as already said. Let
the opponent therefore show that God has removed the determination of this
portion.

*Consideration 12.* This command is not merely set forth like all the rest:
*You shall sanctify*. These words are further added: *you shall do no work,
neither you, nor your son, nor your daughter, nor your male servant, nor your
female servant, nor your beast, nor any guest who stays within your gates.*
When he had first said, *Remember the Sabbath day to sanctify it*, that, as in
all the other precepts, sufficiently indicated that this precept concerns all
people as such and must be particularly applied by everyone to himself. It
thus indicated a duty common to all, whether parents or children, servants or
masters. But when he adds what follows, he indicates a special duty,
especially of superiors and in some manner of everyone: namely, that all,
particularly parents, masters, and consequently magistrates, are bound to
take care, each according to his office and power, that this precept is kept
in holiness by all inferiors and subjects. The persons bound by this
prohibition are not merely designated here, as the celebrated Rivet holds,
because that would have been entirely superfluous when all these persons had
been included in the words, *Remember the Sabbath day to sanctify it*.
Moreover, beasts would then have to be numbered among these persons, which is
absurd. The precept is not given to beasts, but to people concerning beasts.
Therefore, since this peculiar duty of superiors is expressly enjoined only
in this precept—although it must be applied to all the others—and this precept
is especially directed to superiors, because they are bound not only to
observe it themselves but also to take every care that their inferiors and
subjects likewise observe it, is it not manifest that God wills the greatest
care to be used in observing this command? Who, then, will say that a command
is not moral

[Printed p. 249 | IA PDF 257 | IA leaf 257 | IA decausadei02brow]

when care for its observance is particularly committed to superiors? Will
this clause prove that the precept is merely ceremonial? Or will it prove that
it is less moral than the others to which this clause is not expressly added?

*Consideration 13.* It is implied here that servants are to be given relief
from labor, as is also evident from Exodus 23:12. No one will deny that under
the New Testament masters too are bound to this act of piety and mercy, for
even under the New Testament God mercifully cares for servants. It is
therefore something moral, and thus the precept is in part confirmed as moral,
since what it indicates only in passing belongs to perpetual justice and
equity.

Primerose objects in the place cited above, section 9: [*To give relief to
servants and beasts is naturally just and equitable. But therefore to set
apart for this relief one of seven days more than one of a greater number, or
precisely the seventh itself, is not moral.*] *Response.* If it is naturally
just and equitable to give servants relief, why will it not be just and
equitable to give them relief on every seventh day? Such a determination does
not remove morality, as we made clear long ago in book 1, chapter 14. 2. We
proved above that it is moral to consecrate one day out of seven to God. We do
not need to prove it from this consideration. It is enough that this plainly
does not prove the command ceremonial. If it is not ceremonial, it will be
moral. Furthermore, nothing appears here which can prove that this command is
not moral, and nothing is commanded which cannot be called moral.

*Consideration 14.* It follows from the same words that foreigners were bound
to observe this command. For magistrates and other superiors are here
commanded to take every care lest foreigners within the gates violate God's
Sabbath by their daily works. That to which Gentiles too are obligated is
moral. We shall afterward prove this very thing: that the nations were bound
to observe this precept of the Decalogue. Indeed, this was abundantly
confirmed above in chapter 2 of this book and in book 3, chapter 7.

Primerose, however, judges this vain and frivolous in the place cited above,
section 9: [*If those foreigners were proselytes of righteousness, they were
equally obligated to everything as the Jews themselves. But if they were
resident proselytes, such as those in Nehemiah 13, they were indeed compelled
not to violate the Sabbath publicly, lest by their example they give the Jews
occasion to violate it.*] *Response.* It is manifest that resident proselytes
are meant here, whom householders could restrain from working on the Sabbath
day, just as Nehemiah restrained those Tyrians from violating the Sabbath.
Furthermore, no difference appears here between those *foreigners of the
gate*, or foreigners within the gates, and sons, daughters, male servants,
and female servants. With respect to them all alike, householders and other
superiors are commanded to take diligent care that they do not violate the
Sabbath. Yet this precept obligated sons, daughters, and so forth no less than
parents.

He further objects: [*Those foreigners were forbidden to eat leaven, Exodus
12:19, or blood, Leviticus 17:10, 12, 13. Yet it does not follow that these
precepts were moral.*] *Response.* How will the opponent prove that these
foreigners were resident or gate proselytes and not proselytes of
righteousness? The text itself, Exodus 12:43–45, indicates that gate
proselytes were not permitted to participate in the Feast of Unleavened Bread

[Printed p. 250 | IA PDF 258 | IA leaf 258 | IA decausadei02brow]

until they were circumcised, that is, converted to the religion and made
proselytes of righteousness. Leviticus 17 speaks of the same proselytes, as
Dr. Heidegger proves in *On the Ancient Dietary Law*, pages 104–5: [*1.
Uncircumcised proselytes were not forbidden by the law to eat carrion,
Deuteronomy 14:21. Hence those uncircumcised proselytes were periphrastically
called by the Jews* אוכלי נבילות, *eaters of carrion. Carrion is not without
blood, but was unlawful to the Jews because it had not been drained of blood.
2. The periphrasis used there is* גר הגר, *a foreigner sojourning, which never
occurs of an uncircumcised person. 3. Moses speaks of the same foreigners in
verse 10, where he restrains the eating of blood by law, as in verses 8 and 9,
where he deals with bringing a burnt offering to the entrance of the tent of
meeting. For in each place, in the same manner and the same words of the laws
concerning sacrifice and the eating of blood, the receiving subjects are
established as Israelites and foreigners among them. But foreigners among
the Israelites who are instructed in the rite of sacrificing are not
uncircumcised but circumcised foreigners.*] Afterward, on pages 107–8, he
responds to Curcellaeus's statement, [*The word* גר *does not have the force
of distinguishing a kind and is often used even of a resident foreigner*], as
follows: [*Mercerus indeed favors taking* גר *usually and specifically for a

foreigners among the Egyptians. That reason has no force if we explain it of
a proselyte of righteousness, for the Israelites were foreigners in Egypt in
such a way that they did not share religious fellowship with them.*] In the
same place, to Curcellaeus's statement [*Nor is there greater force in the
conjunction of the two words* גר הגר; *for no distinction is indicated among
sojourners or the causes of their sojourning, unless perhaps this: those who
were accustomed to sojourn among the Israelites were so called, which could
be done for causes other than religion*], he responds: [*A resident proselyte
is never designated by this name, and the Septuagint renders it προσήλυτον
προσκείμενον, as though you were to say, one adhering to the sacred rites of
the Jews. A foreigner of habitation could certainly not suitably be called*
גר הגר, *a foreigner sojourning, for this denotes the act of sojourning. Those

Those who lived outside Judea* ἐν τῇ διασπορᾷ, *among Parthians, Medes,
Elamites, and in Pontus, Asia, Phrygia, Pamphylia, and Egypt, were also genuine

*proselytes are said to have been present from those nations.*] I do not deny
that Gentiles could sojourn among the Jews for a reason other than religion,
as Curcellaeus says. Such were the proselytes called שכירים, hired workers:
that is, artisans, whom the Jews distinguish from both preceding kinds. Such
too were foreigners passing through, or coming there to transact business and
soon preparing to return home. But no one who loves truth will assert that
foreigners of that kind are meant here. For why would God impose the laws of
the Israelites on them? Indeed, why on them more than all others? Did their
casual sojourn in the land of the Jews render itself subject to the barriers
of such laws?] Thus this objection has been abundantly answered, and what we
assert has been further confirmed.

*Consideration 15.* Another reason follows in the precept: *For in six days
the Lord completed heaven and earth, the sea, and whatever is contained in
them, but rested on the seventh day.* These words contain the reason,
argument, and motive for our sanctification and observance of the Sabbath, as
the prefixed causal כי, *because*, proves. This reason drawn from God's example
declares that the precept is moral because it is common and universal to all,
not relative to one people or condition of the church. We do not say that in
all his actions God set himself before us for imitation. But it is manifest
here that God's example is set before us as an effective and universal
motive. Since it is universal in nature and concerns everyone no less than
the Jews themselves, it is plainly established that the duty urged by this
special reason is likewise universal and moral.

Primerose objects in the place cited above, section 15: [*God completed his
work in six days and rested on the seventh because he willed to; the thing
itself possessed no natural equity or manifest morality. Therefore no
obligation to imitate it can naturally be inferred.*] *Response.* It is true
that God, who could have created everything in a single moment, expended six
days on creating all things and rested on the seventh because he willed to. I
also admit that it does not *naturally* follow that we ought similarly to work
for six days and rest on the seventh. Nevertheless, since he sets this
example of his before us for imitation and thereby indicates that he adopted
this order—creating all things in six days and resting on the seventh from all
his works—for the purpose of setting himself before us as an example, can it
not be inferred that this command, whose peculiar reason and virtual
foundation were laid in the first origin of things, is moral and common?

*Objection 2, in the same place.* [*This reason is not immediately added as an
example or cause of obligation, but as the occasion of the precept, because it
is added, “therefore the Lord blessed,” and so forth. This blessing and
sanctification, made known by the command, obligated the Jews to observe the
seventh day, not that manner of God's working immediately.*] *Response.* It is
true that the immediate foundation of obligation is God's command, for it is
the sole foundation of all obedience and of all morality in our human
actions. Yet another reason and motive can be drawn from elsewhere. Does the
Lord not frequently add motives which are not the immediate cause of
obligation? See even the fifth precept of the Decalogue. 2. Just as it adds,
*therefore the Lord blessed*, and so forth, so it first said, *for in six days
the Lord completed*, and so forth. Hence it is evident that these words
contain not only

[Printed p. 252 | IA PDF 260 | IA leaf 260 | IA decausadei02brow]

the occasion of the precept, but also the reason for our sanctification of the
Sabbath. 3. If that blessing and sanctification, made known by the command,
held the Jews bound to observe the seventh day, why will it not hold us bound?

*Objection 3, section 16.* [*This consequence is not good. If it seemed good
to God to give this institution to the Jews on the occasion of the order he
observed in creating all things, then this institution will obligate
Christians too, because it is not founded on anything moral.*] *Response.* 1.
We do not form such a consequence, for we know that very many things were
given to the Jews which do not obligate us. Rather, we reason as follows. A
command given to the Jews is moral when its primary, proper, internal, and
particular reason, on which it particularly rests and by which it is
peculiarly urged, is moral and perpetual, and when it was not given only to
the Jews. But the Decalogue's precept concerning sanctifying the Sabbath is of
this kind. For this reason drawn from God's example is moral, obligating us no
less than the Jews. He cannot deny this. But he says: [*That moral element
consists in this: just as God, after completing all his works over the space
of several days, rested on some day, so we too ought to appoint some day on
which, laying aside our ordinary works, we devote ourselves to God's
worship.*] But who will believe that God's example would bind us more to
sanctify one indeterminate day, unless some command intervened, than to
observe some determined day? Further, did God not freely perform all his works
over several days when he could have created everything in a single moment?
Can such an obligation therefore be naturally inferred from this example of
God? Would the Jews, without God's command, have thought themselves bound by
the force of this example to observe one indeterminate day? Either this is not
the moral character of this example, therefore, or his arguments above are of
no value. Furthermore, I would like it explained how God's example of working
for six determined days and resting on the seventh can ground a moral reason
for sanctifying some indefinite day after several indefinite working days,
but cannot ground a moral reason for sanctifying the seventh day after six
working days. If God had never revealed to human beings how many days he took
to complete all his work, but had merely said that he rested on some one day
after creating everything over several days, then his example would indeed
have grounded only a moral reason for sanctifying some indeterminate day after
several working days. But the Lord expressly indicated that he completed all
his works in six determined days and rested on the seventh; and by the force
of this example he commanded us to sanctify the seventh to God after six
working days. This moral reason therefore touches a determined day.

*Objection 4, section 17.* [*Christians are not moved by this example in
observing the Lord's Day, because although they observe the seventh after six
working days, they do not observe that seventh day on which God rested.*]
*Response.* The moral element of this precept is not that we observe the
seventh day, as will appear later, but one of seven. The reason therefore must
not be extended further. It would be wrongly argued: we must rest on one of
seven because God rested on the seventh in order. It would also be wrongly
argued: we must rest on the seventh day in order, or the last of the week,
because God rested on one of seven. But it is very well argued: we must
sanctify one after six working days, or one in the weekly cycle, because God
did so.

[Printed p. 253 | IA PDF 261 | IA leaf 261 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Consideration 16.* When it is added, *therefore the Lord blessed the Sabbath
day*, it is manifest that the time when the Lord blessed and sanctified the
Sabbath day is designated here: namely, when he rested after those six days in
which he completed the work of creation. Everyone concedes that it is moral
for some Sabbath to be dedicated to God, and all reason requires God himself
to designate and determine that Sabbath. On what ground, then, can it be
denied that God designated this Sabbath at the beginning when he rested from
his works, since it is admitted that he afterward declared this rest of his to
have been the reason or occasion for sanctifying the Sabbath, and since his
rest is here set before us as an example? But we dealt with this more fully
above. This precept, therefore, which commands nothing other than what also
had a place in the state of innocence, cannot be a purely ceremonial precept,
but is moral.

*Consideration 17.* *God blessed and sanctified the Sabbath day.* This must be
carefully noted, for it does not say here that he blessed and sanctified the
seventh day—although he blessed that day too, not insofar as it was seventh
but insofar as it was the Sabbath, as is evident from these words of the
precept—but the Sabbath day insofar as it was the Sabbath day. Therefore, when
God changes the seventh day to the first, this law concerning observance of
the Sabbath, which is God's Sabbath or the sanctified Sabbath, is not changed.
Thus he commands the observance and sanctification of the Sabbath here, not
because it was seventh in order from creation. Rather, he confirmed the
Sabbath of the seventh day because that seventh day was then the Sabbath until
it seemed good to God to change it. But after the seventh day was changed to
the first, while the seventh in number, or one of seven, is always observed,
that first day becomes God's Sabbath. Therefore this Sabbath of God is
immutable and moral, and its reason and nature differ from those of the
seventh day as such.

*Consideration 18.* The Lord *blessed* this Sabbath day and *sanctified* it.
By these two words it is shown not only that God set apart this Sabbath day,
but it is further indicated, not obscurely, that for those who observe it in
holiness it is a day of spiritual blessings, and indeed of temporal blessings
too. It is as though it were said that God wills to bless spiritually all who
observe the Sabbath in holiness and to give increase to their daily works by
his blessing. He blessed this day, or attached a special blessing to it for
the use, edification, and comfort of his own. Those who do not wish the
observance of this day to be moral therefore deprive God's church of this
richer blessing. Do we not need this blessing no less than the Jews? Why not
also the moral means of this blessing?

Primerose objects in the place cited above, section 25: [*God formerly blessed
the Jews not only through observance of the Sabbath of the seventh day, but
also through observance of other Sabbaths and festivals, sacrifices, and so
forth, through which he too is often said to have blessed them.*] *Response.*
We know that all those institutions were means of sanctification according to
that administration. But I do not know where we read that God blessed any
Sabbath besides the Sabbath of the seventh day. 2. It says here not only that
he blessed the Sabbath day, but that he blessed it because he himself rested
on the seventh after the works of six days were completed. But we read no such
thing concerning the sanctification of any ceremonies. No such foundation is
laid beneath their sanctification. This also had a place in

[Printed p. 254 | IA PDF 262 | IA leaf 262 | IA decausadei02brow]

the state of innocence, as we made clear elsewhere. 3. We do not urge this
reason taken by itself, but joined with the preceding ones. At least this is
manifest from it: nothing appears which proves this precept ceremonial.

*Objection 2, section 26.* [*The seventh day was not by its own nature a holy
day or a means of blessing any more than another day, but only with respect to
the religious exercises to be performed on it.*] *Response.* It is true that
the seventh day was not by its own nature holier than the others. It was
nevertheless holier than the others insofar as it was the Sabbath instituted
by God and had a blessing attached to it. It was also a means of blessing to
the Jews, for in the preceding paragraph he himself admits that the Jews would
deprive themselves of the divine blessing if they rejected that day and chose
any other. They would thereby reject the means by which the Lord determined
to confer his blessing on them. Thus this day was not a day of blessing merely
because of the religious exercises performed on it.

*Consideration 19.* Even the position of this precept ought to teach us
something. It is placed in the middle of the Decalogue so that it is, as it
were, intermediate between the precepts of the first and second tables, and
like the *sinew* of all obedience—that is, of piety toward God and justice
toward our neighbor. Thus the obedience to be rendered to the others depends
in no small measure on obedience to and holy observance of this precept.
