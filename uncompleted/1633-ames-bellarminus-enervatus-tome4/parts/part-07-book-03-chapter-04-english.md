### Chapter IV

#### On the Communication of Grace

##### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *The opinion of the Pelagians was that God's grace was prepared
for all men, provided that they themselves were willing to seek and receive
it, and that anyone could, by the powers of free choice and without any other
prevenient grace, desire, invoke, and receive grace itself.*

Protestant reply. Álvarez, disputation 1, recounts the Pelagian opinion much
more distinctly: “The Pelagian heresy had four stages. In the first, it denied
every supernatural help and every inward grace of the supernatural order,
both sufficient and efficacious. In the second stage, it admitted the help of
grace as consisting in outward law and teaching, or even in Christ's example,
so that by grace a man might more easily fulfill what he could do absolutely
by nature. In the third, it admitted inward grace for the ability to act,
namely, an inward illumination of the understanding and an inward awakening
of the stupefied will, so that by it a man might more easily fulfill the
divine commandments. In the fourth, it admitted that this inward grace was
absolutely necessary—not, indeed, for beginning works of virtue, but for
perfecting them.” Cassian too, whom Bellarmine numbers among the Pelagians,
admitted “that God sometimes anticipates the human will, and indeed draws some
who are unwilling and reluctant toward a good will.” *Conferences* 13,
chapters 12, 17, and 18. This, therefore, is the fifth stage of the Pelagian
error.

2. Bellarmine. *Nearly all Catholics teach that sufficient help is not lacking
to any men according to place and time, and yet that without prevenient grace
no one can desire, seek, or receive grace.*

Protestant reply. An ambiguity lies concealed in this expression, “without
prevenient grace.” For Bellarmine himself says in book 1, chapter 2: “Awakening
grace can be called prevenient; operating grace, in turn, is also prevenient;
finally, every prior grace can be called prevenient if it is compared with a
later grace.” In the present words, however, Bellarmine understands only
awakening grace, or sufficient persuasion. The meaning of those Catholics is
therefore this: Sufficient grace is given to all, and yet without sufficient
grace no one can desire, seek, or receive grace. Here, 1. it should be
observed how difficult it is to find some plausible distinction between the
Pelagian and the Catholic-papist opinion. Bellarmine could display none except
in this ridiculous and insensible statement: “Although sufficient grace is
given to all, nevertheless without sufficient grace no one can receive
grace.” For he distinguishes things that are utterly contrary as though they
were merely different: to have grace, and to receive grace without grace. 2.
It should also be observed that the Pelagians never denied universal grace
that was sufficient but not effica-*

[Printed p. 271 | Google Books PA271 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*cious. No one, indeed, raised such a controversy with them, nor is there any
reason why men who were especially natural and carnal should deny that grace.
Rather, as Augustine notes in *Heresies* 88, they were enemies of God's
special and efficacious grace, “by which we are predestined, by which we are
rescued from the power of darkness so that we may believe in Christ and be
transferred into his kingdom, and by which charity is poured into our hearts
so that faith may work through love.” They granted—indeed, contended—that a
sufficient power was given by God, and they did not refuse to call it grace or
to affirm that it was necessary. As yet, therefore, no real difference appears
between this papist opinion and the Pelagian one. We must therefore see
whether and to what extent a difference is found in the eight propositions
which Bellarmine appends by way of explanation.

3. Bellarmine. 1. *The help of grace is not offered to all in such a way that
God waits for men to desire or request it, but it precedes every desire and
every invocation. This proposition is against the Pelagians and the remnants
of the Pelagians.*

Protestant reply. This is a respectable statement, but if it is examined
thoroughly, what Augustine noted about Pelagius may truly be said about
Bellarmine: “Under ambiguous generality he conceals what he thinks.” For 1.
although he does not indicate in these words which help he means—sufficient or
efficacious—it is sufficiently clear from what has already been said that he
affirms this only of the help which he calls sufficient. The Pelagians,
however, did not deny this with respect to persuasion, as may be seen in
Augustine, epistle 107, where the Pelagian opinion of Vitalis is recounted in
these words: “Without God's teaching, the truth to which he may consent by his
own will cannot become known to a man. With persuasion preceding, and his own
consent following, a man follows God's teaching.” Nor, indeed, can anyone
believe or conceive that a sufficient help for willing well requires a good
will as an antecedent condition. 2. Regarding efficacious help, all those who
make the efficacy of grace depend upon the free cooperation of human choice
teach that God waits for a man's free consent before communicating it. Among
these the Jesuits constitute the foremost battle line. Bellarmine taught this
previously in book 1, chapter 11, from the words of Isaiah 5, “he waited for it
to bring forth grapes,” and Revelation 3, “I stand at the door and knock.” Yet
this is the very opinion which the Council of Orange once condemned in the
Pelagians by the words of this proposition: “If anyone contends that God waits
upon our will in order that we may be cleansed from sin, but does not confess
that even our willing to be cleansed comes about through the infusion and
operation of the Holy Spirit, he resists the Holy Spirit himself,” and so
forth.

4. Bellarmine. 2. *The help of God's grace is not given equally to all.*

[Printed p. 272 | Google Books PA272 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. This proposition is indeed acknowledged as true by all
Christians. But if it is understood of sufficient grace, it does not seem
defensible by those who will that it be given to all, unless they wish to call
the grace which is merely sufficient for one man more than sufficient with
respect to another. Whatever the case, this opinion does not distinguish the
papists from the Pelagians, for the latter willed grace to be given according
to merits.

5. Bellarmine. 3. *There would be no injustice in God if he denied sufficient
help for salvation not only to some but even to all men.*

Protestant reply. Although this proposition is most true, it has no bearing
upon explaining the communication of grace—namely, how or to whom it occurs.
We nevertheless accept it as testimony, or as their confession, to oppose to
the clamorous slanders of those who accuse of cruelty the doctrine which
teaches that grace is communicated to the smaller part of mankind.

6. Bellarmine. 4. *Whether sufficient help is given to all or only to some, we
nevertheless ought not to despair of anyone's salvation while he remains in
this life, nor withhold from anyone correction, exhortation, and the other
duties of charity.*

Protestant reply. This proposition also is true, although it does not properly
touch the question proposed. We set it against the madness of those who
prattle that the doctrine of predestination is hostile to such duties of
charity.

7. Bellarmine. 5. *Sufficient help for salvation is given to all, mediately or
immediately, according to place and time. It is given immediately to those
who enjoy the use of reason; to those who do not have the use of reason it is
given mediately, since their parents can procure baptism for them if they
wish.*

Protestant reply. 1. This proposition does not distinguish the papists from
the Pelagians, but together with them it contradicts the apostle in Romans 10:
“How will they believe in him whom they have not heard? And how will they hear
without a preacher?” 2. The assertion about all those who have the use of
reason—namely, that they immediately have grace sufficient for salvation—is
sufficiently refuted by experience itself. For it is evident that many people
in the world have the use of reason who nevertheless have never dreamed of
salvation, grace, faith, or conversion. 3. This mediate gift of grace, placed
in the power of parents, is a contrivance inept in every respect. Not everyone
has living parents; baptism is not known to every parent; not all who know of
baptism can procure it for whomever they wish; and finally, the grace and
salvation of children depend neither upon baptism nor upon the will of their
parents.

Bellarmine. 1. *Psalm 19:7: “There is no one who hides himself from its
heat.”*

Protestant reply. The heat of the sun is not the grace of God.

Bellarmine. 2. *Psalm 25:10: “All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth.”*

Protestant reply. The text adds what Bellarmine improperly conceals: “for
those who keep his covenant and his testimonies.”

Bellarmine. 3. *Proverbs 1:20: The preaching of wisdom is universal.*

Protestant reply. Not every universal preaching is grace sufficient for
salvation.

Bellarmine. 4. *Ezekiel 33:11: “Turn, turn.” This exhortation seems to be a
mockery unless God gave them the powers by which they could turn.*

Protestant reply. That exhortation does not reach everyone who has the use of
reason. Nor does it follow that it would be a mockery unless all whom it
reaches had powers sufficient for conversion, because it declares a common
duty and works efficaciously in some. To perverse reasoning, the exhortation
which God applies, according to Bellarmine, when he sees that it will have no
effect, and does not apply when he sees that it will have an effect, seems
equally a mockery.

Bellarmine. 5. *Matthew 5: He makes his sun rise upon the good and the wicked,
and rains upon the unjust and the just. This should be understood of the sun
of righteousness and the rain of grace.*

Protestant reply. Thus the makers of allegories prove anything from anything.

Bellarmine. 6. *Matthew 11: “Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened.”*

Protestant reply. Not all who have the use of reason labor and are burdened by
a sense of sin.

Bellarmine. 7. *John 1: “He illuminates every man.”*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself answers himself in chapter 3: “This
passage may quite suitably be referred to natural illumination”; or, “The
meaning of the passage is that no one is illuminated except through him.”

Bellarmine. 8. *1 Timothy 2: “He wills all to be saved.”*

Protestant reply. Cajetan answers on the passage: “Here, for the speculative
theologian, the discussion is not about the will of good pleasure but about
the will of the sign.” Bellarmine answers the Pelagians in precisely a similar
way concerning these very words of Bellarmine, chapter 3.

Bellarmine. 9. *1 Timothy 4: “The Savior of all men.”*

Protestant reply. With respect to those evils from which they are saved. “He
saves all with respect to bodily health,” says Thomas on those words.

Bellarmine. 10. *Christ is said to have died for all.* 1 Corinthians 15; 1
Timothy 2; and 1 John 2.

Protestant reply. With respect to efficacy, he died only for the elect from
all nations, tribes, peoples, and languages. Revelation 7:9.

8. Bellarmine. 6. *Although God's help sufficient and necessary for rising
again from sin is lacking to no one according to place and time, it is not,
nevertheless, present at every moment.*

Protestant reply. I. This slippery and sophistical limitation, “according to
place and time,” is explained by Bellarmine in an equally ambiguous phrase in
the title of chapter 6 of this book: “As God judges that his gifts should be
distributed.” If, therefore, it is given only in the first moment in which
someone comes to the use of reason, or in the last moment of his life,
Bellarmine has sufficiently provided for himself, since that is “according to
place and time.” There are other papists who, although they have nothing to
say about “place”—which in truth has no bearing upon the communication of
grace—nevertheless explain “according to time” as the time “of precept and
obligation,” in which a person would sin if he did not rise again from sin.
Becanus, *On Helps*. These men, however, are more trifling in this respect
than Bellarmine himself, except that he too says something of this sort in
chapter 8: “The time is then especially suitable, when they cannot avoid sin
without conversion.” Thus these triflers speak as if it were sometimes lawful
to sleep in one's sins, and as if men were obligated to turn to God only at
certain times. II. This proposition distinguishes the papists from the
Pelagians not so much in reality as in place and time. III. If this sufficient
grace is not always present, Bellarmine was merely playing earlier, in book 1,
chapter 11, when he sought to prove it from these words: “You always resisted
the Holy Spirit.”

9. Bellarmine. 7. *Help sufficient and necessary for avoiding sins is supplied
by the divine kindness to all men and at every time, either immediately or
mediately.*

Protestant reply. I. This proposition indeed seems to sound different from
what the Pelagians said, since it attributes everything to divine help; but
the same opinion is retained with the words changed. For if Pelagius had
thought of any help that is present to every man at every time other than what
he called the possibility of nature and the power of free choice, he would
never have been so insane as to contend, without any necessity, for the powers
of nature in the abstract against a help which is never separated from nature.
Bonaventure therefore observes well, in book 2, distinction 28, *On the Part
of Free Choice*, question 2: “This was what deceived Pelagius: free choice is never
destitute of every grace freely given, and he attributed the ability of grace
to nature itself.” Pelagius's misfortune was that in that age no Catholic
taught that grace for avoiding sins was just as universal as nature itself.
II. If this opinion had long been accepted even among the papists, the
scholastics would not have been so foolish as to dispute anxiously about what
fallen man can perform without grace, because according to this opinion no
man is found anywhere without grace. III. Bellarmine sets forth this assertion
far too con-*

[Printed p. 275 | Google Books PA275 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*fusedly. Durandus, in book 2, distinction 28, maintains that there is one way
of avoiding sins which are against natural precepts and another of avoiding
those which are against supernatural precepts. Most of the scholastics
distinguish between venial and mortal sins, and between the avoidance of all
mortal sins considered distributively and of all considered collectively.
Some also restrict this power of avoiding sins to physical power, because
they do not dare to affirm moral power. These things were not unknown to
Bellarmine, but he judged it safest to keep himself within the obscurity of an
ambiguous phrase. IV. This proposition is so false that it is rejected by
both parties among the papists themselves. Some of them, such as Scotus,
Ockham, and Gabriel, teach that “by its natural powers and without the gift of
grace, free choice can avoid any new mortal sin.” Gabriel Biel, book 2,
distinction 28. Others, however, defend with St. Thomas that “a sinner cannot
avoid all mortal sins unless he has justifying grace by which he may be turned
to God. This proposition,” says Medina on 1–2, question 109, article 8, “is
the Catholic faith, and its contrary belongs to the Pelagian heresy.” The more
moderate Jesuits who write about cases of conscience teach the same thing,
such as Filiucci, treatise 21, number 462: “God did not determine to confer
those helps by which sin might be avoided—at least for a long time—without
habitual grace.”

10. Bellarmine. *That seventh proposition is proved, first, by the testimony
of Scripture, which signifies that no one can cast the blame for his own sin
upon God.*

Protestant reply. I. Nor can the devil cast the blame for his own sin upon
God. Is a sufficient help of divine grace therefore supplied even to him? II.
Bellarmine himself conceded in the third proposition that “there would be no
injustice in God if he denied sufficient help for salvation to all men.” What
greater injustice, I ask, is there if he denies to some a help sufficient for
avoiding sins?

11. Bellarmine. *It is also proved by reason. No one sins in that which he
cannot avoid. Therefore, unless all always had help by which they could avoid
sins, they would not sin when sinning.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine here splendidly defends universal help of grace
when he wills its effect to be that men can sin. Many certainly would have
wished always to have lacked such a help of grace. II. A man sins in that
which he cannot avoid when that inability is contracted by his own fault.

Bellarmine. *Sin intrinsically requires a free will.*

Protestant reply. That intrinsic liberty of nature which is required for sin
is present in every voluntary act which proceeds from deliberation, because
the person who wills something with settled deliberation would will that same
thing

even though by the most absolute power he could will the contrary.
