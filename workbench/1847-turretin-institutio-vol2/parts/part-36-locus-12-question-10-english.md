QUESTION X.

THE STATE OF THE FATHERS UNDER THE OLD TESTAMENT.

*Can the fathers under the Old Testament be said still to have been under the
wrath of God and the curse of the law, and to have remained under the guilt of
sin until the death of Christ; and not to have had ἄφεσιν, full remission of
sins properly so called, but only πάρεσιν? A distinction is necessary.*

I. Just as the preceding question concerning the Surety of the covenant of
grace under the Old Testament arose from the occasion of the present one,
which concerns the state of the fathers under the Old Testament, so its
decision will also contribute not a little to elucidating this question.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. Lest we stray from the state of the question and err on our side, either
*by defect* in excessively diminishing their privileges or *by excess* in
exalting them too highly, as not infrequently happens, the following points
must be observed. 1. The question does not concern the actual procurement of
reconciliation and remission of sins through the payment of the λύτρου,
*ransom*, made in Christ’s death. Both sides agree that this had to occur only
later and did occur under the New Testament in the fullness of time. Rather,
the question concerns the moral application, in the true and full remission
of all the sins of believers, of that payment which was to be rendered at the
time appointed by God, so that the debt could no longer be exacted from them.

III. 2. The question does not concern the power and efficacy of the law
considered in itself with respect to justification. Did remission come to the
fathers through the legal victims considered

[Printed p. 219 | Internet Archive PDF 247 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*in their absolute being*, or *in their legal σχέσει*, *relation*? Everyone
agrees that this could not have occurred because of the law’s ἀδυναμίαν,
*weakness* (Heb. 7:18, 19; 10:1, 2, 3). In this sense it is true that believers
considered in themselves and insofar as they were under the law were under
guilt and could have had a consciousness of sin. Rather, the question concerns
the efficacy of grace and of Christ’s sacrifice shadowed forth by the legal
victims. In the internal administration of the covenant of grace, did they not
obtain full remission of all sins by the power of Christ’s future death?

IV. 3. The question does not concern the degree of the sense of remission of
sins and of the peace and consolation flowing from it. Was it less under the
Old Testament and greater under the New? No one denies this difference of
degree. As the light was more obscure and the measure of the Spirit more
sparingly given, so the sense of remission was less and the consolation
smaller. Indeed, there was also fear of death and a spirit of servitude from
the rigor of the legal administration, as was proved at length above in
Question 7. Rather, given this difference, must the fathers therefore be said
still to have been under the guilt and curse of the law, and not to have had a
true ἄφεσιν of sins, their full remission, but only πάρεσιν, a passing over, in
which they could still be bound to the writing of the bond and to payment of
the debt?

V. 4. The question is not whether they had the remission of sins and the
justification that occurs on account of payment actually rendered and is
joined with complete immunity from the bond, the announcement of righteousness
brought in, and Christ’s death for the blotting out of sin. No one can deny
that this is a privilege proper to believers of the New Testament, in whose
times payment was actually rendered by Christ and the bond that was against us
was blotted out on the cross. Rather, the question is whether only such
justification deserves the name ἄφεσις. Does not the justification granted to
the fathers on account of Christ’s certainly future payment also deserve it?
This is what we maintain.

VI. The question, therefore, comes down to this. Under the Old Testament, were
the fathers’ sins so transferred to Christ the Surety that by the power of the
payment to be made in its own time they obtained the true and full remission
of all their sins? From their sense of it, though not full and perfect, yet
true and real, could they have had a tranquil conscience and enjoyed solid
consolation? Or, notwithstanding Christ’s suretyship, did the fathers still
remain under the curse of the law and the consciousness of sin, so that they
could always be accused of it and payment demanded from them? And if any
remission was granted to them, did it have the character of παρέσεως rather
than ἀφέσεως? We assert the former; the learned men with whom we are dealing
assert the latter.

VII. For just as they wish Christ the Surety to have been only a guarantor and
not an expromissor, as was said before—one who pledged himself for the elect
in such a way that they always remained obligated until payment was
rendered—so they maintain that because of the delay of the expiatory sacrifice,
the fathers were still under guilt and God’s wrath, and remained under the
curse of the law and the consciousness of sin until Christ’s satisfactory
death. They did not have ἄφεσιν, namely, full remission of sins properly so
called, but lived under παρέσει, that is, forbearance, dissimulation, and a
passing over rather than under full remission. Hence they hold that no sin was
properly remitted under the Old Testament; that remission belongs to the New
Testament and is promised as a thing not of the former time but of the later;
and that their justification, because of a certain imputation of sin and the
wrath it produced, possessed only an appearance of παρέσεως.

VIII. Although we do not deny that there is a memorable difference between
the state of believers under the Old and New Testaments, like that between
infant and minor children who are kept under guardians and pedagogues and
treated harshly, and those same children when adult and emancipated, who know
their goods and are admitted to their free use and enjoyment; and although we
also grant

[Printed p. 220 | Internet Archive PDF 248 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

that the law terrified the consciences of sinners with its threats and kept
them under the yoke of servitude, nevertheless we do not think it can truly be
said that the fathers were still under God’s wrath and the guilt of sin, or
that they could not have had a conscience tranquilized by the full remission
of sins.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that the fathers were not under guilt: 1. From Christ’s suretyship;]

IX. First, this is contrary to *Christ’s suretyship*, which entails a true
transfer of sins to him, as was proved in the preceding question, and
consequently the true and complete liberation of the elect from all guilt and
personal obligation. These things mutually follow each other: when he is made
sin for them, they are made the righteousness of God in him; when he is
burdened, they are freed; when sins are cast upon him for his condemnation,
they are taken from them for their justification.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the nature of the covenant of grace;]

X. Second, it is contrary to *the nature of the covenant of grace*, under which
the fathers lived. Since it entails the communion of the covenanters with God
and the communication of all the spiritual goods required for complete
happiness, and thus salvation itself, either it must be said that the fathers
were excluded from it, or, if they were received into it, as cannot be doubted,
they must by that very fact have been freed from the curse of the law and thus
from the curse of God, from the guilt and consciousness of sin, and admitted
into God’s grace and participation in salvation. Otherwise reconciliation
could coexist with wrath, blessing with curse, and the state of justification
with the guilt of sin, which everyone sees to be ἀσύστατον, *inconsistent*.

Nor, if the curse of the law was still sounding in the ears of the ancients,
does it immediately follow that they were still under it. It was set before
them not so that they would remain under it, but so that its threats would
drive them to seek the remedy of grace in Christ. Through the intervention of
his suretyship and the power of his future death, *which profited before it
existed*, as Bernard aptly says, Christ freed them from the curse of the law.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the guilt of sin;]

XI. Third, it is contrary to *the guilt of sin*, under which they maintain the
fathers still remained. Since guilt involves a twofold obligation—either to a
personal penalty to be borne by the sinner himself, or to the penalty owed to
sin in general—if they were under guilt, they were under it with respect to
one of these or to both. But neither can be said. They were not under it with
respect to personal obligation, because that obligation is ἀσύστατος,
*inconsistent*, with the redemption and salvation of believers, which exempts
a person from every personal penalty. Although properly speaking this
obligation was not transferred to the Surety, because it was a personal and
one’s own penalty which cannot be transferred to another, it was nevertheless
so relaxed and dissolved through Christ’s suretyship that it could no longer
obligate believers.

Nor can it be said any more plausibly that they were under guilt with respect
to the obligation to punishment in general, because this was transferred to
the Surety. From it Christ was so obligated to a vicarious payment that their
complete immunity followed from his obligation.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the justification and salvation of the fathers;]

XII. Fourth, it is contrary to *the justification and salvation of the
fathers*. For if they were justified and saved, as both sides confess, how can
they be said still to have been under the guilt and consciousness of sin? That
justification and remission of sins was given to them is clearly proved not
only by God’s luminous promises concerning it granted to them (Exod. 34:7;
Pss. 32:1; 51:3, 4; 65:3; 85:2, 3; 103:3; 130:4; Isa. 1:18; 44:22; Mic. 7:18,
19), but also indisputably demonstrated by the examples of Abraham, David,
Daniel, and others to whom this grace was granted. It does not help here to
have recourse to prophecy, as though the passages in Pss. 32, 51, and 103 were
prophetic and had to be understood of the time and believers of the New
Testament. David plainly applies these things to himself and to the believers
of his own time. No consolation could have come from them

[Printed p. 221 | Internet Archive PDF 249 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to those people if these benefits were to be conferred only many ages later
upon the godly who would live under the New Testament.

It is also added in vain that justification granted to the fathers is not
denied, but only the justification that occurs on account of payment actually
rendered. No one denies that such absolution was reserved for the New
Testament. If nothing else is intended, the entire dispute can easily be
settled. Yet apart from the fact that it is foreign to scriptural usage to
take the word *justification* in that sense, the question will always recur:
Was the remission given to them plenary and properly called ἄφεσις, or did it
have only the appearance of παρέσεως?

[Marginal heading: 5. From justifying faith;]

XIII. Fifth, it is contrary to *the nature of justifying faith*, which the
fathers possessed. Since this chiefly consists in the fiducial apprehension
and application of Christ’s righteousness and his most perfect satisfaction,
whether as already rendered or as infallibly to be rendered, and since it is
followed by peace with God, παῤῥησία, *boldness*, in approaching him,
tranquillity of conscience, and solid consolation in life and death, it cannot
be conceived how one endowed with such faith can be said still to be under the
guilt and consciousness of sin and under the curse of the law. It seems harsh
and little consonant with the grace of God granted to them, in which they so
often glory and exult, to think this of them.

Consider here the examples of Abraham, Jacob, Job, David, Hezekiah, and others,
who repeatedly testify to their trust in God and to the joy and consolation
flowing from it. Indeed, they are also proposed as examples to believers of the
New Testament (Rom. 4:3, 4, 5; Gal. 3:6; Heb. 11:2; 2 Cor. 4:13). Although it
is true that this confidence and consolation was less in them with respect to
degree because of the more rigorous legal administration under which they
lived, it must not be doubted that their faith was the same in kind, together
with the effects inseparable from it.

[Marginal heading: 6. From sanctification;]

XIV. Sixth, it is contrary to *their sanctification*. For if through
sanctification they were freed from the dominion of sin by the power of
Christ’s death operating retroactively, as is evident, they must also have been
freed from its guilt through justification, since the guilt and dominion of
sin mutually follow each other and posit and remove each other. Just as no one
can be freed from guilt through justification without immediately being freed
from sin’s dominion through sanctification, which necessarily follows
justification and cannot be separated from it, so one who is freed from its
dominion must previously have been freed from guilt, which is the cause of
that dominion.

[Marginal heading: 7. From the distinction between παρέσεως and ἀφέσεως.]

XV. Seventh, the distinction alleged between πάρεσιν and ἄφεσιν—as though
πάρεσις existed only under the Old Testament while ἄφεσις is proper to the
New—lacks a solid foundation. 1. It is certain that although these two words
differ in sound and sometimes even possess different meanings, approved
authors nevertheless use them interchangeably when the remission of sins is
under discussion. Budaeus says that Παριέναι means *to indulge, permit, or
pardon*. Hesychius interprets παρίημι as συγχωρῶ and ἀφίημι, and πάρεσιν by
ἄφεσιν and συγχώρησιν.

2. In this matter ἄφεσις is everywhere ascribed to the fathers of the Old
Testament. The verb ἀφιέναι and noun ἄφεσις correspond to the Hebrew verbs
נשא, כפר, סלח and nouns סליחה and כפרה, by which the remission granted to them
is expressed. Thus Ps. 32:1 says, “*Blessed are those* ὧν ἀφέθησαν αἱ
ἀνομίαι, *whose iniquities have been forgiven*”; and v. 5, “καὶ σὺ ἀφῆκας τὴν
ἀσέβειαν τῆς καρδίας μου, *and you forgave the ungodliness of my heart*.”
Likewise Ps. 85:2: “ἀφῆκας τὰς ἀνομίας τῷ λαῷ σου, *you forgave the iniquities
of your people*”; and Isa. 55:7: “ὅτι ἐπὶ πολὺ ἀφήσει τὰς ἁμαρτίας ὑμῶν,
*because he will abundantly forgive your sins*.” Nor can the many passages
that everywhere promise remission of sins to believers be understood
otherwise (Exod. 34:7; Pss. 65:3; 130:4; 103:3; Mic. 7:18, 19).

XVI. 3. If this distinction had a place, neither could John the Baptist,
before the actual expiation made by Christ’s death which ἄφεσις is said to
follow,

[Printed p. 222 | Internet Archive PDF 250 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

have baptized εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν, *for the remission of sins* (Mark 1:4), nor
could Christ properly have said to the paralytic and the sinful woman,
ἀφίενταί σοι αἱ ἁμαρτίαι, *your sins are forgiven you* (Luke 5:20; 7:47), or
commanded the disciples to pray for ἄφεσις of sins, appending its promise
(Matt. 6:12, 14). Nor could Peter correctly have asserted that all the
prophets testify concerning Christ that everyone believing in him would
receive ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν, *remission of sins*, in his name (Acts 10:43).

It is wholly contrary to reason to claim, as the Remonstrants would, that these
things must be understood of New Testament believers who would believe when
Christ had come, and not of those who lived under the Old Testament. Indeed,
it is contrary to the purpose of the prophets who testified concerning Christ.
They said this not only for our benefit but also for the believers of the Old
Testament to whom they wrote, and to whom no less than to us it belonged to
believe in the Messiah and be saved through him. Hence Peter does not speak of
those *who would believe*, but of those *believing*, and indeed of all of them:
πάντα τὸν πιστεύοντα εἰς αὐτόν, *everyone believing in him*.

Finally, neither should Paul have ascribed ἄφεσιν to the justification of the
ancients as he nevertheless does in Rom. 4. There, if anywhere, he should have
taught a distinction between ἄφεσιν and πάρεσιν, if any existed. So far is he
from doing so that he expresses the justification of the ancients and our own
in precisely the same words.

XVII. 4. There must be ἄφεσις where there is plenary remission from every
penal and personal obligation, for remission can be nothing other than pardon
and nonpunishment, so that a person is not summoned to judgment or the penalty
owed exacted from him. It cannot be denied that such remission was given to
the fathers, since they were made participants in salvation. It is objected in
vain that plenary and emphatic remission presupposes payment made. Liberation
from every penalty on account of payment, whether already rendered or still to
be rendered, suffices for remission. Although real expiation cannot occur
except through payment actually rendered, nothing prevents remission, which
is its fruit, from being given to a believer by the power of a payment still
future but regarded before God as made because of its certainty.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XVIII. The passage of Paul that is pressed above all others to establish
πάρεσιν is Rom. 3:25, where he says that “God appointed Christ as a
propitiatory through faith in his blood in order to demonstrate his
righteousness,” διὰ τὴν πάρεσιν τῶν προγεγονότων ἁμαρτιῶν ἐν τῇ ἀνοχῇ
τοῦ Θεοῦ, *on account of the passing over of sins previously committed in the
forbearance of God*. However it is explained, this passage is not properly
drawn into the present argument.

The preposition διὰ can be understood *causally* with some and signify *on
account of*, so that the impulsive cause of this propitiatory appointed by God
and proposed in the gospel is noted, while πάρεσις is referred to the time to
which God’s ἀνοχὴ, *forbearance*, is assigned. Or it can be understood
*finally* with others and have the same force as εἰς, *unto*, so that πάρεσις
is the end for which Christ is proposed as a propitiatory, or διὰ τὴν πάρεσιν
is placed for διὰ τῆς παρέσεως, *through remission*.

If we follow this latter sense, which is more common and more suited to Paul’s
words and purpose, πάρεσις will be the same as ἄφεσις, as though one were to
say *release*. The metaphor is taken from bonds with which sins had, as it
were, bound us under Satan’s power and which had to be loosened through
Christ’s propitiation. Most versions understand it in this way—the French,
Belgic, English, and Italian—and orthodox interpreters have expounded it so
until now, such as Calvin, Martyr, Bullinger, Piscator, Pareus, and others.

The sense will be that God proposed Christ as Propitiator through faith in his
blood in order to declare his righteousness through, or on account of, the
remission of preceding sins, that is, those committed formerly under the Old
Testament in the time of God’s patience. Paul mentions preceding sins
especially, not to exclude present and future sins, but to magnify the power
of Christ’s redemption, which was efficacious not only to expiate present sins
but also preceding ones. For

[Printed p. 223 | Internet Archive PDF 251 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

with respect to present sins this could have been evident enough. But the
question of what had happened to the fathers before Christ’s advent was
difficult and of great importance, and it seemed almost incredible that the
effect of remission existed before its cause and that sins were remitted
before they were expiated. Therefore Paul asserts that Christ’s propitiation
had regard to this. Likewise in Heb. 9:15 he testifies that “Christ was made
the Mediator of the new covenant” so that “through death he might expiate the
transgressions committed under the former testament.” Although Christ is said
to have been proposed as a propitiation for the remission of preceding sins,
it does not follow that they had not yet been remitted to the believers of
that time. Although remission was procured only then, it could already have
been applied before.

XIX. If the former sense is retained, πάρεσις will indeed denote the passing
over and dissimulation of sins whose punishment God did not will to exact
under the Old Testament, but deferred until Christ’s advent, meanwhile
tolerating the sins ἐν τῇ ἀνοχῇ, *in forbearance*, and as if ὑπεριδὼν,
*overlooking them* (Acts 17:30). But in that case it will refer not so much to
the fathers as to Christ the Surety, from whom God did not will to exact the
penalty from the beginning, but left the sins transferred to him unpunished
until the fullness of time.

This πάρεσις nevertheless did not prevent the fathers themselves from then
already rejoicing in ἀφέσει by obtaining full remission of their sins through
the power of the future expiation. Thus the sense will flow suitably enough:
God proposed Christ as a propitiation through faith in his blood to demonstrate
his righteousness on account of the πάρεσιν, the passing over and deferral of
the punishment of preceding sins committed during the time of God’s
forbearance. God deferred their penalty not to leave them altogether
unpunished, but to punish them at the proper time in Christ and to demonstrate
his righteousness against sins, which had not been shown so clearly under
ἀνοχῇ, *forbearance*, while sin was not yet punished.

XX. The promise of remission of sins is ascribed to the days of the New
Testament in Jer. 31:34 in the same way as the other promises of the covenant
are ascribed to them—for example, that “God will be our God” (v. 33), which
was plainly granted to the ancients. It can no more be denied that remission
already existed under the Old Testament because it is promised under the New
than the covenant of grace itself can be denied to have existed because it is
said to be made only in the last days. Therefore, these promises must not be
understood absolutely and simply, as though they had existed in no way, but in
a qualified and comparative sense. They had to be proposed far more clearly
and efficaciously under the New Testament, not with respect to the thing but
to its manner and degree.

For remission under the Old Testament was given through Christ who was to die,
but now it is given through Christ dead, that is, once actual expiation has
been made. 2. Under the Old Testament remission was dispensed through
sacraments and sacrifices that still signified an expiation to be made; but
now under the New Testament, the sacrifices having been abrogated, it is
sealed through sacraments as accomplished. 3. Formerly it was ordinarily given
with less consolation, joy, and certainty than now under the New Testament,
because of the clarity of the gospel, the removal of the legal yoke, and the
power and abundance of the consoling and gladdening Spirit poured out after
Christ was exalted. 4. Formerly it was granted more sparingly and to fewer
people; but now it is dispensed more abundantly and poured out without
distinction upon all of every nation and rank.

Thus far, then, this benefit is correctly promised as proper to the New
Testament. But an argument is wrongly drawn from the distinction of economy
and manner of dispensation to an essential and intrinsic diversity such as is
posited here between πάρεσιν and ἄφεσιν.

XXI. It is one thing to be justified through Christ’s grace from all those
things from which human beings could not be justified through the law, and
another for this justification to be conferred upon us only under the New
Testament. The former is stated in Acts 13:39, but not the latter, because the
opposition is not between the Old Testament broadly taken

[Printed p. 224 | Internet Archive PDF 252 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and the New, but between the law’s external ceremonies and internal communion
with Christ, of which the ancients were participants beyond controversy. Thus
nothing else is intimated than that believers possess the reality in Christ’s
grace whose shadow alone occurred in the external economy of the Mosaic law.
In this manner Paul testifies elsewhere that the ceremonies were the shadow of
things to come, but the body is in Christ (Col. 2:17).

XXII. When Paul says in Heb. 10:18, “where ἄφεσις ἁμαρτιῶν, remission of sins,
is given, there is no longer an offering for sin,” he correctly argues from
the positing of remission of sins to the positing of Christ’s one sacrifice,
which is promised in the covenant of grace and by whose intervention such
remission was acquired, and to the exclusion of another sacrifice of the same
order or a repetition of it. No sacrifice is instituted for any end other
than acquiring remission and it is employed in vain where sin is already
remitted.

But one would wrongly infer from this that remission for sin was not given
under the Old Testament as long as the legal offerings were repeatedly made.
It is evident that remission could not be obtained through such offerings,
and for that reason they were repeated, so that it might be evident from them
that the expiation of sin through them was impossible. Nor could one infer
that remission was not given as long as Christ’s offering had not been made,
because it is certain that its moral efficacy preceded its physical existence.

Further, ἄφεσις can be taken metonymically for its *foundation*, or meritorious
cause, namely, actual expiation through Christ’s blood. Once this was rendered,
the Old Testament sacrifices that shadowed it forth truly had to cease, as did
all other expiatory sacrifices of that kind, which are ἀσύστατα,
*incompatible*, with Christ’s sacrifice.

XXIII. Although payment through Christ was not to be made under the Old
Testament, and thus neither was sin sealed up nor iniquity actually expiated
nor the righteousness of the ages yet brought in, these things nevertheless
detract nothing from the truth and certainty of the justification of believers
of that time. Payment was most certainly to be rendered by the Surety
according to God’s decree, by whose power they could obtain remission of sins,
just as moral causes customarily act before they exist in actuality.

Thus the *actual procurement* of remission of sins is one thing and its
*application* as certainly and infallibly future another. The former occurred
only under the New Testament, but the latter already obtained under the Old
Testament. Christ had to remove sins *meritoriously* through satisfaction, but
God had already removed them *efficaciously* from believers of the Old
Testament through remission.

XXIV. If remission of sins came to no one under the Old Testament through the
Levitical sacrifices considered in their absolute being and under their legal
σχέσει, *relation*, it does not therefore follow that it did not come to the
fathers through the Sacrifice to be offered in its own time, which was
signified and sealed in those sacrifices. Nor, if Paul denies that τελείωσιν,
*perfection*, could be given through the law, which had only a shadow of good
things to come (Heb. 10:1), does he immediately deny that it came to the
fathers who were under the law—not by the power of the law, which was weak
through the flesh (Rom. 8:3), but through Christ’s grace and merit, of which
they too were possessors.

XXV. Although the ceremonial law with its frequently repeated sacrifices was
like a bond which in its legal σχέσει, *relation*, taught what believers were
by their own nature and what sin deserved, inculcating the right of the
violated covenant of works so that in this respect there was an ἀνάμνησις,
*remembrance*, of sins (Heb. 10:3), this nevertheless did not prevent it in
its evangelical σχέσει from shadowing forth to them the future expiation of
their sins in Christ’s blood and sealing justification in him. In this respect
it could serve the fathers who duly observed it in place of a receipt, which
they could use in their own behalf to confirm the account contrary to that
bond. Thus on the one hand it reproached sinners with their guilt and
threatened them with the death they owed in order

[Printed p. 225 | Internet Archive PDF 253 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to strike terror into them; on the other, it shadowed forth the Mediator’s
death and sealed his grace for the consolation of the godly. Therefore the
victims were ἀναμνηστικαὶ, *memorials*, not only of sins but also of the
remission of sins. They reminded them not only that they were sinners and thus
worthy of death from the legal σχέσει, but also that they had remission of
sins in Christ.

XXVI. The *consciousness of sin* of which Paul speaks in Heb. 10:2 cannot be a
consciousness of sin still resting upon the fathers and excluding them from
all communion with God. Christ’s suretyship apprehended by faith prevented
this. Rather, it is a consciousness of sin not yet actually expiated and not
expiable through the legal sacrifices. Those sacrifices were therefore
repeated so that believers might testify that they indeed used them as symbols
instituted by God, but did not have true remission except from Christ’s
suretyship and his future offering, so that through Christ’s grace they might
obtain what the law could not give.

Thus they did not indeed have that tranquillity of conscience which arises
from the λύτρῳ, *ransom*, fully paid by the Surety, nor was such a receipt
given to them as exists in Christ’s resurrection from the dead. Nevertheless,
in Christ’s suretyship received by faith they had as much as was sufficient
for truly tranquilizing the conscience. Through it they could be certain that
all their sins had been erased from their accounts and entered into Christ’s,
because he had taken them upon himself and constituted himself debtor for the
penalty to be borne for them, and indeed in such a way that they were immune
from every personal obligation and the debt could never at any time be exacted
from them.

The conscience certainly cannot rest unless it is actually expiated by a
worthy sacrifice or is certainly to be expiated. For it to rest and enjoy
tranquillity, the divine and ἀμεταμέλητος, *irrevocable*, promise of God’s
grace is sufficient, together with the irrevocable substitution of a
fully-sufficient Surety accepted in God’s judgment, whether the λύτρον remains

XXVII. The actual expiation of sin is not necessary for its remission; it is
enough that it is promised and certainly future. I grant that when the proper
and personal payment of a monetary debt is in question, the debt is not
erased unless payment is rendered. But the case is different with vicarious
satisfaction for a penal debt, in which the suretyship alone, provided it is
infallible, suffices for the liberation of the accused.

XXVIII. It is one thing for sin not yet to have been expiated under the Old
Testament, and another for believers still to have been under its guilt. Sin
indeed still existed, not yet expiated and requiring expiation; nevertheless,
believers were not under its guilt, which had been transferred to Christ.
Therefore, they lived while guilt still existed, but not under guilt resting
upon themselves. By God’s appointment it had been transferred to Christ and
taken upon himself by him, and thus the godly were freed from it. Just as the
guilt no longer touched believers, neither did the curse that follows guilt.
The procurement of liberation from the curse of the law occurred only through
Christ, who redeemed us from the curse of the law by himself becoming κατάρα,
*a curse* (Gal. 3:13), but its fruit and application were already granted to
the fathers by the power of his future death.

XXIX. It is one thing to live under the legal economy, and another to be under
the law as a covenant or to be of the works of the law. The fathers are
correctly said to have been under the legal economy, but they cannot likewise
be said to have been under the law as a covenant from which righteousness and
life were to be sought, or to have been of the works of the law. For all who
are under the law in this way are under the curse (Gal. 3:10), which cannot be
said of believers.

XXX. The time of the New Testament is called “the time of grace and the year
of good pleasure” (Isa. 49:8; 61:2), not absolutely and simply, as though the
fathers had been under God’s wrath, since they lived under the same covenant
of grace and were saved with us by the same grace of Christ, but in a
qualified and comparative sense, by reason

of the clearer, ampler, and more efficacious manifestation of grace. Since the
legal pedagogy, which was more rigorous, has been abrogated and the
ἀβαστάκτῳ, *unbearable*, yoke of ceremonies under which they were oppressed
with respect to the external dispensation has been removed, we experience
God’s grace and benevolence in Christ far more luminously.

XXXI. Since the devil has no power over a person except from sin—whether by
reason of guilt, by accusing him before God; by reason of dominion, by tempting
him and keeping him under the state of sin; or by reason of penalties, by
afflicting and tormenting him according to the order of God’s avenging
justice—it cannot be truly asserted that the fathers were still under the
devil’s power. Through justification they had been freed from the guilt of
sin, through sanctification from its dominion, and through Christ’s redemption
and the power of the covenant of grace from the penalties following it.

Although sin had not yet been abolished, that is, expiated by actual expiation
through Christ, nevertheless with respect to believers it had been abolished
through remission already granted by the power of its transfer to the Surety
and of the satisfaction to be rendered through him. Thus although the devil
could reproach believers with their sin and trouble their consciences with
this scruple, as he still does daily, he could not thereby obtain dominion
over them. They could easily overcome that temptation and repel the accusation
by faith in the Redeemer promised to them and in the grace to be obtained
through him.

This can be gathered from the illustrious example in Zech. 3:1, 2 of Joshua
representing the whole people. While Satan is said to have stood at the right
hand of Joshua as he was being justified in order to oppose him as an accuser,
the Son of God, our Redeemer and true Jehovah, powerfully intercedes for him
and pleads his cause against the unjust Accuser. Indeed, he sharply rebukes
him and teaches that he no longer has any right over Joshua because of God’s
election and redemption, by which he was made like a brand plucked from the
fire not only of Babylonian captivity but also of infernal captivity, of which
the former could have been a type: *“May the Lord Jehovah, who chose Jerusalem,
rebuke you, O Satan! Is this man not a brand rescued from the fire?”*

Thus divine justice was so far from supporting the devil’s power to accuse
the believers of the Old Testament that it deserved the gravest rebuke both
from the Father as Judge and from the Son as Surety. This is demonstrated
excellently by the reverend and celebrated Leidecekerus in the book cited
above. To him we refer the studious reader who desires to attain a deeper
knowledge of this whole controversy and of the other matters pertaining here,
which that most learned man treats individually and solidly.

XXXII. From these things, that we may add no more, it is sufficiently evident
what must be thought concerning the state of the fathers of the Old Testament.
They were sons and therefore participants with us in the
same covenant of grace and all its benefits and salvation, but they were still
minor sons (Gal. 4:2), who did not have full knowledge, sense, or use of their
goods, but were treated harshly like servants under the legal pedagogy. Thus
their state differed from ours not with respect to the thing itself or to the
essence of saving doctrine, which was the same under both, but with respect to
degree and a different mode of dispensation. We think we should rest in this
simplicity, which is abundantly sufficient for salvation, without any need to
weary ourselves further in untangling the various less necessary questions
customarily raised here. They can break the bond of peace and divide the minds
of brothers into contrary parties, with great offense to the godly and harm to
the faith.
