## Against an Assertion of Jakob Andreae

[Printed page 625; PDF page 2; logical scan 649]

### To the Most Illustrious Duke Christoph, Duke of Württemberg, Count of Montbéliard and Teck, etc.

Jakob Andreae's two-day response compels me, most illustrious prince, once
again to trouble Your Highness about the same matter. Such was his readiness
of tongue that, without waiting for the publication of my complete little
book, he answered three of its pages in two days. In doing so he passed over
some arguments of the greatest importance, compounded certain errors of
Brenz, and finally fastened upon me certain things of which I had never
thought. I have resolved to explain these three points briefly, so that at
last the darkness of error may be scattered by the radiance of truth.

The question is what the hypostatic union of the two natures is. Brenz and
Andreae explain it in one way, whereas we define it in another on the
authority, as we judge, of God's Word and the decree of the orthodox Council
of Chalcedon.

The substance of the Brenzian position, then, is this. To say without
qualification that the divine nature is *united* with the human means that it
fills the human nature with its essence and, further, imparts something to it.
Indeed, on this account all human beings are composed in a certain way of
those two natures inseparably united for as long as the composite endures. But
for those two natures to be united *personally* in Christ means that the
assuming nature—the divine—does not merely fill the assumed human nature with
its essence, as it fills other human beings and indeed every creature, and
impart some gifts to it. Rather, it also adorns that nature with all—yes, I say
all—divine gifts, and especially with omnipresence. In short, if we believe
Brenz, the essential union of the divine and human natures is common to Christ
and all human beings, in whom that same divine activity (*energeia*) is also
present, though only to a degree. The personal union, which is proper to Christ
alone, is nothing other than the most perfect activity of the Godhead: namely,
one in which humanity possesses by grace and accidentally whatever the Godhead
possesses by nature and essentially.

Before all the churches I have charged, and still charge, that this doctrine
has been compounded out of the mutually conflicting blasphemies of Nestorius
and Eutyches. It is Nestorian in that, so far as the divine nature is
concerned, Christ is constituted God in no other way than other human beings
are and consequently is plainly dissolved into two hypostases or persons. Its
Eutychian folly consists in supposing that all the properties of the Godhead
have been conferred upon—or, as Brenz also puts it, poured into—the humanity.

[Printed page 626; PDF page 3; logical scan 650]

I demonstrated both charges by the arguments that immediately follow, as well
as by others.

**Argument 1.** God the Father and the Holy Spirit fill all things, yet they
are not united essentially—much less personally—to any object. Therefore, for
the divine nature to unite itself with something, so that the thing may in any
sense be said to consist of the divine nature and another nature proper to
itself, is far different from filling something with its essence. The premise
is proved because, if created things consisted to any degree of the divine
nature, they would to that extent be called consubstantial with God. Yet this
belongs in the case of the one person of Christ, and that only with respect to
the Word, since in Christ alone he is substantially united with the assumed
nature.

Andreae answers this argument as follows: “It is astonishing in Beza,” he
says, “that he censures Brenz for saying that human nature, even outside the
humanity of Christ, is united to human beings, and indeed inseparably united
to some.”

[Marginal reference: Brenz, p. 16 and 20 of the *Recognition*; Andreae, p. 16
and thesis 20 of the *Assertion*.] But I wonder, Andreae, how it happens that
what Brenz repeatedly says about any and every human being—indeed, about every
sort of thing—and what you yourself extend even to stones in this very
*Assertion*, you restrict here to certain people only, namely the saints. Even
in them you plainly confuse PRESENCE with UNION.

Andreae then adds: “Let Beza in turn hear what Christ prayed: ‘The glory,’ he
says, ‘which you gave me, I have given them, that they may be one just as we
are one: I in them, and you in me.’ Does it seem to you, too, that Christ spoke
improperly?”

Certainly not. But what Christ said about the bond of will and love you
ignorantly adapt to a union of substance. Moreover, if what you intended to
prove by this testimony were true—unless perhaps you have forgotten the
question proposed—namely, that sustaining presence is UNION, why would Christ
ask that something be given to his people which belonged already to all
creatures?

**Argument 2.** A demon fills a demoniac with its substance, and with the
greatest efficacy, yet it is not united to him. Therefore, to fill another
thing with one's substance, even efficaciously, is far different from being
united according to nature. Luther himself uses this argument, as I showed in
my little book by quoting his testimony.

Andreae replies that this illustration is quite absurd. “For a demon,” he
says, “does not join itself to a person in such a way as to be united with him
in this manner, but rather so as to afflict and tear him apart.”

But that does not answer the argument. We are asking not *why* it is united,
but *whether* it is united. If your doctrine were true, a different or even
contrary end of the union would not prevent us from rightly saying that the
demon was united to the demoniac according to nature. Luther is therefore far
more correct when he locates the distinction in the thing itself—that is, in
the fact that the devil cannot become a human being. From this it follows that
the natures are not united and consequently that filling something present
with one's substance is far different from being united according to nature
or substance.

Andreae therefore added: “Nevertheless, no intelligent person will deny that
a demon and a human being may in their own way be called one, even if you
decline to call this union either essential or personal. It is in this sense
that Christ said, ‘Did I not choose you twelve? And one is a devil.’”

Let Andreae add, if he pleases, that Peter was one with Satan because the Lord
called him Satan, and that those obstinate Jews were devils because the Lord
said they were of their father the devil. But we are now discussing those whom
a demon fills with its substance, not those whom it governs by its influence.
His reply is therefore inept and bears no relation to the proposed question
about the union of the natures themselves.

**Argument 3.** If the personal union is the conferral of all the properties
of the assuming nature upon the assumed, it will follow that the conferral of
some good things is to a degree a personal union. Consequently Christ, insofar
as he is God, differs from other human beings—and even from all other
creatures—not generically but only by degree. Or, just as God

[Printed page 627; PDF page 4; logical scan 651]

and every created thing are two subsistent entities, so too the man Christ and
the Word acting within him with however much efficacy will be two hypostases.
That doctrine is openly impious, because it is Nestorian. Personal union is
therefore something other than the bestowal of all gifts, whether particular
or universal.

Andreae says nothing in answer to this argument. Although—as I shall explain
in its place—he may appear to recognize in Christ a union other than the
participation of gifts, he immediately relapses wholly into his doctrine of
divine activity. Indeed, if I do not agree with him, he turns this very
argument back against me, claiming that I establish no distinction between
Peter and Christ. But why evade the issue?

If in the person of Christ alone, as we affirm, the Word is substantially
united to the assumed flesh, why seek the distinction between Christ and
others in a perfect or imperfect degree of activity?

But if, as he openly writes in his theses, God's presence in Christ is no
different in substance from the presence of the Godhead in all other
creatures, how will he excuse Nestorianism? For even the accidental conferral
of every property upon the flesh—or, as he himself calls it, their
outpouring—cannot make anyone God by nature, but only by divine grace.

Now among those properties of the Godhead they especially press
omnipresence. It is certain that they stirred up this unhappy controversy for
the sake of this property alone. I therefore thought that something should be
said specifically against it.

Brenz complains everywhere that we dissolve Christ's person unless we
maintain that Christ is everywhere according to both natures. It follows that
he sets down omnipresence as the specific difference of the hypostatic union.
He does add certain others—such as all-wisdom, omniscience,
all-linguistic ability, and things of that sort—all of which he includes under
the name of omnipotence, a fact I neither ignored nor concealed. Yet it is
certain that they labor especially over omnipresence, or ubiquity, so as to
persuade us that Christ's body is eaten with the physical mouth, in, under, or
with the bread, by anyone who partakes. He argues as follows:

> If the divine nature in Christ has united the human nature to itself no more
> closely than to leave it with its majesty in one determinate place while the
> divine nature itself fills the whole universe, how, I ask, does Christ differ
> in this respect from any other human being? Peter's humanity is in only one
> place, while the Godhead by which it is both filled and sustained diffuses
> itself everywhere. And since for this reason Peter and God are two distinct
> hypostases or persons, why would the son of Mary and the Son of God who is in
> the son of Mary not become two persons for the same reason?

Against this plainly worthless argument I nevertheless offered a many-sided
reply, which I have decided to arrange under these heads.

First, I said that we nowhere write that the human nature is abandoned by the
divine. On the contrary, we have always held beyond dispute that the union of
the two natures in Christ is inseparable, even though both do not fill all
things, because the finite and the infinite do not extend equally. There are
two foundations for this reasoning. The first is that the Word does not adhere
to the human nature at some point of itself, as happens with united bodies. Nor
is he bound to the body; rather, he contains it in such a way that he is both
in it and beyond all things without quantity, as Athanasius wrote truly and
learnedly. The second is that, just as things separated in substance can
nevertheless be so near that one is present to the other, so things
substantially united can be such that one is not present to some third thing
to which the other is present. For example, Peter and John ascending together
into the temple are present to one another, yet they do not therefore cease to
be two beings separate in substance. Conversely, what prevents a person's
head from being present to me while the feet that essentially adhere to it are
absent from me, without the head therefore being separated from those feet?

Second, if the union of a composite is necessarily dissolved unless wherever
one part is, the

[Printed page 628; PDF page 5; logical scan 652]

other is there too, it will follow that when Christ's soul was separated from
his body—as happened in his death and burial—the hypostatic union of Christ
was dissolved. Yet that is false, because both the soul and the body always
remained united to the Godhead, although the one was truly absent from the
other for a time.

I therefore concluded that a composite is not dissolved even if the other
part is not also present to everything to which one part is present, on
account of an inequality of location. I showed this in the preceding analogy
of the head and feet. Nature itself supplies another: the body of the sun
adheres to its heaven, yet it is not wherever every part of that heaven is,
since the body of the heaven extends much more widely than the body of the
sun. How much less surprising, then, is this in the union we are discussing,
in which two natures so unequal in their essence are united: one enclosed
within the narrow bounds of a human body, however glorious, and the other as
infinite as infinite infinity itself?

Third, who will grant Brenz his premise that Peter and the Godhead are two
distinct hypostases precisely because Peter's humanity is in one place only,
while the Godhead diffuses itself everywhere? For if that were so, at least
where these adhere they would be one hypostasis, which it is nevertheless
false and ridiculous to suppose. Peter and God, therefore, are not only
distinct hypostases but also hypostases separated in substance, because God is
nowhere substantially united with Peter in the way that in Christ the Word
himself and the assumed flesh coalesce into one person.

[Marginal reference: p. 27.] What does Andreae say to this? Absolutely
nothing, except that he calls those analogies utterly inept because Christ's
humanity is not united to the Word in the way that the body of the sun is
united to the heaven or the feet to the head.

As though we used those analogies to express by them that ineffable union of
the Word and the flesh! Our purpose was instead to demonstrate plainly that,
whenever unequal things are joined together, the substantial union is not
dissolved even if the one is not placed wherever the other is, and hence that
the foundation of Brenz's argument collapses. This, therefore, does not untie
the knot.

Fourth, in those three little pages I also used this argument, which Andreae
nevertheless passed over untouched. Wherever the Word is—and he is
everywhere—the Father and the Holy Spirit are there too, yet the Word alone is
united to the assumed flesh. Therefore, for one thing to be wherever the other
is cannot be the specific difference of the personal union.

**Argument 4.** If all the properties of the Godhead have been poured into the
assumed flesh, and especially omnipresence, which is diametrically opposed to
locality, then the confusion of natures taught by Eutyches must necessarily be
introduced—a confusion which the orthodox church deservedly execrated.

[Marginal reference: p. 11.] Though this argument is confirmed both by the
truth of the matter itself and by the authority of all orthodox writers,
Andreae makes this grand reply:

> “We seek no hiding places,” he says, “but answer plainly, so that the
> doctrine of our churches may be understood by everyone as correctly as
> possible. If the human nature is understood in and of itself and absolutely
> with respect to its essence, we affirm that in its state of self-emptying it
> was physically and naturally circumscribed in one place and was not diffused
> into all places. So too we now teach concerning the glorified human nature in
> Christ that, even in the glory with which the other saints also will be
> adorned, it is not diffused everywhere. We simply retain the article of our
> faith that it is in heaven, although heaven is defined not by place but by
> supreme blessedness, because God will be all in all. Yet we deny that it is
> diffused everywhere or, as our adversaries put it, bodily coextensive with
> the Godhead. We say instead that it is in the state concerning which the
> apostle says that ear has not heard, etc. And in this manner we also say that
> the human nature retains not only its essence but the properties of a
> glorified body, etc.”

Soon afterward he says: “Dr. Beza now has what he asks: namely, not only the
human nature preserved, but also the properties of that nature preserved.”

If this were the truth, how gladly I would agree! But I answer frankly that I
see no light here at all. For what does “WITH RESPECT TO ITS ESSENCE” mean? Or
“NATURALLY”? Or simply “RETAIN the article of faith that it is in heaven,”
while nevertheless not defining heaven by place? Or “retain the PROPERTIES of
a glorified body,” while not explaining what those properties are? Now hear in
turn whether it is we who seek hiding places in our answer.

[Printed page 629; PDF page 6; logical scan 653]

The question is not whether the man Christ is everywhere. Far be it from us to
deny that, since one and the same Christ is God and man. The question is
whether Christ's human nature is everywhere. On the authority of Scripture we
emphatically deny that it now exists anywhere other than above the heavens,
beyond the spaces of this world. Andreae therefore devises a distinction here:
namely, that Christ's human nature, considered in itself, remains finite and
circumscribed even in glory. In this matter I commend his candor, for he
departs somewhat from his teacher by counting locality or circumscription not
among properties that are accidental and appropriate only to infirmity, but
among the body's essential and inseparable properties.

Yet when he invests this one and the same body with such majesty that it is
omnipresent because the Godhead is joined to it, what is he doing except
positing the consequent without the antecedent? He wants that which is in
itself finite and circumscribed nevertheless to enjoy a property of the
infinite nature joined to it. When he says this, beyond saying absurd and
wholly incoherent things, does he not see that he is being swept into the
abyss of Eutyches? Eutyches was censured not only for mixing the creatures
themselves, but also for confusing even incommunicable properties. Yet in my
judgment Eutyches was somewhat more acute, because he correctly saw that
essential properties, since they are inseparable, cannot be communicated
except together with the essence itself. Andreae, however, seems to himself
to have devised a marvelous scheme whereby a nature that remains finite in
itself may nevertheless borrow infinity from elsewhere. But whom will he
finally persuade of these monstrosities, supported by neither the authority of
God's Word nor any plausible argument—indeed, refuted by as many testimonies
of Holy Scripture as confirm the abiding reality of Christ's human nature?

Yet he wished, I suppose, to state the matter as it is. For elsewhere he
writes [marginal reference: p. 13 of the *Assertion*]: “The divine nature is
said to be present essentially, because it is an infinite essence; but the
human nature, which is and perpetually remains a finite essence in itself, is
said to be present personally, because through the personal union it has been
so assumed by the Word that through it and with it he does all things, not
naturally but personally and truly present.”

If we believe him, therefore, PERSONAL and NATURAL are opposites. As soon as
Andreae can distinguish them as he should—that is, as soon as he distinguishes
abstract terms from concrete ones—the controversy will be settled. For what
he says, though it too needs a favorable interpretation which we shall discuss
in its place, must be attributed not to the human nature but to the man. The
human nature in Christ, whether considered in itself or in relation to the
Godhead to which it is personally united, always remains distinguished by its
essential properties and hence finite and circumscribed in a determinate
place. The man, however, does not, because even the name *man* by itself
denotes the whole subsisting entity, who is God and man. The phrase *human
nature* certainly does not. He wants me, I suppose, to speak more crudely.
However one considers the humanity, it cannot itself be the Godhead, either in
essence or in any essential and incommunicable property; otherwise the doctrine
of Eutyches would be true. But it is true that this assumed man is
substantially God the Word, because this assumed man and the Word who is God
are one person. Therefore, what belongs only to the other nature considered
separately is also truly predicated of the person.

Consequently, if Andreae were to write that Christ the man, considered in
himself or insofar as he is man, is now circumscribed in heaven and thus is as
far removed from the earth as possible, but that insofar as he and God the
Word are one and the same person he is everywhere present, he would speak the
truth and we ourselves would agree entirely. For this rule is firm: the parts
of a whole or complete composite are said to be present even when, considered
in themselves and as they actually are, they are otherwise absent. Since
examples of this way of speaking occur everywhere, I hope he will never again
raise a dispute about it.

If I were to cite passages in which, because of this unity of person, human
things are attributed to the divine, he would raise an objection that I shall
discuss later in its place. I shall therefore take one or two examples in
which divine things are attributed to the human.

“Before Abraham came to be,” says the Lord, “I am.” When he speaks in this
way,

[Printed page 630; PDF page 7; logical scan 654]

does Christ divide himself? Certainly not. It is therefore truly said of the
whole Christ—that is, insofar as he is one person—that he existed before
Abraham and indeed from eternity. Although this is not true of the human
nature considered in any way, it is nevertheless true of the man: not in
himself, but insofar as he is now one with the Word, who was God in the
beginning.

In the same way, the Son of Man was in heaven while speaking with Nicodemus on
earth; the human nature, however, was not. How is that so? Because the very
same one who is man according to the flesh is God according to the Spirit.
Consequently, the one who according to the human nature is only on earth is,
according to the divine nature, also the man in heaven.

[Marginal reference: p. 17.] Not unlike this is the distinction among the
properties of the divine nature devised by Brenz and the people of his party:

> “For,” they say, “the properties of the divine nature in Christ, considered
> absolutely and in themselves in that nature, are wholly incommunicable and
> do not signify an accident in a subject. For the Word is not powerful by a
> power or wise by a wisdom; the essence of the Word is itself power and
> wisdom. Therefore, just as humanity cannot become divinity, so a man can
> never become powerful and wise in the manner in which the Word is. Yet those
> properties are called communicable in this sense: not that a creature is
> made God, but that it participates in the divine nature, that is, by reason
> of the gifts which God confers upon creatures through the communication of
> himself. Peter speaks of this when he says that we become partakers of the
> divine nature.”

Andreae recognizes his own words, I suppose. I take their meaning to be that
things which are essential in God are communicated accidentally, and only as
to their efficacy, to creatures. Consequently, the Godhead's properties
remain intact even if all their efficacy is poured into Christ's flesh.

For my part, I grant that what he discusses concerning the essence itself and
participation in it according to efficacy is true, but not without
qualification. First, I say that certain things are essential in God in such a
way that they are imparted to no creature by any means whatsoever. Such are
being from himself, being free of every quantity, being without beginning and
end, and other things of that sort. When I say this, I am not contemplating
degrees in God's essence, as if one thing in him were more or less essential
than another. I mean instead that God wills certain things to be so uniquely
proper to himself that creatures borrow no qualities from them. This does not
hold for all God's attributes, if I may speak in this way, because if God so
confined himself within himself as to irradiate nothing with his light,
nothing at all would exist in the order of things. He therefore willed all
things to borrow from his own Being so that they might exist, and he imparted
various qualities to existing things so that they might be so many patterns of
his perfection. Thus we call some wise, just, and courageous through the
efficacy of that essential wisdom, justice, and courage which exists in God
alone. But let Andreae teach us that any things have been made infinite by
God's infinity.

The second qualification is that, when Christ is under discussion, it is
wrong to take away from the man what does not belong to the human nature. For
although humanity never becomes deity, the man nevertheless has become
substantially God—not in himself, to be sure, but according to another respect
and in relation to the Word, by whom he was assumed into one and the same
person. Consequently, although the assumed flesh is not wise in the way the
Word is wise, what Andreae writes—that this man is not wise as the Word is
wise—is nevertheless utterly false. On the contrary, if we wish to be
Christians, we must acknowledge that this man is that very Word. Thus, although
the humanity did not become the Word, it is united to the Word in such a way
that the Word is one thing and this flesh another, but the Word is not a person
other than this man; conversely, this flesh is one thing and the Word another,
but this man is not a person other than the Word. Unless we confess this, we
must cross into either Nestorius's camp or Eutyches's.

Because Brenz did not understand this distinction between abstract and
concrete, he also argued as follows: “If Christ's divinity does not have the
humanity it assumed joined to it everywhere, it will follow that Christ is God
and man in only one place, but in every other place only God.”

I refuted this argument as follows. I conceded the premise: namely, that
wherever Christ's divinity is—and it is everywhere without quantity—it
inseparably has the humanity joined to it. Nor is that humanity ever without
the Word, in whom it is sustained in the unity of the person. But I denied
that Brenz's desired conclusion follows, namely, that

[Printed page 631; PDF page 8; logical scan 655]

both natures are present everywhere. What follows instead is the firmly
established truth that the whole Christ is everywhere God and man, although
according to the human nature he is now nowhere but above the heavens. What
belongs to the whole considered together, or to the person, does not therefore
apply to his abstract “parts,” so to speak—that is, to the natures.

Following all the ancient orthodox writers, I explained this with an analogy
drawn from a human being, who likewise is composed of two plainly different
natures—soul and body—substantially and personally united. We therefore call
the human being mortal and dead, although that belongs only to one part,
namely, the body. Conversely, we call the person wise and intelligent, although
this is so proper to the soul that it in no way belongs to the body; for a body
is not wise, but belongs to someone wise.

So when speaking about the person of Christ, if we call him wise, we speak
with complete truth, although wisdom does not fall within his body. Likewise,
if we affirm that he lay dead in the tomb, we say something which cannot be
attributed either to his Godhead or to his soul; yet the composite is not
therefore dissolved.

Among other things, however, I said that this is peculiar to the personal
union in Christ: in him two whole or perfect natures coalesced personally,
whereas this is not so in a human being. Neither soul nor body is the human
nature; each is only one part of the human nature. But the Word and an entire
human nature constitute the person of Christ. Thus a human being is called by
the single concrete name *man*, but Christ by two, namely *God* and *man*.
Indeed, because he is one who consists of those two perfect natures, whenever
he is named from only one nature, whether God or man, the whole subsisting
reality of Christ is thereby understood. Consequently, the incommunicable
attributes of the abstract natures are in turn predicated of the whole person,
even though he is named from only the other nature.

Thus we say that the Son of the eternal God was conceived in the Virgin and
later died, because this same and single one who is God is also man. And we say
that this man existed before Abraham because he is man in such a way that he is
the eternal God. Yet no orthodox person would say that Christ's divinity was
conceived in the Virgin or died, or that his humanity existed before Abraham.
Why is this? Because the abstract names *divinity* and *humanity* do not denote
the subsisting person himself, but only the natures of whose union he consists.
Divinity is always one thing and humanity another. It therefore follows that
what belongs to the humanity cannot be attributed to the divinity, nor what
belongs to the divinity to the humanity—a fact Eutyches did not understand.
But what belongs to the man is rightly attributed to God, and what belongs to
God to the man, because one and the same person is God and man. The Word is
never a person other than this man, nor this man a person other than the Word—a
fact Nestorius either did not know or maliciously concealed.

Since all orthodox writers long ago so often impressed these truths upon us,
and our own writers have recently done so with such clarity, who would not be
astonished that Andreae is again shipwrecked upon this unhappy rock? Yet let us
consider his reply.

He turns this very analogy back against me in order to establish his own
definition of the personal union, which we shall discuss in its place.

He then adds:

> “Beza understands that the whole Christ, not half of him, is present, because
> the Word is separated neither from the man Christ nor from us, and is as it
> were an intermediary between the man Christ in heaven and us existing on
> earth”

(an improper expression for “between Christ's human nature and us),

> “which in reality is nothing other than to say that only the Word is
> present, who, although he is of infinite essence, has the humanity joined to
> himself somewhere in heaven”

(I grant this if he takes *the Word* abstractly for Christ's divine nature; but
if concretely, I deny it, for since Christ is one, the man too is present
insofar as he is God),

> “which does nothing with us—where does that lead? In this way he deceives
> others too.”

Fine words, Andreae. You will not find me to be a deceiver. See to it, rather,
that you do not act the slanderer here. For demonstrate, if you can, where I
have said that Christ's humanity—through which we cohere with our Mediator and
from whose fullness flows whatever blessedness we possess—does nothing with
us. If you cannot, I say that you do me a signal injury, though I nevertheless
forgive it from the heart.

Andreae adds [marginal reference: p. 16]:

> “Asked about the whole Christ, Beza answers that the Word is separated
> neither from Christ's flesh nor from us. But the question is not whether

[Printed page 632; PDF page 9; logical scan 656]

> the Word is present, which no one doubts. The question is rather this: since
> the whole Christ is not only the Word but also man, is only the Word present,
> or is the man present too—in other words, the whole Christ according to both
> natures? Here Dr. Beza answers nothing except that the man Christ is present
> because the Word who is present is not separated from Christ's flesh, which
> is in heaven. Is this really to act in good faith?”

In the very words which he drew from my little book I said two things plainly
and clearly. Let him refute them if he can; if not, let him give glory to God
and acquiesce in the truth. The first is that, although the humanity is not
everywhere but in one determinate place, it does not follow that this man is not
everywhere and that his person is consequently divided. For one who says only
one nature is present does not thereby separate it from the nature which is
absent from us. The second is that, although Christ's flesh, carried up to
heaven, is now absent from us who live on earth, nevertheless this same one who
is man is one with the Word who is present to us. Therefore we possess the
whole Christ present—not according to both natures, but according to the
inseparable unity of the person. These are my claims, which he ought first to
have refuted before saying I had spoken so ineptly, as if asked about an onion
I had answered about something else.

To settle these matters once for all, I say there is a wide distinction
between WHOLE and THE WHOLE, and between ANOTHER PERSON and ANOTHER THING,
because the former terms are said of the person and the latter of the natures
constituting the person. Against Eutyches, the Word is one thing and the
assumed man another, because both the natures themselves and their properties
remain unconfused in the personal union. It follows that the Word endowed with
the divine nature is one thing, and the man constituted by a human nature
another. Against what Nestorius believed, however, the Word is not one person
and that assumed man another, because this one person is God and man.

The whole Christ—that is, Christ considered personally—is God; likewise, the
whole Christ is man. But the whole of Christ is not God, nor is the whole of
Christ man.

Thus, for the reason I have often repeated, it is truly said of the whole
subsisting person that the whole Christ is everywhere. But that the whole of
Christ is everywhere—or, as they say, that Christ is everywhere according to
both natures—is something no one but a Eutychian ever asserted.

Andreae adds further [marginal reference: p. 16]:

> “If the man Christ is said to be present for no other reason, why could we
> not for the same reason truly say that Peter too is present to us, since the
> Word is not separated from Peter, wherever Peter may be in heaven? What,
> then, is the distinction between Christ and Peter?”

Here I am compelled, most illustrious prince, to adjure Dr. Andreae by the name
of Christ himself, concerning whom we dispute, to show greater regard both for
himself and for those who depend upon him. “The Word,” he says, “is not
separated from Peter.” If he opposes separation to presence, I concede the
point. But what has this to do with the matter? My argument is not from
presence but from a union of substance. If he opposes separation to union, as
he ought to have done, will he dare deny that, although all things are present
to the Word, the Word nevertheless is not only distinct but entirely separate
from every created thing except that assumed flesh? If I walk next to Andreae
or sit beside him, will he and I therefore not be two beings separated in
substance?

My argument was instead this: the Word and the man assumed by him are one
subsisting reality, and the Word is everywhere. Therefore that man too is
everywhere—not insofar as he is man, but insofar as he is sustained in the
unity of the person in the Word who is present. Let Andreae now see whether
the same can be said of Peter or of anything else. Since it cannot, let him
acknowledge the evident fault in his argument. It cannot be said because the
Word and Peter do not constitute one subsisting reality, but are two entirely
separate hypostases. Andreae should therefore have argued as follows: Peter and
the Word are two separate subsisting realities; consequently, although all
things are present to the Word, it does not follow that wherever the Word is
(and he is everywhere), Peter is there. Andreae now has that distinction
between Peter and Christ which he so earnestly sought. Since it is so
manifest, it is truly astonishing that he was unable for so long to notice it.

**Argument 5.** In the fifth place, I cited the decree of the Council of
Chalcedon, and proved by what I judge the strongest reasons that Brenz had
neither

[Printed page 633; PDF page 10; logical scan 657]

transcribed it in good faith, nor distinguished it appropriately, nor
explained it correctly.

What does Andreae say to this? Certainly nothing except the things I have now
refuted again. Since this is the case, I conclude both that Brenz wrongly
confuses union in general with presence and that he very badly estimates the
hypostatic union not from substance but from the activity and communication
of properties. Thus with Nestorius he divides the person, and with Eutyches he
confuses the properties of the natures.

For this reason Andreae, although he too looks entirely to activity,
nevertheless attempted to explain the mystery of the hypostatic union by an
account different in some manner—if not in reality, at least in its words. We
must examine it as well.

First [marginal reference: p. 6], he attests that he believes the humanity
began to exist and was assumed at the same time. Anyone who denies this surely
denies that Christ was conceived by the Holy Spirit and consequently wanders
from the right faith. So far as I know, however, there is no controversy
between us on this point. The question is what it means to assume
hypostatically, or to unite oneself. There was thus no need at all for this
preface, unless in this way he wished to persuade us that he is no Nestorian,
since he confesses Mary to be *Theotokos*, the God-bearer. We shall soon see
how correctly he explains the name *Theotokos*.

“The question is,” he says [marginal reference: p. 7], “whether this is only a
bare designation by which Christ, insofar as he is man, is also called the Son
of the Most High, or whether some reality underlies the designation on account
of which he is so called. Beza answers that the reason is that the Word made
the human nature his own.”

Andreae says something, but not everything. How much has he passed over here?
I wrote as follows: “That is a personal union in which the assuming nature
makes the nature which it assumes its own, yet in such a way that, with their
properties preserved, they constitute one subsisting reality.”

I then showed how this one kind of union stands opposed both to Nestorian
separation and to Eutychian confusion. Tell me, then, why he seized only one
part of my definition and passed over the other.

Andreae does agree that in the hypostatic union the assumed nature becomes
proper to the assuming one. “But this,” he says, “is to explain what is unknown
through something equally unknown.” Of course—because he knowingly and
deliberately omitted the explanation at precisely the point where it most
needed to be added! What could be clearer than these words: a nature is united
personally when it becomes proper to another in such a way that together with
the other, without any confusion of either essence or properties, it
constitutes one subsisting reality?

Andreae's eventual approval of this formula is welcome. It was drawn by all
the orthodox writers, and by both the Ephesian and Chalcedonian councils, from
Scripture properly explained. It is preferable to those monstrous novelties
of expression with which Brenz's books, Andreae's own theses, and their
defenses teem—provided he does not overthrow the formula with a perverse and
plainly false explanation. His explanation is as follows.

He imagines a twofold glory of the human nature in Christ. By one it is
blessed in itself and excels among all the saints through the communication of
gifts. By the other it is blessed for us, and on account of this Christ is
called Immanuel, that is, God with us: namely, because the Word so united the
human nature to himself that he does nothing without it, but does all things
through it and with it, having assumed it into partnership in the governance
of heaven and earth. If we believe Andreae, this is the entire explanation of
the hypostatic union. To prove it he applies the article concerning Christ's
session at the Father's right hand and everything the Scriptures relate about
the excellence of the incarnate Word. Thus for Andreae, *to sit at the
Father's right hand*, *for the divine and human natures to be hypostatically
united*, and *for the assumed human nature to become proper to the assuming
divine nature* all denote one and the same thing. Yet he is not wholly
consistent: sometimes he wants the Father's right hand to be the Word himself,
and sometimes all the majesty and power which the Word conferred upon the
assumed flesh, so that he does nothing without it and accomplishes all things
through it.

[Printed page 634; PDF page 11; logical scan 658]

This, as I understand it, is his position—certainly no less false than absurd.
First, passing over that futile and indeed false distinction according to
which Christ is blessed either for himself or for us, if what Andreae writes
is true, the thing itself and its effect will be the same. That is no less
ridiculous than if I said that the sun's rising and the illumination of the
world, or the dispelling of night, denote the same thing. Andreae should
remember, therefore, that we ask not what the force and activity of the
hypostatic union is—which we readily confess to be ineffable—but what the
hypostatic union itself is.

Second, who does not see that he is deceiving us when he distinguishes
participation in gifts from this majesty of the flesh? For however he may
finally define that majesty, what else could it be but a gift conferred by the
Godhead upon the assumed human nature?

Third, if this assumed man is God precisely because the Word does nothing
without him but does all things through him, who is so blind as not to see
that this man is constituted merely godlike, or God accidentally, and that
Nestorius is consequently summoned back from the underworld by this doctrine?

Fourth, since Andreae cannot deny that God does almost countless things
through his saints, and that the saints themselves are in their way God's
co-workers, where will he finally tumble with this novel, unheard-of, and
utterly intolerable explanation of the hypostatic union? Surely to the point
where he is compelled to say that, according to the very majesty which he
ascribes to Christ's flesh and by which alone he acknowledges Christ as God,
Christ differs from other people only in degree: God does some things through
other people but all things through this man, and does something without them
but nothing without him.

Fifth, if not from God's Word, then at least from common usage he ought to have
distinguished a thing's property from the use of that property. When we say
that one nature has become proper to another, we are discussing a proper and
real union of the natures and substances themselves, not a communication of
attributes. Thus, just as the Word is said to have made the flesh itself his
own, so the flesh is said to have become proper to the Word. This did not
happen so that God might exist emptied of himself by receiving human
properties, or a man adorned within himself with the whole majesty of God—for
in that event the former would not be God, nor the latter man. Rather, it
happened so that one and the same person might be true God according to the
divine nature and true man according to the human, with both the substances
and their properties remaining unconfused. Andreae's perpetual error lies
here: he transforms the God-man into a divine man.

Sixth, if the hypostatic union must be defined not from the indwelling of the
hypostasis of the Word itself, but from the activity of this indwelling,
Andreae must give two reasons which he will never find. One must explain why
this union is called *hypostatic*, for he knows that the name *hypostasis*
signifies the thing itself which subsists. The other must explain why the one
Christ is called consubstantial with the Father according to the divine nature
and with us according to the human. The term *substance* can neither be
explained differently in the one nature than in the other, nor ought it to be.
Although all things in God exist essentially in reality, Andreae cannot call
Christ's flesh consubstantial with the Father simply because the Word adorns
it with however great a majesty and acts through it and with it to however
great an extent. It follows that Andreae passes over the very thing proper to
the hypostatic union: the concurrence of the substances themselves to
constitute one subsisting reality without any transformation or confusion.

Seventh, if the hypostatic union and the session at the Father's right hand
denoted the same thing, it would always follow that at the very moment the
human nature was assumed, it was placed at the Father's right hand. They
affirm this everywhere, but we demonstrate from Paul and from the reason of
the matter that it is utterly false. “It was necessary,” Paul says, “for Christ
to suffer and so to enter the Father's glory.” If Andreae objects that these
things are said with respect to us, but not with respect to Christ himself,
let him see how little distance separates him from the madness of the
Marcionites. But if he prefers to flee to that fictitious form of a
servant—for we by no means deny the true one—and transform the form of God,
according to which the Son is consubstantial with the Father, into that
participation in all majesty, then I shall agree with him when he has refuted
what I argued in my little book against these inventions from God's Word and
the perpetual consensus of all the orthodox.

[Printed page 635; PDF page 12; logical scan 659]

From these points one can easily understand why we reject this doctrine as
unheard of in God's church, and what an injury Andreae does us when he treats
us as though we denied the supreme majesty and glory of Christ our head and
cast out that consolation-filled article concerning Christ's session at the
Father's right hand. For which party, I ask, strips Christ of his deity: those
who make him only a most divine man, or those who proclaim him to be God
consubstantial with the Father, because one and the same person is
substantially God and man?

Eighth, I ask Andreae to see where his argument carries him. “Just as,” he
says [marginal reference: p. 9], “God the Father created all things not without
the Word but through the Word, so the Word, through the man whom he assumed,
re-created and restored the nature that had fallen in the human race.” What is
the purpose of this? It is to make us approve his explanation of the
hypostatic union: just as the Father made all things with and through the
Word, so the Word does nothing without this human nature, but does everything
with and through it. Yet if this argument is valid, what will prevent him from
saying that the Father too is hypostatically united with the Son? He knows how
false that is.

“But,” he says [marginal reference: p. 9], “the Word did not make the human
nature his own so as merely to possess it or communicate nothing to it. He
made it his own so as to confer all his majesty upon it.” I grant what he says;
indeed, I concede far more. I say that the Word united not only all his
majesty—which is inseparable from him—but himself to that assumed nature, so
that this man is now not merely most divine but truly and substantially God.
In what matter, then, do we disagree? In this: Andreae wants the human nature
to have received in itself the entire majesty of God, which is plainly partly
Nestorian and partly Eutychian. I, however, confess that this human nature in
itself is ineffably exalted above even the angels and every name on account of
the activity of the Godhead united to it. But I confess that this man,
according to the other nature by which he was assumed in such a way that
divinity is one thing and humanity another, yet God is not one person and the
man another, is truly and substantially that only-begotten God from God.

I now come to the analogy of body and soul, which Andreae develops so as to
turn it back against me.

First, when he denies that their union is personal, I say that he is plainly
mistaken. It is true, as I have never concealed, that there is a great
difference between this analogy and Christ's personal union in many respects,
and especially in this: the soul is the form of the body, whereas against
Apollinaris it was rightly determined that the Word is not a form in matter.
Body and soul concur to constitute one human nature; in Christ, however,
divinity and humanity constitute not one nature, as Eutyches supposed, but one
person. Yet this does not prevent the former union from truly being and being
called hypostatic in another respect: in both instances a person is
constituted—Peter, for example, from this body and this soul, and Christ from
the Word and that assumed human nature.

Andreae reasons as follows: “Just as the soul does everything through the body
and nothing without the body, so the Word accomplishes all things through the
assumed nature and does nothing without it. Meanwhile the difference between
the natures remains, together with the properties of each considered in
itself; yet their difference detracts nothing from the essential union.”

First, even if I conceded all this, how does it address the question? We are
asking not what the activity of the hypostatic union is, but what the
hypostatic union itself is: namely, the union in which the things themselves
are joined, without any confusion, to constitute one subsisting reality.

Second, how fittingly does he describe the hypostatic union from that
activity? The Father accomplishes all things through the Son, and nothing was
made by the Father without him. Are the Father and Son therefore
hypostatically united? Suppose a craftsman does nothing without some
particular tool and does everything he does through it. Shall we therefore say
that this tool and

[Printed page 636; PDF page 13; logical scan 660]

the craftsman are hypostatically united? These claims are therefore plainly
futile and unworthy of refutation.

Third, who would not think that Andreae is evading the issue? “The soul,” he
says, “does whatever it does through the body.” Therefore, I reply, it
understands through the body. Shall we therefore say that the body
understands? Certainly not. Yet we call the human being intelligent—not
according to the body, but according to the soul alone. Where, then, is that
entire majesty poured into Christ's flesh really and not merely grammatically,
as Brenz and Andreae say? Where is that form of God, that is, the perfect
equality of substance which they imagine to exist in Christ's very flesh? Why
do they not instead speak with the truth itself in this way? Christ is that
only-begotten God, whole everywhere and present everywhere, yet not according
to the human but according to the divine nature, because he is hypostatically
one and the same God and man in such a way that the divine nature is not the
human, nor the human the divine. So Peter is an intelligent animal, yet not
according to his body but according to his soul, because Peter's body and soul
are hypostatically one and the same Peter in such a way that the soul is not
the body, nor the body in turn the soul. This is assuredly the correct way to
speak about these matters. If they follow it, the controversy will be settled
and we shall all together give glory to God.

Fourth, how can he fail to see where this way of speaking carries him? When
Peter's soul understands and wills something, the operation is one, because
the nature is one. In understanding and willing the soul so uses Peter's body
that the body undergoes but does not act. If the divine nature's operation
through the assumed human nature has the same character, how will Andreae
escape the doctrine of the Eutychians and Monothelites? But if with the
orthodox he confesses that, just as there are two natures in Christ, so there
are two actions of the same Christ, why does he not place the distinction
between Peter and Christ here, instead of in participation in gifts or the
outpouring of all majesty?

The matter stands as follows. In its proper and intrinsic acts, such as
understanding and willing, the soul uses the bodily organs in such a way that
the soul acts while the organs merely undergo; with respect to these actions,
the body is therefore called only the soul's passive instrument. Yet there is
a great difference between this instrument and an axe which a craftsman uses,
for example. The axe is an external and adventitious instrument, but the body
is a proper and conjoined instrument—indeed, this soul and this body are one
hypostasis, which cannot be said of the craftsman and his axe. This is what the
Fathers signified when they said that in Christ's hypostatic union the assumed
nature becomes proper to the assuming one, so that one thing and another exist
in one subsisting reality, but one person and another do not. If this union of
soul and body is compared with the union of the two natures in Christ, it
agrees to this extent: in each case a person is constituted hypostatically.
But there are many differences.

First, to omit the rest, because the Word assumed an entire human nature which
has within itself an intrinsic principle of its own motion, it follows that
the Word moves this assumed human nature in such a way that it also moves
itself by its own intrinsic principle. Thus it is acted upon in such a way that
it also acts. In this consists that supreme voluntary obedience of the assumed
man by which all are constituted righteous, just as one man's voluntary
disobedience introduced sin and death into the world.

Further, the Word assumed that human nature on the condition that, before he
exalted it above every name, it had to be made a curse, so that through its
satisfaction the world's sin might be removed—that is, so that it might first
become the victim for our sins before we became God's righteousness in him.
Therefore, although the Word was united hypostatically—that is, in his own
person—with that man from the moment of his conception, nevertheless, as
regards activity, it was necessary for the Word to insinuate himself into that
nature, as it were, by definite stages. Otherwise the Word would have swallowed
up the flesh's infirmity by his power. That flesh consequently could not have
undergone the punishments due to our sins, and we would have remained under
God's wrath. In reality, therefore, Christ advanced in wisdom, knowledge,
majesty, and glory not insofar as he is God and man, but only according to the
human nature, which was exalted by definite stages above every name.
Consequently, he was not placed at God the Father's right hand until after the
contest. From this, contrary to Andreae's opinion, it follows that the Word did
not immediately do everything he did through the human nature, or immediately

[Printed page 637; PDF page 14; logical scan 661]

exercise in full all his activity in the human nature united to him. Christ
was ignorant according to the human nature of certain things which he could
not be ignorant of according to the divine, since ignorance cannot fall within
God. I truly wonder that this seems more astonishing to Andreae than our claim,
which he cannot deny, that immortality—itself no less proper to the divine
nature than omniscience—was not immediately communicated to the assumed
flesh.

Nor should we pass over this: at the final moment of that emptying, the very
activity communicated to Christ's flesh before his triumph was, as it were,
interrupted. The ancient orthodox Fathers signified this when they said that
the Godhead in Christ rested for a time. Otherwise let Andreae explain this
knot, if he can: how at one and the same moment Christ's body lay dead in the
tomb and nevertheless remained hypostatically united with the Word, who is
life itself.

I shall say something more. I confess that the assumed flesh was ineffably
exalted above all created things. I also confess that, insofar as it is assumed
into the same person, the Word and the assumed flesh have one glory—that is,
the glory of the one person of Christ—and for this reason the whole Christ is
also worshiped by us. Nevertheless, I affirm that the finite was never made
equal to the infinite either in equality or consubstantiality. Paul's statement
stands: “The head of Christ is God.” Christ certainly would not be our
Mediator if according to both natures he were equal to the Father, or if
according to the human nature he were equal to his own divine nature.

What is the purpose of all this? It is to establish three points. First,
Andreae wrongly adapts what he discusses about activity to explain the
distinctive character of the hypostatic union. Second, in his exposition of
this Athanasian analogy he commits serious errors by transferring to activity
what was said about the union of the things themselves. Third, he wrongly
confuses the session at the Father's right hand, or the full communication of
dominion and glory, with the hypostatic union, or incarnation of the Word,
without taking any account of their times.

I now come to another application of the same analogy. “Just as,” he says, “we
say of the whole human being, ‘Wherever the body is, there is the soul,’ and
conversely, ‘Wherever the soul is, there is the body,’ on account of their
essential union, so on account of the personal union we rightly say, ‘Wherever
the Word is, there too is the man assumed into the unity of the person,’ and
conversely, ‘Wherever the man Christ is, there too is the Word who assumed the
man,’ while the property of the human nature remains, by reason of which in
its glory it is said to be in heaven.”

To this I give the answer I repeated a thousand times in my little book: we do
not at all deny that wherever the Word is, there too is the man assumed into
the unity of the person. Consequently the whole Christ is everywhere, if he is
considered personally, that is, insofar as he is one. In what, then, do we
disagree? Andreae wants not only the man Christ to be everywhere—a man who is
also God, not in himself but according to another respect and in the Word
hypostatically united to him. We readily concede this, lest we divide the
person. But he also wants the human nature to be everywhere, which we firmly
deny and execrate as Eutychian. Why? Because that God is indeed the man on
account of the hypostatic union, but that human nature is not divine, nor is
the divine nature human, whether you regard them in themselves or conjointly.
They are not confused; rather, each remaining preserved and unconfused with
its properties, they are united into one subsisting reality.

But, he will say, wherever the soul is, the body is also, and conversely. What
if I deny that wherever the body is, the soul itself is there too? Andreae
knows that there is no lack of philosophers of the highest learning who assign
the soul a determinate seat in such a way that they maintain it is only there,
although its power passes through all the members, just as the unmoving body of
the sun illuminates the scattered regions of the world. But let us allow them
to debate that point.

To grant Andreae what he seeks, namely, that the whole soul exists in each
part of the body, what person of sound judgment will concede the following
inference? Because two things of equal extension are such that wherever one
is, there the other is also, the same follows if unequal things are joined to
one another? Let him therefore demonstrate either that something finite and
circumscribed, whether in

[Printed page 638; PDF page 15; logical scan 662]

itself or joined and united in any manner with anything else, is equal to the
infinite with which it coheres, or let him notice and correct the fault in his
argument.

So much, then, against those things which I have said—and still say—that Brenz
and Andreae drew partly from the pools of Nestorius and partly from those of
Eutyches. If I do them any injury in these charges, most illustrious prince, I
shall not be reluctant to appear before a lawful judgment of the church, not
only to acknowledge all fault but also to undergo the penalty imposed.

It remains now to answer certain things falsely written against me.

“We shall not contend,” Andreae says [marginal reference: p. 7], “whether it is
said that the two natures come together IN one hypostasis or TO one
hypostasis.” What is the purpose of that? I said nothing on this subject. I
said instead that this Brenzian expression—“individual natures come together
in the one person of Christ”—is ambiguous, because *one* can be taken to mean
*only*, and that one should therefore say rather that in Christ the two
natures come together to constitute one hypostasis or person.

From page 7 onward, Andreae treats me as though I had defined the hypostatic
union simply as that union by which the assuming nature makes the assumed
nature its own. He wrongs me by passing over the explanation I added: this
appropriation happens in such a way that the two natures constitute one
subsisting reality.

On pages 15 and 16 he fabricates two claims for me: first, that I say only the
Word is present; second, that I hold Christ's humanity does nothing with us. I
answered these slanders in their place. Meanwhile I attest what I both believe
in my heart and impressed a thousand times in my little book: since one Christ
is God and man, we must necessarily confess that the whole Christ is present
to us. Conversely, because Christ's flesh has been carried away from us into
heaven, we must confess that THE WHOLE OF CHRIST is not present to us. I have
explained in its place how these assertions do not conflict. Let Andreae show
where I wrote that Christ's humanity does nothing with us, or let him cease
slandering his brother. Perhaps he infers it from my saying that the flesh is
now as far removed from us as the highest heavens are from the earth. If so,
let him learn at least from the centurion to reason otherwise, for the
centurion believed in and actually experienced the power of Christ even when
Christ was absent according to the flesh.

On page 18 Andreae says that the communication of the Word's majesty to the
assumed flesh is not verbal or grammatical but real, as though we taught the
contrary. I answer as follows. If he opposes *real* to what is fictitious and
empty, or to that which does not exist in reality, I agree that every
communication of attributes is real, and I utterly execrate all those
Marcionite ravings. Likewise, if Christ is considered personally, or insofar
as he is one subsisting reality, I again profess what I did above; neither
Andreae nor anyone else will prove that we ever taught otherwise. But if the
natures themselves are considered, then I affirm that whenever divine things
are attributed to the human, or human things to the divine, on account of the
unity of the person—as when we say that God died or that Christ's flesh
descended from heaven—each such communication of attributes is not real but
only verbal. The reason is that in reality these properties do not inhere in
the nature to which they are attributed because of the unity of the person.
Anyone who denies this must confess himself a Eutychian.

On page 21, because I censured Brenz's Nestorian expression that THE SON OF GOD
was in THE SON OF MARY, Andreae cannot excuse it. Instead, he complains that I
spoke even more harshly when I said that there are two hypostatic unions in
Christ: one in the body and soul personally united together, and the other in
the Word and the man coming together personally to constitute one Christ. “For
how,” he asks, “will there not be two persons in Christ if you say there are
two personal unions in Christ?” I answer that positing two hypostatic unions is
far different from positing two hypostases. I expressly stated this when I
said that the man—who, considered in himself, is hypostatically composed of
body and soul—is united with the Word by the other hypostatic union, so that in
this way the one Christ is constituted personally. Since I added this
explanation, there is no danger that anyone might suppose that I divide Christ
into two persons. If someone objects that Christ nevertheless seems in this
way to be constituted from two persons and not two natures, I answer

[Printed page 639; PDF page 16; logical scan 663]

that not even this can be inferred from my statement. Not everything that
coalesces in a hypostatic union is itself a hypostasis, unless it subsists
separately and in itself, as happens in all other individuals of human nature.
This certainly does not occur in what the Word assumed, since it never subsists
separately and in itself but always in another—namely, in the hypostasis of
God's Word—through that ineffable union made at the very moment the individual
humanity came to exist. This is why that which would be called a person if it
subsisted separately in itself is in Christ not a person, but is and is called
a human nature subsisting in the person of the Word. Nevertheless, I do not
refuse to correct what was ambiguously written. If only Brenz and Andreae would
follow this example instead of stubbornly defending so many things wrongly
written.

On page 22 Andreae says: “It is also quite absurd that Beza affirms that Paul
calls Christ *capable of suffering*. Who,” he asks, “would dare attribute that
either to the Word or to the soul? Did Christ's soul not suffer when Christ
said, ‘My soul is sorrowful even unto death’?” But I ask Andreae, most
illustrious prince, what it means to slander if this is not slander. My words
were: “We shall also say with Paul that Christ was capable of suffering—indeed,
even dead. And who would dare attribute this either to the Word or to the
soul?” These are my words. Who does not see from them that I restrict the
capacity for suffering here to death? But you, Andreae, should see with what
faith and conscience you expunged the words “INDEED, EVEN DEAD” when citing
me, in order to pervert my meaning. As though I had made so little progress in
the very elements of theology as not to know that Christ endured in his soul
those foremost sufferings for our sins, or were so devoid of common sense as
to believe that a body without a soul could undergo those sufferings.

On the same page he writes: “No less absurd is Beza's addition: ‘We say that
he also knew all things, although this belongs neither to his flesh nor to his
soul’”—that is, Christ's. “Should he therefore not be said to know all things
who for a short time as it were divests himself of his majesty, and in whom
nevertheless ALL—yes, I say ALL—the treasures of knowledge are hidden? Calvin
himself also explains it in this way: when Christ said that he did not know
that day, he looked to the office enjoined upon him by the Father, just as when
he said that it was not his to place these or those at his right hand.
Therefore Dr. Beza wrongly denies all knowledge to the human nature.”

I answer Andreae in the words of Dr. Luther himself, who treated that passage
of Mark 13 in a sermon which I also cited in my little book:

> “They bring forward an ill-timed account here,” he says, “declaring, ‘The
> Son does not know,’ that is, ‘He did not wish to know.’ What need is there
> for these inventions? Christ was true man; therefore, like another mere but
> holy man, he did not at every time think, say, will, and understand all
> things. Certain ignorant people make the man omnipotent in him, mixing the
> two natures and their operations without sufficient care. Just as he did not
> at every time see, hear, and sense all things, so in his heart he did not
> always know all things, but only as much as the Lord led and taught him.”

These are Luther's exact words. Anyone can see that they plainly affirm the
very thing Andreae denies: according to the flesh, before he was glorified,
Christ did not know something—not because he did not wish to know, but because
he was not yet able to know all things, namely, because he willed not to be
able. Let Andreae first settle this with Luther, if he wishes, and then dispute
with me.

As for his objection from Calvin of blessed memory, I wonder with what sense
of shame he makes it. “He looked,” Calvin says, “to the office enjoined upon
him by the Father, just as he had said above that it was not his to place these
or those at his right or left hand.” I acknowledge that Calvin wrote this. But
why does Andreae not give the whole passage? “I understand it in this way too,”
Calvin says: “insofar as he had descended to us as Mediator, until he had
discharged his office, that had not been given him which he later received
from the resurrection.” A little earlier he also wrote:

> “Because many thought this unworthy of Christ, they attempted to soften the
> harshness of the statement with their own invention. Perhaps the malice of
> Arius, who attempted to prove from this that Christ is not true and unique
> God, also drove them to seize upon an escape. According to them, therefore,
> Christ did not know the last day because he did not wish to disclose it to
> others. But since it is plain that an ignorance common with the angels is
> attributed to Christ, we must seek another, more suitable sense.”

And soon afterward:

> “We know that the two natures were so joined in Christ into one person that
> each retained its own property. In particular, the Godhead rested and did
> not exercise itself whenever, for the fulfillment of the Mediator's office,
> it was important for the human nature to operate separately in what belonged
> to it. Therefore, there was nothing absurd in Christ, who knew all things”

(according to the Godhead, of course, or insofar as he was the Word),

[Printed page 640; PDF page 17; logical scan 664]

> “not knowing something according to human understanding. For otherwise he
> could not have been subject to pain and distress and like us.”

These are Calvin's words. It is now apparent, I think, what basis Andreae has
for boasting in his testimony.

On page 23 Andreae denies that it follows from his doctrine either that the
divine nature died or that Christ's humanity existed from eternity. But what
is the point? I did not impute this to him. To prove that something is
sometimes said of the whole or person which in reality belongs only to one
nature—the failure to understand this caused Brenz and his followers to stir
up these controversies—I wrote that Christ is called both eternal and dead,
although one cannot say without blasphemy either that the divine nature died
or that the human nature was eternal. These are the words I wrote, and I do
not believe even Andreae can deny them.

But now I write this plainly. If what they believe is true—namely, that every
property of the Godhead, without any exception whatever, has been conferred or
poured “really,” as they put it, into the assumed humanity—it necessarily
follows that the human nature possesses by gift whatever is proper to the
divine nature from itself. Let them show, therefore, why they except eternity,
and why it is more absurd to say that Christ according to both natures is
without beginning and end than to say that he is everywhere according to both
natures, as they insist throughout. For I say the property of ubiquity is no
less incommunicable than eternity.

As for the other communication of attributes, in which human things are
attributed to divine, Andreae was necessarily stuck; we should consider by
what tunnel he tries to escape. “There is,” he says, “an enormous difference
between saying that God died, ate, or drank, and saying that the man knows all
things and governs while present everywhere. For nothing is added to the
Godhead, but through the union something really was added to the humanity
which is not of its essence.” What then? What can he infer from these now
commonplace mysteries? Surely that the Godhead's properties—which he includes
under the name of supreme majesty—are truly and really attributed to the
humanity because they were really added to it by the personal union; but that
the opposite occurs whenever human things are attributed to the Godhead,
because, as he says, nothing was added to the Godhead.

But if the matter stands as he says, what follows except that there is some
verbal rather than real communication of attributes—the very thing he is
accustomed, whenever he pleases, to deny most vigorously?

I also pass over his highly improper expression “nothing is added to the
Godhead,” where he ought rather to have written “nothing is taken away from
the Godhead,” since the necessity of dying, drinking, and eating, if attributed
to God, signifies a taking away rather than an addition. I pass over this,
since the matter itself shows that Andreae was far too hasty when he wrote his
two-day response against me.

But I cannot pass over his addition: “Thus it is said differently concerning
the same Christ with respect to him as man, both that he existed before
Abraham and that the Word now governs all things through him.” What am I
hearing? These words do not answer the preceding argument. Andreae had
proposed two kinds of predication as different: one in which human things are
attributed to divine, as when God is said to have died; and another in which
divine things are attributed to human, as when the man is said to know all
things and to govern while present. But both forms of speech which he now adds
belong only to the latter kind: eternity before Abraham and installation in
supreme majesty, or being made omnipresent, are both divine things attributed
to Christ's humanity. His excessive eagerness to answer has deceived him
again. It would have been far better to devote a month or two, rather than two
days, to so serious a subject.

Nevertheless, he denies that those two propositions are to be understood in
the same way:

> “For Christ's humanity,” he says, “did not REALLY exist before Abraham, but
> now all things REALLY—REALLY, I say—are and have been subjected to Christ
> even according to his humanity, so that, placed in the highest majesty, he
> truly reigns and governs all things with the Word. If anyone were to say that
> these things are predicated of Christ's humanity in the same way as it is
> said that he existed before Abraham, everyone would pronounce it blasphemy
> and a manifest denial that the man Christ sits at God's right hand. Yet the
> adversaries present them to the less educated without any distinction, and
> they are easily deceived in this way. If this happens through ignorance, it
> must be corrected; if deliberately, no good person can approve it. For it is
> a palpable error to explain through one another things which are said of the
> same subject in entirely different senses.”

[Printed page 641; PDF page 18; logical scan 665]

Is that so? I have said, and without blasphemy still say, that the propositions
“Christ the man existed before Abraham” and “Christ the man, present, governs
all things” are to be explained in one and the same way. On account of the
unity of the person, the usage of Scripture permits not only attributing to
the person what belongs to only one nature—as when Christ is said to have died
and to have existed always—but also verbally interchanging properties of the
individual natures which are in reality incommunicable. All the ancients called
this figure a communication of attributes which is verbal rather than real,
if the nature to which the property is attributed is considered separately.
It is therefore true that Christ the man existed before Abraham, but only
according to the divinity with which this man is endowed. Likewise, it is true
that Christ the man, present, governs all things, but according to the divinity
with which he is now so endowed that he is also glorified together with it.
So too it is true, as Scripture says, that the Lord of glory was crucified and
redeemed the church with his own blood—but according to the humanity alone, or
in the assumed flesh.

Conversely, I say and affirm that the following propositions are alike false,
blasphemous, and wholly foreign to God's church: “Christ according to the
humanity,” or “Christ's humanity, is everywhere present”; and “Christ's
humanity existed before Abraham.” They are false and blasphemous for exactly
the same reason as “The divinity died” and “Christ according to the divinity
exists nowhere but in heaven.” The reason is that the abstract terms
*humanity* and *divinity* signify the natures separately, whose properties in
the hypostatic union are altogether incommunicable both in reality and in
words. But by the concrete term *God* or *Word*, or *man*, the whole subsisting
reality or entire person is denoted; because it is one and inseparable, it
truly admits the communication of attributes.[^p641-abstracto] I affirm these
things, Andreae, to escape the charge of blasphemy, far from fearing that I
will thereby appear a blasphemer or deceiver. May the Lord forgive what you
have unjustly written against us here.

    hominis vocabulo*. The argument and Beza's repeated terminology require
    the contrast “concrete”; this translation makes that sense explicit rather
    than reproducing the apparent printing error.

On pages 25 and 32, and several times elsewhere, Andreae fastens a new and
utterly insane doctrine upon me: that I supposedly think Christ is now present
to us with respect to his humanity in no other way than he was present to
Abraham, at a time when neither his soul nor his body existed in the order of
things. I do not object to judgment being made on this question from the very
words of my little book which he quotes. In sum, I said that on account of the
personal union, which makes the undivided Christ one, the proposition “The
whole Christ, God and man, is everywhere present” is entirely true, just as is
“Christ existed before Abraham,” although what is said collectively of the
whole does not belong to each part. I then added the following, which Andreae
should consider with what good faith he omitted:

> “This consequence would be inept: ‘Christ existed before Abraham; therefore
> both his divine and human natures existed before Abraham.’ We say this
> consequence is no less absurd: ‘Christ is everywhere; therefore both
> Christ's humanity and divinity are everywhere.’”

I wrote this to teach that both propositions should be explained alike,
discussing the form of speech itself, in which something is said of the whole
person that, considered separately, is predicated only with respect to the
other nature, namely the divine. Is it not a signal slander to transfer this to
the subject matter of the assertion itself? If I say that these are similarly
true—“Andreae teaches at Tübingen” and “Beza teaches at Geneva”—will it follow
that I teach at Geneva the same things he teaches at Tübingen?

Not only here but elsewhere Andreae has played the sophist with the same word,
“just as.” In order to evade Augustine's statement, “It does not follow that
what is in God is everywhere as God is,” he wrote that Augustine indeed
believed the humanity to be everywhere, but in another manner than the
divinity. Who does not see the fallacy resting on a single word? To return to
the point, by God's grace I am not so insane that I cannot distinguish what
exists in the order of things from what does not yet exist except in hope. I
care nothing for these disorderly outcries that I supposedly deny that Christ
is man or has come in the flesh—that is, foul slanders manifest, I hope, to
everyone.

Let the same answer be given to what Andreae writes on page 26 and several
times elsewhere: “I wish to advise the godly reader to pay careful attention:
wherever Beza speaks of the whole Christ, he must be understood to mean only
the divinity and a half Christ—God alone, not man.” What impudence is this, I
ask: to say that because I deny Christ is present according to his humanity, I
deny that Christ the man is present? What then? Are the man

[Printed page 642; PDF page 19; logical scan 666]

and the human nature the same? Christ the man is God, which you cannot deny
without dividing Christ; but is Christ's human nature divine? You see the
great difference between these formulas. The concrete name *man* refers to the
person who is one and consists of two natures; but the abstract name
*humanity*, as I have now stressed a thousand times, denotes only the one
nature by itself. Who taught us to speak in this way? The Holy Spirit himself.
The statement “The Lord of glory was crucified” is true, but “The divinity of
glory was crucified” is false and blasphemous. Likewise, “Christ, God and man,
existed before Abraham; the Son of Man was in heaven while speaking with
Nicodemus on earth” is true. But “Christ according to the human and divine
natures existed before Abraham” and “Christ's humanity was in heaven while he
was on earth” are false and blasphemous. Andreae should similarly learn that
it is true to say that the whole Christ, God and man, is everywhere, because
one and the same undivided person is both God and man; but it is a false and
purely Eutychian blasphemy to say that Christ according to both natures is now
present on earth. Let him learn these things both from Scripture and from the
perpetual consensus of all the orthodox, whose countless testimonies we have
cited elsewhere, and finally cease his slander.

On page 28 he thrusts at us the apostle's statement, “In him all the fullness
of the Godhead dwells bodily,” and adds:

> “Did the one who says all the fullness was communicated bodily leave
> anything uncommunicated? Insofar as God dwells in the saints, he communicates
> himself to them, namely, according to the measure of Christ's gift; but in
> Christ there is the fullness of deity. Whatever the Word is and can do,
> without exception, has been communicated through the personal union to the
> assumed man.”

I am ashamed to stress the same points so many times, but necessity demands
it—if only with some profit. When the unity of the person is considered, what
belongs in itself to one part must be common to the whole, unless we wish to
tear one thing into several. For example, we call Peter a mortal rational
being, and truly so, because Peter is one undivided hypostasis for as long as
he is Peter. Yet it is certain that Peter's body is not rational and Peter's
soul is not mortal. In this sense, then, who among us has ever denied—much more
in this ineffable union of the two natures—that nothing whatever in God's
Word, not even the Word himself, is incommunicable to the assumed man? Indeed,
the Word regards whatever belongs to that man as his own and proper to himself,
because he made the very man in whom those things inhere so proper to himself
that the assuming person is not another than the assumed person, although the
thing assumed is one thing and the one by whom it is assumed another.

So far are we from detracting anything from that fullness of deity in the
person of Christ that our chief disagreement with them on this matter is the
opposite: we say that the very substance of the Word, with all its properties,
was personally united to the assumed man, by which Christ differs from all
other human beings. But they openly deny that God's substance dwells in Christ
in any manner different from the way it dwells in other human beings, and thus
transform the union of substance or of deity itself into a communication of
activity. In this way Christ is divided according to Nestorius, since he does
not differ from other human beings according to substance but according to
activity—or, as Nestorius spoke more precisely, according to dignity, being
the most divine of all. But if you consider the natures themselves, then we
say that both the natures and their essential properties are wholly
incommunicable. Since Andreae himself affirms this in his latest writing, he
must now consider how to interpret his statement that whatever the Word is and
has has been personally communicated to that assumed man, lest he disagree
with himself.

His customary distinction is utterly false and empty: that the human nature,
considered in and of itself and absolutely, retains its properties; but that
insofar as it is joined with the Word, it was exalted to supreme majesty—by
which he means especially omnipresence—from the very moment of incarnation.
For however we consider the human nature, we shall never find it adorned with
the essential properties of the Godhead, just as conversely, however you view
the Godhead, you will never find that it was emptied. What we do find is that
the Son of God was emptied and that the man received all power in heaven and
on earth—truths which far be it from us to deny.

In what, then, do they err? In confusing the man and the human nature and
failing to notice that this assumed man is a whole constituted from both,
endowed by substantial union with a twofold nature: one his own, which was
assumed, and the other the divine

[Printed page 643; PDF page 20; logical scan 667]

nature which assumed. Divine properties are therefore attributed to the
assumed man, though they belong not to his human nature itself but to the other
nature in which he subsists hypostatically. This, then, is the fullness of
deity which the apostle rightly proclaims and on account of which Christ is
also our head. It is not we who deprive Christ of this dignity, for we confess
him bodily to be the eternal and true God—not indeed according to the flesh,
but according to the deity—and therefore with Thomas we worship and invoke
him. Those who deprive him of it are precisely those who estimate his deity
from activity in the flesh, whether the flesh is considered separately or
together with the divine nature, so that Christ the man differs from other
human beings only by degree.

On the same page Andreae affirms that by reason of the personal union there is
one action of the Word and the assumed man. Unless a proper interpretation is
added, I say this is false and was deservedly condemned by the Sixth Council.
Although Christ's person is one and therefore Christ is one agent, since
Christ consists of two perfect natures, there must also be two principles of
Christ's actions. Consequently, Andreae again recalls the Monothelite doctrine
from the underworld, unless perhaps he has in view a union of agreement—which,
however, needed to be distinguished from the hypostatic union we are
discussing.

If Andreae, who establishes no action in Christ except a personal one from the
very moment of incarnation, has to explain Christ's statement “Not as I will,
but as you will,” how will he defend that action of his as numerically one, as
the person is numerically one?

On the same page he says: “Beza asks us to show him even one writer who stated
that all the properties of divinity were communicated to the human nature. We
send him to the testimonies of Christ, John, and Paul cited above.” Very well.
What godly person would reject them not only as witnesses but as judges? Let
Andreae's party therefore produce even one testimony from them proving any
communication—much less a universal one—of the natures themselves or their
essential properties, such as they imagine, and we shall readily assent. What
I mean is this: the Word indeed became flesh, and therefore whatever belongs
to the Word belongs substantially to Christ the man, but in such a way that
the divinity did not become humanity—for God is immutable—nor did the humanity
become divinity, whether you regard the deity itself or its properties
properly. It is therefore entirely false and Eutychian when they invent,
against God's Word and the whole orthodox church, an outpouring from the
divinity into the assumed flesh of all-wisdom, omniscience, all-glory, all
majesty, and finally omnipresence. Nor is it an objection that all things are
essential in God. Our writers have abundantly explained elsewhere how certain
things are nevertheless attributes of God.

On the same page Andreae says: “If Beza demonstrates to us from Holy Scripture
that the session at God's right hand signifies anything other than this very
communication of supreme majesty by which the Word communicated the fullness
of all divinity to the man Christ, we are prepared to recant before Christ's
universal church and publicly retract the error, if there is one.”

I make three replies. First, there are as many testimonies of Scripture
condemning their doctrine—which locates the fullness of Christ's deity not in
substantial union with the Word but in the Word's full and perfect activity—as
there are testimonies affirming that Christ is God. That error is Nestorian
and in some measure Eutychian. Second, the term *communication* is far too weak
in comparison with *union*. Third, we by no means deny that the session at the
Father's right hand signifies the full glorification in heaven of Christ's
assumed flesh, than which God could grant no greater to any creature. But
Andreae cannot say that this supreme degree of glory was communicated to
Christ's flesh from the moment of incarnation, or that Christ was exalted to
the Father's right hand from the beginning, without denying in Christ not only
death, but also mortality and indeed his entire emptying.

The dispensation Andreae imagines does not help him here at all. Before his
ascension into heaven, Christ was either really like us in all things except
sin, or he was so only in appearance—not in himself, but only with respect to
our senses. If Andreae affirmed the latter he would be an outright Marcionite,
which I do not suppose. But if the former is true, because Christ first had to
suffer and so enter the Father's glory, Andreae must either prove that the
flesh had been given all majesty and placed at

[Printed page 644; PDF page 21; logical scan 668]

the Father's right hand while it was nevertheless in reality in disgrace and
death, or confess with us that Christ's flesh was not placed at the Father's
right hand until, having finished with every infirmity and been carried above
all the heavens, it became in reality what it now is.

It follows that the hypostatic union is wrongly confused with the session at
the Father's right hand, since the latter followed the former much later. To
prove this I employ all the passages in which that session at the Father's
right hand is explained in Holy Scripture.

We must also consider that, although the glory of Christ's person is one and
the same, this does not prevent the divinity from possessing its own dignity
and excellence. Therefore that omnipresent majesty supposedly poured into the
flesh cannot stand. Paul's assertion remains: God is the head of Christ—even
with respect to the assumed humanity after glorification. And it is written
that God, who subjected all things to Christ, is excepted. The Fathers
therefore rightly said that Christ according to the humanity is less than
himself insofar as he is God, and in a certain manner subjected to himself.
Much less is Christ, even when glorified or insofar as he is exalted to the
Father's right hand, made equal to God according to the flesh in equality,
consubstantiality, activity, or any other respect. That would be plainly
Eutychian.

Finally, if Andreae demonstrates to us from God's Word or from any orthodox
writer whatever that the church of God ever received the doctrine that the
session at the right hand signifies Christ's omnipresence according to the
flesh, or that Christ according to the flesh now exists anywhere but in the
heavens—I mean not allegorical or portable heavens, but those which signify
the regions beyond the world, whatever and of whatever sort they finally are,
which we shall understand in due time—then we are prepared to recant and will
in reality do what we say.

I shall say something still more, most illustrious prince. If God's Word and
the perpetual consensus of all Greek and Latin writers do not establish that
this entire doctrine—that Christ is everywhere according to both natures—is
purely Nestorian and Eutychian and execrable in every way, I shall not refuse
whatever equitable judges decide. Only let those who ought to assemble come
together; let the churches be summoned; let the equity of princes restrain
the obstinacy of certain pseudo-theologians; let God's Word be heard; let the
authority of two universal councils, whose fidelity is beyond suspicion, be
joined to it; and at last let the truth prevail. If this request is fair, why
is it not granted? If unfair, why is some better means of ecclesiastical
concord not disclosed? For my part, before God and the angels I clear my
conscience.
