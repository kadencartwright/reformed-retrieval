Verse 17. A lover of pleasure will be a man of poverty; and a lover of wine and oil will not be rich.

In מחסור (*egestatis*, poverty) there is a metonymy of the adjective. In שמן (*oleum*, oil) there is a synecdoche of the part, namely oil for ointment, of which oil is the principal part, and for richer and more delicate foods, as at Neh. 8.10, just as wine is taken for every delicate drink and fatness for every delicate food.

The purpose is to draw people away from luxury and exhort them to temperance.

In this Proverb there are two things. The former is a dissuasion from a more profuse use of bodily pleasures; the other is the argument upon which this dissuasion rests and depends. In the former there are two things: dissuasion in general, and then a descent to certain species.

In the former we are forbidden to love pleasure. 1 John 2.5, 6, 7 exhorts us not to love the world or the things which are in the world, because if anyone loves the world, the love of God is not in him. After enumerating the parts, he declares what things are in the world and which cannot coexist with the love of God. Among these the first of all is the desire of the flesh, from which the Prophet also by this Proverb

[1632 witness | PDF p. 472 | printed columns 928-929]

dissuades us. For feasts and symposia, or frequently holding revelries, are among the things which the flesh desires. From these testimonies of the Prophet and the Apostle, therefore, it is evident what is to be determined concerning those who do not hesitate to profess of themselves with a loud voice and supreme confidence that they are lovers of pleasure. For what else is this than openly to profess that they do not love God? What could be said more unworthy of a Christian man than this?

What, then, you will say? Is it not lawful for me to rejoice? Am I not permitted sometimes to laugh, to hold a festival, and to furnish a feast or banquet? If this were denied, you would drive everyone away from the Christian profession. I do not deny that you may; but I deny that it is lawful to love these things, set one’s heart upon them, and hold them among one’s delights. We read that it is reckoned to the praise of Job’s sons that they invited one another with their sisters to banquets. But they did this not because they delighted themselves at banquets, but because they nourished and confirmed mutual concord among themselves, so that they might render themselves readier and more cheerful for the worship of God and the discharge of the duties of this present life. The Apostle also sets a limit to this joy: that we should so rejoice as though we did not rejoice, 1 Cor. 7.30; that is, we should not spend much time in pleasure, as appears from the reason set before it, because the time is contracted. Therefore, to prolong midday banquets or symposia until night, and nighttime ones until midnight, is to violate the Apostle’s command. Next, to do this continually and every day is beyond duty. See Luke 16.19. Therefore the banquet which Ahasuerus furnished for his nobles for 180 days is set forth as a kind of monstrosity. See other sayings of Epicurus, Eccles. 9.6, 7, 8.

Then he descends to particulars, where by the synecdoche of wine and ointment, which are employed at sumptuous banquets, he understands all the other delights of foods and musical instruments which are procured to cheer the spirits. He makes especial mention of wine and ointment, however, because there is a singular power in wine to cheer the spirits and in the application of ointment to make the face shining, Ps. 104. A more liberal use of wine, therefore, is prohibited even to those whose ordinary drink was wine weakened with water. How much more to those for whom it ought to serve as medicine rather than as drink! Let us therefore examine ourselves and test ourselves by this rule: whether wine is among our loves.

Then follows the argument upon which this dissuasion rests, from its accompanying disadvantage—namely, the utter poverty by which these drunkards will be oppressed, to such a degree that if the person who loves wine is poor, he will never be rich; but if rich, he will be reduced to poverty. From this it must also follow that at last he will be deprived of the wine and delicacies in which he delights so greatly, and that in old age or sickness, when their use will be most necessary. His desire for wine will bring him more sorrow and torment than he ever received pleasure from its abundant use. Thus it will come about that the love of pleasure and wine exhausts the fountain of pleasure itself. If love of wine rendered them poor where it grew everywhere and its price was low, how much more will it reduce us to poverty on this island, where it is sold so dearly?

Consider your ways, therefore: whether poverty has invaded you and this island of yours. See also whether, in so fertile a land which you enjoy from God, this is not one of the chief causes—that, as lovers of wine, you are excessively addicted to drinking it.

But a far more grievous and perilous poverty clings to them than that which is expounded by Solomon, the one marked at 1 John 2—namely, that lovers of wine hate God, which is the most dreadful of all evils.

Verse 18. The wicked man is a ransom for the righteous, and the treacherous man in place of the upright.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 473 | printed columns 930-931]

The purpose is to show God’s care for the godly while the ungodly are neglected. See 11.8.

From Mal. 3.14, 15 it is evident that there were then people in the Church who did not hesitate to say openly that it was vain to serve God. “For what profit is there,” they say, “when we have kept his observance and have walked clothed in black from fear of Jehovah of hosts? Indeed, now we pronounce the proud blessed. For those who practice wickedness are built up, and those who test God are delivered.” In this unhappy age of ours there are far more people who scatter these ἀθέας, godless, voices into the air; there are more who nourish this monster suppressed within. Indeed, the godly are sometimes exercised by this temptation, as is evident from Ps. 73. Hence at Ps. 44.13 there stands the Church’s complaint that God sold her into the hands of enemies without accepting any money and without any price.

Since this opinion sits deeply in the minds of people of every kind, this error chiefly turns upon the things which concern this life. For the opinion that after this life it will be well with the godly and ill with the ungodly is almost always fixed in both the godly and the ungodly; but that God undertakes such great care for the godly in this life seems scarcely probable when the prosperity of the wicked and the frequent tossings of the godly are considered. Therefore, in order that Solomon might meet this error, he teaches that God, disregarding the life of the wicked, undertakes singular care for the godly. And since their tears are carefully gathered by him and stored in a bottle, Ps. 56.9, so that he will exact penalties from those who struck them out, and since he even has the hairs of their heads numbered, so that not one falls without his singular providence, who doubts that God takes care of their life?

The truth of this Proverb is then established not only when some one wicked man is substituted in place of a godly man in danger, as above at chapter 11, but also when, after due punishment has been taken upon the wicked by the Magistrate, some common calamity raging among the people is appeased. See 2 Sam. 21; Josh. 7.20; Jonah 1.12.

When the godly are in danger, it often comes into their minds that no way of escape lies open to them because they lack the money and price by which they might free themselves from evils, seeing that they are either poor or worn down and exhausted by their calamities. Therefore, to break this temptation, let what is said in this passage come to mind: that the wicked, of whom there is a great multitude, are to be substituted and put in their place.

The source of this benefit, however, as of God’s other benefits toward us, is no merit of ours, but that Christ, God’s most righteous Son, a λυτρὸν, ransom, in our place, having been delivered for us to a most unjust death, made us righteous.

The godly and the ungodly are described together by two separate epithets opposed to one another. For just as the wicked man is opposed to the righteous, so the treacherous man is opposed to the upright. From this it is clear that the wicked and criminal are the same as the faithless: they keep their faith and promise neither to God nor to people; whereas, on the contrary, those who are godly keep faith with people as they do with God. This deservedly reproves the sloth and ignorance of very many who hope that people will be faithful to them when they persuade themselves that not even a crumb of piety is in those people. See 1 Kings 22.30. Hence David professes that he will admit no one but the faithful into his house as members of his household, Ps. 101.6.
