CHAPTER V

On the Dignity and Power of Bishops in the English Church

Tilenus says that Sosthenes, Silvanus, Timothy, Titus, Tychicus, Crescens,
Luke, John Mark, and any others of that kind held the very same episcopate
which the English have retained from that genuine antiquity. Furthermore,
*Luther was no less hostile to the Roman pontiff than Beza. Yet although he
left no stone unmoved in disturbing the tower of Babylon, he raised no
dispute with anyone over the rank held by legitimate bishops such as England
has. Having discovered that the institution could not be abolished without
ruin to the church, he did the one thing he could: he abolished the name and,
after erasing the purer Greek word, substituted a rather un-Latin one. In
place of bishops he called them superintendents, and in place of archbishops,
general superintendents. Even those who, throughout Germany, Sarmatia, and
neighboring regions, have shaken off the pontiff’s yoke without approving all
of Luther’s teachings do not disdain these names.* In the preceding chapter
we proved that Timothy, Titus, and any others of that kind were evangelists,
not ordinary pastors. Not even the apostles themselves, much less the
evangelists, held an episcopate such as the English prelates hold. Where
churches had been established, they governed the churches together with the
presbyters. By apostolic authority they alone delivered to the churches the
things the Spirit revealed, but they did not judge alone in the external
forum. Just as the high priest alone announced the will of God revealed
through Urim and Thummim, yet did not judge alone in the external forum, but
in the Sanhedrin and together with it, so too among the ancients after the
time of the apostles bishops did not preside over the churches alone. Every
approved elder also presided, and every church had a senate of presbyters,
without whose counsel nothing was done, as we showed in the preceding
chapter; the hierarchists themselves cannot deny it. The English bishop, by
contrast, excludes the presbyters and spurns the counsel, aid, and fellowship
of the ecclesiastical senate. He has claimed for himself and his chancellors,
officials, and archdeacons—who cling to the bishop as a shadow to its
body—election, deposition, excommunication, the collation of benefices,
visitation, and all the church’s other rights, so that he alone may lord it
over the Lord’s inheritance by a singular power; and, wandering beyond the
sphere of ecclesiastical jurisdiction, he entangles himself in the affairs of
the commonwealth and kingdom. Therefore, he does not hold the same episcopate
as those ancient bishops, much less the one held by those extraordinary
ministers of the truly primitive church, the apostles and evangelists—for
that golden age recognized no episcopate other than the indefinite and
extraordinary ministry of the apostles and

evangelists, if it is to be called an episcopate, or the defined and ordinary
ministry of presbyters—but the very one that they have held under Antichrist
for the last five hundred years. If anyone travels through Italy, Gaul, and
Spain, he will find no other form of episcopal government than that found in
England, except that, in place of the subordination to the Roman pontiff found
among them, in the English church they have erected a new papacy, enriched
with the spoils of the Roman pontiff, to which the bishops are subordinated.
By that subordination they have also become more powerful than they ever were
under the Roman pope. Apart from that subordination, they are in every respect
like the Italian, Gallic, and Spanish bishops: the same form of government,
the same consistories and external courts, the same disputes and cases, the
same clamor of trials, the same taskmasters—chancellors, officials,
archdeacons, vicars-general—and the same whole foul cesspool found in the
courts of the pontiffs. The English episcopate, therefore, is neither divine,
which the apostles instituted, nor human, which the ancients introduced, but
satanic, which the fully grown Antichrist—the son of perdition, Satan’s most
wicked instrument—and Antichrist’s followers exalted to the ruin of the
church and the devastation of the Lord’s vineyard. Luther did not doubt that
by divine law bishop and presbyter had been the same. Having recited Jerome’s
opinion somewhere, he has these words: *Jerome not only makes bishops equal
among themselves but also compares presbyters with bishops, declaring that
one is the name of age and the other of office.* Again: *The apostle Paul
defined bishops in Acts 20 by attending to the flock and governing the church
of God. “Take heed,” he says, “to yourselves and to the whole flock, in which
the Holy Spirit has placed you as bishops,”* etc., *although he was speaking
to presbyters, as blessed Jerome openly gathers from the text.* And in 1 Peter
5 he showed that bishops were none other than presbyters. Let us also hear
other theologians of the German churches. In the Smalcald Articles on the
Primacy and Power of the Pope and on the Power and Jurisdiction of Bishops,
presented to the princes at the assembly held at Schmalkalden, the same Jerome
is introduced, and from him it is gathered *that the distinct ranks of
bishops and presbyters, or pastors, were established by human authority alone.
The matter itself says as much, because the office and commission are plainly
the same, and ordination alone afterward made the distinction between bishops
and pastors. For afterward it was instituted that one bishop should ordain
ministers of the word in several churches. But because by divine law there is
no distinction between a bishop and a pastor, there is no doubt that the
ordination of suitable ministers performed by a pastor in his own church is
valid and approved by divine law,* etc. *Thus far we have spoken about
ordination, which alone, as Jerome says, distinguished bishops from
presbyters. Therefore there is no need for any discussion of the bishops’
other offices, unless perhaps it were necessary to speak of confirmation, the
baptism of bells, and similar impostures, which are nearly the only things
bishops have retained. But something remains to be said about jurisdiction.*

*It is established that the common jurisdiction of excommunicating those
guilty of manifest crimes belongs to all pastors, and that bishops have
tyrannically drawn it to themselves alone in order shamefully to fill their
purses. For officials have exercised their intolerable license and, from
avarice or other desires, have afflicted people excessively and
excommunicated them without a lawful and just hearing. What tyranny is this,
by which an official in some city has power, according to his own depraved
inclination and without a just hearing, to condemn, harass, coerce, and
excommunicate people? Since, therefore, bishops tyrannically transferred this
jurisdiction to themselves alone and have shamefully abused it, and since,
moreover, there are sufficient reasons why they should not be obeyed, it is
certainly permissible to take this jurisdiction, stolen and seized by force,
away from them again; to restore it to the pastors to whom it belongs by
Christ’s command; and to see that it is lawfully exercised for the amendment
of life and the manifestation of God’s glory. There remains jurisdiction in
those cases which by pontifical, or canon, law belong to the ecclesiastical
forum, especially matrimonial cases. Bishops seized this jurisdiction too by
human law alone, and by one not very ancient, since it appears from
Justinian’s Code and Novels that matrimonial cases were to be adjudicated by
the civil magistrate, who certainly ought to adjudicate them, especially if
the bishops pronounce unjustly or are negligent, as the canons also testify.*
Here with every word they depict the English bishop in their own colors and
sting him. For the English episcopate is of the very kind they condemn among
the German papists. These articles were approved by Johannes Bugenhagen,
Urbanus Rhegius, Georg Spalatin, Andreas Osiander, Martin Bucer, Philipp
Melanchthon, Paul Fagius, Johannes Aepinus, Johannes Fontanus, Johannes Brenz,
Anton Corvinus, Dionysius Melander, and others whose names the reader can see
in Osiander’s Century 16. I could cite a great many testimonies of other
Germans to the same effect, but they are readily available to everyone. But,
says Saravia in the prologue before his treatise on ranks, the German churches
*retained the thing but changed the names: in place of bishops they created
superintendents, and in place of archbishops, general or provincial
superintendents, as though this controversy concerned names and not the thing
itself.* So also says Tilenus, as we have just heard, who can understand
nothing on this subject beyond Saravia. Therefore, you will say, the German
churches do not lack bishops and archbishops in actual fact, but after
exchanging good Greek names for bad Latin ones they call them superintendents
and general superintendents. I would certainly be astonished if they were so
foolish as merely to exchange Greek words for Latin ones while retaining the
thing itself uninjured and intact. What Augustine writes is indeed true: the
episcopate is the name of a work, not of an honor. *For the word is Greek and
derived from the fact that the one who is placed over others superintends
those over whom he is placed, namely by taking care of them: ἐπὶ means
“over,” and σκοπὸς means “attention.” Therefore ἐπισκοπεῖν, if*

[Marginalia: *De civit. Dei* lib. 19. c. 19.]

*we wish, we can say in Latin, “to superintend.”* These words were
inserted into Gratian’s Decree. If you consider the primary meaning of the
words, it is true that superintendents can be called bishops. Thus all parish
priests who have care of souls can be called bishops, as they are so called
in Scripture. Thus the aediles Ceriales, who had charge of bread and other
goods for sale which supplied the people of cities with their daily
sustenance, were called bishops. Homer calls Hector the bishop of the city.
Among the Athenians young men from their eighteenth through their twentieth
year were compelled to survey neighboring regions with their eyes so that,
when necessity arose, they would be acquainted with the regions, roads, and
places; and they were called ἐπίσκοποι τῆς χώρας, inspectors of the region.
Cicero says in book 7 of his letters to Atticus that Pompey appointed him
bishop of all Campania. Ezekiel, who in chapter 33 is called *tsophe*, a
watchman, can be called a bishop. See more on p. 182. All who preside in any
manner can be called ἐπίσκοποι, ἔφοροι, superintendents, overseers, and
inspectors. Everard therefore rightly says that an argument drawn from a
word’s etymology is not sound when the word has an excess of meaning, as when
its meaning exceeds the thing signified and can be shared with other things:
*bishops,* he says, *are so called as if they were superintendents, but there
are also other superintendents.* As for the things themselves, the offices of
archbishops and English bishops are not the same as those of the
superintendents of the German churches. Among those who approved the
Smalcald Articles discussed above were certain superintendents: Urbanus
Rhegius, superintendent of the churches in the Duchy of Lüneburg; Aepinus of
Hamburg; and Fontanus, superintendent of Lower Hesse. Saravia admits that
superintendents differ from English bishops. *The form of ecclesiastical
government of the English church differs in some things from theirs*—that is,
from the Germans’. He should have said “in many things,” and that its form of
government is entirely different. Schultingius says in his *Hierarchical
Anacrisis*: *Although all the Lutherans and even some Calvinists, such as
Zepper, pastor at Herborn, in his books on polity, and others, establish
superintendents, they nevertheless disagree so greatly among themselves that
no one is willing to yield to another or follow another’s opinion, and
therefore they cannot tolerate any general head either. Instead each man in
his own place desires to be a cock in his own henhouse, and to be both bishop
and pontiff.* They desire to be bishops; he does not, therefore, think they
truly are bishops. *Bishops, or inspectors of the churches*—whom Zepper also
calls deans and superintendents at the beginning of the chapter—*are exempt
or immune from no part of the pastoral office. They hold a rank not of power
but of order, and one that is temporary and defined. They are subject to the
common discipline and ecclesiastical censures. They make no laws by their own
will. They do nothing by their own authority, but do everything by the common
consent and decree of the synod of the brethren and the godly magistrate.*
Again: *Just as the senate requires a consul to bear the commonwealth’s chief

lead others by counseling, admonishing, and exhorting, and to execute decrees

order, not power, and one that is temporary and defined. He presides like a
consul in the senate and is subject by a reciprocal obligation to those over
whom he presides for the sake of order. Even if a Lutheran superintendent is
of this kind, or if he has a little more power and is a permanent president in
synods, classes, or consistories, he nevertheless differs greatly from an
English bishop. The English bishop is elected by the chapter at the king’s
command and consecrated afresh, because he professes that the episcopate is an
order distinct from the presbyterate. He is not only the inspector but also
the pastor of all the churches of his diocese, not of any particular church
or parish, and by himself he exercises jurisdiction with a singular power
over all those churches and their pastors, either personally or through his
chancellors, officials, commissaries, and archdeacons. Nor is he subject by
any reciprocal obligation to the perhaps three hundred or five hundred
ministers of the churches over which he presides. I pass over the pomp,
luxury, secular dignity, baronies, and similar things that are accessories of
the English episcopate. Is a Lutheran superintendent of this kind? By no
means. Without a synod and bishops, an English archbishop can remove his
fellow bishops, receive appeals from suffragan bishops and their consistories,
and, insofar as he is also a primate, even bypass the intermediate level. He
can also make laws concerning ecclesiastical polity together with the prince,
and do most of the other things discussed above in chapter 3. The archbishop
of Canterbury has the Courts of Faculties and Dispensations, the Prerogative
Court, the Court of Audience, the Court of Arches, and the Court of Peculiars.
Archbishops take precedence over all the dukes of the kingdom and are adorned
with the title “Your Grace.” Are the German superintendents of this kind,

Add to this that these same men, enriched with the most ample stipends, feast

are also more insolent in this respect: they boast that they possess their
power by divine law, while the Germans profess that theirs rests only on human
law and the free concession of the pastors.

In the English episcopate these four things must chiefly be considered: 1. the
derivation of its power; 2. its exercise by one person alone; 3. the
appointment of others to exercise it; 4. its extensive power.

As for the first, they derive their jurisdiction immediately from the king as
its head and source, just as the papists derive theirs from the supreme
pontiff. Among them the power of jurisdiction resides wholly in one pontiff as
in

[Marginalia: Pag. 379.; *In Lucam homil.* 132. folio 344.]

[1623 printed p. 309 | IA leaf 321 | PDF p. 321]

its own source; in inferiors it is only derived and limited. So also among the
English the power of jurisdiction in all its forms is united to the royal
Crown, and from the royal Crown it flows to the commissioners of the High
Commission, to bishops, and to other ordinaries. The illustrious Edward Coke,
a vigorous defender of the liberties of his country and a man most skilled in
English law, has these words in the fifth part of his *Reports*, namely, on
the king’s ecclesiastical right, in the Latin edition, folio 8: *The law
divides all these*—namely, the members of the body politic—*into two classes,
clerics and laity, both of whom are subject and obedient, immediately and
directly under God, to their head*—namely, the king, of whom he had spoken
shortly before. *The royal head of this body politic is also furnished and
armed with full and complete power, prerogative, and jurisdiction to
distribute to each member of this body, of whatever place or order, what
belongs to him in all causes ecclesiastical or secular; otherwise he would
not be the head of the
whole body. And just as in secular matters the king judges and determines
temporal causes in judicial courts according to the temporal laws of England,
so in ecclesiastical and spiritual causes—namely, blasphemy, apostasy,
heresies, schisms, the conferral of orders, admissions and institutions of
clerics, the celebration of divine services, matrimonial rites, divorces,
general bastardy, the right to tithes and the withholding of them, offerings,
revenues, dilapidations, repairs of churches, probate of wills,
administrations of estates, simony, incest, fornication, adultery, assaults on
chastity, pensions, procurations, ecclesiastical appeals, commutation of
penance, and others*—the cognizance of which does not belong to the common
laws of England—*they are to be determined and decided by ecclesiastical
judges according to the royal ecclesiastical laws of this kingdom. Just as
the laws which the Romans transferred from Athens, once approved and
confirmed by them, were nevertheless called the civil law of the Romans; and
as the Normans designated most of the laws they borrowed from England by the
name of the laws or customs of Normandy; so, although the kings of England
derived the ecclesiastical laws they possess from others, nevertheless all of
those which were approved here by the general consent of all are aptly and
rightly called the royal ecclesiastical laws of England. Whoever denies this
also denies that the king has full power to administer justice to his
subjects in all their causes, or to punish crimes and offenses within this
kingdom. For, as is now clearly established, the determination of so many
cases of such great importance does not fall within the cognizance of the
common law, and consequently the king is not an absolute monarch or the head
of the whole, undivided body of this kingdom.* The meaning is that, just as
the king judges and determines temporal causes in judicial courts according
to the temporal laws of England, so he judges and determines ecclesiastical
and spiritual causes in ecclesiastical courts according to the ecclesiastical
laws of England. The illustrious man does not dispute whether it is done in
this way by divine law; he only relates the laws and infers what he said
above. In the same

sense Whitgift, archbishop of Canterbury, writes: *We acknowledge that all
jurisdiction which any court in England, whether civil or ecclesiastical, has
or exercises is exercised in the name and by the authority of the Queen’s
Majesty and descends from her as supreme governor.* Again, *The queen
exercises her supreme government in all causes, in civil matters through the
chancellor, and in ecclesiastical matters through the archbishops. Although
the formulas of proceeding and the proceedings in ecclesiastical courts,*
says the illustrious man, *are under the names of the bishops,* etc., *it does
not follow that those courts are not the king’s courts, or that the law
according to which they proceed is not the king’s law. Let this one example
stand for many. Every view of frankpledge is held by a subject in the name of
his lord, and yet it is the king’s court, and all proceedings in it are
directed according to the king’s laws. Very many subjects hold courts of
record and other courts in England, yet they proceed in them according to the
king’s laws and the customs of the kingdom.* *Reports*, part 5, folio 39. Let
the reader see more above, pp. 33–34. The acts of ecclesiastical jurisdiction
are therefore exercised by authority derived from the king. If one wishes to
call that authority civil, then acts of a spiritual nature, such as
excommunicating and removing from office, are performed by civil authority,
which is absurd. But since it is impossible for civil authority to be raised
above its own nature so as to become spiritual, it follows that spiritual
power is united to the king’s Crown and that the ordinary jurisdiction of
bishops is derived from it. The king can also create bishops without a
chapter, whenever he wishes. Annosus Verementanus concludes, among other
things, from the fact that the pontiff appoints or can appoint bishops not
elected by the people and clergy over churches, that episcopal power is
contained in the Roman pontiff as in its source and is derived from him to the
bishops. *You may freely assail the Roman pontiff as a violator of divine law
because he appoints bishops not elected by the people and clergy over
churches. But you will find no way of doing this without by the same action
attacking the most serene king of Great Britain. For in his kingdoms he
governs ecclesiastical affairs as supreme pontiff, appoints whatever bishops
he pleases over the churches, and does not permit them to be elected by the
clergy, much less by the people.* These things were said against Spalatensis
by that writer, whoever he is. We spoke above in chapter 1 about the creation
of bishops, the chapter’s imaginary nomination and election, and the royal
power to create bishops without that imaginary election by the chapter, as
also about the derivation of the bishops’ power from royal authority.
