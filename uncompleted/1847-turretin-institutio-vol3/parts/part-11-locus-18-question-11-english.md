QUESTION ELEVEN

ON THE INFALLIBILITY OF THE CHURCH

Is the church infallible? Or can it err concerning the faith? The former is
denied and the latter affirmed against the papists.

I. Of all the disputes we have with the papists concerning the church, the
question of the church’s infallibility is the most fiercely contested and is
of such importance that the papacy appears to rest upon this principal
support. They contend so keenly for the church’s infallibility for no other
reason than to assign this privilege to their own church, which they claim is
the only true one, and thus to vindicate for it a supreme and unaccountable
judgment in matters of faith, to which every person without examination must
submit with blind obedience.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

II. To grasp correctly the state of the question, which is often not stated
accurately enough, the following must be observed. 1. The question is not
simply whether the invisible church of the elect, or the whole body of
believers, can err in the foundation. Our doctrine of the perseverance of the
saints sufficiently proves that this is attributed to us by slander. Rather,
the question is whether it is to be called infallible in such a way that it is
liable to no error, which we deny. 2. Properly speaking, the question does not
concern the visible church as the collective body of believers, but chiefly
the representative church of pastors—not the first pastors who founded it,
such as the apostles, whose infallibility is confessed by all, but the
subsequent pastors who preserved and governed the church already founded. Nor
are they considered separately, for our opponents do not deny that they can
err in that capacity, but jointly and assembled in a council.

III. Bellarmine, *On the Church*, book 3, chapter 14, explains the opinion
received among them in this way:

[1847 vol. III printed p. 61 | IA leaf 95 | PDF p. 95]

“Our opinion,” he says, “is that the church absolutely cannot err, either in
things absolutely necessary or in other things it proposes to us as to be
believed or done. And when we say that the church cannot err, we understand
this both of the whole body of believers and of the whole body of bishops, so
that the sense of the proposition, ‘The church cannot err,’ is this: what all
believers are bound to believe is true and of the faith, and likewise what all
bishops teach as pertaining to the faith is true and of the faith.”

Although Bellarmine here extends this privilege to the entire multitude of
believers, elsewhere, with little consistency, he restricts it to the bishops.
In *On Councils*, book 2, chapter 2, when he intends to prove that approved
general councils cannot err, he says: “Because the entire authority of the
church formally exists only in the prelates, just as the sight of the whole
body formally exists only in the head, therefore the church’s inability to err
in defining matters of faith and the bishops’ inability to err are the same
thing.” If it is asked whether this belongs to all bishops individually, he
teaches in the same place that it is to be understood only of those assembled
in a council, “because individuals separately can err.” Again, if someone asks
whether it belongs absolutely and simply to bishops assembled, he is unwilling
to acknowledge even this unless the councils are approved by the pontiff.
“All Catholics,” he says in *On Councils*, book 2, chapter 2, “steadfastly
teach that general councils confirmed by the supreme pontiff cannot err.”

It follows that the foundation of this infallibility is traced back to the
pontiff alone, “upon whose judgment it depends to establish decrees and to
alter those established,” as Baronius says under the year 373, number 21. Thus
under the name of the church they understand the Roman pontiff, its head.
Meanwhile Bellarmine teaches in *On the Pontiff*, book 4, chapter 2, that this
infallibility of the pope must not be understood absolutely. He says that
“Catholics agree that the pontiff, even as pontiff and with a body of advisers
or with a general council, can err in controversies of fact that depend upon
human information and testimony; and that the pontiff as a private teacher can
err even in universal questions of law, whether of faith or morals, through
ignorance, as sometimes happens to other teachers.” Yet elsewhere, in *On the
Roman Pontiff*, book 4, chapter 6, he says it is probable and can piously be
believed that even as a private person the pope cannot be a heretic.

He then adds that “Catholics agree that the pontiff with a general council
cannot err in establishing decrees of faith or general precepts of morals; and
that the pontiff, whether he can be a heretic or not, can in no way define as
to be believed by the whole church anything heretical.” In chapter 3,
proposition 1, he says, “When the supreme pontiff teaches the whole church in
matters pertaining to faith, he can err in no case,” in which he sufficiently
reveals his inconsistency. Andrew Duval does no less in *On the Supreme Power
of the Pope in the Church*, part 2. He says, “Although it is not of the faith
that the supreme pontiff separately from a council enjoys the privilege of
infallibility, even when he acts as pontiff”—which he proves soundly by various
reasons—“yet it is absolutely certain and undoubted.”

But the Jesuits did not stop there. They went further and extended the pope’s
infallibility not only to questions of law but also to questions of fact, as is
evident from the theses defended in Paris at the College of Clermont in 1661.
They assert that “Christ entrusted the government of his church to the popes
in such a way that he granted them the same infallibility he himself possessed
whenever they spoke from the chair.” From this they conclude: “Therefore, in
the Roman church there is an infallible judge of controversies of faith even
apart from a general council, both in questions of law and of fact.” The
Parlement of Paris courageously opposed this assertion, previously unheard of
in France, by its decree of January 22, 1663.

IV. It must nevertheless be admitted that all the papists do not hold the same
opinion on this point.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 62 | IA leaf 96 | PDF p. 96]

For not a few think that the church can err in matters that have not been
revealed, as Cano does in *Theological Topics*, book 4, chapter 4; or in things
that do not pertain to faith; or in questions of fact rather than law, as
Alfonso de Castro does in book 6, chapter “Heresy.” Likewise, concerning the
pontiff there are many of great reputation among them, and even councils
themselves, who do not approve this infallibility but openly deny it.
Bellarmine does not deny this in *On the Roman Pontiff*, book 4, chapter 2,
when he reports the opinion of those who maintain that “the pontiff as pontiff
can be a heretic and teach heresy if he defines something without a general
council, and that this has in fact sometimes happened.” He attributes this
opinion to Nilus in his book against the primacy of the pope, to Gerson,
Almain, Pope Adrian VI, and the Parisians, who place infallibility of judgment
in matters of faith not in the pontiff but in the church, or a general council.

The fathers of the Council of Basel confess in their third synodal letter
“that they had often experienced and read that the pope errs.” The gloss on
*Si Papa*, Distinction 40, says that “the pope is to be judged by no one unless
he is found straying from the faith.” Why would this restriction be added if
that were impossible? Peter d’Ailly, Cardinal of Cambrai, in *On the Authority
of the Church*, part 3, chapter 3, and Cusanus in *On Catholic Concordance*,
book 1, chapter 15, held the same view, as did the Gallican church and the
Sorbonne generally. They did so by name in the censure presented to the king
in 1663 of propositions by Amadeus Guimenius and Vernant which maintained
papal infallibility. In its sixth proposition the Faculty says that “it is not
the doctrine or dogma of the Faculty that the supreme pontiff is infallible
when no consent of the church is added.” The assembly of the French clergy
confirmed this two years ago, decreeing among various points in its fourth
proposition that “the pope has chief authority in matters of faith, but his
decisions are not certain without the consent of the church.”

V. Thus the papists divide into different parties among themselves concerning
this infallibility of the church. The orthodox opinion amounts to the
following. 1. The invisible catholic church can in some sense be called
infallible objectively, insofar as it has the infallible norm of God’s word by
which it is directed, and subjectively, insofar as it cannot err fatally in
the foundation so that it ceases absolutely to exist, as was proved above.
Nevertheless, while it wages war on earth, it is always liable to various
errors no less than to sins. 2. The visible church, which is assembled in
particular congregations throughout the world—whether considered collectively
or representatively in its rulers, either as individuals separately or as all
joined together in a council—is not infallible. It can err fatally both in
faith and morals, in questions both of law and of fact; and the privilege of
sinlessness was given to no church or person after the apostolic age, nor does
it any longer belong to anyone.

[Marginalia: The church is proved not to be infallible. 1. From the
imperfection of regeneration.]

VI. There are various reasons. 1. The first is drawn from the imperfection of
regeneration, because infallibility presupposes a perfect knowledge of things
and excludes all ignorance, which is the mother of error. But this perfection
belongs to no church or believer while living on earth. Indeed, the triumphant
church differs from the militant in precisely this: seeing God face to face,
the former is completely perfect and free from every error and vice in both
knowledge and holiness. The latter, because it carries the remains of sin with
it and sees only in a mirror and in a riddle, is still liable to both: “For we
know in part and prophesy in part” (1 Corinthians 13:9), and “we walk by faith,
not by sight” (2 Corinthians 5:7). Therefore, just as there is no one who does
not sin, so there is no one who does not err or cannot err. “Who will
understand errors? Cleanse me from secret ones” (Psalm 19:13).

[Marginalia: 2. From the nature of the promises given to the church.]

VII. 2. From the nature of the promises given to the church, which always
include the tacit or express condition of perseverance in the faith. “If you
abide in my word,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 63 | IA leaf 97 | PDF p. 97]

you will truly be my disciples” (John 8:31); “He who has my commandments and
keeps them is the one who loves me, and he who loves me will be loved by my
Father, and I will love him and manifest myself to him” (John 14:21); and “If
anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will
come to him” (verse 23). Thus God’s kindness is promised to the Roman church
provided it remains in it; otherwise cutting off is threatened: “Behold,
therefore, kindness and severity: severity toward those who fell, but kindness
toward you, provided you continue; otherwise you also will be cut off” (Romans
11:22). Since these promises are conditional, they cannot take effect unless
the condition is fulfilled; and when it is absent in various persons, this
privilege also must cease. I admit that God also works the condition itself in
the elect so that they are immune at least from fundamental error, but this
cannot be said with respect to others.

[Marginalia: 3. From the opponents’ hypothesis.]

VIII. 3. From the opponents’ hypothesis. According to the papists the church
consists not only of true believers but also of unbelievers and reprobates,
who can lack all inward virtues—faith, hope, and love—as Bellarmine says in
*On the Church*, book 3, chapter 2, as seen above. No one would say that the
privilege of infallibility has been given to them. In vain he adds in the same
place that he believes all virtues are found in the church, for he expressly
professes that this does not belong to its necessity.

[Marginalia: 4. From the fallibility of individuals.]

IX. 4. From the fallibility of individuals. Individual pastors and rulers of
churches are fallible, as our opponents admit; therefore, all of them are also
fallible. What individuals do not possess separately, they cannot possess
jointly, nor can an assembly or council consisting of fallible members be
infallible. The presence of Christ renders them no more infallible than it
renders two or three believers gathered in Christ’s name, in whose midst
Christ promises that he will be present.

[Marginalia: 5. From the silence of Scripture.]

X. 5. From the silence of Scripture. If the church had to be infallible, the
Holy Spirit would undoubtedly have warned believers about it somewhere as a
matter of the greatest importance for confirming the faith of the godly and
forestalling every deception. Otherwise he would seem to have failed in the
most necessary matter of all, which it was especially important for the church
to know. But nowhere is it mentioned that this privilege is given to any
particular church. For however many passages our opponents bring forward to
prove it, it will later be shown that they do not establish it. So far is this
from being said to have been granted to the Roman church that, if any such
thing belonged to it, it would be altogether necessary to explain why Paul,
on the contrary, carefully warns it of the danger of defection and the fall
threatening it: “They were broken off through unbelief, but you stand by faith.
Do not be high-minded, but fear; for if God did not spare the natural branches,
take care lest it happen that he does not spare you either,” and so forth
(Romans 11:20). Why would the apostle have said such things if he believed the
gift of infallibility had been conferred upon it? In Romans 16:17 he warns them
“to watch those who cause offenses and dissensions contrary to the doctrine
they learned and to turn away from them.” Anyone who compares the present
Roman church with the primitive church will easily discover that this warning
was not vain.

[Marginalia: 6. From the experience of every age.]

XI. 6. From the experience of every age, because it is evident that the
church often erred under the Old Testament. The Israelite church erred when
Aaron cast a golden calf and erected an altar before it (Exodus 32:2, 5). It
matters little whether this happened by an orderly judgment of the church or
by a tumultuous gathering of the people, provided it is evident that the
rulers of the church themselves fell into such a sin with the people. Nor
does it help whether they remained in that sin for a long or a short time. It
is enough that such a crime was committed to prove that this church was not
infallible. Although Aaron had not yet been solemnly consecrated as pontiff,
he had been

[1847 vol. III printed p. 64 | IA leaf 98 | PDF p. 98]

already designated and appointed by God and acknowledged by the people. This
is evident both because the priests are commanded to sanctify themselves
(Exodus 19:22) and because the people approached him to make the calf, which
they would not have done unless they had already regarded him as a priest. The
statement that the Levites did not acknowledge fathers or brothers in the
vengeance taken upon the idolaters (Exodus 32:26; Deuteronomy 33:9) shows that
several Levites could have been found who did not pollute themselves with this
crime, or who immediately acknowledged the sin committed and attempted to
wash it away by this deed. But it does not prove that the Levitical tribe was
absolutely immune from it, since Aaron, the head of the Levites, was the author
of that sin.

XII. The Israelite church erred under the judges when, after Joshua’s death,
they forsook the Lord and served the Baals. The greatness of this corruption
is graphically described in Judges 2. It erred under the impious and idolatrous
kings both in Samaria and Jerusalem, in the times of Joram, Ahaz, Manasseh,
Ahab, and others. Under them the people fell into foul idolatry; God’s temple
was closed more than once; and public worship was neglected and miserably
corrupted. Lest anyone say that the prophets, priests, and the greater and more
distinguished part of the people were not infected in the same way, the
prophecies must be consulted. They establish that the rulers of the church,
the priests, and the prophets were not immune from it.

Isaiah most sharply attacks the priests and pastors of the people for becoming
drunk, being blinded, and being overcome by a spirit of sleep (Isaiah 29:10–
11); for being blind and deaf (42:19); and for being all blind watchmen, dumb
dogs who sleep, love dreams, and lack understanding (56:10–11). Jeremiah
reproaches them for lying and falsehood, idolatry and greed, treachery and
defection from God (Jeremiah 2:8; 5:30–31; 6:13; 8:8–10; 10:21; 23:27–28;
50:6). Ezekiel charges them with doing violence to the law, failing to
distinguish sacred from profane, and polluting the sanctuaries (Ezekiel 7:26;
22:26). The same occurs throughout the other prophets.

Nor can a distinction between the Old Testament church and the New Testament
church be brought forward here, because Bellarmine in *On the Church*, book 3,
last chapter, admits that “the Jewish priests possessed the privilege of not
erring until Christ came.” It is no better to say that the prophetic words
are figurative and directed to all but are not to be understood of all, only
of many. Although God always preserved for himself remnants of grace in the
most corrupt condition (Isaiah 1:9), it is nevertheless clearly evident from
this that the multitude of priests who claimed the title of the church for
themselves erred shamefully.

Nor should it be added that this is said not of a council of priests but of
pastors and prophets who separately deceived the people. Those who were such
when separate would have been no better when joined together. Moreover, the
Holy Spirit speaks not of one or another separately but of the entire order
and whole college of priests: “The priests will be astonished, and the
prophets will be terrified” (Jeremiah 4:9); “The law will perish from the
priest, and counsel from the elders” (Ezekiel 7:26); “Night will be to you
instead of vision and darkness instead of divination, and the sun will go down
upon the prophets” (Micah 3:6). Hosea compares the very society of priests to
robbers who lie in wait in narrow roads (Hosea 6:9). Finally, the example of
the four hundred false prophets opposed to Micaiah (1 Kings 22) proves that
not one or another but the college itself erred. They were not prophets of
Baal, since Micaiah does not reproach them with that, but prophets of Jehovah,
who boasted in that name, though falsely. Hence Jehoshaphat asks beyond them
whether there was “yet another prophet of Jehovah” (verse 7), as Cajetan
rightly observes.

XIII. The synagogue erred most grievously in Christ’s time by making God’s
commandment

[1847 vol. III printed p. 65 | IA leaf 99 | PDF p. 99]

void through its traditions (Matthew 15:1–2), depraving the law by its
corruptions (Matthew 5), infecting purer doctrine with the deadly leaven of
various errors, and stumbling over the stone of offense. It was not the few
and obscure but the leaders of the people, priests, scribes, and pastors who
possessed the succession of places and persons. They acted not only separately
but jointly in the Sanhedrin, where it was decreed not only that anyone who
followed Christ should be put out of the synagogue (John 9), but also that
Christ should be condemned and killed as an impostor and false Messiah (John
2:50; Matthew 26:65–66). Since no one can deny that this was the greatest of
all crimes, it must also be admitted that the synagogue, with which the visible
church then rested, erred most shamefully.

XIV. All the escapes the papists seize upon to evade the force of this
argument pressing them are vain. 1. “Caiaphas did not err in this oracle” when
he said it was expedient that one man die for the people, because John says
that “he prophesied,” that is, spoke by a prophetic spirit things that served
to execute the divine decree. But who can hear this without horror, as though
the devil and the impious did not sin when God uses them, contrary to their
intention, to execute his counsel? Nor does the statement that he prophesied
by speaking thus mean that he did not err. He did not do this knowingly and
willingly, but by the counsel of the Holy Spirit, who willed that he utter
words which disclosed this counsel of God, though beyond his own intention.

2. “He erred in his mind, not in his words.” But Bellarmine rightly refutes
this from Matthew 26:65 in *On Councils*, book 2, chapter 8, where Caiaphas
says of Christ, “He has blasphemed; what further need have we of witnesses?”
Bellarmine says, “Then surely he did not prophesy but blasphemed.” Moreover,
when it is asked whether councils can err, the dispute concerns not the words
but the mind of a council. Laws consist not in words but in their meaning; nor
should those be said to obey councils who cling to the words but spurn their
judgment and intention.

3. “They erred in fact, not in law, condemning not the Messiah but one whom
they falsely believed had usurped the Messiah’s name.” Bellarmine also rightly
refutes this, because this very matter pertains to law and concerns the most
momentous dogma of all, the true Messiah. Ignorance of fact is at the same time
ignorance of law when there is no reason to doubt the fact. Otherwise the Jews
of today would be excused, for in blaspheming Christ they suppose they deny
not the true Messiah but an impostor.

4. “This council was not legitimate.” Although it was not legitimate
intrinsically, since they were not gathered in God’s name and did not set
God’s glory before themselves but their own vicious passions, it cannot be
called illegitimate extrinsically. Nothing was lacking for the constitution of
an extrinsically legitimate council: the head presided, its lawfully summoned
members were present, and a decree was made after the matter had already been
brought before the council more than once.

5. “It indeed erred, but in Christ’s presence the privilege of not erring had
ceased. While Christ was acting on earth, whose authority was supreme and
altogether infallible, it was unnecessary that the council be unable to err;
indeed, it had been foretold that it would err.” But first, either that
privilege ceased when Christ came in the flesh or only at his death. If the
former, why did Christ so often send people to the priests? Why did he command
that those sitting in Moses’ chair be heard? Why did he frequent the temple
and take part in the sacred rites performed by them? If the latter, as Lombard
maintains in *Sentences*, book 3, distinction 3, “All legal ordinances were
finally terminated at Christ’s death.” Therefore the offices of the priests
and the privileges attending those offices still remained, and they ought not
to have erred.

Second, if the fall of the synagogue was foretold, so was that of the
Christian church. If the church could err so shamefully while Christ lived and
spoke on earth, what prevents it from happening today, when Christ is absent,
received into heaven, and speaks only in the Scriptures?

[1847 vol. III printed p. 66 | IA leaf 100 | PDF p. 100]

[Marginalia: 7. From the predictions of the New Testament.]

XV. 7. From various predictions which establish that the New Testament church
was bound to err. Speaking of the apostasy of the last times, Paul says that
“the day of the Lord will not come until the apostasy comes and the man of sin
is revealed, the son of perdition, opposing and exalting himself above
everything called God or worshiped, so that he sits in the temple of God,
showing himself as though he were God” (2 Thessalonians 2). Here there are
almost as many weighty reasons as there are words. “Apostasy” is mentioned,
which was to come from the faith, through “the man of sin and the son of
perdition”—that is, a notorious apostate who was to be the author of so great a
defection, both most wicked in himself and the author of error and sin in
others, and therefore devoted to eternal perdition and destruction. He boasts
that he is lawless and outside the law, freed from all laws, to be judged by no
one while arrogating to himself authority to judge everyone. He must “sit,”
that is, exercise authority and dominion, in the “temple of God,” or the
church, “as though he were God,” exercising dominion over consciences. He
“exalts himself above God” by opposing his own authority to God’s, prohibiting
what God permitted and permitting what he prohibited; and “above what is
called God,” namely kings and princes, who are often designated by this name
in Scripture, by requiring worship, submission, and obedience from them. If
such and so great an apostasy must have a place in the church, who would say
that the gift of infallibility belongs to it?

XVI. This apostasy cannot be understood as: 1. A political defection from the
Roman Empire rather than a spiritual and ecclesiastical defection from the
faith of Christ. Neither the words nor the accompanying circumstances by which
it is described permit this. The words do not, because whenever the New
Testament uses *apostasia* or the verb *aphistēmi*, it denotes defection from
the faith (Acts 21:21; 1 Timothy 4:1). The circumstances do not, because this
defection is joined with lying wonders and every deception of iniquity (verses
9–10), works the mystery of iniquity (verse 7), and exerts itself through the
power of error so that those who refused to believe the truth believe a lie
(verse 11). Hence elsewhere it is described as a defection from the faith, an
attention given to deceiving spirits and doctrines of demons, and spiritual
fornication and adultery (Revelation 17), or idolatry, by which the marriage
bond between God and the church is broken.

2. Nor can it mean defection to Mohammedanism. It does not belong to Mohammed
to sit in the temple of God; Turkish dominion raged more by violence than
fraud, and not so much against consciences as against bodies; nor is it
recorded that he used such lying signs or tricks to instill his poison. It is
one thing to compel by the sword as a tyrant and another to lead astray by the
keys as a false bishop. It is one thing to gape for prey with the dragon’s open
mouth and another to belch blasphemies under a lamb’s skin with the dragon’s
horns. Finally, Paul speaks of a mystery which was already beginning then and
whose outbreak and open revelation the Roman Empire was delaying, which the
apostle understands by “the one who restrains.” This cannot be said of
Mohammedanism.

3. Nor can it mean defection to paganism. It is a mystery whose revelation
something hindered, but nothing then hindered paganism; indeed, everything
seemed to recall people to it. What external force or the enticements of the
flesh openly urged should not have been called a mystery. Moreover, the son of
perdition could not have been said still to be revealed, because paganism,
which prevailed among almost all nations, was already manifest enough.
Finally, he is said to be going to sit in God’s temple, which does not pertain
to the Gentiles. Hence it is evident that this apostasy can refer only to the
general defection which had to arise in the Christian church through the
coming of Antichrist, as was proved more fully elsewhere in Disputation VII,
*On Separation from the Roman Church*, where the fiction

[1847 vol. III printed p. 67 | IA leaf 101 | PDF p. 101]

of Grotius concerning Caligula and Simon Magus is refuted and this passage is
discussed more fully.

XVII. To this belong many other predictions about corruptions and errors to be
introduced into the church in the last times by false Christs and deceiving
spirits, against whom believers are commanded to guard themselves. “Beware of
false prophets who come to you in sheep’s clothing but inwardly are ravenous
wolves” (Matthew 7:15); “False Christs and false prophets will arise and give
signs and wonders, so that, if it were possible, even the elect would be led
into error” (Matthew 24:24); “Pay attention to yourselves and to the whole
flock, in which the Holy Spirit placed you as bishops, because after my
departure ravenous wolves will enter among you, not sparing the flock, men
speaking perverse things to draw away disciples after themselves” (Acts
20:28); “The Spirit expressly says that in the last times some will depart
from the faith, giving heed to deceiving spirits and doctrines of demons,” and
so forth (1 Timothy 4:1); “There will be false teachers among you who will
introduce destructive sects,” and so forth (2 Peter 2:1); and “Little
children, it is the last time, and as you have heard that Antichrist is coming,
from this time there are many antichrists,” and so forth (1 John 2:18).

But this appears especially in Revelation, where the great Babylonian whore
and mother of fornications is said to make the kings of the earth and
inhabitants of the world drunk with the wine of fornication, that is, idolatry
(Revelation 17–18). Therefore God calls his people out of her lest by sharing
her sins they also share her plagues. Since it is evident from all these
passages that deadly errors were going to have a place in the church, everyone
sees that infallibility is falsely predicated of it.

[Marginalia: 8. From the examination of doctrine.]

XVIII. 8. From the examination of doctrine imposed upon believers. They are
recalled “to the law and to the testimony” (Isaiah 8:20), that is, to the law
which contains the testimony of God’s will toward us. They must consult it
alone, despising magicians and diviners, so that whatever is handed down by
them may be tested against this norm. Elsewhere they are commanded to “search
the Scriptures” (John 5:39) and “test the spirits whether they are from God”
(1 John 4:1)—that is, against the touchstone of the Scriptures—not only by the
church’s public judgment but also by the private judgment that belongs to all
the godly so that they may establish their faith from God’s word. Thus Paul
wishes believers to “test all things” (1 Thessalonians 5:21), that is, all
dogmas proposed to them according to the guiding star of the word and analogy
of faith, and to “hold fast what is good,” that is, sound doctrine after
rejecting error and falsehood. Elsewhere he commands the Corinthians to judge
what he said (1 Corinthians 10:15), comparing it with the Scriptures to see
whether these things were so, after the example of the Bereans (Acts 17:11).
Thus he testifies that he “commends himself to human consciences through the
manifestation of the truth” (2 Corinthians 4:2), which could not be done
without examination.

Why would this examination or judgment be commanded so earnestly to believers
if infallibility had been given to the church? Would it not have been enough to
warn believers of this privilege so that with absolute submission and without
examination they would receive whatever the church handed down? Since this is
nowhere done, but on the contrary each person is commanded to live by his own
faith, it is plainly evident that the fiction of infallibility is advanced
wrongly and without foundation.

[Marginalia: 9. From the uncertainty of this dogma among the papists.]

XIX. 9. From the uncertainty of this dogma among the papists themselves. They
do not yet agree about the seat of this infallibility, nor is it yet known
with whom it must rest—with the pope or with the council. Some claim this
privilege for the council, as all those do who believe the pope is fallible and
therefore wish the council to be above him, as was decreed at the councils of
Constance and Basel and is steadfastly believed by the Gallican church. Others
attribute it to the pope, as do those who claim that he is

[1847 vol. III printed p. 68 | IA leaf 102 | PDF p. 102]

superior to the council, as the Council of Trent decreed and the canonists and
scholastics hold with it. Again, even if it were agreed with whom such
infallibility rests, faith in this mystery would be no less tottering. On their
hypothesis that the minister’s intention is necessary to the essence of a
sacrament, no certain argument can establish either that this assembly is a
lawfully convened ecumenical council consisting of suitable members or that
the man who sits at Rome is a true pontiff into whose infallibility our faith
must be resolved. According to them, it is not of the faith that this
particular pope now sitting is a legitimate successor of Peter and vicar of
Christ, much less that those gathered in a council are legitimate bishops,
rightly baptized and ordained. How, then, can they be certain of their
infallibility or demand from others a certainty that they themselves cannot
possess? Thus everything totters and remains uncertain among them, because the
final resolution of faith is made not in Scripture alone, which is inspired by
God, but in the empty fiction of the church’s infallibility. They disagree
among themselves about its certain seat, and it is most certain that it cannot
be found either in a council or in the pontiff.

XX. As for the council, most papists maintain that even general councils are
liable to error, and thus divide them into rejected, approved, and mixed, and
make their infallibility depend upon the pontiff’s confirmation. Moreover,
sinlessness can no more belong to all the council’s members jointly than to
individual pastors separately, whom they believe to be fallible. Nor is any
promise of infallibility given to a council rather than to private persons.

Moreover, it is well known that various councils erred. These include the
councils of Ariminum and Seleucia under Constantius, which confirmed Arianism,
and the second, or “Robber,” Council of Ephesus under Theodosius the Younger,
in which the Eutychian heresy was approved, besides many others. This is
clearly confirmed by the discord and contradiction of councils which enacted
conflicting decrees. A Roman council under Stephen rescinded the acts of
Formosus; a Roman council under John IX invalidated Stephen’s acts and
restored Formosus. Nicaea II set up images, while Constantinople and Frankfurt
overturned them. The synods of Constance and Basel subjected the pope to a
council, while the Lateran and Tridentine councils exalted him. Constance took
the cup from the people; Basel restored it under certain conditions. As for
the spirit present in the later councils which the papists chiefly use, even a
blind man can see it from the acts of Nicaea II and Constance. The former
sacrilegiously tramples upon the second commandment of the Decalogue and the
latter upon Christ’s command that the Supper he instituted be received whole.

XXI. Many things also prove that the pontiffs are not infallible. 1. The
testimony of history establishes that many of them were not only utterly
wicked—magicians, simoniacs, poisoners, atheists, and devoted to every vice,
like those of whom Baronius complains under the year 900 as so many monsters
thrust into that see—but also fell into heresy. Genebrard says in his
*Chronicle*, book 4, that “for about 150 years approximately fifty pontiffs,
from John VIII to Leo IX, wholly fell away from the virtue of their
predecessors, being apostatical and apostates rather than apostolic.” Among
those who fell into heresy were Liberius, who subscribed to Arianism;
Honorius I, who upheld Monothelitism and was therefore condemned in the
thirteenth session of the sixth general council; Marcellinus I, who sacrificed
to idols; Zosimus, who favored Pelagianism; John XIII, who denied the
immortality of souls and resurrection of the flesh and was condemned at the
Council of Constance; Gregory VII and Boniface VIII, who claimed supreme power
over kings and princes; and many others of whom the papists and historians
themselves do not remain silent. 2. The pontiffs themselves did not believe in
this infallibility, for they so often rescinded the acts of their predecessors
and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 69 | IA leaf 103 | PDF p. 103]

enacted conflicting decrees concerning many articles of faith, in which later
popes withdrew from the opinions and decisions of earlier ones. They surely
would not have done this if they believed infallibility was a privilege of
that see. 3. Many councils exercised their power over pontiffs by deposing
them for heresies and crimes and pronouncing them heretics. 4. The appeals
often made to a future council against the undertakings of the pontiffs and
Roman curia testify to the same thing. 5. There would have been no need to
summon councils with such great labor to deliver a judgment of doubtful
authority if an infallible judgment could be found in the pontiff. For it is
vain to do through more what can be accomplished more conveniently and
certainly through fewer. Finally, to avoid accumulating the many other reasons
that could be brought forward, if the pope were infallible, he would be the
principle and norm of belief for himself, and at his death the church’s
infallibility would fail.

XXII. The papists are not helped at all by their ridiculous petty
distinctions, through which they try to deceive the simple and somehow plaster
over this absurd fiction. They claim, 1. “The pope can indeed err as a private
teacher but not as pontiff speaking from the chair.” Although the same person
can sometimes assume a public relation as consul and a private one as head of
a household, according to his twofold status and office, there is no place in
this argument for that abstraction, because the same teaching office and the
same doctrine to be taught are at issue throughout. How can it happen that as
pontiff he determines and judges contrary to what he himself believes as a
private person? Can sweet and bitter water flow from the same mouth? Thus the
same man as pontiff will be orthodox but as a private man a heretic. As an
infallible pontiff he can be absolved and saved at God’s judgment, yet as an
erring private man condemned at the same judgment. Why does this man not
consult himself so that he may cease to err and not perish?

Again, since it is not yet agreed what this chair is, how can it be known and
by what marks will it be distinguished when he speaks from the chair and when
he does not? Indeed, since the pope is considered to occupy that sacred chair
of Peter from the moment he is elected and inaugurated, there can be no moment
when he can be sought or conceived outside it and therefore none when he does
or determines anything outside the chair that is liable to error. If God had
willed the pontiff to be infallible for the church’s salvation, would he not
have provided that he determine nothing erroneous by a private and personal
act no less than by a public and judicial act?

XXIII. 2. “He can err in questions of fact, not law.” First, however, the
Jesuits do not admit this, for they attribute both kinds of infallibility to
him. Although this opinion brings with it various most grievous absurdities,
as the censors of the Clermont thesis observe, all who defend the pope’s
infallibility in controversies of law nevertheless fall back into it, because
a question of fact is often resolved into a question of law. For example, one
who can err about a fact can err in applying passages of Scripture to disputed
articles of faith, but this necessarily involves a question of law. Likewise,
one who can err about fact can also doubt the creation of the world, Christ’s
coming into the world, and his resurrection, all of which draw after them most
momentous questions of law.

3. “He can err in matters pertaining to morals, but not in matters of faith.”
Bellarmine maintains the contrary in *On the Roman Pontiff*, book 4, chapter
5: “The pontiff cannot err not only in decrees of faith but also in precepts
of morals prescribed to the whole church.” Further, he cannot err against good
morals by determining from the fullness of his power something in itself
unjust and unlawful without first being held by this most grievous error in
faith: that such a right belongs to him. Finally, if the perpetual assistance
of the Holy Spirit is given to bestow infallibility upon the pontiff in
matters of faith, why could he not have the same assistance in matters
pertaining to morals? Nor is there force in the distinction between

[1847 vol. III printed p. 70 | IA leaf 104 | PDF p. 104]

dogmatic and practical infallibility, as though the former but not the latter
were rightly attributed to him, after Peter’s example, who though infallible
in faith nevertheless sinned in morals. The issue here is not human
impeccability in the actions of life, which we read was granted to no one, not
even the apostles, but the public exercise of office, the establishing of
dogmas of faith and precepts of morals. Concerning these, the distinction
between dogmatic and practical infallibility has no place.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XXIV. The church governed by Christ and the Holy Spirit alone and always does
not err. But the militant church is not governed by the Holy Spirit alone or
always, because it is also often governed by the flesh, which lusts against
the Spirit (Galatians 5:17). The error or weakness of the church or its
particular members can no more be attributed to Christ and the Holy Spirit,
who give life to this mystical body and each of its members, than a dislocation
of the foot or hand can be attributed to the soul, which informs the natural
body and each of its members. Here Augustine’s statement must always hold:
God so administers everything he created that he also permits those things to
exercise their own motions. Thus the same perfection must not be sought in
the church as in the primary teacher of truth, who is the Holy Spirit. He acts
in the church as its capacity and measure permit, no more wiping away every
spot here than every tear.

XXV. Although Christ is the church’s bridegroom, it does not immediately
follow that the church must be infallible. If he wishes to preserve it from
every fundamental error so that it does not err totally or finally, he does
not therefore determine to remove every error from it. On the contrary, he
wishes at times to test its faith by permitting it sometimes to fall into
errors. Nor, if the church dozes and sometimes fails to hear the bridegroom’s
voice, does it therefore utterly desert or repudiate the bridegroom. Likewise,
one who turns a deaf ear to reason’s dictate in some action does not
immediately cast reason itself away. One who undermines the doctrine of faith
in some way does not immediately become an unbeliever; only one who overthrows
the foundation and does not hold the head does so (Colossians 2:19). For as a
carnal marriage is not dissolved by every quarrel, so the bond of spiritual
marriage is not broken by every error or any particular heresy. Although
idolatry, which is spiritual adultery, provides the most just cause for
divorce, God nevertheless calls his bride to better fruit with long patience
and mercy before repudiating her.

XXVI. Although the church possesses a perpetual canon and most certain norm
of doctrine which admits no stain of error in itself, it does not follow that
the church is absolutely infallible. The church, which crawls in filth, does
not fully grasp that exactness. No one can affirm otherwise unless he
confounds the beginning with the end, the starting gates with the goal, and
the road with the homeland. Therefore, insofar as the church believes the
doctrine of the divine word, it is immune from falling; but insofar as it
believes imperfectly and is carried about by the wind of human doctrine, it
errs.

XXVII. It is one thing for Christ always to be present with the invisible
church of the elect to preserve it from fundamental error and a total and
final fall; it is another for him to be present with the visible, and
especially the representative, church to confer infallibility upon all its
decrees. Christ promises the former (Isaiah 59:21; Matthew 16:18; 28:20; John
14:16), but not the latter. These promises are fulfilled only in true
believers and the elect. No one can know that all who compose ecclesiastical
assemblies are among their number. Indeed, various examples establish that the
number of the impious and worldly often prevails in them.

XXVIII. When Christ promises his disciples the presence of the Spirit, who
will lead them into all truth (John 16:13), he primarily and chiefly regarded
the apostles. As founders of the church and establishers of the canon of faith,
they had to be infallible, as Toletus, Jansen, and other papists

[1847 vol. III printed p. 71 | IA leaf 105 | PDF p. 105]

acknowledge. Secondarily it is indeed extended to ordinary pastors and
individual believers, but not in the same degree and measure of gifts;
otherwise all would have to be equally infallible. Therefore, the guidance of
the Holy Spirit must not be understood absolutely and simply with respect to
all truth universally, but in a limited and qualified sense with respect to
the subject matter, that is, truth necessary for salvation, as the Carthusian
observes. In this way the Corinthians are called “rich in every word” (1
Corinthians 1:5), and Paul is said to have “declared the whole counsel of God”
(Acts 20:27).

Ministers are led into all truth by the Spirit of God speaking in the
Scriptures, and they are governed by the Spirit as long as they hear the word
proposed by the Spirit and do not turn aside from this rule. But no one should
dare say that they will never turn aside from this path, since Paul testifies
to the contrary (Acts 20:29–30), as does the experience of every age.
Otherwise, just as it belonged separately to each apostle, so it would belong
separately to every pastor, which our opponents deny. Therefore, this promise
of Christ, first given to the apostles, is rightly said to extend to their
successors, but in the lower degree which can belong to ordinary ministers,
not with respect to the apostolic preeminence of infallibility, which was
proper to them. Moreover, it pertains only to the apostles’ genuine successors,
who are none other than those who retain their doctrine.

XXIX. When Paul calls the church “the pillar and foundation of truth” (1
Timothy 3:15), 1. he indeed denotes its office and duty in proposing,
retaining, defending, and vindicating the truth, but not its immutable
privilege—what it is bound to do, not what it always does. In the same way,
“the lips of the priest” are said to “keep knowledge, and the law is sought
from his mouth, because he is the messenger of Jehovah of hosts” (Malachi
2:7). This is not because he always does so, since the next verse immediately
adds, “But you have turned aside from that way; you have caused many to
stumble at the law,” but because this duty is imposed upon him. Thus words
signifying action must often be understood of duty (Ezekiel 34:2; Malachi
1:6). 2. The particular Ephesian church is at issue here, in which Timothy as
its bishop had to labor, but Rome would not wish to attribute infallibility to
it. 3. This is said of the collective church in distinction from the pastors,
not the representative church about which they properly raise the question.
For more on the meaning of this passage, see Part 1, Locus II, *On Scripture*,
Question 6, Thesis 25.

XXX. Things said only conditionally must not be understood absolutely, such as
“He who receives you receives me” (Matthew 10:40) and “He who hears you hears
me” (Luke 10:16)—namely, if you hear me and propose nothing but my word,
handing down nothing other than what you received from me and speaking nothing
other than what I taught you (Ezekiel 3:17; 1 Corinthians 11:23). The facts
cry out that pastors do not always do this. If they instead propose the
inventions of their own minds and human doctrines, so far are they from being
heard that they must be rejected and regarded as accursed (Galatians 1:8).
Thus the scribes and Pharisees sitting in Moses’ chair are commanded to be
heard (Matthew 23:2–3), that is, as long and insofar as they sit in it by
retaining and preaching Moses’ doctrine, although elsewhere Christ commanded
the godly to beware of their leaven, namely false doctrine (Matthew 16:6, 12).

XXXI. 1. The church is no more to be considered infallible when people are
commanded to hear it (Matthew 18:17), or leaders when we are commanded to obey
them (Hebrews 13:17), than any ordinary judge whom people are likewise to obey
(Deuteronomy 17:12), or any parents to whom we are religiously bound to listen
according to Solomon’s precept (Proverbs 6:20–21). Therefore, this manner of
speech aims only to gain authority for those in public office, but always
under the condition that they prescribe nothing shameful, for then there is
room for resistance. Thus it is false that under pain of anathema we are
absolutely bound to believe

[1847 vol. III printed p. 72 | IA leaf 106 | PDF p. 106]

whatever the church commands. As Christ commands pastors to teach what he
himself commanded (Matthew 28:20), so he commands believers to believe those
same things and no others. This is so certain that Paul does not hesitate to
pronounce anathema upon anyone preaching another gospel (Galatians 1:8). 2.
That passage concerns the presbytery of each particular church, to which no one
would dare attribute infallibility, and the exercise of discipline, not dogmas
of faith. Nor should the grave penalty pronounced upon the person unwilling
to hear—that he should be as a heathen and publican—be urged, because grave
penalties are also pronounced upon those who do not obey magistrates and
parents, yet no one has called them infallible. Likewise, the anathemas that
councils hurl against heresies which they condemn from the Scriptures no more
prove their infallibility than the solemn denunciations of divine wrath
against sins made by individual pastors prove those pastors infallible. To
this belongs the statement of the Master of the Sentences, book 4,
distinction: “God does not always follow the church’s judgment, which sometimes
judges through surreption and ignorance, but God always judges according to
truth.”

XXXII. Although God did not give infallibility to the church, it cannot
therefore be said that he failed to provide it with necessary things. The
church need not have a supreme and infallible tribunal among human beings upon
whose decrees and responses our faith depends. It is enough for it to have the
infallible norm of faith and morals in Scripture. As long as it adheres to
that norm, it will not stray from the right path.

XXXIII. From Christ’s promise to be in the midst of two or three gathered in
his name (Matthew 18:19–20), it is rightly concluded that he will give godly
pastors and any believers who assemble in his name as much grace and help as
will be necessary for their condition and calling. To assemble in his name is
to do so by his call, with his name invoked, so that they seek his glory and
conduct his cause, and with the intention of following his command. But one
would wrongly infer either that all who gather assemble in Christ’s name in
this way or that absolute infallibility is given to those who assemble
properly. They do not need it in their calling, since they ought not to be the
norm of faith; that belongs to the Scriptures alone, by which everything must
be tested. Otherwise the same privilege would belong to any laypeople
assembling in Christ’s name.

XXXIV. Once the church’s infallibility is denied, it does not follow, as the
Bishop of Meaux claims in his conference with the celebrated Claude, that
every private person, however ignorant, is bound to believe he can understand
God’s word more correctly than all universal synods and the whole true church.
We neither believe nor say this. We say only that private believers endowed
with the Holy Spirit are bound to examine by God’s word whatever is proposed
to them for belief or action by the rulers of the church, whether by
individuals separately or by many collectively gathered in a synod. They are
bound to believe that by the Holy Spirit’s guidance, with godly prayers and
diligent study of Scripture, they can better grasp the sense of Scripture in
matters necessary for salvation than entire synods which depart from God’s
word, and better than a society which falsely claims for itself the name of
the true church.

Therefore, the examination they are bound to make is not done to correct the
true church’s meaning and discover something more correct, as though they
could be wiser than it, but to investigate and follow that meaning. The right
of examination is not founded upon our needing to believe that we are more
learned and clear-sighted than entire synods and the whole true church. It is
founded upon the fact that God gave the privilege of infallibility to no
church or pastor; that we cannot certainly know whether those who compose
ecclesiastical assemblies are members of the true church and faithful servants
of God who partake of the Holy Spirit and follow his guidance; and that it can
happen, and has happened far too often, that such assemblies err in

[1847 vol. III printed p. 73 | IA leaf 107 | PDF p. 107]

their decisions. Thus no other means remains for a believer to recognize the
legitimate authority of those assemblies and the decisions they make with the
certainty of faith than to compare and examine them with God’s word. God not
only permits this as possible and lawful but commands it as right and
necessary. It therefore cannot be considered rashness or pride, because it
belongs to the performance of an indispensable duty imposed upon all
believers. Under the pretext of avoiding pride, believers must not blind
themselves or be deprived of their right so that consciences are reduced to
slavery through blind obedience.

XXXV. It is not absurd that believers who search the Scriptures piously and
in the fear of the Lord should grasp their meaning better than worldly and
hypocritical rulers of the church who, following the guidance of blind reason,
endeavor to palm off the inventions of their own minds and human traditions as
God’s word. Thus in his most free grace God “reveals his mysteries to little
children while hiding them from the wise and understanding” (Matthew 11:25).
Thus he gives “the disciples knowledge of the mysteries of the kingdom of
heaven, which he denies to others” (Matthew 13:11). Thus “the secret of the
Lord is for those who fear him” (Psalm 25:14). And “if anyone wills to do the
Father’s will, he will know concerning Christ’s doctrine whether it is from
God or whether he speaks from himself” (John 7:17).

XXXVI. Since faith is not perfected in us in a moment but advances and
exercises itself little by little and by degrees, there is nothing absurd in
saying that a child or recently baptized Christian who has not yet seen or
read Scripture cannot immediately elicit an act of divine faith concerning
the infallibility of Scripture considered formally. He can, not indeed
deliberately doubt its truth or falsehood through ignorance arising from an
evil disposition, but simply not know whether such Scripture exists through
purely negative ignorance; or he can as yet believe only by human faith what
kind of thing it is. This is not human with respect to the object or principle,
but with respect to the argument and motive, which is merely human, such as
instruction and authority from parents or the church itself. This precedes his
believing it by divine faith, that is, by divine arguments and motives drawn
from reading Scripture itself.

Moreover, although there can be a recently baptized Christian who does not
yet know Scripture formally and therefore has not yet exercised any act of
faith concerning it, no moment can be conceived in which, if he has the use of
reason, he ought to possess no knowledge of the principal heads of religion
contained in Scripture. Faith in these is ordinarily required from an adult
being baptized and from a baptized child as soon as he reaches the age of
discretion, and concerning these he can therefore elicit an act of faith.
Besides, nothing can be objected here about the impossibility of an act of
faith concerning Scripture in a recently baptized Christian which cannot be
turned back with equal, indeed far greater, force concerning an act of faith
in the church and its infallibility.

XXXVII. Although the authority which according to us belongs to synods is
great, it is not absolute and unlimited, so that we are bound to subject
ourselves to it with blind obedience and without examination. It is limited
and ministerial and depends upon two conditions. The first is that they decide
nothing except from God’s word. The second is that they always leave believers
the freedom to examine their decisions.

The clause of submission appended to letters to a synod signifies nothing
else when the churches declare that “they will submit to everything to be
decreed in the sacred assembly, persuaded that the Holy Spirit will be present
there and will lead its members into all truth and equity by the rule of his
word.” This clearly includes the tacit condition that the decisions be made
according to the rule of the word. If they are found to have departed from it,
all obligation of submission immediately ceases. Therefore, this persuasion
concerning the presence and guidance of the Holy Spirit is a persuasion of
charity, which judges well of and hopes concerning that assembly that it will
decide everything from God’s word until the contrary

[1847 vol. III printed p. 74 | IA leaf 108 | PDF p. 108]

appears, and it includes a tacit prayer for such guidance. It is not a
persuasion of infallibility which entails absolute submission and excludes
the right of examination. Nor can the favorable presumption we may have
concerning the learning and orthodoxy of those who constitute a synod prevent
believers from always being bound, after the Bereans’ example, to compare
whether whatever is decreed there agrees with Scripture.

XXXVIII. Even if the condition were not expressly and explicitly stated in
the letters, this silence could not be considered deception, equivocation, or
a mental reservation. The condition is understood by law, is natural and
fundamental, flows spontaneously from our principles, and is expressly stated
in article 5 of our confession and in other writings of our theologians, so
that it is evident to anyone and can be hidden from no one.

Although the clause of submission has the force of an oath, it does not
immediately imply the certainty of infallibility. There is one rationale for
oaths made concerning accomplished and past things, which ought to rest upon
the certainty of the facts and not mere presumption, and another for oaths or
promises concerning future things, which rest upon presumption and include a
tacit condition. Thus the oath of loyalty a soldier makes to his commander
rests only upon a presumption of the commander’s loyalty to the prince and
necessarily presupposes that condition. The rationale of covenants is no
different: they rest upon a similar presumption and always include a
condition, if not express, then tacit and implicit.

XXXIX. Although the decision of a national synod is final in the order of
human means, admits no appeal to a higher tribunal, and is one to which
believers are bound to submit in the external forum to preserve the church’s
peace and good order, it is not therefore considered infallible so as to bind
the conscience itself. The believer always possesses the right to examine
whatever is proposed to him for belief, and that decision binds his conscience
no further than it is evident to him that it conforms to God’s word. More must
be said about the nature and force of these decisions in what follows, when
ecclesiastical power is treated.
