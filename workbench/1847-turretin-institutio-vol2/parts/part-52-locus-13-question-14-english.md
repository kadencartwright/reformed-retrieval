QUESTION XIV.

THE PASSIONS OF CHRIST.

*Did Christ suffer for us only bodily punishments in his body, or indeed in
his soul but only with respect to its lower and sensitive part? Or did he also
sustain in himself properly, and from a sense of God’s wrath, the very
spiritual and hellish punishments of sin in both the higher and lower parts?
We deny the former and affirm the latter against the Roman Catholics.*

I. Since Christ’s passion is the chief part of the λύτρου, *ransom,* paid for
us by Christ and the principal foundation of our confidence and consolation,
it must also be the primary object of our faith and subject of our meditation,
so that with Paul we set nothing before ourselves except the knowledge of
Jesus crucified. We must apply ourselves to it all the more diligently the
more violently Satan rages to obscure the truth of these passions and deprive
us of their saving fruit.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question here is not whether Christ suffered anything. There were
indeed long ago some who, thinking it unworthy of Christ’s divine glory
that he should be seized by pains and sorrow, went so

[Printed p. 310 | Internet Archive PDF 338 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

far as to say that Christ’s body was impassible and therefore that he suffered
not truly but only κατὰ δόξαν, *in appearance.* This was the heresy of the
Aphthartodocetae, about whom Nicephorus writes in *Ecclesiastical History*
17.29. Hilary seems to approach them to no small degree when, in book 10 *On
the Trinity,* he maintains that because of its union with the Word, the human
nature in Christ was subject to no passions—if this is indeed Hilary’s
position. Here Lombard, *Sentences* 3, distinction 15, excuses him as though he
had denied to Christ not so much the sensation of suffering as the cause and
desert of suffering. Yet this opinion has long since been so thoroughly
rejected as contrary to the truth of the gospel history and our redemption
that there is no need to bring it back upon the stage. Rather, the question
concerns the nature and subject of the passions: namely, what were his
passions, and in what subject? Were they only in the body, or also in the soul?
And if in the soul, were they only in its lower part, called the sensitive, or
also in the higher and rational part?

[Marginal heading: Christ’s spiritual passions are proved. 1. From Scripture;]

III. In order to defend their dogma of the perfect blessedness of Christ’s
soul throughout the state of emptying and reconcile it with the history of
the passion, the Roman Catholics maintain that Christ suffered in body but not
in soul. If pressed, they distinguish the soul’s lower and sensitive part from
its higher and rational part. With respect to the former, to which they refer
the affections and disturbances, they concede that Christ suffered, but by no
means with respect to the latter. Or if he suffered anything in it, he did not
suffer properly and in itself from a sense of God’s wrath, but only either διὰ
παράστασιν, *by assistance,* or διὰ συμπάθειαν, *sympathetically,* because of
the soul’s communion with the body. The orthodox, on the contrary, refer
Christ’s passions to both soul and body and to both the higher and lower parts.
They hold that in his body he endured bodily pains and torments and the most
cruel temporal death of all, while in his soul he endured spiritual and
internal pains—namely, that most grievous and supremely dreadful weight of
divine wrath and the κατάραν, *curse,* owed to us. The reasons are:

IV. First, Scripture attributes Christ’s passions to both parts, and especially
to the soul, when Christ’s soul is said to have been troubled (John 12:27),
περίλυπος, *surrounded and, as it were, overwhelmed with sorrow on every side*
(Matt. 26:38). Thus this occurred not only in the lower part but also in the
higher. This is all the more so because, since the soul is ἀμέριστος,
*indivisible,* it cannot happen that when it is affected by one quality all
the others are not also affected. The gravity of this sorrow is demonstrated
by its being called *unto death,* that is, extreme, to mark the weight of the
tribulation, as the Gloss has it; by the soul’s disturbance being described by
τάραξιν, *trouble* (John 12), ἀδημονίαν, *distress* (Matt. 26:37), and
ἐκθάμβησιν, *dread* (Mark 14:33), when the mind, as it were, collapses before
horror and nearly gives way beneath the weight of grief; and by ἀγωνίαν,
*agony,* namely such anxiety and anguish of mind that it wrung bloody sweat
from him (Luke 22:44), along with the most fervent prayers which he offered to
the Father with loud crying and tears that the cup might pass from him, when
he had need of a comforting angel. To what could that extreme dejection and
disturbance, that immense horror and dread, and that fiercest struggle with a
sense of divine wrath and the curse be referred, unless we say that these
passions were not only in the body but also in the soul, and not merely in the
lower part of the soul but also in the higher? Otherwise we would have to make
Christ inferior to innumerable martyrs who endured bodily death and the most
terrible torments not only patiently and calmly but even with joy and
exultation.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the necessity of salvation.]

V. Second, the necessity of our salvation required this. For just as we had
sinned in soul and body, so Christ the Surety had to suffer in both

[Printed p. 311 | Internet Archive PDF 339 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

parts, so that he might pay a λύτρον, *ransom,* sufficient for divine justice
to redeem both soul and body. This is confirmed because the punishments
threatened against sinners by the law are not only bodily and external, but
especially spiritual and internal in the sense of divine wrath and curse
(Lev. 26; Deut. 27–28; Gal. 3:10, 13). Hence Irenaeus, book 5, chapter 1, says,
“The Lord redeemed us with his own blood, who gave his soul for our souls and
his flesh for our flesh.” Hence he is called not only ἀντίλυτρον, *a ransom in
exchange,* but also ἀντίψυχος, *one who exchanges soul for soul,* who pledges
and exchanges soul for soul.

VI. Third, the punishment of abandonment which Christ suffered and about
which he complains in Matthew 27:46 was not a bodily passion, but a spiritual
and internal one. It arose not from just any torment, however terrible, which
he could feel in his body—for not a few martyrs could experience such things,
yet they are not read to have complained of this abandonment—but from the
most grievous sense of God’s wrath resting upon him because of our sins. This
desertion must not be conceived as absolute, total, and eternal, such as
befalls only demons and the reprobate, but as temporal and qualified. It did
not concern the union of nature, for what the Son of God once assumed he never
laid aside; nor the union of grace and holiness, because he was always ἄκακος,
*innocent,* and ἀμίαντος, *undefiled,* endowed with inviolate holiness; nor
communion and protection, because God was always at his right hand (Ps. 110:5)
and he was never left alone (John 16:32). Rather, it concerned participation
in joy and happiness, as God for a time suspended the favorable presence of
grace and the influx of consolation and blessedness so that Christ could
suffer all the punishments owed to us. It concerned the withdrawal of vision,
not the dissolution of union; the lack of a sense of divine love, interrupted
by the sense of divine wrath and vengeance resting upon him, not its real
privation or extinction. And, as the scholastics speak, it concerned the
affection for advantage, so that he was deprived of the ineffable comfort and
joy which arise from the sense of God’s fatherly love and the beatific vision
of his face (Ps. 16), but not the affection for justice, because on this
account he sensed nothing disordered in himself which tended toward despair,
impatience, or blasphemy against God.

VII. Fourth, Christ was made a curse for us (Gal. 3:13), when the curse of the
law owed to us was imposed upon him because of the suretyship he assumed. He
was not only ἐπικατάρατος, *accursed,* but κατάρα, *a curse,* the abstract
being put for the concrete for ἐπίτασιν, *intensification,* because he endured
every curse which the law threatened against sinners. This certainly does not
regard the body alone, but especially the soul, which can be affected by such
a sense, as not a few Roman Catholics acknowledge, including Maldonatus on
Matthew 26, Ferus on Matthew 26 and 27, and others.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VIII. Although Scripture usually ascribes salvation to Christ’s cross, blood,
and death, this does not exclude his spiritual and internal passions, but
names the whole by synecdoche from the sensible and external part exposed to
the eyes. Nor can the cross or death be recalled without the notion of the
curse annexed to them being involved at the same time, because this is no
other death than that threatened by the law.

IX. Although the internal and spiritual torments could not be figured
specifically and particularly by the death and shedding of blood of the
victims, they could nevertheless be contained under them generally. Since
fire is a symbol of divine wrath, nothing prevents the burning of sacrifices
by fire from being referred to the sense of divine wrath which Christ endured
in his soul. Likewise, the blood of the sacrifices, as the seat of the soul,
can very well be called a symbol of the soul

[Printed p. 312 | Internet Archive PDF 340 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

also. Add that a particular type was not lacking in the passions of David,
which are described in Psalms 22 and 69 and were not only external but also
internal.

X. It is not necessary for the sacraments to represent every part of Christ’s
passions. It is sufficient that they exhibit a certain part which could be
signified by such symbols, but from which the rest can readily be gathered,
since they necessarily accompany one another in Christ. Nor can Christ’s
death be foreshadowed without the curse joined to it presenting itself to our
mind.

XI. Christ’s sacrifice is called *the offering of the body* (Heb. 10), not in
an opposition of the body to the soul, which he is also said to have laid down
for us (Isa. 53:10; John 10:15), but by antithesis to the Levitical sacrifices
so often repeated, about which the apostle was speaking. Hence this manner of
speaking was most suitable.

XII. Hence it can readily be gathered what must be judged about Christ’s
passions: whether they may properly be called hellish and Tartarean, as our
theologians hold. Since *hell* in Scripture denotes not only the grave or the
abode of the damned, but also the extreme condition of misery and calamity, as
Arias Montanus rightly observes, and since *the pains of hell* are put for the
most grievous pains, nothing prevents Christ’s passions from being called
hellish in this sense because of their dreadfulness and intensity.

XIII. Yet on this account you would not rightly say either that he entered the
place of the damned and was damned, for he could indeed bear the punishments
of those who deserved damnation, but not of the damned, so as to descend into
the infernal place prepared for them, from which no return is granted; or that
he was consigned to eternal punishment, because the eternity of the
punishments owed to us was abundantly compensated in Christ by their gravity
and extremity.

XIV. Much less should despair, which befalls the damned, be attributed to
Christ. It does not belong to the essence of punishment considered in itself
and as inflicted by the Judge, but to the defect of the suffering subject. As
that subject considers the eternity of the most dreadful evil and the
impossibility of escaping it, it cannot but fall into despair. This was
utterly alien to Christ, who, although he felt the dreadfulness of the torments
he had to endure, was nevertheless certain of their happy outcome and end.
Hence the falsity is evident of the atrocious calumny most unjustly cast upon
our Calvin by adversaries such as Bellarmine, *On Christ* 4.8, Genebrard on
Psalm 22, Maldonatus on Matthew 27, and others, because Calvin says on Matthew
27:46, “But it is absurd that a word of despair escaped Christ.” Who does not
see that this is an objection which he proposes to himself from Christ’s words
“My God,” and so forth, and which he answers in such a way as to assert most
clearly that Christ was always utterly removed from despair and full of
confidence? “The solution,” he says, “is easy: although the sense of the flesh
apprehended destruction, faith nevertheless remained fixed in his heart, by
which he beheld God present, of whose absence he complains.” But if faith was
fixed in his heart, how could despair befall him? He confirms this still more
strongly in *Institutes* 2.16.12, as is evident from inspection of the passage.
But if one wished to make a countercharge, how much more justly could this be
cast upon the adversaries, among whom not a few are found guilty of this very
charge? These include Maldonatus on Matthew 27, Ferus on the same passage, who
expressly attribute despair to Christ, Cusanus *On Christ’s Torments,* and
others. See our disputation *On Satisfaction,* p. 283.

XV. By a similar calumny the great man of God is accused of having said that
Christ was damned, because in section 10 of the Catechism he says that Christ
was placed in such damnation that he complained of being abandoned by God.
But: 1. Who does not see that *damnation* is put here for *condemnation,*
according to the style of the French language most commonly used at that
time? 2. If Christ is called a curse, why can damnation not

[Printed p. 313 | Internet Archive PDF 341 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

be attributed to him? 3. The fathers and not a few of the adversaries have
spoken in this way. Cyprian, *On Christ’s Passion,* says, “He was damned so
that he might free the damned; he grieved so that he might heal the sick; he
feared so that he might make them secure.” Gregory of Nazianzus, oration 2 *On
the Son,* says, “He united that which was condemned so that he might free the
whole from condemnation.” Athanasius, *On the Incarnation,* says, “There was
need of that very Judge who had issued the decree, so that he himself might
dissolve the sentence in the form of the condemned.” Gregory the Great,
*Morals on Job* 3.11, says, “He assumed the form of a damned man,” and “he
damns the one who is without sin for sinners.” The Vulgate Bibles have the
word *damnation,* as do the Louvain Bibles of 1533. Cusanus speaks no
differently in *Exercises* 11.
