CHRIST’S MEDIATORIAL OFFICE

QUESTION I.

*In what sense does the name Mediator belong to Christ?*

I. Having spoken of Christ’s person and state, we must now treat his
mediatorial office, for the sake of which he became the God-man (θεάνθρωπος)
and had to undergo a twofold state. Here we must first consider the force of
the name *Mediator,* which is customarily attributed to him in this matter.

[Marginal heading: The derivation of the word Mediator.]

II. *Mediator,* which is μεσίτης among the Greeks, is derived from *middle.*
It is commonly applied to one who stands in the middle between parties at
variance and mediates (μεσιτεύει), since, as the apostle says, “a mediator is
not of one,” Galatians 3:20, but of several parties contending with one another,
whom he is to reconcile. Hence Suidas explains *mediator* (μεσίτην) by
*peacemaker* (εἰρηνοποιόν). The Latin term *sequester* is also used, the word
Tertullian employs to designate Christ in *Against Praxeas.* A *sequester* is
one who stands between two disputants and with whom the thing in controversy
is deposited. He is so called from *following,* because each of the
contestants follows his pledge.

III. Christ’s mediation can be viewed either with respect to his person or
with respect to his office, either insofar as he is intermediate or insofar as
he mediates. Hence some have distinguished mediation into two kinds. They
called the first *substantial,* by which Christ is intermediate between God
and man, and the second *effective and operative,* by which he executes the
task entrusted to him by the Father. The former denotes the state of the
person, the latter his operation. The former designates the union of the
natures, the latter the execution of the office in the person. But because the
former is less properly called mediation, we do not consider it here, but only
the latter, which belongs to this locus.

[Marginal heading: In how many ways is he so called?]

IV. Therefore Christ is properly called mediator in act and exercise because
he exercises the mediator’s office to establish union between God and human
beings who have been separated from each other by sin. Now a mediator among
human beings is of several kinds. He may act as a mere intermediary, who is
the interpreter of each party, as Moses is called mediator in the Old
Testament because he stood between God and the people, Deuteronomy 5:5. Or he
may act as an arbitrator, who is chosen by the litigants

and has authority over the whole affair to settle it, not by strict law but
“according to equity” (κατ’ ἐπιείκειαν). Or he may act as an intercessor and
advocate, who makes entreaty and pleads for one party before the other. Or he
may act as a surety and satisfier, who reconciles the parties at variance by
satisfying the offended party and by giving security for the future fidelity
and obedience of the offending party, so that no cause of dissension may
afterward arise between them. Christ is rightly called mediator according to
these various relations (σχέσεις).

[Marginal heading: Christ the Mediator, as an intermediary;]

V. First, he was an intermediary with respect to doctrine, insofar as he was
the interpreter of each party, and an ambassador (πρέσβυς), insofar as he
declared God’s will to human beings, John 1:18; Luke 4:18. In this sense he is
called the messenger of the covenant, Malachi 3:1, and counselor, Isaiah 9:6.

[Marginal heading: As an arbitrator;]

Second, he can also be called an arbitrator, as Ambrose calls him in his work
on 1 Timothy 2 and Augustine in *Discourse 4 on Psalm 103,* because in
procuring our reconciliation with God he did not act by strict law but
tempered justice with grace and mercy.

[Marginal heading: As an intercessor;]

Third, because our affair is with the offended supreme power, he had to
discharge the office of intercessor and advocate, pleading our cause before
God against the devil and obtaining the grace necessary for us. In this sense
he is called an advocate (παράκλητος), 1 John 2:1.

[Marginal heading: As a peacemaker.]

Fourth, because the dissension arising from sin between us and God could not
be removed except by satisfaction of God’s justice and expiation of sin, our
mediator had to undertake not only the parts of an intermediary and
intercessor, as Socinus would wish, but especially that of a reconciler and
peacemaker (εἰρηνοποιοῦ), who would procure peace for us with God by his blood
and, by paying a counter-ransom (ἀντίλυτρον) for us, free us from the guilt and
dominion of sin. And because it was not enough to reconcile human beings to
God once if the same dissension should afterward be renewed by human sin, he
had constantly to confirm by the efficacy of his suretyship the reconciliation
once accomplished, so that we might be united to God forever. The first and
second modes pertain to prophecy, the third and fourth to the priesthood and
kingdom.

VI. Since the mediator had to act on each side, both with the offended God and
with offending man, to remove the obstacles to reconciliation, he actually
acted in both ways: for us before God, by satisfying and interceding as priest;
and for God before us, by teaching and directing as prophet and king. He acted
first for us and then worked in us, as was discussed more fully above in Locus
XII, on the covenant of grace, Question 2, section 16. Hence all these acts are
distinguished into *impetratory* acts, by which that reconciliation and the
things dependent upon it were acquired, and *applicatory and preservative*
acts, by which all those things are applied and preserved. Through the former
Christ is mediator and Savior by merit; through the latter, by efficacy.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

VII. Our dispute here is with the Socinians, who, in order to overthrow the
whole doctrine of satisfaction, indeed confess that Christ is mediator, but
only: 1. Of divine institution or revelation, by which Christ declared the
life of salvation, which according to them consists in obedience to God’s
commands. 2. Of example, because he entered the same way and furnished his
followers with the most perfect example. 3. Of confirmation, because he
sanctioned that obedience by his death and passion. Although we do not deny
that Christ discharged these parts, we deny that his mediatorial office
consisted in them alone. Rather, it consisted principally in reconciliation
and satisfaction, insofar as he was the peacemaker (εἰρηνοποιός) who made
peace through blood and reconciled us to God.

[Marginal heading: Christ is proved to be a peacemaker (εἰρηνοποιόν).]

VIII. The reasons are: 1. Scripture describes him not as a mere intermediary
but as a true peacemaker (Εἰρηνοποιόν) and pacifier, and it describes his
mediation as redemption and

[Printed p. 333 | Internet Archive PDF 361 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

reconciliation. Thus he is called mediator insofar as he gave himself as a
counter-ransom (ἀντίλυτρον) for us, 1 Timothy 2:6; as the one who “made peace”
(εἰρηνοποιήσας) through the blood of the cross and reconciled us to God,
Colossians 1:20; as the guarantor (ἔγγυος) and surety of the new covenant,
Hebrews 7:22, who therefore had to pay and make satisfaction for us; and as
the mediator who by death procured redemption for the called and by his blood
obtained eternal salvation, Hebrews 9:12, 15. Hence “the blood of sprinkling”
(sanguis ῥαντισμοῦ) is joined to the mediator, with an allusion to the blood
of the legal victims dying for sinners, Hebrews 12:24. All these things imply
not a simple declaration but a true satisfaction, as will be proved more fully
in its proper place.

IX. 2. The necessity of salvation required this. Since the dissension which
arose between God and man through sin rendered human beings “haters of God”
(θεοστυγεῖς), Romans 1:30—that is, not only those who actively hate God but
also those passively hated by God—it could not be removed simply by doctrine
or example. Rather, it required a true ransom (λύτρον), by which not only man
would be reconciled to God through repentance and the pursuit of holiness, as
the Socinians contend, but God would also be reconciled to man. For God was
rightly angry with him from the nature of his avenging justice and therefore
could be appeased only by an adequate satisfaction, through the substitution
of the mediator in man’s place, who, by devoting himself for him, would receive
in himself the punishments owed by man and, by bearing them, free him from
those punishments.

X. 3. If Christ were mediator in no other way than by doctrine and example,
he would be placed in the same order as the prophets and apostles, especially
those who suffered martyrdom, who by their doctrine and example manifested
the way of salvation and expounded to us the whole counsel of God, Acts 20:27.
Yet the apostle denies that this can be said when he calls Christ our one
mediator, 1 Timothy 2:5. 4. He would be Savior only morally and accidentally,
because each person’s salvation would actually depend upon the one who obeyed
the divine commands, as will be shown more fully when satisfaction is treated.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XI. Christ is called mediator “for a testimony in its own times” (εἰς
μαρτύριον καιροῖς ἰδίοις), 1 Timothy 2:6, in order, that is, to give testimony
to the truth at the proper time. This is not only because he bore testimony to
the truth of the gospel through prophecy, but also because by his death he
gave us assurance of the benevolence obtained for us and the reconciliation
made with God. Or *testimony* (μαρτύριον) can be understood passively of the
thing to which testimony had to be borne, because all the men of God had to
testify about it.

XII. The similarity between Moses and Christ does not immediately imply
equality, as if Christ were mediator in no other way than Moses was as an
intermediary, Deuteronomy 5:5; Acts 7:38. For Moses was only a typical mediator
and thus far inferior to the antitype, whereas Christ is the true mediator
properly so called. Moses was a mere man; Christ is the God-man. Moses existed
to carry God’s oracles to the people, not to reconcile the people to God or
appease God’s wrath by his blood, since he trembled at the voice of God the
lawgiver, Hebrews 12:21. But Christ made peace by his blood and presented
himself to God as our surety (ἔγγυον). Thus as a partial and inadequate type,
Moses could foreshadow Christ in certain parts of the mediator’s work, namely
with respect to prophecy or kingship, but not in them all, especially not in
the priestly functions, which were signified by other types, namely priests
and victims.

XIII. Nor does Galatians 3:19 help the adversaries, where the law is said to
have been given “in the hand of a mediator” (ἐν χειρὶ μεσίτου). Besides the
fact that this can rightly be referred to Christ, as most Greek and Latin
writers, both ancient and more recent, have done, including our Calvin, there
is also the fact that the name mediator is nowhere else attributed to anyone
but Christ, who is also called the only mediator, 1 Timothy 2:5. Moreover,
Moses is elsewhere said in Acts 7:35 to have been constituted ruler and
deliverer of the people “in the hand of the angel” (ἐν χειρὶ Ἀγγέλου), that
is, by the authority and direction of the angel who appeared to him in the
bush, the eternal Son of God, who is also said in verse 38

[Printed p. 334 | Internet Archive PDF 362 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to have given Moses the “living oracles” (λόγια ζῶντα), namely the law which
he was to deliver to the people. Thus the sense is either that the law was
delivered to the Israelites through the hand—that is, the authority and
direction—of Christ the mediator, who in this way prepared that people for his
coming, or that Christ, in bringing forth the law by his own hand, interposed
himself as a surety in the middle, who would both fulfill the law perfectly in
due time and free us from its curse by drawing that curse upon himself. If it
is referred to Moses, as Epiphanius and others held, whom Beza follows here,
because “in the hand” (ἐν χειρὶ) denotes ministry rather than supreme power,
which would be less fitting for Christ, and because it is evident that the law
was given by Moses, John 1:17, as it is elsewhere said to have been given by
God “through the hand of Moses,” Leviticus 26:46, nothing is thereby taken
away from our position. For Moses will be called only a typical mediator, just
as the law was a type of the gospel, not so as to equal Christ in all things
but only faintly to foreshadow him in some.

Furthermore, the addition in verse 20, “A mediator is not of one, but God is
one” (μεσίτης δὲ ἑνὸς οὐκ ἔστι, ὁ δὲ Θεὸς εἷς ἐστι), may be elucidated without
difficulty as follows—to say something in passing about this obscure and
difficult passage—from the apostle’s purpose, which is to show that life and
justification are not given through the law. For since a mediator was needed
in the giving of the law, it is evident that the law is not the bond of our
union with God but rather an indication of our alienation and dissension from
him. “A mediator,” he says, “is not of one” party, but of several or at least
two, and indeed of parties at variance; for where there is union, no mediator
needs to intervene. But the addition “God is one” refers not so much to God’s
unity and simplicity as to his constancy and immutability, no less in nature
than in counsels and decrees—that is, he is constant and always like himself.
Therefore, since he prescribed in the law the perfect righteousness necessary
for obtaining life, and since sinful man could not render it to him, it is
evident that the union of those two parties at variance—namely, God and
man—could not be accomplished through the law but only through Christ’s
righteousness and satisfaction, which the gospel announces to us, as the
apostle had noted in verse 13. Some, following the Ethiopian interpreter,
explain it thus: “A mediator is not of one, but God is one”—namely, one of the
two who are at variance with each other. But this seems rather weak, because
no one could have been unaware that God was one of the parties at variance
when the mediation was being conducted before him.
