# CHAPTER XXII

## Whether GOD's free good pleasure is the first rule of all moral goodness in
creatures

It is very worthwhile here also to consider what opposition exists between
God and sin. Sin is certainly ἀνομία—lawlessness—and removes something
privatively. But it does not remove the soul's essential powers or the
entitative acts of those powers. For I think that such privation is neither
transcendental and entitative nor, indeed, moral. Rather, sin privatively
removes that obediential rectitude which ought to inhere in the soul's
inclinations and acts. There are two kinds of repugnance: one internal, such
as that between two accidents, hot and cold; and another external, which is
incongruity, as between things primarily diverse, such as substance and
quantity. In the former manner there is no repugnance between God, who is
superessential essence, and creatures, or between GOD and sin. For every
repugnance is founded upon some agreement which the opposition removes. But
GOD and creatures agree in being only analogically. Sin, however, does not
privatively remove being, which is predicated of God. Otherwise, once sin
were posited, this statement would be true—“GOD is non-being”—which is
blasphemous. Nor is sin opposed to God privatively, contrarily, or relatively.
For sin does not privatively remove GOD, because the destruction of the
highest good is simply impossible, whereas the existence of sin is not
impossible. Those who maintain that sin is contrarily opposed to God or to his
nature or holiness, in my humble judgment, sin no little against God's
thrice-holy αὐτάρκεια—self-sufficiency—and blessedness, to which be all praise
and glory forever. I believe this is abundantly refuted by Eliphaz and by
David. What then? Are we to dream of a relative opposition here? Yet when a
relative is posited or removed, its correlative is posited or removed. But is
God posited or removed when sin is posited or removed? Away with the
blasphemy. Nor is the mind satisfied if we say that sin is contradictorily
opposed to God or GOD's nature, because God is not sin. But what then? God is
also not obedience-*

[Marginal summary: The sense in which sin is opposed to God.]

[Marginal references: Job 22:2–4; Psalm 16:2.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 313; IA PDF page 373; BSB canvas 365]

*, not heaven, not the sea, and not an angel. But the adversaries say that sin
is opposed to GOD's nature. I ask whether eating from the tree of knowledge or
any free act of a creature has a natural agreement with GOD's nature. If it
does, God would do something repugnant to his nature if he commanded eating
from the tree of knowledge or commanded David to kill his son Solomon.
Neither Scripture nor reason would admit this. Hence it follows that sin
removes neither GOD nor anything of the divine nature by any opposition.
Therefore sin privatively removes something approved as good by GOD's free
will: namely, it removes the rectitude which GOD's free will requires in our
inclinations, powers of the soul, and acts. Nor is sin properly admitted into
GOD's will. For *“who has resisted his will?”* The person who sins does not act
against what God wills and decrees to occur and exist, but against that thing
which God approves as right and honorable. You will say: If sin is not
formally opposed to GOD's holiness and the divine nature, then it follows that
God can sin. I answer that this blasphemous consequence is invalid. On the
hypothesis that this is sin, it is hypothetically repugnant for God either to
do or command it, because of the holy immutability of the divine will. In the
same way, once it is posited that God has decreed to create the world, the
world's non-creation is repugnant to divine constancy in that respect. For
just as God freely and without any necessity of nature wills rectitude in our
acts, so in itself it is not repugnant for him not to will that same
rectitude, although after the decree has once been established it is
repugnant. But more on this elsewhere.

[Marginal reference: Romans 9:19.]

7. Vorstius wrongly denies that God can exercise, or does exercise, his
dominion except over those things upon which he has conferred a benefit. Thus,
he says, *“GOD cannot by his dominion create people for destruction or destine
them to it before they are created.”* But this antecedent is false, for GOD can
exercise absolute dominion over many possible worlds and many angels whom by
his dominion he can create or not create, although God has conferred no
benefit upon the many possible worlds. What he likewise says is false: *“GOD's
right and dominion rests upon some benefit which he himself conferred upon
creatures, or upon some evil deed of the creature; and only to that extent
does GOD have mercy upon whom he wills and harden whom he wills, for otherwise
GOD's right would be tyrannical.”* But if GOD's right rests upon a benefit
divinely bestowed upon the creature, then it was by no holy right-*

[Marginal reference: Vorstius, *Friendly Duplicate*, p. 343.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 314; IA PDF page 374; BSB canvas 366]

*but by sheer tyranny that God created the world for his glory, for God
conferred no benefit upon the created world when it was still pure nothing.
Indeed, God could not, from his absolute freedom, create the apostate angels
whom he foreknew would fall into eternal destruction without necessarily
acting as a tyrant in creating them. Thus GOD's right of showing mercy and
hardening is limited to the creature's merit and demerit—which is a Pelagian
right.

[Marginal references: Proverbs 16:4; Revelation 4:11.]

8. In this whole matter the adversaries do not understand that GOD's good
pleasure is the first rule and cause of all purely created goodness. For GOD
made all things for himself and did so by his free will. Likewise: *“For you
created all things, and by your will they exist and were created.”* Nor are we
alone in this cause. Thus Augustine says, *“Because GOD is good, we exist.”*
Anselm says, *“That which you will is just.”* And Hugh says, *“The first cause
of all things is the Creator's will, which no preceding cause moves and no
subsequent cause confirms, since it is just of itself.”* Hugh of St. Victor
also says: *“GOD himself does nothing from obligation, but from goodness
alone. People act from obligation, because through acting they become better,
or worse if they do not act.”* The Jesuit John de Lugo explains this rightly:
*“GOD is bound by no law to choose the more perfect object, for by willing one
of two opposing objects—even if it is in itself less perfect—he thereby has
an act just as perfect and morally honorable as if he willed the more
honorable object.”* The same author says: *“The infinite perfection of the
divine will cannot consist in complete conformity with the intellect, because
GOD's intellect can always present endlessly better things to his will.”* It
necessarily follows that GOD's volitions borrow or beg no honorableness or
moral justice from the created objects willed, but that some object is
honorable because GOD's will willed it. Thus it is good and honorable for
people to be created to manifest the glory of justice and for the means which
manifest it, because God willed it. Here an infinitely good and just God is
not to be measured by the short yardsticks and measure of human and created
goodness. Hence Ferrariensis and Driedo say, *“GOD's will is the first cause
that fire is hot.”* Thomas rejects the opinion of certain people *“that from
God's understanding himself such a disposition of things proceeds from him,
as though he did not dispose all individual things by his judgment.”* So also
Capreolus, Francis Cumel, and-*

9. But I wish it noted that one love or volition of God is benevolence, and
another is friendship. GOD's volition of benevolence is that which I maintain
to be the cause of the being and all created goodness of things. Because GOD,
from his benevolent affection, wills to give being to a human, an angel, and
the world, therefore a human exists, an angel exists, and the world exists.
But if we speak of the love of friendship, God then wills and loves many things
because they are good in themselves: for example, he loves holiness because it
is holiness, since the love of friendship presupposes the love of benevolence.

[Marginal summary: The volition of benevolence and of friendship in GOD.]

I hear that some are displeased that I asserted that loving GOD considered
physically is ἀδιάφορον—indifferent—and not morally good. But it ought not to
have displeased the orthodox that the entitative and physical act, clothed
with no morality, which is present in the moral act of loving God, is
indifferent. And I say it is true that God cannot command an act of idolatry,
adultery, or hatred of himself, for the Holy One of Israel—whose thrice-holy
holiness, worthy of everyone's adoration, is so dear to me that I have a
witness in heaven—hates and detests such acts because they are formally sins
and because he himself is holy. But I deny that the entitative acts underlying
the malice of adultery, idolatry, and hatred of GOD are contrary to GOD's
nature. By the act of loving GOD considered physically, I do not designate the
act as specified by its object—namely, GOD—but mean the entitative act of
loving taken physically, which underlies the act of loving GOD. I add the
reasons. Every human act which lies on the merely physical plane is indifferent
in itself as to whether it is clothed with moral goodness or malice, for
according to all the scholastics-*

[Marginal reference: Exercise 2, chapter 3, sign 12.]

[Marginal summary: All entitative acts on the physical plane are in themselves
indifferent to good or evil.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 316; IA PDF page 376; BSB canvas 368]

*, moral goodness or malice are merely accidental differences of human acts.
(2) The adversaries themselves concede that in acts commanded or forbidden by
a positive command of GOD, entitative acts are freely commanded or forbidden
by GOD. For no one denies that GOD could have commanded eating from the tree
of the knowledge of good and evil. But this reasoning holds in intrinsically
evil acts, because no entitative act is intrinsically—that is, essentially—
evil. For all malice is a privation of the rectitude which ought to inhere;
but every privation of rectitude in a physical act is accidental and foreign
to the nature of the physical act, just as every rectitude is accidental to
the physical act. Form and privation are accidents of the subject in which
they inhere, as sight and the privation of sight—that is, blindness—are
accidents and foreign to the subject's nature and visual faculty.

10. Vásquez objects as follows: *Our moral malice is an incongruity with
rational nature as its rule, just as the heat of water is incongruous with
water. Therefore hatred of GOD and perjury are, in themselves and not from
GOD's will, incongruous with rational nature; otherwise it would also follow
that hatred of GOD and perjury could have been good if GOD had not forbidden
them from eternity. Therefore the acts of hatred of GOD and of perjury are in
themselves evil, apart from every act of GOD's divine will.*

[Marginal reference: Vásquez on I–II, tome 1, disputation 71, question 1,
chapter 3, article 6, signs 6–7.]

I answer: (1) It does not follow. Hatred of GOD is indeed evil in itself and
without any act of the divine will, which I certainly concede to be true. But
it does not follow that the entitative act in that hatred of GOD is evil in
itself, for it is certainly indifferent in itself, although as clothed with
detestation of GOD it is evil in itself. (2) Others say that the incongruity
of malice, at least not all of it, is contrary to rational nature as its
regulating, first, and adequate rule. For reason is not the regulating and
adequate rule of our acts, but a rule regulated by GOD's free will. Indeed,
the killing of his son Isaac was incongruous with and contrary to Abraham's
rational nature, yet God rightly commanded Abraham to kill his son. And that
killing, contrary to Abraham's rational nature, was good and honorable by the
very fact that God commanded it, and again evil because that same God also
forbade it. This proves that the act of killing a son is morally good or evil
not from its agreement or disagreement with Abraham's rational nature but
from GOD's free will commanding or forbidding it. The same must be said of the
entitative act in hatred of GOD and in perjury-*

[Marginal summary: Natural reason is not the first regulating rule, but the
secondary and regulated rule of moral actions.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 317; IA PDF page 377; BSB canvas 369]

*. God can command or forbid these acts while they remain on the purely
physical plane, because no act is essentially evil; to say otherwise would be
a grave injury to God, the author of every positive being.

11. Hence I infer that what is evil for a rational creature is not evil for
GOD. For Abraham's act of killing his son is something evil for Abraham,
because Abraham is subject to law and to violate the law is evil for the
violator. But it is not evil for GOD, who is above the law. Thus Beza rightly
says: *“If you consider GOD's decree, evil itself has the character of good,
even if it is evil in itself.”* Augustine also says: *“It is good that evil
occur, although evil is not good.”* Alvarez says, *“GOD wills every aspect of
good in evil things because every good by participation is from the highest
good.”* Indeed, the Jesuit Ruiz holds our position in just so many words,
although softened by a gentler preliminary treatment. He says: *“It belongs
properly to sin that its intrinsic malice is malice only in comparison with
the sinner, and that it is evil only for the sinner, not another. Thus,
although in comparison with the person who sins, sin is a greater evil than
the lack of any created good, whether natural or supernatural, yet in
comparison with GOD, an angel, or some holy person, even mortal sin is a light
evil or none; and the absence of the manifestation of GOD's glory is a greater
evil than sin. Therefore GOD should not prevent sin but rather occasion it,
because it is not evil for GOD but good.”* Hence it is clear that, from his
absolute freedom to display his glory, God decreed from eternity the
usefulness which sin has for manifesting GOD's glory. We puny humans can no
more contain the abyss of divine wisdom by our crude ingenuity than someone
could try to contain every river, spring, lake, pool, and finally the vast
ocean in a two-foot vessel. Ah, luxuriant and empty reason, give glory to
JEHOVAH and be silent. With these words Ruiz establishes Calvin's and our
position on this matter.

[Marginal reference: Beza, *Christian Questions and Answers*, question 143.]

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *Enchiridion*, 96.]

[Marginal reference: Alvarez, epitome *On the Helps*, book 2, chapter 6.]

12. A certain manuscript by a disciple of John Cameron also came into my
hands, bristling with insults and accusations, as though I had inflicted a
signal injury upon Cameron by corrupting his position in the question whether
GOD's good pleasure is the first rule of all created goodness and justice. But
I am conscious of no fault against Cameron. In that manuscript he contends
that Cameron speaks not about GOD's absolute power, but only his ordinary
power on the hypothesis of the decree; and that Cameron's meaning is this:
GOD-*

[Marginal summary: Cameron's position on whether God's will is the first rule
of moral goodness is not corrupted by me.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 318; IA PDF page 378; BSB canvas 370]

*does not will things because they are good and agree with GOD's essential
purity and justice, but things are good and bear GOD's image when God has thus
decreed by his free good pleasure. And this holds concerning things prior to
GOD's decree; but after God has by his good pleasure willed this or that thing
to be good and holy and to bear his image, he then loves it not because he
willed it, but because it is now good and holy in itself. But surely this
pleads Cameron's cause badly. If Cameron were alive, he would pour as many
insults upon his ignorant advocate for attributing such a thing to him as that
man poured upon me. For Cameron maintains that *things which represent God as
being are good because God decreed their goodness, but things which represent
GOD as holy and just—that is, which represent GOD's image—are good and holy
in themselves and by their own nature, apart from every act of the divine
will.* This is clear from the words I cited. For Cameron censures this
position: *“Nothing is loved by GOD because it is good; rather, each thing is
good because it is loved by God.”* Cameron answers: *“Then neither will he love
himself because he is good, but he must be regarded as good because he loves
himself. But if this is absurd to them, the same must hold in GOD's image. For
if God inclines toward love of himself by a natural and immutable propensity,
he embraces his image with an entirely similar affection.”* Thus I argue:
It is absurd that GOD is good because he loves himself. And certainly, as
Cameron rightly says, it is utterly absurd that God would not be good unless
by his free decree he had decreed himself to be good. But what GOD freely
decrees, he could from eternity have refrained from decreeing. Thus God could
in this way have been not good, and so God would be good not essentially but
freely. I assume further that Cameron says the same must hold in GOD's image,
and that if GOD inclines toward love of himself by a natural and immutable
propensity, he embraces his image with an entirely similar affection.
Therefore, just as God is essentially good, it is repugnant for GOD to decree
himself not good and not to love himself, and God is good prior to GOD's
decree, so the same will hold in GOD's image. The thing which is God's image
will likewise be essentially good and holy prior to every free decree of GOD.
Thus Abraham's not killing Isaac but preserving him alive will be as
essentially good, prior to every decree of GOD, as GOD is good; and with an
entirely similar affection by which GOD inclines toward love of himself—that
is, with a natural and immutable propensity—GOD will incline toward Abraham's
not killing his son-*

[Marginal references: Cameron, tome 3, *Miscellanies*, p. 575; and tome 2,
lectures on Matthew, chapter 16, pp. 140–141.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 319; IA PDF page 379; BSB canvas 371]

*but preserving him alive. For a parent's act of preserving his son's life is
a part of GOD's image, although it is not essentially part of that image; but
it ceases to be natural holiness if God commands the father to kill the son.
Therefore, on Cameron's position it would be as repugnant for God to command
Abraham to kill his son as for God himself to hate himself, and the command,
“Abraham, sacrifice your son Isaac to me,” was impious, repugnant to the divine
nature, and contrary to GOD's natural and immutable propensity. But the
conclusion is absurd. Let Cameron see this; let the manuscript's author see
it. If the manuscript's author says that once it is posited and decreed that
this thing is God's good and holy image, then, because of the immutability of
the divine nature and constancy of God's decree, it is as impossible for God
not to love his image because it is his holy image as it is for God to deny or
hate himself—and that Cameron speaks in this sense—I answer: Once the
creation of the world, Judas's betrayal, Peter's walking on the sea, and those
things Cameron calls ἀδιάφορα, such as the Mosaic ceremonies, are decreed, in
this sense both Cameron's and the manuscript writer's distinction between
things prior and subsequent to God's free decree vanishes. So too does the
distinction between things good, evil, and indifferent in themselves and their
own nature. For once God's decree is fixed, Cameron must say that, just as GOD
inclines toward love of himself with a natural and immutable propensity, with
an entirely similar affection—that is, a natural and immutable propensity—
GOD inclines toward the creation of the world, Peter's walking upon the sea,
all the Mosaic ceremonies, the roar of lions, the bleating of sheep, and the
fictional discourses of the Pythagoreans. For, once the decree concerning
their future existence has been fixed, it is repugnant to divine immutability
for God not to will them. To what end, therefore, these utterly empty
distinctions?

13. I proved that GOD does not love his image by a natural propensity, because
for a father to cherish his son and provide for his life is a part of God's
image. Yet God rightly commanded Abraham to kill his son and the Israelites
not to repay the Egyptians' loan, and so forth. Therefore God can refrain from
willing something which is a part of his image; he does not embrace his image
with a natural propensity of the sort by which GOD inclines toward love of
himself, as Cameron says. The manuscript's author answers the first point:
*“Unless Rutherford proves that GOD willed and loved Abraham to put aside all
natural love and stain cruel hands with the blood of his offspring-*

[Chapter XXII; printed page 320; IA PDF page 380; BSB canvas 372]

*, he has used this example in an unsuccessful attempt. God certainly neither
willed nor could will Abraham to efface no small part of GOD's image and be
cruel to his son; he willed only that Abraham should by that deed declare that
he loved God before all things.”* I answer that in an unsuccessful attempt the
manuscript's author states my argument badly. I did not say that God willed
Abraham, after putting aside all natural love, to stain cruel hands with the
blood of his offspring, because if Abraham had laid hands upon him at God's
command and God had not withdrawn the command, those hands would have ceased
to be cruel; indeed, they would have been just and obedient. But this confirms
my argument successfully, because God's command thus makes cruel hands not
cruel but innocent and just. Had those hands killed the son without any
command of God, they would have been utterly opposed to God's image and like
the hands of the Gentiles and idolaters, who sacrificed their children to
demons, not indeed from natural hatred but from an insane zeal for religion.
Thus God's command caused the killing of Isaac—which without that command
would have been a horrendous killing of one's child—to be no cruelty but a
part of God's image. Therefore I say that the father's external act of
preserving his son's life and not killing him is in itself a part of the
divine image and a most holy act of the law of nature engraved on every heart
by plainly divine authority; yet that same act of not killing his son, when
GOD, the author of life and death, commands it, is horrendous rebellion. Thus
the act of killing the son when God commands it is a part of the divine image.
My argument therefore clearly establishes that the same act passes from an
act of homicide to an act of most religious obedience according as God has or
has not commanded it. It also establishes that it is utterly false that God
loves what is his image *“by a natural and immutable propensity and with an
entirely similar affection by which GOD inclines toward love of himself,”* a
blasphemy which Cameron maintains. For God can pass from loving himself to not
loving himself on no command and no supposition except one involving an
everlasting contradiction. He cannot fail to love himself unless he ceases to
be God; but he can refrain from willing or even command that which without
that command would have been part of his image.

[Marginal references: Deuteronomy 12:31; Deuteronomy 32:17; Jeremiah 7:31.]

[Marginal summary: Abraham's killing his son when God commanded it ceased to
be a part of God's image.]

14. To the other point the manuscript's author answers: *“The question is
whether, after GOD has decreed that repayment of a loan is a part of his image,
he can decree the contrary—namely, that non-repayment is a part of GOD's image
and love it. GOD cannot do this.”* He adds that the Egyptians were unjust
possessors of the gold and silver-*

[Chapter XXII; printed page 321; IA PDF page 381; BSB canvas 373]

*and that these things were not theirs but GOD's; and that the Israelites
improperly received them as a loan and therefore were not obligated to return
them. Thus GOD does not will or love non-repayment of a loan.* I answer: You
invent a chimerical question. Did I say that after GOD willed and decreed the
repayment of a loan to be a part of his image, he then willed it not to be a
part of his image? This never entered my mind even in a dream. Rather, as
before, so I still say that the Israelites' repayment of the loan to the
Egyptians would have been a part of the divine image and right and just in
itself, as is *rendering to each person his own,* unless the Legislator himself
had commanded non-repayment. For if we receive gold as a loan from the most
abandoned slaves of the devil and the most unjust possessors of their own
property, and return it on the appointed day, we observe the law of nature
holy in this. If we refuse to return it, we sin most gravely against the law
of nature and the remnants of the divine image. The same reason obligated the
Israelites unless JEHOVAH commanded non-repayment. Therefore GOD's command
makes that non-repayment—which without that command would have been a sin
repugnant to GOD's image—a part of GOD's image and obedience. *GOD therefore
does not will that which is a part of his image by that natural propensity by
which he inclines toward love of himself,* but can rightly will that this
repayment of a loan not be a part of his image. Nor does it matter that *the
gold was not the Egyptians' but GOD's,* because it truly would have been the
Egyptians' by civil right, which may not be violated unless God commanded
non-repayment. Yet by no right short of the loudest contradiction *can GOD not
love himself.* But after God commands that the gold not be returned, the
non-repayment, by virtue of that command, ceases to be sin or contrary to
GOD's image; it ceases to be the non-repayment of a loan or the non-restoration
of another's property and begins to be the religious preservation of one's
own proper good and part of GOD's image. In this most successful attempt the
unwitting manuscript author establishes my argument and attacks Cameron rather
than vindicating him.

15. I attacked Cameron's distinction *between good things which are commanded,
evil things which are forbidden, and indifferent things which are neither
commanded nor forbidden,* not absolutely but in this question, because all
beings and all acts are morally ἀδιάφορα—indifferent—in themselves. The
manuscript author rejoins: *“Well said: things considered nakedly in themselves are good,
because being and good are convertible.”* But because they are entitatively
good, they are morally indifferent, neither good nor evil in themselves, and
from GOD's command or prohibition become formally morally good or evil-*

[Chapter XXII; printed page 322; IA PDF page 382; BSB canvas 374]

*. The manuscript author adds: *“Worshiping GOD is good in itself, in its
internal principle and proper form, because GOD decreed the nature of
worshiping GOD to be good in itself. Idolatry is evil in itself, not by the
extrinsic character of a command, but in its internal principle. It is from
GOD's free decree that the nature of idolatry is evil in itself. The Mosaic
ceremonies are ἀδιάφορα because by GOD's decree they have no character of
moral good or evil in their nature, but are merely extrinsically good or evil
by reason of the command. GOD cannot prohibit the first; he cannot command the
second—for example, hatred of himself; those which belong to the third kind,
GOD can command or forbid.”*

I answer: I do not deny that God, in freely willing the existence of a rational
creature, simultaneously and freely wills both that it love GOD and that the
creature be subject to the Creator. I speak of moral or obediential
subjection, not the natural subjection of dependence. But this is a
co-volition—if I may be permitted to speak this way—so that the subject and
the subject's natural property exist simultaneously, as I will say later.

I answer first: No one who has even greeted philosophy or theology from the

it sins. Such a reason is not in GOD with respect to subsequent things willed,
