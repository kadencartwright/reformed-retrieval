VERSE 7. All rivers hasten to the sea, yet the sea is not filled; to whatever
place the rivers hasten, to the same place they return swiftly.

The fourth and final argument is drawn from water. The sea is not full when
rivers and streams pour themselves into it, both because a great quantity of
the sea's waters is drawn into the air by the heat of the sun, and because the
sea in turn pours back the water that it received from rivers and little
streams. From all these things it is established (as it is in Psalm 39:6) that
man is the vainest vanity, even when he is most firmly established and fixed.
Likewise, every man is only vanity, both the man born among the common people
and the man born of nobility. If placed in the scales together with vanity, he
would rise in comparison with vanity itself. May all these things instruct us
in the greatest humility of mind; may they also stir us to hope for a better
life, since we are surpassed by most creatures in the duration of this life.
Since we have so many and such constant teachers of this fact that, wherever
we turn our eyes—upward, downward, to the right, and to the left—this vanity is
always represented to us, we must be dull or stupid if we make no progress
under so many and such constant teachers. Yet these things must not be
understood as if God had not at times changed this governance of the world by
his mighty arm. For men have been found who, when their sun set in death,
returned in resurrection to what was, as it were, its rising. And there was a
time when the sun's swiftest and most strenuous course was slowed in the ages
of Joshua and Hezekiah. Joshua 10:13; 2 Kings 20:11.

*Translator's note:* The edition prints the singular construction `Sol morte
occidens` ("the sun setting in death") with the plural verb `recurrerunt`
("they returned"). The translation preserves the plural reference to the men
while rendering the solar metaphor without emending the Latin transcription.
