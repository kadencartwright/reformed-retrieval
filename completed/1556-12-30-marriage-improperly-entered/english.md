# On a Marriage Improperly Entered

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
[`latin.txt`](latin.txt). The printed-page markers correspond to the 1871
*Calvini Opera* witness.

**[CO X/1, p. 242]**

Although amid so great a number of people seeking advice there is not always
time to comply with everyone's wishes, I am nevertheless not so severe that I
take offense at their godly concerns. Your explanation concerning your letter
was entirely true. For after I had looked over it hurriedly and in haste, I
intended to read it again a little more attentively; but, something that very
rarely or never happens to me, I lost it. I thought it rash to answer until I
had learned better not only what you were asking, but what knots you wished to
have untied for you.

All good people rightly condemn clandestine marriages, which furnish occasion
for—and indeed open the door to—many disgraceful acts. Would that this man,
whoever he is, had taken another woman as his wife rather than his cousin.
Although that has always been permitted both by the law of Moses and by the
common law of nations, Paul's warning must be observed: believers should not
allow themselves more than is beneficial (1 Corinthians 6:12). Certainly that
degree of kinship is still excepted in marriages among us, lest Christ's
gospel be exposed to reproaches. But while these people indulge the desire of
their flesh too much, they

**[CO X/1, p. 243]**

prefer to incur the disgrace of fornication rather than acknowledge the
marriage. Yet if it was necessary to choose one of two evils, it would have
been better to purchase freedom with money than to plunge themselves into
this labyrinth. What followed was that the woman, as soon as aversion to her
husband crept in, disregarded her pledge and obtained a divorce; with equal
fickleness the husband, without the case being examined, flew into a new
marriage. Both deserve severe correction, and neither deserves that so much
labor be spent investigating this perplexing case for their sake.

For what resolution can now be found? By casting off her husband's yoke, the
wife violated the pledge she had given. This voluntary divorce therefore came
close to the character of adultery, whether peevishness or lust drove her to
it. The husband, however, since the matter still remained intact despite
attempts at reconciliation—indeed repeated but unsuccessful attempts—ought to
have undergone any danger to compel her to perform her duty rather than both
break the sacred and indissoluble bond by private judgment and assume the role
of judge in his own case. Thus bringing in the second wife is nothing other
than sheer adultery veiled by the honorable title of marriage. He was not
freed by the first wife's departure until it had been lawfully determined
that she had forfeited her right. He therefore deceived the second woman, to
whom, as though free, he pledged fidelity that was already bound elsewhere.
Nor does the first woman possess any greater freedom, for she furnished the
occasion for adultery. Her remaining silent

**[CO X/1, p. 244]**

indeed, when she saw an adulterous marriage being arranged, was nothing other
than giving assent. Surely she is held guilty of the foulest pandering, since
she willingly and knowingly allowed her husband to prostitute himself in this
way.

You will ask, then, what resolution there is. Unless people are to be allowed
to acquire a greater license to sin by sinning, each remains bound by the
first pledge. No other way of resolution appears except that the man
acknowledge that he became an adulterer when he improperly passed to the
second wife, and that the woman likewise acknowledge herself an accomplice in
the crime—indeed, that the first origin of the fault rests with her.

Now, if he is unwilling to return to the former wife, there will be no small
suspicion of collusion, and the affair will set an evil and disgraceful
example. I do not see that the woman who rashly obtained the divorce has a
free right to marry until she has offered herself for reconciliation. What is
the man to do? Unless I am badly mistaken, he must remain caught in the
perplexity. I therefore cannot find what judgment to pronounce without
understanding the circumstances better. I ask you, excellent man, to pardon
my uncertainty if I do not dare render judgment on so confused a matter among
people unknown to me. Perhaps you expected something more from me; kindly
accept what I am able to give.

Farewell, my honored brother. May the Lord always be with you, govern you by a
spirit of prudence and uprightness, protect you by his defense, and sustain
you by his power. The third day before the Kalends of January 1557 [30
December 1556].

## Translation notes

1. *Consobrina* identifies a female cousin but does not by itself determine a
   modern jurisdiction's precise degree terminology. Calvin says the union was
   biblically lawful while explaining why Genevan practice still excluded it.
2. *Libertatem pecunia redimere* means purchasing or securing freedom by a
   payment. The missing case history leaves the precise civil mechanism
   unstated.
3. *Marito permisit se ita prostituere* is syntactically compressed. In
   context, the most coherent referent of *se* is the husband as the logical
   subject permitted to act: the first wife knowingly let him “prostitute
   himself” by contracting the second union. The alternative grammatical
   attachment would make the wife the object of the prostitution.
4. CO prints *3 Calendas Ianuarii 1557*. By inclusive Roman reckoning this is
   30 December 1556 in the modern calendar; the repository slug uses that
   resolved date while retaining the printed form in the text.
