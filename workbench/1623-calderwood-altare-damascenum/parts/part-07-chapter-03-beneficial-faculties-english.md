2. Likewise, to abolish simoniacal canvassing whenever it occurs.

Simony is defined by the canonists as a deliberate intention to buy or sell
something spiritual or annexed to a spiritual thing. Benefices are annexed to
spiritual things, and therefore simony can be committed in benefices too. To
portray the simony of the English church as leprosy roaming through the whole
body of ecclesiastics, I shall transcribe from Brightman's commentary on
Revelation:

*But first of all its shameless begging is remarkable. Run through the entire
clergy with your eyes and minds. Do you wish us to begin from the lowest? The
men they call parish priests are beggars both in reality and in everyone's
judgment. In them one may see what had been foretold of Eli's household: men
bowing down for a silver coin and a morsel of bread and asking to be joined to
the priesthoods by anointing, so that they might eat a scrap of bread (1 Samuel
2:36). Among the rest, who walk more confidently because supported by greater
abundance, what running about, what bribery, what importunate begging! What
flatteries of total compliance and fawning in every duty, so that they may
obtain ecclesiastical offices for themselves! Many fly to the royal court or
to the house of that most noble man, the Lord Keeper of the Great Seal. These
places are like the Beautiful Gate of Solomon's temple: crowds of men enter by
it, and there is great hope of carrying off alms (Acts 3). Others pursue
magnates and nobles, becoming either their household chaplains or, so to
speak, their clients. For what reason, I ask? So that as soon as some
“benefice,” as they call it, happens to fall vacant, they may enjoy it as the
gift of their own lords. And this seems the honorable way to obtain an
ecclesiastical province.—The remaining crowd in the countryside courts the
common run of patrons, lying at their doors, flattering their wives, fawning
upon their children, cultivating their servants, and everywhere presenting
the miserable appearance of beggars. Some act more craftily. Just as those who
sit at crossroads and on public roads offer travelers peeled switches in
order to obtain a coin, so these men open an approach for themselves either by
a gift of money in hand or by an agreement for a fixed payment. But let us go
on. Once the thing has been begged from a patron, how much business remains
for the more ignorant crowd before institution is obtained from the bishop!*

[Marginalia: Brightman, *On Revelation*, chapter 3, verse 17.]

[1623 printed p. 95 | IA leaf 107 | PDF p. 107]

*Here supplication must be made not only to the supreme lord, but to his bedchamber examiners, notaries, stewards,
cooks, and every lowest servant. Ignorance will not bar anyone from entrance;
rather, greater profit usually comes to the servants when someone needs more
favor. No fortress is so well defended that an ass loaded with gold does not
capture it, and scarcely anyone is so inept as to suffer rejection. By what
engines they accomplish it, let them see to that. The same holds for
prebendaries, archdeacons, and deans. Are the bishops themselves free from
this filth? What, then, is the purpose of that constant courting of the court
and the magnates? Why do they not wait until they are summoned? Why are they
not dragged unwilling from the Muses? Indeed, if anyone appealed to their
consciences, would the rich estates of their bishoprics have been leased
spontaneously to eager tenants, or were gifts promised to the brokers of their
dignity? But are they beggars only in seeking office? Some have advanced so
far in beggary that, if their see is to be changed, they do not pay the first
fruits until they have shamefully scraped together an “alms” from poor rectors
under the name of a goodwill payment. Therefore, O English angel, you are a
beggar. And so it is customary with you that the best are passed over and
others less worthy are placed in the better position. For just as we give
something to the blind, lame, and ulcerous, but stop our ears against those
whom we see to have a healthier body and more respectable dress, so men of
blind mind, misshapen and maimed in learning and ability, collect a great many
alms, while those who excel more in learning and judgment are allowed to
hunger and die of starvation—whether because they cannot shout so loudly,
because they are more ashamed to beg, or because people pity them less, and
so forth.*

I could also bring forward Cartwright and other witnesses against the
simoniacal canvassing of bishops and ministers of lower rank, but Brightman's
testimony is so explicit that there is no need of others. Since the price in
simony is a gift either from the hand, from the tongue, or from service, they
obtain orders and benefices for themselves not only by money paid or promised,
intercession, entreaties, and flattery, but also by temporal or spiritual
service. For not only bishops' chaplains but even their attendant servants are
advanced to sacred orders and benefices. The author of the *Petition to the
Queen* is my witness that English bishops establish attendant servants and
other baser and indolent men in the sacred order of ministers. Although no
express bargain has preceded, both chaplains and others bind themselves to
bishops and render service in this hope. Simony is committed not only by the
giver and recipient, but also by the one dispensing; the dispensation of
simoniacal canvassing itself does not occur without simony. The same holds for
the other dispensations concerning irregularity, impediments to marriage,
nonresidence, plurality of benefices, eating meat, and so forth. In
dispensations the equity of distributive justice must be preserved, and a
dispensation must be denied or granted in accordance with the reason for the
case. When that equity is removed, ecclesiastical discipline is corrupted and
enervated. For a dispensation is either sought for a legitimate reason, and
then it is owed by justice and no

price can rightly be demanded, or the reason is not legitimate, and then it
is shamefully sold. If they say
dispensations are odious and therefore must be made burdensome to petitioners,
lest excessive license be given to seek dispensations too importunately, we
answer that if they are sought for a just and legitimate reason they are not
odious; if not for a just reason, they are deservedly odious and therefore
ought to be denied, not granted with a burden upon the petitioner and profit
to the dispenser. We say nothing here about the officials' wages, the seal,
paper, rescripts and certificates, or the expenses paid for them, but only
about the profit that goes to the dispenser's advantage. Therefore nothing is
left for them to allege except what the Roman pontiffs allege: that something
must be exacted to sustain the burdens of the dispenser's dignity. In that
way sermons, the administration of sacraments, and whatever is most sacred in
the church can be put up for sale under the guise of a stipend rather than by
the name of a price. Yet it is certain that a stipend is given on account of
the person, because the laborer is worthy of his hire; if he demands anything
further for each individual act of his office, whether an act of order or of
jurisdiction, he commits simony. The burdens of the dignity ought to be
sustained by everyone, not by individuals in each individual act.

But to return to the first matter, simoniacal canvassing both for an order and
for a benefice: the archbishop is said here to be able to remove and abolish
it and to dispense from all its penalties—major excommunication, suspension,
deposition, irregularity, and the intervening infamy. Thus, if the canons and
ecclesiastical constitutions are too hard and rigid, simoniacs have here a
ready remedy for softening their severity, by which they can cure their
sorrow. We shall speak later about the shameful profit from leasing
ecclesiastical jurisdiction to officials and chancellors, and likewise about
what they call fees for letters of orders. I add only Emperor Anthemius's
splendid constitution:

*If it happens, with God as author, that anyone is advanced to the rank of
bishop in this royal city or in the other provinces spread throughout the
whole world, let him be brought forward by the pure minds of men, the bare
conscience of election, and the sincere judgment of all. Let no one purchase
the priestly rank by selling a price; let each be assessed for how much he
deserves, not how much he is able to give. For truly, what place will be safe
and what cause can be excused if God's venerable temples are captured by
money? What wall of integrity or rampart of faith shall we provide if the
cursed hunger for gold crawls through the venerable inner chambers? Finally,
what can be guarded or secure if incorrupt holiness is corrupted? Let the
profane fire of greed cease to threaten the altars, and let the accursed crime
be driven from the sacred shrines. Let a bishop in our times be chosen so
chaste and humble that wherever he goes, he purifies everything by the
integrity of his own life. Let a bishop be ordained not for a price but by
entreaties. He ought to be so far removed from canvassing that when sought he
must be compelled, when asked he withdraws, and when invited he flees. Only
the necessity of making excuses should support him. For he is truly unworthy
of the priesthood unless ordained*

[1623 printed p. 97 | IA leaf 109 | PDF p. 109]

*against his will. Indeed, if anyone is discovered to have entered this holy
and venerable episcopal seat through the intervention of money, or if anyone
accepted something to ordain or elect another, let him be withdrawn from the
priestly rank as though charged with a public crime and treason. We decree
that he not only be deprived of this honor henceforth but also condemned to
perpetual infamy, so that one similar penalty may attend both men whom an equal
crime defiles and makes equal.*

3. To grant a vacant benefice in trust (which they call *in commendam*) for a
time or during life.

When the care and administration of a church is entrusted to someone, and the
fruits of the benefice, or at least a part, are assigned for this care, while
the benefice itself is not conferred as a title, it is called a *commendam*.
The commendatory is not a prelate or the church's proper rector, but only a
kind of agent or governor. Commendams were initially introduced for the
benefit of a vacant church, so that while deprived of its proper rector it
would not meanwhile be deprived of all government. But they were temporary
only and were limited to a period of six months, as stated in the Sixth Book
of Decretals:

*Henceforth let no one presume to commend a parish church to someone who has
not attained lawful age and the priesthood, nor even to such a man more than
one church, and that only when evident necessity or the church's own advantage
recommends it. We declare that a commendam of this kind, duly made as stated,
shall not last beyond a period of six months. We decree that whatever is done
otherwise concerning commendams of parish churches is null by the law itself.*

But now, rather for the commendatory's benefit, not only parish churches and
lesser benefices but also major benefices and bishoprics are granted for
several years or in perpetuity. For that reason, as someone wittily said, they
ought to be called not *commendams* but *things to be eaten*. *Richard Cheyney,
consecrated on April 19, 1562, presided over the church of Gloucester and at
the same time the church of Bristol (which he governed in commendam by the
queen's dispensation for more than sixteen years),* as Godwin attests. *So too
John Bullingham, likewise bishop of Gloucester, administered the church of
Bristol by dispensation until 1589, when it pleased the queen to give the
people of Bristol their own bishop,* according to the same Godwin. At the
Council of Chalcedon it was decreed that *a cleric ought not to be enrolled at
the same time in the churches of two cities.* Gratian, however, adds: *But, to
the contrary, sometimes the bishop of one city is made archbishop of another
city yet does not abandon his former church; rather, the former church is left
as if commended, while the second is held as if under title.* A corrupt gloss!
It is the same as if he had said that, although one man may not have two wives,
he can have one under the title of wife and the other under the title of
concubine. This is one of the devices by which they can enlarge and expand
their bishoprics if they happen to be rather poor, although

591–592; Cause 21, question 1, chapter *A Cleric*.]

commendams are also granted where a bishopric's revenues are not meager, as we said of
Gloucester and Bristol. A second device is the trust and commendam of lesser
benefices. A third is the union of other benefices to the episcopal table. In
our own church we have known bishops who, when advanced from a lesser benefice
to a greater one, took the lesser benefice with them and united it to the
bishopric in order to augment the revenues of the greater benefice. They did
this by royal authority without the church's consent, as they do nearly
everything else. Yet the Tridentine fathers were sounder, for they decreed
that the bishopric's poverty must first be judged in a synod:

*Because many cathedral churches have revenues so meager and circumstances so
straitened that they in no way correspond to episcopal dignity or suffice for
the churches' necessity, let the provincial council, having summoned those
concerned, examine and carefully consider which churches should be united to
one another because of their straits and poverty or increased with new
revenues. Let the records drawn up about these matters be sent to the supreme
Roman pontiff; instructed by them, the supreme pontiff, in accordance with
what his prudence judges expedient, may either unite poor churches to one
another or increase them by some addition from the revenues.*

I do not want anyone to infer from what has been said that English bishoprics
are rather poor in revenue. For bishops, nobles, and magnates surpass most
other men in their annual income. If perhaps two or three, such as Llandaff
and Carlisle, have rather meager revenues arising from the bishopric itself,
it must be attributed not to the reality but to the bishops' insatiable greed,
for they can never be satisfied even by the greatest wealth. Nothing is too
much for buttressing this episcopal dignity with worldly pomp and splendor.
Moreover, they enrich the bishopric's revenues with the spoils of lesser
churches, and so remedy its poverty. We said more about commendams in Chapter
I.

4. That a son may immediately succeed to his father's benefice.

A constitution of John Peckham, archbishop of Canterbury, appears in Lyndwood
in these words:

*Since it is forbidden by law that without an apostolic dispensation sons of
rectors or presbyters be appointed in churches in which their fathers served
immediately, or most recently, and it is known that those benefices are vacant:
if anything contrary has been attempted in fact, we command that the prelates
carefully inquire about churches vacant in this manner and not delay to make
provision concerning them according to the requirement of law, taking more
careful precaution hereafter that they do not admit such men to benefices of
this kind under any title, lest contrary to justice an opportunity for
succession to the Crucified One's inheritance be opened through
surreptitious dealing.*

“Immediately” means that no intervening person served there, for an
intervening person would purge the defect, Lyndwood says in the same place. He
draws this from a papal rescript to the archbishop of Canterbury:

*We have learned from the complaint submitted by the cleric G. that you
delayed receiving him at present into a certain benefice*

[Marginalia: *Provincial*, book 1, title *On the Sons of Presbyters*;
*Decretals*, book 1, title 17.]

[1623 printed p. 99 | IA leaf 111 | PDF p. 111]

*of the church of N. upon the presentation of the abbot of Castra, because his
father served in the aforesaid church. After his death an intervening person
held it, to whom the said benefice was conferred. We therefore command you, if
this is so, not to postpone admitting the aforesaid William to the said
benefice, while no less observing the decree of the apostolic rescript which
detests hereditary succession in the church of God.*

A presbyter's son was born either from a condemned and illegitimate union, or
from a legitimate one—that is, according to the canonists, from a lawful wife
before the reception of orders. One born from a condemned union was held back
from being advanced to his father's benefice by two canonical impediments: (1)
he was illegitimate, and (2) he was a priest's son. We spoke of the former
case above when we dealt with irregularity. As for the latter, provision must
indeed be made that no one dare claim his father's benefice as hereditary, or
on the ground that his father possessed it immediately or mediately. But we
judge it unjust that he cannot be advanced simply and without qualification
when the church elects and asks for him—or, as they wish, when the patron
presents him—and when no other canonical impediment stands in the way.
Therefore no dispensation is needed here, whether they were born before or
after their fathers received orders.

5. That for some period of time (for some weightier reason) a beneficiary is
not bound to reside, but may serve through another suitable person.

If anyone counted the beneficiaries who do not reside in the English church,
foreigners might perhaps not believe the number. The chaplains of the king,
the prince, the nobles, the bishops, and others would alone make up a great
crowd. They are clothed in soft garments and live softly in the courts of
kings, princes, nobles, and bishops from the revenues of a parish church's
benefice, while the parishes are deprived of spiritual food. In a sermon
delivered before Edward VI, Hooper said: *Your Majesty must begin with your
chapel and chaplains and compel them to serve those souls who labor for their
living. If Your Majesty does not do this, your soul is in danger.* Lever,
before the same Edward, said: *My lords, both lay and clerical, in God's name I
warn you to observe that when the Lord of lords sees his flock scattered and
lost and seeks out its blood, he will find in this court and in your households
those thieves who slaughter and destroy Christ's flock, whom you foster.*

The courts of the prince, nobles, and bishops are thus dens of these robbers.
So too the universities of Oxford and Cambridge are hiding places for the
robbers who do not reside. There those called fellows in theological colleges
enjoy the benefices of parishes while the parishes are neglected. Cathedral
churches too are dens of idle, lank fellows, as the authors of the admonition

[1623 printed p. 100 | IA leaf 112 | PDF p. 112]

to Parliament report. Certain men insufficiently fitted for ministry, even after
they have received orders and some benefice has been conferred, are permitted
to remain at the university for three years, while the wretched churches drift
through all three years in the midst of the sea without a pilot, as Travers
reports. *Why, then,* he asks, *do these men become wise too late,
and why do they not send them to the university to learn before admitting
them to teach? Or what perversity of Epimethean intelligence is this, that
they appoint to teach a man who must learn in the teacher's chair?* The
papists themselves are ashamed of this corruption. *The residence of parish
priests is now much more strictly enforced,* says Zypaeus, *than before the
Council of Trent. For the sake of studies, they could be absent for seven
years with the ordinary's permission. Today ordinaries cannot give such a
license, nor can absent parish priests receive the revenues. For since they
are now conferred by competition, it is presupposed that the man elected as
more suitable is sufficiently learned.*

There are others who are not fixed stars but wandering stars, roaming the
provinces, to whom the archbishop grants this faculty of nonresidence. The
archbishop dares what the archangels themselves do not dare: he does not so
much give as sell prerogatives of nonresidence. The rule always held that
those engaged in sacred service should not depart from the place of their
priesthood. “Exile holds some men to one place, priesthoods others,” says
Seneca in *On Tranquillity of Life*. This is why Solomon took care that
dwellings be built around the temple (1 Kings 6), and the apostle says in 1
Corinthians 9:13 that those who attend the altar share with the altar. The
word προσεδρεύειν signifies that this office demanded perpetual and constant
care, so that the priests could not be free to seek their living in another
way, as Beza notes.

Pastors are watchmen; the church is a besieged city. There is no peace and no
truce. The faithful are sheep of the Lord's flock; the minister is shepherd
of the sheep. He leads them to pleasant pastures and, so far as lies in him,
points out the wolf and urges on the dogs. Woe to the shepherd who eats the
fat and clothes himself with the wool, slaughters what is well-fed but does
not feed the flock, does not strengthen the afflicted, heal the sick, bind up
the broken, bring back the scattered, or seek the lost (Ezekiel 34:6). Christ
said to all pastors in the person of Peter, “Feed, feed, feed,” to commend
constancy to them. “Either you ought never to have undertaken to guard the
Lord's flock,” Bernard says, “or, having undertaken it, you ought never to
abandon it.” When Pope Innocent asked him to come to him, he answered that he
was plainly nursing little children and therefore could not do so without
danger to them. When Paul took leave of the

letter 38, letter 153.]

Ephesian presbyters in Acts 20:28,
he exhorted them to watch over themselves and the flock in which the Holy
Spirit had appointed them bishops to feed God's church. Peter gives the same
admonition in another form in 1 Peter 5: “Feed God's flock which is among you
(or depends upon you), devoting yourselves to its oversight, ἐπισκοποῦντες,”
to show that the name “bishop” is not empty but a name of work and labor, not

If anyone ran through all a pastor's duties—the preaching of the Word,
administration of the sacraments, public and private admonitions, consolation
of the anxious, ready resolution of cases of conscience, and an exemplary
life—he would admit that a pastor cannot abandon the flock without danger.
“For who doubts,” as that man says, “that a doorkeeper must remain at the door,
a steward at the estate, a ship's pilot at the helm, a teacher at the school,
and others at their own duties?” as stated at the Council of Bologna in 1586.
A pastor must discharge all these duties in his single office—the duties of a
doorkeeper, steward, pilot, and teacher. Therefore he must reside.

Paul commands them to fulfill their ministry. *They know that they can in no
way fulfill it if they desert the flocks committed to them after the manner
of hirelings and do not at all attend to the care of their sheep, whose blood
will be required from their hands by the supreme judge. For it is most certain
that a pastor's excuse is not admitted if the wolf ate the sheep and the
pastor did not know it,* as the Tridentine fathers themselves said. He must be
a rule and example to others in integrity of conduct and piety. But if the
rule is far removed from what it regulates, how will they conform their
conduct to its example?

To demonstrate that the residence of bishops is of divine law, Cajetan argues
as follows. A bishop is bound by divine command to that which is necessary for
the end of a bishop, which is the care of souls. But a bishop's residence is
necessary for the care of souls. Therefore a bishop is bound by divine command
to reside personally in his bishopric. The major premise is self-evident and
is clear by induction. Anyone simply bound to do something is bound to the
means necessary for that action. A servant is bound to follow his master not
simply but on the supposition that the master has supplied him with a horse. A
soldier is bound to fight on the supposition that weapons have been provided
to him. But a bishop is bound to the care of souls not on a supposition but
absolutely and simply, and therefore he is bound to a means within his power
that is necessary for the care of souls. Nor is he merely bound to it; by the
same necessity by which he is bound to the end, he is bound to the means,
since the means is so necessary that without it he cannot attain the end.

The minor premise is proved: a bishop is bound to the care of souls by means
of these personal acts—teaching, administration of sacraments, ordinations,
visitation, guardianship, and diligence. Therefore a bishop's personal
residence is necessary for the care of souls. Hence Cajetan concludes that
*the many nonresident bishops raging in courts are not custom but abuse.*
When this question was debated at the Council of Trent in the time of Paul
III—whether the pontiff could release bishops from residence—most theologians
denied that he could, for residence was of divine law.

[Marginalia: session 6, decree on reformation 1; II-II, question 185, article
5.]

[1623 printed p. 102 | IA leaf 114 | PDF p. 114]

And therefore no one was permitted to release anyone from it. They held this
to be established by Leviticus 18, where the Lord commands the Levites: *Day
and night you shall remain in the tabernacle, observing the Lord's watches,
lest you die.* When he learned of this debate from his legates' letters, he at
once ordered them to postpone determination of the question to another time.
Meanwhile he sent a strong force of forty bishops as reinforcement so that,
by the number of votes in deciding that question, he would retain the power to
release from residence. This is what happened, as Gentiletus reports.
“Ambrosius Catharinus rose up against the most common and most true opinion,”
Spalato says, “and with his customary boldness reduced the obligation of
residence to positive ecclesiastical law alone in written pamphlets.”

What has been said about the residence of bishops must be adapted to the
residence of parish priests. The recent canons of 1603 decree as follows:

*Every dean, provost, warden, or principal rector of any cathedral or
collegiate church shall reside in the said cathedral or collegiate church for
at least ninety days in each year, continuously or at intervals, unless
detained by other weighty and urgent causes approved by the diocesan bishop or
dispensed for some other lawful reason.*

It will therefore be enough if he is present for ninety days. Since the decree
has an exception for legitimate business, a pretext will not be lacking, with
the bishop approving or the archbishop dispensing even in that three-month
period, to evade the decree's force. Again:

*No prebendary or canon of a cathedral or collegiate church who holds one or
more benefices with cure (and is not a residentiary in his cathedral or
collegiate church) shall be absent from his benefices with cure under the
pretext of his prebend for more than one month in any year, unless for an
urgent cause and for a definite period approved by the diocesan bishop.*

Residentiary prebendaries and canons *after completing the term of their
residence, defined by local statutes or the custom of their church, shall fly
back as soon as possible to their benefices, or to one of them, or at least to
some other cure where their presence is required by law, so that they may
perform the duties owed there.*

Who, then, will feed the famished parishioners during the period of residence
in a cathedral or collegiate church? And when he is released from that
residence, he must nevertheless be distracted by the care of several churches
if he has several benefices. As for the monthly or quarterly sermons of
nonresidents, they do not purge the offense of nonresidence. Jerome says that
bishops ought to remain without interruption in the Lord's tabernacle, so that
they either learn something from God or themselves teach the people by reading
the divine Scriptures and meditating on them frequently. In his work on the
Ten Commandments Babington acknowledges that the talent of a man who does not
preach every Sabbath is mute. They say the command of residence is
affirmative; affirmative commands, although always binding, do not bind at
every moment, as the scholastics say, but according to place and time. As to
place, there is no doubt that the pastor is bound to

[Marginalia: Gentiletus, *Examination*; session 23, decree 1; Spalato, book 2,
chapter 5, no. 14; canon 43; canon 44; distinction 36, chapter 3, *If Anyone*.]

[1623 printed p. 103 | IA leaf 115 | PDF p. 115]

his own parish or church. As to time, judgment must be made from pastoral acts and
duties. Some have a determined and fixed time, such as the Lord's Day and the
stated times for preaching and celebrating the Supper. Some have an
undetermined but more frequently recurring time, so that there is no
exception of time, as with the act of feeding Christ's flock. “A pastor must
be so constant in pastoral acts,” Cajetan says, “that he feeds the sheep as if
continuously.” He says that the acts stated above in Ezekiel are also required
of a pastor, and it is plain that they demand either continuous or very
frequent time. On their account a bishop is bound to reside continuously in
his bishopric, insofar as such frequency of time demands continuous residence,
both from the frequent exercise of acts of this kind and from preparation for
them.

*For although all time is not occupied in exercising a pastoral act, there is
nevertheless no exempt time in which the bishop ought not to be prepared to
exercise a pastoral act. Therefore, speaking absolutely, a bishop is bound by
command to continuous residence in his bishopric. Just as this affirmative
command has a determined place by the nature of the thing commanded—namely,
his own diocese—so it has a determined time—namely, constantly. And thus,
although affirmative commands formally bind always but not at every moment,
rather at the proper place and time, and in this differ formally from negative
commands, nevertheless this command, by reason of its subject matter, binds at
every moment, or constantly, which is almost always, and to a fixed place.*

They further allege the work of vicars. Canon 46 of the same constitutions of
1603 says:

*A beneficiary not admitted to preach shall procure that at least one sermon
each month be delivered in the church entrusted to him by a lawfully approved
preacher, provided that in the ordinary's judgment the value of the benefice
will permit it. And on each Lord's Day when there is no sermon in his church,
another beneficiary shall read, either personally or at least through his
curate, one of the homilies prescribed or to be prescribed by public
authority.*

Here a man unfit to preach is permitted to enjoy a benefice and to hire the
work of a mercenary preacher for a monthly sermon. The flock has plainly been
well provided for! The next canon says:

*Every beneficiary to whom, on account of the necessity of another office
incumbent upon him, a dispensation from residence in his benefice has been
granted by the laws of this realm shall provide for his cure through a curate
who is a suitable preacher and approved by sufficient authority, provided the
value of the benefice does not prevent it.*

It will prevent it if the bishop and beneficiary have reached a satisfactory
agreement; thus the parish will not even enjoy a hired mercenary. Damasus
wrote elegantly about the vicarious work of hirelings: *They seem to me,* he
says, *like prostitutes who, as soon as they give birth, hand their children
over to other nurses to be raised so that they can more quickly satisfy their
lust. So too these men hand their children—that is, the peoples entrusted to
them—over to others to be raised, so that they may satisfy their*

[Marginalia: canon 46; letter 53.]

[1623 printed p. 104 | IA leaf 116 | PDF p. 116]

*lusts. The Lord did not teach this way, nor did the apostles institute it.
Those who undertake the care must themselves perform it and present their own
sheaves to the Lord. For he himself diligently sought the lost sheep; he
himself found it; he himself carried it back on his own shoulders; and he
taught us to do the same.*

They put the ark of the Lord, which they should carry on their own shoulders,
upon a cart and draw it with cows. Indeed, the majority do not even hire a cart
and cows, as Cartwright reports. Those who employ the vicarious work of others
in discharging their office are condemned by Innocent III in these words: *We
also command that churches not be entrusted to hired presbyters and that each
church able to support one have its own priest.* In the third book of the
Decretals it was decreed that one who does not reside in his benefice, even a
benefice of modest value, be deprived of it. “A cleric who deserts his church
is not unlike a deserter from military service,” Duarenus says; “therefore it
has been established that he lose the belt of his honor and be punished for
that desertion.”

At a bishop's ordination it is said, “Go, and preach to God's people”; it is
not said, “Send someone to preach.” Peter was told, “Feed,” not, “Feed through
another.” Cajetan rightly says:

*In personal obligations a debtor is not discharged if he acts through
another. And when it is said, “The salvation of souls can be procured without
the bishop's personal care; therefore it does not necessitate him to personal
care,” the answer is that in moral matters possibility is not considered
according to logical power—that is, what does not imply a contradiction—but
according to the moral power of offices. It is evident from the Lord's
statement that a hireling's office does not extend to what is principal in a
pastor, namely, love of his own sheep, not another's. Therefore it is said
that, speaking absolutely, the salvation of souls so necessitates a pastor to
personal care that it cannot ordinarily be secured without the bishop's own
personal care, although by accident this might perhaps occur. But what is by
accident does not change the nature of human moral acts. Consequently, a
bishop is not excused from personal residence because he provides the church
with an excellent vicar.*

A hireling lightly and perfunctorily performs the parts of the office
prescribed by the beneficiary, not as one bound by love. He cares for another
man's flock, not his own, and for a paltry profit. It is asked whether in no
case it is permissible to be absent from the flock for a while, apart from the
need to recover health or attend to family affairs. “That which is called
continuous admits of interruption,” Lyndwood says. “Dripping water, though it
does not fall at every moment, has a continuous cause. A witness who testifies
that he saw Titius continuously possess something for ten years proves it
well. For it is not required that he saw him at every moment; it is enough
that he saw him enjoy it several times every year. Words are to be understood
in a civil manner.” Necessity arising from an impediment and the nature of the
business release from the bond of continuous residence. An impediment arises
from within or from without. External persecution sometimes demands a
pastor's flight. But the reason for absence or presence, even amid danger,
must be taken from the church's good.

[Marginalia: *Second Reply*; Cause 27, question 2, chapter 5; book 3, title 4,
chapter *Consequente*; *On Ministries*, book 8, chapter 5.]

[1623 printed p. 105 | IA leaf 117 | PDF p. 117]

An internal impediment, such as bodily illness, sometimes compels an invalid to
be absent from the flock to recover his health. The nature of the business
must also be weighed. Sometimes business that withdraws the pastor's presence
for a time tends toward his own flock's advantage and is a part of the
pastoral office. Sometimes the business concerns the benefit of associated
churches. “For the good of the part is ordered to the good of the whole as its
end,” Cajetan says. But the common business must be necessary.

*In a case in which the good of the whole is endangered, the parts are
ordered to the good of the whole even at the loss of their own natural
conditions. But when a case of necessity, or danger to the whole, is excluded,
the nature of the whole does not require a defect in some part, but strives to
preserve each part in its natural condition. Thus every particular church is
ordered to the good and service of the universal church absolutely, indeed,
while preserving the perfection of each church; but in a case of necessity it
is ordered to the good of the universal church even with loss to itself.*

He therefore approves that someone be sent as a delegate to a synod, but
utterly condemns that a man be a bishop and meanwhile reside at Rome as
secretary of the apostolic see or auditor of the Rota, *because the services
rendered at Rome in offices of this kind do not ordinarily demand by any
necessity that bishops be occupied in such offices; such men are in the church
of God like monsters in nature.* Thus when English bishops frequently attend
court, preside in the privy council, serve as judges in secular cases, or act
as the king's almoner, they are not performing an office necessary for the
salvation of the whole church. But we shall speak later about bishops' civil
jurisdiction.

In former times nonresidents were compelled to return to their churches by
withholding revenues; if they still delayed, they were fined by loss of the
benefice. *Fifth, it is certain that bishops and others having the care of
souls, if they are absent from their churches without legitimate cause, ought
to restore the revenues in proportion to their absence. The reason is that,
as stated in the rule of law, a benefice is given for the sake of an office.
Therefore, if anyone does not perform the required office, he receives its
revenues unjustly. If you say that satisfaction can be given to the people by
vicarious ministers and that therefore he ought not in such a case to return
the revenues against his will, the contrary is true: the people contribute
revenues not merely so that satisfaction may be given to them by spiritual
ministry in any fashion, but so that satisfaction may be given through the
office of their true and proper pastor when no legitimate cause demands
otherwise. For in this matter the people look to what is best and most useful
for them. The best method, and the method in itself most suited to promoting
the subjects' spiritual good, is that they be cared for by their own pastor,*
says Gregory of Valencia.

I do not care to linger longer over an obvious matter. What is needed here is
an orator rather than a dialectician to thunder against these murderers of
souls: against the chaplains; against those fellows who, like tortoises—as
someone said—dissolve and grow old in their colleges; against prebendaries who
hold benefices; and against all other nonresidents. The mouth of those who
tread out the grain

[Marginalia: tome 3, disputation 10, question 3, point 5.]

[1623 printed p. 106 | IA leaf 118 | PDF p. 118]

is muzzled; faithful pastors are thrust into prison; meanwhile asses or drones
are fed.

6. That a layman devoting himself to the Muses may retain a prebend, yet not
be compelled to enter the ministry.

We readily grant that prebends can be conferred for any other lawful use
rather than upon canons, subcanons, and other men of that kind. I therefore
will not anxiously inquire whether those prebends are annexed to canonries or
not.

7. That one initiated into sacred orders and otherwise suitable according to
the laws may retain two ecclesiastical benefices—benefices with cure within a
certain distance, and those without cure regardless of distance.

One has the care of souls, understood broadly, Lyndwood says, *whether he has
it in the penitential forum or otherwise; whether it is a cathedral or parish
church, or a chapel having care of parishioners by law or in fact, provided it
has a parish in which it exercises parochial right; and whether it is a
dignity, an office, or a church, as there are many archpresbyters, archdeacons,
and deans who have no church over which they preside but nevertheless have
jurisdiction over many churches.* Canisius writes in his *Summary of Canon
Law*: *Benefices with cure are said to be those which have the care of souls
annexed to them, and that in two ways: either because the beneficiary
administers the ecclesiastical sacraments to definite persons, namely those of
his parish, and proclaims God's Word; or because he exercises the things
belonging to spiritual jurisdiction, such as visiting, investigating,
suspending, excommunicating, and absolving. For these too concern the care of
souls. Benefices without cure are those to which no such care is annexed.*
Mucket says that dispensation is made for benefices with cure within a certain
distance, *so that he may more conveniently perform his office in both.*

The author of the *Petition to the Queen* relates that the English canons
forbade anyone to possess more than two benefices, and those no more than
twenty-six English miles apart, but that very many nevertheless possessed
three or four as far as a hundred miles apart. Cartwright reports that a
certain man possessed the headship or mastership of a college in one corner
of the realm, a deanery in another, and a prebend in a third. By the statute
of 21 Henry VIII, each of an archbishop's six chaplains, and likewise each of
a duke's, is permitted to possess two benefices with cure; each of the five
chaplains of a marquess and earl may possess two; and likewise each of the two
chaplains who serve the widows of dukes, marquesses, earls, and barons may
possess two. Each of the two chaplains of the king's secretary, treasurer,
county sheriff, keeper of the rolls or archives, dean of the royal chapel, and

[Marginalia: book 3, title *On Prebends*, chapter *Having Heard*; p. 344;
*Petition*, p. 91; *First Reply*, p. 46.]

[1623 printed p. 107 | IA leaf 119 | PDF p. 119]

royal almoner may possess two.

Ecclesiastics, or those called “spiritual lords,” who are members of the
king's council may possess three benefices with the care of souls. Chaplains
of the king, queen, either prince, any of the king's children, or the king's
brothers, sisters, paternal and maternal uncles, and paternal and maternal
aunts may each possess two benefices. Indeed, in the grievances presented to
the estates in 1610, it is said that the king's chaplains may possess as many
benefices as they please; some possess four at once in different counties,
some two, and nevertheless reside in none of them while they render service to
the lords whose chaplains they are. This, it says there, is certainly not to be
tolerated in a Christian commonwealth. The defender of the petition for
reformation reports an innumerable crowd of beneficiaries who possess two
benefices with cure and some, besides, two, three, or four ecclesiastical
dignities.

Thus, when they earlier enacted the constitutions of 1604, they did not act
seriously but in order to mock us with some appearance of reformation:

*Henceforth no faculty or dispensation for retaining several benefices with
cure at once shall be granted except to one who is judged worthier for his
learning, capable and suitable for performing his office more fully—namely,
one who has been advanced at least to the degree of master in one of our
universities, is a public and suitable preacher, and has been lawfully
approved. It shall always be provided that he is bound by sufficient security
to reside personally in each benefice for a substantial part of each year;
that benefices of this kind are no more than thirty miles apart; and finally,
that in the benefice in which he will not reside, he retain a duly approved
preacher who can usefully teach and instruct the people.*

Let us hear Brightman's censure of a similar canon made in 1597:

*What wrong have masters of arts done you, that you wish them above all others
to be bound by so great a crime? You admit that plurality is evil and must be
restrained, yet you indulge this pestilence in them. A splendid privilege of
their degree, indeed, by which it is especially lawful for them to be evil!
But perhaps it is enough for you to deceive men merely with the title of
restraining plurality. By God's grace, there are so many masters of arts that,
if all of them, hindered by no other religious consideration, used the liberty
of your constitution, I believe more parishes would lack pastors residing
with them than lack them today. Thus you prudently restrain the evil by
enlarging it.*

Meanwhile, reader, you see what distance between two benefices with cure is
lawful according to their canons: thirty miles. The interval between us and
the sun is longer than between us and the moon, yet it is equally impossible
to touch the moon and the sun with our hands. No one can serve two masters. We
spoke above of hired vicars' work. If those hirelings are fit for ministry,
why are they not themselves appointed pastors over those flocks? If they are
not fit, why is their work hired? But many do not even have

[Marginalia: p. 100; p. 132; canon 41; Brightman, *On Revelation*, chapter 3.]

[1623 printed p. 108 | IA leaf 120 | PDF p. 120]

hired hirelings.

The author of the booklet *On True and Genuine Religion* reports that among
pluralists there is no limit in number, location, or quantity. *There is no
limit in number,* he says, *because they possess not only two or three, as it
were, personal rectories, but add to them one prebend and then another in
several cathedral churches. Indeed, a man who is dean in one cathedral does
not disdain to sit as canon and prebendary in another cathedral's choir. These
things are done and practiced in the Roman Church, to which our churches are
unhappily similar.* Duarenus asserts that such deformation of the church has
arisen from this plurality that no power of human genius is sufficient to
express it. *Truly monstrous,* he says, *is this accumulation of benefices in
the church, which is received everywhere without discrimination. Men who
sustain so many persons in the church seem to me no less monstrous than the
Briareus of the fables who had a hundred hands, or Geryon, who is said to have
had three bodies.*

Calvin says nearly the same: *But I say both are monstrous crimes utterly
opposed to God, nature, and ecclesiastical government: that one robber lies
upon several churches at once, and that a man is called pastor who cannot be
present with his flock even if he wishes.* What Aquinas says of the plurality
of prebends can be affirmed with stronger reason about benefices with cure
properly so called. He says the plurality of prebends contains within itself
many disorders:

*For example, it is impossible for someone to serve in several churches in
which he is a prebendary, since prebends appear to be the stipends of those
ministering to God there. A diminution of divine worship also follows, when
one is installed in place of several. In some cases there also follows the
frustration of the testators' intentions, who gave these goods to churches so
that there would be a definite number of men serving God there. Inequality
also follows, when one man abounds in several benefices and another cannot
have even one. Many other things of this sort also follow.*

Sylvester has these words from Aquinas in his *Summary*. Gregory the Great
writes to bishops in this manner: *We command that each office of
ecclesiastical law be entrusted to a separate person.* Again: *The offices of
two matters must not be entrusted at one time to one person, however
experienced, because if the whole body is an eye, where is the hearing?*
Again: *Just as it is unbecoming in a human body for one member to perform
another's office, so it is surely harmful and at the same time most shameful
if the separate ministries of things are not distributed to the same number
of persons.* Pope Alexander II says, *No presbyter is to have two churches.*
The Seventh Synod forbids someone to be enrolled in two churches, saying in
canon 15: *From this time a cleric is not to be enrolled in two churches. For
this belongs to commerce and base gain and is wholly foreign to
ecclesiastical custom.* At the Lateran Council it was decreed that several
dignities or churches not be conferred upon one person. The words in the third
book of the Decretals are as follows: *Because some men, setting no limit to
greed, strive to accept diverse ecclesiastical dignities and several parish
churches*

[Marginalia: p. 124; *On the Sacred Ministries of the Church*, book 8,

Gratian, distinction 89, chapter 1; Cause 16, question 7, chapter *Through Lay
People*; Cause 21, question 1, chapter 1, *Cleric*; title 4, chapter 3,
*Because*.]

[1623 printed p. 109 | IA leaf 121 | PDF p. 121]

*contrary to the institutions of the sacred canons, although they are
scarcely able to fulfill one office while claiming for themselves the
stipends of very many, we strictly prohibit this from happening hereafter.
Thus, when a church or ecclesiastical ministry must be entrusted, let a person
be sought for it who can reside in the place and personally exercise its care.
If it has been done otherwise, let the man who received what he accepted
contrary to the sacred canons lose it, and let the giver be deprived of the
power to bestow.*

In the following title there is also another decree of Alexander III at the
Lateran Council:

*Because the ambition of some has advanced so far that they are reported to
have not two or three churches but more, although they cannot provide due care
even to two, we command through our brothers and fellow bishops that this be
corrected. We wish the lack of men able to serve churches to be relieved from
the multitude of prebends hostile to the canons, which gives occasion for
licentiousness and wandering and contains a certain danger to souls.*

Othobon, the pontiff's legate sent into England, writes thus in his legatine
constitutions: *The truth of the Christian religion has so abandoned many in
the pride of their hearts that, although each man is scarcely sufficient for
the care of his own soul, weighing themselves in a false balance, they
fearlessly undertake the care not merely of one benefice but of many.* At the
Council of Chalcedon it was decreed that a cleric not be enrolled in two
churches at once, and that anyone who acted otherwise fall from his own rank.
“Because ecclesiastical order is perverted and fuel is supplied to greed when
one man is permitted to occupy the offices of several clerics,” Molanus says,
“it was beneficially provided by the Council of Trent, following many sacred
canons, that a single benefice be conferred upon each minister.”

After plurality of benefices arose, all the best men always opposed it, and
there were those unwilling to prostitute their consciences to the polygamy of
churches. Yet the English are not ashamed to foster their dualists, trialists,
pluralists, and “as-many-as-they-please-ists”—one may adapt barbarous words to
barbarous customs—and to deprive pastors of blameless life of their single
benefice or stipend. For every pluralist and “as-many-as-he-pleases-ist” they
have, they have one most ardent defender of the episcopate and the other
remnants of the papacy. Some observe that there is no English bishop who did
not possess several benefices before taking up his bishopric and open a way to
the greater benefices, or bishoprics, through the revenues of lesser
benefices.

8. Mucket adds: *That a man prepared for sacred orders may receive the order
of diaconate and presbyterate at the same time and at a time not appointed.*

The recent constitutions of 1604 decreed as follows: *Since according to the
opinion of the ancient fathers and the practice of the primitive church the
office of deacon was established as a certain step toward the dignity of the
ministry, we decree and ordain that henceforth no bishop shall constitute a
person of any*

[Marginalia: title 5, chapter 5, *Because*; *On Institution and Collation*,
chapter 1; Chalcedon, chapter 10; *Practical Theology*, treatise 5, chapter 13;
*On Polity*, p. 309; canon 32.]

[1623 printed p. 110 | IA leaf 122 | PDF p. 122]

*condition whatever, however commended for gifts of mind, as
deacon and presbyter on one and the same day. Rather, let the rite prescribed
in that matter in the book on ordaining and inaugurating bishops, presbyters,
and deacons be strictly observed. We do not prohibit all deacons from access
to the presbyterate for an entire year when the bishop has otherwise found a
just cause to admit him. But since four times in each year have been appointed
for the ordination of deacons and presbyters, let at least some interval be
given to test each one, to see what sort of men they proved themselves in the
office of deacon before they are received into the order of presbyters.*

It is well that they do not defend their canon by divine law. The apostles did
not institute that no one be advanced to the presbyterate unless he first
served his apprenticeship in the diaconate. They are different functions and
ministries and have different gifts, neither of which includes the other. A
deacon is a minister of tables and widows; a pastor is a minister of discourse
and the Word. Someone may be equipped with gifts for discharging the diaconate
who will never be suited for the ministry of the Word. Thus, when the apostle
says that a deacon acquires a good standing for himself, he does not mean a
step toward the ministry or episcopate, but honor and dignity. When the
apostle commands Titus to appoint presbyters throughout the towns and cities,
were there beforehand deacons in those churches not yet constituted who were
being advanced to the rank of presbyters? Had all the Ephesian and Jerusalem
presbyters previously been deacons? Histories attest that laymen were
advanced to the episcopate, much less to the rank of presbyters. Yesterday
Ambrose was a catechumen; today he is a bishop. Severus was called from the
wool trade to the archiepiscopate.

If, then, a man can be made bishop from being a deacon while passing over the
presbyterate—a custom Photius reports existed in some eastern churches,
according to Spalato—can he not much more be made presbyter while passing over
the diaconate? But is it not sheer mockery that, although it is held a serious
offense to create a presbyter directly and by a leap from a layman while
passing over the diaconate, they first constitute him deacon, and he either
performs the title rather than the ministry of a deacon for a period of three
months, or by dispensation can receive both at once without observing the
intervals of time and before he has yet exercised himself in the lower
ministry? Thus in the papacy those who are going to ascend to the greater
orders take on the titles of the lower orders for pomp and appearance rather
than reality, without ever exercising their acts and offices. The English
prelates pursue these shadows and masks of the papacy. We shall speak below
about the time appointed for ordinations.

9. Generally, whereby the archbishop may dispense in all causes not contrary
to God's Word,

[1623 printed p. 111 | IA leaf 123 | PDF p. 123]

provided either that such dispensations were customarily obtained in the Roman
curia or, if they were not customarily obtained, that the prince himself or
those of his privy council have permitted it.

The causes in which the pope customarily dispensed are well known. If the
archbishop can grant the canons' favor in the same causes, he can dispense in
usury, perjury, degrees of consanguinity and affinity, and similar matters;
indeed, in more causes than the Roman pontiff customarily did, provided the
prince and the members of the privy council permit it. The addition “not
contrary to God's Word” helps little. The Roman pontiff has not rubbed his
forehead so bare that he openly asserts it is lawful for him to dispense in
causes contrary to God's Word. Although certain shameless canonists say the
pope can dispense in divine law, the sounder theologians deny it. They
maintain that he can dispense only in positive canon law and only declare and
interpret divine law. Under the guise of declaration and interpretation of
divine law, he really dispenses in causes contrary to God's Word. In the same
way the English primate can cover his dispensations with the veil of
declarations and interpretations.

Does not that English primate dispense in causes contrary to God's Word when
he can dispense concerning nonresidence, simony, plurality of benefices,
commendams, and irregularities of divine law? Let him go, then, and claim for
himself the same liberty in usury, perjury, and prohibited degrees. But I ask,
who constituted the Roman pontiff or the English primate a dispenser? Not
God's church. Therefore they are not lawful dispensers. All ecclesiastical
laws belonging to human law are subject to the power and rule of the churches
themselves. The churches can change or abrogate them and for the weightiest
reasons dispense from all of them, “especially in synods,” says Spalato, “and
the greater the synods, the greater the power they also have for this.” So
great a mass of ecclesiastical affairs ought not to depend upon the nod and
judgment of one Roman or English pontiff.

You see, reader, that the archbishop of Canterbury has been enriched and made
wealthy with the pope's spoils after papal rule was driven from England. For
this court of faculties and dispensations was established only after papal
authority was eradicated. The authors of the admonition to Parliament
deservedly called it “a foul whirlpool of abominations and a poisoned lake.”
For in that pool the filth of simony, nonresidence, and plurality of benefices
is washed away, and by the archbishop's gracious dispensations the guilty man
comes out whiter than snow, leaving the filth behind him in the Court of
Faculties.

Besides the Prerogative Court, the Court of Audience, the Court of Arches, and
the Court of Faculties, Canterbury also has another court called the Court of
Peculiars, *which specifically belongs to certain parishes separated from episcopal*

[Marginalia: Spalato, book 3, chapter 9, no. 22; *In Britain*, Frankfurt
edition, p. 110.]

[1623 printed p. 112 | IA leaf 124 | PDF p. 124 | ends before Chapter IV]

*jurisdiction in several dioceses and to the archbishop of Canterbury,* as
Camden writes.

He retains the same number of parishes in other bishops' dioceses that he
possessed under the papacy. If dioceses were divided for the convenience of
ecclesiastical government, why, I ask, are some parishes exempt from the
diocesan's jurisdiction and immediately subjected to others outside the
diocese? Who will deny that this exemption of parishes was introduced not for
the sake of order but to enlarge archiepiscopal rule?

In the constitutions of 1604 certain cases of absolution are reserved to the
archbishop. Canon 2 decrees that *anyone who presumes in any way to injure or
diminish the prince's royal supremacy in ecclesiastical causes is
excommunicated by the fact itself and is not to be restored except by the
archbishop.* Canon 5 decrees that *anyone who holds that any of the Thirty-nine
Articles of doctrine published in 1562 is in any respect superstitious or
erroneous, or anything of that kind, and so forth, is excommunicated by the
fact itself and is not to be restored except by the archbishop.*

You see the archbishop's authority growing daily, not diminishing, as under
the papacy. He is a lesser pope of a lesser world or, according to Bancroft, a
pro-pope, enriched with the spoils of provincial synods and the Roman pontiff
and armed with temporal vengeance in the court of royal commissioners.
Consequently he is more powerful in intensity than he ever was under the
papacy. He will also extend this authority to the bounds of the British empire
if the two kingdoms are united by the same laws, just as they have now been
united under the rule of one king.
