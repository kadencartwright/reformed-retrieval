### Erastus's Objection 14

“But,” he says, “if the Apostle had previously commanded that he be excluded,
he would have commanded that he be admitted again. Instead, he says only that
the punishment inflicted upon him is sufficient, and mentions neither
exclusion nor restoration.”

First, we reply again that whether the Corinthians did everything Paul had
commanded has no direct bearing on the matter. The question concerns what Paul
himself commanded.

Second, although the Apostle does not expressly say that the man must be
received again, he nevertheless indicates it clearly enough when he says that
what had been done concerning him by the majority—that is, by the presbytery—
was sufficient, and when he then adds: “You should rather forgive him and
comfort him, lest he somehow be swallowed up by sorrow.”

### Erastus's Objection 15

Erastus further objects: “If delivering someone to Satan means casting him out
of the Church, outside which there is no salvation, Paul would not have said
that he did this so that the spirit might remain safe on the day of judgment.”

First, I answer that Erastus suppresses what we have always been accustomed to
add, as the Apostle himself does: to be delivered to Satan is to be declared
cast out of the Church, outside which Satan reigns. This is not done as though
the man were to remain with Satan and perish with him, but, on the contrary, so
that, moved by a sense of his most wretched condition, he may repent. Thus,
when the flesh which formerly ruled him has been destroyed, the spirit may gain
the upper hand, and in that way he may be saved. This will at last appear fully
in him and in all the elect on the day of the Lord. We therefore compared
excommunicated persons above to branches that are indeed withering and, unless
they take heed, will finally be cut off entirely and cast into the fire. Yet
they are still attached to the vine itself and, as charity bids us hope well,
may in due time be revived once more by the vine's sap. All of this was omitted
from Erastus's argument.

[Scan 96; printed p. 74]

Second, I add that the Apostle did not write, “so that the spirit may remain
safe,” but “so that the spirit may be saved.” The incestuous man, otherwise
overcome by the flesh, could rightly be regarded as almost already lost and as
certain to perish unless he repented.

### Erastus's Objection 16

“Indeed,” says Erastus, “if you understand delivery to Satan to mean exclusion
from the Supper, to which one must again be admitted in order to be saved, you
have understood it incorrectly. The Supper is not absolutely necessary for
salvation. A person who desires to participate but cannot, or is not admitted,
can be saved without it.”

We readily acknowledge that neither simple deprivation of Baptism nor simple
deprivation of the Supper is deadly, but that contempt of them ultimately is.
Yet what this argument adds about someone who desires to approach the Supper
but is prohibited cannot happen where excommunication is used lawfully.
Whoever desires restoration to the Church is not merely unobstructed but is
restored with the greatest joy of the Church and of the angels, for nothing
could happen to the Church more welcome than the renewed life of a withering
branch. The Church requires only that he duly attest that this desire of his is
true and sincere. Otherwise, deceiving himself and others, he might heap
judgment upon judgment on his own head through a fresh profanation of sacred
things. Those who are kept away are expressly warned that they are nevertheless
still reckoned within the Church; they therefore have no reason to suppose
that they are being shut out from salvation. Even so, the greatest
consideration is also given to their desire, lest severity applied as a
health-giving medicine become deadly poison to them.

Second, Erastus should know that unrepentant excommunicates are not in a state
of condemnation because they are barred from participating in the Supper. On
the contrary, they are excluded from the Lord's table because they are in a
state of condemnation on account of their crimes, until they repent.

### Erastus's Objection 17

“But,” he says again, “how many people among the ancients were baptized only
at the very end of life? Before they were baptized, were they delivered to
Satan? What if they were not delivered to him because, though they had not
been prohibited from Baptism, they abstained for other reasons? Surely those
who abstain from the Supper of their own accord are to be judged better than
those compelled to abstain from it.”

I do not see what direction this argument is meant to take. Those who delayed
Baptism so long while nevertheless wishing to be regarded as Christians acted
very badly, in my judgment. I cannot help strongly condemning the gross
negligence of the ancient bishops in this matter. They ought to have taught
the ignorant,

[Scan 97; printed p. 75]

rebuked the superstitious, and severely judged those who showed contempt. Nor
do we doubt that those who neglect the Lord's express command to eat this bread
and drink this cup sin gravely and in some sense excommunicate themselves. It
is also clear that the ancient canons were extremely severe against such
people. But to be delivered to Satan is something different from either
delaying Baptism or voluntarily abstaining from the Lord's Supper.

### Erastus's Objection 18

He further objects that the Apostle admitted to the Lord's Supper contentious
people, authors of schism, those who became drunk in the very celebration of
the Supper, those puffed-up Gnostics, those who ate things offered to idols,
and others living impure lives. He only exhorted them to endeavor to approach
the Lord's Supper worthily and warned them to examine themselves, without any
mention of ecclesiastical judgment.

First, neither do we teach that one must immediately leap to the most extreme
remedies. Every other measure must first be tried.

Second, I say that the Apostle expressly judged that those who subsequently
continued eating in an idol's temple were to be barred from the Lord's table,
when he said: “You cannot participate in the table of the Lord and the table of
demons.”

Third, the Apostle also taught what the Church must determine concerning the
greedy, drunkards, fornicators, and people of similarly and publicly polluted
lives who nevertheless claim the name of brothers and do not repent when
admonished. He did so not in this particular passage—for not everything can or
should be said at once—but in the same epistle and in the very chapter where
he dealt with delivering the incestuous man to Satan. There he commands that
the old leaven be purged out and prohibits even eating with such people—that
is, with those first condemned by the Church's lawful judgment. As we said
elsewhere, to leave this to the discretion of private persons would have been
far too dangerous and very harmful to the whole Church. Jude, moreover,
expressly commands that these same people be saved through fear.

Fourth, if Paul commands that incestuous people be delivered to Satan, why
would he spare others who have no better claim upon God's kingdom?

### Erastus's Objection 19

“It appears,” he says, “that this custom of barring people from the sacraments
crept in when men began to attribute too much to the sacraments and to ascribe
salvation to them. Or perhaps, precisely because the sacraments were denied in
this way, men began to imagine that they confer salvation. Along with its other
fruits, therefore, this prohibition of the sacraments either produced this
belief among us or nourished and fostered it.”

So he says—but where is even the slightest proof of this claim? What wonder is
it that evil men abused ecclesiastical discipline, since the same thing also
happened to doctrine itself, hardly any part of which they did not transform
into monstrous superstitions? While the farmers slept, the evil one sowed the
weeds

[Scan 98; printed p. 76]

which choked the crop for a time. Yet since it is agreed that doctrine is not
to be abolished but purified, why should we not judge that the same must be
done with the whole of discipline? The same God is author and guardian of
both. Doctrine is necessary for salvation, and discipline, in its own manner,
is necessary for preserving doctrine. In light of experience alone, it is
astonishing that anyone can fail to see this.

Second, the utter emptiness of Erastus's claim is evident from the fact that—
to omit the testimony of Holy Scripture—he could never produce any age or any
church in which the right of excommunication was not received, even if he
searched through all ecclesiastical history down to our own time.

### Erastus's Objection 20

“Finally,” he says, “in the Apostle, ‘remove from the midst’ and ‘deliver to
Satan’ mean the same thing. But ‘cast out of the Church’ and ‘remove from the
midst’ do not mean the same thing. Therefore ‘deliver to Satan’ and ‘cast out
of the Church’ do not mean the same thing either. The Apostle is thus alluding
to Deuteronomy 13, where Moses commands that the false prophet who teaches
apostasy from the Lord be removed from the midst—that is, killed. At that time
the Church lacked faithful magistrates and could not remove those polluted by
crimes in any other fashion. It therefore obtained such punishments from the
Lord by prayer. God had given this power to the Apostles, who became renowned
for miracles both of healing and of striking, but he did not give it to some
paltry college of elders.”

First, I answer that Erastus himself supplies us here with our answer. We have
proved from the very writers who refer the expression to bodily torment
inflicted by Satan—and whose authority Erastus misused against us—that to
deliver someone to Satan is not the same as to kill him. Therefore removing
someone from the midst and delivering him to Satan were not the same.

Second, let us approach the matter more closely. The Apostle did not write
simply that the man should be “removed from the midst,” an expression which
signifies capital punishment and, generally, the removal of something so that
it no longer exists, as the same Apostle speaks in Colossians 2:14 and 2
Thessalonians 2:7. He added the pronoun “of you,” which Erastus ought not to
have suppressed. At the end of the same chapter the Apostle speaks even more
plainly: “Remove the wicked man from yourselves”—that is, as the barbarous
Latin expression has it, “from among you.” Everyone knows that being removed
“from the midst,” since it is stated without qualification, generally means
being removed from among humanity—that is, being killed. Being removed from
the midst of the Corinthian church, however, means being expelled from its
assembly, or, so to speak, sent into spiritual exile. Nevertheless,

[Scan 99; printed p. 77]

among competent Greek authors the bare phrase “from the midst” sometimes
signifies nothing more than a change of location.

Third, Erastus's claim that the Apostle alludes to Deuteronomy 13 appears to be
true. Indeed, he alludes not merely to that passage, for in almost innumerable
places we find such phrases as, “He shall be cut off from the midst of his
people,” “You shall remove the evil from your midst,” and many others of that
kind, though Moses does not always use a single term. But if, wherever these
phrases occur, we understand them as capital punishment inflicted by the civil
magistrate, there will be no remaining reason to accuse Draco of writing his
laws in blood, since Moses will be found much more bloodthirsty. Sound
interpreters therefore warn that these phrases sometimes refer to God's own
judgment, by which he threatens to avenge crimes of this kind through the
destruction and extermination of entire families, as when he killed Er and
Onan. David's statement also belongs here: “Men of blood and deceit shall not
live out half their days.” At other times, however, the phrases refer to civil
magistrates, who do not bear God's sword without cause. Thus in the passage of
Deuteronomy, God explains the formula in verse 5, “You shall remove the evil
from your midst,” with the words in verse 9, “You shall surely kill him.” By
contrast, when Exodus 12:15 says, “That soul shall be cut off from Israel,” it
is explained in verse 19 as, “That soul shall be excluded from the assembly of
Israel”—that is, he shall not be reckoned among the people of Israel. Nor can
the sanction of the law of circumcision in Genesis 17:14 be understood
otherwise. It says that the uncircumcised man is cut off from his people as one
who has violated the covenant. Erastus wrongly interprets these passages as
capital punishment.

The excision we are now discussing was therefore among God's people the same
sort of thing as the penalty used among the Greeks, who from common moral sense
detested murder and similar crimes. They called such people “polluted,
defiled, accursed, unholy,” and so forth; said that the Furies pursued them; and
held that they must be purified by fixed rites, as is evident from both Greek
poets and historians. The Latins intended nothing different when they said
that someone was “forbidden fire and water,” as in their penalty of
deportation. This punishment was therefore partly civil, since it removed the
rights of Israelite citizenship, and partly spiritual, since it added a
further stain to the preceding uncleanness.

Fourth, what Peter did once, outside the ordinary course, to Ananias and
Sapphira, and what Paul did to Bar-Jesus through a miracle and an extraordinary
impulse of God, certainly must not be confused with the Apostles' ordinary

[Scan 100; printed p. 78]

ministry.

Fifth, by what right could we say that the power to remove someone from the
midst of the Church was taken away from the elders—that is, from the governors
of the individual churches—and given to the Apostles alone? Paul says that the
Corinthians ought already to have done this, namely, removed the man from the
midst of the Corinthian church. Whether someone interprets that removal with
Erastus as killing or with us as excommunication, why would Paul have rebuked
the Corinthians so severely unless it belonged to the ordinary power of the
Corinthian presbytery? Why would he command that it be done by them when
assembled in the Lord's name—those whom he elsewhere calls “the insiders”? In
the Epistle to the Galatians the Apostle likewise says, “I wish they would cut
themselves off.” Why would he desire the Galatians to do this if the cutting
off, however we explain it, did not lie within their power? His earlier
discussion of leaven makes sufficiently clear that this does not mean asking
God to cut them off, but excluding them so that this cautery might cleanse the
Church. The same Apostle commands the Corinthians to remove the leaven in
order to heal the same disease.

No one will ever persuade me that the Apostle commanded Christian churches to
ask God to remove this or that man from the midst, however harmful he might be
to the Church's flock through false teaching or evil example. Paul desires the
greatest possible concern to be shown even for the salvation of those who were
excommunicated, as Christian charity requires. When he discusses the pastors'
duty in this matter, he uses no harsher words than “admonish” and “instruct.”
Nor did he kill the Jews who resisted the truth with the most obstinate hatred,
or ask that they be killed. On the contrary, he wished, if it could have been
done, that he himself might be anathema, separated from Christ, on their
behalf, imitating in this matter Moses' example on behalf of apostates and
detestable idolaters.

This is not contradicted by the special prayers made outside the ordinary
course by prophets against particular persons, or by Paul himself against the
craftsman Alexander. Something similar also happened when Chrysostom and
Epiphanius were at odds with one another. Deeds performed by a particular
inspiration of God must not rashly be made into general examples. Christ
himself taught his disciples this both by words and by his own example in the
very agony of death, as Stephen likewise did while he was being stoned. This
neither detracts from the authority and office of magistrates, whom God has
also armed with the sword to terrify evildoers, nor deters pastors from
exercising ecclesiastical censures.

[Scan 101; printed p. 79]

Let that, then, be the answer to each of Erastus's arguments.

### Confirmation of the true interpretation

In the received interpretation of this passage, I certainly find nothing that
can fairly be called forced.

First, where in the whole of Scripture can even one stroke of a letter be found
to prove that “deliver to Satan” means “kill by means of Satan”? Satan's realm
is the world. A man ordered to live in exile from the Church is therefore
rightly said to be delivered to Satan. Why, though, does the Apostle speak this
way? So that it may be better understood how dreadful it is to be cast out of
the Church's assembly.

Second, the Apostle is speaking about spiritual matters and in doing so is
exercising his apostolic office. Why, I ask, should we say that he lays down
laws about bodily punishments, whether extraordinary or ordinary? There is no
wonder in Moses, who also gave political and forensic laws, making decrees
about such matters. But I never find Christ or the Apostles even contemplating
anything of the kind, especially where churches or their pastors are being
addressed.

Third, if anything of this kind had been regularly practiced throughout the
churches, what a broad entrance would have been opened for adversaries to
slander the gospel's doctrine as the cause of all godlessness—the very charge
now leveled, all too truthfully, against the false Roman church! Indeed, would
Christians not easily have been convicted of the secret murders of which they
were accused, or of eating human flesh, their accusers undoubtedly seizing an
occasion from the spiritual eating of the Lord's flesh and drinking of his
blood?

Fourth, if Christian magistrates did what Erastus here desires and removed all
drunkards, fornicators, and similar people by the sword, how many thousands of
executioners would be needed today? How greatly the gospel would stink! What
good would finally result? At most, many people would hate to sin from fear of
punishment. Spiritual punishment, however, produces a love of virtue. It so
affects a senseless conscience with awareness of its sin that it simultaneously
offers a remedy which both heals the wound and destroys the author of the
wound—namely, the flesh and Satan.

Fifth, add that nothing is more usual for the Apostle and the other sacred
writers than to use the name **flesh** both for that filthy corruption and for
a person insofar as he is unregenerate. Opposed to it is the **spirit**, which
sometimes means the very power of the Spirit by which we are sanctified and at
other times the same person as the “inner man,” insofar as God's Spirit directs
him, as Holy Scripture commonly speaks. Thus the Apostle's phrase here, “for
the destruction of the flesh,”

[Scan 102; printed p. 80]

could not be better interpreted than by these words of the same Apostle: “that
the body of sin might be destroyed, so that we might no longer serve sin.” In
the same sense he says that the thing by which we were held has died.

Sixth, this gift of God necessarily follows the spiritual killing and
abolition of the flesh, or unregenerate nature, so that the man who has thus
been made spirit instead of flesh may be saved. But how or when can a person
subjected to capital punishment, especially with Satan as executioner, be said
to repent through this punishment? After death in the age to come?

Seventh, if someone terrified by the announcement of so great a punishment
immediately repented, by what right would the Church deliver him to Satan—that
is, kill him—in order that his spirit might be saved, when he was already safe
in both body and soul? It follows that only the unrepentant would be delivered
to Satan to be killed. But where do we read that the spirits of the unrepentant
dead will be saved on the day of the Lord, when repentance and forgiveness of
sins are two things most closely joined?

Eighth, if “spirit” means the soul and “flesh” means the body, how can the body
be said to be destroyed, that is, to perish, while the soul is saved? Can the
soul of a person whose body is condemned be saved? If instead you refer “the
destruction” merely to the decomposition of the body, why does Paul mention
the salvation of the spirit alone, since the repentant man is saved in both
soul and body? But more than enough has now been said about this passage.

### Argument 9 for excommunication

Another apostolic passage fits with this one. In precisely the same sense Paul
says that he delivered Hymenaeus and Alexander—blasphemers, that is, opponents
of the doctrine of the resurrection—to Satan, so that they might learn not to
blaspheme. How could these words possibly be applied to the killing of the
body? Nothing more ridiculous can be imagined than to say that someone was
killed so that he might be taught to think and speak more reverently about God.
Far more appropriately, Virgil's Phlegyas preaches from the underworld, but to
the living: “Take warning, learn justice, and do not scorn the gods.”

The Apostle does not say that Satan is to teach them not to blaspheme, as we
demonstrated above from Chrysostom on this passage, for Satan is no teacher of
piety or any good. Rather, they themselves are to be taught not to blaspheme.
By whom? Obviously by God's Spirit, who chastises those whom he loves. Charity
commands us to think and hope well of everyone and is especially solicitous
for those who are near destruction. It is also likely that those not utterly
beyond hope will at least be corrected by their own wounds. But come: let us
also examine the particular objections which Erastus makes against this
passage.

[Scan 103; printed p. 81]

### Erastus's Particular Objection 1

“They seem,” he says, “to have been killed, because the word *paideuthōsi*
indicates something past and completed. If the Apostle had merely cast these
men out of the assembly of the faithful, they would not on that account have
ceased cursing and blaspheming. Indeed, they might perhaps have done so far
more boldly and licentiously.”

First, as for the grammatical argument, it is beyond controversy that,
especially in the subjunctive mood, those tenses are very often interchanged.

Second, however, we need not resort to that point. The Apostle is speaking of
the past because the whole event was already past. If, in narrating a past
event, I said, “Christ prayed for Peter that his faith might not fail,” I would
then have to use *hina mē eklipoi*. But when Christ speaks of his prayers
concerning a future matter, he rightly says, “I
have prayed that it may not fail,” using *hina mē eklipē*. Now suppose for the
sake of example that Peter's faith had failed—for that makes no difference to
our present question. Would the outcome, contrary to Christ's desire and
prayers, have changed anything in the form of expression itself? Not at all.
Therefore nothing can be inferred from *paideuthōsi* about whether the result
Paul intended when delivering them to Satan did or did not occur. Indeed, this
point is wholly irrelevant.

We may add that no one ordinarily speaks, or even can speak, this way: “He
delivered them to Satan so that they **have been taught** not to blaspheme.” The
particle *hina* indicates only the agent's design and purpose, not the outcome
of the design. The latter would be expressed by *hōste* with the infinitive,
not by *hina*, which is a final conjunction, but by what the Greeks call a
consecutive conjunction and the Latins render “so that” or “to such a degree
that.” This passage must therefore be translated, “so that they may learn,” if
the outcome was still pending, or “so that they might learn,” whether the
result had or had not already occurred.

Third, we cannot sufficiently marvel that Erastus makes the result of this
action depend on an event which, had it occurred according to his
interpretation, could never have produced that result: namely, that the men
should first be killed and thereby learn not to blaspheme. If “learn not to
blaspheme” meant “be unable to blaspheme,” the Apostle would instead have said,
“so that they might no longer blaspheme.”

Fourth, Erastus imagines that if they had been cast out of the assembly they
would have blasphemed more boldly, but this does not agree with Paul's hope and
purpose. Whenever we employ such severity, charity commands us to hope well
rather than badly and to direct everything toward that end—the edification of
our neighbor. If this hope

[Scan 104; printed p. 82]

disappoints us, provided that the fault is not ours, the blame will be imputed
to the man who through his own ferocity or even impiety turned the healing
medicine necessarily offered to him into something deadly.

### Erastus's Particular Objection 2

Erastus also advances the following: “The situation of those who had made
shipwreck concerning the faith was different from that of the Corinthian.
Hence the Apostle did not wish simply to deliver the Corinthian to Satan, but
only to the destruction of his bodily life, so that his soul might be saved on
the day of the Lord Jesus. But he wholly adjudged these men to Satan so that
they might learn not to blaspheme.”

We have already said more than enough about the Corinthian. As for these men,
although their cases were different—the one was incestuous and these were
blasphemers—does it therefore follow that their punishment differed from his?
“Not,” you will say, “in the thing itself, but in Paul's intention.”

We understand Erastus to locate this difference of intention as follows. The
Corinthian was delivered to Satan in such a way that, even if Satan killed him,
the Apostle nevertheless wished good hope to be maintained concerning the
salvation of his soul. The others, however, were wholly adjudged to Satan. If
this is true, what follows except that Erastus approves a greater
excommunication, or anathema, even though he does not approve exclusion from
the Lord's Supper? For this would be a true and irrevocable anathema—or
something even more severe.

Third, Erastus wishes it to be more severe to be delivered to Satan in order
to learn not to blaspheme than to be delivered to the destruction of the flesh
so that one's spirit may be saved. But on what possible grounds? I hear none.
In my judgment one and the same thing is signified in both places. Blasphemy
and heresy are also fruits of the flesh, abolished in the person who turns
from a blasphemer into a godly man. The man who has learned not to blaspheme
has assuredly returned to the way of salvation from which he had strayed.

### Argument 10 for excommunication

In that same chapter the Apostle rises from the particular case to the general
principle. After severely rebuking the Corinthians for not previously being
sufficiently concerned to purify their assemblies, he warns them to take care
that nothing of this kind happens among them again. On this matter he cites a
precept of the Mosaic law, explained allegorically or anagogically, as he also
does in the Epistle to the Galatians. He commands the removal of all leaven,
which typifies all who might in some manner leaven the whole lump—that is, the
entire assembly of the Church—by rendering it liable to the same vices and
crimes. He also cites the command to eat unleavened bread, which typified the
purity that ought perpetually to flourish in the Christian Church. For the
Church celebrates a kind of perpetual Passover festival, answering the
sacrifice of that true Lamb, Jesus Christ, with holiness of life.

[Scan 105; printed p. 83]

I say that this is the plain and transparent explanation of the passage and
that it manifestly agrees with what precedes and follows. To encompass
everything briefly, it most clearly compares the offenses of the impure to
leaven, the purity of the saints to unleavened bread, the Church itself to the
lump, its public assemblies to the Passover festival, and excommunication to
the removal of leaven. In my judgment, whoever denies this might as well deny
that the sun shines at noon. Erastus nevertheless attacks this position with
the following arguments.

#### Erastus's Objection 1

“It is not very likely,” he says, “that God would command something in express
words and figuratively prohibit the same thing at the same time. He expressly
commands every male to celebrate Passover, excepting only the unclean. He did
not therefore intend figuratively to prohibit certain other people from
celebrating it.”

We concede the entire argument. Whom do we bar other than those judged unclean?

#### Erastus's Objection 2

“But,” he says, “there was no need then to represent evil people figuratively,
since there were quite enough of them. Figures represent things past or
future.”

Indeed, if figures did not also represent present things, how much would have
to be erased from God's Word? Circumcision of the foreskin certainly set
before their eyes both the present, inborn, inward stain and the inward
sanctification of the heart, likewise in the present. Purifications, the
slaughter of victims, and their burning indicated both of these same things.
Those rites depended upon what Christ would fulfill in such a way that they
presented for contemplation the present efficacy of the things to be fulfilled,
namely, forgiveness of sins and the grace of regeneration. From another point
of view, they represented nothing but something present—the inborn filth and
present most wretched condition of the human race—and so were like bonds
acknowledging a deserved death. Both people and priest openly testified to
this. When the Lord commands observance of the Sabbath, does he not show that
it is also a symbol of present sanctification when he says, “That you may know
that I am the Lord who sanctifies you”? Likewise, when the Lord forbids a
priest to marry a widow, a divorced woman, or one who has been profaned, he
similarly gives as his reason that he is the Lord who sanctifies the priest.
Our Baptism certainly sets before our eyes both the present filth of our birth
and its present washing away.

Second, Erastus immediately acknowledges that Paul compares the filth of the
whole Christian life with leaven and its purity with unleavened bread.

Third, will he say that these rites only began to be figures of these realities
for us, for whom they have been abolished, and were not much more figures for
those who were commanded to perform them?

[Scan 106; printed p. 84]

#### Erastus's Objection 3

“But,” he says, “Moses does not command the man who had eaten leaven to abstain
from eating unleavened bread, but to be killed. If one chooses to transfer this
to the Lord's Supper, therefore, the same thing would have to be done.”

As though either every Mosaic ceremony or whatever Israelite judicial penalty
must be introduced into the Church today! Let the magistrates indeed do their
duty. For the Church it is enough to hear the Apostle say, not, “Kill the man
who eats leaven,” but, “Remove him from your midst”—that is, as he himself
explains it, “Purge out the leaven, so that the lump may remain pure.”

Second, I deny that those who ate leaven were subjected to capital punishment.
As we said earlier, Moses immediately explains the formula he had previously
used—“That soul shall be cut off from Israel”—as expulsion from the assembly of
Israel. This means nothing other than barring the unclean and profane man from
celebrating the festival, namely, until the second month, as is provided for
unclean persons. Elsewhere the same formula, “to be cut off from Israel,” means
nothing other than ceasing to be reckoned among the Israelites and circumcised
people. The person who had touched a corpse remained unclean until sprinkled
with the water of purification, as is expressly written there.

The formula in the passage commanding Passover also appears clearly to have
announced some additional divine judgment, as is evident from what happened
under Hezekiah. Many from Manasseh, Ephraim, Issachar, and Zebulun had not been
purified but ate the Passover contrary to the prescribed law. We do not read
that Hezekiah killed them, though he was a truly godly and religious prince and
would certainly not have acted against the law at the very time when he was
expressly restoring God's worship. Instead, after sacrifices for sin were
added, he is said to have prayed that God would be propitious to them. “The
Lord heard Hezekiah,” says the writer, “and healed the people,” upon whom he had
evidently sent some kind of illness. Paul testifies that the Lord did the very
same thing at Corinth: “For this reason many among you are weak and sick, and
many have fallen asleep.” This therefore has no bearing on capital punishments
inflicted by magistrates and does not abolish excommunication. Instead, it
teaches that neglect of excommunication is avenged by God with severe
punishments.

#### Erastus's Objection 4

Erastus objects in turn that the Jews ate leaven throughout the whole year
except for those seven days. “Therefore,” he says, “if you wish to apply this
to the Lord's Supper, it will be lawful always to live impurely except when

[Scan 107; printed p. 85]

the Supper is celebrated.”

This argument commits what is called the fallacy of composition. Paul does not
interpret leaven considered absolutely as our impurity, but leaven insofar as
it was prohibited. Nor does he take every kind of fermentation to represent
the contagion of an impure life, but the fermentation produced by old leaven—
that is, leaven which is corrupt and corrupts the whole lump. The Apostle
wishes what was a seven-day festival for the Jews to be perpetual for
Christians: namely, Christ's spiritual banquet opposed to worldly life, in
which, as Christ says elsewhere, he lives in us and we in Christ.

#### Erastus's Objection 5

“But,” says Erastus, “the Apostle does not compare the Jews' Passover with the
Lord's Supper, but with our entire life.”

Let it be so, although the matter itself cries aloud that Paul had a special
regard here for the good order of a particular church, to which the term
“lump” also points, and therefore compares the Passover festival as figure with
its embodied reality.

Second, I by no means deny that the Apostle extends this teaching to the other
parts of the Christian life as well, as he soon discusses barring the impure
even from private fellowship. But since Erastus has conceded the whole, by
what right does he except the most excellent part of the entire Christian
life, namely, the banquet of the sacred Supper?

#### Erastus's Objection 6

“But,” says Erastus, “as the Scholastics rightly say, the anagogical sense is
not probative.”

Entirely correct, if this is understood of human allegorical expositions. I
acknowledge that through such expositions, with Origen in the leading place,
the whole of Holy Scripture was miserably carried far away from its true
meaning. But far be it from us to assign the allegorical explanations of the
legal mysteries and shadows, and even of sacred histories, which the Holy
Spirit has delivered to us through the Apostles' mouths, any place other than
that which all Christians must assign to the law delivered on Mount Sinai and
to the sacred prophetic and apostolic writings.

#### Erastus's Objection 7

“But,” he says, “Passover never stands for the Lord's Supper. The Apostle says
that Christ our Passover was sacrificed for us, not the Supper itself.”

Yet Erastus himself previously wrote that the Lord's Supper succeeded in place
of Passover, and from the fact that everyone was not only summoned but even
compelled to attend Passover he concluded that no one should be prohibited
from the Supper. Nothing is more customary than this interchange of the names
of type and thing signified by the type, and of figure and truth itself. Thus
the fathers are said to have been baptized in the cloud, and we are said to
have been circumcised in Christ with a circumcision

[Scan 108; printed p. 86]

made without hands.

Second, “Passover” is commonly used both for the lamb and for the festival
itself. Why, then, should we not similarly use the same name both for Christ,
who is the truth of that lamb, and for the Christian sacramental Passover—that
is, the Lord's Supper, in which we solemnly commemorate his passage and our
true deliverance? The Apostle's words, commanding us “to keep the feast” and
to do so “with the unleavened bread of sincerity,” certainly lead us here. It
was also by far the most ancient custom of the Christian Church to call the
day on which we celebrate the
Lord's resurrection “Passover” and then to celebrate a solemn “resurrection
assembly.”

Third, this point is plainly incidental, since neither our argument nor,
rather, Paul's own argument depends in the least on the single word
“Passover.”

#### Erastus's Objection 8

Erastus further says: “The Jews were commanded to remember two principal
causes in celebrating the festival of unleavened bread: their sudden and
miraculous liberation from that most bitter servitude to the Egyptians, and
the preservation of the firstborn. We ought to be content with these causes
and meanings of eating the Passover lamb without leaven, which Moses and
therefore God himself delivered.”

Let those things indeed have been memorials for the Jews of benefits past. Yet
one cannot deny, without stripping the apostolic writings of all authority,
that they were also types for us of those realities. That very deliverance
from Egypt therefore foreshadowed something far more excellent. What was
represented even then through the removal of leaven, the eating of unleavened
bread, the lamb itself, and its slaughter looked to Christ himself and extends
to the consummation of the ages.

Can we rest wholly in the externals reported by Moses without refusing to
follow Paul as, so to speak, he advances beyond the marked boundary? Erastus
does not deny this, for he immediately adds: “These things also served for the
recognition of the future Christ and of his office.” Entirely right—and what
are the two things which Erastus previously called principal in comparison
with these? Yet consider what he immediately adds: “If it is maintained that,
beyond representing things that were past, they also represented something
future, leaven could signify false doctrine or a shameful life. But this is not
so certain and established for us that without another command we may transfer
the penalty from the figure to the thing figured.”

We must conceal nothing here. These words are such that they could appear
twisted against the Apostle himself. For Erastus himself concedes that Paul
plainly says that the prohibited leaven was a type of impure people from whom
the lump—that is,

[Scan 109; printed p. 87]

the Church—is to be cleansed. Why is any condition needed here? Why is there
any need for debate? Whether we transfer any punishment from the figure to the
thing figured beyond Paul's words—than whom no better interpreter of the
Mosaic command is to be sought—let anyone judge from the answers we have given.

#### Erastus's Objection 9

“But,” he says, “each and every person, not even excepting the wicked, is
expressly commanded to celebrate Passover together with the other solemn
festivals, and they are commanded to celebrate it without leaven. We are also
taught what this ceremony signifies. We therefore seek another signification
in vain.”

Indeed, when everyone is commanded to celebrate Passover, worthy preparation
is commanded at the same time. It is agreed that anyone judged polluted was
not admitted even to a private table—much less to eat the Passover—unless he
was first purified. On the basis of Paul's words, I affirm that this was
already signified and commanded by the very ceremony of removing every trace
of leaven.

Second, both the Jews then and Christians afterward would have been ashamed to
prostitute their holy things to dogs and pigs—since even profane nations judged
that every criminal must be driven away from their rites, whence the sayings,
“Far, oh far away, you profane!” and “Away, away, whoever is sinful!”

### Argument 11 for excommunication

The Apostle commands the same Corinthians not even to eat with those who,
though otherwise called brothers—that is, reckoned within the Church—lead
shameful and criminal lives. Writing to the Thessalonians, he similarly
commands us to withdraw from those who live disorderly lives, that is, who do
not permit themselves to be brought into order. He also commands them to be
marked and prohibits associating with them. He most clearly declares that
ecclesiastical judgments were established for this purpose, not only when he
mentions those assembled in the Lord's name, but also when he says in these
express words, “Do you not judge those who are inside?” From these passages it
clearly follows that the discipline of excommunication rests simultaneously
upon an express apostolic command and example.

We therefore argue as follows: if it is unlawful even to take ordinary food
with those whom the Church has removed from its midst, it is much less lawful
to share sacred things with them. Erastus answers again.

#### Erastus's Objection 1

“The Apostle's command,” he says, “must not be understood so as to conflict
with Christ's command and example. But I demonstrated above that God does not
drive sinners away from the sacraments, but rather gathers them to the
sacraments so that they may more easily be invited to repentance.”

I concede the major premise. I distinguish the minor, which has already been
refuted very often. Sinners were indeed summoned to sacrifices, but only those
who publicly attested repentance and so, after purification, were reconciled
to God and the Church. It follows that if they did not do this, they were
excluded. Do we deal differently with

[Scan 110; printed p. 88]

sinners who have been judged?

#### Erastus's Objection 2

“Indeed,” he says, “the Apostle would contradict himself if he intended the
passage to be understood this way. For when he wanted the impure Corinthians
to abstain from crimes, he did not threaten them with exclusion from the
Supper. He said instead that God would not spare them even if they participated
in the same sacraments as the holy and good.”

What could have been said more plainly than that Paul commands the criminals
whom he calls leaven to be purged from the lump, and prohibits even eating with
the rejected? From this too it is plainly gathered that “Remove him from
yourselves” can mean nothing other than this cleansing or expulsion from the
Church. Only a madman would forbid people to eat with those delivered to
Satan—that is, on Erastus's own interpretation, with dead men—and elsewhere
command that the dead nevertheless be admonished as brothers.

Second, Paul's statement that the Lord will not spare those who approach the
same sacraments as the good in an unworthy manner by no means overturns the
rejection of evil people from the Lord's table. It refers partly to those whom
the Church has not yet judged, whether because they are not yet under judgment
or because the Church still patiently tolerates them. It is spoken partly
against the very people who despise this discipline, of which the Lord will be
the avenger, so far is it from abolishing it.

#### Erastus's Objection 3

“There is also,” he says, “a very great difference between forbidding someone
human companionship and forbidding him the use of the sacraments. Many people
voluntarily abstain from the company of the evil, yet do not hesitate to pray
in church and use the sacraments with them, since good people are not polluted
by evil ones in the use of ceremonies instituted by God.”

We do not deny that the two differ. Indeed, we say that they differ in such a
way that prohibition from using the sacraments is far greater than prohibition
from private human company. But if a person is denied the lesser thing, how
can one conclude that the greater is granted?

“The former,” he says, “is commanded, but the latter is not.” Indeed, even if
the other express commands we have already set out did not exist, the latter
would be sufficiently understood as commanded in the former.

To make clear that the Apostle intended to argue in this comparative manner,
we must observe that he did not write, “Do not take food with such a man,” but
adds, “Do not even eat with such a man.” As everyone knows, the particle “not
even” has the force of implying
that, when the lesser is denied, the greater is denied all the more strongly.
The sentence must therefore be completed: “Do not even take food with him,
much less hold in common with him something greater than eating together.” It
is as if I said, “I do not have even a penny, much less possess wealth”; or, “I
did not even think it,

[Scan 111; printed p. 89]

much less do it.”

Fourth, it is clear that this passage is not a simple and general discussion
of avoiding the company of evil people, as the same Apostle teaches elsewhere
and even quotes a line of Menander. Paul in the same context mentions those
assembled in the Lord's name. Most decisively of all, he plainly distinguishes
those who are inside from those who are outside, wishing the former to be
judged by the Church but leaving the latter to the Lord's judgment. This
distinction has no place in the general command to avoid evil company.

Fifth, in the Epistle to the Thessalonians the Apostle does not use the verb
*sēmainō*, which denotes signifying something by words, letters, or some other
means. He says *sēmeiousthe*: “Mark him, and as it were prick him with a branded
mark, so that he may finally begin to be ashamed of himself.”

Sixth, far be it from us to deny that good sacraments remain good for the good,
even if some evil people approach them. It by no means follows, however, that
no one is to be barred from the Lord's Supper. We do not offer pollution of the
sacrament as the reason for barring anyone from the Lord's table. Rather, evil
people who do not hear the Church and consequently are cast from the Church's
assembly by the Lord's command have no share in God's kingdom until they
repent. Pastors therefore cannot knowingly admit them to the Supper without
contradicting themselves, aggravating the wound whose healing they ought to
pursue, and ultimately failing in their duty. Indeed, if private persons treat
such judged people as holding a place different from that determined by the
Church, John testifies that they participate in their evil works, and they
violate Christ's command: “Let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector.”
We discussed elsewhere the tax collectors with whom the Pharisees would not
eat, even though they shared sacred rites with them.

#### Erastus's Objection 4

“But,” says Erastus, “the ends of these two things differ greatly. Private
companionship nourishes vices, while its denial produces shame. Yet if, as you
say, denial of the Lord's Supper produces shame, will you say that use of the
Supper fosters vices? That is not written. What is written is that the Supper
was instituted so that the Lord's death might be celebrated together. If
someone desires to do this with others, will he not be impiously driven away?”

Many things are wrapped together in this argument; we will answer them one by
one. The purpose for which the Supper was instituted should not have been
compared with the purpose of forbidding private company. The purpose of
forbidding the Supper should have been compared with it, so that opposites
would face each other directly. To make this clearer, let us establish the
correct comparison as follows.

[Scan 112; printed p. 90]

The purpose of private company is to foster mutual fellowship among private
persons. The good should therefore flee the evil lest they too become evil.
The purpose of the Christian synaxis is to foster the participants' mutual
union in Christ, whose death they celebrate together. Care must therefore be
taken that stubbornly evil people, after lawful examination of their case, are
barred from the synaxis. What fellowship can light have with darkness? How can
those who do not hear the Church and have therefore been cast out of it
proclaim the Lord's death? You thus see that the purposes and uses of these two
things are by no means opposed, and that parallel arguments are therefore
drawn from both in 1 Corinthians 5, 6, and 11.

Let us now compare the purpose of denying the two forms of fellowship. Since
it is manifold, we will choose the very purpose Erastus mentions. The purpose
of forbidding private fellowship is to make someone ashamed, for it is
disgraceful to be judged unworthy of other people's company. The purpose of
forbidding the sacred assembly is the same, and carries all the more weight in
proportion as public things excel private and sacred things common. Therefore,
when this single remedy of shame remains, it must be employed: a person whom
the Church has so judged is to be barred as unworthy from the brothers'
private company and consequently from their sacred synaxis.

Second, let that be our answer to Erastus. We add, however, that these two
things are not contradictory but fit together perfectly: the Supper was
instituted so that Christ's death might be celebrated jointly, and it is
forbidden so that someone, most sharply wounded by awareness of this disgrace,
may repent and celebrate the Lord's death properly.

Third, Erastus's closing statement—that people who desire to celebrate the
Supper with others, that is, to proclaim the Lord's death, are impiously
rejected—requires qualification whenever a person has been judged unworthy.
What if he does not sufficiently understand what he desires? What if there is
just suspicion, or evidence from some other source, that he acts deceitfully?
What if postponing his desire is in the whole Church's interest? That maxim
therefore must not be observed rashly. In general, however, we are so far from
repelling those who truly desire to return to the Church's favor—and no one
who first refuses to satisfy the Church can be said truly to desire it—that on
the contrary we chiefly use excommunication so that the person who sinned may
learn earnestly to seek for salvation the good he had spurned to his own ruin.

### Argument 12 for excommunication

The same Apostle says in the same Epistle to the Corinthians: “If anyone does
not love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be anathema. Maranatha.” This is

[Scan 113; printed p. 91]

plainly the most severe form of excommunication. There is therefore no doubt
that Paul says those judged to be Christ's enemies by impurity of life or
doctrine ought to be accursed by everyone, even if they otherwise wish to be
reckoned within the Church.

### Argument 13 for excommunication

The same Apostle says to the Galatians: “If anyone preaches to you a gospel
besides that which you received, let him be anathema.” Therefore stubborn
disturbers of sound doctrine must be excommunicated.

### Argument 14 for excommunication

He likewise says in the same epistle, “I wish those who unsettle you would cut
themselves off.” Therefore those who obstinately impede the Lord's work must be
cut off. If someone asks why the Apostle himself did not cut them off, since he
possessed this power and while absent had delivered the incestuous Corinthian
to Satan, I reply that although by his own right the Apostle might perhaps have
done this, he never claimed that authority for himself privately. He expressly
wished the Corinthians to be assembled and the wretched man to be delivered
to Satan by their votes, not by his sole decision. Here too, then, Paul first
commands what the Galatians must do when he says, “Let him be anathema.” He next
explains his own desire and goes no farther, undoubtedly because he considered
not merely what was lawful but also what was expedient. Let this also answer
Erastus's objection that Paul did not forbid the Lord's Supper to any of the
Galatians.

### Argument 15 for excommunication

In more than one place the same Apostle commands that authors of dissensions
and offenses be diligently observed and avoided. He especially commands
pastors to withdraw from sophists who corrupt sound doctrine with their empty
arguments, and to reject a heretical person after diligent admonition. Unless
all these commands refer to this ecclesiastical discipline, they cannot be
properly employed at all. It does not belong to just anyone to take notice of
heresies, and to leave it to every private person's judgment whether this or
that person should be avoided is nothing other than to open the door to schisms
and countless offenses.

### Argument 16 for excommunication

John demonstrates it still more clearly when he does not permit such people
even to be received into a house or so much as greeted. Charity certainly does
not allow this to be done unless the matter has been judged. And whom more than
pastors does it befit both to flee such people and to proclaim to others that
they must be fled? If private homes should not rashly be opened to them, what
true pastor will introduce them as far as the Lord's table, unless they have
repented or changed, without participating in their evil works, as the same
John speaks?

### Argument 17 for excommunication

Jude likewise, as we said elsewhere, commands that those whom he calls stains
and blemishes upon the love feasts should, if otherwise incurable, be saved by
fear. Indeed, we are to flee even contact with them, much less place holy
things into their hands to be polluted.

[Scan 114; printed p. 92]

Since these things are so, anyone can now readily decide whether to agree with
those who utterly reject exclusion from the Lord's Supper as established by
neither command nor example drawn from God's Word, or with us. We affirm that
God employed it from the beginning of the world and afterward sanctioned it;
that it was observed in God's Church under the law, later expressly confirmed
by Christ, and diligently inculcated and employed by the Apostles. I think no
one will dare deny that the churches have never lacked it from apostolic times
down to our own centuries, not even when the bitterest persecutions raged. Nor,
until a few years ago, was it any more doubted that excommunication is of
divine right—legitimate excommunication, I say, not an oligarchic or tyrannical
kind—than it was doubted whether the gospel is to be believed.

## The Presbytery

The last of the subjects on which we promised to say something remains: the
college of elders which the Jews called the *synedrion* and Christians, on the
Apostle's testimony, called the presbytery. The question is whether the
Apostles instituted such a college and entrusted it with the authority of
ecclesiastical government. We affirm that they did, while Erastus wholly
denies it. Following our usual method, we will examine the arguments he puts
forward here and there, then present our own reasons, and finally say something
about the actual use of excommunication.

### Erastus's Argument 1 for the twofold Israelite jurisdiction

Erastus recalls us to the condition of the Israelite commonwealth. He contends
that while it flourished the Israelite magistrates simultaneously possessed
power over religion and civil affairs, and asks whether the Christian
magistrate today should not guard both tables and establish the whole of
religion just as he was bound to do under the Old Testament.

I accept the condition, but with qualifications added from God's Word. The
Israelite civil polity was distinguished from the Levitical priesthood, and
Christ distinguished the purely ecclesiastical administration in the
Christian Church from the office of Christian magistrates. I will discuss the
former distinction first and the latter afterward.

I therefore acknowledge that both then and now authority to preserve the law
of both tables has been and is entrusted to the godly and religious magistrate:
both the spiritual law which concerns conscience and the law which concerns
the peace of human society. Yet this is not without an important distinction.
God alone has power to establish divine law, whether we consider doctrine
itself or the outward form of its external administration;

[Scan 115; printed p. 93]

he has reserved that power to himself alone, condemning all will-worship. In
establishing religion, God spoke to the fathers at many times and in many ways
through the prophets, and especially through Moses, adding the dreadful
warning that nothing should be added to or taken from his Word written through
Moses. In these last days, however, he spoke first by the mouth of his Son and
then by the Holy Spirit speaking through the Apostles' mouths. The Father's
whole will concerning our salvation has thus been most fully revealed to us
and delivered also in the apostolic writings. Consequently it never has been
and never will be lawful until the consummation of the world to add anything to
it or take anything from it, either in the doctrine of faith itself or in the
substance of its outward ecclesiastical administration.

I do not deny that the appearance and form of the outward order, insofar as it
depends upon varying circumstances of place, time, and persons, have sometimes
necessarily had to vary in the Christian Church, now gathered from every
people. Yet I add two conditions. First, everything must be tested with the
greatest diligence by the norm and plumb line of the very substance of the
order divinely instituted and unchanging until the last day. Second, nothing
here may be established by magistrates, however Christian, according to their
own judgment and sovereign command. Indeed, neither pastors, teachers, nor the
presbytery may make and unmake laws about these things. Where there is a
Christian magistrate, proposed changes should first be discussed together,
devoutly and prudently, by both the magistrate and an assembly of those who
hold ecclesiastical offices, and should finally be determined with the consent
of the whole Church. The magistrate is not the author of this ordering or of
the whole sacred ministry, as he is in the civil commonwealth, where it is
lawful also to enact and repeal law. He is its guardian and defender.

The godly magistrate must therefore take diligent and primary care that the
Church is rightly constituted from God's Word; that all who hold ecclesiastical
offices perform their duty; and that those who obstinately disturb the Church's
peace are punished by civil penalties—fines, imprisonment, scourging, and
finally, if necessary, capital punishment. God appointed him not only so that
equity and upright conduct may be preserved in human society, but especially
so that he may kiss the Son of God, serve as a nursing father to the Church,
and ensure that those committed to his trust by the King of kings live holy
and godly lives. This cannot be done unless the solid foundation of every
kingdom—the sacred ministry rightly constituted and administered—is first laid
down and kept whole and sound.

[Scan 116; printed p. 94]

Conversely, the duty of those who hold offices in the sacred ministry is to
administer only “the things of God.” They teach God's Word not merely publicly
but also, as needed, privately and to individuals; warn, convict, and rebuke;
and sometimes, applying a healing cautery, prudently and devoutly separate
those who do not hear the Church. They must do this without taking even the
smallest part of the magistrate's civil right. Finally, with consolation
applied, they loose those who were previously bound and have now repented.
Those whose duty is to govern consciences with authority from God's Word also
have the duty to chastise wandering consciences with divine censure, as
Tertullian elegantly speaks, and to receive those who return to favor with God
and the Church.

It is therefore apparent from the matter itself that these jurisdictions are
different in their very nature. One rests purely and properly upon divine laws,
the other upon human prudence and authority. Whether administration of both
was committed to the magistrate is what we must now inquire, in order to meet
the antecedent of the preceding enthymeme.

So far was Erastus from rightly deriving the union of both jurisdictions from
the order which the Lord established through Moses in Israel that it was
precisely then that the jurisdictions, just as they differ in nature, first
began to be entrusted to different administrators. Melchizedek was at once
king and priest of the Most High, undoubtedly according to the custom among
those chief patriarchs of the human race before and after the flood whom Moses
did not thoughtlessly name, pass over, and describe in their succession. Holy
Scripture also testifies that Abraham was a prophet and distinguished teacher
of his household, which he also circumcised, and that he undoubtedly offered
sacrifices. We read that Job did the same. Judah son of Jacob acted in his
household both as a civil magistrate in condemning his daughter-in-law and in
the functions of divine worship. The redemption of the firstborn also belongs
here, if we believe the Hebrew interpreters, when the Lord transferred the
spiritual sacred ministry to the tribe of Levi alone.

In those times, when the human race had not yet grown so greatly and the
memory of the teaching of those long-lived patriarchs, handed down from one
generation to the next, was still fresh, both religion and civil government
could conveniently be administered by the magistrate. But as the nations
gradually fell away from the true God and his sincere worship, it pleased the
Lord our God to set these matters down through Moses in a definite order for
his people, then gathered into the single family of Abraham.

[Scan 117; printed p. 95]

Eli's union of the offices of high priest and judge, Samuel's restoration of
both the civil commonwealth before Saul's election and the ecclesiastical
order, and the practice of those called the Maccabees were singular and
extraordinary. They must not be turned into examples which disturb God's
order. Nor do we concern ourselves with what the priests finally dared through
ambition or rather tyranny, since it was unlawful to depart from the divinely
instituted order. From Moses himself we demonstrate that this order was as we
have described it.

First, as regards administration of doctrine and all sacred rites, Moses did
indeed establish the entire Levitical priesthood, with God dictating every
detail to him. But did Moses himself perform anything belonging to that
priesthood, even though many things about Moses were extraordinary? The same
is clear of Joshua. Although David was a prophet, we nowhere find that he
claimed any part of the priestly office for himself. Solomon built the temple
and ordered the courses of priests and Levites, but had been taught through
the prophets' mouth, as had his father. He also preached to the people,
invoking God in solemn prayers to ratify the work he had performed, and wrote
books which testify to his remarkable wisdom. By the sovereign authority
discussed above, he removed the pontificate from Abiathar, who was guilty of
treason, but nowhere intruded into the
priesthood itself.

Jehoshaphat restored both commonwealths after they had fallen. Hezekiah and
then Josiah followed his example. Yet Jehoshaphat did not imitate Saul when he
sacrificed without waiting for Samuel or Uzziah when he was struck with
leprosy. Who today would not desire God to raise up many such princes? Let
that suffice concerning the distinct offices of teaching and offering.

As for purely ecclesiastical judgments distinct from civil ones, judges drawn
from the leading citizens were established in each city according as some
surpassed others. In many passages of the Gospel history they are called
elders, and they decided civil cases, including capital cases when the law and
facts were established. Levites were also present in the synagogues. The
spiritual administration belonged to them, with the participation, as is
probable, of certain distinguished citizens. Hence the reference to rulers of
synagogues, who governed the assemblies. We have two examples of this order:
one in Luke 2:46 at Jerusalem, where there were many
synagogues, as Acts 6:9 reports; and the other in Acts 13:15

[Scan 118; printed p. 96]

at Pisidian Antioch. Their duties undoubtedly included refusing admission to
the synagogues to those whom the Jerusalem Sanhedrin had judged expelled from
the synagogue, since otherwise that proscription would have been void. Christ
also calls these assemblies “councils”: the civil assemblies in Matthew 5:22,
where civil prosecution of injuries is discussed, and sacred assemblies in
Matthew 10:17 and 23:34. After the age of the Hasmoneans especially, however,
the priests gradually claimed the whole power for themselves, as we also see
happening in the Christian Church. Whether by right or wrong, they claimed not
only the right to scourge, as with the Apostles in Acts 5:40, but even to kill.
They did so either through a riot, as happened to Stephen in Acts 7:58 and as
Christ foretold in Matthew 23:34, or through a plot, as they devised against
Paul in Acts 23:21 when they had previously failed to slaughter him openly in
Acts 21:31. Josephus testifies that James, the Lord's brother, was killed in no
different manner. The fact that the civil judgments were exercised at the
city gates, while sacred judgments occurred in the synagogues, also plainly
shows that Moses originally distinguished these assemblies and that sacred and
civil matters were not simply mixed together.

Among the judgments established throughout the cities stood higher councils
to which the most serious matters were referred. God originally established
two of them through Moses. Both eventually had their seat at Jerusalem, until
the priests' ambition and greed fused them into one, though the Romans resisted
them as regards capital punishments. I consider the existence of two councils
more than sufficiently established by the following. Moses constituted his
own tribunal, the civil one, from seventy men of the people joined to him. But
the Aaronic tribunal—as is most fully related in the history of Jehoshaphat,
who undoubtedly followed Moses' prescription—was to be composed partly of
those called *cohan* in Hebrew and “chief priests” in the Gospel, partly of
Levites, and partly of heads of the fathers, that is, citizens holding leading
authority among the people. Joseph of Arimathea, whom Luke calls a council
member, was among their number. Distinct from them and separate in jurisdiction
were those whom the Gospel calls, with the added description, “elders of the
people,” that is, laymen. The Jews called the former *Avothenu*, “our fathers,”
whom the Evangelists commonly call simply “elders” and sometimes number with
the scribes. Various passages of Gospel history show that there was in turn
more than one kind of scribe. Some served in the ecclesiastical council and
the synagogues as teachers of the law and guides, while others, as we just
said,

[Scan 119; printed p. 97]

belonged to the civil council.

To return to the matter, that Moses established two councils is also clear
because they were not both instituted at the same time. The number of the
civil judges is given as seventy, excluding Moses. In Moses' place there first
succeeded the Judges, as God raised them up outside the ordinary course, and
finally the kings. The number of the ecclesiastical council is not given, but
its jurisdiction is distinguished from the civil one in the plainest words.
They are said to be appointed to show the distinction between holy and
profane, clean and unclean, and to teach the children of Israel all the
statutes which the Lord spoke to them by the hand of Moses. I maintain that
these few words describe everything that was then truly ecclesiastical:
distinguishing holy from profane and clean from unclean, teaching the Lord's
law, and consequently judging controversies arising from those causes.

The tablets of the civil laws written in Moses' books were also in the keeping
of the Levitical tribe. At another place Moses therefore goes farther and
wishes decisions in difficult civil controversies, when the judges were
uncertain about interpreting the civil law, to be sought from God's Word and
thus to belong also to the priests' jurisdiction. That other Mosaic passage
therefore mentions disputes between blood and blood, cause and cause, and
stroke and stroke. Since these controversies are civil by nature, someone
might infer from them that civil and ecclesiastical jurisdiction were mixed.
It must therefore be observed that when such a case occurred, the
ecclesiastical council answered from the sacred tablets, like the ancient
Roman jurists, not concerning the fact—which was purely civil—but concerning
the disputed law. The judge, that is, the man who presided over the civil
council, such as Zebadiah in King Jehoshaphat's name, judged the fact according
to the answer on the law supplied by the high priest and his council. To this
civil council lay the final appeal from inferior tribunals. We demonstrated
just above from the same Moses that the civil judge and high priest had two
different councils.

Moses therefore wishes both the high priest and the judge to be approached in
this passage so that they may give the word of judgment. The high priest and
his assessors come first, so that, as Moses says, “they may teach the word of
the law”—that is, answer the legal question from the law. The judge—that is,
the president of the civil council—comes afterward and declares the judgment
itself, that is, pronounces concerning the civil controversy itself. His
sentence had to be obeyed without appeal,

[Scan 120; printed p. 98]

Jehoshaphat therefore undoubtedly followed Moses' prescription when he
restored the fallen civil and ecclesiastical administrations. He separately
entrusted certain judges, after reminding them of their office, with deciding
“matters of judgment”—cases ordinarily litigated between plaintiffs and
defendants—which he also calls the **king's business**. In his own name he
placed over them Zebadiah, one of the princes of the house of Judah. Separately
again, he commanded an ecclesiastical assembly—composed of the people I have
described, namely, selected chiefs of the priests and men among the tribes
distinguished by authority and piety, and therefore called elders—to handle
the very matters Moses had described, but spiritually rather than civilly,
just as the tribunals were distinct.

“Whatever case,” he says, “comes to you from your brothers living in their
cities, between blood and blood, between law and commandment, between statutes
and judgments”—that is, as in Moses, whenever the inferior civil judges could
not agree about the law, since judicial laws too were contained in Moses'
writings—“you shall warn them not to offend against the Lord, lest his wrath
come upon you and your brothers. Do this and you will not sin.” The proper
office of the ecclesiastical council thus lay not in adjudicating civil
controversies between plaintiffs and defendants but in instructing and
edifying each person's conscience. We demonstrated in the proper place from
God's Word and from the purer Jewish traditions that the elders proceeded so
far against those guilty of more serious offenses as to pronounce first
*Niddui*, then *Cherem*, and finally *Schammatha* against them.

Jehoshaphat finally adds: “Behold, Amariah the chief priest is over you and
your brothers in the **Lord's business**, and Zebadiah son of Ishmael, a prince
of the house of Judah, is over the **king's business**.” These words most
plainly distinguish civil business, whose head was the king, from purely
spiritual business, whose head was the high priest, and distinguish the two
councils. Jehoshaphat finally adds: “The Levites, the *Shoterim*, are before
you.” These were evidently the men appointed town by town, as were the civil
magistrates, so that no one would always be compelled to approach those two
supreme councils for every ecclesiastical or civil matter.

From these testimonies, which in our judgment are extremely plain, we affirm
that this was the form of the two distinct Jewish councils until changing
circumstances and certain men's bribery caused everything to go very badly.
Nor do we doubt that

[Scan 121; printed p. 99]

Christ looked back to that true institution of the ecclesiastical council.

### Refutation of the arguments for twofold Jewish jurisdiction and one council

First, Erastus believes that by divine right the ecclesiastical council also
had cognizance of forensic disputes. I consider this wholly incorrect, much
less could it have possessed capital jurisdiction except after Jewish affairs
had become confused and the law's discipline was greatly corrupted, as its
doctrine also was. It would not be difficult to confirm this with additional
arguments and testimonies. Certainly, when Pashhur the high priest and his
colleagues prosecuted Jeremiah as guilty of blasphemy, a capital crime, the
high priest did not sit as judge, not even on the lowest judicial bench. He
acted as accuser before the civil and royal magistrates while a crowd of the
people stood around, and Jeremiah was acquitted by Ahikam, who presided over
the council in the king's name.

“The power of Caiaphas and his colleagues against Christ was therefore the
same,” says Erastus.

That power was partly a remnant of the confusion which had begun with the
Maccabees and could not immediately be abolished even by Herod. After the
Romans reduced Judea to a province, although they otherwise valued the Jews'
divine laws at nothing, they readily granted the Jews autonomy, but only in
matters concerning their rites. We do not deny that in those matters, like
many municipal magistrates elsewhere, they possessed the power of arrest and
minor coercion. But the questions are whether God's Word permitted them to
confuse two councils and two jurisdictions, whether it was lawful for the high
priest and his colleges to exercise capital jurisdiction, and whether Christ
approved such a transgression. We stated our judgment on these questions just
above.

Second, the question whether the council to which the Lord referred when he
said, “Tell the Church,” possessed capital jurisdiction in Christ's time does
not bear greatly on the issue. Nevertheless, since Erastus and certain others
appear to me to defend a bad case here too, we will not leave even this part of
his writing untouched.

When I survey the entire history of the Roman Empire while it stood, I find
provincial governors administering justice to provincials both in private
disputes and public crimes, except for those granted immunity or liberty or
treated as friends. The autonomy which the Jews obtained, several decrees
concerning which Josephus supplies, is no objection. It was confined within
definite limits, since they had not been granted immunity. They were permitted
to remain in their religion,

[Scan 122; printed p. 100]

to have their own high priests and elders who decided among themselves
controversies arising over their rites, perhaps also to exercise certain civil
judgments of lesser importance insofar as Caesar's authority could tolerate
it, and to exercise lesser punishment against those said to have violated
religious law, as in Matthew 10:17 and 23:34, Acts 5:40, and 2 Corinthians
11:24. But I entirely deny that in Christ's time or at any subsequent time
they possessed the right of the sword—that is, power to judge more serious
crimes.

Thus they arrested and bound Christ because the case concerned blasphemy,
investigated him, and condemned him for blasphemy according to the law. Why,
then, did they not kill him, when they desired nothing more? Certainly because
they did not possess the power of the sword. “Indeed,” says Erastus, following
Chrysostom, “because they were not permitted to crucify him.” But how will
that finally be proved? The seven sons of Saul are said to have been hanged by
the Gibeonites. Not to range farther afield, unless crucifixion is also
understood by the term “hanging from a tree,” the saying “Cursed is everyone
who hangs on a tree” was not fulfilled in Christ. Yet Paul applies this
testimony to Christ in such a way as to infer from it, as from a special mark,
that Christ became a curse for us.

Moreover, what attentive person could be persuaded that the Jews burned so
greatly not merely with desire to kill Christ but specifically to crucify him
that, forgetting themselves, their immunities, and the hatred with which they
pursued the Romans, they preferred to hand Christ to Pilate—not without the
risk of an uncertain outcome—rather than stone, hang, or even burn him alive,
if this had been within their power?

“But,” Erastus says, “Pilate gives them power to judge Christ by the law, and
undoubtedly does not do so to give them some new right.” Let it be so. This was
not in Pilate's power but Caesar's. The Jews understood this very point: when
Pilate spoke that way, he neither intended nor was able to grant them capital
jurisdiction, than which nothing would have been more desirable to them. He
only intended them to proceed against Christ according to the law insofar as
their granted autonomy allowed. They refused to rest in what he told them
because they wanted Jesus killed. The law indeed permitted this, but the Romans
did not grant it to them. That Pilate's words “and crucify him” were ironic is
clear because, if they were not, the Jews would have obtained the single thing
they sought. Since the Jews were by no means ignorant of Pilate's meaning and
were utterly determined to kill Christ, they appropriately answered that he
was indeed guilty of death according to the law, but that they themselves

[Scan 123; printed p. 101]

were not permitted to execute what the law commanded in these matters. The
Jews' knowledge of this is also evident from their next action. They understood
well enough that even if a charge of blasphemy committed against the law were
proved, Pilate would attach no great importance to it. They therefore fastened
upon Christ the new charge of treason against Caesar. Abandoning the former
accusation, they finally obtained his condemnation from Pilate on this charge.
Otherwise, who does not see that when the Jews saw Pilate searching for every
opportunity to free Christ, they would then most eagerly have accepted what he
offered them?

John writes that the saying of Jesus, signifying by what death he would die,
was thus fulfilled. This must not be referred merely to the particular form of
punishment but more generally to Christ's statement that he would be handed
over to the Gentiles, by whom he would finally be condemned and crucified. If
the Jews had also been permitted to exercise jurisdiction over the crime of
sedition, why would they have allowed Pilate to imprison Barabbas, and why
would they not have demanded his restoration to them by full right? The Jews'
statement against Paul in Acts 24:6—that they had intended to judge him
according to the law—must not be understood of capital punishment. If this had
been legally permitted to them, they would not have needed to devise his
murder by ambush, but only to ask that he be given to them, as Acts 25:3 shows.

Erastus further produces the stoning of Stephen and calls false our assertion
that Stephen was killed in a riot, even though, he claims, the opposite is
plain to anyone from the history.

Yet in the history we find that Stephen was indeed brought to the council,
accused there by false witnesses, and began his defense. But we do not find
that the judges' opinions were even requested, much less pronounced, nor do we
read that a judge condemned him. Before he had finished his speech, they began
to grind their teeth, rushed upon him, threw him—not in the manner of courts—
outside the city, and stoned him there. Is this judging, or rather raging?

Erastus also cites Saul's savagery in dragging living men and women into
prison, having even received letters from the high priest to the synagogues.

We do not deny that the high priest and even the synagogues possessed arrest
and some minor coercion in religious cases. But this derived from license and
the confusion of both jurisdictions, as we said, which Christ never approved.
He afterward most expressly distinguished one from the other in the Christian
Church so as even

[Scan 124; printed p. 102]

to separate them.

Second, Paul's admission about himself might appear pressing. He confesses not
only that he shut many saints in prison, but adds *katēnenka psēphon*: “When
they were killed, I cast my vote.” From this it might appear that votes about
the defendants' lives were indeed requested and pronounced in the council.
But Paul's own history plainly proves that *katēnenka psēphon* must be
interpreted there generally as agreeing and approving, and that during that
time certain people besides Stephen were killed with impunity, contrary to law
and right. So today in French anarchy, not only inferior judges but anyone at
all is permitted to do anything against us. They were plotting this very thing
against the Apostles too, had Gamaliel not opposed it in Acts 5:33. It is also
agreed that James the Lord's brother was finally killed in such a riot by a
certain fuller.

No one should be surprised that this occurred. He should know that for many
centuries afterward, however crushed the Jews were, a kind of tribunal called
the “Zealot's judgment” was extremely common. Christian emperors finally had
to curb this madness with the most severe prohibitions. Constantine speaks as
follows: “We wish the Jews, their leaders, and patriarchs to be notified that
if after this law anyone dares to attack with stones or any other form of
violence—as we now know is happening—a person who has fled their deadly sect
and turned to God's worship, he must immediately be given to the flames and
burned with all his accomplices.”

Third, Gallio's response to the Corinthian Jews prosecuting Paul sufficiently
shows that they could determine among themselves some matters belonging to
disputes over their law, but that for even minor injuries, at least there, the
Roman governor's tribunal had to be approached. The statement of the Ephesian
town clerk can be understood no differently, even of the Ephesian citizens
themselves, much less the resident Jews. Suppressing the riot stirred by
Demetrius, he referred them to the proconsuls' tribunal.

### The Christian presbytery succeeded the ecclesiastical council by Christ's institution

We have shown from God's Word what kind of body the Jewish council was and what
its jurisdiction was. It remains for us to demonstrate from that same Word of
God that, by Christ's institution, it was succeeded by the presbytery which we
customarily call the consistory.

Erastus will therefore, we hope, readily concede that even at the beginning of
the nascent Church, before there was a Christian magistrate, it was necessary
to exercise judgment concerning the whole of doctrine,

[Scan 125; printed p. 103]

against false prophets, and concerning the offenses of those whom private
admonitions could not restrain. Suppose, as Erastus maintains and we refuted
above, that it was also necessary either to kill such people by the power
Peter employed against Ananias and Sapphira, or to obtain by prayer from God
that they be removed from this world in some manner. It would then have been
all the more necessary to guard against doing this rashly, and consequently to
establish in the churches some ordinary judicial process, at least for warning
in advance those who were to be delivered to Satan. In short, certain suitable
and selected people had to assemble also for the ordination of ministers, for
establishing colleges of deacons and widows at least, and for other matters of
that kind, so that everything in God's house might be administered decently and
in order.

Neither the Jewish Sanhedrin nor any civil magistrate could then perform this
function. When Paul prohibits litigation over matters of this life before
unbelievers, would he ever have judged that they should be approached about
spiritual matters? We therefore affirm that people of this kind had already
been appointed. Otherwise it would be utterly absurd to say that Christ's command,
“Tell the Church,” could not be used until more than three hundred years after
Christ. Why multiply words? When the Apostle says, “When you are assembled in
the name of the Lord,” and likewise, “Do you not judge those who are inside?”
we must acknowledge that assemblies for judging these matters had already been
established in the churches.

Second, let us now consider who these judges were and what matters they
examined. Many certain reasons establish that they were entrusted with an
office different from those whom Paul commands to be appointed at Corinth so
that no one would be compelled to litigate before unbelievers. The matter
itself first teaches that the assembly whose negligence Paul rebukes and by
which he wishes the incestuous man delivered to Satan had to have been
previously established. Elsewhere Paul plainly calls this assembly the
presbytery. But concerning the future arbiters of private controversies he
says, “Appoint,” not “You have appointed.” Furthermore, the former are
established to judge sacred and purely ecclesiastical matters, while the
latter are to decide matters of this
life. Paul teaches that the latter are appointed only with some disgrace to
the Corinthians when he says, “I speak to your shame,” as though saying, “If
you continue to be so litigious

[Scan 126; printed p. 104]

that you cannot do without judgments of this kind.” But it cannot be denied
that the former are always necessary to the Church and are even its distinctive
ornaments.

Finally, Paul wishes even “those of no account” to be chosen for judgments
about the things of this life, as though those carnal affairs were unworthy to
occupy someone fitted to think about and perform better things. But the matter
itself compels us to acknowledge that the ecclesiastical judges had to excel
both in piety and in every excellent gift of the Spirit.

We need make no effort to prove that those properly called deacons exercised
another office distinct from these governors of ecclesiastical discipline.

It remains to see whether this college was an assembly consisting only of
those devoted to the Word and prayers, or whether certain men had a distinct
calling. It cannot be denied that a principal part of the pastors' office is
to defend doctrine against false teachers, remedy public and private offenses,
convict, warn, rebuke, and so to bind and loose. Christ especially gives this
right—or rather imposes this burden—upon his disciples. The Apostle expressly
joins himself with those Corinthian judges by whom he commands the incestuous
man to be delivered to Satan. Indeed, elsewhere he says that he delivered
Philetus and Hymenaeus to Satan.

We must therefore understand that there is hardly any name signifying an
ecclesiastical office which is not also sometimes taken generally. Hence the
name “apostles” is applied even to those more properly called evangelists;
Paul calls his apostolate a ministry; Peter calls himself an elder; and Luke
calls people elders whom Paul later calls bishops in a general sense. Yet no
one can doubt that the offices are distinct both in fact and when the same
names are taken specifically. Erastus argues that because the same people are
called presbyters and bishops, there were no presbyters other than those who
handled the Word. His argument is no more effective than if he similarly
concluded that there were no presbyters other than Apostles because the
Apostles too are sometimes called presbyters, even though presbyters are
repeatedly and plainly distinguished from Apostles.

### Arguments confirming a distinct office of ecclesiastical governors

We prove from the following arguments that, besides those who presided over
the Word, public prayers, and administration of the sacraments—some of whom
were specifically called pastors or prophets and others teachers—there were
also certain people placed over ecclesiastical government.

[Scan 127; printed p. 105]

First, as we proved in its place, the Jewish ecclesiastical Sanhedrin was
constituted as follows. Christ plainly approved it, and the Apostles later
followed it by the Holy Spirit's guidance insofar as this could and should be
done—that is, insofar as its members sat in Moses' seat and performed the
offices of teaching and judging as God had commanded through Moses. Besides
Levites and priests, it included men selected from the tribes and holding
leading authority. They were commonly called *Zekenim* by the Hebrews and
“elders” by Greeks and Latins. The older and purer Western Church appears
deliberately to have rejected the terms “senate” and “senator” as more suited
to civil dignities and liable to slander, just as it avoided the Greek titles
for rulers, councils of elders, councils, and councillors.

Unless certain people selected from the whole body's membership sit in the
assembly by which the whole Church is governed, the universal name “Church,”
with which Christ himself adorns that assembly, would hardly fit it. Those
selected from every part of the whole Church represented the whole Church.

Second, this arrangement restrained like a bridle the power both of the
pastors and of the people, lest the former degenerate into oligarchy and the
latter into mob rule. In my judgment the Christian Church, like the ancient
Israelite Church, has been most divinely constituted from this threefold
state. Its head and Monarch, towering far above all, is our one eternal High
Priest, whose figure was the Levitical high priest. He is now in heaven in
such a way that by his Spirit he is present with and presides over every
assembly constituted by his authority. These assemblies represent a most
divine aristocracy. Finally, the whole multitude, with its knowledge and
consent from which the aristocratic assemblies themselves are constituted,
presents a perfect example of heavenly democracy. Under God, Christian
magistrates distributed throughout the world are the earthly guardians and
defenders of this whole order, as were the Judges among the ancient people
after Moses and the kings after Samuel. When this order was damaged and
finally entirely overturned, partly by the princes' sloth and partly by the
priests' greed and ambition, the ancient people was rejected by God's just
judgment. We now see the condition to which the Christian Church, suffering
from precisely the same evils, has been reduced.

Third, when writing to the Romans and listing the permanent ecclesiastical
offices in a most beautiful order, Paul

[Scan 128; printed p. 106]

plainly distinguishes “those who govern” from those who teach, exhort,
distribute, and show mercy—that is, from teachers, pastors, and finally the
two orders of deacons.

Fourth, writing to the Corinthians, he lists those same offices and the gifts
necessary for exercising them, though more fully since he also includes those
which flourished in the Church only temporarily, and plainly distinguishes
“governments” from the rest.

Fifth, when in the person of Timothy he speaks most divinely about the lawful
use of ecclesiastical order, he says, “Let elders who rule well be considered
worthy of double honor,” that is, support, “especially those who labor in the
Word and teaching.” These words most plainly distinguish elders who devote
themselves to preaching and teaching the people from other elders.

Erastus nevertheless attempts to wrest even this passage away from us; let us
examine his objection. “The Greek *kopian*,” he says, “signifies something more
than merely devoting oneself to a matter. The Apostle therefore means that
those who labor forcefully and energetically beyond the others should be
preferred to the rest, as if one said, ‘I wish well to all pastors, especially
those who feed their churches with zeal and diligence.’”

Even if we concede that *kopian* has this emphasis, which certainly must not
be overlooked here, what attentive reader fails to see that if this exposition
were valid—that is, if every elder's office were to devote himself to the Word
and teaching—it would have been superfluous to add the whole phrase “in the
Word and teaching” after “labor”? Who speaks this way: “I wish well to everyone
who teaches God's Word, especially those who diligently teach God's Word,”
rather than, “I wish well to everyone who teaches God's Word, especially
those who teach diligently”? But if someone says, “I wish well to all teachers,
especially those who are diligent teachers of God's Word,” who does not
immediately understand that diligent teachers of God's Word are preferred to
teachers of all other subjects? Erastus himself will now, we hope, acknowledge
that his objection is plainly empty and vain.

Erastus next brings forward another objection which has more appearance of
plausibility and is stated even more invidiously against us. “It is difficult,”
he says, “to understand how one body has two heads—that is, two magistracies
with the right to punish and exercise some lordship over subjects.
Excommunication is in the Church what exile is in the commonwealth. These
elders therefore also command and inflict outward punishments with

[Scan 129; printed p. 107]

power and dominion, just as the magistrate commonly inflicts them upon
offending subjects. Exhortation, instruction, admonition, and rebuke, which
are performed by words without coercion, are one kind of thing; external
power and coercion are another.”

I ask whether the Israelite people had two heads under Moses, the Judges, and
the kings, especially under the godly and holy King Jehoshaphat, when one man
distinctly presided over the king's affairs and another over the Lord's
affairs. They presided in such a manner that, just as the high priest had to be
subject under God to the king in civil matters and even in preserving the
condition of religion, the king in turn was subject to the Lord's Word spoken
through the high priest. If someone should answer that Israel perhaps had two
heads, I will ask why the same condition should now be judged improper. But if,
as I think more likely, Erastus says that Israel did not on that account have
two heads, I ask why the Christian Church will have two heads now rather than
then if the same distinction is preserved—especially since we now have no high
priest on earth except in the Spirit, but only his ministers.

Here Erastus responds that we do not preserve the same distinction but
introduce an entirely different one, because the Levitical high priest acted
only by words while our elders also employ external power and coercion.

I demonstrated above that judgments about clean and unclean belonged to the
ecclesiastical council. Consequently, expulsion of people who entered the
sanctuary while polluted and without purification must be referred to its
authority. Why say more? When Azariah, accompanied by eighty priests, told
King Uzziah, “Leave the sanctuary, because you have transgressed, and this will
bring you no glory from the Lord God,” did Azariah sin, or did he rather
perform his office as a man endowed with heroic spirit? Uzziah not only
refused to obey but began to grow angry. By striking the stubborn king with
leprosy, the Lord more than sufficiently testified that Azariah had acted
entirely lawfully.

“But,” says Erastus, “although David was polluted by willful murder and
adultery, Nathan did not excommunicate him.” I concede it. By what right would
Nathan have done so, since he possessed neither this ordinary power nor any
extraordinary authorization from God to act? Yet David's very famous
confession plainly shows that, as prescribed for the unclean, he abstained
from sacrifices until he was purified. These things detract no more from royal
power than royal power detracts from Christ's kingdom

[Scan 130; printed p. 108]

in any respect.

#### Response 4

These two powers are wholly distinct. One of them—the political power,
considered absolutely—stands above
everyone on earth and therefore has no head on earth to which it is subject.
Ecclesiastical power, however, is something real rather than personal and
something divine, not human. It depends entirely upon the command of the one
to whom the Father has entrusted all judgment and who, as the Church's eternal
and sole head, executes from heaven through his ambassadors what he has
decreed in his Word.

#### Response 5

Moreover, no instrument other than the voice does anything in ecclesiastical
punishments, nor is their object to bring something about by violent
coercion. What is unjustly requested is merely justly denied, so that it may
later be justly requested. So far is anything from being extorted from an
unwilling person that, on the contrary, no repentance is accepted unless it
is established to be voluntary. If either the civil magistrate should take
anything away from Christ's spiritual kingdom, or the ministers of the
churches should pluck anything from the civil power, then at last Erastus may
rightly say that there are two civil or two ecclesiastical heads—something we
acknowledge to be plainly monstrous.

#### Objection 3

Someone may object by citing the example of godly kings who established
churches. We have already replied that securing a place for Christ's kingdom,
that is, for the sacred ministry of the gospel—which is the office of holy
kings or princes—is one thing; administering Christ's kingdom itself without
a call is another. Christ expressly separated civil power from the Church's
ministry, and whoever has attempted the latter has experienced God's avenging
hand. Christ distinguished ecclesiastical government from civil government
and separated the one from the other by this single utterance of his: “My
kingdom is not of this world.” He therefore never involved himself in the
administration of worldly affairs, much less bestowed on
either magistrates or pastors both of what are called the two swords—even if I
were to concede to Erastus that under the law the council once wielded both.

#### Objection 4

Again, someone arguing in the opposite direction may object that the arbiters
appointed at Corinth to decide private disputes—perhaps the Corinthians were
instead ashamed of being so litigious that they needed such tribunals—or that
Ananias and Sapphira were killed at Peter's word,
or Elymas blinded by Paul. From these examples he may try to infer that
ministers of the Church are permitted to exercise some unspecified civil
jurisdiction, at least when the aid of a Christian

[Scan 131; printed p. 109]

magistrate is lacking.

#### Response

We reply that those Corinthians did not sit as judges or exercise civil
jurisdiction in any way. They were arbiters and private household
arbitrators, who acted with no coercive authority
but settled disputes they had learned about amicably, provided that the
litigants wished it so. No just law forbids this. Peter and Paul, however,
acted by divine appointment and in a manner extraordinary not only in power
but also in form. Indeed, if such cases were tried today before Christian
magistrates on earth, they would scarcely judge offenses of that sort to be
capital crimes. The magistrate therefore had no reason then to say that a
wrong had been done to him, since it was evident in every way that this was
God's hand. Nor is there any reason now for those who wish to be regarded as
pastors of churches to turn these events into precedents, unless perhaps they
can show themselves endowed with the Apostles' extraordinary Spirit. Let
those who act this way see to that.

### Whether the presbytery possessed the power of excommunication

One matter remains for us to explain in reply to Erastus's book: whether that
presbytery which, as we have shown from the testimony of Moses, Christ, and
the Apostles, was divinely instituted possessed and exercised the power now
under dispute. Erastus denies this too. He seeks to prove that the power of
delivering to Satan—that is, as he understands it, of torturing someone by
means of Satan even to the point of death—was granted to the Apostles alone so
long as the Church had no Christian magistrate, and consequently that this
power now has no place wherever there happens to be a Christian magistrate. I
say, however, that these satanic torments, and much more any killings—if any
occurred—were wholly extraordinary and extremely rare and must in no way be
turned into precedents. Indeed, I think that we should not even investigate
them. But we affirm that “delivering to Satan” and “removing from the Church,”
whatever it finally is—and we think we have shown what it is—belonged to the
presbytery, that is, to the college of elders.

#### Erastus's Objection 1

Erastus replies: “The Apostle threatens that he will come with authority and
with a rod and says that he has at hand the means by which he may avenge all
disobedience. He does not mention that college of elders, however. Therefore
this power belonged properly to the Apostles.”

First, I ask whether, after the Apostles died and at least until the rulers
submitted their necks to Christ, that same power or rod was not equally
necessary for the Church.

Second, I also ask to whom it was transferred, if not to the governors of the
churches constituted according to the Apostles' own direction.

[Scan 132; printed p. 110]

Third, the reason the Apostle does not mention that college in this passage is
plain: the college itself was the principal object of his threat, since it was
not performing its duty. Nevertheless, we do not doubt that the Apostles
possessed a certain primary authority above every ecclesiastical ministry,
because Christ had chosen them to organize that ministry itself and establish
its perpetual order in the churches.

#### Erastus's Objection 2

“But,” Erastus replies, “the same Apostle says that he delivered Alexander and
Hymenaeus to Satan. He mentions neither the Church, this presbytery, nor any
other government, but only himself and his own apostolic power.”

First, let us grant that the Apostle did this as an Apostle, by a peculiar
impulse of the Holy Spirit. What follows from it? Because Paul acted
extraordinarily at God's command, could either Paul himself or someone else
not perform the same act in the ordinary course? But let us now see whether
Paul did it alone and always.

Second, when the same Apostle treats the incestuous Corinthian, he says, “I
have judged that he should be delivered to Satan.” We therefore do not doubt
that Paul, amply endowed with the Holy Spirit, could by a certain apostolic
right decree this—even though absent against one who was absent—and could
even put it into effect. But why does he command the others also, namely the
Corinthian presbytery, to gather in God's name? Was it so that the presence
and consent of the Corinthians might be added as a kind of mere supplement?
We acknowledge that the Apostle openly declares that he does not refer the
whole case to the Corinthian presbytery but instead prescribes to them a
decree from which they could not rashly depart—I say *rashly*. For I showed
above that, once the Corinthians perceived the incestuous man's repentance,
they were content with the rebuke or censure imposed by the majority, namely
the presbytery, until they could learn the Apostle's mind again. I grant this.
Yet if, before the Apostle's letters had been written or at least received,
the Corinthians had not been entitled by a judgment issuing from the
presbytery's ordinary power to decide and execute the same measure—that is, to
deliver the incestuous man to Satan (for Erastus previously wanted “delivering
to Satan” and “removing from among themselves” to mean the same thing)—why did
Paul rebuke them for not having done it long before?

Third, why did he command them to do it?

Fourth, when discussing this very matter—namely, excluding even from
private fellowship those falsely called brothers whose lives were nevertheless
manifestly shameful—and when distinguishing God's hidden

[Scan 133; printed p. 111]

judgments from his manifest and ordinary ones, why did Paul expressly write, “Do
you not judge those who are inside?” Why did he also command the Corinthians
to avoid these same people and have no dealings with them? Why did he command
them to mark them? These things could not have
been done without observing some definite order: precisely the order which,
following the pattern of the true Jewish council, the Apostles had established
at Christ's command—unless perhaps we wish to suppose that some Apostles were
always present to carry out these judgments and that the judgments ceased
immediately when the Apostles died.

The sum of this dispute is as follows. “Presbyters” was indeed used as a
general name for bishops, deacons, and everyone who performed any office in
the Christian Church. Yet certain presbyters also had a narrower function:
they presided together with the pastors over ecclesiastical or spiritual
judgments. Their assembly corresponded exactly to the council which Christ,
having by then changed the name “synagogue” or “council,” called the “church,”
and which Paul called the *presbyterion*. This
body possessed the power to exercise according to God's Word the
ecclesiastical discipline which we defend.

In the final part of this work we shall also say something about the use of
excommunication, for otherwise this subject alone would require a much fuller
treatment.

### To what extent the use of excommunication is necessary

First, then, if someone asks whether we believe that the restoration of this
whole discipline in every church is absolutely and simply necessary, we reply
that among the things required for the Christian Church to be complete in all
its parts, doctrine alone appears to us to be absolutely necessary without
any exception. By “doctrine” we mean what God's own Word teaches us to believe
about God for salvation. Although the use of the sacraments cannot be
knowingly and contemptuously omitted without the gravest wickedness and, in
the end, unless a person seriously repents, without guilt deserving eternal
condemnation, that use is nevertheless not so necessary that someone who,
through no fault of his own, is deprived of their outward use cannot still be
saved without them. We think this principle applies not only to individual
persons but also to the whole Church itself. Thus those journeying in the
wilderness necessarily lacked circumcision, and those in Babylon had neither
temple nor sacrifices, yet they did not cease on that account to be God's
people. The same may be said much more readily of ecclesiastical discipline:
although churches lack it, they may nevertheless be truly godly and Christian
churches if they possess the pure and sincere doctrine of the chief articles
of faith.

[Scan 134; printed p. 112]

There are nevertheless certain matters which, we think, cannot rightly be
disputed. One is that churches which are not governed by the discipline
divinely ordained must either be preserved by God miraculously and out of the
ordinary, or else, even if they do not collapse suddenly, must gradually fall
apart. Besides the fact that good order preserves all
things, a severe judgment of God must inevitably follow contempt for sacred
doctrine and the pollution of the sacraments. These can scarcely—indeed,
hardly at all—be avoided where offenses committed either against doctrine or
against good and holy morals (that is, leaven gradually corrupting the whole
lump) are not purged by some definite order, even if God's Word is heard from
the pulpit, outward idolatry is abolished, and the civil state is not
disturbed.

A second matter which we also think established is that God's Word contains
not only the entire doctrine of the Christian religion, but also gives us the
most perfect form and method according to which that supreme head of the
household wills his own house, which is the Church, to be ruled and governed.
Although many particular details must vary according to the differing
circumstances of times, places, and persons, the Church has nevertheless
always possessed certain fixed standards and immutable universal rules. They
issued from God's mouth at the beginning of the world; were later written down
by Moses and others; explained by the prophets; delivered at last by Christ
himself; and most fully sanctioned by the Evangelists and the other Apostles.
All those particular details must be directed and recalled to these rules.
Accordingly, the first care and concern of godly princes and pastors who labor
to restore the Church's ruins must be this: once purity of doctrine has been
restored, that order instituted by God himself—the most faithful guardian of
this purity—must next be restored to use and preserved inviolate.

We explained above, according to our measure, what we believe this order to
be. It is one in which those who infect the flock with either false doctrine
or wicked conduct are not only reproved and rebuked, but, if they persist in
being stubborn and do not allow themselves to be healed in any other way, are
separated from the sound sheep in the Lord's name after a lawful examination.
They are thereby prevented from destroying themselves through profanation of
the sacraments and from corrupting others by their evil example. This lasts
until, broken both by shame at themselves and by dread of divine judgment,
they attest their repentance and are received into the flock again.

### The magistrate's sword is insufficient to remove offenses

The view of those who think that civil penalties—whether financial, capital,
or of some other kind—are sufficient to remove the offenses of the stubborn

[Scan 135; printed p. 113]

does not satisfy us.

#### Argument 1

Let us leave aside something that is nevertheless of the greatest importance:
namely, that rulers who perform their duty in this matter have always been,
and always will be, extremely rare in the world. Necessity itself therefore
requires another remedy unless we are willing to endure the subversion of
doctrine and profanation of the sacraments. Let us, I say, leave this aside
and grant that the Lord has given every church a ruler endowed with such
wisdom and so inflamed with zeal for the Lord's house that he allows not even
the smallest offense to go unpunished. Have we thereby attained in any way the
end at which ecclesiastical discipline aims? Certainly not. Grant that fear
of civil punishment restrains public sinners to some extent and thus in some
measure checks evil examples. The same inward disposition nevertheless
remains and will someday burst out with greater force.

Removal from the Church's assembly, however, aims not only to keep the
contagion of evil examples from spreading—something which may in some fashion
be attained with the help of a good and diligent magistrate. It is directed
especially to these further ends: that sacred things not be profaned by being
prostituted to people known to be pigs and dogs; that, so far as this can be
prevented, no one be furnished with an instrument for destroying himself; and
finally, that the one who has strayed outside the flock not perish. No civil
magistrate's severity, however keen, will attain any of these things. Since he
cannot make people inwardly better but can only keep their wickedness from
breaking out, sacred things will nevertheless be profaned by unworthy use;
you will, as it were, have handed the wicked a sword with which to kill
themselves; and, finally, you will not have gained sinners for the Lord.

#### Objection 1

Someone will say, “But admonitions and rebukes that bend the conscience itself
will also be added.”

#### Response

Quite right. Nor do we think that the ultimate remedy should be employed
unless extreme necessity demands it. But what if a person increasingly shows
himself before God to be stubborn and incapable of repentance? What will you
say should be done then? “Let him blame himself,” you will say, “because he
refused to obey the admonitions.” Let him indeed be blamed. Yet another
ultimate remedy remains: a cautery, which certainly burns, but burns only
putrid flesh unless the sick person himself prevents it. Why would a prudent
and godly pastor not apply it, instead of abandoning a dying person once and
for all?

#### Argument 2

Moreover, will the example of that untamed stubbornness not infect others too
unless precaution is taken?

#### Argument 3

Finally, although it is ultimately each person's own unbelief and wickedness
that pollutes him, and sacred things remain sacred in themselves no matter to
whom they are presented, those who approach unworthily nevertheless profane
the sacred things for themselves. Is a person not therefore complicit in that
profanation if, knowingly and willingly, he admits to the Lord's table,

[Scan 136; printed p. 114]

without adequate evidence of repentance, someone whom the Church has judged
to be stubborn?

#### Erastus's Objection

“But,” Erastus will say, “it is reasonable to presume—and Christian charity
commands us to believe—that a person who seeks the Lord's Supper with the rest
has repented.”

First, we emphatically deny that this principle applies to the people now
under discussion. Who doubts that a person does not truly repent if, to the
best of his ability, he fails to make satisfaction to the neighbor whom he
has offended? For the Lord also warns us to leave our gift at the altar and
first be reconciled to our brother. But the entire Church was offended by
these people. Will they therefore rightly be presumed to have repented unless
they have first been reconciled to the Church?

Second, this was required under the law even in cases of outward uncleanness
and sins committed in error. Will the Lord's table possess less importance
among us than the legal ceremonies once possessed?

#### Argument 4

Finally, to settle this point once for all, was not the civil sword formerly
in the hands first of Moses, then of the Judges, and finally of the kings? The
high priest nevertheless sat in judgment, sometimes judging spiritually the
very same matters that had been adjudicated politically, as we demonstrated
in the appropriate place.

The sum of all these points—let it be said without prejudice—is this.

### A point to be treated gently

Whatever reasons human prudence may finally suggest to the contrary, and
whatever obstacles Satan—the perpetual enemy of this discipline no less than
of Christian doctrine itself—may raise, I greatly fear that, at the cost of
immense harm, sad experience will at last show that the Lord's house cannot be
governed by any better form or method than the very one whose author is the
head of the household himself, speaking through the mouths of the Prophets
and Apostles. I therefore do not hesitate to exhort all godly people, both
princes and pastors, to devote themselves diligently to restoring this matter
too according to God's Word and exercising it for edification.

### How excommunication should be used

If someone asks how we think excommunication should be used, we answer in a
single phrase from the Apostle: for edification, not for destruction. It
follows that this final remedy must never be used unless the matter itself
absolutely compels it and the Church's necessity demands it. It must be used
in such a way that we are neither more indulgent nor more dilatory than is
proper, whether in binding or in loosing. It also follows that we must
carefully consider what the times and persons will bear, while preserving
such moderation that we neither fear people where they are not to be feared,
nor allow ourselves to be caught by Satan, who devotes himself entirely to
tearing us apart after deceiving us with the appearance of just severity.

[Scan 137; printed p. 115]

Finally, I gladly embrace Augustine's moderation in this matter.

### Princes and pastors themselves are subject to this discipline

If we consider the offenses themselves, the various passages of the Apostle
make it easy to gather which kinds deserve to be struck with the sword of
excommunication. A matter which would otherwise require a longer disputation
can be stated very briefly if we say that untamed stubbornness against the
Church's admonitions alone deserves severance from the assembly of Christ and
the faithful. For Christ expressly said, “If he does not hear the Church,” and
under the law confession had to precede an offering.

There is no sin so great—with the exception of that one which is generally
known to God rather than to us—that it is not immediately blotted out in the
souls of believers by Christ's blood grasped through faith, when they attest
their faith by the repentance that befits it. Nor is there any sin so light
that, when people stubbornly scorn judgment, especially the Church's judgment,
it does not inflict a mortal wound upon their consciences, healable through
Christ by the one remedy of faith and repentance. Thus Christ teaches us to
raise sinners who approach him humbly with that sweetest formula: “Go in
peace; sin no more.” Paul says, “Such things you once were, but now you are not.”
And John says, “Little children, do not sin. But if anyone sins, he has the
righteous Jesus Christ as advocate in heaven.”

If a repentant person is sometimes commanded to abstain from the Supper for a
time, following the example of Miriam, Moses' sister, and of those who because
of uncleanness were prohibited from eating the Passover for a whole month—just
as not a few other impurities were expiated over seven days—this is not done
rashly. It is done partly so that the repentance of the guilty may be tested,
and partly so that they may be a terror to others, whenever this is expedient
because of the magnitude of the offense. They do not on that account cease to
be regarded as members of Christ and of the Church. I affirm that each and
every person who professes faith in Christ in the Church is bound to this
lawfully constituted discipline.

#### Argument 1

I say expressly that absolutely no one counted within the Christian Church is
exempt from this discipline. I am therefore utterly astonished that Erastus
wishes to exempt at least the magistrates. They are not in this way subjected
to human beings, but to God, whose own jurisdiction this is. Pastors themselves
especially, if they transgress, are answerable to him—not only to be admonished,
reproved, and rebuked, but also, if necessary, to be bound and delivered to
Satan. If this is not done, who will finally excuse us from the crime of
showing partiality?

#### Argument 2

There is nevertheless this difference: because a magistrate's authority is
civil, it cannot be abrogated by any jurisdiction except a civil one.

[Scan 138; printed p. 116]

For the actions performed by the Prophets in such matters were extraordinary,
and whatever the Roman Antichrist has dared to do here has flowed from Satan's
spirit. Conversely, if ministers of the Word or people holding some other
office in God's Church fail to perform their duty, refuse to allow
ecclesiastical censures to recall them to the right path, and dishonor their
ministry and disturb the Church's peace by teaching other doctrines or proving
themselves altogether disorderly, it is the magistrate's duty, as the Church's
nursing father, to take care—while nevertheless preserving due order—that the
Christian commonwealth suffer no harm.

#### Argument 3

Both reason itself and the manifest examples of Scripture confirm the
authority of this power of the King of kings over every earthly lord. It is
not oligarchic or tyrannical but truly divine. For if, in matters pertaining
to conscience, Christ is the one absolute King in his Church, and if he has
erected a presbytery before his tribunal for these affairs—which I trust I
proved above—who could exempt kings and princes from this dominion, which is
not human but divine, without being charged with injuring Christ's divine
majesty? That could be maintained only if we thought that the bounds of this
ministry encompassed merely the common people or private persons, and that in
sacred affairs, just as in civil ones, royal crowns are so immediately subject
to Christ alone that everyone who reproves rulers requires a special command
from God.

Certainly, relying on the Prophets' example and Paul's express words, faithful
stewards of God's mysteries not only may but must reprove, correct, and rebuke
rulers from God's Word. May they not therefore also bind them when stubborn
and rebellious, just as they loose them when repentant? Let David himself be
an example of this divine authority. He submitted himself to Nathan's rebuke
in such a way that he wished an everlasting testimony of his confession to
remain. That confession also expresses clearly enough that he voluntarily
subjected himself to the law of sacrifices for transgression.

We have an even clearer example in King Uzziah and the priest Azariah. Ambrose
closely followed Azariah's footsteps against the emperor Theodosius when the
emperor was guilty of the most monstrous cruelty. Who is not greatly astonished
that this deed, which can never be praised enough, could be attacked by anyone
as though it had proceeded from a tyrannical spirit? Anyone who draws this
most noble history from Ambrose himself rather than from certain polluted
pools—that is, from Ambrose's two letters—will first understand that the
atrocity of that crime was so great that no truly Christian bishop should or
could have silently endured it in an emperor

[Scan 139; printed p. 117]

who professed himself a Christian. Second, he will understand that Ambrose did
nothing unless preceded by a synod of the bishops of Gaul, convened in a holy
and religious manner. What, after all, is there in the letters themselves that
does not savor of Christ's Spirit? The gravity of the rebuke is so tempered
that anyone can readily perceive the admirable skill with which the highest
regard was everywhere shown both for the imperial dignity and for a true
bishop's concern for the sinner's salvation. What is more, Ambrose offers a
fresh hearing before another synod if the emperor should desire it. He asks
nothing except that the emperor come to himself, acknowledge his fault, and
correct it—a fault bound up with so great an offense.

Finally, the outcome that followed this rebuke shows clearly enough the source
from which it proceeded. Nothing did more to honor the life of Ambrose as he
fulfilled the office of a true pastor, or the life of Theodosius as he so
Christianly submitted himself to Christ speaking through the mouth of a true
bishop; and nothing commended both men more highly to every succeeding age.

This, then, is our judgment concerning this part of ecclesiastical discipline.
God is our witness that we have employed it with the greatest moderation we
can and that we now defend it in no other spirit than this: following this
rule of ecclesiastical government, handed down to us by God and confirmed by
unbroken examples from the foundation of the world, we believe we have a firm
foundation on which to rest. We say this without prejudice against those
churches which do not proceed as far as exclusion from the Lord's Supper
because they think that they can do without it, provided that no prejudicial
judgment is made against our churches either. Much less can we agree with
Erastus when he declares this discipline tyrannical.

Certainly, in some German churches it is not difficult to learn from the
writings of all the best and most learned men what they always desired, and
what those still living desire even now. This is true both of the older men,
such as Bucer, Melanchthon, Sarcerius, and nearly everyone whose works I have
been able to inspect, and of more recent men, such as Chytraeus and Hemmingius,
with whom I do not doubt that most others also agree. May God, the Best and
Greatest, in his singular mercy grant that, on this matter also, agreement
among truly godly and learned pastors may somehow be established from God's
Word and with the magistrates' good peace, so that in Christ we may all think
and do one and the same thing in every respect, to the everlasting glory of
his name. Amen.
