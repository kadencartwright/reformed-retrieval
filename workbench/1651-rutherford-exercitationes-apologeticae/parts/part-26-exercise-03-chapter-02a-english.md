### Exercise III, Chapter II

### On the Efficacy of Divine Grace

1. When it is asked whether the efficacy of grace depends upon grace or upon
free choice, the state of the question must be fixed carefully. For it admits
various senses. 1. One possible sense is whether grace has from free choice its
intrinsic power of operating in the manner of grace and of a supernatural
agent. The answer must now be altogether negative. For grace has from free
choice neither its being nor its natural and congenital manner of operating.
Hence Grevinchovius says: *The will confers no power upon grace; rather, grace
confers upon the will power and a propensity to consent.* Suarez likewise adds
the reason: *Hence grace is not properly said to depend upon free choice in its
efficacy, because that expresses causality or the subordination of an inferior
to a superior.* Yet I ask in passing what propensity grace confers upon free
choice. For it confers either a propensity determinative of consent—which they
will not admit—or an indifferent one. But the will naturally had an indifferent
propensity from itself. If it is a supernatural indifferent propensity, then it
will be from grace that the will is indifferent to believing or not believing,

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 205.]

[Marginalia: Suarez, *Opuscula*, book 3, *On Helps*, chapter 14, figure 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 396 | IA leaf 414 | PDF p. 414]

and thus grace disposes the will toward *τὸ non credere*, not believing, and
toward *τὸ peccare*, sinning. O
theologians, where is your judgment, where is your wit? But perhaps it confers
only an inclining propensity. Yet let them pardon my dullness: I do not know
how grace inclines the will to a vital and intrinsic act, such as election,
rejection, and suspension, without taking away indifference. For what sane
person denies that inclining an indifferent propensity to action is at least a
removal of some degrees of indifference? Thus that inclination which is from
grace must diminish Arminian liberty. But if it leaves the will altogether
indifferent, I ask how it inclines it; for to incline is to add a dispositional
weight toward *τὸ velle*, willing, rather than toward *τὸ nolle*, not willing.
2. A second sense is
whether the actual efficiency of grace, according to its specification, is
from free choice. This cannot be said either. For the actual, vital, and
intrinsic action which belongs to free choice, and the action of grace, which
is not vital but altogether external and non-vital, are entirely heterogeneous
and unmixed acts. 3. The proper sense of the question is whether it is from
grace or from free choice that here and now assent rather than dissent is
elicited toward the divine action in the business of conversion, so that it
rests with grace that such an act should exist in nature as to its exercise.
The Arminians wish it to be from free choice, not from God's actual motion, that
we assent rather than dissent. We assert that this depends upon the nature of
grace and upon the actual motion of the Holy

[1651 logical printed p. 397 | IA leaf 415 | PDF p. 415]

Spirit, and upon the decree of election by which all who were appointed to
eternal life believe because they were appointed, Acts 13. Thus the question is
not simply whether the actual efficiency of grace is from grace, but why grace
is actually efficacious in second act for producing conversion in Peter rather
than in Judas. We profess that this is wholly from grace; they deny it and
ascribe it to human liberty. This is the theological dispute.

2. The Jesuits wish to appear to disagree among themselves, and yet they are
sufficiently harmonious. For Fonseca and Bellarmine say that if the efficacy of
grace is said to depend not upon God's congruous motion but upon the cooperation
of free choice, then contrary to the Scriptures a man will distinguish himself;
this was the opinion of Pelagius and the Massilians. No one is a more vehement
champion of congruous calling than Suarez, who nevertheless speaks as follows:
*If the efficacy of grace is nothing other than the action or efficiency of
grace, or a designation taken from it, it can also be conceded that the
efficacy of grace depends upon free choice.* Therefore, in the judgment of
Bellarmine and Fonseca, Suarez openly Pelagianizes. Valentia likewise says: *If,
therefore, it is asked why, of two who have entirely equal calling grace, this
one is converted and that one is not, the answer is that, since the former was
free to be converted by God calling efficaciously, and, assisted by the same
God, to accept that very grace by his own conversion as by something prior in
another genus (namely, that of the material cause), he was plainly converted
in this way and accepted that grace, being assisted, namely, by the same one;*

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 6, chapter 2, question 5, section 14.]

[Marginalia: Bellarmine, volume 4, *On Grace and Free Choice*, book 1, chapter
12.]

[Marginalia: Suarez, book 3, *On Helps*, chapter 14, folio 2.]

[Marginalia: Valentia, volume 2, disputation 8, question 3, point 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 398 | IA leaf 416 | PDF p. 416]

*but the other, although free to do it, did not do it.* So Becanus: *If,
therefore, the first root is asked—why this grace is efficacious and that one
inefficacious—recourse must be had to the free cooperation of the will, not to
God's foreknowledge by which he foresees that this and that grace will be
congruous.* Leonard Lessius says the same. So does Luis de Molina: *A place must
be left for free choice in conversion, upon which it depends whether the sinner
is converted or not converted; and for that reason conversion is attributed to
the sinner himself for praise, and indeed even accrues to him as merit for
eternal life, if once he is restored to grace. Therefore conversion must be
referred not only to the influence of prevenient grace, but also to free choice
and to external helps and circumstances.* The Arminians speak in the same way,
but, as is their custom, the Remonstrant Synodists of Dort do so more cunningly:
*If it is asked what is the cause why this one is converted and that one is
not, we answer that God does not convert this one who does not oppose fresh
stubbornness; the other is not converted because he opposes fresh stubbornness.
You will ask, why does this one oppose fresh stubbornness and the other not? We
answer: this one opposes because he wills to oppose; the other does not oppose
because he is moved by grace not to will to oppose. You will ask whether the
one who does not oppose fresh stubbornness, and is consequently converted,
does not have greater grace than the one who opposes and consequently is not
converted. We answer that antecedent and prevenient grace can be equal, but the
former has cooperating grace, not the latter.* Good God, how cunningly! 1. They
wish the one who does not oppose stubbornness to be moved by grace not to will
to oppose; but the other was also moved,

[Marginalia: Becanus, part 5, *Theology*, chapter 3, thesis 7.]

[Marginalia: Lessius, *On the Efficacy of Grace*, chapter 2; Molina, question
14, article 13, disputation 12.]

[Marginalia: In the synodical writings, article 4, p. 21.]

[1651 logical printed p. 399 | IA leaf 417 | PDF p. 417]

and nevertheless he opposed. Therefore the one who is converted does not
oppose stubbornness, not because he is moved by grace, but because he wills.
But not to oppose stubbornness is freely to convert oneself; therefore he is
converted because he wills. Grevinchovius is much more candid: *No other common
cause of this whole complex (namely, why grace is efficacious in Paul and Peter
rather than in Judas) can be given than liberty of the will.* The Arminians
desert Jacob Arminius, for he embraces congruous grace, which they reject. *I
note only this little word: grace is not called efficacious because it is
necessarily accepted, but because it is certainly accepted and rejected by no
one to whom it is applied.* Suarez himself, although he admits congruous grace,
nevertheless resolves it thus: *To this cooperating grace, however, we say
that the free cooperation of our will must be joined, in whose power it is to
hinder the effect of grace* (that is, the will brings it about that it is
inefficacious) *and to effect it* (that is, it makes it efficacious). *If the
will resists grace, it deserves punishment; but if it cooperates with it, it
deserves praise and glory.* Here all the Jesuits, in the judgment of Fonseca
and Bellarmine, are openly in the Pelagian heresy, and with them also the
Arminians, who do not wish to appear so—indeed they plunge themselves in more
deeply than the Jesuits. The latter at least invented congruous grace, which
the Arminians reject, so as to escape the odium of Pelagian heresy, although
they do not escape it in reality. The Arminians, however, wish to be covered by
no veil: they profess the thing but deny the name of Pelagians.

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 214.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, pp. 770–771.]

[Marginalia: Suarez, *Brief Resolution of the Controversy on the Efficacy of
Grace*, folio 55.]

3. The Jesuits and Arminians rely here especially upon the distinction between
sufficient and efficacious grace. For they wish sufficient grace to be given
to all, by which they can be converted if they should will, but not efficacious
grace, by which they are converted. But you will ask why efficacious grace is
not given to all. The Arminians answer that sufficient and efficacious grace
are distinguished neither in nature nor in species, and often not even in
degree. For those who by free choice place an obstacle before sufficient grace
prevent sufficient grace from becoming efficacious. Consequently, from God's
justice efficacious grace is given to those who place no obstacle and from
justice denied to those who place one. But God is ready and inclined to give
efficacious grace to all, if only free choice were not lacking to God who is
inclined to give it. We, however, do not altogether deny sufficient grace, but
we hold that it is distinguished from efficacious grace in species and nature,
and that efficacious grace is denied and given to those to whom it is given and
denied, not formally from God's justice, but from God's absolute good pleasure.
Thus I acknowledge sufficient grace in six senses. 1. Grace sufficient for
*τὸ posse*, being able, in Adam, for he was able by himself. But we deny such
grace for *τὸ posse*, being able, in man's conversion, unless it is understood as the very same
grace which is also efficacious. 2. Grace sufficient in the genus of external
means, whether of the law of nature or of the revealed gospel, by which the Son
of God wills. 3. Grace sufficient for God's eternal intention to manifest the
glory of justice—I add, also efficacious, for God grants the reprobate such

[1651 logical printed p. 401 | IA leaf 419 | PDF p. 419]

grace that, from its rejection, the glory of divine justice shines manifestly
in their destruction. 4. Grace sufficient for *ἀναπολογίαν*, being without
excuse. 5. Grace sufficient in the regenerate elect for being able, not for willing; God
must therefore add actualization to habitual grace. 6. Grace sufficient in the
non-elect for external discipline, as much as is fitting for preserving human
society and equitable for God so to ordain from his admirable liberty. 2.
Indeed, in Scripture's manner of speech the word *sufficiency* is not
distinguished from efficacy. In 2 Corinthians 12:9, *Ἀρκεῖ σοι ἡ χάρις μου, My
grace is sufficient for you*, it is ridiculous to say that God there promises
Paul a sufficient grace
which, even when posited, could leave Paul to yield to temptation and so fail.
For in that way Paul's prayers would be just as much unheard as heard, which no
sane person would say. Matthew 25:9: the wise virgins said to the foolish, *Not
so, lest there not be enough for us and you—εἰκ ἀρκέσῃ, it may not suffice*—that
is, actually, when the bridegroom came, at which time so much efficacious grace
as befits the glorified was necessary. But that grace is not merely sufficient
as distinguished from efficacious. For it certainly would not have been enough
to receive the bridegroom. John 6:7: Philip said, *Two hundred denarii worth of
bread would not suffice for them, so that each might receive a little.* Philip,
ignorant of the future miracle, undoubtedly understood that so much bread was
not actually sufficient to feed so many. John 14:8: Philip said to him, *Lord,
show me the Father, and it is sufficient for us—κἂ ἀρκεῖ ἡμῖν.* The word ἱκανὸς
has the same force in 2 Corinthians 3:5.

4. Let us see whether this distinction bears the file

[1651 logical printed p. 402 | IA leaf 420 | PDF p. 420]

of scholastic examination. I therefore say it is wholly foreign to reason that
a medicine of nine degrees, duly applied to a wound of eight degrees, should
not cure that wound. For if it does not cure it, more degrees of stronger
humors were in the wound than in the medicine; therefore the medicine was not
sufficient but unequal to the wound and less than sufficient. The case is
plainly the same in the operation of sufficient grace. Now one cause is in
potency, as medicine not yet applied can cure a disease; another is in act,
namely, one which actually causes. Therefore the inference from a cause in
potency to an effect in act is vain, as everyone knows.

I now presuppose three things here: 1. that it is a cause; 2. that it is
sufficient—let sufficient grace be as eight, corruption as seven; 3. that it
is a cause in act, not in potency. Now, when all due requisites are present,
grace heals diseased nature; otherwise grace was less than sufficient. But the
Arminians cry out: because the will is a free cause and acts morally, it is
badly compared with a physical cause such as medicine. For grant that grace is
sufficient as eight and corruption as seven; nevertheless liberty is of such
a nature that it can hinder the application of grace, because a free will so
acts that it can fail to act. It can therefore happen that corruption as seven
resists grace as eight. I answer: 1. I did not badly institute the sort of
comparison which the sacred oracles institute, according to the virtual
degrees of acting in grace and in corruption. The application

[1651 logical printed p. 403 | IA leaf 421 | PDF p. 421]

of grace is also from God's grace, not from free choice. Therefore, if a
corrupt will hinders the application of grace, grace so hindered was not
sufficient, contrary to the hypothesis; and the corruption which predominated
over it and hindered the application of grace was stronger than grace. Romans
7:5: *For when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins through the law were
vigorous in our members to bear fruit unto death.* 1 John 2:14: *I write to
you, young men, because you are strong, and the word of God dwells in you, and
you have overcome that wicked one.* 1 John 4:4: *But you are of God, children,
and have overcome them, because he who is in you is greater than he who is in
the world.* Ephesians 3:16: *That according to the riches of his glory he may
grant you to be mightily strengthened by his Spirit in the inner man.* 17. *That
Christ may dwell by faith in your hearts.* 18. *That, rooted and grounded in
love, you may be able to comprehend with all the saints what that breadth is,*
and so forth. 1 Corinthians 10:13: *But God is faithful, who will not permit you
to be tempted beyond what you are able, but with the temptation will also
provide a way out, that you may be able to bear it.* Therefore, if temptation
to sin is unequal in strength to the grace which is resisted, and if grace is
greater, it must overcome corruption. Otherwise John would not ascribe it to
strength and might that believers conquer the demon who strives against us by
the powers of our corruption. 2. For I ask how it happens that we overcome sin:
is it not through God's grace? Then I ask wherein the prevailing power of grace
above the powers of corruption is located. The Arminians answer: in this, that
grace elicits

[1651 logical printed p. 404 | IA leaf 422 | PDF p. 422]

consent to the good. But this is only an actual excess, which can just as well
not exist, if free choice should so will, as exist. The strength of prevailing
grace, however, consists in this: that it removes rebellion, stubbornness, and
hardness, according to Ezekiel 36:26: *I shall remove the stony heart from your
midst.* 3. As for their saying that liberty has a nature which can hinder the
effect of grace, I answer thus. The question is whether corruption is not the
greatest part of the stony heart—both its ability to oppose stubbornness and
its actually opposing it. Surely one must be blind to deny this. For what is
the ability to oppose stubbornness, and actually to oppose it, except the
will's perversity and hardness? Therefore, if grace takes this away, as
Scripture says, then where it is not taken away grace is not sufficient. For a
sufficient cause has overflowing power to produce the effect if applied
actually according to every degree of its power; and such is that action of the
Holy Spirit by which the stony heart is removed. For grace removes no degrees
of hardness, or some only, or all. If none, it therefore does not remove the
stony heart, contrary to Scripture's assertion. Ezekiel 36:26; 2 Corinthians
13:1: *When the heart turns to the Lord, the veil will be removed.* 2
Corinthians 13:7: *Now I pray to God that you do no evil.* Acts 16:14: *The Lord
opened Lydia's heart.* If grace removes some degrees of hardness, for the same
reason it will remove them all. For the reason is the same: why would it remove
three degrees and not

[1651 logical printed p. 405 | IA leaf 423 | PDF p. 423]

seven? They will say, because the will can always refuse, even when the most
sufficient grace is posited. I answer: 1. Then even when three degrees of
hardness are removed, the will can refuse. Therefore grace will remove no
degrees at all, still contrary to the Scriptures. 2. Grant that the power of
pure nature remains; grace must remove the stubbornness and hardness adhering
to that power, for this is (so to speak) the heart's stoniness. In that case, if
that power of pure nature exercises a purely natural act of refusing, it can
happen that a man resists God calling and does not sin, which is absurd. 3. The
ability to place an obstacle before God calling either is corruption—and by
the same reasoning the act of placing the obstacle will be sin—or the ability
to resist God calling is not corruption. But then I ask whether the stony heart
is nothing but actual resistance and is not also the crookedness of the power.
If it is only actual resistance, then by taking away the stony heart God takes
away *τὸ actu resistere*, actually resisting, which the Arminians will deny. If
they say that God takes away *τὸ actu resistere*, actually resisting, upon the
hypothesis, I grant the hypothesis,
namely, that they have not actually resisted. Even if they beat their brains
with a hammer they will not give the hypothesis. 4. No one but Pelagius would
say that no viciousness inheres in the power of the will, or that no habitual
viciousness inheres in us after the fall. But if some inheres, is it not
removed when the stony heart is removed? 5. If the nature of liberty is such

[1651 logical printed p. 406 | IA leaf 424 | PDF p. 424]

that it can prevent sufficient grace from being applied according to all its
degrees, then grace is not a sufficient cause in act. For a cause not applied
is not a cause in act, but only in potency, and thus is not formally a cause.
It is therefore most absurdly called a sufficient cause if our will resists God
as he takes away the stony heart. Thus the stony heart will resist God as he
takes away the stony heart; therefore such a heart is not taken away but
remains, contrary to Ezekiel 36. Consequently, on the Arminian opinion it is
wrongly said that the reprobate resist sufficient grace which is not applied;
indeed, on this account they would not be guilty of spurning the inward call.
6. But they will say they are guilty because they themselves hinder the
application of sufficient grace. Yet this stone will always roll back upon
them: hindering the application of sufficient grace is no small part of the
stony heart. But God promises that he will take away the stony heart from his
elect, something which he does not accomplish because the free will prevents
sufficient grace from being applied. 7. It is asked whether hindering the
application of sufficient grace is not sin. If it is, it is avoidable by
another sufficient grace which operates either in a way that can be turned
aside, because liberty also hinders the application of that grace, or
undeviatingly. If the former, that hindrance is a sin avoidable by another
sufficient grace, and an infinite regress will be given—behold these dizzy
spirits! If the latter, we have our point.

5. Whatever arguments are brought forward by our opponents are an absurd
begging of the question. Never

[1651 logical printed p. 407 | IA leaf 425 | PDF p. 425]

will they give a sufficient cause which is distinguished from an efficacious
one in the Arminian sense. They say that medicine is a sufficient cause for
healing a disease but is not efficacious because the sick man refuses to drink
it from stubborn intemperance; that a fountain of cold water is sufficient for
quenching thirst but not efficacious because the thirsty man refuses to drink;
that God is a sufficient cause for creating several worlds but not efficacious
because he decreed not to create more; and that an orator sufficiently urges
peace between the Thebans and Athenians, but they are not efficaciously
persuaded, although Demosthenes' arguments are in themselves most powerful. I
answer: 1. Those who establish medicine as the cause of healing a disease
certainly need a dose of hellebore. Who suffering from disease would then not
be cured, if a cause in potency were a cause? But I consider medicine taken and
applied to be a sufficient cause for healing disease; and then, if it does not
heal, the medicine was insufficient. Nor is water a sufficient cause for
quenching thirst unless a powerful cause is meant; water taken and drunk is a
sufficient cause. Those who wish God simply to be the creator of the world are
mistaken: for in that case, since God existed from eternity, the world would
have existed from eternity. But God as a free agent is the sufficient cause of
the created world; otherwise God himself is only a cause able to create the
world. Unless this is said, God's free will must have no causative sufficiency
for the actual creation of the world. Finally, no speech is of itself a
sufficient cause of a free effect, because

[1651 logical printed p. 408 | IA leaf 426 | PDF p. 426]

the free determination of the will must intervene for actual causation. All
these causes are able causes, not sufficient ones, because sufficiency requires
a further causative power for producing the effect. 2. To untie the whole
matter, it must be noted that our opponents go most astray here because they do
not observe that a cause is called sufficient in two ways: 1. physically; 2.
morally. Physically, a man skilled in metaphysics is a sufficient cause for
educating the young in that faculty; but he is not immediately sufficient
morally because, as we suppose, he does not will to teach them. Thus a physician
is a physically sufficient cause for curing the diseases of many poor men; but
in the genus of moral cause he is not sufficient, because he cannot and does
not will to cure without payment. Yet the art of medicine taken by itself does
not act, but the art as employed by the physician's free choice does. God is a
sufficient cause by physical sufficiency for creating two or three worlds, but
in the genus of moral cause he is not sufficient, for he did not determine his
most holy choice except to create one world. The Arminians therefore argue with
the Jesuits as follows: grace is sufficient for converting the reprobate;
therefore their failure to be converted ought to be charged to them as a
fault. Although the consequent is most true, the consequence is nevertheless
vain and null. For if grace is only physically sufficient, as in reality it is,
I may argue thus: this physician is a sufficient cause for curing this poor
feverish man; therefore

[1651 logical printed p. 409 | IA leaf 427 | PDF p. 427]

the poor feverish man's failure to be cured ought to be charged to him as a
fault. Nothing could be more foolish than the consequence, for in his poverty
he cannot purchase the physician's work and art with money. Thus here that
Arminian sufficient grace is morally insufficient for this very reason: either
it is not applied, and so is only a cause in potency; or, granted that it is
applied, it is not at least one degree—namely, as eight—more active and powerful
than the rebellious will as seven, and so is not a sufficient cause. And if it
is more active by this degree and duly applied by God, it is undoubtedly also
an efficacious cause. Therefore there is no simply sufficient grace which is
not efficacious. For that sufficient grace either can expel all hardness, and
thus will be efficacious—since no heart resists God calling except one which is
hard, stubborn, and rebellious—or it cannot expel all hardness. Anyone can see
that the Arminians attribute to their sufficient grace no causative sufficiency
by which it applies the will to the work. For they attribute that to the liberty
of pure nature, and thus for them the use of grace is from the liberty of pure
nature, not from God's grace. For in their view it can be sufficient even if
the will is never applied to the work. But among all who have philosophized it
has hitherto always been commonly believed that a cause which cannot produce
the effect is an insufficient cause. Yet they say it is insufficient not from
a defect of power in grace itself, but because grace always acts without
removing *τὸ nolle vel velle Deo vocanti consentire*, the refusal or willingness
to consent to God calling. But

[1651 logical printed p. 410 | IA leaf 428 | PDF p. 428]

I reply: this is the greatest defect in the power of grace, that it cannot take
away the sinful *τὸ nolle consentire*, refusal to consent, and so conquer
rebellion—especially since
in Scripture our victory and triumph over sin, the world, and the devil are
expressly attributed to the strength of divine grace. 1 Peter 1:5; Philippians
4:13; 1 John 4:4; Ephesians 3:16. For according to the Arminians, if sufficient
grace and its action are posited while there remains in me a *τὸ nolle
consentire*, refusal to consent to God calling, I undoubtedly do not overcome
the devil and sin, as
John says, because the Holy Spirit dwelling in me through grace is stronger
than the devil and sin. For even when the strength of the gracious Spirit is
posited, it can happen that the devil who is in the world conquers, and not the
Spirit who is in us. If it is not from the power and strength of grace that
*non τὸ nolle consentire*, no refusal to consent to God calling, is in me rather
than in Judas, then it
will be from the pure nature of free choice, and therefore the victory will be
owed to nature, not grace. Let our opponents give a third alternative, and I
will willingly withdraw from the case.

6. Now, so that the efficacy of grace and actual predetermination may appear
more clearly, I wish this to be noted. When we teach that predetermining grace
by its intrinsic power inclines the will to *τὸ consentire*, consent, rather
than to dissent,
this is understood in three ways. 1. Efficacious grace prevents the impulse and
motion of vicious obliquity and crookedness which adheres extrinsically to
liberty. I gladly admit this sense. For unless God forestalls and preoccupies
actual hardness in acts

[1651 logical printed p. 411 | IA leaf 429 | PDF p. 429]

of grace, I do not see how God takes away the stony heart. Ezekiel 36:17. 2. A
second sense is that predetermining grace by its intrinsic power penetrates, as
it were, to the depth of the vital inclination and elective impetus, and thus
restrains nature's first *ὁρμήν*, impulse, and drive within liberty; the will
inclines
toward the northern angle, so to speak, of such a volition, while God's
predetermination twists it toward the southern angle. Our opponents most
falsely fasten this sense upon us. 3. God's predetermining grace does not
penetrate in this fashion, but, resting upon nature's impetus, so
undeviatingly bends the will that, in a wondrous manner, grace powerfully and
the will vitally and electively conspire toward the numerically same object, at
the same time, place, and intensity. Under that motion of God the will would
tend toward no other object than the same one toward which it would have
inclined itself freely, supposing per impossibile that there had been no
predetermination in God. Yet grace first and principally predetermines this
object and no other, while the will, subordinately and dependently, by the
internal, vital, commanding, and elective power of liberty, tends toward that
same object. This also is the true sense.
