## Part XXXVI: Christ Predestining and Predestined

[Part II | PDF page 201 | logical source scan 200 | printed page 199 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

How many things, I ask, are said in so few words, partly irrelevant and partly false? Andreae constantly calls the decree of predestination itself “absolute,” as though we considered it without Christ and in isolation. As we often showed in the discussion of Christ’s person, so here Andreae thinks whatever is considered distinctly is also separated. The matter stands thus: we never separate the decree of predestination, the intermediate causes subordinated to its execution, and the execution itself. We often distinguish them and sometimes join them, following God’s Word and reason itself, which requires that they be considered sometimes in themselves and sometimes insofar as they cohere. Thus God is said to have elected us before the world’s foundations were laid, namely with the eternal decree considered distinctly and as within God’s own bosom. Augustine writes of this on the final Psalm: “Our predestination was not accomplished in us but secretly with him in his foreknowledge. The remaining three occur in us: calling, justification, and glorification.” Preserving this distinction, we who are predestined by God in Christ are by no means separated from him, but are considered distinctly as called, justified, and finally to be glorified. Thus the predestining one is distinguished from those predestined, and the decree from the causes subordinated to its execution and from the execution itself. In the same way, God’s own Son—

—must necessarily be considered in two ways in this discussion. Insofar as he is one coeternal God with the Father and, as God’s Word, God with God, then with respect to the eternal purpose considered in itself he is not predestined but predestines with the Father and Holy Spirit. He coordinates both himself as future human, everything to be bestowed upon us by the Father, and thus the intermediate causes themselves. Insofar as he is human, however, he is himself not only predestined and subordinated, gratuitously from eternity, but also as the future head of everyone predestined to eternal life—namely every individual actually to be elected, called, justified, and finally glorified in him at the proper time.

Those who either ignore or fail to observe this supremely important distinction entangle everything in this inherently complex treatment and lead themselves and others into trackless places. But Augustine, harassed by the Pelagians, to whom the church owes the true and, when all is carefully considered, clear explanation of this mystery, everywhere accurately observes the distinction I described between the decree itself and the causes subordinated to its execution. So too he notes in many places this twofold distinction of Christ as predestining and predestined, demonstrating it fully in book 1, chapter 15 and book 2, chapter 24 of *On the Predestination of the Saints*. Defining it plainly in Tractate 17 on John, he says: “Before this world existed, neither did we exist, nor did Jesus Christ himself, the mediator of God and humanity.” And shortly after: “No one who attends to the rule of faith will deny that the Son of God was predestined if he cannot deny that he became human. He is rightly said not to be predestined insofar as he is God’s Word, God with God. For why would one be predestined who already was what he was, eternal without beginning or end? What did not yet exist had to be predestined to exist in its time, just as it had been predestined before all times to exist.”

Christ must therefore be considered differently as the efficient cause of predestination with the Father and Holy Spirit, and as the first effect of predestination concerning the elect to be saved through mercy in him. Accordingly, if in what Andreae writes here he considers Christ as predestining, he most foolishly thinks this removes what is established conversely: the decree he spitefully and falsely calls absolute, but which we call hidden because it rests upon God’s will alone, at whose depth the apostle himself stands amazed. Until it is given us through the Holy Spirit to possess the Lord’s mind (1 Corinthians 2:16; Isaiah 25:3), it lies as though in God’s bosom, as we shortly above declared from Augustine. But if he considers Christ as predestined and the head of the predestined, Andreae does what he habitually does: he fails to distinguish the decree from the causes subordinated to it.

His further claim that all human beings are called by Christ to repentance is not true even of the outward call if, as he wishes, it is understood of every individual. We have shown a thousand times that this is not merely falsely but ridiculously asserted, and the matter itself testifies to it from almost every age. Christ certainly said not that all but that many are called (Matthew 20:16). What Christian is ignorant of the inward call, which necessarily follows foreordination to eternal life (Acts 13:48; 22:14), whether called the opening of the heart (Acts 16:14) or drawing (John 6:44), concerning which Christ says, “Everyone who has heard and learned from my Father comes to me” (John 6:45)?
