ON MIDDLE KNOWLEDGE

BOOK ONE — PART FIFTY-NINE

PENOTTUS.

Third, the same truth is proved by reason. If God knows future sins in a decree, he knows them either in a
concomitant, subsequent, or antecedent decree. The first and second cannot be said, because from a
concomitant decree it cannot be known that the will accompanies the divine concurrence. For when two wills
come together at once for one act, either they come together by chance—and this cannot be said of the divine
will—or one moves the other; and this cannot be said in such a case, because the divine will would not be
concomitant but either prior or subsequent to the human will; or one foreknows that, if it joins its
concurrence to the other, the latter will issue into act. In that case God would have to know from another
source than the concomitant decree that the human will would issue into act. But if it is said that this is
an antecedent decree, as in fact some say—that God knows future morally evil acts in a twofold decree, by
one of which he decreed to predetermine the human will to the sin’s natural entity, and by the other decreed
to permit its moral wickedness—

Against this answer I argue: once this decree is posited, does the wickedness of the sin itself follow
necessarily and infallibly, or only contingently and fallibly? If the first is said, the human will is no
longer free to sin but will sin necessarily, and we shall fall back into manifest Calvinism; for Calvin
says nothing, or little, more. If the second is said, then from such a decree God cannot know certainly that
the man will sin. For if, even with the decree posited, he can still refrain from sinning, then it can be
posited in actuality that he does not sin, and so God will not know him to be going to sin.

The argument is confirmed from Thomas’s doctrine. Is the human will necessarily determined by the divine
decree to add moral wickedness to the natural act, or not? If you say the first, God now determines the
will not only to the natural act but to the wickedness of the sin, and is the author of wickedness—which
even Calvin does not dare openly affirm. If the second, I argue thus: so long as an effect is in a
contingent cause not yet determined to that effect, the effect cannot be known infallibly by any knowledge,
created or uncreated, according to Thomas, First Part, question 14, article 13. But when that twofold decree
is posited, the human will still remains undetermined to posit moral wickedness. Therefore, from that
twofold decree no intellect, created or uncreated, can know that the will will sin infallibly.

RESPONSE.

The distinction concerns a decree made from eternity, but its members have no congruity with a decree made
from eternity and instead suit a transitive operation of God occurring in time. For it is impossible that
God’s decree either accompany or follow the creature’s operation. It should rather be framed in this way:
if God knows future sins in a decree, he knows them in a decree concomitantly with the created will to act,
or subsequently, or antecedently. We do not say that God knows sins in a decree only concomitantly or
subsequently to acting. Nevertheless, it will be worth examining how Penottus proves that sins cannot be
known from a concomitant or subsequent decree according to his threefold disjunction. “When two wills come
together at once for one act,” he says, “either they come together by chance, and this cannot be said of the
divine will.” But, I say, neither can it be said of the human will that it comes together by chance in
relation to God as agent. Perhaps this is what Penottus means to signify: that nothing can happen casually
with respect to God, not even the creature’s concurrence with God to produce some act. But how will
Penottus make good this point? What then? Does he not maintain that the secondary cause concurs with God
either casually or fortuitously? For although it does not concur beyond God’s knowledge, it is held to
concur beyond God’s intention. But the fortuitous is defined by the Philosopher as whatever occurs beyond
the agent’s intention. On Penottus’s account God is not held to intend that the creature will concur in
some sinful act, but only to foreknow it. Penottus proceeds to the second member of the disjunction: “or one
moves the other, and this,” he says, “cannot be said in such a case, because the divine will would not be
concomitant but

[Book I, printed p. 139; BSB canvas 00165]

either prior or subsequent to the human will.” But this too, I say, is proposed far too loosely. You prove
only that it is not concomitant in this case; you do not prove that it is not subsequent. Yet you had
undertaken to prove that it is neither concomitant nor subsequent. The third member of the disjunction,
however, is most worthy of a longer examination: “or,” he says, “one foreknows that, if it joins its
concurrence to the other, then the latter will issue into act; and then God would have to know from another
source than the concomitant decree that the human will would issue into act.” But although one foreknows
this, I say, it will not thereby cease to be casual, for what happens beyond the intention is casual, even
if it does not happen beyond foreknowledge. More importantly, when you say that God must know from another
source than the concomitant decree that the human will will issue into act, what, I ask, is that source from
which God must know it? None whatever can be contrived, and nowhere do you even attempt to show whence God
knows this.

Molina seems to ascribe it to the infinity of the divine intellect or divine knowledge. But you by no
means approve this; indeed, in chapter 23, number 1, you do not allow so absurd an opinion to be foisted
upon Molina. For your own part you think it prudent to rest in this: that God knows what a created will
would will if placed in such an order, provided God would concur in producing that same volition. Yet you
do not even attempt to designate whence, or from what source, he knows it. Again, that a creature placed in
such an order will will, provided God lends his concurrence, is by its nature only possible. Who will
explain how it comes to migrate from the condition of a merely possible thing to that of a future thing?
Not Penottus, for he has not explained it thus far in these ten books, nor will he ever explain it in twenty
others unless he has recourse to an antecedent decree of God by which it is determined to be future. We
defend this antecedent divine decree; let us next see how Penottus refutes it. We say that, once the
antecedent decree of God is posited, the wickedness of the sin itself follows necessarily and infallibly by
a necessity from the hypothesis—that is, a qualified and modal necessity—in this form: once it is posited
that God has decreed that such a free act come to pass with respect to the substance of the act, though it
is contrary to God’s law, it is necessary that such an act come to pass. Yet it does not follow that the
will is not free to do what it does, because God decreed not only that such an act come to pass but that it
come to pass in such a manner—that is, not necessarily but contingently and freely. Hence it follows that
it is necessary not only that it come to pass, but also that it come to pass freely. What then? Does it not
lie with God to decree that a creature perform some free act? Nor, if the will conducts itself freely, will
it follow that God cannot foreknow that the man will sin. For although the act is conjoined with the power
to act otherwise or not to act at all, once God’s decree to determine the human will to the substance of
this nonconforming act is posited, neither can another act be produced for that instant nor can this act
fail to be produced. Let Aquinas be heard in First Part, question 19, article 8, in the body: “Because the
divine will is most efficacious, it follows not only that the things God wills to come to pass do come to
pass, but that they come to pass in the manner in which God wills them to come to pass. God wills some
things to come to pass necessarily and others contingently, so that there may be order in things for the
completion of the universe. Therefore, to some effects he adapted necessary causes which could not fail,
from which effects proceed by necessity; to others, however, he adapted contingent and defectible causes,
from which effects occur contingently.”

As for the confirmation, we deny altogether that the divine decree determines the will to add wickedness
to an act. Nor does any such thing as Penottus imagines enter into a human decree, for wickedness results
from positing the act according to its substance when God’s law forbidding such an act is presupposed. No
special act is required to add wickedness to the act. Thus, in a sin of commission the act’s wickedness
requires no determination of the cause to produce that wickedness beyond the determination of the cause to
produce the substance of the act itself. Therefore, Penottus speaks falsely when he says that, once a
decree to determine the substance of the act and to permit its wickedness is posited, the human will still
remains undetermined to posit moral wickedness. Observe in passing that Penottus here concedes that when
Aquinas, First Part, question 14, article 13, denies that God foreknows future contingent effects in their
secondary causes, he must be understood to mean secondary causes not yet determined. By this Penottus
implies that, according to Aquinas, God can infallibly foreknow future contingents in secondary causes
which are determined and will not be impeded. But he denied this earlier and professed that, on Aquinas’s
opinion, future contingents cannot be foreknown at all in their causes, whether considered as undetermined
or as determined, above, chapter 20, number 7.

PENOTTUS.

Fourth, the same truth is principally proved by overthrowing the chief foundation of the contrary opinion.
Its chief foundation is that without God’s special assistance a man cannot avoid temptation and cannot by
his own strength restrain himself from falling. It therefore seems to follow that, if God decreed to permit
a man to fall, he sees in the same decree that the man will necessarily fall. Yet our opinion is especially
proved from that same foundation, if we presuppose one article of faith: that God abandons no man—or at
least no justified man—unless the man first abandons him. To omit Augustine’s innumerable testimonies, the
Council of Trent expressly defined this in Session 6, *On Justification*, chapter 11, where it defines that
God does not abandon those once justified unless he is first abandoned by them; and in chapter 13, where
it says: “Although all ought to place and repose the firmest hope in God’s help, for unless they fail his
grace, God, as he began the good work, will also complete it, working both willing and accomplishing.” The
Council’s reasoning is that, although a man cannot fulfill the divine commandments by the powers of nature
alone, he can fulfill them with divine assistance, which he always has at hand, by which God abandons no
justified person unless that person first abandons God through sin. With this presupposed, I argue thus. A
man’s sinning and God himself foreseeing that the man abandons him through sin are prior to the decree to
abandon the man by permitting him to fall into sin. Therefore, in the decree to abandon the man by
permitting him to fall into sin, God cannot know infallibly that the man will sin.

[Book I, printed p. 140; BSB canvas 00166]

RESPONSE.

Penottus disputes against his own papists. Even while they profess that God foreknows the futurition of sin
in the decree to permit sin, he acknowledges that they rest upon this foundation: that without God’s
special assistance a man cannot avoid temptations and restrain himself by his own powers from falling.
Therefore, when our theologians acknowledge this very thing, they teach nothing other than what not a few
of the papists themselves profess to be true. See also what Thomas Bradwardine, a scholastic of great name
among the papists, taught about this 170 years ago. In book 2 of *The Cause of God Against Pelagius*,
chapter 4, he undertakes to prove that “free choice under temptation cannot overcome any temptation by its
own powers without God’s assistance and grace.” In the following chapter 5, “That free choice, supported by
however much grace, cannot overcome any temptation without another special assistance of God.” In chapter
7, “That no one under temptation, by the powers of free choice alone, without grace or with however much
created grace, can avoid any sin without another assistance of God.” In chapter 8, in the corollary, “That
no wayfarer, by the powers of free choice alone, or grace, or the two conjoined, can persevere for any time
at all without another special assistance of God.” And in chapter 9, “That neither a man nor an angel,
before the fall and with however much grace, could persevere finally or for a time without another special
assistance of God.” But let us examine Penottus and weigh the worth of what he offers. His opinion rests
upon a passage from the Council of Trent, in which they profess that God will work both willing and
accomplishing, provided that we do not fail his grace. Here Penottus had to explain what it means not to
fail God’s grace, upon which condition God will work willing and accomplishing in us. Let us propose an
instance: God will work in us the will to believe and the will to repent, provided we do not fail his
grace. And what else, I ask, is it not to fail divine grace but to heed his admonitions? Therefore, while he
admonishes us to believe and repent, provided we heed those admonitions, God will work in us so that we
believe and repent. But to heed God as he admonishes us to believe and repent is nothing whatever but to
believe and repent. The splendid doctrine of these theologians therefore comes to this: God will bring it
about that we believe and repent provided we believe and repent. And this is God’s not abandoning us unless
we first abandon God: not to abandon God is to obey his voice; if we do that—that is, if we obey his
voice—God will not abandon us, that is, he will ensure that we obey his voice. God commands nothing
impossible, but we say that the things God commands are possible only through grace, not through the
natural powers of free choice. Penottus again repeats his stale refrain: God abandons no justified person
unless that person first abandons God through sin. Thus a man first sins against God, and then God abandons
the man—that is, permits him to fall into sin. Would not even the mad Orestes swear that these are the
words of a man not in his right mind? Yet Penottus presents these inanities as leaves of the Sibyl or so
many oracles. He soon makes clear that he defines God’s abandonment as God’s permission that a man fall into
sin, and openly pronounces that God foresees the man’s sin before decreeing to abandon him—that is, before
permitting him to fall into sin—so that on this point Penottus is far more senseless than our Arminians. But
if God foresees the man’s sin, and that from eternity, then the man’s sin was future from eternity, though
not by its own nature, for by its own nature a man’s sin is only possible. Whence, then, did it come to
migrate from the condition of a possible thing to that of a future thing? Such a migration could not occur
without a cause. Where, then, will Penottus at last contrive some cause of this migration? Will he invent a
cause outside God? Such a fiction would be purely chimerical, for nothing outside God existed from
eternity. What did not exist from eternity could not be the cause of the migration or transmutation of
anything from eternity. Therefore, the cause of this transmutation must be sought either within God or
nowhere. But within God nothing occurs that could be the cause of this transition except God’s will. For
the knowledge of vision, according to Penottus, presupposes future things rather than making them future;
and both God’s essence and his knowledge, when his will is excluded, produce no change concerning created
or creatable things. Therefore, give glory to God and frankly profess that nothing whatever is future
except because it is willed by God, though with this distinction: all good things are willed by God to come
to pass with God doing them, while evil things are so willed by God that they come to pass only with God
permitting them. Bellarmine himself pronounces that it is good for evil to come to pass with God
permitting. Augustine’s statement on this matter is indeed explicit: “Nothing comes to pass unless the
omnipotent wills it to come to pass, either by permitting it to come to pass or by doing it himself.”

PENOTTUS.

To answer this argument, certain more recent authors say that the withdrawal of divine assistance can be
considered in two ways: in one way as a simple negation, and in another as a privation. Although, they say,
insofar as it is a privation a man’s abandonment of God through sin is prior to his being abandoned by God
through the withdrawal of divine assistance, nevertheless, if it is considered as a negation, God’s
abandonment of the man is prior to the man’s abandonment of him. But if one is permitted to speak this way,
it certainly seems a splendid and charming defense of the holy council’s decrees—and the overthrow of its
foundation. For those who answer this way must be asked whether that decree in the divine will to withdraw
the assistance of divine grace was prior to the foreknowledge of the man’s fall or subsequent to that
foreknowledge. If it is subsequent, the sin was not first foreseen in that decree, and recourse must be had
to another ground. But if it is said to be prior, God decreed to abandon the man before the man decreed to
abandon God, which formally and in express terms contradicts the definition.

RESPONSE.

This distinction is employed by Alvarez in disputations 101 and 102 *On the Aids of Grace* and, in my
judgment, is sufficiently scholastic. Although I think Alvarez’s application of the distinction was
conceived beyond all sobriety, Penottus does not attack it with scholastic weapons. In a special
digression, however, I have already taken pains to expose the insanity of his construction. Penottus merely
tries to show that this distinction has no force whatever to weaken his argument drawn from the Council

[Book I, printed p. 141; BSB canvas 00167]

of Trent. Yet Alvarez, in the place cited, mentions no proposition taken from the Council of Trent to whose
interpretation he might adapt this distinction. Still, it is worth testing the weight of Penottus’s
disputation, though he proceeds according to his own pleasure in applying this distinction. “If,” he says,
“the decree to withdraw assistance is subsequent to foreknowledge of the fall, the sin was not first
foreseen in that decree.” Answer: Alvarez discusses the decree to withdraw the assistance for a man’s
conversion after he has fallen into sin; he does not discuss a decree to withdraw assistance from which
the man’s fall into sin follows. The withdrawal of the prior assistance is indeed the cause why the man is
not converted negatively, but not, he says, the cause why he is not converted privatively and culpably. It
is clear that the cause of this must be traced to original sin. It is altogether true that our corruption
is the intrinsic cause of every sin, although we would not sin if God supplied efficacious assistance, as
Thomas teaches in First Part of the Second Part, question 79, article 1, in the body: “God does not give
certain persons assistance for avoiding sins; if he gave it, they would not sin.” But let us ascend to the
first sin. God knew from the decree to permit it that this sin would be future, and according to Alvarez,
disputation 101, number 7, once permission is posited a man sins infallibly. “Therefore,” says the man of
Novara, “God decreed to abandon the man before the man decreed to abandon God,” as though any sober person
would doubt this, since it is sufficiently clear that God’s decrees are eternal while men’s decrees are
temporal. You ought to have said: before God foresaw that the man would abandon his God. But if abandoning a
man is nothing other than permitting him to sin, who but a complete dullard would doubt that God must
permit a man to sin before the man sins, and consequently that the decree to permit sin is prior to the
foresight of sin? But I deny that to permit a man to sin, especially the first sin, is to abandon the man;
for abandonment denotes something penal, whereas it is impossible for permission of the first sin to be
something penal. Everything penal presupposes sin rather than preceding it.

I do not say that God withdraws special assistance, which is Penottus’s phrase, but that he does not give
to some an assistance with which, if he gave it, the man would not sin. This is Aquinas’s perfectly clear
opinion in First Part of the Second Part, question 79, article 1, in the body. Yet exhortations to persevere
and fulfill the precepts of the divine law are not vain. They are not vain even for the reprobate, but
serve to take away their excuse—namely, the sort of excuse by which human pride is accustomed to say, “If I
had known, I would have done it.” Thus Augustine, *On Grace and Free Choice*, chapter 2: “How does he say
that they are inexcusable except regarding that excuse by which human pride is accustomed to say, ‘If I
had known, I would have done it; therefore I did not do it because I did not know’?” How much less will
exhortations be useless for the elect, from whom God will by no means withhold special assistance,
especially since he is accustomed to supply such assistance through ministerial exhortations. Therefore,
although I cannot do without God’s special assistance that to which I am exhorted, it can happen that God
concurs with that exhortation through his Spirit and supplies me that special assistance. And although a
regenerate man can be conscious of his election through the Spirit of God whom he received in his
regeneration, no one can be conscious of his reprobation so as to have cause to say: before God saw me
turned away from him by my own will, he had already decreed within himself to leave me without such
assistance. Those who once raged against Augustine’s doctrine of predestination did not conduct the matter
so crudely, but slandered him for being able to preach the following indefinitely before a congregation of
the people.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On the Good of Perseverance*, chapter 15.]

“The fixed judgment of God’s will concerning predestination is such that some of you, after receiving a
will to obey, will come from unbelief to faith, or, after receiving perseverance, will remain in faith;
while the rest of you, who linger in delight in sins, have not yet arisen because the assistance of
merciful grace has not yet raised you. Nevertheless, if any of you have not yet been called whom God has
predestined by his grace to be chosen, you will receive the same grace by which you will will and be chosen.
And if any of you obey but are predestined to be rejected, the powers of obedience will be withdrawn so
that you cease to obey.” See next how Augustine answered these things. “When these things are said,” he
says, “they ought not to deter us from confessing God’s grace, which is not given according to our merits,
or from confessing the predestination of the saints according to it, just as we are not deterred from
confessing God’s foreknowledge if someone should speak about it to the people in this way: ‘Whether you
live rightly or not, later you will be such as God foreknew you would be—good if he foreknew you as good, or
evil if he foreknew you as evil.’ For if, on hearing this, some turn to sluggish idleness and, inclined away
from labor toward lust, go after their desires, must what has been said about God’s foreknowledge therefore
be thought false? If God foreknew that they would be good, will they not be good in whatever present
wickedness they may be living? But if he foreknew them as evil, will they not be evil in whatever present
goodness they may be seen? There was a certain man in our monastery who, when the brothers rebuked him for
doing things which ought not be done and not doing things which ought, answered, ‘Whatever I am now, I will
be such as God foreknew I would be.’ Indeed, he both spoke the truth and did not advance toward good by this
truth, but advanced so far into evil that, after deserting the fellowship of the monastery, he became a dog
returned to his vomit; and yet what he is going to be remains uncertain.”

PENOTTUS.

And so that the force of this irrefragable argument may appear more clearly: does a man deprived of divine
assistance, insofar as this is a simple negation, necessarily fall into sin or not? If not, from the decree
to withdraw divine assistance from a man, where the withdrawal is a simple negation, God cannot certainly
know that the man will fall. If it is said that he does, the council’s entire argument and exhortation now
collapses. Finally, this answer is refuted from the principles of true philosophy.

RESPONSE.

Penottus does not contend with a new argument but serves up the former cabbage twice cooked. We say that a
man deprived of divine assistance necessarily falls into sin, but only by a qualified and modal necessity,
to be expressed in this way: this being presupposed, it is necessary that he fall into sin—but in what
manner? Not necessarily, but freely. Such is the efficacy of the divine will that not only do the things
God decreed come to pass, but they come to pass in the way God decreed: necessary things necessarily, and
contingent things contingently and freely. Thomas, First Part, question 19, article 8, in the body. Nor is
there any reason for us to dissolve into tears if it follows from this that the whole

[Book I, printed p. 142; BSB canvas 00168]

argument of the Council of Trent goes to destruction. We will doubtless be able to live without it. Yet I
have already taken pains to show how ludicrous and worthless this reasoning of yours is.

But let us come to the philosophical points you offer for consideration. Grant that the absence of a form
in a subject which is obliged to possess it can never have the character of a simple negation. You add that
everyone established in grace is owed actual assistance for avoiding sins and persevering. I say that this
is not only false but absurd beyond measure, as though it lay with a man to gain possession of God’s
special assistance, or as though this grace were given to men according to their own merits, which is
pure, unadulterated Pelagianism. God has mercy upon whom he wills and hardens whom he wills. This must not
be referred as to a cause to a difference in human conduct, as though God had mercy upon some because they
behave better and denied the same grace to others because they behave less well. Meanwhile, we do not deny
that just as, on account of original sin, it would be just for God to cast all into condemnation, so too,
if he denied regenerating grace to each and every person, God would do this by a just judgment—and by no
means a hidden one.

PENOTTUS.

Furthermore, in whomever God establishes the obligation to avoid all sins, observe the divine law, and
persevere to the end, in him he also establishes the obligation of having that assistance without which a
man cannot perform these things. But in every justified person, at least, God establishes the obligation
to do all these things.

Because God, seeing that a man cannot without such assistance keep the divine commandments, avoid sins,
and persevere to the end, nevertheless establishes in him the obligation to perform all these things, he
establishes himself as debtor to give such assistance unless the hindrance lies with the man.

RESPONSE.

By its creation the human race owes God every obedience. Although through sin it has become incapable of
performing that obedience, the same obligation by which it is bound to God remains. God is not bound to
supply a man his assistance so that the man may pay what he owes God. Penottus’s claims to the contrary
belong to one who dictates, not one who argues.

PENOTTUS.

Others therefore answer the aforesaid argument by saying that the Council speaks of sufficient assistance,
with which a man can avoid all sins and persevere to the end if he wishes, but not of efficacious
assistance, with which he in fact avoids those sins, without which, although he can stand, he nevertheless
falls in fact.

Against this answer, which some abuse in a thousand ways, I argue thus. Can a man refrain from falling with
sufficient assistance alone, or not? If you say the second, you now destroy what you say, for the
assistance you call sufficient will not be sufficient. If you say the first, then how does God know that
he will in fact fall, if with such assistance he knows that the man can refrain from falling? To say that
he indeed knows that the man can refrain from falling with such assistance, yet knows that he will in fact
fall, is an arbitrary and irrational assertion. For if he can refrain from falling, no impossibility
follows when the possibility is posited in actuality. Let us therefore posit that he does not in fact fall:
God will not know him to be going to fall.

RESPONSE.

Whatever may be true of that distinction and its adaptation to a legitimate interpretation of the Council
of Trent—which council, or rather conventicle, deserves as much regard among us as the mystery of
iniquity—Penottus’s refutation is worthless. We say that contingent things come to pass with God willing in
such a way that they nevertheless can come to pass otherwise; otherwise they would not be contingent at
all. Nor would it lie with God to ordain by his decree that contingent futures come to pass just as
certainly through contingent causes as necessary things do through necessary causes. Therefore, although
someone has sufficient assistance by which he can refrain from falling, it can nevertheless happen that
God knows for certain that he will in fact fall. Otherwise, either God would not know what things are
future, or none of the things which are future would be future contingently and freely. All the defenders
of middle knowledge acknowledge this with one voice, and I do not doubt that Penottus himself holds this
for certain, although, carried away by the heat of disputation, he here condemns it as an arbitrary and
irrational assertion.

There is only this difference of opinion between us and the defenders of middle knowledge: they assert
this without any foundation whatever for divine foreknowledge, whereas we found this foreknowledge of
future contingents upon the efficacy of the divine will, from which it comes about that not only do the
things God wills come to pass, but they come to pass in the manner God wills—necessary things necessarily,
and contingent things contingently and freely. Thomas, First Part, question 19, article 8, in the body.

PENOTTUS.

Finally, the same truth is proved and all the foregoing answers are rejected at once. Whatever may be true
of man after the fall of our first parents, nevertheless, when speaking of man established in the state of
original justice and of an angel established in the state of the way, God could not in the decree to permit
his fall know certainly and infallibly that he would fall. For with precisely the same assistance with
which man fell he could have remained standing if he had wished. Augustine assigns this as one difference
between the state of original justice and corrupt nature in *On Nature and Grace*, chapters 42 and 49;
book 1 *Against the Two Letters of the Pelagians*, chapter 2; *On the Good of Perseverance*, chapter 7; and
*On Rebuke and Grace*, chapters 10, 11, and 12. There he says that if man in the state of original justice
had not possessed assistance by which he could have remained standing if he had wished, and had fallen
through the withdrawal of divine assistance, his sin would have had to be imputed to God, and so forth. He
adds that the good angels remained standing with precisely the same assistance with which the evil angels
fell. Read the passage in chapter 10 of *On Rebuke and Grace*, at “Therefore we most salutarily,” and
chapter 11 at “But if this assistance had been lacking to angel or man,” and so forth. These will suffice
to dispel the cloud obscuring the rays of truth and overthrow everything said about knowing a future fall
from the decree to permit sin.

RESPONSE.

At last Penottus has come to his triad, and when we have routed them we shall gain the victory—which I do
not doubt we shall accomplish with little effort. Penottus’s argument consists of two parts, one of which
is irrelevant, while the other itself aims at the throat of Penottus’s cause. None of us doubts that both
Adam and the angels before the fall had assistance by which they could have remained standing if they had
wished. By their free choice, thus far whole and incorrupt, they were sufficiently able to

[Book I, printed p. 143; BSB canvas 00169]

remain standing, perform any good work, and avoid any sin, provided they wished. Nothing except the loss of
primeval integrity has brought such incapacity upon man. But unless they were also always to have the will
to do what they could, just as they had the power to do whatever they willed, they would infallibly fall.
Yet we deny that this will could be had unless God gave it, decreed it to be future, and determined the will
to will by efficacious grace.

Therefore, only the latter part of the present argument has any weight or importance for deciding the
present question: that the good angels remained standing with precisely the same assistance with which the
evil angels fell.

Yet Penottus proposes this far too deceitfully and artfully, and it is very likely that he was quite
conscious of his artifice. The deception is so gross that it could not have escaped altogether the notice
of a man as shrewd as this man of Novara represents himself to be. Although the good angels possessed all
the same aids which the evil angels had when they fell, nevertheless, if they had not only those same aids
but others by which provision was extraordinarily made for their perseverance, who does not see that the
origin of the difference between them must be referred less to freedom of choice than to a different
administration of divine assistance? From contemplation of that administration, decreed by God from
eternity, God also foresaw from eternity the fall of the one group and the perseverance of the other.
Therefore, Penottus must either say that the good angels had no assistance for remaining standing other
than what the evil angels possessed, notwithstanding which they fell, or he will not advance his cause by
the smallest degree. Without doubt Penottus wishes to appear to his readers to have aimed at this thus far.
But since he rests solely upon Augustine’s authority for proving it, let the reader know that nothing of
the sort occurs in Augustine. Suspicions that Augustine perhaps thought this might arise in a favorable
reader, but no explicit testimony about it occurs in Augustine, nor can it be concluded from any explicit
testimony there. Penottus would undoubtedly have pointed this out had he found some clear testimony with
which he could contend. He preferred, therefore, to leave the point to be fished out by the reader’s
ingenuity. I, however, have at hand an exceedingly explicit testimony of Augustine with which to maintain
the contrary: the good angels lacked neither sufficient nor efficacious assistance of a kind God by no
means granted the other angels. Clear indications of this occur in Augustine, *The City of God*, book 12,
chapter 9: “Either they received a lesser grace of divine love than those who persevered in it, or, if both
were created equally good, while the one group fell by an evil will, the other were assisted more fully and
attained that fullness of certainty.” In these words Augustine openly professes that the good angels
obtained a greater dispensation of divine grace, or greater assistance, than the evil angels. See also what
Leonardus Coquaeus noted upon this passage of Augustine, especially since he was neither a Jesuit nor a
Dominican but an Augustinian. “By this reasoning,” he says, “a greater benefit is understood to have been
conferred upon the good angels than upon the evil. For although they obtained equal habitual grace from the
beginning, the divine motion and special assistance of God were nevertheless so efficacious in the holy
angels that they turned wholly to God, persevered in goodness, and attained the same blessedness. It
certainly did not come about from the power of freedom of choice that they were affected so differently.
That grace was efficacious in the one and not in the other must be ascribed especially to God. If, of two
men equally affected, one is saved and the other condemned, I understand that a greater assistance of
grace was always conferred upon the one who is saved. I hold this with Augustine in this passage and
throughout his discussions of this matter. Nor do I refer the whole matter to freedom of choice, so that
one wills to consent and respond to grace and the other does not. For God also brings it about that we will
and assent, and I do not think that this infallible consent of ours is only from God’s infallible
foreknowledge. Indeed, if per impossibile we exclude all of God’s foreknowledge, grace itself infallibly
moves man or even an angel in itself, so that although it is not efficacious by a necessity of coercion, it
is by a necessity of immutability; and God’s grace is greater in one than in the other. That one is
converted and the other not, that efficacious assistance is given to one while in the other it is indeed
sufficient but by no means obtains its effect, Augustine everywhere teaches to be a mystery. But if, with
equal grace posited, one were converted and the other not, and this depended upon the consent or dissent of
free choice alone, no mystery whatever would seem to me to be involved. Nor does Augustine here give as the
cause why some angels were saved and others not that God foreknew the one group would be saved or cooperate
with his grace and the other would not cooperate. Rather, it was because those who were saved either
received a greater grace of divine love or, if they were created equally good, that is, in equal grace,
they were assisted more fully. Thus he refers the business of the good angels’ salvation to a greater
assistance of grace. That some fall, do not respond to divine grace, and do not cooperate with it should be
ascribed to their free choice; but that grace is efficacious in others and so they cooperate should be
ascribed especially to God himself.” In the same place Augustine also has another statement no less
illustrious, perhaps even more so: “And when their good will”—that of the good angels—“so acted that they did
not turn to themselves, who were lesser, but to him who supremely is, and, adhering to him, became more
truly themselves and lived wisely and blessedly by participation in him, what else is shown except that
every good will would have been powerless and remained in desire alone unless he who made a good nature out
of nothing capable of himself had made it better by fulfilling it from himself?” But it is worthwhile also
to examine the passages toward which the man of Novara points as though they made against us. The first is
taken from chapter 10 of *On Rebuke and Grace*: “Therefore we most salutarily confess what we most rightly
believe: that God, the Lord of all things, who created all things very good and foreknew that evils would
arise from goods, and knew that it pertained more to his most omnipotent goodness to do good even from
evils than not to permit evils to exist, so ordered the life of angels and men that in it he first showed
what their free choice could do, and then what the benefit of his grace and the judgment of justice could
do.” Augustine says that two things were disclosed by God’s ordering concerning angels and men, of which
the one appears to have preceded their

[Book I, printed p. 144; BSB canvas 00170]

fall and the other to have followed their fall. What preceded—or what was shown to have preceded from the
fall of the one group and the perseverance of the other—was what free choice could do: namely, that the
angels could fall into sin through their free choice and could also persevere in obedience through free
choice. Yet it by no means follows that, in Augustine’s opinion, they could have persevered in good through
free choice without grace or special assistance. This will be proved more fully in the answer to the second
passage Penottus cites. But perhaps you will reply that the benefit of grace is among the things which,
according to Augustine in this place, did not become known until after the fall, just as the judgment of
justice did. I say that it is by no means necessary to understand this benefit of grace as following the
fall; why should it not be thought to have appeared in certain angels being kept from the fall? But grant
that this benefit of grace follows the fall. I say, then, that it must be understood in proportion to the
judgment of justice: just as some were reserved for the judgment of the great day through their
transgression, so others obtained confirmation in their state through their obedience and are reserved for
glory. As Augustine says in *On Rebuke and Grace*, chapter 11: “The good angels remained standing through
free choice and merited to receive the due reward of that continuance—namely, such fullness of blessedness
that it is most certain to them that they will always remain in it.” The second passage is drawn from
chapter 11 of *On Rebuke and Grace*: “But if this assistance had been lacking to angel or man when they
were first made, since their nature was not made such that it could remain if it wished without divine
assistance, they certainly would not have fallen by their own fault. The assistance without which they
could not remain would have been lacking.” But these words signify only that angel and man did not lack
assistance by which they could remain, and without which they could not remain in good will if they wished.
It by no means follows that a still greater assistance was not needed by which they would will. I
acknowledge, with Augustine’s immediately preceding statement, that “the good angels remained standing
through free choice,” but take care not to gather from this that they remained standing through free choice
with special assistance excluded. Indeed, in this very chapter Augustine distinguishes two kinds of
assistance, by one of which a man or angel could remain if he wished, and by the other of which he would
also wish to remain. Let Augustine himself be heard: “Through this grace of God there is in us, in receiving
good and holding it perseveringly, not only the power to do what we will but also the will to do what we
can. This was not in the first man. One of these was in him; the other was not. For he did not need grace to
receive the good because he had not yet lost it, but to remain in it he needed the assistance of grace,
without which he could not do so at all. He had received the power to remain if he wished but did not have
the will to do what he could. For if he had possessed it, he would have persevered.” Augustine plainly
signifies that the will to do what we can is in us through God’s grace, but this assistance was by no means
given to those who fell.

PENOTTUS.

Against this assertion the first argument is taken from divine Scripture, which teaches that the man from
whom God decreed to withdraw his assistance infallibly falls and rushes into evil. Psalm 80: “And I sent
them away according to the desires of their heart; they will go in their own inventions.” The same can be
proved by testimonies of the Second Council of Orange and of many Fathers, which can be seen in Cardinal
Bellarmine.

I answer that there is no doubt about this among Catholics. The doubt lies here: since it is certain
according to the faith that God abandons no one—especially no justified person—unless he is first abandoned
by him; since he abandons no one, however impious, without at least sufficient assistance for salvation,
as will be proved in book 7; since, if he abandons anyone, that person is abandoned as punishment for
preceding offenses; and since whoever sins does so in such a way that he could refrain from sinning—whence
does God know that, if he withdraws efficacious assistance from a man and leaves him sufficient assistance,
the man will infallibly sin? It cannot be said that he knows this in a decree to withdraw some assistance
without which the man cannot avoid sins, nor that he knows it because he knows that the man can indeed
avoid sins with sufficient assistance but in fact will not. For this is the entire difficulty: whence does
he know that, although the man can avoid sins, he will in fact not avoid them?

RESPONSE.

Penottus evidently finds it necessary not to rest in that account of sin following its divine permission,
although he openly professes that there is no doubt about it among Catholics. But if sin always and
infallibly follows the permission of sin, what prevents God from being said to foreknow sin in the decree
to permit sin? Because Penottus cannot yet digest this doctrine, he adds that the difficulty is no small
one and explains at length the precise point at which it lies—all of which is altogether irrelevant. For
whatever difficulty presents itself here, if only it is conceded that the man from whom God decreed to
withdraw his assistance infallibly falls, it will manifestly follow that God’s decree to withdraw his
assistance and aid from a man is a sufficient ground for knowing some future sin. Nor is the difficulty
greater than that of reconciling the contingency of things with their necessity upon the supposition of
the divine decree concerning their futurition. I reconcile them thus: God decreed that some things come to
pass contingently; therefore, on the supposition of that decree, it is necessary that the things which God
decreed not only to come to pass but to come to pass contingently should not only come to pass but come to
pass contingently. Such is the efficacy of the divine will that it can ensure not only that whatever God
wills to come to pass does so, but that it does so in the manner he wills, whether necessarily or
contingently. To come to pass contingently is to come to pass with the possibility of not occurring.
Therefore, from the decree to permit sins, sins will both occur and occur contingently with the possibility
of not occurring, so that he who sins could refrain from sinning.

PENOTTUS.

The second argument is this. If at the time when a man is bound to fulfill some precept or exercise some
act of virtue he is not predetermined by God to such an act, he will not produce such an act and will sin
by a sin of omission. Therefore, in the decree not to predetermine the man to such an act God knows the
future sin. Similarly, if he decreed to predetermine the man to the

[Book I, printed p. 145; BSB canvas 00171]

natural act of sin, he knows that the man will infallibly sin by a sin of commission.

I answer that the antecedent must be denied altogether: if God decreed to predetermine Peter to the natural
act of sin, he will necessarily sin; or if God did not decree to predetermine him to an act of virtue,
Peter will sin by a sin of omission. Or it must be said that the argument proceeds from conditionals which
we deny that God ever actualizes, for if he actualized them, he would overthrow human freedom and
necessitate man to sin.

RESPONSE.

I acknowledge that this argument is of no value, but it proceeds from highly controversial principles.
Nevertheless, Penottus is greatly at a loss while he seeks from every direction a suitable solution to it.
For the antecedent he denies is a most familiar truth: if God decreed to predetermine someone to the natural
act of sin—that is, to the act underlying sin—he will necessarily sin. For it is necessary that a man
produce the act to whose production he is determined by God. Otherwise, the divine decree would not be a
sufficient ground for knowing those things whose cause is the creature as physically determined by the
divine will to produce them—something Penottus nevertheless asserted in precisely these words above,
chapter 21, proposition 2. But if it is necessary for some man to produce the act underlying sin, it is
necessary that he sin. For he who culpably produces a sinful act sins, and everyone who produces the act
underlying sin produces a sinful act. Therefore, if it is necessary that he produce the act underlying sin,
it is necessary that he sin; that he sin, I say, yet not necessarily but contingently and freely. Shall I
say that Penottus confuses these things ignorantly and unlearnedly? Or rather that he does so deliberately,
fraudulently, and artfully?

The latter answer is extremely absurd when it says that the argument proceeds from conditionals which God
never actualizes. The truth of a conditional proposition does not depend upon whether God actualizes it or
not. The philosophers who maintained that God was a necessary agent once argued in the same way from
conditional propositions, and we are accustomed to do so today. The only question is whether the
conditional is true or false; if false, it is enough to deny it. But to speak of God as actualizing a
conditional of this sort is the answer of a dreamer rather than an arguer, and I do not think such an
answer was ever employed before Penottus communicated his ravings in writing. Indeed, I say that every
conditional proposition is actualized by God, whether true or false. For every proposition is either
conceived only in the mind, pronounced by the mouth, or set down in writing. But every conception of the
mind, every utterance, and every inscription or writing is from God. Penottus indeed acknowledges that God
is the principle of being of all future contingents. Certainly God does not always actualize what is
conditionally inferred; or, if he actualizes it, he does not actualize it inseparably from the antecedent
from which it is inferred. Therefore, whenever this occurs, there is just reason to deny the consequence.
But Penottus already did this in his former answer; here he prepares to offer an answer of another kind. To
say, however, that God never actualizes such conditional propositions is an answer absurd beyond measure.

An easy way of answering the proposed argument occurs according to Penottus’s opinion: namely, by denying
the first consequence—not the second, but rather the minor proposition which was required to complete the
whole syllogism. I say according to Penottus, a defender of middle knowledge—not a vigorous defender, but a
zealot. Thus far I have found nothing in him by which he advances the Jesuits’ cause, indeed nothing which
can in any way be compared with their labors already published. Nevertheless, I prove by one argument that
not even future evils can be foreknown by God except in God’s decree. What is made future only through
God’s decree can be known as future only through God’s decree. But no evil becomes future except through
God’s decree. Therefore. I confirm the minor. What neither became future by its own nature nor on account of
another cause besides the divine decree became future only through God’s decree. But no evil became future
by its own nature or through any other cause besides God’s decree. For every evil by its own nature is only
possible, and through no cause other than God’s decree can it pass from the condition of a possible thing
to that of a future thing. If another cause of this migration besides God’s decree could be conceived, it
would have to be sought either outside God or within him. Not outside God, because nothing outside God
existed from eternity; but every evil was future from eternity, and what did not exist from eternity cannot
be the cause of something which did. Within God, however, apart from God’s decree, nothing occurs that can
be imagined as the cause of things’ futurition. For neither God’s essence nor God’s knowledge, when God’s
decree is excluded, is the cause why a thing migrates from the condition of a possible thing to that of a
future thing. I challenge every defender of middle knowledge without exception to solve this argument.
