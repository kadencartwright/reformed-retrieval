QUESTION XXV.

ON COMMUNION UNDER BOTH KINDS.

By God’s command, ought both symbols of the Eucharist to be administered to
each and every adult believer? Or ought the people to be forbidden the use of
the chalice? We affirm the former and deny the latter against the papists.

[Marginalia: Communion under one kind.]

I. The third question concerning the symbols and their use is about communion
under one kind, which the papists press with great vehemence as a consequence
of transubstantiation very well suited to confirming that fiction more and
more.

[Marginalia: How it arose.]

For after this monstrous opinion began to take firm root in men’s minds, a
great fear of spilling the sacred wine seized the Latins. They did not think it
sufficient to prevent this danger by touching the bread to the sacred wine and
administering it dipped; they judged it far more prudent to omit communion in
the chalice entirely. They did so especially because they held that Christ’s
whole body and blood are truly contained under the species of bread. To
satisfy themselves on this point, they rashly undertook to alter the lawful
administration of the most holy Eucharist and introduce a new rite: the
sacrament is indeed to be consecrated in both the elements of bread and wine,
and the priest who consecrated it is to take both parts of what was
consecrated, but to all others who communicate, whether laity or clergy, he is
to distribute bread alone and administer the chalice to no one. Thus the
adversaries tore apart with sacrilegious audacity the two symbols that Christ
joined by divine authority,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 382 | IA leaf 416 | PDF p. 416]

and what Christ instituted for all believers they snatched from the greater
part of Christians, scorning his command and corrupting the testament, and
claimed it for their petty sacrificing priests alone.

II. History teaches plainly how greatly this monstrous change—insulting to
Christ and the apostles, the ancient church, and present-day Christians—stirred
the Christian world, so there is no need to linger over it. Even beginners know
how vigorously the Bohemians opposed this error at the beginning of the
fifteenth century, among them John Hus and Jerome of Prague, men of immortal
memory. It will suffice to quote the decree of the Council of Constance, held
in the printed year 1405, so that the papists’ position may be known with
certainty from it.

[Marginalia: Decree of the Council of Constance.]

III. Thus the council speaks in Session 13: “Since in some parts of the world
certain persons rashly presume to assert that the Christian people ought to
receive the sacrament of the Eucharist under both the species of bread and
wine, and that the lay people ought everywhere to communicate not only under
the species of bread but also under the species of wine—even after supper or
otherwise when not fasting—and obstinately assert that they ought so to
communicate, contrary to the praiseworthy and reasonably approved custom of
the church, which they damnably attempt to condemn as sacrilegious, therefore
this present general Council of Constance, lawfully assembled in the Holy
Spirit and endeavoring to provide for the salvation of the faithful against
this error, after the mature deliberation of many doctors of both divine and
human law, declares, decrees, and defines that although Christ instituted this
venerable sacrament after supper and administered it to his disciples under
both the species of bread and wine, nevertheless, notwithstanding this fact,
the authority of the sacred canons and the praiseworthy and approved custom of
the church has observed and observes that this sacrament ought not to be
consecrated after supper, nor received by believers who are not fasting,
except in a case of illness or some other necessity permitted or admitted by
law or by the church. And just as this custom was reasonably introduced to
avoid certain dangers or scandals, although in the primitive church this
sacrament was received by believers under both kinds, afterward it was
received by those consecrating under both and by the laity only under the
species of bread, since it must be most firmly believed and in no way doubted
that the entire body and blood of Christ are truly contained under the species
of bread as well as under the species of wine. Since this custom was therefore
reasonably introduced by the church and the holy fathers and observed for a
very long time, it is to be regarded as law, which may not be condemned or
altered at pleasure by private authority.” They sanctioned this law by a
deadly example: first the dreadful and monstrous burning alive of John Hus,
and then the protracted imprisonment of his disciple Jerome of Prague, who was
worn down and killed a year after his teacher.

[Marginalia: Confirmed by the Synod of Trent.]

IV. The Synod of Trent in the sixteenth century confirmed this error
established by the fathers of the Council of Constance, supporting it with its
anathemas and curses. In chapter 1 of Session 21 it “declares and teaches that
by no divine command are the laity and non-consecrating clergy bound to take
the sacrament of the Eucharist under both kinds, and that without injury to
the faith it cannot in any way be doubted that communion under either one kind
is sufficient for their salvation. For although Christ the Lord at the Last
Supper instituted this venerable sacrament under the species of bread and wine
and delivered it to the apostles, nevertheless that institution and delivery
do not tend to the conclusion that all Christ’s faithful are bound by the
Lord’s ordinance to receive both kinds.” In chapter 2 it continues: “Therefore
holy mother church, recognizing this authority of hers in the administration
of the sacraments, although from the beginning

[1847 vol. III printed p. 383 | IA leaf 417 | PDF p. 417]

of the Christian religion the use of both kinds was not infrequent, nevertheless
as time advanced and that custom had already changed very widely, approved for
grave and just reasons this custom of communicating under one kind and decreed
that it was to be regarded as law, which may not be condemned or altered at
pleasure without the church’s own authority.” And lest anyone dare to murmur
against these canons, the synod strikes with anathema “whoever shall say that
by God’s command each and every believer ought to take both kinds of the most
holy sacrament of the Eucharist,” and further “those who shall say that the
Catholic Church was not led by just causes and reasons to communicate the
laity, and even non-consecrating clergy, under the species of bread alone, or
that it erred in doing so.”

V. The papists’ position is evident from these statements without further
inquiry. Ours is plainly the contrary. We hold that communion under both kinds
was instituted by Christ and must necessarily be retained, so that not only is
each person free to employ it, but according to Christ’s prescription every
person is bound to it unless prevented by some insuperable obstacle; and that
the chalice could not be taken from the people contrary to the Lord’s express
law without the highest sacrilege. Therefore the question is not (1) whether
communion under both kinds was performed by Christ, for the adversaries do not
deny this, but whether believers ought likewise to perform it, so that it is
not merely an indifferent and accidental matter but necessary and essential
to the sacrament’s perfection. Nor is it asked (2) whether a believer is so
bound to communion under both kinds that in no case can he communicate under
one kind alone without sin. We acknowledge that if an insuperable obstacle
arises—either an internal one proceeding from the recipient’s condition, or an
external one from other causes—by which he is prevented from taking one of the
kinds, such a person deserves compassion rather than reproof. Rather, it is
asked whether the use of the chalice may rightfully be forbidden to the
people, or whether the minister is bound to furnish each person the symbols of
bread and wine; and whether a believer hindered by no necessity ought to
partake of both kinds. The papists deny this. They regard forbidding the
chalice to all laymen as law, except for some to whom they grant the privilege,
such as the emperor, kings, and cardinals who are not priests, that they may
take the blood “with all decency and caution.” We affirm, however, that the
chalice is to be taken from no one, but furnished to everyone. Hence it is
clear that those men distort the state of the question who pose it thus:
whether in instituting the sacrament Christ so commanded the reception of both
kinds to everyone that no one may communicate under one kind alone without
sin. They frame it this way, of course, to make room for the objection about
those who abstain from wine. Instead they ought to ask whether one kind may be
forbidden to anyone, much less to everyone, without sin. They deny this
formulation; we assert it. There are various reasons.

[Marginalia: The use of the chalice is proved. 1. From Christ’s institution, Matthew 26.]

VI. The first is drawn from Christ’s institution. What Christ instituted for
the perpetual use of the church must be violated by no one and cannot be
violated without guilt. But Christ instituted the Eucharist under both
symbols, when he gave the apostles not only bread but also the chalice and
commanded them all to drink from it, as all the evangelists and Paul report
with great agreement: “Drink from this, all of you.” It is then recorded that
they immediately did this at Christ’s order: “They all drank from it,” as Mark
says in the printed reference, chapter 14:13. Various arguments lie within
this. 1. From the object of the command, which is not particular to some but
universal to all: “Drink from this, all of you.” 2. From the conjunction of
bread and wine, for the same necessity that exists for receiving the bread
exists for drinking the chalice, because Christ is said *hōsautōs* (ὡσαυτως),
“likewise,” to have taken the cup and commanded its use—that is, for the same
end and to be received by the same persons. 3. From the additional command to
“do this,” namely to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 384 | IA leaf 418 | PDF p. 418]

minister and distribute both symbols to all, which Christ had done and
commanded the apostles to do.

VII. It is objected in vain (1): “Many things are instituted by God that are
nevertheless not commanded to everyone, such as marriage, preaching the
gospel, and the like.” For who does not see the difference between things that
are instituted and are necessary at least by command, and those that are
matters of liberty; between things instituted for the common use of all and
those instituted only for the particular use of some? The latter can be
instituted to prescribe their lawful manner and yet not be imposed on all. But
the same account does not apply to the former, which, as they are instituted
for all, must be observed by all, as is the Eucharist. (2) “An institution is
not immediately a command.” Besides the fact that an express command exists
when Christ orders his people to do this, it is clear from the end of the
institution that an institution includes a command. Whatever is proposed with
the end that it be done binds men by that very fact to do it and has the force
of a command. Again, the institution of a state—that is, appointment to some
office or way of life—must be distinguished here from the institution of a
rite, which is the appointment of a certain thing to be done by men. The
former does not bind each and every person, but only those who wish to live in
that state, for example in marriage. The latter, however, always binds all who
are obligated to observe that rite, and it can be performed only by employing
those things that the one who instituted the rite is known either to have used
himself or to have commanded others to use. (3) “A divine sanction can
sometimes be omitted in matters of this kind, which are not necessarily to be
observed either in themselves or by the prescription of the natural law, when
grave causes require it. Thus David ate the showbread, the Maccabees fought on
the Sabbath, the church baptizes infants who were not instructed, and it has
judged sprinkling sufficient in baptism.” Plainly disparate things are joined
here. 1. In the cited examples grave causes are alleged: David had the grave
cause of hunger and the Maccabees the destruction of their people. But here no
grave or extraordinary causes are given. Indeed, the gravest causes demand
that Christ’s command not be violated. 2. Those deeds were singular acts, not
institutions or customs, whereas here the issue is the strict prohibition of
the chalice made by a solemn decree. 3. It is falsely assumed that to baptize
uninstructed infants is contrary to Christ’s command, for as Maldonatus rightly
observes, “Christ in Matthew 28:19 does not forbid the baptism of those who
cannot be taught, but only commands that all who first have been taught be
baptized.” 4. It was proved above that sprinkling suffices for baptism, as is
designated by *to baptizein* (το βαπτίζειν) itself. But it is one thing to
change immersion into sprinkling, which can suffice for the end of baptism,
namely washing, and another to prohibit the use of the chalice that Christ
instituted.

VIII. It is objected (4): “The words ‘drink from this, all of you’ were spoken
to the apostles alone and cannot be extended to all men, because it would
follow that the sacrament must be given to Turks, Jews, and pagans; nor to all
Christians, because then infants would be included; nor to all adult believers,
because only the apostles were present.” Although only the apostles were
present at that time, the words were not spoken to them alone, but in their
person to all who would come to communion. 1. Christ directs his speech to
those to whom he said, “Do this,” but according to most papists these words are
directed to all believers. 2. The same would have to be said of the bread,
because he also said only to the apostles, “Take, eat.” 3. These words were
spoken to the apostles alone, but not insofar as they were apostles and
administrators, because Christ alone was then the minister. Otherwise everyone
of a rank and order different from the apostles would be excluded. Rather,
they were spoken to the apostles insofar as they were

[1847 vol. III printed p. 385 | IA leaf 419 | PDF p. 419]

communicants, and in this respect they must likewise be common to all other
believers and
the whole church, which the apostles then represented. 4. Paul, the faithful
interpreter of Christ’s words, refers this to all communicants: “Whoever eats
this bread and drinks this chalice,” etc. 5. It is absurd to speak of Turks,
pagans, and infants, who by Christ’s ordinance are excluded from this
sacrament: infants are incapable of the self-examination required here, while
Turks and pagans are neither Christians nor in the covenant, and consequently
the seals of the covenant do not pertain to them. It is objected (5): “Luke
says that this command was given *meta to deipnēsai* (μετα τὸ δειπνῆσαι),
that is, after the Supper was completed, and therefore the institution of the
chalice did not properly pertain to the Supper.” But here *to deipnon* (τὸ
δεῖπνον) means the paschal meal, at which Christ had already taken the paschal
cup, not the eucharistic one. This is evident from Matthew 26:26, who says that
Christ took the bread and cup *esthiontōn autōn* (ἐσθιοντων αυτων), “while
they were eating.” That is, near the end of the paschal supper Christ
instituted the sacrament by taking the bread and cup. These two are joined as
integral parts of the same sacrament, which the Council of Constance
acknowledges when it recognizes that Christ gave the sacrament to the disciples
after supper under both kinds.

[Marginalia: 2. From 1 Corinthians 11:26.]

IX. 2. From Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 11:26. Reciting Christ’s institution
and the command that follows it, Paul binds the Corinthian church to take both
kinds: “As often as you eat this bread and drink this chalice, you will
proclaim the Lord’s death” (v. 26). And afterward: “Let each person therefore
examine himself, and so let him eat this bread and drink this chalice” (v. 28).
And in chapter 10:16: “The chalice of blessing that we bless, is it not the
communion of Christ’s blood? The bread that we break, is it not the communion
of Christ’s body?” Here eating and drinking are joined by the particle
*hōsautōs* (ὡσαύτως) as two essential parts of one and the same mystery and
united as inseparable. But how could the apostle have done this if they can be
separated without guilt? Why does he determine that the proclamation of the
Lord’s death, which ought to be made in the Eucharist by the use of bread and
the chalice, must be made conjointly rather than by either one separately, if
one kind could suffice? Why does he make no distinction here between layman
and cleric, although in the papists’ view this distinction is of the greatest
importance in using the sacrament? Rather, he speaks generally of “whoever”
and addresses all believers without distinction. These words show more clearly
than the noonday sun that, according to Paul, the use of the chalice no less
than that of the bread was common to all.

X. It is alleged to no purpose (1): “Paul uses a disjunctive in the printed
verse 23: ‘Whoever eats this bread, *ē* (ἢ), or drinks this chalice’; hence it
is evident that some took, or could take, this bread without the chalice.” 1.
If that were the force of the disjunctive, it would equally prove that some
drank without bread as that they ate without drink, which the papists do not
maintain. 2. Paul uses the disjunctive because sin can be committed not only
in the unworthy reception of both but also in that of either one. 3. A
disjunctive very often has copulative force among both secular and sacred
writers. “For the promise did not come through the law to Abraham or his
seed” (Rom. 4:13), that is, both to Abraham and to his seed, as Paul puts it:
“The promises were given to Abraham and to his seed” (Gal. 3:16). “Whether
prophecies or tongues” will be abolished (1 Cor. 13:8), that is, both prophecies
and tongues. “I did not come to abolish the law or the prophets” (Matt. 5:17),
that is, neither the law nor the prophets. “Whatever good a man does he will
receive back from the Lord, whether he is a slave or a master” (Eph. 6:8),
that is, both the slave and the master. Nothing therefore prevents this
particle from being understood copulatively according to common usage,
especially since Paul uses it only once but the copulative four times while
discussing the same

[1847 vol. III printed p. 386 | IA leaf 420 | PDF p. 420]

things. There is no doubt that since he used one particle more often, he
understood it properly rather than the one he used less often. 4. The most
ancient codex preserved in the royal library among the English, which Cyril
sent as a gift to James I, reads: *hōste hos an esthiē ton arton kai pinē*
(ὥστε ὃς ἂν ἐσθίη τον ἄρτον καὶ πίνη), “so that whoever eats the bread and
drinks.” The Syriac renders it with a copulative, as do most copies of the
Vulgate before the Sixtine edition, together with Anselm and Thomas. It is
objected (2): “The passage concerns consecrating clergy, not the laity.” But who
does not see that Paul’s entire discourse is directed to the Corinthian church,
that is, to believers of both orders, lay and clerical—and to the laity more
than the clergy, because it was primarily they who committed the sin that
Paul’s discourse aims to correct? Hence in chapter 10 he denies that the
Corinthians can drink the Lord’s chalice and the chalice of demons. This could
not be said unless laymen had been admitted to the use of the chalice, as
Salmeron, treatise 34, Becanus, Costerus, and Toletus on John 6 acknowledge. It
is objected (3): “The apostle does not prescribe absolutely that believers eat
and drink, but prescribes the manner they ought to use if they wish profitably
to eat and drink.” It is clear that both are implied here: it is not only free
for each person to eat and drink at his discretion, but also necessary by
duty. This is evident both because Paul recalls believers to Christ’s
institution, where an express command exists, and because all those commanded
to examine themselves are commanded to eat and drink—and self-examination
belongs not merely to some but to all. It is objected (4): “From this one can
infer only that communion under both kinds is lawful, but not immediately that
it is necessary.” But if it is lawful, why do they prohibit it? Indeed, what
is proposed to the Corinthians as received from the Lord binds everyone; and
what was practiced in that church ought to be retained because of the kinship
of its doctrine with ours.

[Marginalia: 3. From Christ’s command, “Do this.”]

XI. 3. From Christ’s command, “Do this in remembrance of me.” This must be
referred to the entire action, not only to the consecration but also to the
reception of bread and wine. Both communicants and administrators are therefore
in view here and are ordered to do the very thing Christ commanded: the latter
to take, bless, break, and give; the former to eat and drink the received bread
and chalice. Hence Paul, together with Luke, subjoins these words not only to
the distribution of the bread but also to the presentation of the chalice,
because the rationale is the same. So in the liturgies of Mark, Peter, and
James these words are referred to both kinds. Again, it is evident that Christ
performed a twofold work—consecrating and delivering—and afterward said, “Do
this.” I therefore argue as follows: by the words “Do this,” either both works
are denoted, or only one. Not only one, because it would be uncertain which;
or if anything were certain, the words would have to refer to the nearer work,
namely distribution. Therefore both are denoted.

XII. It does not help to reply (1): “Luke placed ‘Do this’ after the
distribution of the bread but did not place it after the chalice was given, so
that we would understand that the Lord commanded distribution to everyone
under the species of bread but not likewise under the species of wine.” As
Soto rightly observes in book 4, distinction 12, question 1, and Vasquez in
disputation 213 on part 3 of Thomas: “He who commands that some received
benefit be commemorated by a work, by the very manner of commanding also
commands that the work itself be done.” Although Luke did not add the same
words after the giving of the cup, the same must be inferred by parity, as Paul
explains. Nor, if he adds “as often as you drink,” can it be inferred that he
did not command the use of the cup absolutely as necessary no less than the
bread, but only if they should at some time use it—as though drinking were not
commanded but only that, if they drink, they should do it in this manner. For
the same thing is said of the bread and of the chalice in 1 Corinthians 11:26:
“As often as you eat this bread.” Therefore either the one is not said
absolutely of the bread, or the other is said absolutely of the chalice as
well. Nor is a command merely about the manner

[1847 vol. III printed p. 387 | IA leaf 421 | PDF p. 421]

of using involved here, but also about the use itself. It is objected (2): “It
is an affirmative command and therefore always binds, but it does not bind at
every time—only at the time of necessity.” This gratuitously assumes that a
necessity for communion under both kinds does not always exist, although the
contrary is evident from Christ’s command, “Drink from this, all of you.” I
admit that it does not bind at every time, because there is a time when the
Supper is not celebrated; but whenever it is celebrated, communion must be
under both kinds. And how will this affirmative command be reconciled with the
negative command prohibiting the chalice, which ought to bind always and at
every time? It is objected (3): “These words are directed only to priests, to
whom alone it belongs to do what Christ did at the Supper.” 1. If the words
pertain only to priests, the whole institution of the Eucharist contained in
them will pertain only to priests. Jansenius, chapter 131 of the *Harmony*, and
Vasquez, disputation 103 on part 3 of Thomas, chapter 2, acknowledge that
something in the meaning of these words pertains to individual believers. 2.
If *to facere hoc* (τὸ *facere hoc*), “to do this,” is referred to the
consecration and distribution of the symbols, it can be said to pertain only to
priests or ministers. But if it is extended to taking, eating, and drinking,
as it necessarily must be, it regards not only the administrators but also
each communicant. It is objected (4): “It can be said that there is a command
here about the use of the chalice for the whole church, but not for individuals;
for many commands belong to the entire church but are to be fulfilled by some,
not by every person, as is the ministry.” Commands common to all have one
rationale and those peculiar to certain orders another. Both indeed were given
to the church, but the former to each person—as to believe in Christ and love
one’s neighbor—whereas the latter were given only to individuals of a certain
order. Thus all who have parents are bound to honor them, but those who lack
parents are not. The use of the chalice is not among particular commands that
regard a certain order, as is the ministry, but among general commands that
extend to each and every adult. It is therefore not only to be regarded as
given to the universal church, but must be fulfilled by and for every person
who is capable of it.

[Marginalia: 4. From the end of the sacrament.]

XIII. 4. From the end of the sacrament. This mutilation of the sacrament
opposes the twofold end for which the sacrament was instituted. First, it was
instituted as a commemoration of Christ’s death, which cannot be made under one
kind but necessarily requires two, one separated from the other to signify the
separation of the body from the blood. The bishop of Meaux in part 2 does not
deny that the commemoration of Christ’s death is more express in two kinds.
Second, it was instituted as a symbol of our complete and full nourishment
through Christ, which is accomplished not merely by eating but also by
drinking. Therefore, when the end of the instituted cup is enjoined on the
people, the use of the cup is also enjoined on them. This is evident from the
parallel example of the bread, whose consumption is commanded to all. It is
objected without reason (1): “This express commemoration is not necessary in
the sacrament; it suffices if it is made in the sacrifice.” Besides the false
claim that a sacrifice exists there, as will be proved in its place, there is
no reason why, if blood separated and poured out from the body must be offered
in the sacrifice, it should not also be received as poured out in the
sacrament, especially since Paul assigns the proclamation of Christ’s death to
communion, which occurs by eating and drinking, not to an offering on the
altar. It is objected (2): “It is evident from the apostle that one kind
suffices for the commemoration, because he said separately of both the bread
and the wine, ‘Do this in remembrance of me.’” This does not prove that either
one suffices. Paul indeed said that commemoration occurs in eating the bread,
but did not say that eating suffices. Is it the same thing to say that the body
lives by truly eating bread, and to say that bread alone

[1847 vol. III printed p. 388 | IA leaf 422 | PDF p. 422]

suffices for the body to live? Otherwise the same could be said of the
sacrifice, although they do not wish it. It is objected (3): “God sometimes
uses bread and wine to sustain bodily life, and sometimes either one, as he
nourished Israel for a long time by manna alone without drink.” By equal right
I could prove that no kind at all need be used, neither bread nor wine, because
God can nourish and sustain a man in an extraordinary manner without bread and
wine, as he sustained Moses for forty days. The issue here is not what God can
do but what he wills by the institution; not an extraordinary manner of
sustenance but the ordinary one, because the Eucharist was not drawn from the
former but from the latter, namely the customary order of nature. Finally, it
is falsely asserted that Israel was sustained by manna alone, because it also
had water from the rock.

[Marginalia: 5. Because the people are capable of the chalice.]

XIV. 5. Because a sign instituted by Christ must not be denied to those
capable of the thing signified. Since sacraments were instituted to signify a
thing, those who are not denied the end or thing signified must not be denied
the means to that end. Now all believers are capable of the blood, which is
the thing signified by the cup; therefore the use of the chalice must also
pertain to them. It is objected to no purpose: “In bodily things eating and
drinking differ, but in spiritual things they are identical. Thus he who eats
Christ under one kind, although he is signified only as eating spiritual food,
is nevertheless nourished most fully, no differently than if he drank from the
chalice.” Although eating and drinking are spiritually identical, it does not
follow that either element should be prohibited to one who spiritually eats
and drinks. Otherwise one element should be prohibited to the priest, for whom
eating and drinking are no less identical. Indeed, both should be taken away
from everyone, since one who spiritually eats and drinks can eat and drink
fully without bread and chalice. Again, the issue here is not participation in
the thing signified but use of the sign. Christ certainly knew that the thing
signified would be whole under each kind separately, and nevertheless wished
it to be taken under both. He therefore took account not only of the thing but
also of the signs, for a clearer representation of the passion. Since Christ
joined both symbols, no one can or ought to separate them, much less prohibit
what he commands.

[Marginalia: 6. From the practice of the ancient church.]

XV. 6. From the practice of the ancient church and the whole purer antiquity
from the apostolic age, as the adversaries do not deny. The Council of
Constance, as seen above, candidly acknowledges “that Christ instituted this
venerable sacrament under both kinds and that in the primitive church this
sacrament was received by believers under both kinds; nevertheless,
notwithstanding this fact, it declares, decrees, and defines that the different
custom introduced by the church is to be regarded as law, which may not be
condemned,” etc. What could have been said more expressly to attest to all the
impiety and sacrilege and the violation of the order instituted by Christ,
which Christian ears cannot hear without horror? Cassander acknowledges the
same more than once, especially in Letter 25: “The writings of all the ancients
so plainly testify to this custom of joining communion in the blood to
communion in the Lord’s body in solemn and ordinary administration that I do
not think anyone of even slightly sound mind could be so shameless as to deny
it.” And in his *Consultation*, article 22: “Concerning the administration of
the most holy sacrament of the Eucharist, it is sufficiently established that
the universal church of Christ to this very day, and the western and Roman
church for more than a thousand years after Christ, exhibited both the species
of bread and wine to all Christ’s members, especially in the solemn and
ordinary dispensation of this sacrament. This is evident from innumerable
testimonies of the ancient

[1847 vol. III printed p. 389 | IA leaf 423 | PDF p. 423]

writers, Greek as well as Latin.” Dionysius Petavius, a papist of no obscure
name, when disputing with Arnauld about the public penance of the ancients in
chapter 5, says: “What does Arnauld say to this? Will he deny that the use of
communion in the Eucharist under both kinds existed in apostolic times and
afterward persisted for many ages in the church? But he cannot deny or
dissemble this unless he wishes to be reckoned either in the lowest class of
the unlearned or certainly in the highest class of the shameless.” It would be
easy to cite passages of the fathers through different centuries proving that
communion under both kinds was retained through the tenth century and beyond.
For brevity, however, we refer the reader to Chamier, Calixtus, and especially
Dallæus, who treats this most carefully in book 5, from chapter 7 through
chapter 13. In chapter 13 he adds the testimony of the ancient liturgies that
bear the names of Basil, Chrysostom, Peter, James, Mark, and others, in which
communion under both kinds is consistently mentioned.

XVI. It will suffice for us to report two domestic testimonies of Roman
pontiffs, Leo and Gelasius, who support us. The former, in Sermon 4 on Lent,
canon 15, when speaking before a Roman congregation about guarding against the
poisons of the Manichaeans and their foolish and profane superstitions, says
this about those heretics: “Since, to conceal their unbelief, they dare to be
present at our mysteries, they so regulate themselves in communion of the
sacraments that sometimes, to remain concealed more safely, they receive
Christ’s body with an unworthy mouth but altogether decline to drink the blood
of our redemption. We therefore wish your holiness to know this, so that men
of this kind may be disclosed to you by these marks and, when their
sacrilegious pretense has been detected, may be marked and betrayed and driven
from the fellowship of the saints by priestly authority.” From these words it
is perfectly clear that at Rome in those days the sacrament was administered
only under both kinds. If the present custom had then prevailed, they would
absurdly be said to decline the chalice, since the priest would have offered it
neither to them nor to the Catholics communicating there. Yet a clearer
testimony is found in the law promulgated by Gelasius, the Roman pontiff, at
the end of the fifth century. The law exists in Gratian, *On Consecration*,
distinction 2, canon 12, long ago reported by the ancient canonists Anselm,
Polycarp, Ivo, and others: “We have learned that certain people, having taken
only a portion of the sacred body, abstain from the chalice of the sacred
blood. Since they are doubtless taught to be bound by some superstition or
other, let them either receive the entire sacraments or be kept from the
entirety, because division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without
great sacrilege.” Nothing could be said more explicitly to assert the necessity
of both kinds for all without exception. Nor can it be said that this concerns
consecrating priests, on whom Gelasius lays down the law, as the title prefixed
to the canon in Gratian has it: “A priest ought not to receive Christ’s body
without the blood.” No discourse proper to a priest occurs in the law; rather,
it is common to all communicants, as Baronius himself acknowledges at the year
496, section 20: “For in truth no mention is made there of a communicating
priest, so that it can readily be understood that what plainly appears to have
been said altogether generally ought by no means to be restricted to priests.
We therefore reject such a frigid solution.” A truer title should therefore be
restored to the canon, the one Ivo had long before prefixed to it: “The Lord’s
body is not to be taken without the chalice.” Cassander’s manuscript codices
agreed with this. He says in Letter 19: “I also found the title of that canon of
Gelasius in ancient manuscript books that still remain among us: that no one
may receive communion in the flesh alone without participation in the blood.”
Hence it is clear how great the agreement is between the papists of today and
their

[1847 vol. III printed p. 390 | IA leaf 424 | PDF p. 424]

forebears: what the latter condemn not merely as error but as the incomparably
graver sin of sacrilege, indeed great sacrilege, the former now command to all
their people by a law passed in two general councils and anathematize those who
think the contrary.

[Marginalia: 7. From the agreement of other Christians.]

XVII. 7. The agreement of other Christians also contributes much here, for
apart from the Latins alone they all distribute the Eucharist to communicants
under both kinds. Although many things throughout Christianity everywhere on
earth have been changed or innovated, the custom derived from apostolic times
of delivering the Eucharist under both kinds to all communicants persists to
this very day among other Christians, the Latins alone excepted. No one can
doubt that this was the common and universal usage of the ancient church. The
matter concerning the Greeks is well known and is evident even from the first
response of the letter given in 1576 by Jeremias, patriarch of Constantinople,
to the theologians of Württemberg: “You say first, therefore, that both kinds
are to be received in the Holy Supper, and you speak correctly. For we also do
so when we receive the venerable mysteries,” etc. Metrophanes Critopulus,
patriarch of Alexandria, confirms this in chapter 9 of the *Confession of the
Eastern Church*. From various accounts of those who traveled in those regions,
such as Osorius, Alvarez, Gouveia, Godignus, and others, it is gathered that
the same rule is followed by the Muscovites and Ruthenians, Syrians and
Georgians, Jacobites and Armenians, Ethiopians or Abyssinians, and Nestorians.

[Marginalia: 8. From the petition for the chalice.]

XVIII. 8. Nor should the complaints of princes about the chalice’s removal and
their demands for its restoration be omitted. History makes sufficiently clear
how earnestly the Germans, Gauls, Bohemians, and others labored in the Council
of Trent to restore communion under both kinds to the church. This is evident,
for example, from Catherine de’ Medici’s letter to the pontiff in 1561, where,
in treating the church’s reformation, she reports the wishes of the king,
clergy, and people specifically concerning this head of religion: “The
complete use of holy communion, that is, under both kinds, ought to be restored
without distinction of persons, and the authority of the Council of Constance,
which ought by no means to outweigh God’s command, cannot prevent it.” This is
in Thuanus, book 27. Letters from King Charles IX, Catherine’s son, written the
same year to his ambassador de l’Isle at the pope’s court also exist. In them
he says “that by the common admonitions and prayers of the bishops and doctors
of his kingdom he had been asked to intercede with the pope, so that by his
permission and concession they might be allowed to communicate the Holy Supper
under both the species of bread and wine to all subjects entrusted to them as
a sacred charge—a rite known formerly to have belonged to the primitive
church,” etc. Replying to these letters, de l’Isle relates that when he had
presented the king’s instructions to the pope, the pope said “that this had
always been his opinion about communion in the chalice and the marriage of
clergy,” etc., and that “the emperor had first requested the same from him for
his son, the king of Bohemia, and soon afterward also for the subjects of his
hereditary dominions.” Yet the matter was drawn out at length by the customary
arts of the Roman curia and at last came to nothing, with most of the cardinals
vigorously opposing de l’Isle when he pressed the king’s instructions. This
especially includes the cardinal of Sant’Angelo, who, as de l’Isle reports in
his letters to the king, said “that he would never consent that so much poison
be presented to the peoples of Gaul as medicine; and that it seemed far more
prudent to him to allow those afflicted with this disease to perish than to
grant them such a remedy when they requested it.” The distinguished Calixtus
in *On Communion under Both Kinds*, page 13 and following, reports at length
how numerous and great were the Germans’ labors and endeavors from the
beginning of the council to its end and even after its conclusion; and what
efforts were made by the emperors Ferdinand and Maximilian, Albert duke of
Bavaria, and others, to obtain

[1847 vol. III printed p. 391 | IA leaf 425 | PDF p. 425]

communion under both kinds, though with fruitless effort and vain labor.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XIX. The bread Christ offered his disciples at Emmaus in Luke 24:30 was not
the bread of a sacred table but a common one, prepared for eating supper and
not for celebrating the sacrament, as everything suggests—the hour, the
circumstances, and Christ’s custom—and as Lyra and Dionysius the Carthusian
also agree. No consecration is recorded there, although it ought to precede
distribution. No chalice, whether distributed or consecrated, is mentioned,
although according to the papists its consecration is necessary for the
Eucharist. Indeed, by equal right it could be inferred that priests among the
papists can offer without wine, because Christ, who according to them
performed the priest’s function, did not employ wine.

XX. Although Acts 2:42 and 20:7 concern the Eucharist, they do not favor
communion under one kind. The expression is a synecdoche familiar to the
Hebrews, in which the whole is designated from a part. Thus when Genesis 31:54
says that Joseph “ate bread with his brothers,” that is, held a feast, it
would be wrongly inferred that only food and no drink was set before them.
Likewise, when the celebration of the Passover is expressed by “eating the
Passover,” not only the eating of the lamb is meant, but also the use of the
chalice customarily furnished. Otherwise, if the expression is pressed, it
would follow that the apostles consecrated and ate only bread, which the
adversaries do not concede. Bellarmine’s flight here to the Nazirites, who
abstained from wine, is mere mockery: nothing about them is stated, but is
invented by the cardinal; and they were few and rare, abstaining not for life
but for a particular act, so no account of them had to be taken in comparison
with the great multitude. Add that the Nazirites are opposed in vain, since
the papists maintain that not wine but its empty species remains in the
chalice.

XXI. Although we have exchanged immersion for sprinkling in baptism, it does
not follow that the chalice can be taken away in the same way. 1. It is not
likely that the church changed this custom of immersing without some apostolic
authority. Indeed, as said above, it is highly probable that the apostles
sometimes used sprinkling, especially when a great multitude was to be
baptized, as happened on the day of Pentecost when three thousand were
baptized. But nothing of this sort can be said about taking away the chalice.
2. When the church changed the custom of immersing, it substituted sprinkling,
which is equivalent to immersion in this respect because it no less denotes
the pouring out and application of Christ’s blood and has the same efficacy;
hence Christ’s blood is called “the blood of sprinkling” in Hebrews 12:24. But
what did the Roman church substitute when it took away the chalice? Nothing at
all. 3. Immersion is not essential but accidental to baptism; the use of the
chalice, however, is essential and not accidental to the Eucharist, as was
said.

XXII. The fiction of concomitance, by which the whole Christ is believed to be
contained under one kind and to be received equally under one as under both,
cannot help the papists’ cause. 1. It rests on the false supposition of the
transubstantiation of the bread into Christ’s body. 2. It confuses “the
concomitance of the signs” with “the concomitance of the thing signified.” We
indeed acknowledge the concomitance of body and blood in the thing signified
itself, insofar as these are joined by both natural and personal union. But we
deny that it therefore has a place in the signifying signs, because, since
they exist by institution, their conjunction must be the one Christ
instituted. Christ willed that there be two signs and two manners of
signifying, so that the body would be taken with the bread and the blood with
the wine, not that both would be taken with the bread through concomitance.
Therefore, even if the whole Christ were taken under one kind, communion ought
not for that reason to take place under one kind, because Christ’s command must
not be violated under any pretext; and in whatever manner the thing signified
is received, the sign instituted by Christ

[1847 vol. III printed p. 392 | IA leaf 426 | PDF p. 426]

always had to be retained. Otherwise Christ would be accused of ignorance, as
if he
had multiplied unnecessary ceremonies. It is therefore one thing to speak of
the whole Christ and another of both kinds. The latter concerns the signs, the
former the thing signified; and it is not true that if the whole thing
signified is in one kind, both kinds are therefore in one. The question,
however, is whether injury is done to the people by depriving them of the
chalice. Innocent III, the Roman pontiff, acknowledged this in book 6 and book
4, chapter 11 of *On the Mystery of the Holy Altar*: “Although under the
species of bread the blood is taken with the body, and under the species of
wine the body is taken with the blood, nevertheless the blood is neither drunk
nor eaten under the species of bread, nor is the body drunk or eaten under the
species of wine. For just as blood is not eaten nor body drunk, so neither is
drunk under the species of bread or eaten under the species of wine.” 3. It is
one thing to take Christ spiritually and another sacramentally. I grant that
one who takes Christ’s body spiritually also takes the blood, but not one who
takes it sacramentally, which is the reception at issue here. 4. It is one
thing to represent Christ’s living body and another to represent it dead. With
respect to the former, where Christ’s living body is signified, the blood must
be present; but not with respect to the latter. At the Supper, as is known,
Christ is exhibited not as living but as dead, his body as broken, and his
blood as poured out and separated from the body. Therefore, although Christ
cannot be divided in his absolute being, in his relative and sacramental being
he can be presented to us as divided and dead, as flesh separated from blood.
5. The rationale presses just as much against clergy as against laity. If the
bread draws the blood through concomitance, not even the priest will need the
chalice, for no reason exists why the priest should be bound to both kinds and
communicants to one. 6. These predications would be proper: “Bread is blood;
wine is body,” although no one would say that.

XXIII. It is falsely assumed that the chalice does not pertain to the
sacrament’s essence, for nothing belongs more to the essence of a sacrament
than its parts, and the chalice is a part of the sacrament. Nor should it be
said that the matter here is merely some article of discipline that the church
can freely change. No one is given the right to change what Christ instituted,
especially in things that pertain to the essence of worship and are prescribed
to all as necessary. Otherwise the church could take the bread from us as
readily as the chalice, since the necessity of each is equal by Christ’s
command.

XXIV. John 6:50–51, which the adversaries use, does not help their cause,
because it does not concern sacramental communion, as will be proved later,
but spiritual communion, whether in the sacrament or outside it. Christ so
urges it with respect to eating that he does not exclude drinking, but either
tacitly includes it with the other by synecdoche or expressly adds it in verses
35 and 53–56.

XXV. Although the sacrament’s essential effect should be considered to
distinguish what pertains to its substance from what does not, it cannot
therefore be inferred that communion under one kind suffices. Rather, it must
be concluded that two kinds are necessary, because the sacrament’s essential
effect is to commemorate Christ’s death and signify to us the perfect
nourishment we have from Christ, which cannot be done by the species of bread
alone.

XXVI. Although we do not deny that in many things the law did not determine,
the church was free to establish what appeared more suitable, we deny that it
was permitted to take from the people the chalice that Christ instituted,
because this was expressly sanctioned by Christ as a perpetual law to be
observed by all.

XXVII. The arguments—or rather suspicions—that the adversaries bring forward
for dry communion under one kind cannot establish what they intend. 1. A
single institution must outweigh all singular examples,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 393 | IA leaf 427 | PDF p. 427]

because the former has the force of law and rule, while the latter do not.
According to the adversaries themselves, judgment must be made by laws, not
examples. 2. Particular actions of certain persons in certain places must not
be opposed to the agreement of all churches dispersed throughout the world
and the custom received from the beginning and observed continuously without
interruption in the public use of the church through the tenth and eleventh
centuries. 3. The custom the papists allege is neither universal nor of divine
law nor from a command, nor is it accompanied by the prohibition and
condemnation of the opposite custom—all of which occur in our communion.

XXVIII. The singular examples they cite do not help them. Not the communion
of the dying. 1. The question is not how a certain few, compelled by bodily
weakness or another necessity, took the Eucharist in private houses, but
whether ministers in the sacred assembly offered and distributed to
communicating believers, by common right and custom, one kind alone or both.
2. We acknowledge that in the fifth and following centuries the Eucharist was
given to the sick and dying, but deny that it was given under one kind. Indeed,
various examples establish that the dying employed both kinds. Thus it is
reported of Valentinus of Passau in the fifth century “that before death he
received with his own hands the sacrament of the Lord’s body and blood”; and
in the seventh century of Remaclus, bishop of Maastricht, Audomar, bishop of
Thérouanne, and various others reported by the distinguished Dallæus in book
5, chapter 11. Here belongs a canon of the Council of Tours, included by
Burchard in his decrees, which concerns the viaticum or offering to be given
to believers departing this life. It commands “that the sacred offering be
dipped in Christ’s blood, so that the presbyter can truly say to the sick
person: ‘May the Lord’s body and blood profit you.’” What is cited about
Serapion does not compel us. It is one example and cannot be opposed to
innumerable others, indeed to the church’s constant practice. Moreover, it is
evident that he received from the boy sent by the presbyter a morsel not dry
but moistened with wine, undoubtedly because a man exhausted by age could not
swallow the bread unless it were moistened. “The presbyter,” Dionysius of
Alexandria says in Eusebius, book 6, chapter 44, “gave a small part of the
Eucharist to the boy sent to summon him, commanding him to moisten it and drop
it into the old man’s mouth”—*apobrexai keleusas* (ἀποβρέξαι κελευσας), as the
Greek words have it. The example of the dying Ambrose presses no more. In
chapter 25 of his life by Paulinus of Africa, he is said to have swallowed the
morsel and immediately expired. Therefore, if he did not take the chalice, it
was not because it was prohibited to him, but because the chalice could not be
offered to a dead man.

XXIX. Not the domestic communion of those who ate particles of the Eucharist
taken home and preserved dry. Besides the fact that it is one thing to speak
of private use and another of the public use at issue here, if they could carry
bread home, why not wine as well? Albaspinæus, bishop of Orléans, rightly notes
this in book 1, *Observations*, chapter 4, and proves it by the celebrated
example of Gregory of Nazianzus. Speaking of his sister Gorgonia in Oration 11,
he teaches most clearly that believers customarily preserved both kinds of the
sacrament: “If her hand had stored anywhere any of the antitypes of the
precious body or blood, she mixed it with her tears, with which she said that
she bathed her entire body as with a kind of medicine.”

XXX. Not the communion of infants. It is true that from Cyprian’s time until
the twelfth century infants were constantly communicated in the Latin church,
as they certainly are today among the Greeks and Ethiopians. But it cannot be
established that they were communicated under one kind. Indeed, from Cyprian’s
sermon *On the Lapsed* it is gathered that the infants’ communion was full,
not halved, for he introduces them speaking thus: “We did nothing,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 394 | IA leaf 428 | PDF p. 428]

nor did we voluntarily hasten to profane pollutions after abandoning the
Lord’s food and cup.” The same is inferred from Augustine, Letter 107.

XXXI. Not lay communion, to which clerics were condemned. Formerly this did
not signify what it does today, namely communion under one kind, but, as
Cassander observes in *On Communion under Both Kinds*, denoted nothing other
than the order and rank of communicating. He acknowledges that all the most
learned today recognize “that the statements about lay communion found in the
ancients ought not to be understood of a different kind of communion, but of
the rank, order, and place of the communicants, which in another form of speech
was called communicating among the laity, with the laity, or as a layman.”
Albaspinæus solidly proves the same in book 1, *Observations*, chapter 4, and so
explained the true rationale of that lay communion hidden in the monuments of
the ancients that no reason for further doubt remains. He is followed here by
Morinus in book 1, exercise 9 of *Ecclesiastical Exercises*, and Rigaltius on
Cyprian’s passage about Trophimus. Thus we do not deny that lay communion was
given, but it did not create a new kind of communion, only a new order with
respect to the person: one who had formerly been a cleric, or on the clerical
roll, was then placed in the order of the laity and communicated with them.
This had the character of a kind of punishment, or at least of diminished
dignity and ministry, so that, deprived of the ministry of consecrating the
Eucharist, he was content with communion alone. Others maintain that it was
called lay communion from the place, because the laity prayed and communicated
outside the rails by the archdeacon’s ministry—that is, outside the choir, as
they say today—as Pamelius notes on Cyprian, Letter 52. Yet this explanation
cannot establish that this lay communion was under one kind. See Chamier, book
9, chapter 3.

XXXII. Not the communion of the presanctified, so called because the Eucharist
was communicated not on the very day it had been prepared or consecrated, but
on the previous day or even an earlier weekday. In Lent the Greeks indeed
offer only on Sunday and Saturday; on the other days they use *proēgiasmenois*
(προηγιασμενοις), which are so called because they had already been
sanctified. Nothing is gathered from this for the adversaries’ cause as though
communion occurred under one kind alone. Indeed, the word itself proves the
contrary, for *ta proēgiasmena* (τα προηγιασμενα) cannot fittingly be said of
only one kind but of both, and it is evident that the plural number is ascribed
to both. The liturgy of the presanctified mentions participation in Christ’s
body and blood. From the Roman ordinal’s office for Good Friday it is also
evident that on Good Friday the pontiff and people were sanctified or
consecrated even under the species of wine by placing bread in it, and thus
communicated under both kinds. Whatever may be true of that rite, it is certain
that it was fairly recent: it was not in use among the Latins before the
seventh century and has no witness among the Greeks earlier than the Council
in Trullo, as Dallæus teaches in chapter 22.

XXXIII. The church has no more power to change sacramental rites instituted by
Christ than it has to change the word and law. For as the latter contains an
audible sign of the divine will, so the former contain a visible one. But
Christ instituted communion under both kinds, and the chalice pertains to the
sacrament’s external matter and therefore to its essence. The rationale of
*eutaxias* (εὐταξίας), or external order, is different; in this sense the
apostle’s words in the final verse of 1 Corinthians 11 are to be understood. If
this power is given to the church, what prevents it from being said that the
church can abolish the entire sacrament of the Supper? The causes usually
cited for mutilating the Supper were neither just nor grave: a shortage of
wine, danger of spilling, reverence for the sacrament, or the impossibility of
distributing the chalice in places where the people are very numerous and
there is only one priest. Christ foresaw these same inconveniences, but did
not therefore refrain from commanding the apostles to drink and to do this in
remembrance of him; nor did the ancient

[1847 vol. III printed p. 395 | IA leaf 429 | PDF p. 429]

church regard these inconveniences as grounds for mutilating communion.
Furthermore, these cases occur neither always nor for the most part and cannot
prevent the full Supper from being administered. A scarcity of wine in some
places can no more cause wine to be taken from other peoples in sacred use
than in common use. The lack can be supplied by another liquid, its
*antiballomenō* (ἀντιβαλλομένῳ), so to speak, as is done among the Ethiopians
with dried grapes soaked in water. The danger of spilling and reverence for
the sacrament rest on the false hypothesis of transubstantiation. Here the
papists especially regarded its confirmation no less than that of the
sacrifice of the Mass and the elevation of the clergy over the people.

XXXIV. It cannot be said that the same fruit is received from either kind as
from both. This reception of fruit must be measured by the efficacy that lies
in signifying. But bread does not signify blood, nor wine body. Therefore
bread does not effect—that is, represent and seal—the blood, nor wine the
body. Consequently, one who takes only either one cannot, with respect to this
proper kind of efficacy, be said to receive the thing signified by the other.
Hence the adversaries themselves acknowledge that the sacrament’s signification
is more perfect under both kinds than under one, and therefore its fruit is
also greater under both. Finally, Christ’s institution alone ought to be
everything to us in retaining the use of the chalice as common to all.

XXXV. The argument drawn from those who abstain from wine, who communicate
under one kind only, cannot favor the adversaries. An insuperable exception
affecting a few ought not overturn the general rule. Abstention from the
chalice is not voluntary and free—which would be altogether culpable—but
involuntary and unavoidable through natural and inescapable inability. If,
therefore, they do not use the chalice, they are not for that reason to be
judged guilty of violating the divine command. The command regards only those
who can use wine, not those in whom God has placed such an insuperable
impediment and whom he has thereby willed to dispense from this command. So
far is this from weakening Christ’s command that it strengthens it, as an
exception added to a rule usually binds the rule more firmly. Since only those
who abhor wine are dispensed from receiving the chalice, all others are thereby
understood to be bound to observe the command. This is illustrated by the
example of the deaf. Although the command to hear the Word is given to all, it
binds only those who can hear, not the deaf. If the deaf are dispensed from it,
one would wrongly infer that this is also lawful for others.

XXXVI. The article of discipline in our churches by which those who abstain
from wine are dispensed from using the chalice—provided that by merely bringing
the chalice to their mouth they testify to the reverence with which they
regard Christ’s institution and show that they abstain from it not through
contempt but inability—cannot help the papists. 1. Our practice differs widely
from the Roman. It is one thing to tolerate the weakness of those who cannot
use wine; another to take away and prohibit the chalice from those who lack
such weakness. Our people do the former, the papists the latter. 2. The article
of discipline is a work of charity and *synkatabaseōs* (συγκαταβάσεως), or
accommodation, toward a few persons under inescapable necessity. But the
papist dogma is an absolute and unqualified prohibition toward everyone,
without necessity and contrary to Christ’s express command. 3. The discipline
does not wish the kinds of the sacrament to be divided. Indeed, it wishes the
whole sacrament to be retained and jointly offered and distributed to the
people by the ministers, although those abstaining from wine cannot receive
both kinds but only one. Roman doctrine and practice, however, wish the
sacrament to be divided by the priests and communion to be mutilated. According
to us, those who abstain from wine ought not only to desire the chalice but
also leave nothing undone to overcome their inability. The papists so take the
chalice from the people that it is unlawful for them to seek or touch it. In a
word, since according to our position

[1847 vol. III printed p. 396 | IA leaf 430 | PDF p. 430]

no one is kept from using the chalice, the pastors are commanded to offer both
kinds, and believers to do everything in their power to receive it, whereas
among the Romans use of the chalice is expressly forbidden to the people, the
wide difference between our position and the papist one is clear.

XXXVII. Although those who abstain from wine receive the sacrament under one
kind only, that communion is not therefore to be regarded as merely illusory.
(1) The whole sacrament is offered to them by the minister, and if they are
compelled to abstain from one kind, this is not done willingly and from
contempt, but from mere inability and inescapable necessity. (2) Although by
God’s ordinance the kinds are indivisible in the sacrament, this applies only
to those who can use them, not others whom he dispenses through the obstacle
he has placed. As he made the law, so he alone can dispense from it whom he
wills and, in certain cases, separate the kinds that he joined and willed to
be joined. (3) The lack of using or tasting the other kind can in some measure
be supplied both by desire and by sight, touch, smell, and other sensations.
(4) Although both signs are not received, they do not cease to become
partakers of the whole thing signified, which is indivisible.

XXXVIII. The eucharistic chalice was instituted by Christ as the symbol of a
threefold mystical chalice represented to us in the sacrament. 1. The chalice
of Christ’s sufferings in Matthew 26:39, of which the poured-out wine is the
memorial. 2. The chalice of grace and consolation in Psalms 16:5 and 23:5, of
which the cup offered for drinking is the seal. 3. The chalice of praise and
