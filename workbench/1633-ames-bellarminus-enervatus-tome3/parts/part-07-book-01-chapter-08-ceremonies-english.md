## Chapter VIII. On Ceremonies

### Thesis I

Bellarmine. A ceremony, properly and simply so called, is an outward action
which is good and praiseworthy for no other reason than that it is performed
for the worship of God.

Protestant reply. If *action* is taken here to include the thing about which
the action is immediately concerned, we admit this description. From it we
conclude that Bellarmine and others absurdly confuse order, ornament, and
similar things—which have the same use and commendation in civil affairs as
they have in worship—with religious ceremonies.

2. Bellarmine. Christ or the apostles instituted certain ceremonies which we
have from no Scripture but from tradition alone.

Protestant reply. 1. Concerning Christ, Medina answers in *Prima secundae*,
question 108, article 2: “In the evangelical law, no sacramentals”—that is,
ceremonies of the sacraments—“were instituted by Christ the Lord, because of
the dignity and excellence of our sacraments. They are so illustrious that,
even if they were presented bare and supported by no sacramentals, they would
be worthy of every veneration.” 2. The same reasoning applies no less to the
apostles than to Christ himself and is strongly confirmed by the apostle's
words in 1 Corinthians 11:23: “I received from the Lord what I also delivered
to you.” No tradition apart from Scripture can give us the certainty of
divine faith concerning the institutions of Christ and the apostles.

Bellarmine. In 1 Corinthians 11:34 the apostle says, “I will set the other
things in order when I come,” and yet he afterward wrote nothing about the
rite of the sacrament of the Eucharist.

Protestant reply. 1. It is one thing to arrange or order things already
instituted, and another to institute things that are to be ordered and
arranged. 2. The apostle instituted no religious ceremony in that entire
chapter. Such ceremonies therefore cannot be understood by “the other
things,” which must be of the same kind. 3. The apostle arranged nothing there
which the Corinthian church ought not already to have arranged and ordered in
the same manner without any new institution. He therefore promises nothing—

[1632 witness | printed p. 40]

—more.

Bellarmine. Certain fathers affirm that some ceremonies are apostolic and
sacrosanct.

Protestant reply. They affirm this about various ceremonies which the papists
themselves no longer possess. Either they made this affirmation falsely, or
the papists have abrogated apostolic institutions.

3. Bellarmine. Some ceremonies possess spiritual power.

Protestant reply. 1. Why do some, and not all, possess it if they proceeded
from the same Spirit? 2. Since Scripture is the rule that perfectly directs
us in spiritual life, from it alone we must learn which means possess
spiritual power. 3. If certain ceremonies possess this spiritual power,
whether inherent or assisting, they are of greater efficacy and dignity than
the sacraments of the Old Testament or even the baptism of John, according to
Bellarmine's teaching noted above. They are parts of the New Testament, which
alone is the ministry of the Spirit, and consequently, on Bellarmine's
position, the whole New Testament is not contained in Scripture. This is
exceedingly absurd.

4. Bellarmine. The church can institute new ceremonies for spiritual effects:
first, to adorn and represent some mystery of religion and thereby assist the
less instructed; second, to drive away diseases and demons and purge venial
sins, and this, as is probable, through the application of Christ's merits
*ex opere operato*.

Protestant reply. 1. The church is not called to make institutions but to
observe Christ's institutions. Matthew 28, final verse. 2. For adornment and
representation, anyone can institute a ceremony, in Bellarmine's judgment,
just as well as the church or God himself. For so he says in Book 2, chapter
8: “For signification it does not matter who instituted the signs, provided
they represent the same object.” In truth, however, no one can institute a
ceremony to represent a mystery of religion except him who has power over
religion, over people's minds to enlighten them when and how he pleases, and
over people's consciences to subject them to himself and his institutions.
All these powers are required to institute such a ceremony rightly; therefore
no one can do so except God alone. 3. No one can institute a ceremony to cure
diseases, drive away demons, and purge sins unless he has the power either to
bring these things about however he pleases or to obtain that they be done.
Can the church institute a dream so that it signifies rest from sin, drives
away demons, and purges sins? These dreams are plainly deranged. Those who can
do such things can even turn dreams into sacraments. 4. The efficacy of
ceremonies *ex opere ope—*

[1632 witness | printed p. 41]

*—rato* was long ago rejected by the scholastics themselves: Paludanus,
Silvester, Angelo, Tabiena, Gabriel, Abulensis, Petro Soto, Marsilius,
Alexander, Thomas, Bonaventure, and Richard, as may be seen in Azor, Book 4,
chapter 11. There the same Azor also shows splendidly what sort of tables can
be produced for this authority of the church. “The controversy,” he says,
“is whence the church has the faculty by which it establishes remedies for
obtaining the remission of venial sins.” He answers it thus: “It has it from
the same words from which it has authority from Christ to grant, outside the
sacraments, pardons of sins which we call indulgences.” He assumes the claim
of Durandus, Book 4, distinction 20, question 3: “Scripture does not speak
expressly about indulgences.” The church therefore does not have from
Scripture this faculty of instituting ceremonies.

5. Bellarmine. The church is permitted to institute new ceremonies for
certain ends, because private persons, with God inspiring and impelling them,
devised new ceremonies.

Protestant reply. As though God's inspiration did not make God himself the
author of the deed just as much as a command expressed in the word does.
Otherwise, on the same reasoning, the papists could conclude that they have
power to institute even new sacrifices and sacraments.

Bellarmine 2. The synagogue added a new feast. Esther 9; 1 Maccabees 4.

Protestant reply. It was answered above concerning these days that the former
was political and the latter to be condemned.

Bellarmine 3. The apostles instituted a new ceremony in Acts 15.

Protestant reply. It has also been answered elsewhere that no new ceremony
was instituted there; rather, provision was made against scandal by tolerating
an old ceremony.

Bellarmine 4. The church can establish something. Ceremonies do not conflict
with the new law, nor did the Lord prohibit us from adding ceremonies for the
more convenient and useful administration of his sacraments.

Protestant reply. 1. The church can establish no new thing by its own
authority. 2. Carnal ceremonies devoid of the Spirit, as all human ceremonies
are, conflict with the perfection of the New Testament. 3. Human ceremonies
can no more make the sacraments of Christ be administered more conveniently
and usefully than they were administered by Christ and the apostles than
doctrines of faith devised by human beings can more conveniently illuminate
the faith revealed by Christ. Shall we suppose that certain modern people
have a more perfect understanding of which ceremonies ought to be used in
baptism than the holy apostles and Christ himself did?

6. Bellarmine. Ceremonies instituted by the church cannot be omitted without
sin, even apart from scandal.

[1632 witness | printed p. 42]

Protestant reply. This matter was treated in *On the Pontiff*, chapter 5. For
the present it is a sufficient answer that religious ceremonies cannot be
instituted by the church without sin, and consequently can and indeed ought
to be omitted without sin.

7. Bellarmine. Ceremonies are useful, meritorious things and a part of divine
worship.

Protestant reply. Ceremonies instituted by God are useful and a part of
worship, although they are not properly meritorious. The case is far different
for ceremonies instituted by human beings.

Bellarmine. All our ceremonies are indicated by God in general: 1. in Paul's
testimony in 1 Corinthians 14, “Let all things be done decently and in order”;
2. when we are commanded to obey our superiors; and 3. where religion and its
parts are commended.

Protestant reply. 1. Decency and order were maintained very well in the
primitive church without human ceremonies. 2. In that very passage they are
tacitly prohibited, because nothing is left to the church except the decent
ordering of things instituted by Christ. For institutions and their ordering
differ as a subject and its outward adjunct. 3. Superiors must be obeyed while
they carry out the commands of him by whom they are placed over others. 4.
True religion renders honor to God according to his own will, by means
instituted by him; it therefore does not admit human ceremonies.

8. Bellarmine. It is a most wise institution that in the Latin church the
sacraments are administered in Latin.

Protestant reply. This was instituted by God: that they be administered to
Latin speakers in Latin, to the English in English, and to the Dutch in Dutch.
But this matter was disputed in Tome I.

Ames's arguments.

9. Protestant argument. No snare is to be cast upon Christian consciences, 1
Corinthians 7; that is, the necessity of those things for which the Lord has
left liberty is not to be imposed upon them.

Bellarmine. It was right for the apostle not to make a precept of what the
Lord wished to be a counsel, but the reasoning is not the same for other
matters about which the Lord prescribed nothing in particular.

Protestant reply. The argument from the lesser to the greater is exceedingly
strong. If not even those things which the Lord counseled and praised ought to
be commanded, then those things in religion which the Lord approved in no way
certainly must not be commanded.

10. Protestant argument. Christ fully instructed us concerning the true
worship of God.

Bellarmine. This is true of general instruction, but not of particular
instruction.

Protestant reply. General instruction can be of two kinds. Either it is such—

[1632 witness | printed p. 43]

—that matters belonging to particular instruction necessarily follow from it
and are clearly understood without any new institution, or it is such that it
requires new institutions for its particular determination. Unless Christ had
instructed us concerning worship in the former manner, his instruction would
not have been complete. The instruction that we have about celebrating the
Lord's Supper with bread and wine, without a specific determination of this
or that particular bread and wine, must be considered general in this manner,
not particular; no new institution is needed for their selection. 2. Christ
prescribed some worship to us in particular, and he left no instruction for
instituting other worship. Therefore he prescribed all true worship in
particular. 3. All true worship is prescribed generally in the Decalogue
itself, so that nothing is lacking beyond particular institution. Christ's
instruction is therefore particular. 4. If the particular institution of any
worship is left to human beings, then they have a kingdom and authority
divided equally with Christ. 5. If Christ institutes some worship only in
general and human beings institute it specifically, then the human share in
that worship is much greater than Christ's, for a specific nature always
excels a general one.

11. Protestant argument. The apostle teaches that doctrine concerning true
worship is not to be sought from human beings.

Bellarmine. Not from people who teach things contrary to Christ, but indeed
from those who build upon Christ and, according to his rule and teaching,
teach in particular what he indicated only in general. “He who hears you hears
me,” Luke 10.

Protestant reply. 1. On this reasoning a new, particular gospel could be taught
by human beings, because Christ indicated the gospel only in general and left
particular gospels for human beings to teach. For it cannot be denied that the
words “He who hears you hears me” are to be understood of the gospel; and
indeed evangelical worship is a part of the gospel. 2. The apostle expressly
excludes all human beings: “No one adds to it,” Galatians 3:15. He specifically
excludes himself too, together with angels and angelic people: “If we or an
angel from heaven,” Galatians 1:8.

12. Protestant argument. The apostle condemns all human and voluntary worship.

Bellarmine. He condemns worship that is merely human and devised by one's own
ingenuity—that is, worship which does not conform to the faith and principles
of Christ's teaching. But the things taught by the church are not merely
human, since they are instituted under God's inspiration.

Protestant reply. 1. Forms of worship instituted by human beings are either
invented by their own ingenuity or revealed by God for them to institute. The
latter is absurd. 2. Worship instituted by human—

[1632 witness | printed p. 44]

—beings cannot conform to this principle of Christian doctrine and faith:
Jesus Christ is our only teacher, lawgiver, and Lord. 3. To attribute
ceremonies instituted by human beings to divine inspiration is nothing other
than to rave with those Libertines whom Bellarmine himself refutes in *On the
Word of God*, Book 1, chapter 1.

13. Protestant argument. In Galatians 5 the apostle in no way permits the
consciences of believers to be reduced to slavery.

Bellarmine. He is speaking of Jewish slavery.

Protestant reply. Is not the reasoning the same for Roman and Antichristian
slavery?

14. Protestant argument. In Matthew 15 and Isaiah 29, worship that arises from
human tradition is rejected.

Bellarmine. 1. If it conflicts with God's commands. 2. If it is useless and
empty. 3. If more is made of it than of the divine commands.

Protestant reply. 1. None of these applies more to Jewish traditions than to
Roman ones. 2. On this reasoning Christ would have distinguished between
different forms of worship instituted by human beings. But he simply condemns
worship based on human command without any distinction. 3. Christ's disciples
could have continued to wash their hands once those abuses were corrected if
the institution itself had been commendable. Nor could that ceremony be held
useless, since it had a mystical signification and by that means assisted the
affections, understanding, and memory, according to Bellarmine's teaching.

15. Protestant argument. In their ceremonies the Romans have imitated partly
the Gentiles and partly the Jews.

Bellarmine 1. The Gentiles and Jews also had some ceremonies similar to
baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Protestant reply. Yet we receive these sacraments not from them but from
Christ. There is therefore no imitation here.

Bellarmine 2. The Jewish ceremonies were figures of ours.

Protestant reply. God, then, instituted figures of things to be instituted by
human beings. Let this be placed among those blasphemies by which God's name
and tabernacle are assailed. Revelation 13.

Bellarmine 3. The difference lies in the end and intention. Gentile rites were
performed for the worship of demons; ours are performed to worship the true
God.

Protestant reply. Moses explicitly testifies that a different intention and
end do not establish a sufficient distinction, Deuteronomy 12:31: “You shall
not do so to Jehovah your God.” As Cajetan observes, the literal command there
is that they not worship God with ceremonies and rites similar to those which
the Gentiles observed when worshiping their gods.

16. Protestant argument. Augustine in Letter 119 complains that by the mul—

[1632 witness | printed p. 45]

—titude of ceremonies some people had so burdened the church, even in his
time, that the condition of the Jews was more tolerable.

Bellarmine. He is speaking of ceremonies which private persons, especially
silly women, impose upon themselves.

Protestant reply. 1. He speaks expressly of those ceremonies by which religion
was oppressed and subjected to human presumptions. He speaks of burdens such
as the Jewish ones were—that is, public burdens, not private ones.

17. Protestant argument. Ceremonies of this kind remove the distinction which
God wished to exist between Jews and Christians, because they have a pedagogy
similar to the Jewish one.

Bellarmine. The difference consists in this: Jewish worship, insofar as it was
Jewish—that is, of the Old Testament—was principally outward and bodily,
whereas Christian worship is principally inward and spiritual.

Protestant reply. 1. It is plainly impossible for some worship to be
principally bodily and less principally spiritual. For the spiritual always
holds primacy when it is joined with the bodily. 2. On this reasoning, all the
old ceremonies could have been continued under the law and condition that
they be subordinated to spiritual worship.

18. Protestant argument. That law once spoken to the church remains eternal:
“What I command you, this you shall observe to do. You shall add nothing, nor
take anything away.”

Bellarmine. The Lord prohibits a corrupting addition.

Protestant reply. Whatever of law is added to a perfect law, and whatever of
worship is added to perfect worship, is a corrupting addition, because it
denies its perfection and so corrupts it. The corruption of perfection exists
equally in a redundant member as in a defect. But this wretched evasion,
together with the others Bellarmine repeats here, was sufficiently exposed
above: in Tome I, *On the Jurisdiction of the Roman Pontiff*, and in Tome II,
*On the Worship of the Saints*. I therefore have no wish to repeat the same
things here with Bellarmine. It will nevertheless be helpful to add this: by
the confession of very weighty men who favor the papists, various papist
ceremonies are corrupting additions. As evidence, take what Ludovicus Vives
has in his comment on Augustine's *City of God*, Book 8, final chapter: “It is
now the custom, at the time when the sacred memorial of Christ's death is
celebrated, to present the people with spectacles differing in no respect from
those ancient theatrical shows. It is shameful for plays to be performed in
the most serious matter. They are carried out with great wickedness and
impiety, attributable not so much to those who watch or perform as to the
priests who arrange for such things to be done.”
