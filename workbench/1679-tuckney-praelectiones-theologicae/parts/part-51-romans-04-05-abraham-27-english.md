As for Durandus's argument—namely, that if acts of vision and faith existed
together in the heavenly homeland, they would exist either as one act or as
distinct acts—we do not say that they are one act, so his refutation of that
claim does not harm us in the least. We affirm that they are distinct acts,
and Durandus himself asserts that their coexistence is at least possible.
But when he denies their actual coexistence because the cognitive medium of
faith has no place in heaven—holding this medium to be non-evident knowledge
of the authority of Scripture as inspired by God, while the fact of
Scripture's divine inspiration is self-evidently known to the blessed by
that vision—I briefly reply as follows.

1. The very arguments Durandus adduces to disprove the actual coexistence
of acts of faith with vision would equally undermine the compatibility of
faith's habit and act, although he argues extensively for that
compatibility. This could be demonstrated if time permitted.

2. We showed above, and Durandus must pardon us, that the imperfect does not
belong to the formal nature of faith's object, but only the less perfect.
The same distinction can be made between the non-evident and the less
evident. Faith in some sense will therefore not be driven out of heaven
merely because its evidence is less than that of vision.

3. Grant, then, that the divine inspiration of Scripture is self-evidently
known to the blessed from vision. Yet this is not the only medium by which
faith operates and believes. Its medium is whatever God has said in any
manner, whether the thing is less evident in itself or even most evident.
Consequently, if a blessed person believes something because he remembers
it being said in Scripture, or otherwise revealed either in this life or
the next, then even if it is most evident, this believing of God as one who
affirms it will be an act of faith properly so called, in whatever form
faith has in the heavenly homeland.

It appears possible to show that heavenly vision does not entirely remove
this faith.

1. Paul in his rapture, mentioned in 2 Corinthians 12, possessed for a time
a vision of God like that which the blessed inhabitants of heaven possess.
Yet during that time he did not lose his faith, at least with respect to
its habit, just as he was not entirely stripped of his body.

[1679 printed p. 268 | IA leaf 276]

2. Faith in testimony concerning a bodily visible thing is not removed by
the presentation and presence of the thing about which the testimony is
given, nor by scientific knowledge of it through its causes, but is instead
confirmed. We have greater faith in the prophets' predictions when we see
the predicted events accomplished before us. Nor did the disciples in
Matthew 14 have less faith in Christ saying, “It is I,” once they afterward
perceived by clear sight that it was he. Thus when sight confirms testimony,
it does not remove faith in the thing testified.

Furthermore, faith in its essence existed in the state of integrity.
Therefore it will exist in the state of glory.

The saints and angels in heaven believe in God, though not in God considered
in just any manner, but in him whom they see as he is. Faith therefore does
not retain this designation insofar as it regards things not seen and hoped
for. In its essence, however, it remains. Likewise, love is called mercy in
this life because it is exercised toward the miserable. In that respect it
ceases in the heavenly homeland, where no one will be miserable, but in its
essence it remains there.

To conclude everything briefly: what should finally be determined about
faith's continuance in heaven, when arguments and testimonies stand on both
sides? This:

1. It remains with respect to its habit. Many grant this.

2. It remains with respect to the very possibility of its act. Durandus
establishes this.

3. As to the actual exercise of its acts, two things must be said.

1. Faith does not remain with respect to that lesser degree of perfection
which either must belong to it in this life or could belong to it in the
next. For the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 13:10, “When what is perfect
has come, what is in part will be abolished.” Although faith's acts remain
with respect to their substance, they nevertheless cease, first, with
respect to the obscurity or lesser evidence of the thing believed and its
enigmatic cognition insofar as faith is “the evidence of things not seen.”
For then “we shall see face to face,” that is, most clearly, and “we shall
know as we are known,” that is, most perfectly. Second, they also cease with
respect to the futurity of believed things not yet obtained, insofar as
faith is “the substance of things hoped for,” all of which will then be
present. “Why does anyone hope for what he sees?” Romans 8:24. In this sense
I say with Durandus that faith's acts are removed, because their imperfection
and, in this respect, their necessity are removed.

2. Conversely, there is no need for those acts to be removed which denote
no imperfection in themselves and thus can properly belong even to the state
of those who possess the goal. Such acts are these:

1. Firm knowledge of revealed things. In this sense Cornelius a Lapide, on
1 Corinthians 13:13, wishes Tertullian and Irenaeus to be interpreted when
they intimate that faith remains in the future life, and Aquinas holds it
most certainly true that faith actually remains in this way.

2. Firm belief in things testified by God, not on account of their evidence
in themselves but on account of the authority of God who testifies. God's
testimony has no less force among the blessed than among wayfarers.

3. Firm adherence to Christ as the Head and Author of salvation, although
salvation has now been ob-

tained. Since his righteousness is everlasting, Daniel 9:24, they will
gratefully acknowledge it to him forever. Since everlasting joy will be
upon their heads, those redeemed by Jehovah will hear it still and forever,
Isaiah 35:10.

4. Firm persuasion of God's love. Here it rests on God's promise, either
yet to be fulfilled or fulfilled in part; there it rests on the experience
of the promise perfectly fulfilled.

5. Finally, firm trust and confidence that the glory they enjoy through
Christ will continue forever. Here is faith's struggle; there are perfect
victory and perpetual triumph.

Faith has different operations according to the believers' different ages,
states, and opportunities for believing. In the essence of the thing, the
faith of the Jewish children who in that infant age believed in the Messiah
to come was the same as that of adult Christians under the Gospel, who
embrace Christ after he has come. Nor is faith here in seed and firstfruits
specifically different from itself in the full harvest. In this infancy it
first beholds Christ gradually, pants after him, apprehends him, rests upon
him, and at last, having been made certain and secure, enjoys peace,
rejoices in him, glories, and exults. Some of the weaker operations belong
only to wayfarers, but the greater and broader ones would not disgrace even
the blessed and therefore will remain eternally in heaven.

“We do indeed hope that we will always enjoy him, but the light of glory
makes that certainty firmer and more evident than for us to call it by the
name of faith or hope, which labor under various weaknesses.” James
Cappellus, *Observations on Romans 8:24*.

So much for the first question: whether faith remains to some extent in the
heavenly homeland or is entirely abolished there, against Bellarmine. Let
us now proceed to the second.
