SECTION FOUR.

JUNIUS. 1. I prove that the cause of this act is not in the creature, as was stated above at
Proposition 10: first, by the authority of Christ in Matthew 25, and of Paul in Romans 8 and 9 and
Ephesians 1.

2. Second, by reason. For even the first sin itself exists only by a negative act of God, and sin
cannot be a cause of that negative act. For the same thing cannot be both its own cause and its own
consequence. Election and non-election, moreover, anticipate even the first sin, as we demonstrated
above. Likewise, God’s positive and negative acts precede every act of a creature, whether good or
evil. For there is no evil act which is not preceded by God’s negative act of permitting the evil,
and so forth. What, then, is the cause of that negative act except God’s most free will?

3. Third, by the example of the angels. For what restrained the good angels from evil and confirmed
them in good? A positive act of God. What did not restrain the evil angels from evil? A negative
act of God arising from non-election or preterition.

ARMINIUS. 1. But you prove that the cause of this act is not in the human being: first by authority,
then by reason, and then by example. I deny that this is proved from the authorities cited. Let it
be shown how those statements are antecedents from which this consequent is deduced. An answer to
those authorities has already been given above, as the necessity of the matter required.

2. The reason you offer proves it no better. “The first sin,” you say, “exists only by a negative
act of God,” and so forth. I grant all of this when rightly understood. But an affirmative inference
from genus to species is not valid unless a universal sign is added. Many negative acts of God
precede the act of sin; therefore, the negative act of preterition also does so. I deny that this
follows. But that is the very act in dispute. The first sin arises from a negative act of God, but
not from the act of preterition.

3. The example of the angels is dissimilar here. I show this by the following word, and so forth.

RESPONSE. 1. Arminius’s response to those authorities has already been weighed in its proper place;
and since Junius neither frames

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 54 | BSB canvas 66]

arguments nor Arminius repeats them from what went before, why should I labor either to invent
arguments of Junius for me to confirm or responses of Arminius for me to refute?

The act of preterition, I admit, is in dispute. But can you deny that the act of preterition, with
respect to the conferral of efficacious grace for abstaining from sin, preceded sin? How much more
did the decree to pass by—that is, to deny the efficacious grace by which one would abstain from
sin—precede it? But Arminius wishes to enjoy the ambiguous meaning of preterition. Preterition is
either the decree to deny grace or the decree to deny glory. He would not, I think, deny that the
decree to deny grace is antecedent to sin, but he would deny it of the decree to deny glory. Yet it
has been shown more than once how utterly contrary to reason these things are when fashioned by
Arminius. For since the decree to deny grace is the decree to permit sin, we have proved rather
often that a decree of this kind cannot precede the decree to condemn on account of sin. How much
less, then, should it be judged to precede the decree to deny glory? For the decree to deny glory
cannot be said to be subordinated to the decree to condemn on account of sin. Again, if the denial
of grace were intended by God before the denial of glory, then the denial of grace would have the
character of an end, while the denial of glory would assume the character of a means to that end.
But this is in no way fitting. For it cannot soberly be said that glory was denied to human beings
for the end that grace might be denied to them. It is much more plausible to assert that grace was
denied to human beings for the end that glory might be denied to them, although that view by no
means pleases me. What is more, it would follow from this that in execution the denial of grace
would have to be later, while the denial of glory would occupy the prior place. Therefore
preterition, insofar as it signifies the decree to deny efficacious grace, and preterition, insofar
as it signifies the decree to deny glory, are more properly prior to the foreseeing of sin than
posterior to it. Although to one who examines everything more precisely, perhaps neither seems
prior or posterior, but both simultaneous. Now Junius professed that one negative act has no cause
except God’s most free will. He did not, however, infer that it has no meritorious cause from the
fact that it proceeded from God’s most free will, as Arminius imagines. For in the case of later
sins he acknowledges that both a meritorious cause and God’s most free will have a place. For they
deserve to be punished, and nevertheless by his free will God either pardons them or does not
pardon them. Junius had said that the first sin had no meritorious cause preceding the negative act
by which that first sin was permitted. That negative act—whether you refer it to the decree or to
the decree’s execution [printed: *exemtionem*]—he called preterition. But in the case of later sins
he said that there is a
meritorious cause of the negative act; here, however, by a negative act he appears to have meant
the act of not pardoning sins, not without some irregularity of expression. But Arminius, passing
over these points, concludes in this manner: “You see, therefore, that sin is the meritorious cause
which necessarily precedes that negative act of God.” He is speaking, of course, of preterition in
his own sense, not of preterition in Junius’s sense. For Junius discusses preterition and uses the
term to signify the denial of grace or the decree to deny it; Arminius, however, philosophizes
about preterition insofar as it signifies the denial of glory or the decree to deny it. But you
might say that in his proposition Arminius used no other preterition than one which is the denial
of glory or the decree to deny it. Therefore, if Junius departed from this meaning of the word, the
fault must be charged to him, not to Arminius. But then why did Arminius not point out this
departure by Junius? Evidently because he judged it more advantageous to his arguments that these
things be confused rather than distinguished. Junius, however, followed the opportunity that was
his. For when Arminius had placed the foreseeing of sin before non-election or preterition, Junius
observed that non-election or preterition has a place with respect both to grace to be conferred
and to glory, and countered that the foreseeing of sin could not have preceded non-election with
respect to grace efficacious for abstaining from the first sin. This point, I admit, could have been
extended to every sin, without distinguishing between the first and later sin. But I neither wish
nor am able to guarantee everything Junius says; I wish only to vindicate it from Arminius’s
exceptions insofar as right reason permits. Yet this distinction of non-election or preterition, as
it is referred either to grace or to glory, would not have profited Arminius at all. For if the
foreseeing of sin by no means precedes non-election with respect to grace, it certainly should not
be held to precede non-election with respect to glory either. For the decree to deny grace is not
prior by any moment of nature to the decree to deny glory, as has already been proved. But since
those words have the form of an inference—“You see, therefore, that sin is the meritorious cause
which necessarily precedes that negative act of God”—I very much wish to know from what this
conclusion is formally deduced. Nothing occurs which might serve as a premise except what
immediately preceded: “He condemns none but sinners who depart this life in their sin.” From this,
as it appears, he infers: therefore sin is the meritorious cause of that negative act of God. The
point is stated obscurely enough. If he means the denial of glory, it is true. Just as sin is the
meritorious cause why someone is condemned, so it is the meritorious cause why glory is denied to
him. But if he means the decree to condemn or to deny glory—which is what preterition properly
signifies insofar as it is the same as non-election—the inference is altogether invalid. For if
this inference were valid—sin is the cause of damnation; therefore it is the cause of the decree to
damn—it would follow by like reasoning that good works are the cause of salvation; therefore they
are the cause of the decree to save.

3. Arminius maintains that the example of the angels is dissimilar. Come then, let us examine the
ground of the dissimilarity. “God’s negative act,” he says, “arising from non-election or
preterition, did not restrain the evil angels from evil.” Let it be so. “But,” he says, “God’s
negative act by which man was not hindered from evil but was permitted to fall into sin is not the
act of preterition.” Indeed, it is the act of preterition by which he decreed to permit man to sin,
just as the act of preterition concerning the angels was of the same kind: by it he decreed to
permit them to sin. Arminius objects: therefore all human beings are passed by. I acknowledge that
they were passed by in Adam, just as some angels were passed by—that is, by God’s decree they were
permitted to rush into sin. He adds: therefore they sinned incurably. Indeed, I say, that does not
follow. Up to this point all human beings in Adam are alike to the angels who fell in their own
persons, namely, that on both sides they were permitted to rush into sin. But they are unlike in
this respect: it was decreed that the latter would fall incurably, but the former curably, by God’s
mere good pleasure. He adds: therefore some act of election opposed at the same moment to
preterition ought also to have had a place here. I first explain this proposition. Opposed to the
act of preterition which does not restrain from evil is the act of election which confirms in good.
I now answer: it by no means follows that because God so conducted himself concerning the angels,
restraining some from evil and not restraining others, and established his decrees accordingly, he
therefore had to conduct himself in the same way concerning human beings, not restraining some from
evil and restraining others. For this could have occurred only if all human beings had been created
at the same time, just as all the angels were created. But neither could God conduct himself
concerning the angels as he conducted himself concerning human beings. For all the angels could
not have been created in one angel and afterward arisen from him by propagation, as all human
beings were created in the one Adam. But when Arminius adds that “this act was a negative act of
providence, as providence is distinguished from predestination and preterition,” this is false not
only if preterition is taken as the decree to deny grace, but also if it is taken for the decree to
deny glory together with the decree to condemn. For the predestination of human beings differs
under this

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 55 | BSB canvas 67]

description from the predestination of angels: the former is the decree to manifest God’s glory by
means of sparing mercy, while the latter is the decree to manifest his glory solely by means of
rewarding justice. Hence it comes about that the predestination of human beings necessarily
required the permission of sin as serving it; the predestination of angels did not. I have already
said above how little the treatment of adoption as the proper terminus of predestination according
to Junius’s *akribeia* pleases me, and how little that speculation will profit.
