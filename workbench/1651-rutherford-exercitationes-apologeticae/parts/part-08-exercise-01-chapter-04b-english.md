11. Let us now examine our opponents' arguments. 1. Let Jacob Arminius descend
first into the arena: *One and the same thing*, he says, *cannot be both
necessary and contingent at the same time, because necessary and contingent
divide the whole extent of being. For every being is either necessary or
contingent. But things which divide the whole of being do not coincide in any
being; otherwise they would not divide the whole of being.* Reply. 1. Apart
from a certain sophistical sharpness, I venture to say that there was nothing
in that impostor but smoke and words—not even a shadowy knowledge of logic or
metaphysics. Come then, Jacob, please admit whether cause and caused, potency
and act, finite and infinite, do not divide the whole of being. For every
being is either cause or caused, act or potency, finite or infinite; and yet
prime matter, the elements, and almost all beings are a cause with respect to
other beings and caused with respect to the first cause. The elements are
actually elements and mixtures potentially; an embryo is actually an embryo
and potentially a human being. Christ, insofar as he is human, is finite,

[Marginalia: Arminius, reply to article 31, article 5, p. 140.]

[1651 logical printed p. 122 | IA leaf 140 | PDF p. 140]

unless the Sophist, with Arius, would conclude that because finite and
infinite divide the whole of being, Christ cannot be both God and man. 2. The
fact that some things divide the whole of being does not prevent them from
coinciding in the same thing under different respects, because a diversity of
respects unites things most distant in some third thing.

2. Objection. *The contingent and the necessary*, he says, *differ in their
entire essences and complete definitions. For the necessary is that which
cannot fail to be or come about. They are, namely, contradictorily opposed,
and this opposition is infinite.* Reply. 1. Hear these metaphysical trifles.
The relative and correlative differ in their entire essences, and yet Jacob
Arminius was both father and son, teacher and student. Cause and caused, white
and quantified, differ in their whole essences; yet the same thing is both
cause and caused—a cause of this thing, as Polyclitus was of a statue, and
caused by another; a son of his father insofar as he is a generated man; and
the same wall both white and quantified. Even schoolboys know the commonplace
that a diversity of respects removes contradiction when the contradiction is
not *in the same respect, at the same time, and in relation to the same thing*,
as Aristotle says. Jacob, the great metaphysician, maintains the exact
opposite: a contradiction which exists nowhere but in his own brain
overthrows a diversity of respects. 3. He defines the contingent loosely and
carelessly as that which can fail to occur. Yet in that manner the sun, moon,

[Marginalia: The same, ibid., p. 342.]

[Marginalia: What is properly contingent.]

[1651 logical printed p. 123 | IA leaf 141 | PDF p. 141]

plants, and even the most necessary things are contingent. For if God so
willed, they could all fail to be, since he made them all freely. A contingent
is that which, with respect to the natural manner of acting in its cause, can
fail to come about, such as Peter's act of believing; and yet, with respect to
the decree, Peter cannot fail to believe.

3. *The necessity or contingency of the outcome of either thing*, he says,
*must be judged not from one cause but from all the causes joined together.
For if, when ten causes have been posited from which a thing is produced not
necessarily but contingently, one cause is added from which the thing is
necessarily brought about, that thing is immediately said to have come about
not contingently but necessarily.* Reply. 1. But hear what you yourself,
great Gamaliel, taught elsewhere as though from an oracle: *If several causes
concur in producing one effect, that effect indeed has a relation and
connection to each of its causes, but receives its designation only from the
manner in which it exists and is produced from the causes joined together. If
that manner is necessary, it is judged necessary; if contingent, contingent.*
Then I infer from your own principles that an effect contingent from ten
causes and necessary from one cause is partly contingent and partly necessary.
2. Is the movement of the sun not contingent or free in relation to God? For
when God freely so willed, the sun once stood still without motion, while in
relation to the sun its movement is natural. Teach us, then, whether the sun's
movement is necessary or contingent. Here you will be forced to say that it is
contingent under one respect and necessary under another, and thus things
infinitely opposed coincide in the same thing. 3. If those ten causes are
proximate, the whole effect is denominated contingent; and because God decreed

[Marginalia: Arminius, p. 141.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Against Perkins*, p. 710, etc.; Perkins.]

[1651 logical printed p. 124 | IA leaf 142 | PDF p. 142]

to concur with those ten causes, the effect will also be necessary in relation
to God. 4. If God's manner of concurring or acting is partly necessary and
partly free, what sort of thing was the creation of the world? For God
absolutely decreed from eternity to create the world, and yet he created the
world freely. Either the volition of creation arose in God in time and thus is
mutable, or the world's creation will be both free and necessary under
different respects. 5. Peter believes mutably and defectibly, as every second
cause acts; but God brings it about infallibly and immutably that he believes.
Let Arminius see which of us mixes together those contradictorily opposed
things.

4. Objection. *Since God's decree is an internal act of God, it is not the
immediate cause of a thing; rather, whatever God does, he does through power.
Hence nothing is necessary with respect to the decree.* Reply. It does not
follow. Once the decree is posited, the contingent effect will necessarily
come about—not because the decree immediately flows into the effect, but
because in time the decree applies omnipotence to determine the will.

5. Objection. Jacob replies as follows to an objection put to him: if anyone
considers the nature of Christ's bones, they could have been broken; if he
considers God's decree, they could not have been broken. *I reply*, he says,
*by denying that with respect to the divine decree they could not have been
broken. For God did not decree that they could not be broken, but that they
would not be broken. He prevented their breaking not by irresistible power but
by mild persuasion through an argument drawn from its uselessness.*

[1651 logical printed p. 125 | IA leaf 143 | PDF p. 143]

Reply. 1. The reckless Sophist could not have contradicted himself more
loudly. For he grants that God decreed that Christ's bones would not be
broken, and yet, because of the argument from uselessness, the soldiers broke
them contingently. Therefore that breaking was necessary in relation
to God's decree; otherwise God's decree could have been rescinded, while in
relation to the soldiers it was contingent—which is precisely our position.
2. Let him identify anyone among us who asserts that God decreed that Christ's
bones could not be broken. This occurred to him only in dreams. 3. We say that
God decreed that the soldiers would not break the bones, which is what Jacob
says; but I ask whether they could have broken them. Jacob Arminius certainly
acknowledges this when he denies that the soldiers were prevented by God
through irresistible force and maintains that they broke them through
resistible force. For he denies that persuasion determines the will. I ask,
then, how God decreed that the bones would be broken. That decree could
have been rescinded if the soldiers had freely broken them—that is, had broken
them in such a way that they could have refrained. What answer you would give
here, Jacob, I long to know: presumably, that from foreknowledge of the
determination of the soldiers' free choice, God decreed that they would not
break them. Is that so? Then he first foresaw that they were unwilling to
break them and afterwards decreed that they would not be broken. That is, if
the reader wishes to hear trifles, after God foresaw through the window of
middle knowledge that the soldiers would freely refrain from breaking them, he
decreed that they would not be broken by them. Theologians should therefore be
ashamed to adore as a teacher a shallow and uncultivated impostor entirely
ignorant of logic,

[Marginalia: Psalm 34:19.]

[1651 logical printed p. 126 | IA leaf 144 | PDF p. 144]

and to be captivated by him. 4. Like a violent man, he leaves nothing untried
to defend the claim that an effect cannot under different respects be both
contingent or free and necessary. Yet in his reply to article 9, p. 142, he
says: *The world, however, like all other things, is contingent under an
absolute consideration and was produced contingently by God acting freely.
But once it is posited that God wills to establish the world by infinite
power, and posited that God actually employs that power, I shall then say that
the world could not fail to exist from that cause.* There he seems verbally to
acknowledge that the world was created by God both contingently and
necessarily. But I would have the reader observe the cunning of this wretched
petty theologian. He does not say that the world exists necessarily on the
single supposition that God wills by infinite power to establish the world,
but wishes another condition to be posited, namely, that God actually employs
that power. Who doubts that God necessarily establishes the world when he is
actually establishing it? But I ask why, for the necessity of the world's
creation, it is not enough to posit that God wills to establish the world by
his infinite power. If Arminius admits that this suffices, he will be forced
to say that a free act and the absolute decree do not clash head-on, and that
the same act is contingent in itself and necessary in relation to the divine
decree. But perhaps something still more heterodox lies beneath: that God
might have decreed to create the world by his infinite power and yet it need
not be created unless God also actually employs his infinite power. Then

[1651 logical printed p. 127 | IA leaf 145 | PDF p. 145]

perhaps new knowledge and a change of will arise in God; or else he decreed
nothing from eternity, but his volitions are temporal even in effects produced
by God alone, and thus God acts more inconsiderately than human beings.

12. Objection 6. It is a contradiction in terms, he says, for something to
come about contingently—that is, in such a way that it can fail to come
about—and yet for that same thing to be determined toward one side so that
what has been determined by God's absolute decree to occur cannot fail to
occur. Nor does it suffice that freedom remain as regards the creature's
nature. For contingency requires not merely that a potency be present which
can act freely according to its nature, but that the exercise of that potency
and freedom be impeded in no way. What madness it is to grant the potency at
creation and, when it is now to be employed, to take it away! This is to grant
the potency when there is no occasion for its use, but when its use and need
arrive, to impede freedom in the act. He also says the same in his analysis of
Romans 9: freedom requires potency unimpeded *both potentially and actually*.
The Remonstrants in their Confession similarly require not only that the potency
be free but that its exercise also be free and indifferent toward opposites.
Reply. 1. It is no contradiction in terms for something to come about
contingently in relation to the second cause and to be determined in relation
to God's decree, which establishes the eternal futurition

[Marginalia: Reply to article 7, p. 144.]

[Marginalia: p. 700; Remonstrants, *Confession*, ch. 6, thesis 3.]

[1651 logical printed p. 128 | IA leaf 146 | PDF p. 146]

of that act which the will will determinately produce from its own innate
freedom. Nor is it contradictory that, when God's decree is removed, the will
can will this or refuse it, and yet that with respect to
eternal futurition it determinately wills only this and does not refuse it.
For it cannot both will and refuse the same thing, as the Sophist implies,
unless there preceded from eternity both the eternal futurition of the
volition and the eternal nonfuturition of this volition, which is a palpable
contradiction. 2. It is well that Arminius acknowledges that once the absolute
decree is posited the free potency remains. 3. He says that freedom's nature
requires that its exercise not be impeded. But how does the Sophist prove that
the absolute decree impedes the exercise of freedom? Here he certainly becomes
mute; he supplies not even a syllable of proof. You will always see him fall
silent when the time for proofs arrives, as though a gaping mouth or the
brain's empty exertion were enough to overthrow the truth. Nor do the
Remonstrants prove with so much as a word that the free exercise is impeded.
Indeed, we say that when God's decree has been posited, omnipotence eventually
assists rather than impedes freedom. It acts as midwife to freedom, as though
freedom were pregnant with an act to be produced with respect to futurition;
and it concurs with freedom, which is inclined toward this act and not
another, to draw the actualization out of freedom's potency—something
freedom could not draw out by its own powers alone. 4. Here let the reader see
how disingenuously and less than candidly they represent our opinion. For the
will can no more be impeded than compelled. Indeed, for a free will to will
while impeded is at once to will and not will and to will under compulsion,

[1651 logical printed p. 129 | IA leaf 147 | PDF p. 147]

because to impede is to place an obstacle before the will so that it does not
will; it is to cause it to will contrary to its innate inclination and
therefore not to will, since willing includes a vital inclination within its
concept. 5. Arminius says that the exercise of freedom is taken away when it
is needed, and perhaps this means that the act is impeded. But this is false
and absurd, good God, because they imply that the will inclines toward willing
A, B, C, D, while the absolute decree forcibly twists the inclination and
volition of A, B, D and causes the will, impeded and constrained, to will C,
not A, not B, not D. This is their dream, not our doctrine. Indeed, we hold
that the will of itself can will A, B, C, D, and that with respect to eternal
futurition it will most freely will C, not A, not B, not D—even if it is
posited per impossibile that there is no decree in God. But God's decree,
supervening on and antecedent to the volition of C, does not constrain the
will. And it places the will in that vital situation in which, by a most free
choice and a wondrous accord, it is going to will that, and nothing else,
which God by his absolute decree determined should be willed. Conversely, by
his absolute decree God predetermines that, and nothing else, which human
freedom will actually choose. Let these slaves now seek their imaginary
impediment. 6. Arminius, more accurately than he is commonly represented,
wishes potency to be *dynamis*, that is, a free potency. What trifling! A
potency that is a free potency. 7. For the exercise to be indifferent toward
opposites is a contradiction in terms, yet the Remonstrants speak in this way.

[1651 logical printed p. 130 | IA leaf 148 | PDF p. 148]

For free exercise is an act of freedom, and an act of freedom is the actual
determination of the will. But for freedom's determination to be indifferent
toward opposites is for a determination to be determinable and not a
determination, and for white to be black. Furthermore, potency, not an act or
exercise of freedom, is called indifferent toward opposites; for when the
exercise is present, indifference toward opposite acts is removed. But the
Remonstrants in their *Apology*, chapter 6, page 70, say they want no
circumstance to be present to or absent from the will with which the will's
not acting cannot be consistent. This therefore means that the act can
absolutely occur or not occur. Thus they say not only that the potency, but
also its exercise, must be indifferent—that is, not only can free acts be or
not be, but they can also be or not be. For the Sophists do not teach what the
nonimpediment of an act adds beyond the nonimpediment of the potency.

13. Objection 7. From this determination it follows, 1. that God is the author
of sin; 2. that God truly, properly, and alone sins. For once a law forbidding
this act and such a predetermination by which the act cannot fail to be
committed are posited, it follows that God himself is the one who transgresses
the law, since he himself performs the act contrary to the law, while the act
is unavoidable for the human being. Reply. 1. Because God efficiently
determines the positive acts which underlie the sin's unlawfulness, it in no
way follows that he is the author of sin. God's volition concerning sin will
be discussed elsewhere, with God's good help.

[Marginalia: Arminius, ibid.]

[1651 logical printed p. 131 | IA leaf 149 | PDF p. 149]

2. Whoever removes the positive acts in sin from God's efficiency and decree
wrongfully injures providence; nor does it follow that God is the one who
transgresses the law. For he forbade the acts to the creature—but to what
purpose? Let
Arminius show by what law God is forbidden to administer the world, to
determine all beings by an eternal decree according to manner, measure,
number, and circumstances, and to concur as the first cause with the positive
act in sin. Does Arminius wish God to be forbidden by law from letting
creatures live, move, and have their being in him? Acts 17. 3. I ask whether
the positive acts in sin occur by no decree of God, not even a conditional
one, and by no eternal will of God. Yet Arminius says that God wills all those
things *which, from the infinite beings possible to him, he decreed should be
done through the ultimate judgment of wisdom; and he indeed wills to do those
things.* He asserts that God's will is the cause of all things through the
mediation of power, among which things, by his account, contingent acts are
numbered. Hence, according to Arminius, God's will will be the cause of acts
forbidden by law, insofar as they are acts. Therefore, according to Arminius,
God truly, properly, and alone sins, and decreed to sin, since he himself
performs the act contrary to the law. Likewise in disputation 10, thesis 15,
Arminius asserts God's efficiency in concurring without any injustice.
Therefore, I say, God decreed from eternity to concur, and so through a
positive act of his will, by the mediation of power, to effect the sinful act.
But I know that Arminius says that God wills that act by a will

[Marginalia: Arminius, disputation 4, thesis 52.]

[Marginalia: thesis 54, p. 215.]

[Marginalia: Disputation 10, thesis 15, p. 261.]

[Marginalia: Thus Arminius maintains.]

[1651 logical printed p. 132 | IA leaf 150 | PDF p. 150]

that is not absolute but conditional. Come then, Jacob: what is conditional
will? God, foreknowing that the creature freely performs a sinful act, wills
it—that is, after the act exists, he wills it to exist. But a liar ought to
have a good memory. Will God's will, through the mediation of power, be the
cause of contingent acts, as he acknowledged above, only after the contingent
act exists? Then, because God wills it to exist after it already has being,
God's will is its cause. Away with such absurdities, Sophist! 2. See how
Arminius excuses God: God himself truly, properly, and alone wills to perform
an act forbidden by law, not absolutely but with a conditional will—namely,
foreknowing that the creature will perform that act. But to will or approve an
act forbidden by law after it exists is no lesser injustice than to will and
intend it absolutely before it exists. Consequently, this argument will cause
no small harm to Arminian doctrine. 4. The sinful act will be unavoidable for
God; for if such an act comes about by God's conditional will, then it has
being antecedently to his will, and thus God will by fate will an act
forbidden by law. For he cannot cause that what exists does not exist, and he
cannot fail to will that act, because his will cannot fail to be the cause of
all positive acts. Let Arminius free God from fate here. 5. A contingent act
determined by God is in no sense called unavoidable for the creature. It is
done freely by the creature, who freely

[Marginalia: With absolute and indifferent freedom a person sins or does not;

[1651 logical printed p. 133 | IA leaf 151 | PDF p. 151]

wills it and freely refuses another act, according to what is determined by
God's eternal decree. Thus that imaginary unavoidability, if there is any, has
its origin as much from the creature's freedom as from God's decree. 6. By his
absolute dominion, bound by no law, God decrees to effect the positive acts of
freedom as first cause; and creatures subject to the law freely perform the
same acts against the law and the dictate of conscience. Therefore, the
creatures sin here, while God transgresses in no way.

14. Objection 8. Third, Arminius says, it follows that God needed a sinful
human being and sin in order to display the glory of his justice and mercy.
Reply. I waited to hear a proof of the consequence, but Arminius became
silent. He was content to posit rather than prove it, as is customary for him.
But we shall say that God freely declares the glory of his justice and mercy;
he could have refrained from declaring it and does not stand in need of glory
from creatures. 2. Once it is posited that God decreed to display his glory,
he freely decreed to reveal it through the permission of sin—not as one in
need of either sin or a sinner, but as one willing to permit it by a free
decree. But Arminius wishes to teach us that God could declare his glory in no
other way than through sin. Reply. We believe this means of divine wisdom,
which little human beings cannot investigate, to be most fitting on the
hypothesis that God decreed to reveal to creatures the glory of his avenging
justice and sparing mercy. Let them

[1651 logical printed p. 134 | IA leaf 152 | PDF p. 152]

instruct the Spirit of Jehovah; we worship and believe. We leave to God's
infinite wisdom innumerable possibilities by which he could powerfully attain
his ends.

Objection 9. It follows from the absolute decree of contingents that sin is
not sin. Reply. It is easy for Arminius to lay down four words, but it is
equally easy for me to answer that from this decree it follows perfectly that
sin is sin. For there is no necessity here that overthrows freedom.

15. I turn to Suárez. A created will subject to the decree can no more will
anything other than what is determined by that decree than God's decree can
be rescinded. *But it is not enough*, Suárez says, *if we say that the potency
remains once necessity from the supposition has been posited, since under such
a supposition it is lulled to sleep and impeded with respect to all things
except the one which is chosen. It is therefore only a remote potency for
several things, not the proximate potency required for the exercise of
freedom.* Reply. The statement, “the will cannot will anything other than what
God decrees,” must be distinguished. “Cannot” either denotes impotence in the
will, as though it were constrained and fatally twisted by the decree, or else
“cannot” is freedom's elective nonpotency and is the same as “freely cannot”
or “does not will.” For if “cannot” is taken in the broadest sense, whenever
the will wills this, it cannot then fail to will it. But insofar as it is
subject to the decree, the will freely cannot will anything other than what it
wills and what is decreed—not from impotence,

[Marginalia: Arminius, ibid., p. 145.]

[Marginalia: Objection 10; Suárez, *On the Aid of Grace*, part 1, prologue 1,
ch. 6, fol. 27.]

[1651 logical printed p. 135 | IA leaf 153 | PDF p. 153]

as though the decree removed either the judgment of reason, the elective
power, or that commanding inclination of the will. On the contrary, it posits
all these things, because this nonpotency is equally from freedom itself,
although dependently and subordinately, and is principally from God's decree.
These two statements—“the will subject to the decree cannot will anything
other than what was decreed by God,” and “the will cannot will anything other
than what it actually wills”—describe one and the same nonpotency with respect
to object and terminus. For God's decree, by the mediation of power, directs
the will to the same terminus toward which the will itself freely directs
itself. Now the will, freely willing this, cannot fail to will this, and
therefore this nonpotency is free. Thus neither injury to freedom nor injury
to its exercise follows from this “cannot.” But Álvarez says: *For a person
truly to be able to will some object, it is not required that there preceded
from eternity in God an absolute and efficacious decree predetermining his
free choice to will that same object, although this is required for him
actually to will it. Hence, from the fact that there was no decree of this
kind in God, it does not follow that a person cannot will such an object,
speaking absolutely, but only that he does not in fact will it.* From this I
rightly infer that although a person actually and freely wills only that to
which the decree determines freedom through the mediation of power,
nevertheless, whether God had such a decree or not, the will can will the
contrary. For an absolute decree is not required for the *ability to will*, but

[Marginalia: Álvarez, *On the Aids*, bk. 3, question 22.]

[1651 logical printed p. 136 | IA leaf 154 | PDF p. 154]

for the *willing*. Therefore, when the absolute decree is posited, the potency
for willing something else remains, and thus freedom remains, as Aquinas also
noted. 3. It remains under God's decree, Suárez says, but lulled to sleep and
impeded. But what sort of sleep is this—to allege the claim without proving
it, like a Jesuit? Does he think the reader is overcome with sleep? For a
potency which does not freely exercise its power by willing something other
than what it freely wills and what the absolute decree determined that it
should freely will—is this sleep or rather wakefulness? There is no impediment
here, because freedom would certainly be impeded if it did not will this. But
this is the point: according to the Jesuit, it is sleep if freedom is not so
released from providence that, with providence put to sleep, freedom can, with
absolute indifference and with all things required for acting posited, will or
not will. Thus, even when the eternal futurition of the volition and the
eternal nonfuturition of the nolition have been posited, freedom can
nevertheless refuse to will. There would thus be a nolition in time which was
not future from eternity, a position which the Jesuit Vázquez rightly rejects.
But I recall what Suárez says elsewhere: *A potency only in the divided sense*
—the potency, namely, which we grant freedom insofar as it is subject to God's
determination—*cannot be reduced immediately to act. Rather, some disposition
or change must precede by which the potency is rendered proximate, either by
acquiring something necessary for the act or by removing an impediment. And
thus, unless the potency is proximate to acting and not acting, its free
exercise is impeded. Just as the power which a white thing has to become black
is not reduced to act unless*

[Marginalia: Aquinas, part 1, q. 23, art. 6.]

[Marginalia: Vázquez, on I-II, disputation 63, q. 119, fol. 39.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On the Necessity of Grace*, prologue 1, ch. 3, fol. 11.]

[1651 logical printed p. 137 | IA leaf 155 | PDF p. 155]

*the whiteness is removed.* Reply. 1. A will furnished with the habit of grace under
God's decree is in proximate potency for not acting in the divided sense,
namely, if God's decree is removed and the contrary decree posited. For neither
a decree, time, place, nor anything of this sort introduces into the will a new
accession of potency or disposition. An eye in darkness is in this manner in
proximate potency for seeing, since the arrival of light adds to the eye no new
visual potency which it did not have in darkness. 2. The potency of a white
thing to become black is passive potency. But under God's decree the will has
an elective, vital, active potency for not acting, if God's decree is removed
and a contrary decree posited. Nor is the decree an impediment to the contrary
act, both because it is contradictory for the will to be impeded in acting and
because the will cannot then elicit the contrary act, not by impotence, but by
the will's free nonpotency and elective power. 3. Do you wish me to disclose
the Jesuit mystery here? He wishes freedom, when everything required for
acting is present, to be absolutely able to act and not act, so that if it
wills, a decree of willing had preceded in God; if it refuses, as it
absolutely can, a decree of refusing had existed in God. Thus God determined
absolutely nothing whatsoever concerning free acts. Rather, God's will is
suspended today, and from eternity was suspended, as regards approving either
Peter's volition or his nolition. Hence, God's certain and fixed decree
concerning this volition of Peter rather than another

[1651 logical printed p. 138 | IA leaf 156 | PDF p. 156]

or his nolition then arises anew in God when Peter wills. Otherwise the free
potency will not be immediately and proximately indifferent toward acting or
not acting. God's decree is certainly no more the cause of the Chaldeans'
devastation than of their not devastating, and the object of the decree is
something possible. I ask about God's consequence from his decrees, concerning
which Arminius testifies: *The certain futurition of contingent outcomes does
not include necessity.* But what is this other than to say that God certainly
knows nothing and determines nothing by his will concerning future
contingents until they actually occur, and that God's knowledge begins in time,
as do all his volitions? I pass over how absurd it is that a thing's
futurition should not be necessary. For if futurition exists and has been
drawn out of the state of possibility, it is as necessary as it is necessary
that the same be the same and the future be future. Indeed, for the future not
to include necessity is for the future to be able to be nonfuture.

16. Second objection from Suárez. If a supposition so precedes the will that
it is not in the will's power to posit or remove it, and the act necessarily
follows from such a supposition, that act cannot be free. From this the
argument is formed. The absolute decree conflicts with God's justice, because
rewards and punishments are not justly imposed upon those who do not have
