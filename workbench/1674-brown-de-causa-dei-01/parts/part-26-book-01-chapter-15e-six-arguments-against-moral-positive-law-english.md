These are the things which he has here for overthrowing this position. Let us
see what he afterward brings forward to the same end. On pages 18 and 19 he
attempts by six arguments to show that no positive law can at the same time be
moral.

The first is this: “Not only does Scripture not employ these terms, but neither
does the *Defender* employ them in the manner of those who have used them. For
they all take *moral* with reference to acts of the virtues and wish the moral
to pertain to natural right, which therefore admits of no mutability, as we saw
above from the scholastics and from Altenstaig in his *Theological Lexicon*,
and indeed from Ames himself when he did not attend to the hypothesis
concerning the Sabbath. But now, to the contrary, we hear of moral precepts
which depend not upon natural right and the law of truth, but upon a decision,
and which are mutable.” Reply: 1. It is true that Scripture does not use these
terms, and he is all the more blameworthy for raising so great a dispute about
a word which is not scriptural and for wishing to compel us to use the terms
entirely in his own manner, although he cannot prove, either from the force of
the words themselves or from the constant usage of theologians, that this must
necessarily be done. 2. That venerable theologian whom he here adorns with the
title *Defender* had used those terms in the manner of eminent orthodox
theologians, as he himself plainly shows in the *Vindiciae* and elsewhere. 3.
We saw above what the scholastics, Altenstaig, and Ames said. 4. When they all
take *moral* with reference to acts of the virtues, they say nothing against
us. 5. When they wish the moral to pertain to natural right, they do not
contradict us. For by natural right they do not mean only a right natural to
God, but a right natural to us which can be positive to God; otherwise they
would contradict the adversary himself no less than us. 6. Let him explain to
us what “moral precepts which depend upon natural right” means, and what this
“law of truth” is, and how it is opposed to a decision. What? Does he think
that positive precepts depend upon some law of falsehood, or does he think
that God’s decision is a law of falsehood? These things surely need some
explanation, for they sound ill.

What does he say second? “If the moral can be the same as the positive,” he
says, “what will be the difference between the common moral law and the
ceremonial and civil law, since these latter two also concern conduct? They
will certainly also be moral, at least in the common respect, even if not κατ'
ἐξοχὴν, preeminently. Let him identify how they differ from the common moral
law. For in the civil law there is nothing except what concerns the conduct of
the Jewish polity—that is, nothing except the common moral element. In the
ceremonial law likewise there is that moral element, so called in a certain
common respect, and in addition an evangelical reference for prefiguring
Christ, as is said on page 48; that is, an evangelical relationship and a
legal relationship, and thus a moral determination, on page 56. See also page
136. But was that evangelical relationship not just as much from divine
ordination as the legal one? For were the Jews not commanded by divine
ordination just as much to believe, for example, that the Passover was a type
of the Messiah as they were commanded to believe that it must be sacrificed
by them? And since this evangelical relationship concerned conduct just as
much through the precept of obedience, love of God, repentance, faith, and so
forth as the legal relationship did, let him teach us how this evangelical
relationship was not also moral—unless he wishes to say either that the
Israelites did not perceive this relationship, that it was not from divine
ordination, or that they could at least comply with it without good conduct.”
Reply: 1. The Moral Law, properly or preeminently so called, differs in many
ways from the Ceremonial and Civil Law, as is clear from what was said above.
Although the ceremonial and civil law can also be said, speaking broadly, to
concern conduct, the moral law nevertheless differs from them, both because it
concerns conduct peculiar not to this or that people but common to absolutely
every people, and because it concerns perpetual conduct—that is, because it
will have force at every time until the end of the world and will not, like
those laws, cease after some

[1674 logical printed p. 206 | IA leaf 224 | PDF p. 224]

fixed period. 2. The civil law certainly concerned the conduct of the Jewish
polity, but not the conduct of every polity—Greek, Roman, and so forth. 3. That
evangelical relationship which was present in the ceremonial laws was from
divine ordination no less than the legal relationship. By divine ordination
the Jews were bound to believe, for example, that the Passover was a type of
the Messiah, and this by reason of the divine precept. Thus this evangelical
relationship was also moral—or, rather, its observance in accordance with
God’s command was a morally good action—and in this respect it pertained to
the legal relationship. Yet it does not follow from this that there was
nothing in the laws of the Old Testament except what was merely moral, or that
those ceremonial and civil laws were not distinguished from laws properly
called moral, since that common morality which appeared in the ceremonial and
civil laws was peculiar only to the Jews and was to last only for a time,
whereas the morality of the moral precepts was, on the contrary, of an
entirely different nature. Since, however, our colleague points to certain
passages of the *Vindiciae* and bids us look at them, let us oblige him, and we
will easily perceive whether that most celebrated theologian said anything
absurd. On page 48 he has this: “All God’s precepts are moral in a certain
common respect, insofar as they must be observed for the moral goodness of
actions, of which they are the standard. Meanwhile the typical ceremonial
precepts, which are also often called ceremonial briefly and preeminently,
included an evangelical reference for prefiguring Christ as one who was to
come, and therefore were instituted only until the time of διορθώσεως,
reformation (Hebrews 9:10). Similarly, the civil laws of the Old Testament had
a special reference to that Israelite polity in the land of Canaan, and to
that extent they also truly bound consciences in their own times. But those
which are called moral preeminently and are thus distinguished from the
others are prescribed universally by God in such a way that they do not have
such restriction, limitation, or abrogation. Accordingly, they also existed
from the beginning and endure through all ages until the consummation. Christ
says expressly of a certain civil constitution in Matthew 19:8, ‘From the
beginning it was not so.’ But of things called moral preeminently, he says in
Matthew 5:18, ‘Until heaven and earth pass away, not one jot or one apex will
pass from the law until all things have been accomplished.’” What, I ask,
appears here which is either absurd or opposes the commonly received
distinction of laws into Moral, Ceremonial, and Civil? These are the things he
has on page 56: “The properly moral arises from God’s precept, whether it flows
immediately from his most holy nature or through the intervention of a free
determination of the will toward this or that part in the category of conduct.
The ground of the precept is the true moral ground, and things subordinated on
account of the precept ought to be referred to their moral end. 2. The
ceremonial things themselves, such as circumcision, sacrifices, and so forth,
besides an evangelical relationship, by which they were ‘shadows of things to
come, the body [being] Christ’ (σκιαὶ τῶν μελλόντων, σώματος τὸ χριστόν), also
had a legal relationship and thus a moral determination, so that they had to
be performed according to the divine precept and possessed moral goodness
from conformity to it but moral evil from nonconformity. This was thus a
certain common ground of morality. But because their evangelical relationship
itself introduced a determination to a fixed terminus, to the time of
διορθώσεως, reformation, and fulfillment, and because, accordingly, they were
not commanded from the beginning of the creation of human beings, nor all at
once, nor to all nations, nor especially to those who live under the economy
of the New Testament, they are therefore distinguished from things called
moral preeminently.” What appears here which requires any explanation or can
be an offense to anyone? The things he has on page 136 are entirely similar:
“But,” he says, “several kinds of acts of instituted worship under the Old
Testament had a twofold relationship: the former typical and evangelical,
insofar as they prefigured the Messiah who was to come and”

[1674 logical printed p. 207 | IA leaf 225 | PDF p. 225]

“his offices and benefits; the latter legal, from God’s command, which imposed
the necessity of a precept. To that extent there was also moral goodness in
this observance, but moral sin in its omission, and accordingly that practice
was thus also moral, although not moral preeminently, because it was not
prescribed universally but only until the time of διορθώσεως, reformation.”
These things have thus been sufficiently explained.

His third argument follows: “Since all philosophers and theologians who have
written up to this point about human actions oppose voluntary actions to
natural ones, and the former are in turn divided into various differences—
moral or indifferent, theoretical or practical, and so forth—here all
voluntary actions are moral, and thus nothing is opposed to the moral except
what is natural and automatic, such as the digestion of food and drink, and so
forth.” Reply: We agree entirely with the philosophers and theologians. For we
oppose Voluntary actions to Natural ones and divide Voluntary actions into
Moral and Indifferent, and into Theoretical and Practical. Where, then, is the
difference? Is it because we say that actions which, considered specifically,
are indifferent become Moral in the individual instance, namely when they are
clothed with their circumstances? Do not all the sounder philosophers and
theologians also teach this? But, he says, we oppose nothing to the Moral
except what is Natural. So it is, when *moral* is taken broadly for what lies
in the category of conduct. And what philosopher or theologian does not
acknowledge that certain things are considered in the category of being and
others in the category of conduct, and that the latter can be called Moral and
the former Natural? “God constituted some things,” says the most distinguished
Essenius in the *Vindiciae*, pages 38–39, “in the category of being, for the
condition of nature: for example, that besides a rational soul the human being
should have a body formed in this way, with entrails and limbs arranged in
this way, so many fingers on the hand, and so forth; that the digestion of
food should be carried out in this way in the stomach, liver, and heart, and
so forth; that the sun, moon, and other stars should illuminate these earthly
things in this way and influence them in this way, so that there should be
such seasons and changes of the year, and so forth. Properly speaking, there
is no moral evil in these things. Conduct has another consideration, according
to 1 Corinthians 15:33: ‘Evil associations corrupt good conduct’; Hebrews
13:5: ‘Let your conduct be free from the love of money’; and Acts 13:18: ‘He
bore their conduct in the wilderness.’” These things are sufficiently distinct
and clear and agree with the statements of philosophers and theologians,
although our adversary regards them as unheard-of. But what do these things
conclude against what is now disputed? Does this argument prove by a good
consequence that no precept which is positive to God and flows from his free
good pleasure can be called natural or moral to us? Or that no precept at all
can be given which may be called moral-positive? Let him show this if he can.

His fourth argument is this: “And since, according to Augustine, all doctrine
is about things or signs (*On Christian Doctrine*, book 1, chapter 2), and
according to theologians all doctrine of faith and morals is distinct from
the doctrine of the sacraments, according to our brother even the sacraments,
which are signs, are as such”—lest anyone quibble that they also contain the
things which are the subject of the signs—“moral as well, and thus pertain to
conduct or observance. By the very fact that they contain a precept of a sign,
they will therefore contain a precept concerning conduct.” Reply: This
argument does nothing to weaken our assertion. Does he think that we do not
distinguish the doctrine of faith and morals from the doctrine of the
Sacraments because we say that the use of the Sacraments is constituted not in
the category of being but in the category of conduct, and is therefore morally
good or evil according as it conforms or fails to conform to the law? What?
Does he not know that these and other theological expressions of this sort are
not always taken in the same sense, but are used variously to denote different
things, and are

[1674 logical printed p. 208 | IA leaf 226 | PDF p. 226]

sometimes taken broadly and sometimes strictly? If he is not ignorant of this, why does he
dissemble?

Fifth, he argues as follows: “By a marvelous contrivance universally
prescribed moral-positive things are said to flow from nature in the beginning
and from the image of God. For if they are inscribed upon nature, they do not
depend upon a positive but upon a natural precept. For whatever is inscribed
upon nature is natural (Romans 2:14–15). And how can it be positive? Since for
Paul these are different things: not to have the law and yet to have the work
of the law inscribed upon the heart. Therefore such people can lack a positive
precept, but not a natural one inscribed upon the heart. How, then, are these
the same things? Further, is the image of God positive, and is whatever flows
from it imposed by a positive precept?” Reply: Ignorance produces confusion
and empty loquacity. Either he does not grasp, or does not wish to grasp, what
was said by his most distinguished colleague, and hence we have arguments
heaped up without end which nevertheless establish nothing. 2. Things which
are positive to God—that is, which flow from his free good pleasure—can be
inscribed upon our nature and therefore be natural to us and pertain to the
Law of Nature. 3. What does this mean: “If they are inscribed upon nature,
they depend upon a natural precept”? This expression does not seem altogether
precise. 4. It is true that whatever is inscribed upon nature is Natural to us,
but it is not therefore natural to God himself, to whom it can be positive. 5.
These are indeed different things for Paul: “not to have the Law” and yet “to
have the work of the Law inscribed upon the heart.” But here he does not oppose
the positive Law to the Moral Law. Rather, to those remnants of natural light
which are still inscribed upon human hearts he opposes the written Law of God,
which contained not only God’s positive precepts but also all moral precepts
and explained the natural ones distinctly and fully. 6. It is true that those
who have a natural precept inscribed upon the heart can lack a positive
precept. But what follows from this? 7. He asks how these can be the same
things. Certainly, the things which they lack and the things which they have
inscribed upon their hearts cannot, as such, be the same. But it no more
follows from this that nothing positive can be moral than that the moral
cannot be moral. For they did not have all moral things inscribed upon the
heart, but were ignorant of some, as we heard above and as the thing itself
declares. 8. Something which is positive to God and does not necessarily flow
from his nature and attributes can pertain to the image of God insofar as it
is in human beings. “Indeed, on the contrary,” says the most distinguished
Essenius in the *Vindiciae*, page 39, “as the image of God in the human being
comprehends spiritual Wisdom and true Justice and Holiness, so the human
being, adorned with God’s image, by wisdom also acknowledges that duty
prescribed by God; by justice renders it to God as rightfully due; and by
holiness refers it wholly to God’s glory. It is no obstacle that this
constitution depends upon God’s free command, as do several others which God
nevertheless indicates and causes to be observed where he forms and reforms
his image.”

Sixth and finally, he argues against this by saying: “I would like to see this
made digestible, when he says that the image of God in the human being
comprehends not only those things which necessarily flow from the Divine
nature, but also those which depend upon the divine decree and upon the free
determination of the divine will. Therefore everything which God has decreed
and commanded from mere good pleasure is his image. Accordingly, so are the
precept concerning the tree, concerning foods and rites—and what is not? For
nothing is commanded apart from God’s decree, therefore nothing is apart from
God’s image. Does whatever God knows by his decree concerning our actions,
even indifferent ones, therefore depend upon his image? Is there a likeness of
God in all these things? Oh, what kind of theology is this?” Reply: It will be
enough here to repeat the words of the most distinguished Essenius which Dr.
Burmann attempts to refute by this reply. “But the things,” he says in the
passage cited above on page 42, “which are prescribed universally”

[1674 logical printed p. 209 | IA leaf 227 | PDF p. 227]

“and not individually to the polity of the Old Testament, nor merely until the
time of reformation, were also inscribed by God upon human nature and
conscience at the beginning, and are now inscribed anew through sanctification.
Thus they also pertain to the image of God in human beings. For the idea of
such wisdom, justice, and holiness existed in God from eternity; the expressed
copy of that idea in the human being is the image of God in him. But God’s own
wisdom concerns not only nature but also his decree; similarly, God’s own
justice and holiness also pertain to the things which he has decreed. What
wonder is it, then, if the image of God in the human being comprehends not only
those things which necessarily flow from the divine nature but also those
which depend upon the divine decree, insofar, that is, as it pleased God to
extend this likeness?” Here we see that he does not extend these things to all
God’s decrees and precepts, but only to those which were divinely inscribed
upon human nature and conscience at the beginning. For it pleased God to
extend this likeness so far and no farther. 2. The adversary himself will not
say that obeying God in all things, even in foods and rites, forms no part of
God’s image in us. Otherwise contumacy, ἀνομία, lawlessness, and disobedience,
which are the image of the devil, will be the image of God. Oh, what kind of
theology will this be?

Afterward, on page 41, he further answers these things in this manner: “Adam,
then, created for that knowledge of God, knew from the image of God not only
the things which flowed from God’s nature but also those which pertained to
his decree. And since the image of God was natural to the human being, while
the things which depend upon God’s decree are properly positive and arbitrary,
the human being nevertheless knew God’s arbitrary Precepts from his own
nature.” Reply: Adam, created for the wisdom of God, doubtless knew not only
the things which flowed from God’s nature, but also those which flowed from
God’s free good pleasure and whose knowledge it pleased God to inscribe upon
his heart. 2. The image of God was indeed natural to Adam with respect to that
natural condition, and by a nature furnished in this manner he knew as many
positive and arbitrary things as it seemed good to God to manifest to him or
inscribe upon his heart. And what absurdity follows from this?

“The image of God,” he continues, “consisted in rectitude (Ecclesiastes 7:29),
and, as regards its wisdom, the Apostle calls it ὁσιότητα τῆς ἀληθείας,
holiness of truth (Ephesians 4:24), to which he immediately opposes falsehood,
τὸ ψεῦδος, in verse 25. Thus ὁσιότης τῆς ἀληθείας is the pursuit of holiness,
or holiness according to the truth—that truth, namely, whose knowledge was necessary for
rendering to God and neighbor what was due. In this way holiness of truth is
a good will or love of truth, and a love of what truth teaches must be loved,
together with action consonant with that love. What does knowledge of things
which depend upon God’s decree have in common with this holiness of truth? Is
there a likeness of God in such things? When God himself decrees such things,
he does not do it from his image and holiness, but from his good pleasure and
free will; nor is knowing such things the image of God, as is evident in the
devils.” Reply: 1. That rectitude in which the image of God consisted and for
which Adam was created demanded conformity in mind, speech, and action with
God’s will—that is, with the rule of rectitude which God proposed—whether it
flowed from God’s nature or from his free good pleasure. 2. Let us admit this
gloss upon the Apostle’s words in Ephesians 4:24, although Beza says that they
signify “true holiness,” that is, not counterfeit in the least but genuine and
sincere, according to the Hebrew idiom. What can be inferred from it? He
certainly asks what knowledge of things which depend upon God’s decree has in
common with this holiness of truth. Although knowledge of all things which
depend upon God’s decree has nothing in common with this holiness of truth,
nevertheless knowledge of those things which were necessary for

[1674 logical printed p. 210 | IA leaf 228 | PDF p. 228]

rendering to God and neighbor what was due according to God’s will did have
something in common with that holiness of truth. 3. Certainly, in all those
things which, although they depend upon God’s free good pleasure, are
nevertheless proposed to human beings for imitation by the supreme lawgiver
and form part of a constant rule which will endure through all ages, there is
some likeness of God. 4. It is true that God, in decreeing such things, does
not act from his holiness but from his good pleasure and free will. But what
will follow from this? Will the adversary himself say that God gave all the
precepts of the second table from holiness and not from good pleasure? 5. The
devil knows not only the things which depend upon God’s free will, but also
other things which are most moral, so to speak. Therefore even knowing these
things will not be the image of God. To what purpose, then, do these things
tend? Or what can be concluded from them? The things which follow do not touch
us, because in this matter we have nothing in common with Socinus, nor is it
necessary for us to have anything in common with him.

He has similar things on page 39. “An act,” he says, “which agrees with God’s
precept—unless that precept concerns an act good in itself—is never thereby
morally good, that is, naturally and immutably good. For good cannot be evil,
nor evil good. But what is commanded as a δόγμα, an ordinance, by a mere
precept can be forbidden, and thus would become evil from good. These things
are absurd.” Reply: 1. What does he mean by an act good in itself? He does not
mean an act which has some fundamental congruity with human nature before the
command, for he mocks this elsewhere. Does he mean a morally good act? Then
the sense is that an act agreeing with God’s precept is not thereby morally
good unless it was morally good before the precept. What, then, does that act
possess through its agreement with God’s precept? Absolutely nothing? This is
surely a new theology. 2. He wishes *morally* to be the same as *naturally* and
*immutably*, and this antecedently to every precept of God. Then, it seems, God
can make nothing morally—that is, naturally and immutably—good by a precept,
whether natural or positive. Therefore no moral goodness depends upon God’s
precept. 3. It is true that good can never be evil, nor evil good, but the same
moral action can become evil from being good or good from being evil by God’s
mere precept. For the action of sacrificing Isaac was morally evil; yet when
the divine precept was posited, the same action was good. It is therefore clear
that it is not necessarily required in every moral action that it be
immutably such with respect to God. 4. Accordingly, it is by no means absurd
to say that an act good by a mere precept can become evil when the precept
ceases.

“It pertains to obedience to a δόγμα, an ordinance,” he says, “not to glory in
it as in a good act. Although obeying—that is, acknowledging and loving the
lordship and power of the lord—is good, nevertheless, as was said above, one
who thinks that an indifferent thing, when commanded, is good and contributes
to life does not obey God but denies God, because God is not one who delights
in indifferent things.” Reply: 1. Whoever glories ought to glory in God alone
(1 Corinthians 1:31). And when someone complies with a divine ordinance, he
ought to regard that work as good in the category of conduct and not morally
evil. 2. If it is good to obey and acknowledge the lordship and power of the
Lord, will it also be good to do the same here and now—that is, in this or that
particular commanded action? And if so, will that particular action in which I
comply with God be good? 3. Anyone who thinks that an action good in itself,
much less an indifferent one, contributes to life now in our corrupt condition
does not obey God but denies him, if he is speaking here about our
justification. And if he is speaking about sanctification, this is no more

[1674 logical printed p. 211 | IA leaf 229 | PDF p. 229 | ends before Hîc etiam examinandum venit]

true of indifferent things which fall under a precept than of other things. 4.
Although God does not delight in indifferent things as such, just as the human
being ought not to delight in them as such, he nevertheless delights in the
obedience which shines forth in indifferent things when they are commanded.
And in the other acts which he himself calls moral, God delights only insofar
as the obedience which is due shines forth in them.

“The goodness of an act,” he continues, “does not depend simply upon a precept.
For by it only a δόγμα, an ordinance, is made a decision; rather, goodness
depends upon truth and justice. Thus it is ὅσιον, holy, to love τὴν ἀλήθειαν,
the truth, and to hate τὸ ψεῦδος, falsehood, and both are δίκαιον, just, because
they are ὀφειλόμενον, something owed, by reason of the human being. And by
reason of God, he cannot ἀπαρνεῖσθαι ἑαυτὸν κἂν κρίμα ἑαυτοῦ, deny himself and
his own judgment.” Reply: 1. Moral goodness depends upon the precept, as has
often been said and proved. 2. Is the decision which is made solely by the
precept morally evil, morally good, or finally neither morally good nor evil
but indifferent? It will not be morally evil. If it is good, what we wish is
conceded. If it is only indifferent, then God’s precept cannot oblige us to
perform this ordinance and omit that one, because, notwithstanding the
precept, all things remain in the state of indifference and thus the things
which are commanded and prohibited can licitly be done or not done, omitted or
not omitted. For this is the common doctrine concerning indifferent things. 3.
He does not clearly prove to us that the moral goodness of an act depends upon
truth and justice. For it is holy to love truth and hate falsehood because the
former is commanded and the latter forbidden, and both are just because they
are due by reason of the command. 4. God certainly cannot deny either himself
or his own judgment, and therefore he must judge that what he commands is good
in the category of conduct and that what he prohibits is evil.

“By the law,” he says, “of the Precepts of which it is said, ‘The one who does
them will live in them,’ those things are indicated which rest upon truth and
justice, and things conforming to such a precept are morally good. But things
conforming to a precept which proceeds from dominical power are only δόγματα,
ordinances, and are, as it were, dogmatically good, not morally good; that is,
they are good in the category of ordinances, but not in the category of
conduct. In other words, although they are performed well or badly insofar as
they are performed with a good disposition of mind, in faith and love, or
without these, nevertheless they are not morally good or evil—that is, they
are not actions which commend us to God or make us unlike him.” Reply: 1. Will
he say that the law of ordinances indicates things which rest upon falsehood
and injustice? 2. We heard above this distinction between the precepts of
which it is said, “The one who does them will live in them,” and ordinances,
as well as his explanation of it. We will immediately see what more he says
about it, but we have not yet heard a solid confirmation. 3. What does
“dogmatically good,” and so forth, mean? He faults us, though undeservedly, for
coining new terms or new explanations of customary terms. And behold a term
which, so far as I know, was never heard before this: “Who could endure the
Gracchi,” and so forth. 4. It is surely an evil consequence that an action
commanded by God and duly performed with a good disposition of mind, in faith
and love, should not be called morally good. 5. Let him explain how actions
which are doubtless moral, or morally good, commend us to God, and perhaps we
will see that at least some things which depend upon God’s free good pleasure
can be numbered among them.
