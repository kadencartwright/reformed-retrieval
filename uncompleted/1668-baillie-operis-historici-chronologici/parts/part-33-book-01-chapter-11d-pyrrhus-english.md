We have said how easily Pyrrhus wrested the Macedonian kingdom from Demetrius
and how easily Lysimachus in turn expelled him from it. After the Macedonian
people’s fickleness caused him to lose the kingdom, he sat anxiously in Epirus
considering what enterprise he should next take in hand. He was a man of the
most restless spirit, always devising new plans before he had completed those
already before him. Then behold, envoys arrived from Tarentum inviting him
into Italy to protect them, the Bruttians, and the Lucanians against the
Romans. Until that day the Romans had never set foot outside Italy. All their
wars had been waged only with their neighbors, and through Pyrrhus they first
experienced the arms of a foreign enemy. Once they had successfully overcome
these, they dared to raise their eyes to Sicily, Sardinia, and Africa, then
soon to Spain and Asia, and thus to the whole world.

> Marginalia, printed p. 212: *Pyrrhus is summoned to Italy by the Tarentines*.

The Tarentines’ embassy was most welcome to Pyrrhus. From it this man of the
greatest ambition immediately conceived the hope not only of subduing Italy,
but also of extending the Epirotes’ frontiers as widely through the West as
his kinsman Alexander had expanded Macedonian dominion in the East. He knew
well how greatly his arms frightened all his neighbors and how gladly they
would see him far removed from their borders. Therefore, to free themselves
more quickly from his dangerous proximity, they all granted his request for
ample assistance in the distant war against Rome.

> journey*.

After his father’s murder, Antiochus governed all his affairs in Asia
sluggishly and effeminately. Philetaerus, the eunuch who guarded Lysimachus’s
treasures at Pergamum, founded by both skill and force a kingdom that passed
to his brother Eumenes, then to Attalus and other descendants for many years.
A new kingdom in Bithynia and another in Pontus were also established in
Antiochus’s days; according to Ammianus Marcellinus, Arsaces too began the
long-lasting and powerful Parthian empire during his reign. Pyrrhus asked
Antiochus for money against the Romans.

> Marginalia, printed p. 212: *Antiochus Soter, a sluggish man*.

Antigonus Gonatas—so called from Goni, the Thessalian city in which he had
been raised—was Demetrius’s son and a most humane and fortunate prince. He
then possessed a great part of Greece and later Macedonia as well, which he
transmitted to his descendants. From him Pyrrhus requested ships.

His principal assistance, however, came from Ptolemy Ceraunus, who feared him
above all. After murdering Seleucus, Ceraunus immediately occupied Thrace,
Macedonia, and all Lysimachus’s dominion. Although Ceraunus had wickedly
murdered his father, Antiochus, distracted by various wars throughout his
vast Asian kingdom, granted him peace without difficulty. Since his brother
Ptolemy enjoyed the Egyptian kingdom in quiet, he did not trouble Ceraunus in
Thrace. Ceraunus defeated Antigonus Gonatas in battle and compelled him to
make peace when Antigonus sought Macedonia, his paternal kingdom—for he was
the son not only of Demetrius but also of Antipater’s daughter Phila. To keep
Pyrrhus as a friend, however, Ceraunus sent him an excellent reinforcement of
five thousand infantry, four thousand cavalry, and forty elephants, and also
gave him a daughter in marriage. When Pyrrhus departed for Italy, he entrusted
his fifteen-year-old son Ptolemy and the kingdom of Epirus to Ceraunus, now
his father-in-law. But the divine power did not long leave Ceraunus’s crimes
unpunished.

Ceraunus’s sister Arsinoe, Lysimachus’s widow, was the mother of two sons,
sixteen-year-old Ptolemy and thirteen-year-old Alexander. Guarding against her
brother, she lived with them in the very strongly fortified city of
Cassandria. Ceraunus pretended to feel the greatest love for Arsinoe and her
children and asked her to surrender herself to him as his wife and them as
his wards. Arsinoe long refused both requests, but at last his many solemn

Capellus, ordinarily a most keen-scented man, falls here into two errors. On
page 470 he makes Lysandra the wife of Lysimachus, though she was the wife of
his son Agathocles. On page 473 he claims that Ceraunus, who so cruelly mocked
his sister Arsinoe, was Philadelphus’s younger brother by Berenice and was
sent by Philadelphus into Greece to act as guardian of Lysimachus’s sons. Yet
he was actually the elder brother, king of Macedonia, and Seleucus’s murderer.
For these gross confusions of names Capellus cites Justin and Pausanias. I have
not yet examined Pausanias, but Justin openly contradicts Capellus and
supports the common opinion, which is also ours.

Covered with so many crimes, Ceraunus did not long escape the hands of divine
justice. Several hundred thousand of the strongest young men had poured out
of Gaul. One body moved into Italy and captured Rome apart from the Capitol;
others passed into Illyria and from there into Macedonia. They offered
Ceraunus peace for a fixed price, but he rejected the barbarians with
excessive pride and rushed headlong into battle. There he was killed with
almost all his forces, and the Gauls carried his head about upon a spear in
mockery. His brother Meleager succeeded to the Macedonian kingdom and tried to
raise an army, but he too was killed a few days later, and all Macedonia was
most wretchedly devastated by the Gauls. A certain private man named Sosthenes
several times drove the Gauls from Macedonia, but he too was soon overwhelmed
and left the Macedonians beneath their feet. They were finally rescued from
that servitude with great difficulty in the following manner.

Brennus set out with one hundred fifty thousand infantry and sixty thousand
cavalry to plunder Apollo’s treasures. There, amid thunderbolts, earthquakes,
and extremely difficult terrain, the defenders destroyed most of his forces.
Tormented by the pain of his wounds, Brennus killed himself with his own
sword. Once these immense Gallic forces had perished and another body of them
had crossed into Asia with Lutharius, Antigonus Gonatas expelled the rest from
Macedonia with little trouble and occupied the now-vacant kingdom without a
rival.

Meanwhile Pyrrhus was waging hard wars with the Romans. As he crossed from
Epirus to Tarentum, a foul storm battered his fleet severely; he threw himself
into the sea and only with the greatest difficulty reached land. As soon as he
arrived at Tarentum, the consul Laevinus came to fight him at once, before the
auxiliary troops from the Lucanians, Bruttians, and others could join Pyrrhus.
The great-hearted man hated to decline a battle, and so he met the Romans with
his own troops alone while his allies delayed and waited to see the battle’s
outcome. Both sides fought very fiercely, with victory inclining to neither
for a long while. At last the elephants—then brought into Italy for the first
time—were sent against the Romans’ front line. Their strange appearance,
enormous strength, and odor, which the unaccustomed horses could not endure,
trampled everything down. Pyrrhus thus obtained the victory and the Roman
camp. Yet, marveling that all the fallen enemy had wounds upon their fronts
and had fallen facing forward, he buried the dead with honor, released the
captives without ransom, and sent envoys to the Senate with very generous
terms of peace. He asked only that the Tarentines be permitted to live in
liberty and peace. The Senate was inclining to Pyrrhus’s terms when Appius
Claudius Caecus, who for many years had remained hidden at home because of age
and infirmity, was carried into the senate house. In a long and vehement speech
he dissuaded them from every peace with Pyrrhus so long as Pyrrhus remained
under arms in Italy. Peace was therefore refused. Pyrrhus, now joined by many
Italian allies, prepared for a second battle. He won this too, but with such
slaughter among his own men that he declared a third victory would utterly
ruin his affairs.

Meanwhile envoys arrived from the Sicilians, begging for help against the
Carthaginians. Pyrrhus gladly seized the opportunity to extricate himself
from the Roman war on an honorable pretext. He left a garrison at Tarentum
and crossed into Sicily, where he defeated the Carthaginians in various
battles and drove them from the island. Yet his arrogance and cruelty toward
the Sicilians became so great that Syracuse, Agrigentum, and most of the other
cities rose against him. While this rebellion vexed him, messengers arrived
from the Tarentines and the other Italians urging his return to Italy, so that
he might rescue his allies from their imminent defeat by the Romans. When he
reached Italy, the consul Marcus Curius Dentatus routed him in a third battle.

> Marginalia, printed p. 213: *Pyrrhus drives the Carthaginians from Sicily*.

In an elegant problem Titus Livy considers what Alexander the Great’s fortune
would have been if he had turned his arms against Italy, either before or
after subduing the East. With excessive partiality toward his native country,
he flatters the Romans and concludes that Alexander would have been defeated
in Italy no less than Pyrrhus. Raleigh refutes Livy’s vanity here soundly
enough. In a later age, when the Romans were much better prepared for war than
before and stronger through various reinforcements, Pyrrhus and Hannibal all
but destroyed them. How, then, could they have withstood the assault of
Alexander, who without controversy far surpassed both Hannibal and Pyrrhus in
skill, courage, and military fortune, and who commanded an army far superior
to either Pyrrhus’s or Hannibal’s forces? Yet Raleigh himself plunges into far
greater flattery than that which he censures in Livy. For who can endure the
unbridled vanity of a man who in military affairs prefers his own Englishmen

according to Raleigh, were superior to the Romans and Macedonians and thus to
all mortals? But these comparisons of nations are utterly vain. Those who in
Alexander’s day were the strongest people on earth were, half a century later,
defeated with little trouble by any who attacked them—the Athenians,
Thracians, Epirotes, Gauls, and others. For many centuries now neither Greeks
nor Romans have raised any dispute with their neighbors over the privilege of
courage. Every generation so easily declines from its fathers’ praises that
the proud boasting of any one nation above another about any virtue is
unworthy of serious men and ought to be restrained. But let us proceed with
the history.

As soon as Pyrrhus returned to Epirus with his defeated forces, he immediately
turned his mind to invading Macedonia. In this enterprise he found success
beyond his hopes and expelled Antigonus Gonatas from the whole kingdom with
very little effort. From this he raised his hopes to subduing all Greece and
Asia, but fate soon brought the tale of that vast ambition to an end. The
Spartan kings Areus and Cleonymus were at odds, and Areus was away with an
army in Crete. Cleonymus therefore invited Pyrrhus to seize the city. Pyrrhus
rushed there with twenty-five thousand infantry, two thousand cavalry, and
twenty-four elephants—equipment ample enough under his leadership to subdue
all Greece, considering the condition of the times. But behold, when he came
to Sparta, while its young men were absent in Crete, the women together with
the old men and a few of the young defended the unwalled city so vigorously
against Pyrrhus’s entire assault that the slaughter of many of his men forced
him to withdraw.

Departing from there, he was summoned to Argos, a city divided into factions.
He approached by night with all his troops. His friends opened a gate, and he
sent in as many cavalry and infantry as he thought sufficient to master the
city. The elephants could not fit through the gate until the towers were
removed from their backs. While the towers were taken down and then replaced
upon the elephants with some commotion, the townsmen awoke and the cry to arms
was raised. Pyrrhus’s soldiers, ignorant of the terrain, were pressed from
every side by the townsmen in the darkness, and the king recalled them to the
gate. But an elephant fell in the gate and caused such confusion that they
could neither advance nor retreat. Pyrrhus himself fought most courageously
in the marketplace. As he attempted to cut down one of the citizens, the
man’s mother, a poor old woman, threw a large tile from a building’s roof.
Striking Pyrrhus’s helmet, it broke the vertebrae of his neck. After he fell
to the ground with this mortal wound, a common soldier cut off his head.
When Alcyoneus threw it at the feet of his father Antigonus Gonatas, the
moderate king rebuked his son, covered the head with his own cloak, and sent
it with the body to Epirus for honorable burial. He immediately recovered
uncontested possession of Macedonia and most of Greece. After Pyrrhus’s death,
Antigonus lived in peace to the age of eighty, the forty-fourth year of his
reign in Greece and the thirty-fourth in Macedonia; his son Demetrius
succeeded to his dominions.
