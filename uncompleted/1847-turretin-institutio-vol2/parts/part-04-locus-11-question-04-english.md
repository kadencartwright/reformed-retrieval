## QUESTION IV

Has anything been added to the moral law by way of counsel?

It is denied, against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: What the evangelical counsels are. The opinion of the
Roman Catholics.]

I. This question, which is disputed between us and the Roman Catholics,
differs from the preceding one. There the dispute concerned *precepts*; here
it concerns *counsels*. They call counsels “good works not imposed upon us by
Christ but pointed out, not commanded but commended,” as Bellarmine
defines them in book 2 of *On Monks*, chapter 7. They call them *evangelical*
and *counsels of perfection* because they are proposed and recommended in the
gospel as in a more perfect law, and because they lead all who bind themselves
by a special vow to observe them to a greater perfection of holiness. Thus,
according to the Roman Catholics, counsels are distinguished from *precepts*:
1. On the part of their *matter*, which in counsels is far more difficult and
perfect than in

[Printed p. 27 | Internet Archive PDF 55 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

precepts. 2. On the part of their *subject*, because counsels pertain only to
a particular order of men, but precepts to everyone universally. 3. On the
part of their *form*, or observance, which is optional in counsels but
necessary in precepts. 4. On the part of their *end and effect*, because the
observance of counsels obtains a greater and more excellent reward—namely,
aureoles and a more sublime degree of happiness—than does the observance of
precepts. Although they do not agree about the number, some maintaining that
there are more and others fewer, nevertheless there are three chief counsels
upon which they erect the monastic life: *continence, obedience, and poverty*.
These correspond to the three vows imposed upon monks: *perpetual continence,
regular obedience, and voluntary poverty*.

[Marginal heading: The position of the orthodox.]

II. The orthodox do not deny that Scripture allows certain indifferent works
to each person's discretion, and that with respect to them counsels can be
given in the Word of God that are properly distinguished from precepts that
must necessarily be observed. Such is Paul's counsel about giving or not
giving a virgin in marriage (1 Cor. 7:26, 36) and about Timothy's use of wine
(1 Tim. 5:14 [sic]). Yet such things are not pleasing to God in themselves,
incur no
punishment if neglected, and merit no reward if observed. The orthodox deny,
however, that there are such counsels concerning moral matters, since these
fall under a precept properly so called, not under counsel. Again, they
acknowledge that different kinds of precepts can be given. Some are
*universal* and bind all people without exception, such as the precept to love
God and one's neighbor. Others are *special* and apply to particular persons
in a particular condition and kind of life, such as the duties of a
magistrate, father, husband, and the like. Others are *private* and imposed
upon certain individual persons, such as the precept given to Noah to build
the ark and to Abraham to take up his son. But the orthodox deny that any of
these fall under counsel, since they are expressly commanded.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

III. The question therefore comes down to this: Has anything been added in the
gospel not by way of precept, but by way of counsel; not concerning
indifferent things, but concerning moral things; not with respect to everyone,
but only to certain people; and not merely for the perfection of the Christian
life, but to obtain a certain more perfect state and establish merits of
supererogation? Our adversaries affirm; we deny.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that there are no evangelical counsels.]

IV. The reasons are: 1. No morally good work exists that is not contained in
the moral law and therefore does not fall under a precept. For the moral law
is the most absolute rule of all rectitude and perfection, and nothing can
pertain to the moral perfection of a person, whatever its degree, that is not
included in love for God and one's neighbor. 2. Whatever falls under counsel
in moral matters also falls under a precept. For by the very fact that God
counsels something, he commands it. Hence in Proverbs 1:30; Luke 7:30; Acts
20:27; and Revelation 3:18, *counsel* is used for a command. 3. Whatever
contributes to the perfection of the Christian person is not merely advised
or commended, but commanded by Christ: “*Be perfect, as your Father is
perfect*” (Matt. 5:48). 4. The things pressed upon us as evangelical counsels
either are commanded in the Word—such as love for an enemy (Matt. 5:44; Rom.
12:20), patience under injury (Matt. 5:39), chastity, or even celibacy if the
gift of continence is present and it is required for some cause (1 Cor. 9:5),
and voluntary poverty for Christ and the gospel (Luke 14:33; Matt. 16:24)—or
they are devised by men outside the Word and can have no other character than
will-worship. Such is *regular obedience*, which conflicts with the freedom in
which Christ has established us (Gal. 5:1) and according to which we ought no
longer “*to become slaves of men*” (1 Cor. 7:23). Such is *perpetual
continence*, because Christ testifies that not everyone possesses that gift
(Matt.

[Printed p. 28 | Internet Archive PDF 56 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

19:11), and through the apostle commands the person who cannot exercise
self-control to marry, since it is better to marry than to burn (1 Cor. 7:9).
Such is *voluntary poverty* of the kind commended by the Roman Catholics,
because no one ought to cast away his possessions without a special calling
or necessity (Matt. 19:21, 27), lest he put God to the test (Matt. 4:7) or be
compelled to neglect charity toward his neighbor (Eph. 4:28).

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

V. The command Christ gave to the puffed-up young man, who boasted in
Pharisaic fashion of perfectly observing the law—“If you wish to be perfect,
go, sell what you have, and give to the poor,” and so forth (Matt. 19:21)—was
not a counsel of perfection but a “personal and testing precept” intended to
blunt his pride and uncover his hypocrisy. Nor was it proposed by way of
counsel, whose observance or nonobservance would have been left to his
discretion, but by way of a precept he was bound to observe. 2. The perfection
proposed here is not the perfection of glory that he would have attained above
others, but the *perfection of righteousness* obtained in this life by the
pursuit of good works. It is as if Christ were saying, “If you wish to show
yourself perfect, as you boast you are, you must prove it by this test: sell
everything.” This is clear from the young man's question and Christ's answer
to it. 3. Christ's statement that the young man “lacked one thing” does not
prove that Christ approved what he had said about perfectly observing the
law; otherwise he would not have refused to obey Christ's command. It was done
only as a test so that the hidden defect of hypocrisy might be uncovered. 4.
Although the command to sell everything is not general and common, it does not
follow that it is a counsel, because it can be a special and personal
πειραστικὸν, *testing*, precept proposed for particular reasons. 5. The
statement that Christ “loved him” (Mark 10:21) does not imply that the young
man spoke truthfully when he professed to have observed all the law's commands
from his youth. The sorrow he conceived at Christ's command sufficiently
proves that his love for God was not perfect. Rather, Christ's love was a sign
of benevolence because of the desire the young man professed to obtain eternal
life and the effort he had expended in fulfilling the law, even though he was
not without his own blemishes.

VI. When the apostle in 1 Corinthians 7:25 contrasts γνώμην, *judgment*,
with ἐπιταγῇ, *command*, and says that he does not have an ἐπιταγὴν, “*command
concerning virgins*,” but gives a γνώμην, “*judgment and counsel*,” as the
Vulgate renders it, this provides no support for the Roman Catholics'
evangelical counsels. The subject is a free and indifferent matter whose
performance or omission incurs no guilt. Whether a virgin marries or does
not, she does not sin at all, since either condition can be pleasing to God if
we conduct ourselves purely in it. 2. Nor, when Paul adds in verse 38 that the
one who leaves the virgin unmarried “*does better*,” κρεῖσσον ποιεῖν, does he
mean so much what is *honorable and morally good* as what is *useful and
advantageous*. According to Paul himself in verse 28, the virgin does not sin
if she marries, and Hebrews 13:4 says, τίμιος ἐστ γάμος ἐν πᾶσι, “*Marriage
is honorable among all*.” Rather, marriage brings various troubles after it,
especially on account of the circumstances of that time and the impending
persecutions, in which the unmarried were bound to fare better than the
married. The apostle indicates this in verse 28: “*Such people will have
affliction in the flesh*,” and in verse 35: “*I say this πρὸς τὸ ὑμῶν συμφέρον,
for your advantage*.” 3. If the woman who remains in the state of celibacy is
called “*happier*” in verse 40, this must not be understood of a more perfect
degree of glory, but of her condition in this life, because she was bound to
be exposed to fewer afflictions and temptations.

VII. The *eunuchs* treated in Isaiah 56:3–4, to whom “a place within the walls
of Jerusalem and a name better than sons and daughters” is promised, are not
voluntary eunuchs who have bound themselves by a vow, but natural eunuchs or
those made so by force. Under the New Testament, provided they cultivate
piety, they are promised admission into the temple and city of God—that is,
into the church—

[Printed p. 29 | Internet Archive PDF 57 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and the excellent name of God's children, which is better than offspring
because it is eternal. This is notwithstanding the civil law concerning
eunuchs by which they were excluded from the congregation of the Lord in the
Old Testament (Deut. 23:1–2). Since the law promised fruitfulness to the
obedient, childless eunuchs, ἄτεκνοι, could have thought their devotion would
be in vain, especially since they were held in disgrace and excluded from the
people's privileges. Therefore, God says that if they are faithful, he will
compensate with other and greater benefits for what they lack in this respect,
so that no one's labor will perish. Thus under *foreigners and eunuchs* Isaiah
includes all who seem unworthy for God to number among his people, whether
because they were excluded from the covenant or because they bore some mark
of disgrace by which they were removed from God's people. He addresses them
so that they will not lose confidence, because there is no partiality with God;
rather, in every nation the person who fears him is accepted by him, as Peter
says in Acts 10:34–35.

VIII. The *eunuchs* who are said εὐνουχίζειν, “*to make themselves
eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven*” (Matt. 19:12), do not denote those who on
the basis of a counsel have vowed perpetual celibacy whether or not they will
burn. Such people could not be excused from guilt, because it is better to
marry than to burn. Rather, they are those who voluntarily impose upon
themselves the necessity of remaining celibate and maintaining such a state
of life so that they may serve God more conveniently and advance his kingdom,
for as long as God grants them the gift of continence. This qualification is
gathered from verse 11, where the Lord testifies that not everyone is capable
of celibacy and therefore marriage is necessary for some, and from verse 12,
where he says, “*Let the one who can receive it receive it*.” There he exhorts
to the exercise of continence those who are conscious that they possess this
gift, and only for as long as they have it.

IX. Some apostles were celibate, but without a vow, while their freedom to
enter marriage remained intact if circumstances required it (1 Cor. 9:5).
Others remained married even after their calling (ibid.; Luke 4:38). They did
indeed leave everything, as Matthew 19:27 says, but only those things that
might hinder them from following Christ. It is insulting to number wives
among such things, both because the wives themselves followed Christ (Luke
8:2–3) and because they are said to have remained faithfully attached to their
husbands. As for possessions, if the apostles had left them, they would have
done this not by counsel but by Christ's command (John 1:40 and following;
Matt. 9:9). Yet the gospel teaches that they did not renounce ownership of
them, since they retained houses and boats (Matt. 8; John 19; 21), but
abstained from their ordinary use and undertook manual labor so that they
might devote themselves wholly to their ministry.

X. The preaching of the gospel without charge, of which Paul speaks in 1
Corinthians 9:12, 15, was not imposed upon the apostle by a common command;
otherwise neither he nor others would have been permitted to receive a
stipend. It was incumbent upon him by a singular calling, in view of the
circumstances of the people and places, so that he might properly discharge
the apostolic office Christ had imposed upon him, on account of his rivals,
the false apostles, who were acting in this way to commend themselves to the
churches. The glory Paul consequently ascribes to himself in verse 15 he sets
against the slanders of false brothers, as though he made the gospel a source
of gain. He does not claim it as though he were performing a work not owed
and of supererogation, but insofar as he diligently and faithfully fulfilled
the parts of the office entrusted to him.

XI. When Christ commands the young man to follow him (Matt. 19:21), he does
not give a counsel but a precept common to everyone, because no one can have
hope of salvation unless he follows Christ (1 Pet. 2:21), although for a
particular reason it is specially applied to him.
