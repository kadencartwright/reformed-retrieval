## CHAPTER XXVIII.

### The Sixth and Last Defect in England Is That the Hierarchists Attack the Discipline of the Reformed Churches.

The sixth and last defect consists in this: the Hierarchists are so far from
imitating the zeal of the reformed Churches in restoring discipline that they
even attack it sharply and bitterly. After Tertullian had set out the example of
the Churches of his own time, he draws this conclusion: “What shall we observe?
What shall we choose? We cannot reject a custom which we cannot condemn, since
it is not foreign, because it does not belong to foreigners, with whom, of
course, we share the bond of peace and the name of brotherhood.”

Yet our men can now do what Tertullian could not. Although they say that they
cannot condemn the discipline of the reformed Churches, they nevertheless
reject it as foreign, even though it does not belong to foreigners. Meanwhile
they observe and choose as though it were native a discipline which truly does
belong to foreigners—men whom they both condemn and with whom, if one leaves
discipline aside, they cultivate no bond of peace but only the name of
brotherhood. If this is not to make a schism from the Churches, what is? Indeed,
what else is it but to fly openly from the brothers into the enemy camp?

If this principle did not apply, Augustine says, “to dispute whether what the
Church practices throughout the world ought to be done is the height of
madness.” Thomas Bilson not only cites but also approves this. Why, then, would
it not be insolence or folly for the Hierarchists not to do what the reformed
Churches do throughout Europe? “But you are mistaken,” they answer, “or you
mislead others when you most falsely boast that either all the reformed Churches
or the greater part of them stand with you.”

After Richard Bancroft had reviewed many Churches, he makes Martin Junior’s
judgment—that these Churches are maimed and imperfect—common to us all. He then
infers that our attacks strike these Churches just as much as England itself,
and finally concludes that “we recognize and embrace no reformed Church in the
whole Christian world as a lawful Church, and are willing to submit to none
unless perhaps it favors the Presbyterian schism.” But how do these things hang
together: that we call these Churches imperfect, yet deny that they are lawful
Churches? For one who calls Churches imperfect thereby recognizes them as
lawful Churches. Is imperfection, Bancroft, a term that destroys a Church?

John Whitgift certainly did not think so. He condemns Cartwright for Anabaptism
because Cartwright had said that the Church’s polity, as regards its general
constitution, could be perfect even in this life. Yet both things—discipline
quite as much as doctrine itself—can be perfect, as Cartwright truly said. Still,
none of our men has ever dreamed of perfection in discipline, either in opinion
