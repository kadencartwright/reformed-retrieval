# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 10.* The Gospel differs from the law in this: the law demanded
works as the condition of life, while the Gospel requires no such thing from
us but works the condition itself in us.

*Answer 1.* We know that the law, strictly so called and understood as the
law of works or as the covenant made with Adam, demanded our works as the
condition of life. And the Gospel, insofar as it is distinguished in
opposition to this law and is called the *law of faith*, requires not works
but faith in Christ. But nothing against us follows from this. For believers
under the Old Testament were not under the law understood in this way, but
under the Gospel, in which life was promised to the sinner who believed in
the promised seed of the woman. Nevertheless, they were not exempt from the
obligation of the moral law.

2. Although the Gospel does not require works in the sense in which they were
necessary under the covenant of works, it nevertheless does not entirely
exclude all works or every use of the law, as is evident in those who lived
under the old administration of the covenant of grace. The whole doctrine of
the Gospel also confirms this. See, among very many other passages, Titus
2:11–12: *“For that saving grace of God has shone upon all kinds of people,
instructing us to renounce ungodliness and worldly desires and to live
temperately, justly, and devoutly.”* And verse 14: *“He gave himself for us,
that he might redeem us from all iniquity and purify for himself a peculiar
people kindled with zeal for good works.”* Likewise, chapter 3:4: *“This is a
sure saying, and I want you to affirm these things, so that those who have
believed God may take care to excel in doing good.”* As for the respects in
which good works under the Gospel are to be called conditions, our writers
teach this abundantly against the Roman Catholics.

3. This is certain: under whatever consideration or reduplication good works
are or are not required under the Gospel, they are nevertheless required.
Consequently, the moral law, which is the norm of good works, has not been
abrogated. The Gospel requires us to perform good works, not so that we may be
righteous or justified before God, but because we are righteous and
justified; not so that we may satisfy divine justice or render God propitious,
but so that we may be pleasing to God, who is pleased with good works.
Therefore, because *pleasing God* and *appeasing God* differ greatly, it is
absurd to say that the law by which we learn to *please God* has been removed
because we cannot *appease God* through it.

*Objection 11.* Christians ought not now to do or omit anything by the force
and power of a command, but by the force and power of the Spirit.

*Answer 1.* It is true that Christians ought not to do or omit anything by
the force and power of a command as though they borrowed from the command the
strength and power to act, for all this comes from the Spirit of Christ.

[Printed p. 64 | IA PDF 72 | IA leaf 72 | IA decausadei02brow]

2. But it is false that they are not bound to do or omit anything by the
force and power of a command as a rule which moves them objectively.

3. These things are subordinate and do not conflict: acting by the power of
the Spirit as the efficient cause, and acting by the power of a command as
the rule. For the Spirit leads us in obedience and by his influences causes
us to obey the command; and through the command he often communicates the
power to act (Acts 2:38, 41; Matthew 9:9). Hence the teaching of the Lord
restores the soul (Psalm 19:8).

4. Indeed, unless a believer acts by virtue of a command, whatever he does,
he will not be obedient. The person who does not act from consciousness of a
command, and who does or omits this or that not because God commands it to be
done or forbids it, does not serve or obey God. Whoever he may be, he does or
omits this or that for himself, not for God or God's glory.

5. Can this same thing not be said of believers under the Old Testament?

*Objection 12.* Believers are urged to render obedience by motives different
from those applied to the ungodly. Therefore, they have no need of the same
rule that the ungodly need.

*Answer 1.* The conclusion does not follow. For however different the
motives may be, the rule can nevertheless be the same. A head of household
commands both his son and his servant to apply themselves to performing one
and the same task. Each has the same rule according to which he ought to
perform the commanded task, although their motives are different: the former
because he is a son, the latter because he is bound to work as one hired for
wages.

2. This argument also, if it proves anything, will prove that the moral law
was abrogated under the Old Testament. For without doubt faithful Jews were
bound by motives very different from those applied to unbelievers or Gentiles
to render obedience to God.
