### QUESTION II

#### The Question Concerns the Period of the Fourth Beast of Daniel 7:7 and the Following Verses

Answer. As many and as great difficulties and disagreements among authors
occur here as in the preceding question. But because they are almost the same
as those, we shall touch on them briefly.

Jerome and Theodoret, in their commentaries on the passage, and the other
Fathers, Greek as well as Latin, whenever they come upon that passage in their
writings, understand the leopard with four wings and four heads as the kingdom
of the Greeks under Alexander and his successors, and likewise interpret the
fourth Beast as the Roman Empire. All the Roman Catholics follow them, as do
many of the most eminent Protestants named in the preceding question. For
everyone, without a dissenting voice, makes Nebuchadnezzar’s vision in chapter
2 and the prophet’s vision in chapter 7 the same in substance. They maintain
that the bronze belly and thighs signify the same thing as the third beast,
and the iron shins and feet the same thing as the fourth beast. But almost all
the Protestants who wrote after Johannes Bodin, just as they understood the
iron shins and feet as the kingdoms of Alexander’s successors, especially the
Seleucids, likewise interpret the fourth Beast as those same successors and
the Seleucids. Franciscus Junius leads the chorus here, followed by Piscator,
Diodati, and the others named above. The primary question between the parties,
then, is whether the fourth beast is the empire of the Greeks under Alexander’s
successors or that of the Romans. Yet each side has some extravagances, so to
speak, which we shall discuss briefly in advance to prepare the way for the
principal question. On one side Hugo Grotius and Constantijn L’Empereur have
their peculiar views; on the other Thomas Parker and Ephraim Huit have theirs.
Indeed, Johannes Calvin also has certain views in which he has few followers,
if any.

> Marginalia, printed p. 238: *Everyone before Bodin interpreted the fourth
> Beast as the Romans*.

On the principal question Grotius follows Junius, but in his explanations he
has the following peculiar views. First, contrary to all the interpreters on
both sides and even to Daniel himself in 8:22, he does not understand the four
heads of the Leopard as four kingdoms of the successors, but as four of
Alexander’s generals: Perdiccas, Meleager, Seleucus, and Ptolemy. The first two
of these had no kingdoms and no successors. Certainly Alexander had many
generals, more than four, who at the outset possessed greater authority,
power, and reputation than either Meleager or Seleucus: Antipater, Lysimachus,
Antigonus, Peucestes, Leonnatus, Neoptolemus, Craterus, Eumenes, Cassander, and
others.

> Marginalia, printed p. 238: *Grotius’s absurdities*.

[Printed p. 239 | IA leaf 269 | access PDF 269 | OCR object 268]

Second. He refuses, with Jerome and the truth, to understand the ten horns of
the fourth Beast as the kingdoms into which the Roman Empire passed away.
Instead, with Porphyry, the enemy of the Christian name, he understands ten
kings of Syria and Egypt. And although he professes to follow Porphyry, he
devises kings different from Porphyry’s own. Porphyry placed Ptolemy Philometor
and Artaxias among the horns. Grotius strikes them out, but enumerates others
as supposed persecutors of the Jews: five Ptolemies—Lagi, Eupator, Euergetes,
Philopator, and Epiphanes—two Seleucuses, Callinicus, and also Antiochus the
Great. Some of these conferred great benefits upon the Jews; certainly there
was scarcely any notable wrongdoing against them by any of these men.

Third. Contrary to the historical record, he interprets the three kings
destroyed and uprooted by the little horn as Seleucus Philopator, the firstborn
of Antiochus the Great, Seleucus’s son Demetrius Soter, and Ptolemy Philopator.
Antiochus Epiphanes did indeed wage war with Ptolemy Philopator, but he never
either wished or was able to drive him from Egypt’s throne, much less root him
out. He remained on good terms with his elder brother Seleucus until the
latter’s death. He never had any quarrel with his cousin Demetrius either.
Seleucus died before his brother Antiochus Epiphanes came to him from Rome,
and Demetrius remained a hostage at Rome beyond Epiphanes’ death.

Fourth. What is supremely displeasing in Grotius’s commentary, however, is
that he most perversely weakens Daniel’s clearest testimony concerning the
Trinity of the divine persons and the divinity and office of the Mediator. All
Christian interpreters, ancient and modern, understand the Ancient of Days and
the Son of Man approaching the Ancient of Days as the eternal Father and his
Son Jesus Christ. Grotius, however, understands the Ancient of Days as no
person at all, but only God’s well-matured counsels, as he puts it. And, most
wickedly and absurdly, by the Son of Man he understands nothing but the Roman
people. He wishes them to be called the Son of Man and to come in the clouds
because that people, consisting of private persons without kings, seized
everything with the greatest speed.

Is this Grotius’s piety—to go beyond Socinus and exclude Jesus Christ from
Daniel’s monarchies? Is this learning—to reduce the Romans, the masters of the
world, though stripped of emperors and kings, to private men? Is this
Grotius’s common sense—to transform the Roman people, although consisting of
private persons, into the Son of Man and a single person?

Fifth. Grotius also has this peculiarity: he interprets the fifth, universal,
and eternal empire of the Son of Man and God’s saints, not only with Porphyry
as the kingdom of the Maccabees, but also as the kingdom of the Romans—whether
pagan, Christian, or antichristian. By the kingdoms of Christians, however,
he understands only worldly kingdoms, entirely excluding the spiritual
dominion of Christ and the saints either in heaven or on earth. Surely this is
the truest horn in a cornucopia of profane absurdities.

Constantijn L’Empereur wrongly corrects his rabbi Jacchiades here. Jacchiades
correctly understood the fourth Beast, stronger than its predecessors, as the
Roman Empire, which L’Empereur rejects without reason. We would have pardoned
this error, which he shares with others, more readily had he not piled still
more peculiar errors of his own upon it. For first, he interprets the fourth
beast’s ten horns solely as the kings of Syria and the Seleucids.

> Marginalia, printed p. 239: *L’Empereur’s peculiar views*.

Next, to fill up the number ten among the kings of Syria, he counts both
Ptolemy Philopator and Ptolemy Euergetes among the Seleucids, because, having
won some victories over Antiochus the Great, they ruled Syria for a time. But
he would have acted much more correctly had he counted both Antiochus the
Great and Antiochus Epiphanes among the Lagid kings of Egypt, for they won
more victories against the Ptolemies than the Ptolemies won against them.
Moreover, Ptolemy Lagi and Philadelphus, as well as Antigonus and his son
Demetrius, ruled in Syria longer after Alexander than either Philometor or
Euergetes.

Third. To make Antiochus Epiphanes the little and eleventh horn of the fourth
beast, he makes Alexander the Great that beast’s first horn. L’Empereur is
peculiar in this, having no learned man as a σύμψηφον, one who casts the same
vote. The prophet Daniel in chapter 8 does indeed join Alexander with his
successors in the third beast and depicts him with one horn and them with
four. Yet he distinguishes him from them most clearly: Alexander was a single
horn, broken before any one of them arose, whereas they existed on the goat’s
head at the same time after Alexander’s death. L’Empereur alone affirms, but
does not attempt to prove, that Alexander, the Seleucid kings of Syria, and
Antiochus Epiphanes constitute the fourth beast’s ten horns.

Finally, in order to defend Junius’s opinion that makes Antiochus Epiphanes one
of the ten horns, L’Empereur wrongly corrupts the sense of Scripture by his
new commentary. In Daniel 7:24 the little horn is called *another* after the
ten horns. L’Empereur says that the word should have been translated not
*another* but *last*, because among the Arabs אחר, from which Daniel’s אחרון is
derived, signifies *last*. L’Empereur takes refuge among the Arabs here in
vain, for there is no

[Printed p. 240 | IA leaf 270 | access PDF 270 | OCR object 269]

need to inquire into the meaning of the root אחר, even though it nowhere in
the sacred writings signifies *last*. We readily grant that its derivative
אחרון, used by Daniel in many places of Scripture, is correctly translated by
the Septuagint as ἔσχατον, *last* or *latest*. Nevertheless, we deny that it
can be rendered so in the passage before us, for there the little horn is not
called simply אחרון but אחרון אחריהון, *another after them*. This cannot be
understood as one belonging to the same number, but as another outside that
number, or another after those ten kings, as not only all interpreters but
Daniel himself expressly has it.

Thomas Parker, in his commentaries on Daniel, proves by many of Graser’s
arguments, as he professes, that the fourth Beast was the Roman Empire. But
three of his peculiar views are not pleasing. First, he holds that the ten
kings are the Britons, Saxons, Franks, Burgundians, Goths, Alans, Suebi,
Vandals, Alemanni, and Greeks. Most of their kingdoms, short-lived and of
meager power, vanished long ago. And if kings of this sort may be counted,
there is no reason why the kings of the Scots, Danes, Normans, Lombards,
Hungarians, Turks, Saracens, Italians, Spaniards, and others might not more
properly enter this reckoning.

> Marginalia, printed p. 240: *Parker’s peculiar views*.

Parker’s second paradox is that he interprets the three kings overthrown by
the little horn as three Saracen princes killed by Christians in the sacred
war for Jerusalem. It is difficult to ascribe to the little horn that sacred
war against the Saracens, true enemies of Christ, even though it was
undertaken at the Roman pontiff’s instigation. Moreover, those Saracen princes
did not make up any of the fourth beast’s ten horns. Finally, although those
three and many more Muslim princes were killed by Christians in the sacred
war, none of their kingdoms were subjected to Christ except for a very short
time.

What is especially displeasing in Parker, however, is that he interprets *a
time, times, and half a time* as as many years as there are days in three and
a half years. Thus he places the fall of Antichrist and the beginning of the
kingdom of the new Jerusalem in the year 1649. More about this dream in its
proper place hereafter.

Ephraim Huit has these paradoxes on this question in his excellent commentary
on Daniel. 1. The little horn denotes the kingdom of the Turks, not
Antichrist. 2. The fifth kingdom of the saints will be the civil dominion on
earth of converted Jews over all nations. 3. *A time, times, and half a time*
denotes one hundred, two hundred, and fifty years, that is, the 350 years of
the Turkish kingdom. Beginning in the year 1300, they were to end in 1650, in
which year the conversion of the Jews and the beginning of Jewish rule
throughout the world are foretold. The passage of time itself refuted the
vanity of these paradoxes five years ago.

> Marginalia, printed p. 240: *Huit’s paradoxes*.

Johannes Calvin also wanders far outside the bounds in this question. He has
very many paradoxes in which, so far as I know, he has no followers. For why
should not good Homer sometimes nod? He is entirely sound on the foundation,
for he maintains that the fourth beast is the Roman Empire, but he goes far
astray in explaining parts of the prophecy. For 1. he understands the ten
horns and ten kings as proconsuls and praetors sent annually into the
provinces by the Roman senate. Yet he admits that they were not ten, but,
according to the number of provinces, sometimes more and sometimes fewer; nor
did they possess any royal power, for their authority was only annual and
wholly precarious and ὑπεύθυνον, accountable, first to the senate and later to
the emperors.

> Marginalia, printed p. 240: *Calvin’s paradoxes here*.

Second. He maintains that the little horn signifies Julius Caesar, Augustus,
Tiberius, and the other Caesars down to Trajan, as well as other non-Roman
emperors. Surely the emperors were horns from their very first beginning, far
greater and stronger than any proconsul or praetor in any one of the ten
provinces.

Third. He understands the three horns uprooted by the little horn as the three
provinces that Augustus Caesar reserved to himself, in which he appointed
annual magistrates without a decree of the senate. It certainly does not
appear from history that Augustus or any other emperor reserved only three of
the ten Roman provinces to his private jurisdiction. Or, if they did reserve
them and in their own name appointed proconsuls and praetors to govern them,
it does not follow that they thereby uprooted the power of those magistrates
more than that of the magistrates in the other provinces.

Fourth. He holds that the judgment of the Ancient of Days amid streams of
flame, the opening of the books, and the coming of the Son of Man in the
clouds were fulfilled in Christ’s first advent and the preaching of the
gospel, even though Christ was then humbled and God’s judgments against his
enemies had not yet burst into flame.

Fifth. He thinks the destruction of the fourth beast was accomplished as soon
as Trajan the Spaniard, Maximus the Thracian, and other Greek, Gallic,
Arabian, or British soldiers obtained the empire. Thus, at the very time when
the Roman Empire flourished most and savagely tore the world and the church
with its iron claws, one must suppose that this most savage Beast had been
killed and was dead.

[Printed p. 241 | IA leaf 271 | access PDF 271 | OCR object 270]

Sixth. He holds that the saints of the Most High possessed the fifth,
universal, and eternal kingdom even at the very point in time when God handed
them over into the hands of the most ferocious tyrants to be slaughtered,
indeed during the very period of the ten persecutions.

This most substantial theologian has still many other paradoxes in this
question, which we pass over so that we may at last approach the primary
question: Does the fourth Beast signify the Roman Empire, or the Syrian empire
of Alexander’s successors and the Seleucids? All the authorities and the
eleven arguments presented in the preceding question support the former, for
it is agreed that the fourth Beast and the iron shins and feet signify one and
the same thing. Yet we shall add the following to the preceding arguments.

> Marginalia, printed p. 241: *Arguments for the truth*.

First. Alexander’s successors are represented by the third Beast, and
therefore not by the fourth. Otherwise the Beasts would not be different from
one another, as Daniel expressly asserts concerning all the beasts and
especially the fourth in verses 3, 7, 19, and 23. The antecedent is clear from
verse 6, where the four kingdoms of Alexander’s successors constitute the
Leopard’s four heads. To make the Seleucids and Lagids first two of the third
Beast’s heads and then the whole body of the fourth beast, and finally the ten
horns of that same fourth beast, would wretchedly confuse the distinction
between the beasts most clearly expressed by the Holy Spirit.

Second. The fourth Beast arose from the destruction of the third, and the
fourth kingdom abolished the third, just as the third did the second and the
second the first. For just as the Persians subjected the Chaldeans and the
Greeks the Persians, so those who followed the Greeks ought to have subjected
and destroyed the Greeks. Yet Alexander’s successors did not subject or
destroy Alexander’s kingdom, but merely appropriated its parts to themselves.
This subjection and destruction of the Greeks was accomplished by the Romans
alone. Therefore the Romans, not Alexander’s successors, must be regarded as
the fourth beast.

Third. The fourth Beast was stronger and more terrifying than all the others,
and crushed the whole earth with its iron teeth. This is most plainly true of
the Romans, but utterly false of the Seleucids, especially in the time of
Antiochus Epiphanes, when they maintain that the beast was strongest. For this
prince was so weak that, at the height of his flourishing, a single man, the
Roman proconsul Popilius Lenas, drew a circle around him with a rod and ordered
him to retreat from Egypt at once with his victorious army. He not only had
to obey, but, far contrary to his own feelings, to affirm that he obeyed the
Roman senate most gladly indeed, as though it were his earthly God.

As for the common evasion that Antiochus’s strength and power are meant not
absolutely but in relation to the Jews, we said in the preceding question how
futile this is.

Certainly the Prophet’s many emphatic descriptions of the fourth Beast’s very
great strength demonstrate that he speaks of the Beast’s strength in itself
and by comparison with the first three. Nor can the extent of its kingdom over
the whole earth be confined to the extremely narrow boundaries of only that
part of Palestine within which the Jews were contained in the days of
Antiochus, since the kingdom of the saints that follows the fourth extends to
the whole earth under all heaven.

Fourth. Ten horns grew upon the fourth beast. But in the kingdom of the
Seleucids, or even of all Alexander’s other successors, ten horns did not
appear, but either many more or fewer. If you count all the satrapies that
arose after Alexander, and both the first kings and their descendants within
them, you will have more than thirty princes. If you inspect only the
Seleucids from the first to the last, there will be nearly twenty; if you
exclude those after Antiochus Epiphanes, the Seleucids will not reach ten. We
disposed of the evasions from this argument above.

Fifth. If the fourth Beast signifies the Seleucids, then the little Horn will
denote Antiochus Epiphanes. But the latter is false, for the little horn
uprooted three kings before it, and this cannot be shown of Epiphanes, as we
proved above.

Sixth. The fourth Beast spoke supremely proud and blasphemous words until God
killed and removed it. But this is not true of the Seleucids, or even of
Epiphanes himself. Before his death he most humbly submitted himself to God
and was far removed from every proud blasphemy, if either Josephus or the
author of Maccabees is to be believed.

Seventh. The fourth Beast was removed by the last and universal Judgment at
the end of the world, for the judgment that Daniel describes with so many
remarkable circumstances can apply to none other than the last and universal
one. Yet no such extraordinary judgment was needed to remove the Seleucids.
Like other ordinary tyrants, they were removed easily enough, and Epiphanes
himself was afflicted by no notable calamity beyond that of his father and
other tyrants, as may be seen in the history of the Maccabees itself.

Eighth. The fourth Beast continued in power until the saints were admitted
into possession of their universal and eternal government, verse 22. But this
is not true of Epiphanes or any Seleucid. For although there are various
opinions concerning this government of the saints, in no sense is it true
that any Seleucids reigned until the saints

[Printed p. 242 | IA leaf 272 | access PDF 272 | OCR object 271]

obtained their spiritual, universal, and eternal kingdom.

Ninth. After the little horn’s fall, the fourth beast was destroyed and
burned with fire. Yet after Epiphanes’ death neither the kingdom of the
Seleucids nor that of Alexander’s other successors was destroyed. Therefore,
if Antiochus was the little horn, as the opponents here maintain, neither the
kingdom of the Seleucids nor that of any of Alexander’s successors was the
fourth Beast.

Certainly Alexander’s successors reigned in Syria, Egypt, and Macedonia long
after Epiphanes’ death. As for their dominion over the Jews, we have already
said how empty this evasion from the arguments is. Surely, when the whole
fourth beast is said to be burned at the little horn’s fall, it seems
exceedingly harsh to understand this only as a relative, not an absolute,
destruction of the fourth kingdom.

Finally, after Antiochus Epiphanes the Jews were often quite grievously
harassed by the Seleucids. Judas Maccabaeus and all his brothers—Eleazar,
Simon, and Jonathan—were killed, and by the Seleucids’ force and fraud the
people were afflicted with many calamities. Nor were they freed from the
Seleucids’ tyrannical proximity for a long time after Epiphanes, as will be
plain to anyone who considers the history.

Amandus Polanus brings six arguments in mood and figure for the contrary
opinion, which he calls Demonstrations. It is easy to erase them all with one
stroke, for they are all mere assumptions of the point at issue without any
proof.

> Marginalia, printed p. 242: *Polanus’s objections refuted*.

The first is this. The fourth kingdom hindered the worship of God in
Jerusalem before Christ was born. But the Roman monarchy did not do this.
Answer. The major premise is false. For its proof Polanus brings nothing but
a bare citation of Daniel 7:25, 26, and 27, in which there is not one iota
signifying that the kingdom and wickedness of the little horn occurred before
Christ was born.

Polanus’s second Demonstration is of the same stamp. The fourth kingdom, he
says, ruled Judaea before the temple was cleansed again by Judas Maccabaeus.
But this was not true of the Roman monarchy. Answer. He does not prove the
major premise, but assumes as beyond dispute that Antiochus was the little
horn, which is the πρῶτον ψεῦδος, the first falsehood.

Polanus’s third Demonstration. The fourth kingdom was destroyed before
Christ’s kingdom was established. But this is false of the Roman monarchy.
Answer. The major premise is true of Christ’s glorious kingdom in heaven, not
of the ecclesiastical kingdom on earth.

Fourth. The Roman monarchy flourished after Christ’s kingdom was propagated
on earth, but the fourth kingdom did not. Answer. The distinction is the same
as before.

Fifth. Only ten kings of the fifth kingdom ruled the Jews. But far more than
ten kings of the Roman monarchy ruled the Jews. Answer. The major premise is
false, for those ten kings named in verse 24 were to arise at the end of the
fourth kingdom, after the death of many emperors and the division of the
empire at its extremity into the feet’s ten toes.

Sixth. The tenth king of the fourth kingdom destroyed three kings before him.
But the tenth emperor of the Roman monarchy did not do this. Answer. The major
premise must be distinguished. It is true that the tenth king at the beginning
of the Roman monarchy did not destroy three kings before him. But at the end
of the monarchy, after the empire was divided into ten horns, one little horn
among them destroyed or will destroy three in the various ways stated above
and to be stated below.

To Polanus’s six arguments the most recent English Annotators add a seventh,
often repeated by them. Those who interpret the fourth kingdom as Roman, they
say, must interpret the little horn as Antichrist. But this favors the Roman
Catholics, who chiefly from this interpretation devise for themselves an
Antichrist lasting three and a half years. Answer. There is no necessity in
either the major or the minor premise. As for the major, many who interpret
the fourth Beast as the Roman kingdom interpret the little Horn as Muhammad;
some of them interpret that little horn as the rabble of sectarians that arose
in our last century and disturb the church together with Antichrist. Moreover,
until God in his own time reveals more of his counsel, there is nothing absurd
in confessing our ignorance of who this little horn will finally be. As for
the minor, the greatest enemies of the Roman Antichrist deny it: Luther,
Bullinger, Calvin, and others. Although they believe that the little horn is
the Roman Antichrist, they do not therefore restrict his kingdom to three and
a half years. What the duration is of the intervals mentioned by Daniel—*a
time, times, and half a time*—we shall see in the following question.
