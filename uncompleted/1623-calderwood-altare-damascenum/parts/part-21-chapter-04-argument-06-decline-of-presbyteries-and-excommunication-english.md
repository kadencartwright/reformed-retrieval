# David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum* — Part 21

Fresh English translation of the paired normalized Latin transcription from
the 1623 first edition. The Princeton/Internet Archive page images for
`altared00cald` are the controlling witness; OCR and the 1708 Leiden reset are
reading aids only. This part begins in the lower portion of printed p. 268
exactly at `Presbyteri, qui erant in puriore Ecclesia negotiorum & causarum
iudices`. It contains the complete treatment of presbyters’ exclusion from
common government, the answer to the argument from synods and canons, and the
proof that the power of excommunication belonged to the presbytery. It ends
on p. 275 after `Atque haec de potestate iurisdictionis`; the immediately
following synthesis of the two powers of Order and jurisdiction is excluded.
All quotations, marginalia, citations, and Greek in the paired Latin are
retained. This AI-assisted draft has not received human review and is not
publication-ready.

The presbyters who were judges of affairs and cases in the purer church were
at last excluded from common government and the administration of affairs;
and little by little the bishop as president was changed into a prince—indeed,
into a lord worse than any prince. For although princes are bound to use the
counsel of others in administering the commonwealth, bishops are not bound to
use the counsel of the ecclesiastical senate; indeed, according to the
hierarchists, not even its presence is necessarily required. Thus those who
once were judges are now not even spectators or counselors. Bilson says that
the presbytery’s assistance, even for counsel as formerly, is not necessary.
*For they did not yet have another method of directing themselves besides
their own presbyteries. Afterward, as the gospel grew and flourished, synods
began to be convened more frequently, and the pastors of many churches—both
by sending letters back and forth and by holding assemblies—deliberated on
the order and discipline to be established in their churches. The
presbyteries of particular places were given more leisure and release from
the church’s greater affairs, because the provincial councils took up for
themselves the matters which*

[Marginalia: *De gubern.* c. 14. p. 355.]

*had previously tended to require the presbyters’ care. Likewise, in the
beginning there were neither councils to prescribe canons nor Christian
princes to enact laws; instead, each place was left to its own prudence and
counsel in directing itself. Therefore, lest the bishop’s will alone be the
rule of everything to be done in the church, ecclesiastical administration
was initially arranged in such a way that neither were the presbyters
permitted to do anything without their bishop nor was the bishop permitted
to conduct more important business without his presbytery.* Dounamus makes
similar statements. He says that in the first three hundred years after
Christ it was especially necessary for the bishop to use the presbyters’
counsel, since Christians had neither frequent synods for deciding
controversies, nor synodical constitutions for directing bishops, nor the
magistrate’s authority for reforming abuses in the church’s government.
Otherwise, the bishop’s will would have stood as law, and his government
would have been exposed to error in itself, irreparable injury to clergy and
people, and the reproach and scandal of many. But when provincial synods were
convened more frequently to decide controversies, review injuries, and frame
as many canons as seemed sufficient for directing bishops, and the assistance
of the Christian magistrate was added, the presbytery’s counsel and
assistance were no longer so necessary: the presbyters desired leisure and
release for study, and the bishops sought exclusive government. This is how
he interprets the words of Ambrose—or of that author who was a little older
than Ambrose. Ambrose speaks about the seniors whom they call laymen, as we
shall show in its proper place, not about presbyters who both taught and
governed. For the testimonies we adduced above from Jerome, Gregory, and
others make it plain that the use of the ecclesiastical senate had not died
out long after that author’s time. Moreover, presbyteries are the church’s
ordinary assemblies, while synods are extraordinary. Extraordinary
assemblies ought not to abolish ordinary ones. Provincial councils were more
frequent even before the year 300, yet presbyteries had not been abolished.
Nor was this the sole use of presbyteries—that whenever some doubt arose they
would furnish counsel—but they also exercised the authority and power
entrusted to them in correcting faults. Therefore, as I said, they were judges
of cases. Moreover, doubts always arise and new cases emerge that require an
immediate remedy, especially in these times, when provincial synods are not
held twice a year as among the ancients. Indeed, is there not very often
dispute about the law and about the interpretation of canons previously
framed in provincial and national councils? Besides the canons of councils,
particular statutes are also needed as the circumstances of the particular
churches in each city demand. Finally, the execution of canons is no less
necessary for the church’s well-being than the prescription of canons,
Bilson says. *For when the canons of councils and the constitutions of*

*Christian princes, by regulating and settling all those controversies and
questions which previously tended to be handled in presbyteries, brought it
about that presbyteries were neither so useful nor held in such honor, they
entrusted the execution of all the laws to the bishop alone, without the
presbyteries’ knowledge or counsel, because the greater courts were unwilling
to submit their decrees to the judgments of the lesser decuries.* We have
already spoken about the canons framed to inform the bishop, and about the
execution of the canons, which also belonged to the presbyter. Bilson’s
statement that the greater courts were unwilling to submit their decrees to
the judgments of the lesser decuries is therefore frivolous. That is not what
we maintain. We maintain instead that the lesser decuries executed the
decrees of the greater courts—that is, that presbyteries administered the
canons of provincial synods no less than the bishop did; otherwise the
bishop’s sentence was not canonical. A bishop can be carried away by his
passions in executing canons just as in prescribing them. Even after many
canons had already been framed in both general and provincial councils,
Jerome admonishes that presbyters ought to govern the church in common with
the bishop. For if they governed the church by common counsel before
dissensions arose and before any bishop was placed over them, all power of
government must not be snatched from them by any decree or custom of the
church. Rather, bishops ought, out of respect for the Lord’s arrangement by
which presbyters governed the church by themselves, to hold them in honor and
govern together with them. When the apostles were present in established
churches, did they use the presbyters’ counsel to inform themselves because
canons had not yet been framed, or did they instead use their assistance and
their partnership in power in administering the keys? Let us now grant that
bishops were placed over presbyters by decree of the apostles. Either they
were placed over them in such a way that they governed the church in common,
or it was left free to the bishop to use or not use their work and
assistance. If the former, then governing in common is of apostolic
institution. If the latter, then the apostles instituted the bishop as a
monarch and prince with exclusive government and power. Yet even after the
apostles’ time presbyters were judges both in councils and in presbyteries.
Moreover, I do not see how the following statements are consistent. When
Bilson teaches on what occasion the power of presbyteries first declined, he
says, *It was not because bishops usurped their rights or seized dominion over
them, but because the things which previously tended to be handled in
individual presbyteries then began to be considered in synods and in the
great councils of the bishops and pastors of an entire kingdom and
province.* Yet elsewhere he says: *Ambrose does not rebuke them for having
driven the lay seniors from the government of the church, but because, in an
excessive affectation of wisdom, they had removed from sacred councils the
brothers and fellow servants whom they had been accustomed to use as
assessors and counselors in all causes both of doctrine and discipline.* And
Dounamus says that this happened through the arrogance and pride of the
teachers—that is, of the bishops—when they wished to be the only men

[Marginalia: *De gubern.* cap. 14. pag. 358.; Cap. 11. pa. 210.; *Def.* l. 1. ca. 8. pag. 192.]

[1623 printed p. 271 | IA leaf 283 | PDF p. 283]

of any account. If the presbyters’ counsel and attendance were not needed
because so many canons had been framed, the bishops were not to be condemned
for pride and arrogance. But the claim that everything previously handled in
individual presbyteries was handled in councils is also false. That was
impossible. And if it were true, what, I ask, was left to the bishop any more
than to the presbytery? You will say the execution of the canons. But the
administration of the canons was also handled in the presbytery. Either the
execution of the canons was handled in detail in the councils or it was not.
If the former, the bishop was no more needed than the presbytery, for there
the administration of laws took place no less than their prescription. If
the latter, then not everything handled in presbyteries was handled in
councils; and in executing the canons the presbytery is no less necessary
than the bishop, so that it may oversee the execution of the laws in view of
the bishops’ negligence or arrogance and their other corrupt passions.
*Nevertheless, in order to prescribe a certain method and rule for their
government, so that it would resemble paternal power, such as had always
existed in the house of God from the beginning, and would not equal royal
power through fear of domineering over the Lord’s inheritance,* Bilson says,
*Christ’s church permitted a bishop to undertake nothing in certain more
important affairs without a synod or the consent of his presbyters.* He cites
Carthage IV, canons 23 and 32, and Seville II, canon 6; then he infers: *There
are several matters of this kind in which a bishop was by no means permitted
to involve himself without his presbytery or a synod. Some of them have been
changed by laws, while others remain in force at present.* He said above from
Ambrose that nothing was done without their counsel, and from Jerome that
everything was conducted by common counsel; now he says that provision was
made for matters of greater importance. But why, at least, are all matters of
great importance not handled by the presbytery’s common counsel and consent?
Is the alienation of ecclesiastical property the only matter of great
importance? The same fate certainly befell presbyteries as befell synods. As
we showed above, the metropolitan took away the synods’ rights, and so the
bishop took away those of the presbyteries. The progression is now not from
the presbytery to the synod, but from the bishop to the metropolitan and from
the metropolitan to the king’s delegates. Traces remained not only of common
counsel but also of common government: the bishop could not alienate the
church’s goods without the chapter; and, as Dounamus admits, *when the see is
vacant, custody of the episcopate and of the episcopal rights in a certain
manner returns to the chapter.* Nor was the power of the keys completely
taken away from presbyters. *As far as we are concerned,* Bilson says,
*although we hold that the power of the keys in its proper degree is common
to all who have the pastoral care of souls, nevertheless, in order to avoid
the innumerable storms of excommunications which would otherwise break upon
and overflow all churches and parishes, and the intolerable disputes and
conflicts that would necessarily follow from them if every presbyter were
permitted to excommunicate at his own discretion, we especially praise the
wisdom of God’s church because it has given*

[Marginalia: *De gub.* cap. 14. p. 362.; Cap. 11. pa. 224.; *Def.* li. 1. ca. 8. pa. 190.; *De gub.* cap. 14. p. 369.]

[1623 printed p. 272 | IA leaf 284 | PDF p. 284]

*no one belonging to any inferior place the power of excommunicating except
with the bishop’s consent and authority.* We do not give any individual
presbyter a personal power of excommunicating, nor do we hold that the
bishop’s consent and authority are sufficient. Rather, we have said that
presbyters excommunicated by the authority and consent of the entire
presbytery, both in the apostolic church and in the purer ages that followed.
From where do so many senseless thunderbolts of excommunication proceed if
not from the bishops’ consistories, from which their chancellors and officials
hurl them even over a monetary matter and other utterly trivial things? The
same Bilson says: *Yet we do not so completely adjudge all power of the keys
away from presbyters that they cannot prescribe to offenders who confess
their guilt that they abstain from the Lord’s table for a time. But we in no
way permit them the public administration of the keys, which consists in
excluding recalcitrant and obstinate sinners from every communion of the
faithful, both sacred and civil, because Christ’s church has always granted
this to bishops alone.* What was Bilson just now afraid of if presbyters
possess only the power of minor excommunication and not of major? For if they
cannot excommunicate with major excommunication, so many storms of
excommunications could not break upon the churches. But just as that was a
groundless panic, so we do not grant to every presbyter the power to exclude
someone from the holy Supper at his own discretion, but only by the authority
and consent of the presbytery. We proved above, not only from Scripture but
also from fathers such as Tertullian and others, that major excommunication
belonged to the presbytery and ecclesiastical senate. Add Theophylact on
Matthew 18, who says: ἐπὶ τῶν τῆς ἐκκλησίας προέδρων δημοσίευσον τὸ πλημμελές,
*publish the offense before the presidents of the church.* He assigns several
presidents or prelates to one church, before whom the sin was to be made
public and who possessed the power of binding and loosing. Dounamus says that
not all power of the keys was taken from presbyters: *But they exercise as
much power of the keys through the word and sacraments as belongs to them by
right: on the one hand, binding those who are manifestly scandalous and
impenitent by denouncing God’s judgments against them in the preaching of the
word and by repelling them from the sacraments; and on the other hand,
loosing faithful penitents by applying the free promises of the gospel and
adding the sacraments as seals.* Here he grants presbyters only minor
excommunication and only in a certain case, but not major excommunication in
the consistory. Did Christ entrust to some men the power of major
excommunication, to others that of minor excommunication, to some a power
over manifest sins, and to others a power over sins that are not manifest?
But what if Dounamus deceives us even concerning minor excommunication? *In
the rubric of the English liturgy there is indeed a certain canon by which a
pastor is permitted to repel notorious offenders*—namely, from the Lord’s
Supper—*but the practice of our church has now fallen into a worse condition,
and this canon is evaded by the canonists’ interpretation. They say that the
pastor is to regard no one as notorious unless he has previously been
condemned as notorious in the court of the cathedral church. This coincides
with the Whitgiftian position that all persons, however notoriously known,
remain in*

[Marginalia: Cap. 14. pa. 366.; in Matt. 18.; *Defens.* li. 1. cap. 2. pag. 42.; Parkerus *de Polit.* li. 3. c. 4. n. 10. pag. 163.]

[1623 printed p. 273 | IA leaf 285 | PDF p. 285]

*the church if they wish to approach the sacraments until they are separated
by excommunication.* It is well, however, that they verbally grant this tiny
concession to presbyters, for it is a trace of the authority and power of the
keys which they once possessed in full. *In others,* Durandus says, *there is
only a derived and limited power, as it pleases the pope. Among these the
lowest degree is held by simple priests, to whom by the law of which the pope
is the maker either no jurisdiction or only a minimal one belongs—for
example, to excommunicate for notorious thefts and matters of that sort in
places where this is established by custom.* Those who can excommunicate for
notorious thefts can also excommunicate for other crimes. For the ancients
did not distinguish between thefts and other sins, much less did the apostles
or Christ when he entrusted the keys and said “whatever you bind,” not “what
thefts you bind.” And what Durandus says is a trace of the power of the keys
which once belonged to presbyters. Alexander III writes to the bishop of
Florence: *When an ecclesiastical sentence is promulgated by the prelates of
churches against certain wrongdoers, it ought to remain valid and firm and to
be observed inviolably until suitable satisfaction has been made. Therefore
we command that, if at any time the parish priest of St. P. has reasonably
pronounced a sentence of interdict or excommunication upon his clerical or
lay parishioners, you cause it to be observed inviolably and do not relax it
without suitable satisfaction and without the concurrence of that same
parish priest.* Here we see that parish priests and plebans can pronounce a
sentence of interdict and excommunication upon lay and clerical parishioners.
In his commentary on this title Filefacus says: *The gloss on this chapter
teaches us that prelates—a name under which parish priests are also
included—can excommunicate with the consent of all.* The same Alexander III
writes as follows to the bishop of Langres concerning a man who furtively
received the order of deacon: *if he was not forbidden by you, or by one of
your archdeacons or prelates, under threat of anathema,* etc. From this
Filefacus infers: *Parish priests can forbid under threat of anathema a man
who furtively received an order from ministering in the order he received.*
In the capitularies of the emperors Charlemagne and Louis it is enacted as
follows: *It has been resolved that the faithful should not treat the
excommunication of those priests as a trifling matter, since if they do so
they will justly be separated from the assembly of Christians.* Filefacus
says: *Here it is more plainly indicated that priests of an inferior order
under the bishops themselves, whom we call parish priests, have the power of
excommunicating.* In the same capitularies it is enacted: *Let a person
excommunicated by a priest not presume, so long as he remains under that
excommunication, to accuse his elders or bishops. If he should presume to do
so, let him not be received, but let him either be subjected to a greater
excommunication or expelled from the church.* Filefacus says: *There is no
doubt that these words indicate priests other than bishops, and that these
men are none other than parish priests.* It was enacted in the Council of
Nantes that bishops in all assemblies and the other priests in their churches
should publicly forbid incestuous marriages from being contracted and should
also subject to excommunication

all who, after the present synod’s action, entered into the bonds of incestuous

understood as parish priests. Odo, bishop of Paris,* the same Filefacus says,
*in his synodical constitutions four hundred years ago, has the following in
the chapter on marriage, where he admonishes parish priests: Let parish
priests firmly forbid by excommunication that sacrileges be committed by
laymen. Let the priest publicly forbid under excommunication in the church
that either spouse enter the religious life, or be received into it, except
through the bishop. In the chapter on common synodical precepts, number 37,
let priests forbid by excommunication, especially at vintage time on every
Lord’s Day, that any Christian keep with him the residue of the vintage which
Jews tread in any manner, on account of that horrible impurity which they
commit in contempt of the sacrament of the altar; and if any residue remains,

their meat unless the Jews keep all of it. At number 41, under pain of
excommunication let priests forbid their parishioners to hear unknown
preachers, because of the danger of heresies and errors which they sow.*
Likewise: *And indeed, if according to the opinion of blessed Thomas it is
granted by law to parish priests to pronounce excommunication for theft,
robbery, and similar cases, this will not occur without examination of the
case. Those who say that excommunication is pronounced only by judges—why
should they not believe that parish priests too are judges? For the title we
have undertaken to interpret, On the Office of an Ordinary Judge, since it
includes parish priests, as everyone grants, also implies judicial power.* He
answers the objection that excommunication belongs properly to prelates and
pertains to jurisdiction, while neither the name of prelate nor jurisdiction
belongs to parish priests: *Calling parish priests themselves prelates is so
common and is repeated so often in canon law that there is no reason for this
designation to be considered improper. This is manifest in this chapter,
where the parish priests themselves are expressly called prelates and, to
this purpose, mention is made of the parish priest or pleban of St. Pancras.
And in Bernard of Pavia there is a title On the Office of Both Prelate and
Ordinary Judge. And book 5 of the Decretals, title 30, chapter 1, On One Who
Furtively Received an Order, from the letter of Alexander III to the bishop
of Langres, mentions prelates who are themselves subject to the bishop. These
are none other than abbots, parish priests, and similar persons, as the text
easily demonstrates. In the same book follows title 31, On the Excesses of
Prelates. No sensible person will deny that parish priests too are included
under that name, as the final chapters sufficiently indicate, and especially
chapter 7, which is repeated from the Lateran Council under Alexander III,
part 1, chapter 4, and from the Lateran Council under Innocent III, chapter
33, where rural deans, when they receive the proper procedure for visiting
the churches entrusted to them, are numbered among the prelates. Yet these
men are parish priests. In book 3 of the Decretals, title 6, On a Sick Cleric,
chapter 4, the duty of prelacy is called the burden of governing a parish.
The gloss does this in very many places, whether in this very chapter, in the
title On the Excesses of Prelates,*

[Marginalia: Pag. 125.; Pag. 125.; Pa. 137.]

*or in 1, question 6, “No One,” on the word “Excommunicate,” and in other
places cited above. As far as jurisdiction is concerned, they also add the
examination of a case, which is joined to it. The gloss on the word
“excommunicate” just mentioned, and On the Excesses of Prelates, chapter 2,
attributes jurisdiction and the examination of a case to parish priests
themselves, although not such jurisdiction as a bishop possesses.* He infers
the examination of cases from the cited passage of Aquinas in the addition
to the third part, question 22, article 1. We have therefore seen that in more
recent centuries, while Antichrist reigned, parish priests could
excommunicate, though not with the solemn rite treated at 11 q. 3, chapter
*Debent*, and copiously by Burchard in book 11. But if we ascend to the purer
ages, we shall discover from the things said above that the most solemn
excommunication was pronounced only by the authority and consent of the
presbytery. Yet since solemnities neither add nor alter anything essential,
it was enough that even in the most corrupt centuries the essential elements
of excommunication were permitted to parish priests. Indeed, what was
permitted to them was excessive: they could excommunicate without the
authority and consent of any presbytery or ecclesiastical senate. We cannot
tolerate that in either a bishop or a parish priest, or in either a higher or
lower prelate. We saw above that Spalatensis asserted that the power of
excommunicating is no different from the power exercised through the keys in
the penitential forum, and that the most secret penance is wholly the same as
that most public penance which our modern writers call solemn. Yet they have
never denied this secret penance to presbyters. Finally, Dounamus himself does
not deny that presbyters could publicly reconcile penitents, as we showed
above. So much for the power of jurisdiction.
