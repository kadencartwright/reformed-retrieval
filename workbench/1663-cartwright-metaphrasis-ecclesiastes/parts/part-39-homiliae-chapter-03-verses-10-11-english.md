VERSE 10. I have seen this occupation which God has given to the sons of men,
that they may occupy themselves in it.

VERSE 11. He makes each single thing beautiful in its own time; he also sets
in their mind how long the age lasts (except that man does not attain it), the
work which God himself does from beginning all the way to end.

VERSE 12. I know that there is no good among them except that each should be
glad and do good in his life.

VERSE 13. Indeed, whenever any man eats and drinks and enjoys good from all
his labor, this is a gift of God.

VERSE 14. I know that whatever God does endures forever; nothing can be added
to it and nothing can be taken from it, and God himself brings it about that
men fear before his face.

VERSE 15. That which has been now is, and that which will be has already been;
finally, God himself brings back the things which follow one another.

The summary is an explanation of the preceding teaching about the
inconstancy of all things, once the thorny difficulties by which it seems
obstructed and entangled have been cleared and disentangled. Here tacit
objections and replies to the objections are considered.

The first objection, by which doubt is cast upon the preceding teaching from
the inconstancy of all things and their change into the contrary, is this:
everything is whirled about by the blind impulse of fortune, and nothing is
administered by the deliberate and watchful providence of God. Here it must be
noted that it is because of the blindness and perversity implanted within us
that everything seems to be carried and whirled about rashly and confusedly.
In thinking this, what else do we do but take the reins and rudder of the world
away from God and transfer them to an idol which exists nowhere? We do the
same thing as the ungodly man in Job, chapter 23: “Is not God in the height of
heaven? Look at the summit of the stars, how lofty they are. Therefore you
say, ‘What would the mighty God know? Would he judge through the darkness?
Dense clouds are his hiding place, so that he does not see, and he walks about
the circuit of heaven.’”

Thus, in paintings skillfully executed with great exactness of art, only
artisans and those who

are endowed with trained eyes know how to pass judgment upon the artistry and
exquisite workmanship. In the same way, only those whose eyes God has anointed
with the eyesalve of his Spirit can behold God's wisdom in the government of
the world. Solomon replies to this objection that God governs all these
things. For the case is different with the workmanship of God and that of men.
The things men have fashioned and made they afterward either cast away into
some corner or pass from themselves to others. God, however, never lets go of
the things which he once created. He is always present with them, turns them
this way and that by his hand according to his will, and never withdraws his
hand from the things he has created.

Solomon confirms that God governs everything from the attributes of his
works: because each thing which happens happens at its own time, he made them
beautiful. So far is any deformity and confusion which men imagine for
themselves from adhering to them. Indeed, if account is taken of men and
malicious spirits, whom God uses as servants and instruments for administering
those events, they are more than full of Babylonian confusion. Yet when mind
and thought are called away from those agents and transferred to God, who sets
one end before himself and produces another effect—either an effect of just
judgment upon the ungodly or of the testing of faith in the godly—the events
prove most orderly.

Everyone acknowledges peace to be beautiful and lovely, but war horrible;
love beautiful, but hatred shameful; the construction of houses welcome, but
their demolition grievous. Yet in the judgment of God and of men who judge
rightly, the latter things are beautiful just as the former are. So it was
with Joseph's sale by his brothers, Esther's marriage to a profane king, and
the church's persecution by Paul. Through these most unworthy crimes provision
was made for God's church: for sustenance in a time of famine; for safety in a
time of impending `πανωλεθρίας`, “utter destruction”; and, in the time when
the nations were called, for a most majestic and by far the most powerful
herald for consoling sinners.

Nor in the memory of all ages has any deed more unworthy or more atrocious
existed than the betrayal of Christ to death. Yet if you consider God's
counsel, nothing more beautiful or more glorious has ever been heard in
report. God alone is like the philosopher when he possesses the stone by
which he makes the choicest gold out of iron, indeed out of dross. He is
likewise a skilled apothecary or compounder who makes medicine beneficial to
men from the poisonous deeds of men.

Solomon strengthens this very point in verse 10 from an attribute. God has
commanded men to investigate this, so that they may occupy themselves in
discovering the beauty of everything that happens. In the first chapter he
taught that God gave them the task in which they occupy themselves by
searching out the natures of things. Now he teaches that another task rests
upon them, one much holier and more majestic than the former: carefully and
exactly to reckon the admirable power and wisdom of God in governing the
universe.

The book of the world which God has set forth for everyone to read is indeed
more extensive, but this Book embraces things more abstruse and deeply hidden.
The book of the world has more readers, but this one has better and more
select readers. The former is understood by acuteness of reason, the latter is
perceived by the illumination of the Holy Spirit. The former brings grief and
trouble upon its readers, all the greater the longer and more vigorously they
devote themselves to it; the latter bathes its readers in great delight.

We are encouraged to inquire into this matter because in what follows we are
granted to attain this very thing. It is confirmed from God as its efficient
cause, because God lays it open to their mind and understanding. We are thus
taught to join prayers to God with meditation and reflection upon his
government, for every good gift and every perfect bestowal flows from him.
Certainly, if anyone directs his mind and looks attentively at the time, place,
persons, and

especially the outcomes of events, he will easily understand that the very
things which at first sight threatened destruction ultimately proved
beneficial. Hence the saying by a man alienated from God, “I would have
perished had I not perished.” Conversely, things which brought men great hope
of prosperity afterward ended in their harm. Finally, by use and observation
the godly discern that all things work together for good for the godly, even
the greatest evils, while for the ungodly all things turn to evil, even the
greatest goods. What could be said or conceived more beautiful or fitting than
this?

But here Solomon excepts certain things which, being reserved for the
knowledge of God alone, cannot be investigated by men. Hence Romans 11:33–34:
“O the depth of the riches, wisdom, and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are
his judgments and how untraceable his ways! Who has known the mind of the
Lord, or who has been his counselor?” Such plainly is the matter the apostle
treats there: the election of the Jews into the covenant people while the
nations were excluded, the unbroken wall for keeping them away being left in
place, and afterward the expulsion of the Jews from the kingdom so that a
place might be given to the nations. Job said many excellent things to this
effect against his friends. It is an honor to a king that he reserve some
secret to himself; how much more is this true of God? If anyone assaults the
approach to God by breaking through the doors, as it were, he will receive the
reward worthy of such audacity.
