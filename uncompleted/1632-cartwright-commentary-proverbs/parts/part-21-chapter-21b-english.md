Verse 4. The loftiness of the eyes, and the breadth of the heart, the plowing of the wicked are sin.

In רחב (*amplitudo*, breadth) there is a metaphor transferred from physical things, which are extended to a great breadth, to the mind, by which a proud mind full of arrogance is denoted. In נר there is likewise a metaphor transferred from agriculture to designate human actions: for just as a good or bad harvest comes from plowing, so reward and recompense depend upon upright or wicked human actions.

The purpose is to set the exceedingly unhappy condition of the wicked before our eyes, so that, when it has been duly considered, we may come to our senses in good time.

Of the three evils proposed here, breadth of heart, which occupies the middle place, is first by nature and in the order of treatment, although it obtains the second place because of the verse and meter, as it seems. For elevation of mind is the origin and seed of haughtiness of the eyes and of wicked plowing. Hence at Ps. 131.1, when it has been placed first, haughtiness of the eyes and occupation with things which are too high for us and transcend our condition are immediately joined to it. Although he uses another metaphor there, for he sketches the arrogance of the heart in that passage by a measurement of height, whereas here he delineates it by the dimension of breadth. At Hab. 2.4, however, it is described by another metaphor, that of a swelling which rises and bulges into the shape of a little hill. From all these the Holy Spirit depicts for us a distinctive mark of pride and arrogance in the mind. For just as a heart in the human body which exceeds its proper size, whether in height or thickness, and is displaced from its proper location brings serious

[1632 witness | PDF p. 458 | printed columns 900-901]

harm to the whole body and all its parts, and in a short time brings present death, so a mind swollen with its opinion of itself casts us into the certain ruin of eternal death. Although *broad of heart* is sometimes taken in a good sense, at 1 Kings 4, for one who is adorned by God with a broader knowledge of things than others. Likewise Ps. 119, section 4. But the other metaphors are not so used.

Haughtiness of the eyes follows. Although this is generally seen in those who assume lofty airs, yet if we refer this haughtiness of the eyes to the eyes of the body so that it is a sign and indication of pride, it is certainly not a τεκμήριον, a conclusive proof. For we know that there is great dissimulation in the face and that outward gestures are often fashioned for deception and imposture. At Isa. 58 we read that this was charged against the Jews in their fasting: they bowed their head to the ground like a rush while meanwhile they stood upright in their minds. The little brethren of the Pontiff furnish us with an illustration of this matter. For them it is a religious scruple to lift up the head, as they always keep their eyes fixed on the ground; nevertheless, nothing can be found more arrogant or prouder than they are. Therefore this haughtiness of the eyes is related in a certain principal respect to the ambitious desires of the mind, when people, not content with their condition, seek things higher than befit them.

Finally, he affirms that the plowing of the wicked—that is, whatever they think, say, or do—is sin; that nothing can flow from them which escapes the guilt of sin; and that they sin not only when they openly violate the commandments by committing theft, adultery, or murder, but when they eat, when they drink, when they sleep, when they wake, when they play, when they work, when they grieve, and when they laugh. What is greater, they sin when they pray, when they fast, when they give alms to the poor, and so forth. For since their heart is not purified by faith, Acts 15, it is necessary

that everything which flows from that impure treasury of the heart be thoroughly imbued with the heart’s impurity. Since they are not good trees, they cannot bear good fruit; on the contrary, since they are rancid trees, they must produce rancid fruit. This is not because there is no great difference between a wicked person who is sober and one who is drunk, between one who lives chastely and an adulterer, or between an idle person and one who seeks his living by honorable labor, and so forth—not only before people but also before God. For those who offend against the outward respectability of life will be bound to far more grievous punishments than those who observe civil respectability.

From this it is clear how horrible and dreadful the condition of the wicked is. The Holy Spirit therefore sets it before us both so that the wicked may turn to God early and speedily and so that, when a contrast has been drawn, the godly may acknowledge their singular happiness in Christ. For everything they themselves do is pleasing and delightful to God in Christ, if you except only those things in which they are found to contravene God’s most holy commandment. Thus they please God not only when they serve his worship, but also when they eat, drink, play, and so forth.

Verse 5. The thoughts of the diligent tend only to gain; but everyone who is hasty is borne only toward want.

The purpose is to commend the diligent person to us by contrast with his opposite, namely the hasty person.

He appears to contrast them both in their causes and in their effects or outcomes.

First, then, he distinguishes them by their cause. In the case of the diligent person he attributes thoughts sent before and in advance of the work to be done, which by this very fact he appears to deny to the hasty person, because, without any mention of thoughts, when he speaks about him

[1632 witness | PDF p. 459 | printed columns 902-903]

his deeds, achievements, and actions can be seen to be excluded, not obscurely, from preceding thoughts and deliberations. But since counsels and deliberations preceding the conduct of affairs are, as it were, the bases and foundations of affairs to be conducted excellently, it is just as easy to conduct affairs rightly without them as to erect a building without a foundation.

Moreover, this appears to be confirmed by the etymology of the word itself, which denotes a person who defines and determines each particular within certain bounds and barriers, considering with himself what must be done by each person, and where and when each thing must be done. Christ appears to portray this virtue excellently at Luke 14.28–33. Then, when he has formed a mature counsel, he immediately puts his hand to the business and lets no opening or opportunity for conducting it pass by. An exceptional and illustrious example of this diligence appears in Gen. 24, in Abraham’s servant Eleazar, who was sent by his master as a matchmaker for Isaac.

By contrast, a hasty person immediately pursues whatever first comes into his mouth; his hand outruns his mind and brain. To be sure, he is assiduous in work, no less than the diligent person, and sometimes he wearies himself still more in doing the work, as well as others under his command. Yet this yields no good either for him or for them, because he undertakes his affairs rashly. Hence the proverbs arose: “Soon enough, if well enough”; “Make haste slowly.” And in the common proverb among us it is said, “Hast makes wast”: haste is joined with waste. Conversely, it is said, “Soft fier makes sweet malt”: a slow fire makes steeped barley sweet.

See an example of this precipitancy at 1 Sam. 14. Saul likewise rushed rashly onward in his eagerness to pursue the victory, to such an extent that he prohibited everyone from so much as tasting food. But the same thing happens to people of this kind as to travelers who, by hurrying and by excessively intense exertion, complete a great part of their journey on the first day but thereafter cannot advance farther because of weariness and the excessive straining of their sinews. For in this way that deed of Saul very greatly curtailed the extent of the victory. If he had considered with himself the dangers which were going to flow from that ἀνοίᾳ, thoughtlessness, he would have abstained from so inconsiderate a command.

Here, therefore, a vigilant watch must be diligently kept against disturbances and sickness of mind which drive a person headlong.

Next, the contrary outcomes are proposed. Nothing but gain falls to the diligent person—see the example of Ruth—but poverty falls to the rash and hasty person. Therefore, by setting a reward before the diligent person, he confirms him in his diligence; but by laying punishment upon the hasty person, he deters him from his rashness. On the former see what has been written on 10.4; 12.24 and 28.

Verse 6. The making [or acquisition] of treasures by a tongue of falsehood is a driven vanity of those who seek death.

In לשון (*lingua*, tongue) there is a synecdoche of the part. In שקר (*falsitatis*, of falsehood) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

The purpose is to deter people from wealth wickedly gotten.

That he marks the tongue as the instrument and, as it were, the broker of riches acquired through injustice is evident because this Proverb is to be understood not so much of burglars who secretly and furtively steal another’s goods, or of those who commit robbery on the highway. For the former commit their thefts in complete silence; the latter either say nothing, lest they betray themselves by their voice, or, if they say anything, nevertheless do not disguise the matter with lies. Rather, just as they order a traveler to give them his purse, so they take away the purse if they can. It does not, therefore,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 460 | printed columns 904-905]

speak only of those who shun the light and take flight after committing theft, but especially of those who perpetrate thefts and robberies in broad daylight, through the middle of the marketplace and without fear.

From this it is evident how unworthy it is to adapt the tongue to so vile a purpose, so that it becomes like rouge and paint by which people’s eyes are deceived: so that what is vile may seem precious, what is shameful may seem beautiful, and, finally, what is evil may seem good. Thus what ought to be a tree bearing wholesome fruits for refreshing people is not merely barren in these things but produces deadly fruits. Finally, that which ought to render a person superior to all living creatures—whence in Scripture it is called the *glory of man*—renders him worse and lower than all living creatures.

This is not because it is unlawful to make a profit with the tongue and seek a living and wealth, as orators and Ministers do, but because to misuse it for shameful gain is base and ignominious. Since, therefore, we are members of one another, care must be taken—as the Apostle admonishes—that, having cast away lies, we speak the truth to our neighbor; Eph. 4.52.

Next he proposes the effects of treasure acquired by a deceitful tongue, by which he deters people from this sin. One is—is in the wealth acquired; the other is in the person who amassed it. As for the treasures, he affirms that they are vanity. Here it is worthy of observation that someone can grow rich by a false and lying tongue and acquire an immense quantity of money, furnishings, and revenues. People must not therefore flatter themselves that, because they have amassed great wealth, they are approved by God or their frauds are not hateful to God. Instead, he teaches that such wealth and treasures are vanity, that is, most vain and exceedingly fleeting and unstable.

Someone may object here that everything under the sun is said by Solomon to be vanity, and indeed the greatest vanity, and that for this reason the title of vanity detracts nothing from riches wickedly gotten, since within the compass and embrace of this vanity are included even treasures gotten justly and honestly.

I answer that in the passage of Ecclesiastes all things whatsoever apart from the fear of God and true piety are included, because their use and fruit are limited to the narrow span of this life. This is confirmed by the proverb at 13.11, where riches gotten by vanity are said to be diminished, whereas this proverb affirms that riches themselves are vanity. And it is certainly most fitting that things gotten by vanity should be vanity. But in this passage by *vanity* he understands a still more fleeting condition, and for this reason he joins *driven* to the title of vanity to signify the greatest inconstancy and lightness, so that treasures of that kind are like chaff or straw which the slightest breeze scatters here and there.

We therefore see how true is that statement at Ps. 120, where by speaking ἐρωτηματικῶς, interrogatively, he most vehemently affirms that a deceitful tongue profits its user nothing.

The other effect is that it brings death upon those who have amassed these treasures. Here it is entirely certain that by the word *death* not only temporal but also eternal death is understood. Indeed, riches acquired in this way often hasten even temporal death, since people are not lacking who lie in wait for the life of one who has grown rich by right and wrong. Then divine judgment, hanging over the necks of those who pass their life in wickedness, also hastens this death in accordance with the threat of the law. But by the word *death* the Prophet principally signifies eternal destruction, since godly rich people do not escape this temporal death and indeed are sometimes carried off by a more untimely death than those who have been enriched by frauds. Thus this Proverb comprehends both effects, as Peter aptly and briefly declared to Simon Magus at Acts 8.20: “Your silver”

[1632 witness | PDF p. 461 | printed columns 906-907]

“perish with you.” Thus it comes about that those who amass treasures by fraud amass for themselves, in corresponding proportion, a treasure of divine wrath for the day of wrath and vengeance.
