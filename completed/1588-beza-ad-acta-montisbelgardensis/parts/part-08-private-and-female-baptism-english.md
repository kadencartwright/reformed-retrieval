## Part VIII: Private and Female Baptism

[Part II | PDF page 139 | logical source scan 138 | printed page 137 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

But not everyone agrees how this contempt should be defined. This first principle, however, appears to require establishment without controversy: one cannot be regarded as having despised baptism when a necessity imposed from elsewhere wholly prevents every use of baptism. This is so if someone is overtaken by death, lacks water or something able to take water’s place, or finally is situated where he cannot possibly be baptized. Many cases of this sort occur, whether infants or adults are in question.

The controversy turns instead upon this: some think that baptism must be sought and administered by any private person, indeed even by a woman, rather than be lacked. The error is certainly not new, but if one considers the matter thoroughly it casts a very great and most troublesome scruple into consciences. This dogma has advanced so far—

[Part II | PDF page 140 | logical source scan 139 | printed page 138]

—that some have not feared to consign to eternal punishments infants in whom this remedy was neglected, while others, trying to mitigate that severity, have cast their own and others’ consciences into the most absurd dreams. What is this but imagining oneself captive in the midst of liberty and laboring over the method and manner of untying a knot by which one is not bound at all? The Lord commands that the Sabbath be sanctified, sacrifices offered, and feast days celebrated; but other laws prescribe the time, manner, order, and persons by whom these things are to be done. Therefore one who wished to fulfill those laws but could not satisfy his desire in the place, order, and through the persons required has not neglected them. Indeed, if he were intent upon the single law commanding this or that deed while neglecting the laws prescribing how it must be performed, he would transgress the very thing he was striving to fulfill. I say that those who not only tolerate baptism by private persons and especially women, but command it in the crisis of their fictitious necessity, commit this same fault.

Who can doubt that administration of the sacraments is the second part of the public gospel ministry? “Go,” says the Lord in Matthew 28:19, “teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.” And since the testimony and profession either of parents in the case of infants or of the baptized themselves in the case of adults is required, to whom does the office of baptism belong if not finally to those who possess authority from Christ to demand it? Therefore, just as not everyone in the church has authority to preach the gospel, neither does everyone have authority to baptize. From where, then, comes this imaginary necessity of seeking baptism from private persons or women? Who attributed any right of administering baptism to such persons?

Indeed, since the one who commanded baptism also prescribed the law for seeking it and the manner of administering it, how can those who knowingly and deliberately violate the manner of administering baptism be said to have obeyed baptism’s precept? How can those who suppose one law binds them to violate another appear to possess sufficient wisdom from God’s Word? And why should someone whom God does not enable to fulfill the command in the order God appointed not rather rightly judge himself divinely released from that law? Examples also prove that the ancient Fathers judged this way against that exceedingly strict but fictitious necessity. Why, when the young Nazianzen was caught in an evident shipwreck as if in the midst of death, did he lament so greatly that he faced danger of death unbaptized, when he could so easily have avoided what he feared? Certainly neither water in the midst of the sea nor men and women on the ship were lacking, by whom he could have been baptized amid so great a danger of damnation.

The definition of this controversy rests upon two firm and immovable foundations. First, no one in the church assigns himself authority to administer God’s house, but only one who is—

[Part II | PDF page 141 | logical source scan 140 | printed page 139]

—called. This is true whether God raises someone up outside the ordinary order to oppose robbers who did not enter through the door, or whether the call is ordinary, the kind to be observed while the polity of the sacred ministry remains in force. Accordingly, in my antithesis to Dogma XI I expressly mentioned the ministry REMAINING IN FORCE; but in a serious error they wrote and published “PRESSING” in place of “REMAINING IN FORCE.” Second, the Apostle prohibits women from speaking in the church, that is—as our adversary himself correctly interprets it on page 500—in a public assembly. Therefore he also prohibits them from preaching and administering the sacraments.

Andreae argues to the contrary on pages 499 and 500: *If in a case of necessity—that is, when ministers of the church or other private men are absent—women may do what is greater, namely comfort dying people with the promises of God’s Word and absolve them from sins (for what else is the PREACHING of the gospel than absolution from sins?), then they may also do what is less, namely baptize in a case of necessity. Nor*, he says on page 501, *can or should women by any law or any express Word of God be kept from this ecclesiastical ministry. Therefore*, he says again on page 501, *we ought not keep women from the OFFICE OF TEACHING the gospel and comforting the sick.*

Such, in sum, is his case for baptism by women. In it, after the sophists’ fashion, he first presupposes the very thing in question: namely, that a necessity can arise for violating an order which the Lord willed to be perpetual. That one must never imagine a necessity which the law itself does not impose, or which releases people from the bond of a law that does not exist, is sufficiently clear from numerous passages of Scripture: from the miserable end of Aaron’s sons who offered strange fire; from Uzzah, who supported the ark when he thought it otherwise about to fall; from Saul, who sacrificed without waiting for Samuel; and similar instances.

Andreae himself rightly acknowledges on page 499 that baptism is part of the gospel ministry and thereby sufficiently refutes himself. For if it is ecclesiastical, it is not private; therefore it is not female—unless he first demonstrates that a necessity can occur in which baptism ceases to be part of the ecclesiastical office, and unless he either restricts the Apostle’s saying in Hebrews 5:4, “No one takes this honor for himself, but he who is called by God,” to the high priesthood alone or teaches that it must be limited by an exception for his fictitious necessity. Nor do singular examples involving an extraordinary impulse of God tell against us: Phinehas’s killing without formal proceedings, Elijah’s burnt offering, and many events occurring especially in the time of the Judges. To these must also be added acts which God tolerated though they were not rightly done, such as Zipporah’s circumcising her son with great anger rather than from true piety. God undoubtedly tolerated this, content with Moses’ measured chastisement, lest he add affliction to the afflicted.

To approach the support of Andreae’s argument, with what shame can he attribute to women the office of teaching the Word and absolving?—

[Part II | PDF page 142 | logical source scan 141 | printed page 140]

—He says, *Because preaching the gospel is nothing other than absolution from sins.* Indeed, it is also binding. What then? What can be concluded from this except that preaching the Word also belongs to women—ordinarily to men, but granted to women whenever, according to his definition of necessity, someone lies dying and neither ministers of the Word nor other private men are present? Yet in the apostolic writings, so far is *preaching*—whose very etymology declares it not to be a private act—from being attributed to women that even *teaching* is spoken only of those who fulfill a public ministry of teaching in the church. Indeed, when Luke speaks in Acts 18:25 of that most holy married pair, Aquila and Priscilla, by whom Apollos of Alexandria was more fully instructed, it was not without reason that he used the verb *to explain*.

Lest I seem to hunt syllables, I acknowledge that preaching the Word and administering the sacraments may be compared as two parts of the sacred public ministry, of which the former is primary and the latter its appendix, as we will shortly discuss. But I say in turn that each of those administrations of the Word which forms part of the public ecclesiastical ministry differs altogether in kind both from the duty by which all Christians are bound by the law of charity to instruct, rebuke, and comfort one another at every time and place and on every offered occasion, and from the authority parents possess over families, masters over pupils, and lords over servants, even with some coercion. Household government and the other ranks of human society, and therefore a master’s power, are prudently distinguished from the command of magistrates, although a magistrate does many things with his subjects that private persons do among themselves, but by another and altogether different authority.

Andreae therefore wrongly calls things differing wholly in kind by the same name of the office of teaching and absolving, as if they were one and the same. In his argument from comparison of lesser and greater, he also wrongly compares that office as something greater with administration of the sacraments, which he again wrongly attributes to private persons and women. For although there is one Christ and one faith, and in all people we are bound to hear God speaking, rebuking, and comforting, nevertheless acting from brotherly charity and also by paternal or household authority is one thing; serving as *ambassadors for Christ*, as *ministers and stewards of God’s mysteries*, and speaking, exhorting, and rebuking *with all authority*, not simply or by some private paternal or household right, is another. The Apostle attributes these latter things not to Christians indiscriminately, but only to those called to the gospel ministry, who in a proper and peculiar sense are called men of God and servants of God. Of these at last the Lord says, “As the Father sent me, so I send you. He who hears you hears me, and he who—

[Part II | PDF page 143 | logical source scan 142 | printed page 141]

—despises you despises me,” etc. Likewise, the dreadful judgment, “Let him be as a Gentile and a tax collector,” is prescribed not against those who despise brotherly correction made even with two or three witnesses, but only against those who despise the church—that is, the presbytery.

Thus the foundation of this argument from comparison collapses: the compared things are not merely unequal in form but dissimilar. It is also utterly overthrown by this further reason. Andreae asks on page 500: *Which is greater, the Word and preaching of the Word, or the sacrament of baptism and its administration?* Before answering, I will report—not by divination, but from what he answered me on page 501—what he said when I asked whether, since both sacraments have the same rationale, he could permit the Lord’s Supper to be given to a sick and dying person in the pastor’s absence by a private man or woman. He denied that he would permit this. *Their rationale is dissimilar. For an infant*, he says, *has never yet been baptized when a woman baptizes it in a case of necessity. But an adult Christian placed in his final struggle has often received the Supper before in an ecclesiastical assembly. If a woman comforts him by the voice of the gospel and reminds him of it, there is no danger even if he dies without receiving the Lord’s Supper. But baptism has a far different rationale, concerning which Christ’s words remain: “Unless one is born again,” etc.*

As though there could not be someone placed in evident danger of death who had never received the sacred Supper and despaired of his salvation no less than an unbaptized person requesting baptism! When I objected this to Andreae and he could not adequately untie this knot, he poured forth that shameful answer which his secretaries omitted. I mentioned it once in the preface to this reply and have not thought it necessary to repeat it here.

Moreover, it is false to judge baptism more necessary than the Lord’s Supper if we believe Augustine, who urges Christ’s words, “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man,” etc., no less rigidly upon infants than “Unless one is born again,” etc. For my part, I do not doubt that each sacrament is equally necessary to those for whom it is commanded—baptism being commanded also to infants, but the Lord’s Supper only to adults, since they are commanded to examine themselves. This necessity extends so far that deprivation of neither sacrament is fatal, but contempt alone; and one who could not obtain either in the divinely established order is judged to have despised neither.

But grant that Augustine’s opinion was rightly rejected as concerns giving the Lord’s Supper to infants too. If what is more necessary is also of greater importance, while baptism, if Andreae is believed, is more necessary than the Lord’s Supper and must therefore be conceded also to private persons and women, but the Lord’s Supper not likewise, it follows that in this controversy the question should not have been which thing—the—

[Part II | PDF page 144 | logical source scan 143 | printed page 142]

—Word or baptism—is by nature more excellent, but which of the two is more necessary. Something may be more necessary in use while nevertheless lesser and inferior in itself and by nature. On this premise Andreae’s argument will destroy itself. For by what reasoning will baptism be conceded to women as more necessary, but not the Lord’s Supper? One would likewise have to say that the power to teach God’s Word, as necessary even above baptism itself, must be permitted to women and indeed anyone, but not the power to baptize. That the Word is more requisite than baptism or even the Lord’s Supper is beyond dispute. You therefore see both that his argument drawn from comparison of these things’ natures is inept and that the consequence as he states it is false.

Yet let us speak methodically about the proposed question and say something especially pertinent. First, I distinguish the simple Word from the sacramental Word, that is, the Word clothed, as it were, with external and visible signs and rites. Then in the sacraments themselves I distinguish the word of promise from the elements and signs themselves. Although the simple Word is especially necessary, since the sacramental actions were instituted to confirm it, and the greatest care must be taken that it be continually and diligently administered and heard—in which sense the Apostle also says he was sent not to baptize but to evangelize—the thing itself testifies that the sacramental Word is something correspondingly greater and therefore its administration correspondingly more excellent. It is presented to more of the senses of each person approaching, and the Holy Spirit thereby uses more instruments to foster and increase faith within us.

Since this is so, even if I conceded that administration of the simple Word is common to private persons, and therefore to women, together with the church’s ministers, it would by no means follow that the power of administering the sacramental Word must be attributed to them. Granting the lesser does not immediately grant the greater. I acknowledge that the word of promise joined to the sacred signs is something far more excellent than the elements themselves, which are no less appendices to it than public seals appended to instruments. If it belongs not to private persons but to the authority of the public magistrate alone to confirm and authenticate both public acts and transactions between private parties by affixing the public seal, and if a private person attempting this would rightly be guilty of falsehood and indeed violating public authority, whatever necessity he might allege, the thing itself demonstrates that the same must much more be held of administering the sacraments. It is a purely public act entrusted only to God’s servants called to the church’s ministry.

This too must be observed: the promise of grace joined to the elements is not contained wholly in that formula of a few words according to whose prescription Christ commanded the Christian church to administer the sacraments individually to Christians. Rather, an exposition of that promise and, especially in baptism, an express stipulation of that formula must also be employed: partly so that what is offered to individuals may be confirmed by the common confession and approval of the entire present assembly, and partly so that each person, having been admonished, may take ever greater care not to partake of the sacraments unworthily—

[Part II | PDF page 145 | logical source scan 144 | printed page 143]

—Although infants themselves do not then comprehend the baptismal liturgy, when they grow up they afterward can and ought to meditate upon it. It is also important for the church to possess, so far as this can be done, authentic testimony concerning each of its members. Nor can what each person promised at his baptism be sufficiently impressed upon him, even when another’s baptism is being administered. I pass over the church’s common prayers joined to this liturgy, which certainly can hardly be useless to the infants themselves. That this was always customary in the church once the order of the sacred ministry had been established is clear from the pure ancient liturgies. From there came the “answer” of a good conscience mentioned in 1 Peter 3:21, though later generations corrupted those ancient liturgies in astonishing ways.

If the narrations and explanations of the sacred mysteries must be joined to their application itself, and Andreae thinks that these too must be permitted to private persons and indeed women, let him proceed to place private persons and women in the pulpit too in his case of necessity. The ancient Fathers certainly add not even the slenderest exception of that necessity: the Fourth Council of Carthage, Canon 100, against women-baptizers; Tertullian reproving the Marcionites; Epiphanius the Pepuzians and Collyridians; and Ambrose the Cataphrygians. Yet they certainly would never have omitted such an exception if anything of the sort had then prevailed in the church.

And I ask whether God formerly would have tolerated anyone other than members of Levi’s tribe performing either the priestly or the inferior Levitical ministry. Witness Micah in the time of the Judges, Gideon’s ephod, and Uzziah’s leprosy. Nor does a certain charge said in the Fourth Council of Carthage, Canon 12, to have been entrusted to widows—to instruct unlearned and rustic women who were to be baptized so that they knew how to answer the sacred questions—tell against us. Nor is Epiphanius’s statement against the Collyridians relevant: that deaconesses—whose order was plainly altogether distinct from the ministry of the Word—were charged to keep the naked body of a woman at women’s baptism from being seen by the men performing the sacred rite.

Augustine writes in *Against Parmenian* 2.13 that he does not know whether baptism administered by a layman—that is, a private person—compelled by the necessity of someone perishing should piously be repeated. With all respect to so great a man, this is assuredly another blemish proceeding from the same source as that other error concerning administration of the Supper to infants—

[Part II | PDF page 146 | logical source scan 145 | printed page 144]

—namely, the opinion of an imaginary necessity of the sacraments: in the case of baptism from Christ’s saying, “Unless one is born again,” etc.; in the Eucharist from the same Christ’s words, “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man,” etc. Augustine’s ambiguous and uncertain speech sufficiently indicates that his opinion concerning baptism administered by private persons was not then received in the church but was only gradually beginning to be introduced, just like no few other corruptions in the Christian religion. Before him, Alexander, bishop of Alexandria—otherwise an orthodox man and a chief enemy of the Arians—wandered so far (for why should we not say this frankly?) that he did not regard even a baptism administered by boys playacting the rite of sacred baptism as vain and invalid, and therefore denied that it should be repeated in the great Athanasius. Yet I think scarcely any orthodox person would now agree with him.

Finally, as concerns those two passages of the Apostle cited by Andreae, his memory failed him concerning the first, 1 Corinthians 14:34. There is no mention there of the office of teaching, whether in or outside the church’s assembly. Rather, the Apostle commands women to remain so silent in the sacred gathering that they do not open their mouths even to ask a question. The gospel history establishes that even unlearned men customarily did this in the Jewish synagogues; and that it was also done in Christian gatherings appears from several passages in the apostolic epistles and from Tertullian. But Andreae ought to have remembered the other passage, 1 Timothy 2:12, where, simply and without mention of any time or public assembly, the Apostle prohibits women from usurping the office of teaching. As we said before, the verb *to teach* denotes an office belonging only to ecclesiastical ministry.

The other passage, Galatians 3:28, is applied still more violently to the matter at hand. It is manifest that the Apostle speaks neither of the office of preaching the Word and administering the sacraments nor of even private callings by which members of the Christian church are distinguished from one another. He speaks instead of that saving grace of the gospel offered to everyone through the gospel, with all outward circumstances of nation, status, and sex removed, both in the Word and in the sacraments. This is therefore nothing other than wandering outside the course and, as self-willed and arrogant people are accustomed to do, rashly seizing anything to prove anything.

Thus far concerning baptism. Let us now hear what propositions we faithfully gathered from the whole preceding disputation, both concerning churches and images and concerning baptism, and at that very time presented to the Württemberg doctors, but which they rejected.
