On the Notions of the Divine Will

CHAPTER I

*On the Will of Sign and of Good Pleasure*

2. The will of sign—or what is otherwise called the revealed, approving, or
commanding will—is considered in two ways. First, insofar as its object was
decreed by God from eternity actually to exist. For just as God by the will of
sign commands the elect to make themselves a new heart, so he decreed from
eternity to work this in them and promises it in time, Ezekiel 36:26–27. Thus,
as to its object, the revealed will coincides with the hidden, decreeing, or
efficient will; and here the controversy about it is not properly very
intricate. Second, this will of sign is distinguished from the will of good
pleasure in this way: God reveals to the creature a good and holy thing which
obliges it to obedience, whether that obedience actually exists or not. This
must be carefully noted: actual obedience or disobedience, considered as
willed or not willed by God, is something altogether accidental to the will
of sign. Whether what God reveals in the Word is actually done or not, the
essence of the will of sign is preserved. Therefore the revealed thing,
insofar as it is willed—that is, approved—is the object of the will of sign;
and that same thing, insofar as it actually exists or does not

[1651 logical printed p. 215 | IA leaf 233 | PDF p. 233]

exist in reality, is the object of the will of good pleasure. 3. Hence Aquinas,
Valentia, Durandus, and Suárez hold that, in this division of God's will, the
will of sign is improperly called will, and that the division is of an
equivocal term into its equivocal meanings. Thus Durandus says at folio 13:
“For a command is not directly and always a sign that the commander wills the
commanded thing to be done, but only that he wills to bind the subject.”
Aquinas likewise considers it improperly a sign of will, just as punishment is
a sign of anger when formally there is no anger in God. Because we become
angry when we punish, punishment is called a sign of anger. They would
therefore say that in his revealed will God behaves after the manner of one
willing rather than properly wills. And that unblemished flower of learned
men, William Twisse, holds that God's will does not pass over into the thing
commanded, but rather into his commanding it. This is certainly most true in
this sense, with respect to the good willed externally. For it does not
properly follow from the will of sign that the thing willed exists or does not
exist. God thus wills many things—such as the obedience of the reprobate—which
he does not will actually to exist. Yet this must not be overlooked: the will
of sign is most properly will with respect to its own object. For what God
commands, he wills and approves as good and holy; what he forbids, he
disapproves as evil. He also decreed from eternity by his decreeing will of
good pleasure that the thing should be good. Thus every object of the will of
sign is willed by the will of good

[Marginalia: Aquinas, part 1, question 19, article 1; Valentia, vol. 1,
disputation 1, question 19; Durandus, book 1, distinction, folio 48, question
3; Suárez, vol. 1, *On Divine Aids*, book 3, chapter 5.]

[1651 logical printed p. 216 | IA leaf 234 | PDF p. 234]

pleasure; but it does not immediately follow that every object of the will of
good pleasure is decreed by God and willed by the will of sign. Only that which
is revealed in the Word as just or unjust is so willed. In this sense the will
of sign terminates in and passes both into the command and prohibition and
into the thing commanded or prohibited.

4. You will ask, then, what the will of sign expresses to us. I answer: 1. It
expresses that it is the nature of the Word earnestly to urge and commend
faith and good works. 2. It reveals the law and gospel—namely God's wise and
most holy decree and good pleasure concerning the reward of the obedient and
the punishment of the disobedient. Micah 6:8: “He has shown you, O man, what
is good.” Romans 12:2–3. 3. It reveals that it is everyone's duty and
obligation to obey. Obadiah 13: “You ought not to have looked on the day of
your brother.” 4. Finally, it expresses to us that God simply approves, and by
an act of complacency wills, human obedience, whether men actually obey or
not. 1 Samuel 15:20–22. But as for actual obedience, the will of sign does not
formally will it—that is, it is not intended by this will. Thus the grave and
learned Dr. William Ames observed that God virtually and eminently wills the
things he commands.

[Marginalia: Reply to article 1 of the Remonstrants' written Remonstrance.]

5. Hence the will of sign and of good pleasure differ in many respects. 1.
They differ in their acts. The will of good pleasure is God's decree and
eternal determination about whether a thing is to be done or

[1651 logical printed p. 217 | IA leaf 235 | PDF p. 235]

not. The will of sign is an act of divine complacency concerning a thing not
as it is to be done, but a simple love of the thing whether it is done or not.
2. They differ in their objects. The object of the approving will is good as
good; insofar as it forbids, its object is moral evil—that is, good or evil as
it agrees or disagrees with the law or gospel. Yet it is such moral good as in
itself abstracts from the actual existence of the thing commanded or
forbidden. The object of the decree, however, is not moral good as such, but
good, or that which has the character of expediency and conduciveness in
relation to God's most wise freedom. It does not abstract from existence, but
regards the thing as inevitably to exist actually through God's decree, or not
to exist. Hence God approves some things, such as the obedience of Judas and
Pharaoh, which he nevertheless never decreed to have real existence. And he
decreed some things under the aspect of a conducive good, though not under the
aspect of moral uprightness—such as the sale of Joseph and the killing of the
Savior by savage and brutal assassins—which he approves by no act of
complacency whatever. Hence, 3. the object of the approving will is much
narrower and more restricted than the object of the decree. For the object of
the approving will is moral good and evil insofar as it binds us by legal
command and prohibition. The object of the decree is this and, besides it,
whatever God determines and decrees should occur, not from duty or debt but
from his most wise freedom as universal and supreme Lord and governor of all
things—and not only what he decrees as lawgiver.

[1651 logical printed p. 218 | IA leaf 236 | PDF p. 236]

It must therefore be observed that God's decrees are not confined or limited
in their equity by the approving will. On the contrary, the approving will is
limited and determined by God's free decree. Thus God is not bound by duty or
constrained or governed by any law other than his own good pleasure in making
his free decrees. Nor is a thing good to God and a fitting object of decree
because it agrees with the approving will and is just, as though God would act
unjustly if he decreed the contrary. For then he could rightfully decree only
what the law or gospel commands, and would be bound by necessity of justice
not to decree what the law or gospel forbids. This is plainly false. Let the
adversaries tell me where it is written in law or gospel that God is bound in
justice either to permit sin or not to permit it. Where in Scripture is God
commanded to give one person an abundance of effectual grace and deny the
same to another; to elect this one and reprobate that one; to bestow the light
of the gospel on this nation and deny it even to a better nation? The
Arminians themselves confess this. The Remonstrants after Arminius, and
Corvinus, teach so in their writings at the Synod of Dort. Consequently,
although God cannot decree what is contrary to his will—for he would then
contradict himself—he is nevertheless not regulated by that will in his
decrees. Rather, the approving will

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, writings at the Synod, articles 3 and 4, p. 9;
Corvinus, *Against the German Remonstrance*, part 2, chapter 7, p. 187.]

[1651 logical printed p. 219 | IA leaf 237 | PDF p. 237]

is regulated by the free decree. God approves this by law and gospel and
disapproves that because he has so willed in his wise freedom. And who does
not know that God could forbid many things he commands and command things he
has forbidden? Thus they dream who call the decree of predestination properly
just: it is properly free. But that justice of God revealed to us in Scripture
is posterior in nature, in the order of reason, to the decree of
predestination. He elects or reprobates not properly because it is equitable
and just—as though these acts flowed from justice as a formal principle. For
then it would be unjust if others, or more, or fewer, were elected or
reprobated than those who now are. He does so because he has decreed it in his
wise freedom and dominion. 4. Hence it follows from the approving will that a
thing is just or unjust, but not that as such it will or will not actually
exist. From the decree there follows not the legality, equity, or iniquity of a
thing, but only its necessary existence at last at the time prescribed by God.
For the will of sign concerns τὸ ποῖόν τι, namely the quality and nature of the
deed which the reprobate as well as the elect are bound by duty to perform,
apart from consideration of the efficient causation or actual existence of
the commanded or forbidden thing. The decree, however, concerns not ποῖον but
τὸ ὄν: that the thing will at last inevitably occur and actually exist,
whether it is equitable or inequitable.

5. The approving will does not reveal to us God's intention or decree. Such
commands as “Cain, Saul, Pharaoh, Judas, obey

[1651 logical printed p. 220 | IA leaf 238 | PDF p. 238]

and believe” do not signify or convey this as something willed by God: “This
is my intention and my decree from eternity, that you should obey.” If God
expressed this in words, those who posit in God a will of sign and of good
pleasure in this sense would undoubtedly have to confess contradictory wills
in God concerning one and the same uniform object. The Arminians most falsely
impute this to us, either from ignorance or sheer lust for slander. Rather, by
the command proposed to Cain, Saul, and Pharaoh, God says only: “I declare and
reveal to you by command that obedience is pleasing and acceptable to me. You
are bound by law and obligation to render it to your Creator if you wish to be
acceptable to him, whether you actually obey or not.” They do not obey, but
there is not even a shadow of contradiction here. God does not say, “I will
that you obey, while inwardly I decree and will that you do not obey.” He says
only, “My will approves your obedience and loves it by an act of simple
complacency, by which I love good and turn away from evil; and I also will and
decree that you never actually obey.” What prevents God from approving many
possible beings according to omnipotence—many worlds, many good angels, and
the obedience of good angels who will never be created—although he decrees or
intends that they will not exist? Someone may say that the gospel, and often
the law too, reveals God's very intention and decree which he executes, as
when he promises

[1651 logical printed p. 221 | IA leaf 239 | PDF p. 239]

that he will produce a new heart in the elect, harden the reprobate, and
devote the disobedient to eternal destruction. Reply. It cannot be denied that
the will of sign sometimes reveals God's decree. The decree then passes
through that revelation into the will of sign, as the examples cited show.
But the present discussion chiefly concerns the will of sign by which God
reveals to creatures what they are bound to do, whether they do it or not, and
as it is distinguished from the will of good pleasure, by which God freely
decrees what he himself will do, either immediately by himself or through the
intervention of creatures. I say that under this restriction the will of sign
in no way reveals God's intention—what he intends to effect of that which he
reveals by command, or to avert or prevent of that which he prohibits. 6.
Whatever God decrees, he freely decrees and wills, and he could not will it.
The approving will, however, necessarily loves good—by hypothetical necessity,
because this will presupposes God's free and wise decree by which he
determined that this is good. God then cannot fail to will it, that is, to
love and approve it by an act of simple complacency, although perhaps he
decreed that it should never occur. Hence God approves good according to its
essence even when it does not exist, as he approves the obedience of all, both
elect and reprobate. It may be asked why God can approve a good that never
actually exists, yet

[1651 logical printed p. 222 | IA leaf 240 | PDF p. 240]

cannot decree it. Reply. He can decree whatever he approves, but he does not
will to, because he freely decrees whatever he decrees. Yet, on the hypothesis
that something already possesses the essence of goodness through the free
decree, he cannot fail to approve it. The reason is twofold. 1. Good is an
attribute of being which accompanies being not only as it actually exists,
but also belongs to it according to its essence. Hence good according to its
essence can move the appetite of creatures and terminate God's appetite,
although it never actually exists. 2. What God by free decree willed to be
good—for example, that men worship God and honor their elders—God cannot fail
to love and approve in all, though not all actually do it, because what God
willed in the decree he cannot fail to will. Add that approval is the will's
simple complacency, by which it consents to the good as good; therefore good
according to its essence cannot fail to be approved by God. But the decreeing
of a thing is God's free volition that it should actually exist at some point
of time. Hence it is clear that God can consent by his will to a thing as good
and yet will that it never actually exist. Otherwise God would necessarily
decree and therefore effect the actual existence of all possible good things:
thousands upon thousands of worlds, angels, men, and acts of obedience of
intelligent creatures which are possible to God—which is absurd.

7. The will of sign is revealed in the Word, and we are bound to apprehend it
by faith;

[1651 logical printed p. 223 | IA leaf 241 | PDF p. 241]

for God binds us to the law and testimony, Isaiah 8. But God's decrees, as
distinguished from this will, are disclosed by the event, not properly by the
Word, except insofar as a decree passes into the will of sign. Thus when God
commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son, Abraham was not bound to believe that
Isaac's sacrifice had been decreed and intended by God, but that it was his
duty to sacrifice Isaac. If God expressly says that he will do something,
then indeed the decree itself must be believed; but decrees properly are
revealed by the event. If I believe, then it is evident that God decreed my
faith. If someone never believes, it is evident that God never decreed that he
should believe. Hence it is clear that the Arminians weave sandy consequences
for us: God commands everyone in the visible church to believe; therefore he
conditionally, or by an antecedent will, decreed that everyone should believe.
We deny the consequence, for what God wills to occur cannot fail to occur. Nor
is any such intention written concerning me or you by name; the decree is
disclosed by the event. Thus these good men command people to believe a
falsehood, which they spitefully ascribe to us. This is our position on this
distinction.

6. From this I prove the thesis:

*God commands many things in the Word which he did not will to occur—that is,
which he never decreed or intended actually to exist.*

Allow me to begin the adversaries' case from their own

[1651 logical printed p. 224 | IA leaf 242 | PDF p. 242]

confession. Thus Vorstius: “Although perhaps God did not will that his people
be released by Pharaoh so quickly, after he had decreed fully to punish and
expose his malice, nevertheless he truly willed at last that Pharaoh perform
what he had so often commanded him.” So Corvinus: “God willed the disobedience
of Pharaoh to continue and endure, so that the punishment might be all the
more just.” It follows that God commanded Pharaoh to do things he did not will
to occur. Corvinus, trying to soften Vorstius's words, says that Vorstius's
“will” here means the same as “permit.” Is that so? His own words cry out
against it. 1. He uses the doubtful word “perhaps,” as Corvinus himself notes.
Does Vorstius believe that God perhaps permitted Pharaoh not to release the
people? On Vorstius's account God not perhaps but undoubtedly permitted it.
Corvinus therefore corrupts Vorstius's meaning and words. 2. Vorstius says,
“Nevertheless he truly willed at last that Pharaoh perform what he had so
often commanded him.” I ask Vorstius whether, according to his doctrine, God
willed the release truly not only at last but always, whenever he commanded
Pharaoh to release them, or whether he truly willed it only at last—that is,
after the tenth plague had been inflicted. Whatever he says, he will be
caught. If God did not truly will it after the first, second, and third
plagues, he commanded something he did not truly will. Yet this, on their own
admission, posits contradictory wills and hypocrisy in God; and surely “he
truly willed” is the same as saying, “Previously he did not truly will, but
only pretended.” But if he truly willed the release from the beginning and
always,

God repeatedly said to him, “Release my people,” as sacred history testifies.
Therefore God spoke hypocritically and deceitfully at least to disobedient

its own force. Corvinus says that this is hypocrisy: “if by a command God

and those made incorrigible by voluntary wickedness, as Arminius has it in the

What does Corvinus say here? After Pharaoh is disobedient, he says, God can
press the command toward an end contrary to the former end originally
intended, namely that the tyrant might be punished more justly. But this will
serve our position. Their teaching therefore comes to this: God may say one
thing and will another, and deal hypocritically and deceitfully with Pharaoh
once he is disobedient, though he cannot so deal with him while obedient. Why?
Because man sins, may God rightfully act the deceiver and sin, to the end that
the obstinate may be more justly punished? Really? Does man's sin loosen
Jehovah's reins so that he may sin? O monstrous theology! Nor can a good end
excuse an evil deed such as hypocrisy, as Jacob Arminius would have it. Here
they must come over to us or remain stuck forever, or these will be Arminian
theses:

1. *There are two contrary wills in God.*

2. *God deals hypocritically and deceitfully with the obstinate.*

3. *The obstinate are bound to believe that God speaks false and contradictory
things.*

7. But I proceed by the Scriptures. Exodus 7:2: God addresses Pharaoh that he
should release the children of Israel from his land. Verse 3: “But I will
harden Pharaoh's heart, that I may multiply my signs and wonders in the land
of Egypt.” Exodus 10:1–2, addressed to Pharaoh: “For I have hardened his heart,
etc., that you may tell your son what splendid things I have done among the
Egyptians.” Hence I argue: if God says to Pharaoh, “Release my people,” so that
the glory

[1651 logical printed p. 227 | IA leaf 245 | PDF p. 245]

of his justice, patience, and power may be revealed, and hardens Pharaoh's
heart to this end, that such glory may become known to posterity, then he does
not command with the intention or will that Pharaoh obey and be saved; nor
does he truly will that Pharaoh obey and be saved. The former end is expressly
stated in Exodus 9:13–15 and Romans 9:17; see also Ezekiel 28:22 and 27:17.
Therefore the consequent is true. I prove the major: Scripture nowhere has a
single syllable or jot about the Arminian end, namely that Pharaoh should obey
and be saved. But the end assigned here—that God should display signs, that
God's name and power should be declared, and that wrath should be manifested—
is expressly stated. God cannot both intend that Pharaoh obey and the glory of
justice not be manifested, and that he not obey and the glory of mercy be
manifested. For then there would be contrary volitions in God. Nor can it be
said that God intends either the glory of mercy or, failing that, the glory of
justice; for then God, no differently from an earthly judge, would hang in a
doubtful and suspended intention between contrary ends, and would intend what
he foreknows will never be—which is absurd. Again, when God addresses Pharaoh,
he considers him either as obedient or disobedient. Not as obedient, because
on Arminian principles he could not thus harden the heart of an obedient
Pharaoh or intend his hardening. They hold that alien to God's justice. But
after the fifth or sixth plague had been inflicted, Pharaoh cannot be
considered obedient,

[1651 logical printed p. 228 | IA leaf 246 | PDF p. 246]

but as already deserving hardening. In that state, when God commanded him to
release the people, he in reality neither willed nor intended their release,
but the addition of more signs so that God's name might be known to posterity.
The Arminians do not deny this. Let the reader please see what Jackson says to
these things. He says that Pharaoh's example is extraordinary and is set
before us so that we beware of desperate principles of trifling with God. In
the same place he says that after the seventh plague it was not God's will
that Pharaoh release the people; and yet, he says, after Pharaoh's heart had
been so hardened that he could not repent, God punished him as though it were
free and possible for him to repent and grant the people a friendly departure.
But that crude innovator's teaching comes to this: God cannot ordinarily, but
can in some extraordinary case, use deceit and Jesuitical equivocation with
men. For after the seventh plague God commanded Pharaoh, Exodus 9:15, to
release his people, and when he refused inflicted the eighth plague. Thus God
speaks to Pharaoh after the seventh plague: “I command you to release my
people; for this I will and decree. But once you have become rebellious, I
command you to release them, yet will and decree that you not release them.”
Is this for God to speak contradictions? All is well, they say: he does it
only extraordinarily. 2. The Arminians often accuse us of making God, as
avenger of sins, necessitate men to their commission. Here we have the guilty
party confessing that God punished Pharaoh after

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Divine Essence*, chapter 15, folio 2, p. 179.]

[1651 logical printed p. 229 | IA leaf 247 | PDF p. 247]

the seventh plague when he could no longer repent. Here too God will be
unjust, angry at a thing which he wills and decrees to exist—something this
good man says on p. 170 could not rightfully be done even by the inhabitants
of hell. But the case is saved by a little distinction: God is ordinarily
just, but extraordinarily unjust, and does to Pharaoh what none of hell's
inhabitants could rightfully do. 3. Pharaoh's example is not so extraordinary,
because Romans 9:17 makes it an example of all who harden themselves and are
hardened by God.

8. Deuteronomy 2:27. God speaks to Sihon through his people and requests a
friendly passage for them. I ask whether God intends that Sihon obey and keep
his land for himself. Not at all. Verse 30: he was unwilling to permit passage.
Why? Because Jehovah hardened his spirit, namely that he might hand the land
over to the people—the land God had bound himself by oath to give Abraham and
his seed. They will not say that God had contrary ends here: that when God
addressed Sihon with gentle words, he intended both that Sihon obey and keep
the land of promise, and that he not obey and give that land to Abraham's
descendants. Likewise Joshua 11:19: no city made peace with the Israelites
except the Hivites. Why was this? God offered them peace, but with the mind and
will that they embrace the offer, as the Arminians dream? Not at all. It was
from Jehovah that they hardened their hearts to resist the people, so that God
might devote them to destruction for the abominations with which they had
polluted the land.

[1651 logical printed p. 230 | IA leaf 248 | PDF p. 248]

2 Samuel 12:11. God threatens that David's bed will be defiled by Absalom's
incest, and that before the sun. Does he not intend that defilement as David's
punishment? If in that threat he does not intend it toward David, he says one
thing and intends another, which is to act the hypocrite. Meanwhile in the law
he says to Absalom's conscience, “You shall not defile your father's bed.” If
he says this to Absalom with the will and intention that Absalom obey and not
defile his father's bed, then in the threat he told David what he did not
intend, which is still absurd. Consequently, by the prohibition, “You shall
not defile your father's bed,” proposed to Absalom's conscience, God neither
willed nor intended that Absalom obey. The Arminians must therefore remain
stuck here.

9. God commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son, but neither willed nor intended
Isaac's sacrifice; he decreed that Isaac should live and be the son of
promise. Therefore God does not always will what he commands. The Arminians
say this was a test, an exploratory command, not a command of duty. Reply. 1.
The testing is the end of the command proposed by God; but whatever the end,
God cannot say one thing and will another, or deal deceitfully with the
creature for a good end. For a good end cannot make God sin, since an end is
extrinsic to the act. 2. It is false that this was not a command of duty. God
would then have commanded something contrary to the law. Yet by this very fact
that God commanded

[1651 logical printed p. 231 | IA leaf 249 | PDF p. 249]

something, it was equitable and holy. Indeed, if Abraham had refused the
sacrifice, he would have sinned grievously against the sum of the whole
Decalogue, since he would have loved his son more intensely than God. Nor can
God in testing command something which it is not the creature's duty to do.
And supposing that Abraham had refused obedience—which is possible according
to the Arminians—I ask whether Abraham would have sinned against God's decree
and eternal intention concerning Isaac's sacrifice. Believe me, they will be
silent here. God never decreed or intended any such thing. Yet it cannot be
denied that Abraham would have grievously sinned against the will of sign.
Therefore someone may sin against the will of sign by doing the very thing
which agrees perfectly with the decree and will of good pleasure. Therefore
God commands something which he does not will to occur.

10. Through Paul, 1 Thessalonians 5:4–6, and through Peter, God exhorts the
saints to expect the day of Christ's second coming. Yet he did not decree that
the day should come while they survived; indeed Paul teaches the contrary in
2 Thessalonians 2:2–3. Therefore God often proposes by command something he
has not decreed to occur. He wills only that which he approves and accepts,
and which belongs to our duty and due obligation.

11. Hence it is clear that those who explain the will of sign as God's
intention, purpose, decree, or predetermination concerning the doing of this
or that—as Thomas Jackson does with the Arminians—

places no efficiency in God, not even executively or effectively. But the
decree and will of good pleasure, although an immanent act in God no less than
the will of approval, nevertheless entails in execution that God will
actually effect what he decreed to effect. The approving will as such does not
entail this at all. Hence we set down these assertions:

1. God seriously wills—that is, loves, approves, and is carried by an act of
complacency toward—the obedience of the reprobate, insofar as it is a good and
holy thing, whether that obedience actually exists or not.

2. There is no will in God contrary to this; and after he has decreed that
this thing, not that, is moral obedience, God cannot fail seriously to will
and approve such a thing.

3. To say that God decreed or intended from eternity by exhortations and
threats that the reprobate should obey and believe, while meanwhile he
reprobated them and by that decree denied them the effectual grace by which
they would believe, would be to posit contradictory wills in God.

4. To say that God by the will of sign wills men to obey the gospel while he
is unwilling to reveal the gospel to those same men is contradictory.

4. That God intends and decrees the death of the sinner,

[1651 logical printed p. 233 | IA leaf 251 | PDF p. 251]

and that God does not delight in the sinner's death, as he swears in Ezekiel
33:11, are in no way contradictory. An earthly judge, without contradiction—
provided he can most justly determine the wills of his subjects—resolves to
manifest his justice and yet does not rejoice in the death of good men. It is
most certain that God decrees the destruction of the reprobate, and that by an
absolute will, Romans 9:11–13. Yet in Ezekiel 33 he does not delight in the
sinner's death in the sense in which the blasphemous Jews objected against
God. In verse 10, seizing an occasion from chapter 24, verse 23, they claimed
that the prophets promised them life in vain because God had said that they
would be consumed in their sins. Thus they said that under this will of divine
complacency it was impossible for them to live, even if they had repented of
their ways. “No,” God says, “this is my will of complacency: that men should
not die but, repenting, should live, and not repenting, should die.” Thus,
contrary to the opinion of many, God does not swear there simply that he does
not delight in the sinner's death, but that he does not delight in it in a
certain respect and in the blasphemous sense of the Jews who insolently
reproached Jehovah for the injustice of his ways. The argument is therefore a
transparent sophism, from what is said in a certain respect to what is said simply. Anyone can see from the text that
God is not speaking there about his decree, but about his approving and
revealed will. For he says there that he delights in the wicked person's
repenting from his way; this is precisely the revealed will. From this rebuke
(since God rebukes none but offenders) it is clear that the Jews believed God
did not delight even in the life and

salvation of those who repented, but dealt with them so legalistically and
harshly that he willed them to waste away in their sins, notwithstanding that
he had determined to promise them life through the prophets and had appointed
them as watchmen for this purpose. God solemnly affirms under the sacred oath
of a covenant that he does not will or delight in the destruction of sinners
in this manner. But if God wills everyone's salvation and no one's
destruction until a person becomes utterly wicked, it follows that his love
and will to save cease only when wicked acts begin, and that God's will
changes. What says Jackson here? It is not the will or decree that changes, he
says, but the object of the decree. But I ask whether God decrees life for
Pharaoh, Saul, and Judas until they fall into the gravest sins, and only then
begins to decree their destruction. I also ask the ground of justice in that
decree by which he decrees life for those who never believe—for Pharaoh never
believed. The Arminians do not wish the decree of election to be devoid of
justice. Yet, they say, God loves the salvation of all by a natural and
antecedent love insofar as they are human beings. Is that so? Love with
respect to effect, not affection, is in God. But with respect to affection it
is nothing other than an act of the divine will by which he wills good to the
creature. Does God will eternal life to a man merely insofar as he is a
creature? Is it a just

love to will the crown to one who has not yet entered the contest? For a man
as man or as creature does not believe. This love, on Arminian principles,
opposes God's justice.

12. I confess that it seems very hard to human reason that God approves and
commands in the Word, with respect to its essence, what with respect to its
existence he neither wills nor ever decrees will occur. But you will say: the
Jerusalemites are punished because their actual gathering to Christ does not
actually exist. For by an approving will they willed their gathering to the
Son of God with respect to its essence, and did not deny that by duty and
obligation they owed obedience to God; thus their will conformed to God's
approving will. But they did not will their own gathering with respect to its
existence; and God likewise did not will that gathering with respect to its
existence. Their will therefore conformed also to God's decree. Why, then,
were those punished who in every respect willed what God willed? Reply. 1.
God's approving will also approves existing obedience, although its essence is
preserved in the simple love of obedience whether existing or nonexisting. 2.
It is the duty of the reprobate both to will and actually to perform, so that
their obedience may actually exist; and God wills this to be the duty of the
reprobate, that they should actually both will and perform obedience, although
he himself did not decree from eternity the existence of that obedience.
Because the reprobate most freely fail to perform it, they are rightfully

[1651 logical printed p. 236 | IA leaf 254 | PDF p. 254]

punished. You will say, then, that God's approving will is still not fulfilled,
because the reprobate do not actually perform the duty whose existence God
approves; but it is absurd for God's will not to be fulfilled. Reply. God's
approval as such does not formally terminate in the existence of obedience as
though God had decreed actually to cause that existence. The approving will
only applauds obedience, existing or nonexisting, as a good and just thing.
Because the reprobate cannot make this thing cease to be pleasing and
acceptable to the God who approves it, they do not—and cannot—contravene God's
approving will. Nor is it enough that the reprobate approve the good which God
approves. God may approve a thing which he is not bound to effect without
violating any duty, because God is not subject to law; the law does not demand
from God the effecting of the good he approves. But the law demands of man
that he both approve and actually effect the good.

14. Further, although men are subject to a law in expressing their thoughts,
so that without the stain of deceit they cannot speak otherwise than their
words, as ordinary symbols of the mind, convey, yet what audacity it is to bind

properly in God, why not also the stirring of the inward parts, Jeremiah
31:20; weariness, Isaiah 1:24; or depression of soul, Judges 10:16? Arminian
dogmas tend toward fashioning God for us, with the Anthropomorphites, as
human, Epicurean, mutable, and subject to passions. 3. I ask whether there is
something involuntary in the fruitless wishes which they ascribe to God. If
there is, they must ascribe no small imperfection to God: that the thing whose
contrary God wills to occur nevertheless occurs. Suárez strives vigorously,
but in vain, to avoid this. If, however, there is nothing involuntary in God,
those wishes do not posit that God wills and wishes for what does not occur.
