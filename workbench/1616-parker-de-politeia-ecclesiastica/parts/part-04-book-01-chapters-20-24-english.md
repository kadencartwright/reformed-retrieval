CHAPTER XX.

The Second Delay: Discipline Does Not Suit These Times.

The second kind of opponents are those who attack not so much the necessity of
reformation in discipline itself as its suitability for these times. Among them our Dr.
Some speaks thus: “I would gladly know whether it is possible or safe to plant
discipline in this land before God’s sacred truth is both soundly taught and received,
and suitable ecclesiastical rulers and a people fit for exercising discipline are
found.” But I, for my part, would gladly know whether this is not precisely the Jews’
delay which Haggai rebukes: “The time has not yet come for the house of God to be
built,” chapter 1, verse 2. Is it not precisely that idleness which Solomon judges
intolerable in a farmer, Ecclesiastes 11:4, who lets the seed slip from his hands while
he waits for more favorable weather?

Woe to those who do the Lord’s work negligently. God’s business is not to be done
slowly. We should instead imitate the Apostles, who never planted Churches without
establishing discipline together with doctrine, without delay, in their very infancy,
Acts 14:20 and Titus 1:5. When this diligence and prudence of the Spirit is neglected at
the beginnings of Churches, those responsible vainly await a more suitable time for
introducing discipline later. Meanwhile they inflict a wound which can either never be
healed or be healed only too late. Homer therefore fittingly makes Ate, who brings
injuries upon mortals, a winged and swift goddess, but introduces the Prayers, whose
duty is to repair those injuries, walking only with a slow step and following far
behind.

Indeed, the experience of every age has confirmed how much this delay in reforming
discipline is to be feared. Emperor Sigismund pressed for the reform of discipline at
the Council of Constance. The Pope answered that he strongly approved his plan and
would assist it with all his power, but that there was not now enough time left for so
great a matter. More important things, he said, had to be completed first; when these
were finished he would remember this reformation. What happened? The pious Emperor was
evaded, the godly were disappointed, and in the end a ridiculous mouse emerged.

The princes had assembled for the sake of reformation at Nuremberg in 1523. Pope
Adrian VI wrote to them thus: “No one ought to wonder if he does not see every error and
abuse corrected immediately. The disease is too deeply rooted; it is not simple but
varied and manifold. Its cure must proceed step by step, and the more serious and
dangerous problems must be met first, lest in wishing to reform everything at once we
throw everything into confusion.” Here the Pope sold smoke—

[Marginal references: *Treatise on the Decisions of Certain Questions*, page 36;
Homer, *Iliad*, book 9; *Catalogue of Witnesses*, page 1876.]

[1638 control | printed p. 55 | BSB canvas 65 | bsb10507786_00065]

—for by that delay all the plans which godly princes were considering for reforming
the Church then went up in smoke. This is therefore carnal human prudence, and it must
not be tolerated. Some says that suitable rulers and a people fit for exercising
discipline are lacking. After so many years? Why, I ask, are they lacking? How also
does it happen that after the Gospel has been preached for a long time its truth is
still neither soundly received nor taught? Is it not because that discipline is absent
through which the preaching of the Gospel is made effective?

Let those things which Christ himself writes in his Apocalypse to our England—the
counterpart of the Church of Laodicea—be carefully weighed, Revelation 3:14–15 and
following. He says that he has shown himself the faithful witness in England, and
complains only of this: that although doctrine has been received, discipline is so
exiled that only a certain lukewarm reformation has been accomplished, hanging midway
between Rome and the reformed Churches. Thus much for the second delay in reforming
discipline.

CHAPTER XXI.

The Third Delay—That Discipline Is Already Perfect in England—Is Removed: First, by
Exposing Its Disease.

The third is the most obstructive of all. “Many,” someone says, “would reach perfection
if they did not already believe that they had reached it.” Very many of our people
believe that they have already reached perfection of reformation; the result is that
we must despair of their ever reaching it. The Hierarchists’ own writings sufficiently
display this disease of self-love in our Church. For when the King proposed the matter
of reformation at the Hampton Court Conference, John Whitgift sprang forward, fell to
his knees, and solemnly took it upon his soul that there was then nothing in the English
Church which needed reformation.

Richard Bancroft says: “Our ecclesiastical government in England is truly apostolic and
is to be preferred by many degrees to every government used anywhere in all
Christendom today.” George Downham similarly writes: “There is no Church under the sun
which comes so near the pattern of the first, Apostolic Church in the substance of
doctrine and the form of discipline as these Churches of ours which lie under your
gracious rule.” Is that truly so? So much Adonijah-like boasting, and such blindness
amid the most
destitute poverty! “Indeed,” says George Downham, “the discipline of the reformed
Churches is only silver, but our English discipline is gold.” A truly golden dream.
Wake up, then. You will hear Christ complain that his golden discipline is absent from
England, the counterpart of Laodicea, and when you have awakened you will say: It was a
dream.

Nor is there any reason in what you say: that since England’s discipline is golden,
the contrary discipline among the reformed Churches can be silver. That discipline
must be hay and stubble which is built apart from—

[Marginal references: preface to the *Survey*, to the reader; dedicatory epistle to the
King; book 4, chapter 6, section 70, page 145.]

[1638 control | printed p. 56 | BSB canvas 66 | bsb10507786_00066]

—the foundation, without any foundation in the divine Word, 1 Corinthians 3:12. But if
you are afraid to say this of the reformed Churches, why
are you not afraid to call England’s discipline golden—especially since England could
not be poor if it possessed as gold the discipline in which it is deficient? In truth,
it has neither golden nor silver discipline, since, as you yourself confess, it is not
prescribed from the divine Word. That Word is the sole standard of every right and
lawful reformation, as Zanchi testifies.

Writing to Lord Knollys on the reformation of the Church, Zanchi introduces as its rule
the saying of an orthodox man in Theodoret: “I obey divine Scripture alone.” He then
descends specifically to the reformation of discipline, of which he says: “Churches
must be reformed according to its best form. No better form of the Church can be found
or conceived than that which Christ, through himself and his Apostles, established and
confirmed for the Church at its beginning.” And a little later: “All teachings of
worship and discipline must be tested not by the Lesbian rule of human judgment, but by
the Lydian touchstone of the divine Word.”

How can the English reformation answer this rule, especially in the judgment of
Downham and the others? They wish discipline to be mutable according to the flexible
Lesbian rule of human judgment, and affirm that there is no Lydian touchstone in the
Word by which discipline may be tested. This is the first defect of the English
reformation.

[Marginal references: Zanchi, *On the True Manner of Reforming the Church*; Theodoret.]

CHAPTER XXII.

Second, by Bringing It Back to the Standard of the Primitive Church.

The second defect sins against that second rule of Zanchi which he next proposes about
imitating the primitive Church, differing from the first perhaps not so much in
substance as in its rationale. “What the form of the ancient and Apostolic Church was
in the time of Christ and the Apostles,” he says, “appears clearly and certainly from
the books of the Evangelists and Apostles, and with probability from the remaining most
ancient ecclesiastical writers after the Apostles. Discipline must therefore be
reformed according to that form of the Church delivered in sacred Scripture, and then
according to that which appears in the most ancient writers, insofar as the latter does
not conflict with the former.”

This accords with Isaiah 1: “I will smelt away your dross to purity and remove all your
tin. I will restore your judges as they were before and your elders as in ancient days;
after this you shall be called the city of justice, the faithful city.” Jeremiah 6:16
similarly says: “Thus says the Lord: Stand in the ways and look around; ask after the
ancient paths, which is the good way.” Finally, when Christ was to reform marriage in
Matthew 19, he recalled the Jews to the first institution of marriage, saying: “But
from the beginning—”

[Marginal reference: in Hadrian Saravia, *On the Threefold Episcopate*, page 104.]

[1638 control | printed p. 57 | BSB canvas 67 | bsb10507786_00067]

“—it was not so.” The necessity of this rule shone so clearly upon Downham and Saravia
that they saw no
way to defend the English reformation unless they advertised it as apostolic and
primitive. Yet Whitgift, Lous, Bridges, Sutcliffe, and others show how frivolous and
boastful—how truly Laodicean—this claim is. Since they saw that England differed from
the Apostolic and firstborn form of the Church, they found no way to excuse it except
upon this foundation: that we are not bound by the primitive Church’s example and that
discipline is mutable and ambulatory, so that it is lawful to depart from the ancient
Church as each Church judges suitable.

Thus John Whitgift writes: “This is the Puritans’ rotten foundation: that we must retain
the same discipline which existed in the Apostles’ times.” Again: “Many things which
the reformers devise were plainly in use in the Apostles’ times.” Again: “The difference
between the Apostles’ times and ours requires a different form of government.” And:
“In the Apostles’ time the state of the Church was democratic, because there was then
no Magistrate; it is not so today.” Once more: “The alteration of ecclesiastical
government is well expressed by Ambrose on Ephesians 4: At the beginning everyone was
permitted to preach and baptize, but when the Church multiplied, other governors were
ordained in the Church. Therefore the Apostles’ writings do not agree in every respect
with the order which is now in the Church.” George Major cites and approves this
position from Rabanus in his commentary on Philippians 1.

Thomas Cartwright cites the Helvetic, Zurich, Bernese, Genevan, Polish, Hungarian, and
Scottish confession. Treating the equality of ministers in chapter 18, it says that
“no one forbids us to return to the ancient constitution of the Church and to follow
it rather than human custom.” Whitgift opposes this testimony and evades it by this
device: “This confession says only that we may return; it does not say that we must.”
Again: “The Apostles’ times are no more to be followed in the election of ministers
than in the community of all goods. I add that although that manner of electing
ministers was suitable and useful in the Apostles’ times, it would now be most
destructive and harmful in the Church’s present condition.”

Lous sings the same tune. He constructs a long argument to prove that the Apostles
prescribed no definite, immutable manner of governing the Church. He affirms that the
Church itself taught this, since from the Apostles’ times onward it admitted various
kinds of administration. John Bridges follows the same position: “The first government
of the primitive Church has not been restored in every respect among the reformed.
Judgment is made by laws, not examples. Although it had been restored by our neighbors,
we are not bound to follow their example; indeed, we are not bound even to follow the
example of the primitive Church itself.” Again: “They can perhaps show some example
from the primitive Church concerning certain things, although it contains neither a
general nor a perpetual—”

page 174; the same, chapter 6, division 5, page 182; the same, chapter 6,

chapter 4, division 4, page 61, and division 8, page 169; Lous, *Ecclesiastical

“—rule which binds every age and Church to its example.” Again: “Does every single
example of the primitive Church create a prescription or have equal force with a rule
and command? If it does not have equal force, how can it bind us?”

There is no need to speak of Matthew Sutcliffe, since he devoted the whole thirteenth
chapter to proving that the Church’s government is mutable because no definite rule
for it can be sought either from the Word or from the first Church’s example. Richard
Bancroft does not so much argue as mock and slander in his usual manner. “We live,” he
says, “in an age in which people are accustomed to repeat, ‘The first institution! The
first institution!’ The words are good if they are applied well. But something far too
hidden lay in that priest’s application when, among a rebellious crowd of commoners,
he cried out: ‘When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then a gentleman?’”

I have been more generous with these testimonies so that no one might think I wronged
the Hierarchists by saying that they deny—or, what is more, attack—the imitation of the
primitive Church in reforming ecclesiastical polity. Although reciting these statements
is sufficient to refute them, their error still seems to require fuller exposure by
the addition of certain reasons.

CHAPTER XXIII.

The Opponents Are Accused of Montanism, and Discipline Is Proved to Require Formation
According to the Standard of the Primitive Church.

First, then, I say that this attack upon the primitive Church gives sail to Montanism.
It raises the far from slight suspicion that the primitive Church was rougher and more
imperfect, so that Churches now brought to maturity are not bound to imitate its
infancy. Whitgift diligently tries to turn aside the suspicion of this heresy and
therefore infers: “It is no disparagement of the Apostolic Church if its ordinances are
changed in certain matters. Although they were suitable for that time, they are not
suitable for this age in which we live. Thus the form of the Apostolic Church could
have been perfect and complete, although it now admits alteration in certain
circumstances.”

But a Montanist could have answered for himself in the same manner: that the Apostolic
Church was indeed perfect for its infant and childish age, though imperfect in
comparison with the manly and mature age which followed. Indeed, one may infer that our
adversaries smell more than enough of Montanus’s principles from the fact that Whitgift
was not afraid to take refuge in Tertullian at the very place where he displays deeply
impressed traces of his Montanism. Against Cartwright, Whitgift proves that discipline
is mutable from this testimony of Tertullian: “While the rule of faith remains—”

[Marginal references: Whitgift, tractate 3, chapter 6, division 14, page 192;
tractate 2, chapter 2, division 1, page 10; Tertullian, *On the Veiling of Virgins*,
chapter 1.]

[1638 control | printed p. 59 | BSB canvas 69 | bsb10507786_00069]

“—other matters of discipline and conduct admit the novelty of correction, as God’s grace
works and perfects them to the end.”

See the Hierarchists’ foundations for proving that the rule of faith is immutable but
discipline is not. Is this statement of Tertullian not thoroughly Montanist? Does it not
touch the very heart of Montanism when he dreams of a richer grace of God in succeeding
ages than was poured out in the Apostles’ times? In the words immediately following he
compares the Apostolic Church to a budding tree which had to be brought to maturity by
the coming of the Paraclete. Since human limitation could not grasp everything at once,
it would be brought to perfect discipline in the following age by that vicar of the
Lord, the Holy Spirit. These are openly Montanist claims.

Second, sacred Scripture itself makes brilliantly clear that the primitive Church was
so perfect that it bound every succeeding age to the same form of discipline. Why
would Paul press the custom of the first Churches if they did not bind us to imitation?
“The Churches of God have no such custom,” 1 Corinthians 11:16. “As we see in all the
Churches of the saints,” 1 Corinthians 14:33. “You have known my conduct in the
Churches,” 2 Timothy
3:14. What—so that Timothy might cast it behind his back? No, Paul immediately adds:
“Continue in the things which you have learned.” So in Philippians 4:9 he commands them
to attend to the things which they had seen in him or heard about him. Each of these
passages plainly shows that not only the Apostles’ command but also their example,
together with the example of the first Churches in matters pertaining to discipline,
binds in perpetuity.

In Revelation 2:5 the Ephesian Church and its counterpart—the first Church failing
around the year 300—are commanded: “Do the former works.” What do you conclude from
this, you ask? That primitive government is common to every time and place; that in
reforming the Church one must return to first principles; and that the discipline of
the whole world and of one city is the same, both under a Christian magistrate and
when there was no magistrate. The passage concerns the correction of discipline.
Although in the Ephesian counterpart Constantine was already at the threshold, about
to take up the reins as Christian Emperor, Christ nevertheless presses the ancient
discipline of the first Church which he had instituted through the Apostles.

“Remember how you have received and heard; observe it and repent,” Revelation 3:3. Does
Christ not here warn the Sardians to return to the first institution and amend what has
collapsed according to that standard alone? If, as Brightman thinks, the Lutheran
Church is the counterpart of this Sardian Church, the same principle of reformation
applies. Every teaching which the Lutheran Church first received from Luther was formed
according to the first restoration, as is clear from Luther’s own declaration: “I wish
them to be assured that I am utterly unwilling to be compelled by the authority of any
father, however holy, except insofar as it is proved by the judgment of divine
Scripture.” Revelation 12 describes the state of the Church before Constantine, under
Constantine, and after—

[Marginal reference: Luther, preface to the assertions of the articles condemned by
Leo X’s bull.]

[1638 control | printed p. 60 | BSB canvas 70 | bsb10507786_00070]

—Constantine. I therefore ask: Does the Holy Spirit propose the state of the Church which began
under Constantine for imitation? Certainly not. It was then that the Church began to
be driven into the wilderness, Revelation 12:6 and 14. Nor must we imitate that later
part of the Church which immediately preceded Constantine, which had been attacked by
the Dragon with all his strength and had a third of the stars cast from heaven to earth
by his tail, verse 4. We must imitate only that first part, adorned with its twelve
stars—that is, the Apostles—and shining like the sun with the truth delivered by
Christ, verse 1. This is the Church which is called God’s paradise in Revelation 2:7.

This refutes Bancroft’s argument, if it is an argument, which we touched upon above.
Just as we ought to aspire to the first happiness of Adam and Eve, so it is proper to
cut away the thistles and thorns of later ages in every reformation of the Church, so
that the first purity, integrity, and loveliness of the Church may be restored.

CHAPTER XXIV.

The Same Is Confirmed by Human Testimonies.

Third, the Fathers and all learned writers of every age and region teach that we must
return to the standard of the primitive Church. Tertullian’s entire purpose in his
*Prescriptions against Heretics* is to prove that one must return to the first sources.
The orthodox therefore have the stronger right because they are the Apostles’ heirs,
because they existed from the beginning, and because they did not move the ancient
boundaries. Against Marcion he writes: “In sum, if it is agreed that what is earlier is
truer, that what is from the beginning is earlier, and that what is from the Apostles
is from the beginning, it will equally be agreed that whatever was sacred and
inviolable among the Apostles’ Churches was delivered by the Apostles.”

Cyprian similarly writes: “If a water channel which at first flowed copiously and
abundantly should suddenly fail, does one not go to the spring? God’s priests must do
the same now, keeping the divine commands, so that if truth has altered or wavered in
any respect, we may return to the Lord’s origin and the Apostolic tradition. Thus the
principle of our origin may arise from the same place from which its order and origin
arose.” Jewel recalled Harding to the primitive Church after Cyprian’s example; why not
recall those Hierarchists too? That Cyprian understood these things as much of
discipline as of doctrine is clear from this alone: elsewhere he recalls deacons to that
first institution of deacons in Acts 6 and commands them to remember it.

Vincent of Lérins wrote a book against the profane novelties of the heretics. Its sum
is that the more recent anything is and the further it departs from the orthodox
consensus of godly antiquity, the less it accords with truth. But lest either the
Papist or the Hierarchists think that by saying this of the Fathers he means they are
to be received on some authority of their own, I will not hesitate to add certain of
his words. The Apostle says, “Guard the deposit”; Bilson and Saravia teach that
discipline lies hidden in Timothy’s deposit. “What is the deposit? What was entrusted
to you, not what you invented; what you received, not what you devised; a thing not of
ingenuity but of teaching, not of private usurpation but of public tradition; a thing
brought—”

[Marginal references: Tertullian, *On Prescription*, chapters 37–38; *Against
Marcion*, book 4, chapter 5; Cyprian, *Letter to Pompeius*; Jewel, *Apology*, part 2,
division 1, page 25; Cyprian, book 3, letter 9; canon law, distinction 93.]

[1638 control | printed p. 61 | BSB canvas 71 | bsb10507786_00071]

“—down to you, not brought forth by you. In it you ought not to be an author but a
guardian, not an originator but a follower, not leading but following. You received
gold; return gold. I do not want you to substitute one thing for another. I do not want
you shamelessly to put lead in place of gold, or fraudulently to substitute ornaments.
I do not want the appearance of gold, but its true nature.”

Isidore of Pelusium touches our matter more closely. He therefore defends Hierax for
declining the episcopate, because the episcopate had degenerated from the primitive
Church’s simplicity. He writes: “You perhaps seem unaware that the dignity of
gentleness and meekness has passed into tyranny. For if you can show that the ancient
manner of feeding the flock still flourishes, it is certainly fair for you to praise
the man who judged himself unequal to this task. But if matters have turned to the
contrary, they are hardly to be sought from him,” and so forth.

Does Bernard not strive everywhere to recall the discipline of the Church, fallen in
his age, to the first Church’s purity, simplicity, and integrity? “Who will grant me,”
he says, “before I die, to see God’s Church as in ancient days, when the Apostles let
down their nets for a catch—not a catch of gold or silver, but a catch of souls? How I
wish you to inherit the voice of the man whose seat you have obtained: ‘May your money
perish with you.’” Elsewhere: “When did any Apostle ever sit as a judge, a divider of
boundaries, or a distributor of lands? I read that the Apostles stood to be judged; I
do not read that they sat in judgment.” Elsewhere: “You go forth as a gilded pastor,”
and so forth. Did Peter act in this way? Did Paul play this game? Elsewhere: “Before
you there were men who devoted themselves wholly to feeding the sheep. Where, I ask,
is that custom now?” He adds: “The pursuits are very different and have changed far in
another direction—and would that it were not for the worse.”

What does this have to do with England, you ask? As though it never happens today that
one longs to see God’s Church in England as it was in ancient days? As though, contrary
to primitive Church custom, the English Bishops do not also sit as judges in earthly
matters? As though gain, pomp, and neglect of preaching the Gospel do not prevail, and
the primitive Church’s custom has not changed for the worse? Why should we not also
imitate the Waldenses? They judged that every matter of discipline should be recalled
to the primitive Church’s pattern, as Chytraeus demonstrates at length.

In a sermon delivered before the Pope, Nicolas Oresme has much to say about restoring
the primitive Church’s integrity. “You have not remembered the days of your youth”—that
is, the Church’s first state. “For it would come to pass that the prelates would not be
afraid to disgrace the venerable chastity of the primitive Church.” Durand of Mende,
shortly before the Council of Vienne, wrote at Clement V’s command about holding a
council. He judges that everything should be reformed according to the pattern of the
primitive Church: “Indeed, recalling how this sacred planting of the Church was
instituted, we can sorrowfully say that, although not everyone has departed from that
holy institution of the primitive Church, nor in every or most matters, the departure
nevertheless seems so great in many matters that some of us appear to have forgotten
the first institution.”

Nilus of Thessalonica, whom many think to be Nicholas Cabasilas, wrote against the
Pope’s primacy. Everywhere he strikes it down with the argument that it has departed
from the primitive Church’s practice. Peter Damian was a most ardent defender of—

[Marginal references: Isidore of Pelusium, letter 223; Bernard, letter 137; *On
Consideration*, books 2 and 4; Chytraeus, *Chronological Lectures*; *Catalogue of
Witnesses*, book 18, page 1779; Durand of Mende, preface to part 2; Nilus of
Thessalonica, *On Primacy*, book 1, chapter 29.]

—discipline according to the primitive Church’s pattern. “Peter, prince of the Apostles,” he says,
“did not resist his subjects’ complaint by an appeal to his power. When he was
questioned about entering among the Gentiles, he humbly satisfied them by giving an
account.” This is spoken against the English Bishops, who refuse to be subject to their
presbyteries.

Rodrigo Sánchez de Arévalo, bishop of Zamora, lived under Paul II and in his *Mirror of
Human Life* sets forth the primitive Church as a mirror of reformation. “In the
primitive Church,” he says, “many true saints flourished. I regard them as the truest
mirror of a perfect and holy life.” Jean Gerson wrote much for the Church’s reformation.
That he wished it to be reduced to the primitive Church’s form is clear from these
words alone: “The authority of the primitive Church was greater than that of the Church
which now exists. For it is not in the power of Pope, Council, or Church to change the
traditions taught by the Evangelists and Paul, as certain men rave.” Let Downham and
all the others who contend that the Apostolic institutions of discipline are mutable
look to themselves here.

Pierre d’Ailly, at Sigismund’s command, presented articles on reforming the Church to
the Council of Constance. That he judged everything should be recalled to the pattern
of the first institution is clear from the fact that he inclined toward those who
thought the order of Cardinals should be abolished because it had been instituted by
neither Christ nor the Apostles. Nicholas of Cusa wrote the same thing at the same time,
making this the first step in reforming the Church: with the Cardinals’ authority
removed, the Church should be brought back to the state in which it existed from the
beginning. What they then argued about Cardinals, we may now argue about Bishops.
Gregory of Hamburg wrote about reforming the Church in 1434 and judged that the pattern
of the primitive Church during nearly its first three hundred years should be imitated.

Budé does two things. First, he deplores the Church’s degeneration from the ancient
Church: “The gray age—or rather decay—of the canons is now of no use, but has been
driven from the forum like a raving old woman. Canonical discipline, now older than
sixty and even six hundred years, has long since been cast from the bridge of the
Paraclete’s assemblies,” and so forth. Second, he considers discipline to have been
perfectly described by Christ and regards it as worthy of reverence: “A ship of
discipline was left to us by the Savior, the Church’s founder, furnished by him or
under his auspices with song, ministry, equipment, and miracles. He also promised a
wind from the stern which would carry the voyagers to the desired place if the Church
held the rudder straight and extended its sails to be opened to the Spirit’s breath.”

Let us come to modern writers. Nicholas Sander writes: “No more correct standard for
governing the Church can be sought from anything other than Christ’s deeds and
institutions. The whole rule of Christian government both depends upon Christ alone and
depends upon the visible and outward example of that time.” Hadrian Saravia affirms
everywhere that the polity of the primitive Church is an immutable standard. If one
departs from it, he says, the Church is deformed rather than reformed. Yet there is a
point on which both he and Sander err: one thinks the rule of immutability must be
sought from Christ’s own government and the other from the Apostles’ government itself
must be—

d’Ailly, chapter 18; Nicholas of Cusa, *On Catholic Concordance*, book 2; *Catalogue

book 2; Sander, *On the Visible Monarchy*, book 1, chapter 6.]

—sought, as was demonstrated above. William Whitaker writes: “The council
held in Acts 15 ought to be the standard for every other council; all the rest must
resemble it.” John Reynolds teaches from 1 Corinthians 14 that spirits are to be judged
by the agreement of the Churches from which the Word of God went forth and to which it
came in the first Church. The Hierarchists therefore err when they deny that
reformation must necessarily be made according to the pattern of the first Church.

Thus much for the second defect in the English reformation of discipline: namely, that
on the confession of at least some of its opponents it has not been formed according
to the pattern of the primitive Church.
