[Marginalia: Lecture VII. Transition.]

And so you see that God’s gracious purpose to make us accepted and adopt us
in Christ is actually included and necessarily presupposed in this grace of
adoption. It remains for us to gather several observations from these three
verses as thus expounded, according to the method of teaching established
above.

First, from the fountain of all spiritual blessings which the Apostle here
traces so far back, there meets us the most certain and firmest foundation of
our salvation. God himself laid it in and with himself before all the ages,
before he laid the foundations of the world: namely, his eternal decree to
choose us in Jesus Christ, adopt us as sons through him, and take us up to
attain life and salvation through and in him. From this eternal counsel of God
it is first evident in the general proposition that our most wise God executes
nothing here in time which he did not long before—that is, from eternity—
determine and decree. Whatever he actually does and executes, in whatever
manner, by whatever means and method, and with whatever particular
circumstances of things, places, times, and persons, he decreed from eternity
to do and execute with those same circumstances most freely, wisely, holily,
justly, and steadfastly. For he blessed us in Christ just as he chose us in
him—that is, as he determined with himself from eternity to bless us in him,
to do us the greatest good, freely bestow grace, life, and eternal salvation
upon us, and so make us blessed in him.

[Marginalia: First observation. First consequence.]

Then, in the particular case before us, just as in time he sent Christ to us
as the Mediator and Redeemer of the elect, so from eternity he designated and
appointed him as our head and mediator, through whose intervention he had
decreed to unite us to himself. Hence he chose us in him before the
foundations of the world were laid—in him, I say, as head and mediator.
Christ was therefore our mediator from eternity. Thus not only before we
ourselves existed, but even before he assumed our flesh, he was always the
Mediator of his Church by this eternal ordination of God the Father. Yet this
was with respect to the human nature to be assumed in its time. In that
respect he is called “the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world” in
Revelation 13:8: namely, with respect to the flesh which he would one day
assume and the things he would undergo and suffer in the flesh. In God’s eyes,
for whom all things are present and nothing is future or past, these were of
the same price and value as if Christ had suffered, died, and been exalted to
glory from the very beginning of the world.

[Marginalia: 2. Revelation 13:8.]

Next, this same passage furnishes a very powerful argument for refuting the
errors of many. It first refutes those who, seized by a perverse fear, scarcely
dare mention eternal election at all. Or if it must be mentioned, they think
this doctrine should be confined and shut within the walls of schools,
proposed only to teachers and academic societies, but not openly proclaimed
to the people in public preaching. They suppose it contains material for
despair and anxiety and that people will seize from it an occasion for a
troubled mind, because we cannot penetrate that secret and eternal counsel of
God.

[Marginalia: Second observation. First member. Objection.]

Yet from this memorable example of Paul we learn especially the contrary. He
scarcely presses any part of saving doctrine more frequently or clearly, and
sets no topic of Christian religion or article of faith more diligently before
the faithful, as the firmest and most invincible argument of consolation. And
rightly so. What else, I ask, would it be to wish to keep silent in God’s
Church about our eternal election in Christ but to suppress the first
foundation of our salvation and thus a principal part of the gospel? Human
reason itself tells us that we truly know a thing only when we know it *a
priori*, that is, through its cause. Will we therefore ever rightly know that
God our Father has blessed us abundantly in Christ if we do not know that
eternal counsel to bless us in this way, from which the grace bestowed upon us
flowed?

[Marginalia: Answer.]

It is no wonder that many are offended by this doctrine, some taking from it
an occasion for fear and despair, and others for loosening the reins of carnal
license. The gospel itself is to many the savor of death unto death, namely,
to those who perish in their blindness and unbelief; and nothing is so sweet
and saving to chosen and devout hearts that it does not turn to the ruin of
reprobate and perverse minds. In reality the doctrine of election is so far
from being a cause for despair that, on the contrary, there is nothing in
which true faith rests more firmly and gladly. The Holy Spirit clearly teaches
and shows us in Scripture the manner of investigating its certainty and
confirming it in our hearts from effects of the kind which each and every
believer can feel and experience in himself: namely, holiness and sincere
love, which the Apostle mentions here and which truly can be found in no one
who has not been chosen for life. Consequently, as certain as we should be of
our sanctification through Christ’s Spirit, so certain we can and should be
of our eternal election. Their pretext is therefore utterly false when they
suggest that no one can be certain of his election because no one can
penetrate God’s secret counsel.

[Marginalia: Anticipation. Answer.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 61 | Caput I p. 19]

That is just as if someone were to say that no one can meet the king in his
private chamber because no one can fly into it at once, even though a person
can ascend to it by known steps and ways. So in investigating our election we
must not immediately storm the citadel of the divine counsel itself, but
begin from what is later and ascend through certain effects better known to
us. These are so many steps revealed to us by God himself in his Word and laid
down for that end; the chief of them is our sanctification, which our Apostle
proposes here for the same end.

[Marginalia: Comparison. Elaboration.]

But if we feel that these means and indications of election are very obscure
and slight in us; if we discover only very faint traces of faith, the life of
Christ, and holiness, and this examination consequently casts us into greater
doubts, then first, I ask, let us attribute the cause of this evil not to the
doctrine of free election but to ourselves and our depravity. Next, let us
neither lose heart on that account nor ever cease to devote ourselves to the
pursuit of godliness and holiness with our whole heart and all our strength,
until we feel surer testimonies of the grace of him who, as the Psalmist says,
“is near to all who call upon him, to all who call upon him in faith or
truth.”

For the question concerns neither a house nor a field nor a heap of bronze or
gold, though people of this age avoid no labor or vigilance to obtain assured
and firm possession of those things. It does not concern, I say, a trivial or
worthless matter, but the gravest matter of all and by far the greatest that
can ever concern us in life: the certainty of our election to eternal life.
Woe to us unless we carry some pledges of it in our breast; woe to us again
and again, and forever, unless we labor uniquely in this matter and set this
work and pursuit before us throughout our whole life. Otherwise, why do we
live, study, read, write, teach, and learn? Why do we do anything sooner or in
preference to this business? If it is certainly established for us, all will
go well with us; but so long as nothing is clearly established for us about
it, neither is it established whether we were born to live with Christ
forever or to be tormented forever with the devil in hell.

[Marginalia: Psalm 145:13. Exclamations.]

If we apply ourselves seriously, heartily, and constantly to this pursuit of
making our election sure and attested to us, I would dare promise every person
desirous of his salvation that such pursuit and labor cannot fail to turn out
as desired. The outcome of this work, counsel, and effort cannot fail to be
most blessed and filled with every spiritual joy and consolation. For whom
has he ever cast away, when he seeks and requires nothing else from us than
that we turn to him with our whole heart, seek, ask, and knock with tireless
zeal until he himself opens the doors of his grace to us? He stretches out
open and welcoming arms even to the stubborn and obstinate, even to those who
delay and loiter, inviting them to himself by so many means and in so many
ways, so graciously and patiently.

[Marginalia: Confidence.]

From this it is evident that the doctrine of election becomes saving for the
faithful in every way. If they feel its living and firm testimonies within
themselves through the grace and power of the Holy Spirit working powerfully
in them, all is well: they will rejoice with solid joy, acknowledging that
this work of holiness depends upon and is built upon that immovable foundation
of salvation, eternal election itself. But if everything remains obscure,
doubtful, and slight, and they find nothing solid or certain with and within
themselves to seal and, to use a lawyer’s term, certify their election, then
certainly—if they belong to God and have even a crumb of good sense—they will
seize even from this an occasion to apply themselves more keenly to
repentance, godliness, holiness, and newness of life. Nor will they ever cease
to weary God, as it were, with their most ardent prayers until, together with
Jacob, they wrest a spiritual blessing from his hand, which can be overcome
and conquered by this force alone. Through this blessing they become certain
and persuaded of their salvation and eternal election in Christ, the
remission of sins, their adoption, and God’s favor and paternal grace—all of
which follow and accompany one another in an indivisible bond. I wished to
pursue this more fully because the hinge of our salvation turns here, and
here lies the firmest foundation of all our joy and consolation both in life
and in death.

[Marginalia: Conclusion. Entreaty.]

Second, because we are said here to be chosen so that we might be holy and
blameless, the impious invention of the most perverse people concerning
foreseen faith, holiness, and good works is refuted. By these the ancient
Pelagians, and the modern Semi-Pelagians even now, attacked God’s free grace
at the very foundation of election and attempted to weaken and undermine that
foundation, if not by open force, at least by oblique tunnels, as it were.
They asserted that we were chosen by God from foreseen faith, good works, and
holiness of life. For they said that God foresaw and foreknew from eternity
which people would believe the gospel and which would not, which would live
well and holily and which would persist in sins. He therefore rejected the
latter but chose the former because of the good works and holiness of life
which he foresaw would be in them. The impious Pelagians of old called this
the meritorious cause of election. Those who have refurbished their heresy,
the modern Ubiquitarians, perversely teach and speak of foreseen faith as the
instrumental cause of election, since they are ashamed to call it
meritorious.

[Marginalia: Second member. Note the impious opinion.]

Others add faith as a condition of election, asserting that God chose no one
absolutely, but under the condition of faith—that is, if they believed or
would believe. Foolish people! They do not know how to distinguish the
external calling—the promulgation of the gracious covenant in which salvation
and forgiveness of sins are offered to everyone who believes, that is, if
they believe—from God’s eternal and secret decree by which he determined
with himself whom he had chosen for life and therefore also for faith. Or, if
they do know, they are certainly perverse. Thus we see that falsehood never
holds consistently together.

But in this passage the Apostle says that we were chosen not because we would
be holy or because God foresaw that we would be such, but so that we might be
holy; not because we would do good works, but so that we might do them and
walk in them; and finally, not because we would be faithful, but so that we
might become faithful. So the Apostle expressly declares of himself in 1
Corinthians 7:25 that he obtained mercy so that he might become faithful, not
because he was faithful. Faith itself is among God’s gifts, as Augustine
frankly acknowledges in book 1 of *The Predestination of the Saints*,
correcting an error he had once held. From where, I ask, do faith,
sanctification, good works, or anything of this kind come to us except from
God’s free gift and his free election? For because he chose us for life—

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 7:25. Augustine.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 62 | Caput I p. 20]

—he therefore also gave us belief, gave us the will, and will give us
completion. This accords with Philippians 1:29: “It has been freely given to
you in Christ’s cause not only to believe in him but also to suffer for him.”
And, as the following chapter puts it, God gave both willing and completing
according to his free benevolence. Finally, nothing is clearer or more
explicit than the testimony cited from Acts 13:48: “As many as were ordained
to eternal life believed.” Faith is plainly subordinated there to election
or ordination to salvation as an effect flowing from that cause.

[Marginalia: Philippians 1:29 and 2:13. Acts 13:48.]

Third, from this same passage we also meet the profane thoughts of impure
people who, under the pretext of eternal election, intend to loosen the reins
of carnal license. Because they hear that all who will be saved were chosen
for life from eternity, they conclude that they should live securely as they
please. But here we are taught that first of all we were chosen for this very
purpose: to be holy and blameless before God in love. Consequently, no one
can attain the further end of election, eternal salvation, unless he first
passes through this means of holiness. It is certain that he who chose us for
the end also chose us for the means through which that end is attained.
Among these means holiness of person and life stands foremost, “without
which,” says the Apostle in Hebrews 12:14, “no one will see the Lord.” Indeed,
what else is our sanctification in reality but the prelude and firstfruits of
our glorification? What else is it but the first rudiment of the image of
Jesus Christ which is to be fully and perfectly restored in us, and a begun
conformity with our head? The Apostle elsewhere says that God our Father
predestined us to this in that golden chain or series of the causes and means
of our salvation, Romans 8:29: “Those whom he foreknew,” he says, “he also
predestined.” To what? “To be conformed to the image of his Son, so that he
might be the firstborn among many brothers.”

[Marginalia: Third member. Hebrews 12:14. Romans 8:29.]

Knowledge and certainty of the end is therefore so far from producing
carelessness about the means, or contempt and neglect of them, that, on the
contrary, the greater the desire with which someone is held to attain the end
itself, the more he should be inflamed to pursue the very means which were
ordained from eternity for that end, as we see from this passage. If I
earnestly wish to arrive somewhere and reach a certain place, what, I ask,
would I do sooner or in preference to entrusting myself to the road leading
to that place and preparing myself at once for the journey, certain that
otherwise I would never arrive at that place and desired goal? Thus this
passage has answered various corruptions of human depravity.

[Marginalia: Comparison.]

Third, we should observe here a necessary connection between that inward
holiness and sincerity of soul in God’s sight and love, which regards our
neighbor. God is owed the soul’s inward sincerity and the integrity of the
understanding, heart, will, and affections, as a certain *logikē latreia*, or
spiritual worship. In the same way, the affection of Love which the Apostle
commends here regards our brothers and neighbor. I mean that affection of the
regenerate soul which springs as from a root from a sincere love of God in
Christ and drives us to exercise works of holiness and righteousness toward
others. Thus it in some way applies and turns to the use of our brothers and
neighbor, through good works, whatever good or divine gift is in us—that is,
whatever has been bestowed upon us by God’s grace, whether faith, knowledge,
holiness, zeal, resources, assistance, or anything else. From this impulse of
love we render all of it useful and fruitful to our brothers.

[Marginalia: Third observation. The nature of love.]

From this we learn in passing the nature and native property of love. It does
not constitute, even in part, the nature or essence of the things which act
through it, and therefore it is falsely called by our adversaries the form of
faith, that is, its very essence. Rather, it is an effective instrument
through which we apply faith, knowledge, holiness, and every other talent of
divine grace to the use and edification of our neighbor. Hence the Apostle
says in 1 Corinthians 8:1 that love edifies, that is, it refers and adapts
every gift to our neighbor’s benefit. Faith is an instrument which apprehends
Christ crucified with his benefits and applies him to us for life and
righteousness, and therefore properly regards our own good. Love, by
contrast, is an instrument in us effective toward others; it applies and
communicates to others every good thing in us, diffuses itself to those
outside us, namely our brothers and neighbors, and therefore properly regards
the good of another.

[Marginalia: Comparison between faith and love.]

The nature of faith differs from that of love as much as receiving, applying,
and apprehending for oneself differs from giving to another, communicating,
and extending and diffusing oneself to another. Those who make love the form
and soul of faith—that is, its inward nature and essence—therefore act most
foolishly, since on the contrary it is faith’s effective instrument toward
our neighbor. Faith is said to be effective or to work through love because
faith exercises no act toward our neighbor except through love. This is just
as if they were to say that the hand is the form and essence of the soul
because the soul or the person himself offers something to another and acts
toward another through the hand. Faith itself is the soul’s hand or mouth, or
something of that kind, with respect to Christ whom faith receives and
apprehends. So love is the hand of the soul with respect to one’s neighbor,
toward whom it exercises itself through works of love and whom it pursues with
a devout affection of love. When our soul turns itself to God and Christ, it
has faith ready as a hand which it offers and extends all the way to Christ,
though he dwells in heaven, so that it may apprehend him for itself and apply
him most closely. But when it turns itself to brothers and neighbors as
members of its Christ, it extends to them its other hand, Love, and with it
heartily embraces, cherishes, helps, favors, bears, sustains, and pursues them
with every duty in and for Christ.

[Marginalia: Comparison. Conclusion.]

Fourth, because he says that God predestined us in himself to be adopted as
sons to himself through Christ according to the good pleasure of his will,
and did so to the praise of his glorious grace, we plainly deduce these two
things. First, the first principle, cause, and fountain of our election is in
God himself and in him alone. It should not be sought, nor can it be found,
anywhere outside him in any creature. Hence in Romans 9 the Apostle rightly
deduces the firmness of our election: “So that,” he says, “the purpose of God
according to his election”—that is, “not from works, but from him who
calls”—“might remain firm.” By God’s election he means not only an election
which comes from God as its efficient cause and of which—

[Marginalia: Fourth observation. First member. Romans 9:11.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 63 | Caput I p. 21 (through Lecture VII close)]

—God is the author, but also one which has no cause outside God upon which it
could depend in any way. From this he teaches that the election, or that
purpose to choose us, remains firm because it is not from works but from the
one who calls—that is, from God’s bare will and free benevolence—and depends
upon no cause outside him. It follows from the impious doctrine of those who
seek the cause of election outside God, for example in ourselves, in foreseen
faith and our works, and in other things of this kind, that God’s purpose
according to election cannot remain firm, because on this account it is made
to depend not so much upon the one who calls as upon the works or foreseen
faith of those called. Consequently, so far as it lies with them, such people
utterly overthrow and subvert all the firmness of our election when they put
the most unstable sand beneath it as a foundation in place of this immovable
rock.

[Marginalia: Consequence.]

Second, all the causes of our election or predestination are established
outside ourselves: the efficient cause is God the Father, moved and impelled
to choose us by his own bare *eudokia* and free benevolence; the formal cause
is the decree itself to separate some from the number of the rest and take
them up to life. There is no reason to seek a material cause here, since it
can properly have a place only in substances. Nor would I readily call Christ
the Mediator the material cause of our election, as some do, but rather the
foundation laid by God beneath the very people to be chosen, upon which they
are built through election when they are said to be chosen in him. Finally,
the end is the praise of God’s glorious grace, or God’s own glory, which
properly is not outside God himself but was in God from eternity. The Lord and
Savior himself testifies in John 17:5: “Now therefore, Father, glorify me with
the glory I had with you before this world existed.”

[Marginalia: Second member. John 17:5.]

I acknowledge that glory here should be taken not so much insofar as it is in
God himself, free from all increase, decrease, and thus all change, but rather
insofar as it is revealed and made known to rational creatures, human beings
and angels. In this respect that glorious majesty of God sends down to us some
rays of its inaccessible light. It reveals them especially in his grace,
which he exercised toward the elect in Christ Jesus, even more brightly than
in producing the whole fabric of the world so powerfully, wisely, and
gloriously. For as David divinely sings in Psalm 145:4, 8, and 9, one
generation should commend to another with praises the works of Jehovah which
display his all-embracing power, splendor, and majestic glory. So above all
they should pour forth the memory of his abundant goodness and sing of his
righteousness: “Jehovah,” he says, “is gracious and merciful, longsuffering
and great in kindness; Jehovah is good to all, and his mercy is over all his
works.”

[Marginalia: Psalm 145:4, 8, 9.]

Fifth, observe, I ask, this inference of the Apostle. From the fact that all
the preceding causes of our election lie outside us, and that our eternal
election therefore in no way depends upon us, he gathers its end, namely the
glory of God himself in its entirety. According to that axiom of the logicians,
“contrary consequences follow from contraries,” the contrary will necessarily
follow from foreseen faith or works. That is, from the opinion of those who
teach that our faith and foreseen works, or the foreseen right use of free
choice and prevenient grace, and so forth, were either the principal or
instrumental cause of election, or a condition on which our election in any
way depended or with regard to which God chose us, it will follow that not all
the glory of our election is ascribed to God, but some portion of it is
transferred with detestable sacrilege to the chosen people themselves.

[Marginalia: Fifth observation.]

But the Apostle teaches us here that, when God chose us, he foresaw in human
nature already corrupted by sin sacrilegious pride and arrogance rather than
any faith, good works, or right use of free choice. He therefore willed to
found our whole salvation in himself alone, and not to seek outside himself,
in us or in any creature, the causes of his election or the reasons for his
counsel to take us up to life. He willed, I say, that our election to life
should in no way depend upon us but upon his bare goodness and free
benevolence, so that he might prevent and beat down the presumption of our
flesh, its swollen sense of itself, and that perverse self-love of our nature,
while claiming wholly for himself the glory of his grace, which he regarded
above all in our salvation.

Finally, the harmony and most beautiful agreement between the Apostle’s
doctrine and practice is certainly worthy of notice. He says and teaches that
God has abundantly heaped every spiritual blessing upon us in Christ and
predestined us in him both to grace and to glory, unto his own glory as the
end, so that his glorious grace might be praised. And the Apostle himself,
while recounting these blessings at the very beginning of this epistle,
bursts from his soul into that notable doxology and thanksgiving. By his
example he leaves us to do the same: whenever we set before ourselves this
supreme end of our salvation, election, and predestination, namely God’s
glory, we too should thereby be stirred to glorify his holy name with heart
and voice, since all honor, praise, glory, majesty, dominion, and blessing are
owed to him forever and ever.

[Marginalia: Sixth observation.]

Amen.
