[Marginalia: Lecture X. Transition to the second part of this benefit.]

There now follows in the next verse the second part of the same benefit,
which we referred above to external calling. In it the Apostle explains that
faith, or the inward illumination of the mind, from its instrumental cause,
namely the preaching of the gospel, when he adds, “having disclosed to us the
mystery of his will.” This is the mystery manifested to us in the gospel
concerning the way of saving us in Christ and through Christ, and therefore
of gathering both Jews and Gentiles—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 72 | Caput I p. 30]

—into the communion of the same Church, as he will explain more clearly in
the next verse.

Thus he described the instrumental cause, and again added the same
antecedent cause discussed above when he says, “according to his gracious
good pleasure.” He in turn illustrates and embellishes this by God’s eternal
purpose when he adds, “which he purposed in himself.” Just as God regarded us
in Christ with that gracious goodwill and favor, so also in himself from
eternity he most freely purposed to embrace us, though undeserving, with that
gracious and fatherly favor. Here we have not only the cause of that gracious
good pleasure hidden as it were in God’s own bosom—that is, in his purpose—but
also its supreme freedom, which the very word *purposing* designates. No one
should imagine that God Most High and Most Great was bound or impelled by any
necessity to love and elect us freely.

[Marginalia: Disclosure of the mystery of God’s will. Colossians 1:26.]

Since the other matters set forth in this verse have already been explained,
the only thing that calls for observation here is that description of
external calling by which the Apostle calls it the disclosure of the mystery
of the divine will. He calls the gospel “the mystery of God’s will” and says
that it had been hidden from ages and generations, but now has been manifested
to his saints, as Colossians 1 says. He will say the same below in chapter 3
of this very Epistle, and most clearly in that notable passage in 1
Corinthians 2. There we find a magnificent description of this very word of
God, that is, the gospel: “We speak wisdom among the mature, yet not the
wisdom of this age or of the rulers of this age, who are passing away; but we
speak the wisdom of God which is in a mystery—that hidden wisdom which God
ordained before the ages for our glory, which none of the rulers of this age
knew, for had they known it, they would never have crucified the Lord of
glory.”

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 2:6–8. Sacrament.]

It is clear from these passages that the Apostle explained the force of the
name *Mystery* itself: it signifies not just any secret, but a sacred and
religious secret. We therefore see that the Old Latin Interpreter translated
it rather unskillfully as *Sacrament*, since it is agreed that this word had
a different use and sense among the ancient Latins. Yet this old translation
caused the Latin Fathers to use the name *Sacrament* very broadly. They did
not apply it only to those things which we properly and strictly call
sacraments in the Christian Church, but also to any other heads, figures, and
symbols of the Christian religion that carried a hidden sense and suggested
some spiritual use beyond what they appeared to indicate at first sight.

From this general use of *Sacrament* for anything that signified something
hidden and secret, or something more abstruse and remote beyond the open and
obvious sense, another error arose in the Roman Church when later writers did
not sufficiently notice, or were even ignorant of, that usage. They accepted
as sacraments properly so called things that Christ never instituted for that
purpose, merely because the Fathers had sometimes elegantly called them by
that name as containing something mystical and hidden. It would therefore
have been better to retain the name *Mystery*, which Cicero himself used,
than to substitute *Sacrament* for it and provide an occasion for error.

[Marginalia: Confirmation of the name. 1 Timothy 3:16; 1 Corinthians 15:51;
Ephesians 3:3 and 5:32; Romans 11:24; Ephesians 3:10.]

Moreover, the name *Mystery* designates with excellent reason not only the
whole doctrine of the gospel in general, as the passages just cited show, and
especially that brief but remarkable epitome of the gospel in the final
verse of 1 Timothy 3: “And without controversy great is the mystery of
godliness: God was manifested in the flesh, justified in the Spirit, seen by
angels, preached among the Gentiles, believed on in the world, and received
up into glory.” There Paul calls the entire doctrine of the gospel by the one
name “the mystery of godliness.” The same name is also customarily given by
our Apostle to its individual parts—for example, to the resurrection of
bodies, or at least to that sudden change which at the last day will come
upon those still living in place of death and resurrection, in 1 Corinthians
15; to the calling of the Gentiles below in chapter 3 of this Epistle; to the
spiritual marriage of Christ and the Church, that is, his union with us,
below in chapter 5; to the conversion of the Jews to faith in Christ, which
will occur in its appointed time after the fullness of the Gentiles; and to
other things of this kind. The Apostle rightly calls them secrets or
mysteries.

For who could ever have conceived these things by himself and by nature, even
in a dream, unless God had deigned to reveal them to us through his only
begotten Son, who is and from eternity was in the Father’s bosom? Indeed,
these secrets of our salvation were hidden not only from human beings before
they were revealed in the gospel, but also from the heavenly angels, to whom
they finally became known through the Church, as the Apostle will tell us
below in chapter 3. So far beyond all natural discernment was that unique way
of attaining eternal life and salvation in Jesus Christ.

[Marginalia: The third part of the same benefit. Dr. Beza’s first
exposition.]

Let us now pass to the third part of this same benefit, set forth in the next
verse. Here the Apostle explains what that mystery of the divine will
revealed to us in the gospel is, or at least one eminent part of it which it
is especially necessary for us to know. He adds: “Namely, that in the
dispensation of the fullness of times”—that is, at the full time whose every
moment the Father had most wisely arranged and dispensed in his counsel
according to the decision of his will, so that Christ could be manifested in
the flesh and born in the world neither sooner nor later—“he might in Christ,”
thus made man, gather together and recall to one head and principle “all
things, both those in heaven and those on earth.” This means both the saints
who have already finished this life and triumph in heaven, and even the holy
angels themselves according to the opinion of some, as well as believers who
still live on earth and serve under the banner and standards of Christ their
head.

According to this exposition, the explanatory particle “namely” must be
added at the beginning of the verse for greater clarity, as is very often
done. The preposition *eis*, which in its usual meaning is transitive and
denotes motion, must then be explained as “in,” as though the text read *en
oikonomia*, “in the dispensation,” and so forth. That phrase must be referred
to the following verb *anakephalaiōsasthai*, so that it designates the time
at which the Father determined to gather together in Christ all the things
of which the Apostle speaks here: namely, in the dispensation of the fullness
of times, that is, at that full time which he dispensed, arranged, and
ordered in his eternal, most wise, and most free counsel. This is Dr. Beza’s
exposition.

[Marginalia: The later exposition of Erasmus and Zanchi.]

Or, with Erasmus and Zanchi, we may connect this passage with the preceding
verse in this way: “which he purposed in himself,” that is, which he kept
enclosed as if in the cabinet of his heart and hidden with himself, until the
dispensation of the fullness of times—that is, until that full time at which
Christ would dispense the work of our salvation and—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 73 | Caput I p. 31]

—redemption. He would do so by actually exercising the office of Mediator in
our flesh and undertaking and fulfilling the ministry which the Father had
commissioned him to undertake and exercise when that full time finally came.
Thus “full time” will contain a metonymy of adjunct. We must not understand
Christ to dispense the times themselves, as though the management of time
alone had been imposed and entrusted to him by the Father. Rather, we should
understand it as the dispensation of the things which had to be done and
undertaken by Christ manifested in the flesh when the full time came. Time
is therefore taken metonymically for the things done or occurring in time.

In this way the phrase *eis oikonomian* is connected with the preceding verb
*proetheto*, so that it designates how long God kept enclosed and hidden in
himself his counsel concerning our calling from his sheer grace and gracious
love: namely, until the dispensation of the fullness of times, that is, until
Christ, manifested in our flesh, would undertake the dispensation of our
salvation and that august province of the Mediator, which he would do only in
the fullness of time. In my judgment this exposition too is sound, and I find
nothing forced or violent in it. But since the matter comes to almost the
same thing in both, and the explanation of the mystery before us always
remains the same—namely, the *anakephalaiōsis*, or gathering, of all things
heavenly and earthly in Christ—let us retain Dr. Beza’s former translation
and exposition as received and approved.

[Marginalia: Choice. Division.]

To understand this especially notable passage, which interpreters and both
Greek and Latin Fathers have expounded in various ways, we shall briefly
discuss in order four matters occurring in it: first, what this
*anakephalaiōsis* is—that is, the gathering which the Apostle sets before us;
second, what things are gathered; third, in whom it takes place; and finally,
at what time the Apostle says it takes place.

[Marginalia: What *anakephalaiōsai* means. The first meaning.]

As to the first, we must before all else determine the proper meaning of
*anakephalaiōsai*, so that after discovering its genuine signification we may,
with God’s help, adapt it suitably to the aim and purpose of our Apostle. In
brief, corresponding to the word’s two derivations, two senses and meanings
may be assigned to it. The first is, in Chrysostom’s words, *ta dia makrōn
legomena syntomōs eipein*: to gather briefly what had been stated at length
and reduce it to a few heads. Rhetoricians teach and orators practice this in
the conclusions of their speeches, when they briefly conclude the various
and scattered parts of a speech and the many arguments previously treated at
length, and, as though after adding up the figures, reduce them to one sum.
This is commonly called recapitulation.

On this account the word may be derived from *to kephalaion*, meaning the
brief gathering of many parts into one whole, as it is commonly used by
Greek writers. Although we customarily call the larger divisions of books,
which we then subdivide into clauses or verses, *kephalaia* or chapters, not
the divisions themselves—for example, of the Bible or Aristotle—but rather
their brief prefatory outlines and summary comprehensions or abridgments more
properly deserve to be called *kephalaia*.

[Marginalia: The second meaning. Chrysostom: “to bring all things under one
head.” The difference between the meanings.]

The second meaning of this word will be that derived from *kephalē*, “head”:
to recall to one head and gather under the same head many things previously
scattered; to lead and recall things to the same origin and principle from
which they had fallen and wandered away. Here *ana* stands for *syn*, as
often occurs. It is no wonder that these two meanings are closely related,
since their two roots, *kephalē* and *kephalaion*, have so close a relation.
Nevertheless, I find this difference: the former meaning concerns the summary
collection and union with one another of two or more members; the latter
means their reduction or recall to some third head distinct from either or
all of them.

[Marginalia: Application. Human dispersion. And that twofold. Romans 1 and
2.]

Let us now briefly apply these points to the matter at hand so that we may
better understand how the *anakephalaiōsis* of human beings takes place in
Christ and toward Christ—that is, their gathering, recapitulation, or recall
to their origin and principle. We must first recognize that most wretched
dispersion or scattering of the entire human race caused by sin. Through
that impious apostasy and defection from God, a human being was both torn and
separated from God his Creator, to whom he had formerly adhered through that
original image of God, and also torn and divided from his fellow human. For
where there is no union with God, there is no holy fellowship among people,
no true brotherhood and no brotherly love. The ungodliness which separates
us from God always brings with it a wretched dissolution of human beings
from one another. A person who is ungodly and covenant-breaking toward God
cannot cultivate true justice toward another person. He may advertise
himself before people under some shadow of civil justice, but before God’s
eyes his heart cannot be affected and disposed toward his neighbor as it
ought and as the norm and rectitude of the divine law requires.

At the beginning of the Epistle to the Romans the Apostle graphically depicts
this dreadful and lamentable dissolution of the entire human race through
sin: from God the Creator through every kind of ungodliness, and from one
another through every kind of injustice and most shameful wickedness. He
demonstrates that all are ungodly and unjust by nature, all destitute of the
glory of God, and all shut up under sin, thereby making room for the gathering
which followed through God’s free mercy in Christ Jesus.

[Marginalia: The application itself. The earlier meaning. 1 Peter 2:25; John
10:16; John 11:52.]

With this understood, we say briefly that the Apostle appears to have
regarded and embraced both meanings of this verb in this single word and to
have alluded to both its derivations, whether from *to kephalaion* or from
*kephalē*. He indicates that all believers from Jews and Gentiles, formerly
scattered like a loosened broom and wandering sheep dispersed through the
deserts of vanity, error, ungodliness, and every kind of idolatry, have been
gathered by faith in Christ, compacted as if into one body, united in the
fellowship of one Church, indeed joined into one mystical man by the same
Spirit, faith, and love. The Lord himself speaks of this gathering in John
10: “I have other sheep which are not of this fold; I must bring them also,
and they will hear my voice, and there will be one flock and one shepherd.”
In the next chapter the Evangelist similarly speaks of—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 74 | Caput I p. 32]

—Caiaphas, who, being high priest that year, prophesied that Jesus would die for the
nation, “and not for that nation only, but also that he might gather into one
the children of God who were scattered.” The second meaning necessarily
follows: these same people were recalled to one head. Otherwise, as we shall
soon say, they could not have grown together into one body of the Church
unless they had been brought to one head, united and engrafted into that one
head, and through their head joined to God the Father himself. As the Apostle
says in Colossians 1, it pleased the Father that all fullness should dwell in
Christ and, having made peace through the blood of his cross, through him to
reconcile all things to himself, whether in heaven or on earth. The Apostle
plainly mentions there the same gathering of all things which he sets forth
here and illustrates it by the same division based on the subjects involved.

[Marginalia: Exposition. Meaning I. Romans 13:9.]

To perceive this same matter more clearly, we shall present three ancient
expositions of this verb. All seem to us very useful in clarifying the
Apostle’s mind and purpose, and none should be condemned or rejected, although
I especially think that the Apostle here had regard to the nature of Christ
as head and to the derivation of the word from *kephalē*. He himself will
teach at the end of this chapter and in the next how God recalled Jews and
Gentiles to one head, namely Christ. The first two expositions appear to
regard the former derivation, *to kephalaion*, while the last regards
*kephalē*.

First, just as the whole force and mass of a speech is reduced in summary and
briefly to one concluding recapitulation, and just as the entire second table
of the law is summed up in love of one’s neighbor and thus in this one
commandment, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,” as the Apostle says
in Romans 13, so all the things done with the human race from the beginning
of the world, especially in the Church through shadows and figures, were
summarily fulfilled and recapitulated in Christ when he appeared. For
example, all the legal sacrifices were summarily fulfilled in this one
sacrifice; all the world’s sins were cast and heaped upon this one
propitiatory victim and expiated by this one and only Lamb of God; all the
curses of the law due to us were poured upon this one person so that he might
free us from them all; all the treasures of heavenly blessings were placed
upon his head so that he might fill us all with them; all the promises, and
finally the whole law, had their fulfillment in him, because he alone
fulfilled the law for all his members. In a word, Christ is plainly the
conclusion, aim, sense, soul, and substance of the whole Old Testament; the
kernel of so many husks, the prototype of so many antitypes, the body of so
many shadows, the truth of so many types, and the fulfillment of so many
precepts, promises, and predictions. This is the first exposition.

[Marginalia: Meaning II.]

The second understands the word chiefly of persons. It teaches that human
beings who had fallen and been scattered through sin grew together into one
body through Christ. Indeed, in its own way it applies the same thing not
only to human beings but also to the heavenly angels: through sin human
beings had fallen from communion with God and likewise been torn from the
fellowship and society of the holy angels as slaves of sin and Satan. Yet
believers reconciled to God through Christ grew together into one body of
the Church both with one another and with the holy angels and were restored
to their holy fellowship.

[Marginalia: Meaning III. Note in passing. Confirmation.]

The final exposition extends the word to all creatures, heavenly and earthly.
Its authors say that God determined in his own time to *anakephalaiōsasthai*
them in Christ—that is, to recall them to their head, to their beginning and
principle, and to lead them back to the origin from which they had fallen,
thus restoring and renewing all things in Christ by recalling them to their
original natural condition and former state. The Old Interpreter, whoever he
was, undoubtedly regarded this sense when he consistently translated the verb
*anakeph.* as “restore.” There is thus no need to suppose with our most
learned Interpreter that, while consistently translating it so, the old
translator read *ananeōsasthai* or *anakainisasthai* in place of
*anakeph.*, words which properly and openly mean “renew” or “restore.” From
the single passage of Tertullian which we shall soon produce, and which we
believe older than this Vulgate translation, I am certain that even while
retaining *anakeph.* he intended to translate it only as “restore.” He judged
it equivalent to “to lead all things back to the head, that is, to their
first beginning and origin.”

For, they say, every creature, heavenly and earthly, fell away from the
integrity of its beginning at the very outset and suffered a kind of failure.
God therefore purposed and ordained in his counsel that a certain restoration
and renewal of all things should be made in Christ in his appointed times.
This is manifest regarding humanity, as we have seen; how it pertains to the
angels we shall soon see, God willing. It is true regarding other creatures
only accidentally, insofar as we read that because of sinful, rebellious,
and covenant-breaking humanity they were subjected to vanity, compelled
against their will to serve man, and the earth was cursed for his sake.
Hence Romans 8 says that this created world groans together with God’s
children and travails together, awaiting the day of the restoration of all
things in the hope that it too will be delivered from the bondage of
corruption into the liberty of the glory of God’s children.

[Marginalia: Genesis 3. Romans 8:22. Correction. Tertullian.]

But since it is agreed that the Apostle speaks here only of things gathered
and restored under Christ as head—the things over which Christ has been
constituted head—we must omit the other creatures and understand these words
of human beings and the elect angels alone, if we extend them as broadly as
possible. Tertullian interprets this passage of that restoration in his book
*On Monogamy*, teaching how all things are brought back through Christ to
their principle and former state—or “returned,” as he puts it. He demonstrates
this by the example of Alpha and Omega, the first and last Greek letters,
which the Lord attributes to himself in John’s Apocalypse, “to show,” he
says, “that in himself are both the course of the beginning toward the end
and the return of the end toward the beginning, so that every dispensation,
ending in him through whom it began—that is, through the Word who was made
flesh—should end just as it began.” He then confirms this recall of things to
their principle by examples: “For,” he says, “all things were so recalled in
Christ to their beginning—”

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 75 | Caput I p. 33 (through Lecture X close)]

—“that faith returned from circumcision to the integrity of the flesh as it
was in the beginning; freedom in foods and abstinence only from blood, as it
was in the beginning; the indivisibility of marriage, as it was in the
beginning; and the prohibition of divorce, as it was in the beginning. And
finally, the whole man is recalled to Paradise, where he was in the
beginning.” Thus he argues subtly indeed, but he defends a bad cause with
respect to the aim of that book.

[Marginalia: Cyril. Those three principal points of *anakephalaiōsis*.]

Cyril too, in book 9 on John, takes this word in the same sense: to recall to
the head or beginning and to the former state. He teaches that this
restoration consists chiefly in three things: the abolition of sin, the
destruction of death, and *huiothesia*, or adoption. He confirms it in this
way: in the beginning man was created a son of God, namely through the grace
of creation; holy, whole, without sin, in true righteousness and innocence;
and finally of such a kind that he could have lived forever and never been
subject to death if he had persisted in that state. Through Christ he obtains
all these things again. Therefore, through Christ he is restored to his
former state.

[Marginalia: Conclusion. Oecumenius. 2 Corinthians 5:17.]

These are the principal opinions of the ancients. Insofar as they are offered
concerning man corrupted and fallen through sin but repaired through Christ,
we readily approve and admit them. Nevertheless, we understand the Apostle
here to have spoken chiefly of the gathering of all believers, Gentile and
Jewish, under one head, Christ, and of their union with that one head, Christ
the Mediator. We may therefore compress this entire exposition into the
clearest and excellent words of Oecumenius, who admirably explains the
passage: “To *anakephalaiōsasthai* all things,” he says, “that is, to join and
unite them, making Christ the one head of both angels and human beings; for
the angels had been separated on account of human beings.” Nothing is
clearer than those words.

From this follows, second, at least on humanity’s part, that renewal and
restoration of human nature just mentioned in the views of Cyril and
Tertullian, according to the Apostle’s statement in 2 Corinthians 5: “If
anyone is in Christ, therefore, he is a new creature; old things have passed
away; behold, all things have become new.” In the next verse he adds the
foundation of this renewal: our reconciliation with God through Christ,
which is also our gathering to God under Christ the head, through whose
intervention and mediation we adhere to God. “All these things are from God,”
he says, “who reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ.” Let this suffice
concerning the gathering itself.
