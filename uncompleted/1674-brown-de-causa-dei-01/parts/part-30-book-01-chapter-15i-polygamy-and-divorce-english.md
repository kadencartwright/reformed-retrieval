Third, the precept prohibiting polygamy and rash divorce is moral, yet it does
not flow from the necessity of the divine nature.

What does he say? He answers by questioning in this fashion: “What do I hear?
Did the Fathers, then, not sin by practicing polygamy? Did Christ finally make
polygamy a sin?” Reply: The polygamous Fathers did sin. Those who violate
God’s positive law sin just as much as those who transgress the natural law.
2. Christ did not finally make polygamy a sin. How, I ask, will he be able to
infer this from our assertion? This is thoughtlessness of a sufficiently
supine kind. Nevertheless, he continues his questioning: “Is not the contrary
evident from creation itself? Dr. Ames himself, a champion of the morality of
the Sabbath, openly calls polygamy a sin against the law of nature and right
reason, and proves it in *Cases of Conscience*, book 5, chapter 35, section 7.”
Reply: There are those who think it sufficiently harsh to say that the most
devout Patriarchs lived throughout their whole lives in violation of the
natural law, and that those whose minds were enlightened by divine and
supernatural light nevertheless did not know this natural law—much less that
they sinned against natural conscience. I, however, will freely concede that
they violated the law of nature, and thus I will not contradict Dr. Ames. Yet
I add this: this law was natural for us, but not for God. That is, it did not
flow from the nature and attributes of God but was established by God’s free
good pleasure.

“Did God not,” he continues, “form the principle of all laws in creation? Is
not creation itself a visible and, as it were, speaking law? Does it not teach
that the order established among creatures from the beginning must not be
overturned?” Reply: I not only do not deny these things; I welcome and embrace
them, because they serve my purpose exceedingly well. For it will not be
difficult to prove from this that the Sabbath commandment is also natural,
because in creation God formed the principle of all laws. And because God’s
rest, after creation had at last been completed on the sixth day, is no less a
visible and speaking law than creation itself, creation itself, the rationale
and manner of creating, and God’s rest on the seventh day after creation are
a visible and speaking law enjoining the observance of the Sabbath no less
necessarily than many other things. But, 2. it still does not follow from this
that these things are natural to God himself, since just as he freely created
all things, so he freely created all things in such a manner. Thus he freely
set before us such a law of creation and willed creation to present such a law
to us.

He asks further: “Does not the authority of the husband over the woman flow
from this, because he was created first (1 Timothy 2:12–13)?” Reply: Most
certainly. “Does it not follow from this that what God has separated must not
be joined?” Reply: Without doubt. “Is not polygamy condemned from this, since
God created for Adam no

more than one wife?” Reply: Who doubts it? “Are not the first man and woman
one flesh?” Reply: They are. “Did not Adam, rather than God, say, ‘This is
flesh of my flesh; therefore a man will leave father and mother and cleave to
his wife’?” Reply: Whether those last words, “therefore,” and so forth, were
spoken by God, as some hold, or by Adam makes no difference. We maintain that
the indissolubility of marriage is established from them firmly enough. “Does
a husband owe himself to his wife in such a way that he leaves even his
parents for her, and yet, while preserving chastity and her right, will he be
able to live with several women? And will there be no *tsarah*, distress,
there, which experience itself and nature teach?” (Leviticus 18:18; 1 Samuel
1:6; 1 Corinthians 7:4; Malachi 2:14–15). Reply: There will be. But how is it
proved from this that these things flow from the necessity of the divine
nature? This one thing remains to be proved.

“Indeed, it does flow from it,” he says, “because God himself cannot will that
two people joined in marriage should not be one flesh. If it were otherwise,
since fornication is truly such, why is the fornicator said to make himself
the flesh of a prostitute?” (1 Corinthians 6:15). “How are those who unite to
beget and rear children together not one flesh?” Reply: 1. It is entirely true
that God cannot will that two people joined in marriage should not be one
flesh, because he cannot will that the same thing should not be the same
thing. But was it contrary to the divine nature to create several wives when
he had created one Eve? Was it contrary to the divine nature to give those
several women to the one Adam as wives? Let him show this, I ask, and we will
abandon this instance. 2. He will not say, I think, that a fornicator makes
himself the flesh of a prostitute in the same way that those joined in lawful
marriage become one flesh. Otherwise it would be just as unlawful to separate
the fornicator from the prostitute as it is to dissolve a lawful marriage.
Rather, this is only analogically and in a qualified sense, because, as Dr.
Strang teaches in *On Polygamy*, two are said to become one flesh not only by
reason of the act of carnal union, but much more by reason of the right and
power which each has over the body of the other. 3. We concede that those who
unite to beget and rear children together are one flesh, provided that this
union is lawful and is made in the proper manner. But how do these things
prove what was denied?

“Yet people knew this,” he adds, “apart from the law of God, for even among
the nations some reproved fornication, and holy people abstained from
fornication without a law. Judah, when he had committed fornication with
Tamar, wished to have her burned as a prostitute.” Reply: It can be conceded
that certain people knew apart from a written law that polygamy is a sin. But
how will it follow from this that this natural law flows from God’s own
nature? Can nothing be natural for us which is not also natural for God? Can
God inscribe nothing upon the human heart which does not necessarily flow
from his nature? Let the adversaries prove this. 2. It is conceded that some
among the nations reproved fornication. But what follows *from this*? Some
among the nations also offered sacrifices. Does he therefore maintain that
the precept concerning sacrifices flows from God’s nature? 3. Who are those
holy people who abstained from fornication without a law? Were they the
nations? What, are there also truly holy people among them? If not the
nations, then who, I ask? Did the people of God have no law? 4. We know what
Judah did. But we do not yet know what the adversary has proved from it or
could have proved. Did the people of God then have no law? Does he think that
the practice by which a brother was to raise up offspring for his brother
rested upon natural right? Certainly the Mosaic law had not yet been given;
and if this practice existed without any positive divine law and nevertheless
was lawful, he must concede that it was commanded by natural law or else that
it was not lawful.
