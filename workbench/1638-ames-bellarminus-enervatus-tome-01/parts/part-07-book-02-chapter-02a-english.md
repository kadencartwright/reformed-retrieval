### CHAPTER II

#### On Christ's Descent into Hell. Controversy 2

Book 4, chapters 6, 7, and so forth.

##### THESIS I

The Roman Catholics maintain that there are four hells, or subterranean
receptacles of souls—in keeping with that intimate knowledge they have of
infernal things: 1. the prison of the condemned; 2. purgatory; 3. the limbo of
the righteous Fathers; 4. the limbo of children who die with original sin, or
without circumcision or baptism. Bellarmine contends, moreover, that Christ's
soul truly and properly descended by a real presence to the limbo of the
Fathers, and adds

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 98 | printed p. 88 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

that it is also probable that his soul descended to every place of hell as
well, although it delivered no one either from the hell of the condemned or
from the limbo of children, but only from the limbo of the Fathers and perhaps
also some from purgatory, chapter 16.

We, however—recognizing one hell—say that this local descent of Christ's soul
into hell is an idle invention. Nevertheless, we say that according to the
Scriptures Christ is truly said to have descended into hell in two ways: 1.
Before death, when he endured infernal pains or torments in his soul, which is
to descend into hell metaphorically. 2. After death, when he was held in the
state of death, his body remaining in the grave and his soul separated from
it, which is to descend into the lower regions properly, in a general sense.
3. Bellarmine ascribes a certain impious opinion to some of our writers in
chapter 7, but it is a manifest and thoroughly hellish calumny. Bellarmine
first tries to refute our interpretations and afterward to establish his own,
but in vain on both counts.

##### On the Pains of Christ's Soul

###### The Roman Catholics' Arguments

2. Bell.: *The Scriptures attribute all our salvation to Christ's blood and
bodily death, making no mention of infernal pains or torments in his soul.*
Prot. The Scriptures attribute our salvation to Christ's death, not simply
and exclusively insofar as it was bodily, but insofar as it had the spiritual
curse of God and of the law joined to it, Galatians 3:13, and insofar as it
was the consummation of his obedience and his whole humiliation, which was
partly bodily and partly spiritual, Philippians 2:8. For Christ underwent that
death as our surety, the death which had been declared to Adam and was owed by
us; but that death was not bodily alone. Finally, he is expressly said to have
borne our pains, Isaiah 53. Our pains—that is, those due to our defections—are
not bodily only but also spiritual; and Christ himself testifies in Matthew
26:38 that he bore them in his soul as part of that death which he suffered
for us: *My soul is sorrowful on every side, even to death.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 99 | printed p. 89 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

3. Bell.: *If from that prayer in the garden until the resurrection Christ
was in hell and perceived God as opposed and angry toward him, then he would
not have given so many signs of confidence, faith, and trust.* Prot. 1. We
deny that Christ endured those pains all the way to the resurrection. We also
reject the other expression, *he was in hell.* Bellarmine therefore refutes
his own imagining here, not our position. As for that other expression—*he
perceived God as opposed and angry toward him*—we do not admit it in the sense
that Christ had lost the apprehension of faith and trust, as Bellarmine
himself acknowledges a little later. Rather, he did not feel that joy which
usually flows from faith and trust, and in its place he felt distress and
vexation proceeding from the wrath of God—not against him absolutely, but
insofar as he was our surety. 2. Even in that distress there was some
moderation and mitigation at intervals, so that there might meanwhile be room
for those duties which had to be performed before his death.

4. Bell.: *If we had been redeemed through Christ's infernal penalties, types
ought to have preceded them; there ought also to be some sacrament of that
event; likewise it ought to be celebrated on a feast day and depicted in
paintings. But nothing of the kind has been done.* Prot. 1. Let the Roman
Catholics tell us by which types, sacraments, feast days, or pictures that
agony of Christ is declared which is expressly commemorated in the
Scriptures, Luke 22:44; and for the declaration of Christ's spiritual distress
we will refer them to the very same things. 2. There is no express image of
Christ's divine nature—except among idolaters—nor of his soul or of those
things which properly concerned the soul, because gross, external images are
incapable of such representations. Christ's active obedience itself could not
be suitably represented in an inanimate thing. 3. Nevertheless, Christ's
spiritual sufferings were signified in some manner formerly by the burning of
the whole burnt offering with heavenly fire, and now by the same sacrament by
which Christ's entire passion is declared. As for feast days and images, let
the makers of human traditions and idols see to them.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 100 | printed p. 90 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

5. Bell.: *The Fathers describe Christ's descent into hell as the descent of
a victor and conqueror, not of one under accusation.* Prot. 1. Triumph is of
two kinds: virtual triumph in the field, Colossians 2:14–15, and actual
triumph in a chariot or glorious chair, Ephesians 4:8. In the former sense
Christ was at once the accused and the conqueror; in the latter, Christ's
descent is plainly opposed to his ascent as humiliation is opposed to
exaltation, Romans 10:6–7, Ephesians 4:9, Philippians 2:8–9. Consequently, to
describe the descent of a conqueror in that sense is nothing other than to
define the descent of one who ascends, or the humiliation of one who is
exalted. 2. It is false that all the Fathers referred Christ's descent to his
triumph. Irenaeus is cited by Bellarmine in chapter 14 as declaring: *The Lord
observed the law of the dead, so that he might become the firstborn from the
dead, and he remained in the lower parts of the earth until the third day.*
Basil says: *God will redeem my soul from the hand of hell: he foretells the
Lord's descent into hell.* These are Jerome's words on Isaiah 53: *He was truly
numbered among transgressors, so that he might descend into hell.* These are
the words of Eusebius in *Demonstration of the Gospel*, book 10: *To descend
into hell is the final affliction.* 3. Those who describe Christ's descent in
this way understand either a virtual triumph or a virtual descent—or they
certainly speak not only without Scripture, but even contrary to Scripture.
For Scripture expressly teaches that Christ was delivered from that hell into
which he descended, Acts 2:24, but no one is said to be delivered from an act
of victory or triumph.

6. Bell.: *Calvin's foundation is false: that being in hell is nothing other
than fearing an angry God.* Prot. This is not Calvin's foundation but
Bellarmine's invention. We say, however, that Christ did not only fear but also
felt God's wrath.

7. Bell.: *If Christ uttered words of despair, he appears to have sinned most
grievously.* Prot. We grant the consequence if, as they were uttered by
Christ, they were properly words of despair. But if those same words which in
human beings often proceed from despair, and are therefore called words of
despair, proceeded in Christ from anguish, there is no sin in this.

8. Bell.: *If Christ bore the penalty of the condemned, then he despaired
absolutely. For the penalty of the condemned includes*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 101 | printed p. 91 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*despair essentially.* Prot. 1. Despair is not of the essence of infernal
penalty. God is the author of the penalty; the devil and the sinner are the
authors of despair. Penalty is a human being's suffering; despair is a human
being's action. 2. Despair does not properly have regard to the penalty itself
but to its continuation forever. It therefore has no place in one who
undergoes the same penalty for a short, fixed time known to him, with a sure
hope of deliverance—indeed, with the power to remove it whenever he pleases.
The calumny which follows in Bellarmine concerning Christ's doubt is similar.
3. In *On Purgatory*, book 2, chapters 3, 10, and 14, Bellarmine contends that
souls remaining in purgatory undergo the most atrocious penalties in infernal
fire without any despair. Can those wretched souls endure such penalties
while preserving their hope, and Christ cannot?

###### Our Arguments

9. Prot. Isaiah 53:4, 10. Christ is said to have been struck and crushed by
God. Bell. 1. *Christ is said to have been struck by the Father because he
permitted him to be killed.* Prot. 1. In this sense he was also struck by
himself, because he permitted himself to be killed. 2. The predestination of
Christ's death, Acts 2:23, and the giving, calling, sanctifying, and sealing of
Christ to undergo it, John 3:16, Hebrews 5:4 and following, John 6:27 and
10:36, denote an efficacy of another kind than bare permission. 3. Not even
the Roman Catholics deny that Christ satisfied God's justice by his death.
God's justice, therefore, was engaged with Christ as he died, not mere
permission. Bell. 2. *Jerome and all other expositors refer this striking to
temporal death alone.* Prot. 1. Although this sorrow and anguish was a taste
of spiritual death, it was nevertheless temporal in duration. 2. It also
belonged to bodily death as a partial cause of it and as a conjoined part of
one and the same humiliation, which was most notable in death and is usually
designated by it. 3. What is said of Jerome and the others is false, and an
argument drawn from human authority is invalid. Bell. 3. *Not everyone who is
struck by an angry God suffers infernal penalties.* Prot. 1. All such
penalties are suffered in part by those

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 102 | printed p. 92 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

who are struck in their souls on account of the guilt of sin. 2. Christ, as
the world's surety and mediator, was struck much more
severely than anyone else. Hence even among the schoolmen themselves the
received opinion is that Christ's pain was greater than all pains, Thomas,
part 3, question 46, article 6. 3. Most others, when they are struck in this
life, have some relief and mitigation; but for a time Christ was deprived of
every sense of comfort, Thomas and Medina in the same place.

10. Prot. Isaiah 53:5. The chastisement of our peace is laid upon him.
Therefore he was paying every kind of penalty which our sins deserved, and
thus the penalties of Gehenna. Bell.: *It would likewise follow that Christ's
passion had to be eternal. But just as Christ satisfied for an eternal penalty
by a temporal penalty, so also he could satisfy for the death of souls by the
death of his body, because the price of redemption is weighed according to
the dignity of the person who suffered and according to the love with which
he suffered.* Prot. 1. The reasoning is different for the kind of penalty and
for its duration. Eternal duration and similar circumstances which accompany
the penalties of all the condemned are not of the essence of the penalty
itself, but follow it in those who cannot satisfy and conquer by bearing the
penalty, as Christ did. 2. To inquire what could have been accomplished by
Christ's death according to God's absolute power is a matter of curious
presumption. Nothing can be concluded from it against Christ's spiritual
suffering any more than against temporal death itself, for the Roman
Catholics contend that even one drop of Christ's blood, or even one of his
tears, could have sufficed for the redemption of the world. 3. Given at least
God's will concerning Christ's suffering as our surety and mediator, he could
not have satisfied God's justice by the suffering of only half of his human
nature. 4. Although the value of the suffering must be weighed from the
dignity of the sufferer, nevertheless, for the suffering to be fit to sustain
that value for such a use, a proportion had to be observed between the penalty
owed and the penalty paid.

11. Prot. Christ's anguish and horror in the garden, which struck him even to
the point of a bloody sweat, did not arise from fear of

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 103 | printed p. 93 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

bodily death, which many martyrs and even others underwent with exultation.
Bell. 1.
*None of the Fathers intimated that this fear concerned the salvation of his
soul.* Prot. Nor is this our conclusion, but rather that tasting the cup of
God's wrath was the cause of this horror. Justin, in his dialogue with Trypho,
and Caesarius, dialogue 1, sufficiently intimate this. Bell. 2. *In order that
redemption might be abundant, Christ feared not under compulsion, but because
he willed to undergo the penalty of sorrow and fear. If others do not fear,
it is either because God infuses consolation greater than the penalty, because
their senses are stupefied, or because they turn their mind away from thinking
about the penalties. But Christ neither stupefied his own sense, nor permitted
joy to overflow from the higher part into the lower, nor turned his thought
away from the death at hand, but fixed his mind upon all the torments hanging
over him.* Prot. 1. We do not doubt that Christ suffered by the purpose of his
will. Nevertheless, Medina, with other schoolmen, teaches on Thomas, part 3,
question 46, article 6, that this same will, considered as a kind of nature,
fled from death and pain as contrary to human nature. Nor were Christ's
sufferings voluntary in such a way that they were arbitrary and imposed by no
law, for then they would have contributed nothing to satisfaction. 2. The
question here concerns the objective cause of this horror. For although
Christ willed to undergo pain, he did not will to bring it upon himself
without a suitable cause to produce it. It is not credible, however, that
bodily death was the cause of such terror to him—even to agony, amazement,
distress, and the dissolving of his blood into clots. 3. Deprivation of
consolation is a part of infernal penalty. Thus Medina in the place cited:
*There is a kind of penalty which the condemned suffer in hell, namely, that
they lack all comfort. Our Redeemer deigned to endure something similar to
this, for in his passion he renounced every comfort and remedy of consolation
from himself.* 4. That invented distinction between the higher and lower
parts of the rational soul has no foundation either in Scripture or in sound
philosophy; with respect to this business it is also rejected by the Jesuit
Maldonatus

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 104 | printed p. 94 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

and refuted on Matthew 26 from these words: *Not as I will.* 5. But if joy and
consolation
could be restrained so that they did not overflow from one part of the soul
into the other, the divine nature could certainly restrain the same joy for a
time so that it did not overflow into any part of the soul. 6. It is false
that Christ fixed his mind upon the torments alone, of his own accord and
under no compelling distress. For he endured the cross for the joy set before
him, Hebrews 12:2. 7. All martyrs ought to imitate Christ in bearing death,
and therefore they ought no more to turn their thoughts away from impending
death than he turned his away by deliberate counsel.

12. Prot. By crying out, *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?* Christ
shows that he in some way felt God's wrath. Bell.: *No mention is made of
wrath, but of abandonment, by which the Deity abandoned the human nature amid
its penalties.* Prot. 1. No express mention of favor, as to the word, is made
where God is said to be with his people and present to them, but it is plainly
signified, 2 Chronicles 15:2. So also wrath is signified through abandonment
in the same passage. 2. That form of speech—*the Deity leaves the human
nature*—sounds most objectionable. 3. It is truly said that the human nature
was in some manner abandoned amid its penalties, but, first, not amid external
penalties or bodily death alone, as Bellarmine would have it. For then everyone
who suffered persecution would have to be called abandoned by God, although
Paul makes it a reason for boasting in 2 Corinthians 4:9: *We suffer
persecution, but we are not forsaken.* Second, the abandonment itself was a
penalty; and since it is a part or degree of emptying, fitting the entire
emptied nature, it ought to be understood of the soul no less than of the
body. Bellarmine himself has just conceded that some part of Christ's soul was
deprived of consolation and thus spiritually abandoned. Once that invented
rending of the soul into heterogeneous parts has been removed, he himself
will therefore affirm with us the same abandonment.

13. Prot. Acts 2:24: Whom God raised up, having loosed the pains of hell.
Bell. 1. *In Greek it reads, having loosed the pains of death, not of hell.*
Prot. In some copies the word is *death,* in others *hell,* because in this
matter death and hell mean one and the same thing. The force of the argument

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 105 | printed p. 95 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

is the same whichever way it is read. Bell. 2. *The sense is either that death
was delivered from pains, or that death with all its pains was destroyed.*
Prot. 1. If death was delivered from pains, then death was also raised from
death. For to be loosed from the pains of death is to be raised from death, as
is plainly evident from the words themselves, *raised up, having loosed*—that
is, he raised him by loosing. 2. Death was indeed loosed and destroyed through
this loosing of Christ, but Christ himself first had to be loosed from death,
and death from him, before it could be loosed with respect to the faithful.
Bell. 3. *If, because God's wrath was the cause of death, the pains which
Christ suffered in death were therefore from an angry God, then by the same
reasoning all who die, even martyrs, would have God hostile toward them.* Prot.
It does not follow, for Christ bore God's wrath in order to deliver us from
it. By bearing the full force of death Christ broke it and caused it to assail
the faithful only broken and mitigated. Bell. 4. *The correct explanation is
either that Christ loosed the pains of hell, not those by which he was held,
but so that he might not be held; or those by which others were held.* Prot.
1. We admit that the pains of hell were loosed so that Christ would not be
held by them any longer, but they could not have been loosed so that he would
not be held at all; for then he would have returned to life immediately after
death, without any delay. 2. The being held, however, is expressly referred in
the text to Christ himself as its object, not to others: *it was not possible
that he*—that is, Christ—*should be held by it.*

14. Prot. Hebrews 5:7. Christ is said to have been heard and delivered from
fear. Bell. 1. *It should be read, he was heard because of his reverence.
Εὐλάβεια is not taken for fear of punishment, but for reverential fear.* Prot.
1. It is not denied that reverence is often designated by this word, but even
from the one book of Ecclesiasticus it appears plainly enough that it sometimes
also signifies fear of punishment. Chapter 26:5: *My heart fears three
things,* εὐλαβήθη. And 29:10: *They fear being deprived,* εὐλαβήθησαν. And
41:5: μὴ εὐλαβοῦ κρίμα θανάτου, *Do not fear the judgment of death.* It is
also so taken in Acts 23:10, εὐλαβηθεὶς. In this passage it must necessarily
be taken for fear of impending evil, for the purpose of the passage is to
explain the infirmities assumed by Christ,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 106 | printed p. 96 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

namely, that *he was surrounded by infirmity,* as verse 2 had said. It treats
of Christ's sufferings, as appears from verse 8, *from the things which he
suffered.* The effects of these sufferings were fear and most vehement
prayers, verse 7. Then he is said to have been heard and delivered ἀπὸ τῆς
εὐλαβείας, but he was not heard and delivered from reverence, but from fear of
the cup. Bell. 2. *ἀπὸ does not here mean from or out of, but because of, as in
Matthew 13:44, Luke 24:41, Acts 12:14, ἀπὸ χαρᾶς, because of joy; Luke 22:45,
ἀπὸ τῆς λύπης, because of sorrow; 2 Maccabees 5:21, ἀπὸ τῆς ὑπερηφανίας.*
Prot.
1. The proper and genuine meaning of this particle is *from* or *out of.* 2.
The examples produced by Bellarmine prove nothing to the contrary, for they
can very correctly be read *from joy, from sorrow, from pride.* 3. Although
ἀπὸ with the genitive sometimes denotes an internal cause of the motion or
action signified by the governing verb, as in those examples, it nevertheless
never signifies a cause which externally impels someone to that action. Yet
in this passage, *heard and delivered ἀπὸ εὐλαβείας,* Bellarmine would have
ἀπὸ denote Christ's virtue as the impulsive cause of God the Father's act by
which he heard and delivered Christ.

##### On the Lower Regions in Which Christ Was after Death

15. Bell.: *Bucer, Beza, and in part Calvin teach that in the Scriptures the
grave is understood by the word hell, and therefore that Christ's descent into
hell is nothing other than his burial.* Prot. 1. They rightly teach that in
the Scriptures the grave is often understood by the lower regions, taking the
grave by synecdoche to mean the common state of all the dead; but they do not
teach that it is always so taken. 2. They teach this concerning Christ's
descent into the lower regions undertaken after death, and rightly so. They do
not understand burial itself by itself, but his continuance in the state of
death, which with respect to his body was in the grave.

16. Bell.: *Three things must be proved: 1. That the lower regions are
subterranean places distinct from graves. 2. That the souls of the righteous
were in those lower regions before Christ's death. 3. That Christ's soul
descended directly to those same lower regions.* Prot. As for knowing the
place

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 107 | printed p. 97 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

of the condemned in the lower regions, we are not so concerned as we are with
avoiding their state. We therefore recognize no weighty controversy
concerning it. Bellarmine will never prove
the other two points from the Scriptures; nor will he prove the first in such
a way that it can be laid beneath them as a foundation, namely, that the lower
regions always denote a subterranean place distinct from the grave and from
that common state of the dead, one part of which consists in burial.

##### THESIS XVII

###### ON THE LOWER REGIONS

Bell.: *The Latin word for hell is distinct from the word for grave.* Prot. 1.
It is not always distinct in meaning. In sermons 57 and 58 Ambrose renders the
angels' words in Luke 24, by which they rebuke Mary as she searches for Christ
in the grave—*Why do you seek among the dead him who lives?*—in this way:
*Why do you seek among those in hell? Why do you search for me among the
infernal things?* 2. It is often distinguished from the place of the grave
when it is not distinguished from the common state of those who are buried or
dead. It therefore cannot be concluded from this that the lower regions
always denote the center of the earth.

18. Bell.: *The Greek word ᾅδης in Matthew 11, where the city of Capernaum is
said to be about to descend into hell, cannot be taken for the grave, because
the antithesis between heaven and the grave would be ridiculous. Nor can it
be so taken in Luke 16, where the rich man is said to have been in hell in
torments.* Prot. 1. We grant it concerning Luke 16. Even if we were also to
admit the same concerning the other passage, nothing would follow from it
either for Bellarmine or against our position. 2. Nevertheless, nothing
prevents destruction and an abject condition from being denoted by the grave
in Matthew 11:23. That antithesis between heaven and the grave, which appears
so ridiculous to Bellarmine, is frequently employed in the Scriptures as to
its sense. For heaven is everywhere opposed to earth, and the glory of heaven
to the ignominy of earth. Thus Isaiah 14:12: *You have fallen from heaven; you
have been cut down to the earth*—in precisely the same sense in which Christ
speaks in Matthew.

19. Bell.: *The Hebrew word Sheol means a

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 108 | printed p. 98 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

chasm, and is ordinarily taken for a subterranean place of souls, and either
rarely or never for the grave.* Prot. 1. If it means a chasm, then it is most
suitably
applied to the grave and death, which devours all mortals. Nor is any other
reason usually given why the grave is called Sheol, from the word *Shaal,*
which means to seek, demand, or desire, than either that the grave seeks all
mortals like a chasm with gaping mouth; or that by reason of mortality all are
borne to it as to the end which they seek; or that those who are laid in
graves are sought and desired by the living. 2. Bellarmine's wavering here is
uncertain and insidious: *It is rarely or never taken for the grave.* Because
of the cloud of witnesses by whom he would be pressed he does not deny that
it is taken for the grave, but says that it is rarely or never so taken—that
is, extremely rarely. For the present case, however, it is sufficient for us
if it is so taken in those passages which treat of the descent of the dead
Christ. 3. He manifestly goes astray in this: 1. He would have this word denote
a place of souls alone, although in the Scriptures it is expressly applied
also to the body. Genesis 42:38: *You will bring my gray hairs down to hell.*
2. He takes it for a place alone and not for a state, although in the
Scriptures it is commonly confounded with death and opposed to life, Isaiah
38:18–19. 3. He declares so confidently that the place of souls is
subterranean, although Ecclesiastes 3:21 expressly intimates that the position
received in the Church even before Christ's advent was that *the human spirit
ascends upward.* 4. It is false that this word ordinarily denotes such a place
in the Scriptures.

20. Bell.: *Genesis 37: I will go down mourning to my son in hell cannot be
taken for the grave, because Jacob thought that his son had been devoured by
wild beasts and lacked a grave.* Prot. 1. Those who are devoured by wild
beasts do not lack a grave, for the beasts' stomachs are their graves. Cicero
calls a certain burial unburied. Certainly the Scriptures openly affirm that
all the dead are in graves, John 5:28: *All who are in the tombs will hear his
voice.* 2. Perhaps it can be read *because of my son,* not *to my son,* as in
1 Samuel 1:27. 3. It is not some place, but the state of death that is properly

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 109 | printed p. 99 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

signified there, which with respect to the body is the state of the grave.

21. Bell.: *Numbers 16: They went down alive into hell, covered with earth;
that is, into the place of the condemned.* Prot. 1. Just as it appears from
the preceding passage that Sheol signifies the place of bodies rather than of
souls, because it treats there of the descent of Jacob's gray hairs, so the
same is also confirmed most certainly from this passage. For not only the
people but also all the things which were theirs—that is, their tents and
furnishings—are said to have descended into Sheol, and these did not reach a
place of souls. 2. Cajetan affirms and proves on Numbers 16 that this place of
their bodies was their grave. This is also evident from the words themselves,
in which *to descend into hell* and *to be covered with earth* designate one
and the same thing. The Psalmist confirms this explanation in Psalm 106:17:
*The earth opened itself, swallowed up, and covered.*

22. Bell.: *Psalm 138: If I ascend into heaven, if I descend into hell. These
two places are extremely far apart, but a grave is not two palms distant from
the surface of the earth and is often even above the earth.* Prot. 1. Those
which are above the earth are not properly graves, but monuments of the
buried. 2. Although graves are usually not far from the surface of the earth,
they are nevertheless extremely far from heaven. 3. All subterranean hiding
places are opposed to heaven as things maximally distant in their own class,
although there are various degrees of height in heaven and of depth beneath
the earth which are not designated in these words. 4. That the place of
bodies, not of souls, is denoted here appears clearly from that expression,
*If I made my bed in Sheol.* For souls do not rest or sleep in hell, but graves
are the beds or couches of bodies.

23. Bell.: *Isaiah 14. It is said to the king of Babylon, and in his figure to
the devil, You will be dragged down even to hell, into the depth of the pit;
although the devil is not in a grave and that king is said to be about to lack
a grave.* Prot. 1. To refer that passage to the devil is nothing other than to
trifle. 2. The king of Babylon is not said to be about to lack every grave,
but to be cast out of his own grave—that is, in no way befitting a king to be

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 110 | printed p. 100 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

buried, as can be gathered from the comparison made in verses 18–20. He at
least had an ass's burial, as did Jehoiakim, Jeremiah 22:19. 3. This
very royal
burial which he would lack is expressly called a descent into Sheol in Ezekiel
32:27. 4. The text itself sufficiently indicates that Sheol here means the
abject state of a dead body, not a place of souls, because *the grub* is said
to *be spread beneath* and *the worm to cover,* verse 11; his descent is also
said to be *into the earth* and *to the sides of the pit,* verses 12 and 15.

24. Bell.: *The Seventy translators rendered Sheol everywhere as ᾅδην and
never τάφον; Jerome everywhere as hell and never the grave; and the Chaldean
as Gehenna. So also did Rabbi David, Aben Ezra, Rabbi Solomon, and almost all
who were capable of judging this matter.* Prot. 1. If what Bellarmine here
affirms about ᾅδης and *hell* is true, then there are as many examples of the
Greek ᾅδης and the Latin *hell* employed in the same sense as there are
examples of the Hebrew word used for the grave—and these are not few. 2. That
they ordinarily rendered one Hebrew word by one Greek and one Latin word
arose from the prudence—perhaps excessively cautious, not to say
superstitious—by which they willed to represent for us the tenor of the
original text, not because they judged that one and the same sense lay beneath
it everywhere. 3. Nevertheless, it is false that the Greeks everywhere
rendered Sheol as ᾅδην, for in 2 Samuel 22:6 and Proverbs 23:14 they render it
θάνατον, *death,* and in Ezekiel 32:21 they render it λάκκον, a pit or grave.
4. It is also false that Jerome everywhere renders it *hell,* for at Hosea
13:14 he renders it *death.* 5. What is boasted about the Chaldean is utterly
false. Careful students of these matters observe that, out of all those Old
Testament passages in which Sheol occurs in Hebrew, the Chaldean paraphrase
renders it *Gehenna* only three times, but most often either renders it as
*grave* or retains it in Hebrew. 6. As for the rabbis, Lyra himself testifies
on Genesis 37:36 that *the Hebrews interpret it as a pit or grave.* These are
also the words of the Jesuit Pererius on the same passage: *It is the opinion
of the Hebrews and of many Christians that the word Sheol signifies nothing
in Scripture except a pit, a grave.* Genebrard himself says on Psalm 85: *The
rabbis who expound it concerning*

*David understand the grave, which is poetically called hell.* 7. But it is
plainly Jesuitical impudence for Bellarmine in this matter to boast of the
agreement of almost all who were capable of judging it. For everyone knows
that Cajetan, Isidore Clarius, Arias Montanus, Sanctes Pagninus, Vatablus,
Mercerus, and many others were quite capable of judging the Hebrew word, and
yet long ago publicly declared against Bellarmine.

25. Bell.: *Calvin himself says in the Psychopannychia that Sheol is sometimes
taken for the grave, but for the most part for the state of the condemned.*
Prot. Calvin's words are changed and his meaning is entirely inverted. For he
says this: *Although the dissolution of the present life is more often
signified by death, and the grave by hell, nevertheless in a usage not
infrequent in Scripture these terms are taken for the wrath of God and
rejection.* Apart from the fact that nothing is declared here about Sheol any
more than about death, as if it signified the state of the condemned, much
less a place, Bellarmine changes *more often* into *sometimes,* and *not
infrequently* into *for the most part.*

26. Bell.: *I prove from the word abyss that there are lower regions besides
graves.* Prot. That proves what none of us has ever denied. But if the word
*abyss* is taken properly, it would prove that the place of the lower regions
is in the waters rather than in the earth.

27. Bell.: *Revelation 5 and Philippians 2. By those who are under the earth
are necessarily understood spirits dwelling in subterranean places.* Prot. 1.
If this is granted, nothing will follow from it against us. 2. Those people
may be denoted here by *those who are under the earth* who, with respect to
that part which more nearly approaches our perception, are beneath the earth
in graves. 3. Nevertheless, Revelation 5:13 expressly explains it as every
creature, or all created things, not spirits.

28. Bell.: *I prove it from the Fathers.* Prot. When many of the Fathers
wished to establish something certain concerning an uncertain matter, they
became altogether vain in their conjectures about the place of hell. For some
willed it to be situated in the lower hemisphere, not within but beneath the
earth; others in the darkened air; others outside the whole

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 112 | printed p. 102 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

created world; and others frankly admitted their ignorance of this matter. So
our Parker shows in *On the Descent*, book 2. Nothing certain can therefore be
proved concerning this matter from their common and constant agreement, which
does not exist.

29. Bell.: *Natural reason is added: because the place of the condemned ought
to be extremely far from the place of the blessed.* Prot. 1. Natural reason
knows nothing of these matters. 2. A distance of state is necessary, not of
place. 3. It is remarkable that the Roman Catholics, forgetful of this natural
reasoning, have placed the blessed Fathers in limbo, or on the upper floor of
the hell of the condemned, with almost no distance at all.
