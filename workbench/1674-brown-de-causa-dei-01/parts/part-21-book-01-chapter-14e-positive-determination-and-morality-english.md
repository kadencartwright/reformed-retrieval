From what has been said it is now clear that all moral character depends upon
God’s precept, and that an action is called morally good or evil in relation
to God’s precept, to which it is conformed or contrary. Among the things which
God commands, however, some are by their own nature fitting and congruous,
seemly, consonant, agreeable, and suited to human nature, its condition, and
its relation to God. Consequently they possess a foundational goodness prior
to the precept. But other things are destitute of this foundational fitness
and suitability and, in this respect, are purely indifferent. Strictly
speaking, we call the former *moral* and the latter *positive*. Not only do the
latter become morally good through the precept, speaking commonly, but the
former too become morally good only by the force and reason of the precept.
For suppose that the former were not commanded: all the aforesaid
foundational goodness could not render those actions morally good. Otherwise
we would have to say that the actions of brute animals which are fitting and
agreeable to their natures are *morally* good, which is absurd.

From this it is evident that a divine determination not only does not
extinguish or erase moral character but instead establishes and confirms it.
Indeed, this divine determination is sometimes nothing other than a clear
explanation and distinct declaration of an action or thing which is fitting
and congruous according to the constitution and law of nature. This necessary
determination and explanation when added, therefore, cannot remove all the
moral character of the law. When the moral law commands something fitting,
suitable, and congruous, a further command which explains, designates, and
determines that suitable and congruous good does not destroy—and cannot
destroy—the moral character of that law and action, but instead confirms it.

This can be explained as follows. It is a moral precept that we worship God,
who is an

[1674 logical printed p. 191 | IA leaf 209 | PDF p. 209]

invisible spirit, in spirit, with a pure mind and in a spiritual and fitting
manner. Yet this moral character is not removed or nullified by God’s express
and superadded determination that images be excluded from divine worship,
since it is morally evil to worship God, who is spirit, in an unseemly manner
wholly unsuited to spirit. And what sane person would say that God’s
particular determination and special prohibition against employing images or
carved figures in divine worship renders a moral precept purely positive?

2. This can also be demonstrated by other examples. It is a moral duty to
pray solemnly to God. Yet many matters concern and attend this duty which
depend upon divine determination, or require it, such as those pertaining to
manner or time; but these do not remove the duty’s moral character. For now,
because of God’s special determination, we are bound to pray to him in Christ
and through Christ, or in his name. Yet this determination of manner does not
remove the action’s moral character, nor do prayers become a merely positive
action. Thus by God’s determination, disclosed to us through the examples of
the saints and in other ways, we are bound to pour out prayers to God in the
morning and evening. Must it therefore be said that this is not a moral duty?
See Ames, *On Conscience*, book 4, chapter 14.

3. Likewise, it is a moral duty to hear God revealing his mind to us, to
listen to him, and to incline our ears. Now that it has pleased him to reveal
his mind to us through the written law and through ministers sent by him and
endowed with authority to explain and apply that law, has the moral character
of the precept been removed because of this free and gracious superadded
determination of God? Must it be said that we are not morally bound to read
and search God’s word and to study and meditate upon it, or to hear its
heralds and ministers?

4. It is moral that we humble ourselves under God’s mighty hand (1 Peter 5:6)
and submit ourselves to God (James 4:7). Since, therefore, God has determined
by his express law that when some grave judgment threatens, or a severe
plague is inflicted because of a grave offense, we should testify to our
humiliation by a religious fast, this duty too must be called not merely
positive but moral (Joel 2:12–13; 1 Corinthians 7:5), as was shown above.

5. In the same way, it is moral for the more learned to teach the unlearned.
When, therefore, God has determined by his law that the word is to be preached
publicly by those duly called (Romans 10:15; Hebrews 5:4; 1 Corinthians
12:28–29; 1 Timothy 3:10; 4:3; 5:22), will its moral character therefore
vanish?

6. No one will deny that it is moral for us to praise God. But God has
determined that we celebrate his praise “with psalms, hymns, and spiritual
songs” (Ephesians 5:19; Colossians 3:16). Shall we therefore say that the
action of celebrating God in this manner is not moral but purely positive?

From these examples, to offer no more, it is sufficiently clear that a divine
determination by no means removes the moral character of an action. This is
even clearer from the fact that those positive or superadded precepts
sometimes not only confirm, restore, and strengthen the prior obligation, but
also show the manner in which the required duty is to be rightly performed
and direct its practice. How absurd it is, therefore, to think that through

[1674 logical printed p. 192 | IA leaf 210 | PDF p. 210]

this direction the prior law loses all its force, or that through the addition
of this direction the law becomes merely positive.

Indeed, we see that this is so even when a human determination intervenes.

1. The moral law requires me to exhibit and render honor, reverence, fear, and
obedience in the Lord to my superiors. Yet it depends upon a human
determination that the person who is now my superior is not my equal, as he
was before, or my inferior. Notwithstanding this determination, it is moral
that I exhibit to the person who is now my superior the honor, reverence,
fear, and so forth which are due to him.

2. The moral law also requires subjects to pay taxes and tribute to
magistrates (Romans 13:6–7; Matthew 22:21). The amount of tax and tribute,
however, depends upon the free determination of human beings. Shall we
therefore say that the command to pay taxes has been changed and becomes not
moral but positive and human?

3. Likewise, the moral law requires us to render the duties owed to the
supreme magistrate, whatever form it may take—whether one person, or a body
and several persons. Yet a free human determination intervenes here,
establishing a monarchical form rather than an aristocratic or democratic
one. Notwithstanding that determination, the moral law remains in force, and
by the force of that law the subject is bound to render the obedience and
homage due to that supreme power. Dr. Burmann himself asserted the same thing
above.

4. Our adversary will not deny that the moral law requires each person to be
given what is his. Yet before this is put into effect and obedience is
rendered to it, a free human determination in the division of things is
necessarily required so that what belongs to each individual may become known
and “mine” and “yours” may be established. What then? Will he say that this
law concerning the rendering to each person of what is his is for that reason
purely positive and not moral?

5. Furthermore, we see that one and the same duty is commanded to us both by
the law of nature, and consequently by the moral law, and also by an express
and positive precept. But who will say that the law of nature is rendered void
by this superadded precept? Or who will say that these precepts are therefore
not moral?

6. Nature dictates that every human society ought to hold solemn assemblies
for the worship of God, as will readily be conceded. Yet this is frequently
enjoined upon the Church of Christ in the New Testament, just as it was
commanded in the Old Testament. Who can infer from this that precepts
superadded in this way are not moral, or that we are not bound by the law of
nature to render obedience to them?

7. Nature also dictates that in every society there should be some who are
superior to and rule over others. For where all are independent and wish to
rule, the society is overturned. Christ likewise instituted such an order in
his Church and appointed pastors, teachers, governors, rulers, and leaders—
for they possess various titles, as the gospel teaches us. Shall we therefore
say that this is a purely and wholly positive institution and that the law of
nature has no force here?

8. The natural and moral law requires inferiors to honor and attend with
other due offices upon their superiors and governors. Shall we say that this
law does not

[1674 logical printed p. 193 | IA leaf 211 | PDF p. 211]

bind members of the Church because Christ specifically enjoins them to honor,
esteem, love, and obey the rulers of the Church?

9. Nature dictates that in every society all public affairs should be
conducted in an orderly and decent manner. The Apostle likewise positively
requires this to be done in the Church (1 Corinthians 14). Shall we therefore
say that this command is not moral?

10. Nature also teaches, and divine and natural law require, that appeals or
petitions for review be made from a lower to a higher court. See Whitaker,
*Controversy* 4, question 4, chapter 2. Christ’s positive law also teaches us
that this ought to be done in the Church (Acts 15; Matthew 18). Will the prior
precept be nullified by this superadded one?

11. It is a dictate of the law of nature that every society possesses the
power to expel the insubordinate, or those who refuse to submit themselves to
the society’s equitable and just laws, and to deprive them of its privileges.
This is also expressly and positively enjoined upon the governors of the
Church: namely, that they excommunicate the scandalous and refractory and
strip them of ecclesiastical privileges (Matthew 18; 1 Corinthians 5; 2
Corinthians 2; Revelation 2–3). Will it therefore follow that this command is
purely and wholly positive, or that the prior dictate of nature has no force
here?

From these things it is clear that a superadded institution by no means
removes the prior general obligation, nor does it make any change in the
nature of the duty required and commanded, although it directs us more
specifically in its exercise and teaches us to direct all things toward
special ends consonant with the gospel, to which the light of nature would
never lead or carry us.

It is thus clearly evident—as we undertook to explain and prove—that a natural
or moral law does not lose its force because some positive precept is
superadded to it. And a duty which a positive precept more specifically shows
and explains to us is not purely and wholly positive but moral, when it is
founded upon a more general precept of the natural or moral law. For it is
absurd to think that a duty once enjoined by the natural or moral law changes
its nature because of a superadded positive command, since that superadded
command only directs us in rightly performing the required duty and serves as
an aid so that the general precept may be properly applied and put into
practice without error. This can be further illustrated and confirmed as
follows.

1. The natural law dictates that God is to be worshiped in a fitting manner,
and the second precept of the Decalogue requires this. Yet we do not know—such
is our natural blindness—the fitting manner in which he ought to be
worshiped. A positive precept comes to our aid and shows us the manner which
is pleasing and acceptable to God. What then? Because that positive precept
makes the character and nature of the natural precept clearer to us and
directs us to render the required obedience without error, shall we say that
the moral law does not bind us to observe devoutly and accurately the now
revealed manner of worshiping God?

2. The law of nature commands us to obey God in all things. When, therefore,
he shows us his will through a positive precept and presents a particular
command, and we violate that precept, is not the law of nature itself
violated—the law which commands us to render obedience to God in all things?

[1674 logical printed p. 194 | IA leaf 212 | PDF p. 212]

There is therefore no basis whatever for saying that every positive command,
or every determination annexed to a moral command, changes the nature of that
precept and of the duty entrusted by it, and renders merely positive what was
previously natural or moral.

3. For that positive precept not only does not weaken the moral precept, but
sometimes also explains and declares to us what the light of nature would
never show us. Yet once this has been shown and declared, it appears most
fitting and congruous with reason and excellently founded in nature itself.
Who then will say that these things are not moral which, by the aid of a
positive precept, are drawn from the very recesses of nature and shown to rest
upon natural reason? And who will say that they do not bind us just as fully
as other things which can be recognized without the aid of a positive
precept?

4. Furthermore, that positive precept sometimes only sets forth and enjoins
the manner and circumstances of a duty prescribed to us in the natural or
moral law. It therefore does not weaken but instead strengthens the natural
precept. And by the force of the natural law we are bound devoutly to observe
that manner and the circumstances commanded by the positive precept. I do not
say that everything enjoined by such a positive precept is natural or moral,
strictly speaking—as we explained above. But I do affirm that we are bound,
by the force of the natural command which requires our submission to God in
all things, duly to observe those prescribed circumstances; and that this
determination of manner and circumstances does not change the nature and
substance of the duty or render it something purely and wholly positive.

5. Moreover, that positive precept sometimes only instructs, teaches, and
directs us more fully how a duty prescribed by the moral law may rightly be
performed to the glory of God. Who, however, will therefore say that the duty
itself prescribed by the moral law loses its moral character and that the
moral precept is changed into a positive one?

6. Indeed, when a positive precept adds something accidental to a moral duty,
it does not destroy the moral duty or strip it of its moral character. Instead
it confirms the duty and, by superinducing this new obligation also to observe
the incidental thing annexed to it, renders the duty firmer. We could, if
necessary, demonstrate this in the case of very many moral duties to which
something incidental had been annexed through positive precepts in accordance
with the character of the Church’s different conditions.

We therefore cannot concede that God’s positive determination, annexed to a
moral precept for the aforesaid or similar ends, changes the nature of the
precept and of the duty entrusted through it and makes it positive. For an
intervening positive determination by human beings cannot effect such a
change. Indeed, an intervening positive and express human law—much less a
person’s free determination—cannot weaken and render void any law of God, let
alone a moral law, as is readily conceded by everyone whose head is sound.

Yet those who maintain that God’s intervening free determination removes the
moral character of a law must consistently assert that, in part, all the
precepts of the second table are positive and nonmoral. For in almost all of
them some free human determination appears as an attendant upon certain
duties. But human will and determination is not as friendly to moral character
as divine determination is, since the latter, as was said above, can render
morally good an action which would otherwise be indifferent. We illustrate
this as follows.

[1674 logical printed p. 195 | IA leaf 213 | PDF p. 213 | ends before CAP. XV.]

First, as far as the fifth precept of the Decalogue is concerned, we saw above
that it depends upon the free determination of human beings which persons are
superiors and supreme authorities in the government of a commonwealth.

Next, as far as the sixth is concerned, theologians refer to it “the sober and
moderate use of food, drink, medicine, sleep, labor, and recreation, and so
forth.” See the *Westminster Larger Catechism*, where this moral duty is
confirmed by these testimonies of Scripture: Proverbs 25:16, 27; 1 Timothy
5:23; Isaiah 38:21; Psalm 127:2; Ecclesiastes 5:12; 2 Thessalonians 3:10, 12;
Proverbs 16:26; Ecclesiastes 3:4, 11. Yet here a physician’s determination
sometimes intervenes. What then? Shall we say that this moral precept
concerning the moderate use of these things does not bind us because this
human determination intervenes or attends upon it? Or shall we say that, with
respect to these matters, this precept is merely human and not moral?

Third, a similar thing appears in the seventh precept, which I will show by
this single example. In the catechism just named, the aforesaid theologians
refer to it “modesty in clothing,” which the Apostle also commands in 1
Timothy 2:9. This modesty, however, depends at least in part upon the variable
usage and custom of a place, so that a form of dress which here and now would
signify some immodesty would elsewhere, or in the same region at another
time, display no immodesty. Nevertheless, we are not permitted to pronounce
that moral precept of the Apostle mutable because of this mutable custom.

Fourth, theft is condemned in the eighth precept. But if all things were held
in common—which is not contrary to nature—there would be no theft, or at least
not theft of the kind that now exists. Yet property and ownership were
introduced by the free determination and agreement of human beings. Will
theft therefore not be a moral sin? The jurists teach us that it is. Paul,
*Digest*, book 47, title 2, law 1, *On Thefts*; Ulpian, *Digest*, book 50,
title 16, *On the Meaning of Words*: theft is contrary to the law of nature.

Fifth, we likewise have an example in the ninth precept. For the theologians
cited above refer to it, in the same place, “the pronouncing of truth in
accordance with justice and equity,” according to God’s law in Leviticus
19:15. Yet this justice and equity depends upon the free human determination
which establishes ownership and property. Nevertheless, no one will say that
this precept is not moral.

Sixth, from the same foundation it is clear that the tenth precept too, in
certain respects, should not be called moral. For when “mine and yours,” which
depends upon the free determination of human beings, is removed, the
foundation of this precept with respect to those external goods will likewise
be removed.

We may therefore freely conclude that a positive determination of God
superadded to a moral law by no means weakens it or renders it nonmoral. This
was what had to be demonstrated.
