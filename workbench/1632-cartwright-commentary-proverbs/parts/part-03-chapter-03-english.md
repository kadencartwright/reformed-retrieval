CHAPTER THREE.

Having set promises of long life and prosperity before those who observe and
keep his doctrine in the first two verses, God treats them more particularly
throughout the rest of the chapter.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - a general and concise exhortation to embrace God’s doctrine, verses 1–2;
  - a particular and expanded explanation of the same in the following
    verses.
- In the former are considered:
  - the observance of the commandments, verse 1;
  - the attached promise, verse 2.

Verse 1. My son, do not forget my doctrine; but let your mind keep my
commandments.

Solomon was furnished with an infinite variety and abundance of material. He
could repeat one and the same thing with such variation in wording and form of
speech that it scarcely appeared to be the same. Nevertheless, to provide for
the godly, he repeats one and the same thing in almost the same words.

In observing the commandments, it must be noted, first, that the word תורה
means not merely the law of commandments but the entire body of doctrine in
which the salvation of the elect is contained. Second, “forgetting” means not
the memory’s frailty in retaining commandments, but neglecting to express the
doctrine in life and deeds, just as in the preceding chapter the adulteress
was said to have forgotten God’s covenant. This is also known from the
opposite member: namely, that he should observe it with his mind or inward
affection. Hence those who forget God are also regarded as ungodly. For the
seed of the word laid up in a scarcely active memory rots there and becomes
useless, or rather harmful; but when hidden and sent down into the deepest
recesses of the heart, it takes root and bears fruit.

Verse 2. For they will add to you length of days, a life of many years, and
peace.

[Analytical diagram: The attached promise has two parts: long life;
prosperity.]

Here long life and prosperity are established as the reward of obedience to
God. So Psalm 34. From this it is clear that godliness has promises for the
present and future life. And even if there were no promise for the present
life, the most abundant blessing of the future life would amply compensate
all our labor and zeal in this respect. The Sadducees were concerned about
the law solely on account of the advantages of the present life; and will we
not be moved by promises for both lives? Next, to discuss each separately,
first it is remarkable how people exert themselves to live as long as
possible: what care they exercise in choosing food, drink, clothing, physical
exercise, and medicine, and how they often visit baths far away, and so forth.
Meanwhile they neglect God, the author and giver of life, and cast behind
their backs the observance of the commandments, which is adorned with this
promise. Hence those who abound in all the supports of life very often become
ὀλιγόβιοι [short-lived].

[1632 witness | PDF p. 31 | printed columns 36–37]

It must not be passed over that doctrine and commandments, when observed, add
years and long life. Thus in the fifth commandment parents are said to prolong
the life of obedient children. It is as though he said that these will serve
in place of diet, medicine, guarding, watchmen, and other such means of
preserving life; and that if those ordinary supports of life are less
available, God will nevertheless preserve the life of those who make use of
these as his ministry. From this it is evident how unskillfully those act who
do not care greatly for their children’s godliness—indeed, most do not even
desire it—because they regard some outstanding godliness as an omen of
premature death. Thus, just as they do not wish their children to be without
hope, they also fear for them to be of great promise in godliness. It is true
that such people are sometimes withdrawn from life lest they themselves be
corrupted, or experience something dreadful, or because the world is unworthy
of them. Nevertheless, the Lord perseveres in fulfilling his promise, just as
if someone gave one hundred acres of land to a man to whom he had promised
only one.

The other part of the promise is peace, that is, the comfort of the present
life and outward prosperity. Thus these are joined in Psalm 34. Without this
peace and prosperity life is not, so to speak, living. For if someone living a
long life should become a wandering fugitive like Cain, and continually be
terrified by the stings of divine wrath, who would not have preferred death?
See Job 2 and 3. Here the same complaint that we made above must be renewed—
indeed, especially because they imagine that they secure their peace by
violating the Sabbath. Yet a great difficulty still arises here, since we see
that the godly are for the most part poor and subject to many calamities in
this life. It must first be understood, however, that outward peace, which was
especially suited to the law’s pedagogy, foreshadows peace and tranquility of
mind, which is a continual feast. Next, it must be noted from Psalm 37 and one
or two passages of Proverbs that the little possessed by the righteous
outweighs the greatest riches of the wicked.

Verse 3. Let kindness and faithfulness not forsake you: bind them around your
neck; inscribe them upon the tablet of your heart.

v. 4. Thus obtain favor and excellent success in the sight of God and human
beings.

The other part follows, a particular explanation both of the commandments and
of the promises comprised in verses 1 and 2. First, he distributes the
commandments into kindness toward human beings and truth or faithfulness
toward God, and exhorts that these never depart from us. They are so joined
together that they must never be torn apart. Here he convicts some because
they do not maintain justice, temperance, and sobriety; and others because,
although sufficiently dutiful toward human beings, they are ungrateful and
faithless toward God, neither seeking after his worship nor discharging what
is the duty of the godly.

He adds a promise which is the cause of the former general promise: namely,
favor and prosperity both with God and with human beings. For תורה will add
favor and prosperity with God, and kindness will heap up the same among human
beings. From this will follow both long life and prosperity. Thus David found
favor with God and people, even Saul’s ungodly courtiers; he even found favor
with Saul, his desperate enemy, by kindness

[1632 witness | PDF p. 32 | printed columns 38–39]

and humanity toward him. He found the same also with the kings of the
Philistines and Moabites. But an outstanding example of this matter appears
in Joseph and Moses, who enjoyed the highest favor among the ungodly
Egyptians. These things are also said of Christ when still a child and of the
apostles in Acts 2 and 5. Here let us examine ourselves anew: what favor do we
have with God? Although not all who enjoy peace and outward prosperity are
therefore acceptable to God, nevertheless, if God is more restricted toward
us even in outward benefits, much more in gifts of the Spirit, it must surely
be concluded that he has been offended by us. And what favor do we have with
people? We must not only stop every mouth by our innocence and integrity so
that no one may justly curse us, but also show ourselves so prudent, humane,
and friendly that, if possible, no one may do so even unjustly.

Verse 5. Trust in Jehovah with your whole heart, but do not lean upon your own
understanding.

Of the two duties proposed, one toward our neighbor and the other toward God,
he treats the latter first, according to the Hebrew manner, since it is far
prior in dignity. Intending to declare an enumeration of some parts of divine
worship, he begins with inward worship, which is principal, and by synecdoche
comprehends them all in one of its parts. The first part of divine worship is
trust in God, which he commands before all else. This command could appear for
the most part superfluous. For someone will say, “Who is so ungodly that he
does not trust God?” Yet since God commands this frequently, fortifies trust
in himself with a promise, and even seals it with an oath for greater
certainty, it is readily apparent that trusting God is not so commonplace. We
will see what kind of trust this is once we understand what it means to trust
God. Trust in God is that by which we rest in him for the attainment of every
good and the warding off of evil that will be advantageous to us while we
remain in the station of our calling. Let us therefore see how we have
performed this. We will do so if we review our life and inquire into all its
parts; if we remember how we trembled in honorable causes because the means by
which we could have been safe failed us; and especially if we weigh the manner
commanded here: namely, that we do it not with words but with the mind, not
through hypocrisy and some distraction, but wholly and sincerely—which is
what “with the whole heart” means here.

This is illustrated by its opposite: one must not lean upon one’s own
understanding. By this one species he condemns every vain confidence. Thus
בכל־לב in this passage means not the perfect trust required by the law, but
true and genuine trust. He therefore condemns trust in strength, friends,
riches, one’s own holiness, and so forth. For since no one can serve God and
Mammon, and trust in God is a part of the service rendered to him, who does
not see that no one can trust God and his own understanding at the same time?
Trust in idols is most opposed to trust in God, yet he says nothing about it
here, because that sin is grosser and not the sort into which God’s worshipers
commonly fall. But trusting in our own understanding is a subtler and finer
kind of idolatry in which very many are unwittingly ensnared, as also by
attributing far too much to their own powers, holiness, sacraments, and
external works. It is evident that we are infected with this vain confidence,
because where supports are present we cheerfully undertake things ordinarily
accomplished by them; where they are absent, we immediately give up; and where
they are uncertain, we hesitate. Moreover, he seems to illustrate this
opposite by a similitude when he intimates that, in each person’s opinion, his
own understanding is a staff upon which he may lean.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 33 | printed columns 40–41]

Verse 6. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will direct your paths.

He declares another part of truth toward God in divine worship to be this: a
person should acknowledge God throughout the whole course of his life and in
all his actions; that is, he should always take God into counsel in whatever
he undertakes and should undertake nothing inconsistent with God’s will. Thus
we see that David, the other godly kings, and the patriarchs consulted God’s
oracle when they undertook something of greater importance. David also
affirms that he tested all his ways by the standard of the word, Psalm 119,
section 8. Here, therefore, we are again prosecuted as guilty of contempt for
the divine majesty, since we never diligently invoke God in matters which seem
to lie within our own powers—neither in administering the family, the
commonwealth, nor the church. If we ever approach him, it is only when we
judge ourselves unequal to carrying something out because of its difficulty
or unfamiliarity. Hence many either do not ask for daily bread or ask so
coldly that by their petition they call down evil upon themselves.

A promise is attached to this command: the ways and arrangements of the life
of one who consults God will be straight and complete. He will never depart
from the shortest road or wander here and there through byways so as to fail
to obtain what he seeks and pursues, or fail to obtain it with a great saving
of time. For this life is like a desert and we are like pilgrims; because we
do not take God into counsel, it happens that in forty years we scarcely
complete that journey of godliness and happiness which we could have
traversed in the space of a few days.

Verse 7. Do not be wise in your own judgment; revere Jehovah and depart from
evil.

He illustrates the former command by its opposite: “Do not be wise,” and so
forth. A man who wishes to become wise must first become foolish in order to
become wise. Beware, therefore, how much you attribute to your depraved
reason, unless you test it by the standard of God’s word. This must especially
be guarded against in religion, because, however sharply a person may see
other things with the eye of reason, he nevertheless wanders most shamefully
in religion unless, distrusting his own reason, he seeks eyesalve from God.
Τῆς σαρκὸς φρόνημα, “the mind of the flesh,” is hostile to God and cannot be
subjected to him. Yet we see some who, although as blind as moles in the
knowledge of God’s word, trust in their own prudence and pronounce on the
things of God with as much security and confidence as if they had grown old
in God’s word—especially if they have snatched a little word from sacred
Scripture somewhere with which to cloak their own opinion.

There also seems to be an anticipation here, whose tacit doubt is gathered as
follows: “Although I know that this is precisely commanded me from God’s word,
nevertheless, by departing just a little, I will arrange the matter so safely
that no evil will result from it; at least God’s mercy will support me in my
slight departure.” The answer is: Fear God, and so forth. For just as God is
merciful, so he is also terrible to those who encourage themselves to sin by
this hope—namely, that they will escape.

Verse 8. It will be health to your navel and irrigation to your bones.

Here he further explains a part of the whole which he set down in verse 2 with
the word שלום. Bodily health is no small part of outward peace, without which
neither length of days, riches, nor honor are pleasant. He denotes the health
of the whole body outwardly by the navel and inwardly by the bones: the navel
as a sort of clasp by which the sinews of the upper and lower parts of the
body are connected, and the bones because they are, as it were, the body’s
foundations.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 34 | printed columns 42–43]

If they are well, surely the whole person will fare better. From this we see
that the safest and most delightful medicine is sought from godliness of life
and the worship of God. Therefore, just as God’s commandments serve us as
counselors, escort, and watchmen, so they also serve as medicine both
therapeutic (θεραπευτική) and health-preserving (ὑγιεινή). See the examples of
Moses and Caleb, who had a green and vigorous old age.

Verse 9. Honor Jehovah from your substance and from the chief part of your
entire produce.

Another part of divine worship is generosity for maintaining God’s worship,
especially for supporting the ministry through which that worship is
preserved. To add spurs to this generosity, he employs an argument from its
subject: namely God, to whom the things pertaining to the ministry of worship
are owed. Thus those who withhold tithes or firstfruits from priests or
Levites are said to rob God, Matthew 3:8. [The printed citation reads
`Matt. 3. 8.`; the context points to Malachi 3:8.] It must also be noted here
that he
requires all the chief and best things. Here the world is convicted of the
greatest ingratitude: people reluctantly contribute anything, or contribute
all the worst things, and believe that they have made the ministry indebted
to them, although God declares that it was given to himself, not the
ministers—unless perhaps they are so shameless as to wish in this way to make
God himself indebted to them.

Verse 10. Thus your storehouses will be filled with plenty, and your winepress
vats will burst with new wine.

Here there is an anticipation whose tacit objection is gathered as follows:
“But we will be impoverished by this generosity.” The answer is: No, your
storehouses will be filled in this way, and so forth. See the example of the
widow of Zarephath. See also Haggai 1 and 2.

Verse 11. My son, do not despise the instruction of Jehovah, nor grow weary of
his correction:

v. 12. For Jehovah corrects the one he loves, and indeed as a father corrects
a son in whom he delights.

Here he meets a tacit objection by which the truth of the preceding doctrine
could be undermined: “But we see the most devout worshipers of God subject to
many calamities.” The answer is that they are chastisements and rebukes, not
the punishments and vengeance of an angry God, and that they proceed from
God’s love. He illustrates this by a simile, for in correcting his own God
conducts himself no differently than a father toward a son whom he loves.

Two things, therefore, are considered here: first, the exhortation; and then
the reasons by which it is supported and sustained. The exhortation is that a
believer or godly person should neither despise God’s chastening hand nor grow
weary of it. From this it is clear that by nature we are inclined either to
despise inflicted blows as light, supposing we can repel and shake them off
without trouble, or through faintheartedness to collapse beneath their
weight. From the one source people are found careless and presumptuous; from
the other, devoid of hope. Both are opposed to true patience. Scripture
contains countless examples of the former, testifying that this insensibility
(ἀναισθησίαν) is found not only in the wicked, who imagine themselves finely
armed against every arrow of God, but also often creeps unawares upon the
godly. For people are commonly like drunkards who do not feel the blows laid
upon them. This happens especially with chastisements inflicted by words,
which they consider lighter than wind, and also with those inflicted by
stripes if these are not among the harsher sort that sting more sharply,
[1632 witness | PDF p. 35 | printed columns 44–45]

but gnaw like a moth. At times the godly have hardened themselves even under
the heavier rods of God, as Jonah did when he fell asleep after the storm
arose.

He also notes another defect contrary to the former: when too much is
attributed to God’s chastisements, whether applied by speech or by hand, so
that people are seized with madness from affliction, deprived of memory, lose
every hope of a better condition, or burst into expressions that bear witness
to an impatient mind, as Cain did.

He supplies an antidote for the former when he affirms that God—who is to be
feared in his smallest rods, even in words—is the author of afflictions. For
if some have died at the threat of a prince, much more must one tremble at the
voice of the King of kings. Therefore, to shake off carelessness and vain
confidence, let us remember that our business is with God.

Against the other pest of true patience, it is suggested, first, that
chastisement is not punishment. This is illustrated by the likeness of a
father’s administration toward his son. See Hebrews 12. The other argument is
sought from the cause: namely, God’s love before the foundations of the world
were laid. Nor is it an impediment that David and other parents, from
excessive love for their children, spared them when they sinned gravely,
because that love is more truly called hatred, as Solomon says elsewhere,
Proverbs 13. The thought of divine love is therefore like oil: chastisement
anointed with it is borne much more equably.

Verse 13. Blessed is the man who attains wisdom, and the man who advances
understanding.

There follows an exposition of the words (ἔκθεσις τῶν λόγων) to celebrate the
praises of true wisdom,
that he may add spurs to those zealous for it so they eagerly embrace it and
hold it tenaciously. To affect them all the more, he begins the matter with an
exclamation by which they may be seized with astonished admiration for its
excellence. “O how blessed,” he says, “is the man who finds this true wisdom,
and the man who, expressing understanding in deeds, brings it out before all
and into the open!” And since it is the most excellent of all things, as that
which renders a person blessed, its discovery is moreover most certain.
Unlike what ordinarily occurs in other matters, here by God’s promise everyone
who seeks finds; it is opened to the one who knocks; the one who asks receives.

Verse 14. For its commerce is better than commerce in silver, and its produce
than mined gold.

v. 15. It is more precious than rubies, and all your delights cannot be
equaled to it.

He enlarges the praises of true wisdom by a fourfold comparison from the less.
Three are set down adorned with a particular gradation; the fourth is general
and comprehends the preceding three together with other things of that kind.
This is taught by an enumeration of parts. For since it bestows length of days,
riches, honors, delights, and peace in this life, and in the future life
restores us to the happiness lost in Adam, it is evident that those imbued
with this wisdom are perfectly happy.

Verse 16. Length of days is in its right hand; in its left hand are riches and
honor.

Wisdom is declared to be a more excellent commerce from its adjuncts. By an
allegorical metaphor he imposes upon Wisdom the person of a woman or queen who
holds long life in her right hand, and honor and riches in her left,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 36 | printed columns 46–47]

with which she may endow those zealous for her. Here, therefore, the
perversity of people must be scourged: they not only seek virtue after money,
but further suppose that there is no shorter road to beggary or disgrace than
applying one’s mind to godliness or true wisdom.

Verse 17. Its ways are ways of pleasantness, and all its paths are peace.

He shows the same from its effects, because its ways are full of the sweetest
consolation and pleasure. Here he meets a tacit objection. Someone may say
that understanding wisdom is indeed full of pleasure, but that it will be
bitter when a person brings it into the open. The answer is as before. Thus
Matthew 11: “My yoke is easy.” And since good works make us certain of our
election before the foundations of the world were laid, they cannot fail to
flood us with the most delightful pleasure. This convicts those who take no
pleasure in observing the commandments or in knowing the gospel. It is
commonly said that the one who joins usefulness with pleasure has carried
every point. Since wisdom supplies this, why do we not esteem it, acquire it,
and rejoice when we have obtained it?

Verse 18. It is a tree of life to those who grasp it; in short, everyone who
holds it is blessed.

Another effect of wisdom is that it restores us to the life lost in Adam. He
also illustrates this by the likeness of the tree of life planted in
Paradise, yet on this condition: that we both grasp it eagerly and hold it
tenaciously.

Verse 19. Jehovah founded the earth by wisdom; by understanding he established
the heavens.

Verse 20. By his knowledge the depths were divided, and the clouds above drip
dew.

[Analytical diagram]

- From verse 19 to 21 two things are contained:
  - a repetition of the exhortation;
  - a reason for the exhortation:
    - the antecedent, with its reason;
    - the subsequent, with its reasons.
- There are, first:
  - that the wisdom he praises is the same by which God made all things;
  - second, its effect—namely, that it accomplishes in and for us, in the
    administration of our affairs, what it accomplished in God for the
    administration of his affairs.

In verse 19 the excellence of wisdom is taught from the fact that it has God
as its author, namely Christ, the wisdom of the Father. So James 1:5. If the
wisdom of Solomon, which showed itself in the investigation of natural
things, would carry great weight from its author had it been transmitted to
our age, how much greater weight must this wisdom have, ennobled by God as its
author! Therefore, if disgust with this wisdom should ever steal upon us, let
us immediately turn the eyes of the mind to the fountain from which it flowed.
For since this wisdom is the daughter of the almighty and most wise God, and
as it were the full sister of that divine wisdom which shines in the creation
and government of the world, and so forth, how unworthy it would be not to
pursue it with every affection!

Next he commends it from effects illustrated by a comparison from equals. For
just as the Son of God, the wisdom of the Father, founded all things by his
wisdom and administers those things that pertain to vindicating his majesty
and glory, so by his wisdom in human beings, infused into them by his Spirit,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 37 | printed columns 48–49]

he accomplishes all the things that God undertook to provide suitably for the
worshipers of wisdom. Therefore, if our faith should ever hesitate whether by
zeal for and obedience to true wisdom we will obtain the things God promised,
let us immediately turn our eyes to those works which God skillfully and
thoroughly perfected by wisdom in the creation and administration of the
world. In the absolute perfection of those works through wisdom, as in a
mirror, let us contemplate what Christ will accomplish in us, by the aid and
support of this heavenly wisdom, unto supreme and complete blessedness. I do
not mean that we should be held by any expectation of either creating or
preserving heaven or earth, for this is not fitting for us and no promise of
it exists. Rather, we should believe that the same power of divine wisdom in
us will work toward perpetual salvation and happiness which we experienced in
Christ for undertaking and fully discharging those works. It is as though
someone, having seen the generosity and munificence of his king toward princes
and nobles, does not thereby infer or conclude that he himself will be loaded
with equal gifts, yet easily conjectures from it that he will be rewarded
according to his own place, rank, and due proportion. Indeed, what if God by
this wisdom which he commended to us should accomplish greater and ampler
things, if needed, than he accomplished in creating and governing the world?
For he created heaven and earth for the sake of the godly, not the godly for
their sake.

Verse 21. My son, let them not depart from your eyes; keep that which is truly
wisdom, and prudence.

Here he returns to the preceding exhortation, that all should embrace this
wisdom and always hold it before their eyes like a frontlet or a ring upon the
finger. They must never allow the eye and the mind’s attention to turn away
from it even in the least, either to the right or the left. Rather, just as in
creating and governing the world God always has his wisdom joined and
adhering to himself and makes all things according to its prescription, so we
also should employ this as the standard by which we test all our affairs, so
that it may be a delight to our eyes, by whose sight we are never sated.

Here note that the nouns תושיה and מזמה from the latter hemistich must be
brought into the former, as commonly happens in Proverbs.

Verse 22. And they will be life to your soul and grace to your throat.

In this verse and the following verses through 27 he particularly recalls the
effects of wisdom toward her worshipers, corresponding to those things which
God accomplishes with the aid of his wisdom. First he teaches what will happen
to them in soul and body, then what will happen in all the affairs and
undertakings of their life. As to the former member, he teaches that it will
go well with the soul, which is the chief part of a person, and intimates that
without this wisdom it is dead, however much it animates and invigorates the
body. Just as the body without the soul is a corpse, so the soul without
wisdom is something dead and corpse-like. Hence the dead are said to be raised
from the dead by hearing this wisdom, John 5:25.

Next, he adds grace and dignity to the throat, by which he synecdochically
denotes the entire body. This also comprehends health, for bodies which possess
comeliness and dignity must be healthy.

Verse 23. Then you will travel your way securely, and you will not strike your
foot.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 38 | printed columns 50–51]

In their affairs he promises two things: that they will be free both from
evils and from fear of evil. He proves the former by induction. Whether in
motion or at rest, in business or leisure, by day or night, everything will
proceed prosperously. He proves the former part concerning business by a
comparison from the less: the person will not even dash himself against a
stone. See the example of Peter in Acts 12, how pleasantly and securely he
slept when, loaded with iron and chains in prison, he awaited death the
following day.

Verse 24. When you lie down, you will not be afraid; but when you lie down,
your sleep will be sweet.

v. 25. Do not fear sudden terror, nor the devastation of the wicked when it
comes.

v. 26. For Jehovah will be present as your confidence, and will preserve your
foot from capture.

In these verses he teaches that the person will be immune from the fear by
which the wicked are dismayed. The reason for this immunity is given: not that
he trusts in himself and his own wisdom by which he rightly directs his
affairs, but that God is his confidence and will deliver him both from ambushes
and violent attacks. Hence David says in Psalm 4: “I will lie down and sleep in
peace, because you alone, Jehovah, make me dwell securely.”

Verse 27. Do not withhold good from those who have need, when it is in the
power of your hand to do it.

Thus far he has treated the former part of divine wisdom, which concerns the
true and lawful worship of God. The other part follows, concerning kindness
toward one’s neighbor. To the exhortation to pursue kindness he adds flight
from its contrary, intimating the unhappiness of the latter and the blessing
of the former from the Lord.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - an exhortation to duties of humanity;
  - in which are two things:
    - an exhortation to kindness toward one’s neighbor;
    - a dissuasion from inflicting evil;
  - the rewards of the same from God.

The exhortation is not to withhold kindness, whether in word or deed, in
yourself or in another, lasting or momentary, bodily or spiritual, from the
person to whom it belongs by right as to its rightful owner—not by civil law,
but by divine—when it lies within your ability to supply it with both hands.
For since the law teaches that even his ass laboring and lying beneath a
burden must be helped, much more must the man himself be helped. And if labor
must be given to draw his ox out of the mire, much more must you release your
neighbor himself when you see him fixed in the mire of sin and error.

Next, it must also be noted that the person who does not supply good to his
neighbor according to his ability must be considered to have withheld it.
To this is added the Savior’s statement: “He who is not with me is against
me.”

It must further be noted that he says a person who needs our assistance is,
to the extent we can supply it, the rightful owner and possessor of that aid,
and that we are like debtors and servants to him. Not that he can claim it by
his own right, but because we must pay it as a debt; for just as we owe love
to everyone, Romans 13:8, so we also owe the fruits of love.

Finally, when he says, “provided it lies in the power of both hands,” he

called laborious, 1 Thessalonians 1:3.

Verse 28. Do not say to your neighbor, “Go away and return presently; for
tomorrow I will give it,” when it is in your possession.

It is not enough for someone to be kind unless he confers the benefit at the
proper and timely moment. According to the old proverb, “He gives twice who
gives quickly.” “Do good while it is day,” Galatians 6. For you do not know
what tomorrow will bring; perhaps what is within your discretion today will
not be tomorrow. Or, even if it is still in your possession to confer, the
other person can be so overtaken by calamity that your benefit will no longer
profit him. God loves a cheerful and ready giver. Therefore, do not let the
benefit you can confer sleep with you, nor let the beneficiary sleep without
it, since perhaps it will serve him like a covering laid over him to keep him
warm. Those are rebuked here who defer their kindness until death, resembling
pigs, which are never useful before slaughter.

Verse 29. Do not devise an evil thing against your neighbor, who dwells
securely with you.

There follows a dissuasion from wrongdoing. The first is that one should not
devise evil against a person who rests in his faithfulness and fears no evil
from him. For while every injury is blameworthy, that injury is worthy of the
greatest rebuke which is done not only to the undeserving, but moreover to one
who suspects no evil. Hence David says: “If I have done evil to one who lives
at peace with me.” And elsewhere: “If an enemy had done this to me, I could
easily have borne it; but the one did it who was joined with me in the worship
of God and in companionship.” This strongly opposes the faithless, upon whom
those they receive rely; and servants who, after bringing the master’s mind to
some confidence in them by their diligence and faithfulness, afterward
deceive him.

Verse 30. Do not contend with a man without cause, if he has not done you evil.

He warns that one must not dispute with an innocent person in confidence of
victory or of the ability to obtain what we unjustly seek. See 1 Corinthians
6; Matthew 5; Luke 12.

Verse 31. Do not envy any violent man, nor choose any of his ways.

He tacitly anticipates an objection which could be raised here. From the fact
that he commands us not to litigate with an innocent person, someone could
infer: “Therefore it is lawful to retaliate for an injury inflicted.” By no
means, he says; the right cheek should rather be turned, Matthew 5. Again the
objector says, “But those who act in this way prosper.” He answers: Beware
that you do not emulate them and choose their practices, abandoning the
upright ways in which you began.

Verse 32. For the obstinate person is an abomination to Jehovah, but his
secret counsel is with the upright.

Now follows the reward of each: both the one who keeps himself within duty and
the one who, contrary to duty, leaps into injustice. He especially teaches why
we should not be turned by emulation of the wicked toward their practices:
God pursues the wicked not merely with hatred, but also with abhorrence. This
teaches us to despise their prosperity. For since God’s hatred adheres to
their delicacies, whether in food

[1632 witness | PDF p. 40 | printed column 54 | ends above the ruled beginning of Chapter IV]

or dress, who would envy their prosperity when they eat, drink, and so forth?
The next point is that the upright and simple person is so loved by God that
he admits him to his secret counsels, John 15:15. Who would not rather be of
lowly condition and possess the prince’s favor along with it than otherwise
be raised to the highest dignity along with his indignation? See Psalm 4:6.
Let us examine ourselves to learn whether God has admitted us to knowledge of
his secrets. For unless he has done so, there is no reason by which we can be
rendered secure concerning his love toward us.

Verse 33. The curse of Jehovah rests upon the house of the wicked, but the
dwelling of the righteous is blessed.

He continues in confirming this: God’s curse eats away the house and
possessions of the wicked, however ample, while his blessing enlarges the
meager cottage of the righteous.

Verse 34. Because he himself mocks the mockers, but gives grace to the meek.

Verse 35. The wise possess honor, but disgrace carries away fools.

Here he shows that the unjust, whom he also calls proud, are exposed to
mockery by God; but on the contrary, the afflicted, whom Peter calls meek,
are adorned with favor among human beings.
