QUESTION VIII

Are the Books of the Old Testament Still a Part of the Canon of Faith and the
Rule of Morals in the Church of the New Testament?—Affirmative, against the
Anabaptists.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. This question is disputed between us and the Anabaptists, who reject the
books of the Old Testament from the canon of faith, as though they pertained
in no way to Christians and as though doctrines of faith or precepts of life
ought not to be drawn from them. The Mennonites, in their *Confession*, article
11,
teach that, so far as faith is concerned, all Christians must necessarily
conform themselves only to the gospel of Christ. This is confirmed in the
Frankenthal Colloquy, act 1, page 57. The orthodox, on the other hand, maintain
that the Old Testament pertains no less to Christians than the New, and that
doctrines of faith and the rule of life are to be drawn from both, French
Confession, articles 4 and 5; Helvetic Confession, article 1.

II. The question is not about the Old Testament with respect to the Mosaic
economy, for we judge that this was so abrogated through Christ that it ought
no longer to have any place in the economy of grace. Rather, it concerns the
Old Testament with respect to doctrine: Does it no longer have any use in the
New Testament with respect to the canon of faith and morals?

III. The question is not whether Christ reformed the law given in the Old
Testament under the New by correcting and perfecting it, for this will be
treated later against the Socinians. Rather, it is whether Old Testament
Scripture pertains to Christians, so that the canon of faith and rule of life
ought to be drawn and proved from it no less than from the New Testament, and
whether the religion of Christ is contained in Moses and the prophets no less
than in the books of the New Testament and can be demonstrated from them.
This the adversaries deny and we affirm.

IV. The question is not about the distinction between the Old and New
Testaments and the difference in the doctrine which sounded forth in each.
For we do not deny that it is far clearer in the New Testament than in the
Old, both because of the types in which it was wrapped and because of the
predictions and promises delivered in it. Rather, the question concerns the
principle of the Christian religion: Are only the books of the New Testament
that principle, or also those of the Old Testament? We maintain the latter.

[Marginal heading: That the Old Testament is canonical for Christians is proved.]

V. The reasons are: 1. Because Christ commends the Old Testament and wills
Moses and the prophets to be heard by the faithful, Luke 16:29. Nor is this
said to the Jews to the exclusion of others, because a general command
is given here which pertains to all who wish to avert

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 91 | IA leaf 145 | PDF p. 145]

eternal judgment; and the practice of what is here proposed as a command is
commended by Peter in Christians, 2 Peter 1:19: “We have a most sure prophetic
word, to which you do well to attend, as to a lamp shining in a dark place,
until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts.” Nor can it be
objected that Peter adds a limitation so that this applies only until the time
of the New Testament, when the day was to dawn. For if it is understood of the
New Testament, the use of the prophetic word is not thereby restricted by
this expression to the time of the New Testament, because “until” (τὸ ἕως οὗ)
is not always such an affirmation of the past that it excludes the future, as
is evident from various passages, Genesis 28:15; Matthew 28:30; 1 Corinthians
15:25. But if it is referred to the day of eternal life and to the rising of
the morning star in the hemisphere of glory—which is truly the day
“preeminently” (κατ’ ἐξοχήν), as seems more fitting, because he writes to
believers who had already obtained an “equally precious” faith (ἰσότιμον), and
thus in whose hearts the day of grace and morning star of the gospel had
already risen—the argument will press more forcefully. That is, attention must
be given to the prophetic word until the end of the age, until that blessed
day arises which is the true day, since it is perpetual and terminated by no
night.

VI. 2. Because the church of the New Testament is built upon the foundation
of the prophets and apostles, Ephesians 2:20—that is, prophetic and apostolic
doctrine. Nor can the prophets of the New Testament, who are mentioned in
Ephesians 3:5 and 1 Corinthians 12:28, be understood here, because Paul speaks
of the perpetual foundation of the universal church, whereas the prophetic
gift of the New Testament was temporary. Nor does the order of placement
immediately imply priority or posteriority of time or duration. Thus in
Ephesians 4:12 the prophets of the New Testament are placed before the
evangelists, yet they were not for that reason earlier than the evangelists.

VII. 3. Because “whatever things were written beforehand were written
beforehand for our instruction, that through the endurance and consolation of
the Scriptures we might have hope,” Romans 15:4. Although all things in
Scripture are not equal with respect to their subject matter and use, they are
nevertheless all equal with respect to their origin and “authority”
(αὐθεντίας), and accordingly were given equally for the good and edification
of the church.

VIII. 4. Because the canon of the Old Testament possesses sufficiency for
faith and morals, and those sacred writings in which Timothy had been
instructed from childhood, when the canon of the New Testament had not yet
been committed to writing, could “make him wise” (σοφίσαι) for salvation, 2
Timothy 3:15–16. And if the man of God—that is, the minister of the gospel—can
be furnished from them for every good work, much more are they useful and
necessary for the faith of a private person and the direction of his life.
Nor does Paul refer this only to the times preceding the writing of the New
Testament, because in verse 16 he speaks in general of all Scripture as
“God-breathed” (θεοπνεύστῳ).

IX. 5. Because Christ directs the Jews to search the Scriptures, John 5:39,
as those from which life can be obtained. Nor is this said only indicatively
to the Jews, but also imperatively, because Christ's purpose is to induce the
Jews to read the Scriptures as a means conducive to knowledge of himself and
as testimony beyond all objection. 2. Even if Christ had spoken
indicatively, the matter would come to the same thing, because Christ approves
that practice rather than censuring it.

X. 6. Because the Scripture of the Old Testament contains the same substance
of doctrine as the New Testament, whether with respect to things to be
believed or things to be done. Nor is any other gospel proposed to us today
than what was previously promised in the prophetic Scriptures, Romans 1:3 and
16:25–26. Hence Paul, who announced to Christians the whole counsel of God
concerning salvation, Acts 20:26, nevertheless professes that he said nothing
beyond the things spoken by Moses and the prophets, Acts 26:22. Nor is any
other law prescribed to us than that once given by Moses, which commands

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 92 | IA leaf 146 | PDF p. 146]

the love of God and love of neighbor, Matthew 22:37–38.

XI. 7. Because if the Old Testament does not pertain to Christians, it will
not be possible to prove soundly against the Jews that Jesus Christ of
Nazareth is the true Messiah. This can be done only by comparing the
Scriptures and making a “comparison” (σύγκρισιν) of the marks predicted about
the Messiah in the Old Testament and fulfilled in our Jesus under the New,
as Christ and the apostles performed more than once, Luke 24:27, 44; Acts
10:43; 17:11; and 26:22; Romans 3:21. Nor will the Jews be able to be convicted
of error and brought to faith, because they acknowledge no other principle.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XII. By “the law and the prophets” which were to last until John, Matthew
11:12, we must not understand the books of the Old Testament and their
duration, but the economy of the Old Testament compared with the economy of
the New. The former was prophetic, the latter evangelical; the former was
shadowy and typical, promising that the Messiah was to be presented, while
the latter was clear and open, announcing the Messiah already presented.
Thus, in comparing these two modes of revelation with each other, Christ says
that the first, by “prophecy” (προφητείαν), lasted only until John, because
when the Messiah had been presented, he no longer willed to be foretold as
future; but that the other, by “evangelization” (εὐαγγελισμόν), which announced
the coming of Christ, began from John.

XIII. When the apostles are called “ministers of the Spirit, not of the
letter,” 2 Corinthians 3:5–6, the books of the Old Testament are not meant by
“the letter,” as though they should no longer have any use, since the apostles
nevertheless use them everywhere. Rather, the legal economy is meant in
contrast to the evangelical, which excels it in many ways, not only with
respect to clarity and fullness but especially with respect to efficacy,
because it not only enjoins and commands duty as the law does, but also works
by the Spirit, inscribing the law upon hearts.

XIV. It is one thing for the old covenant to have become obsolete with
respect to the mode of administration and the covenant's accidents—that is,
the external observance of appendages annexed to the covenant—which Paul
asserts in Hebrews 8:13. It is another for it to have become obsolete with
respect to the thing administered and its substance, or the internal form of
the covenant itself, which we deny.

XV. It is one thing to speak of the force of the ceremonies of the Old
Testament, or of the law according to them. It is another to speak of the
duration, knowledge, and meditation of the books of the law and the prophets.
What possesses only the shadow of good things to come does not pertain to
Christians, who possess the express image of the things, with respect to
practice and observance. It can nevertheless pertain with respect to doctrine
and knowledge, and to relation to “the image” (τὴν εἰκόνα). Indeed, from a
comparison of the shadow and the express form, the body itself shines forth
more clearly.

XVI. In Matthew 5 Christ does not dispute against Moses and the very precepts
of the law. Rather, he acts as interpreter and vindicator of the law by
rejecting the corruptions and glosses which had been inserted into the law by
the teachers of the Jews and by restoring it to its original splendor and
genuine sense, as will be seen individually in the topic on the law.

XVII. Although the Scripture of the New Testament is perfect intensively with
respect to the substance of saving doctrine, it is not likewise perfect
extensively with respect to the whole breadth of divine revelation, because
it speaks only of Christ already presented, not of him as yet to be presented,
which was a kind of testimony most useful for confirming faith. Therefore the
perfection of the books of the New Testament does not exclude the use of the
books of the Old, both because they testify that they rest upon the Old
Testament and because the repeated testimony of several witnesses concerning
the same thing is stronger with respect to us and contributes to the greater
“certainty” of our faith (ἀσφάλειαν).

XVIII. What was in no way given by Christ, either mediately or immediately,
does not pertain to Christians. But the law which was given through Moses was
also given by Christ—through Moses as a servant, by Christ as Lord.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 93 | IA leaf 147 | PDF p. 147]

Thus in Acts 7:38 the same angel who appeared to Moses in the wilderness,
verse 30, and who was himself Jehovah, Exodus 3:2, is said to have spoken with
Moses on Mount Sinai, because the Son of God, who is called the angel of the
covenant and of the presence, was the principal author and promulgator of the
law, of which Moses was only a minister. Nor does this remove the distinction
between the promulgation of the law and of the gospel, because the Son of God
worked in the law only mediately and as “without flesh” (ἄσαρκος), but is
called the first author of the gospel immediately and as “in the flesh”
(ἔνσαρκος), Hebrews 2:3.

XIX. Christ is called “the end of the law,” Romans 10:4, both because he was
the goal to which the whole law looked and because he was its “perfection”
(τελείωσις) and fulfillment—not by abrogating every use of it, but by
fulfilling its oracles and carrying it out, both in himself by acting and
suffering and in his own by inscribing it upon the hearts of the faithful.
Hence Matthew 5:17 says that he “did not come to dissolve the law, but to
fulfill it.”

XX. Servants are not to be heard when the master is present if they testify
either without his command or against him. But they can and ought to be heard
if they testify about him by his command. Moses and the prophets did this no
less than the apostles, John 5:46; Acts 10:43. And Christ expressly commands
that Moses and the prophets be heard, Luke 16:29. Nor is this to go backward
from Christ to Moses, but to advance from Moses to Christ, for Moses is a
tutor unto Christ, Galatians 3:24.

XXI. The beginning of John's preaching is rightly called “the beginning of
the gospel,” Mark 1:1, with respect to fulfillment and Christ already
presented, but not with respect to promise and Christ as yet to be presented,
which already had a place under the Old Testament, Romans 1:2; Galatians 3:8;
Isaiah 52:7 and 61:1.
