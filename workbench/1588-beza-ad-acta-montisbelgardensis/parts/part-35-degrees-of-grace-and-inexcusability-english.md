## Part XXXV: Degrees of Grace and Inexcusability

If we proceed farther, namely to the grace of assent—that is, approving and acknowledging the truth perceived through understanding—this gift too will be found to be given to fewer than either of the preceding gifts. How many with diabolical fury fight even acknowledged truth against conscience! If we proceed farther to the taste of that truth, by which the believer personally applies to himself the gospel promises in Christ, this gift again will be restricted to fewer. To how many is that truth so bitter that they cannot bear even to chew it! And among those who swallow this food, how many nevertheless fail to digest it! This is temporary faith. Finally, if we reach the very goal to which all the preceding gifts tend—so that we truly are and persevere in being one spirit with Christ—this is the gift by which those made for honor, truly given to Christ, and to be saved through mercy are distinguished from the rest made for dishonor and just destruction. Christ said to his disciples, “To you it has been given to know the mysteries of heaven, but not to them.” For, as he also says, they are hidden from the wise. God works willing only in his own; the Spirit breathes not everywhere but where he wills; the Son reveals the Father not to all but to those he wills; none can believe except those whom the Father draws; none hear except those in whom ears to hear are given; none believe except those predestined to life; and it is given to become God’s children only to those not born of blood. Salvation is not of one who wills or runs but of God who has mercy. This is the very doctrine our adversary dares call manifestly impious and abominable.

I would also gladly ask him what word he finds in this Ezekiel passage by which an indefinite statement may be changed into a universal one. As I showed shortly above in replying to 1 Timothy 2, even expressly universal words are often restricted—sometimes to believers, sometimes to the church’s whole body, as in “All flesh will see God’s salvation,” “All will be taught by God,” “I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh,” “God raises all who fall,” and very many similar passages; and sometimes to the association of the impious, as in “No one receives his testimony,” “You will be hated by all human beings,” and “All seek their own interests,” of which almost countless examples could be produced. None of this would have been necessary had our adversary not driven us here.

In this passage Ezekiel speaks not about that secret will of God which is not revealed even to the elect with respect to certainly identifying others, but the will manifested through preaching the Word to the whole assembly of the church, into which even the reprobate are gathered for a time. Through hearing this preaching, faith is created by the Holy Spirit in the elect, while the rest—

—are increasingly rendered inexcusable, convicted and refuted by their own conscience’s testimony. Ezekiel properly speaks of the latter here. Even if I granted that the gospel is universally preached to all, it cannot be inferred from this that God effectually wills everyone’s salvation. The many infants who perish outside Christ demonstrate this, as Ambrose abundantly declares in book 1, chapter 5 of *On the Calling of the Gentiles*. So too do the many born mentally impaired and deaf who never throughout life possess the right and proper use of reason. The same is shown because many who live honorably for a time finally perish with the worst end, while conversely those who have lived perpetually in crimes are finally saved when suddenly gifted with faith and repentance. How can it be said in these examples that God effectually willed the salvation of all of them?

Finally, since original corruption alone is more than sufficient to ensure all are born subject to God’s wrath, why do these men labor over this universal grace, whose contempt they consider so necessary to justify God in condemning the reprobate that otherwise he might appear unjust, indeed cruel? Certainly I do not deny that when the violation of natural godliness [εὐσεβείας], which Paul discusses in Romans 1, is added to the corruption of nature sufficient for destruction, all become guilty of eternal death in two respects. If contempt of the gospel is added, who doubts that people thereby bring upon themselves a far graver punishment of condemnation?
