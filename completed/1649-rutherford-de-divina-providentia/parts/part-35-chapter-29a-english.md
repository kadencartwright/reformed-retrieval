CHAPTER XXIX

That Calvin, Beza, and our theologians employ forms of speech consonant with
Scripture and in an orthodox sense, and do not conspire with the Libertines. 2.
The objections of James Arminius and the Jesuits are answered.

I.

Calvin speaks thus: *Whenever it pleased him to take vengeance for the people’s
transgressions, how did he accomplish his work in the reprobate? So that you
may see that the efficacy of acting belonged to him (GOD), while they supplied
only the service.*

[Marginal reference: Calvin, *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 4.]

[Marginal summary: Calvin’s and Beza’s expressions are the same as those of
Scripture.]

The efficacy of acting in evil actions is twofold. One belongs to holy,
judicial, and punitive providence, and this is GOD’s. The other is criminal and
unjust, and this is principal in its own order and belongs properly to sinners.
Scripture speaks no differently, for while evil people sin it calls them GOD’s
servants, rods, staffs, hammers, axes, hands, nets, soldiers, messengers,
fishermen, fowlers, and hunt-

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 479; IA PDF page 536; BSB canvas 531]

-ers. In this sense Calvin says that *the wicked are impelled by secret
providence toward what seemed good to GOD.* Likewise, *they are stirred by a
secret impulse to destroy the people of GOD.* Likewise, *the perverse minds of
the Chaldeans are incited to perpetrate the crime* (against Job). Likewise,
*they are driven as though by a bridle to perform their services. Meanwhile,*
he says, *it must be maintained that the root of malice was in the Egyptians
themselves, so that the fault cannot be transferred to GOD; I say that they
were evil voluntarily and intrinsically, not compelled by an impulse from
another.* Does Scripture not say that GOD stirred Satan against David, that GOD
deceived the prophet, commanded Shimei to curse the Lord’s Anointed, and turned
their heart to hatred of his people? Let one passage from Beza stand for many:
*GOD acts through those instruments in such a way that he does not merely allow
them to act, nor merely govern the outcome, but also arouses, impels, moves, and
rules them—and, what is greatest of all, even creates them—so that through them
he may do what he has ordained.*

[Marginal reference: John Calvin, commentary on Psalm 5:5.]

[Marginal reference: On Isaiah 10:5.]

[Marginal reference: *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 4, section 2.]

[Marginal reference: *Institutes*, book 1, chapter 17, section 1.]

[Marginal reference: Commentary on Psalm 105:25.]

[Marginal reference: Beza, response to Castellio, aphorism 22.]

Instruments are of two kinds. Some are moral; in this way sinners, in relation
to the end appointed by GOD, are passive instruments. This does not mean that
they behave in a merely passive way in doing evil, as the adversaries unfairly
interpret Luther’s words, but that while they execute GOD’s judgments, they are
ignorant: they do not know Jehovah’s thoughts or understand his counsel. Nor do
they intend the same thing as God. *He himself does not purpose it so, and his
mind does not think so, but it is in his heart to destroy.* Indeed, the
instruments are not even formally free in relation to the end appointed by
GOD, because they do not act from knowledge. For Herod, Pilate, and the Jews
knew nothing of the redemption of the lost world while they plotted the death
of the Son of GOD. Other instruments are natural. Our theologians speak about
the former instruments, not about organs of the latter kind.

[Marginal summary: What sort of instruments of GOD sinful people are.]

[Marginal reference: Micah 4:12.]

[Marginal reference: Isaiah 10:7.]

Augustine says elegantly: *It is therefore within the power of evil people to
sin; but that in sinning they do this or that is not within their power, but
within the power of GOD, who divides the darkness,* and so forth. Zwingli,
using a harsher trope, says that people are driven by GOD to evil deeds.
Scripture too uses the verb “to drive” where the will remains free, though not
without a `καταχρήσει`, a misuse or catachresis.

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *On the Predestination of the Saints*, chapter
16.]

[Marginal summary: Protestants teach nothing in common with the Libertines.]

[Marginal references: Genesis 33:12; 1 Samuel 28:23; Mark 6:45; Acts 16:15; 2
Corinthians 12:11.]

2. What then? Do our theologians conspire here with the Libertines? Certainly
not. The Libertines do not champion our orthodox opinion, nor do we champion
theirs. For 1. Quintinus, that lustful swine, taught that God himself is *the
one who formally steals in the thief, fornicates in the fornicator, lies and
perjures himself in the perjurer.* Envy itself has not dared to charge us with
such dogmas. Before GOD and the angels we teach, solemnly assert, testify, and
declare openly that GOD neither sins

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 480; IA PDF page 537; BSB canvas 532]

nor is the cause of sin; rather, the Holy One of Israel abhors, detests,
disapproves, forbids, and punishes sins. 2. Quintinus taught that thefts, lies,
perjuries, and murders are not only God’s holy and praiseworthy works, but also
that they cannot be censured without blasphemy. He said that censuring thefts
and murders was blasphemously to censure God’s holy works, and thus that
sinners themselves should not be censured unless someone were unashamed to
censure God. When someone asked him who had killed a man lying in the road, he
answered, *I, I am the one; indeed, I committed it, God committed it,* says
Quintinus. The Devil himself has not dared to ascribe such things to us, nor
has a Jesuit or an Arminian. 3. The Libertines taught that GOD’s evil works and
human beings’ evil works are in all respects the same and alike—not only
physically and materially alike but also morally—as though God were as worthy
of blame as the sinner, if only blame had a place here, and as though we were
as free from God’s law as God himself. Everyone who has reviewed our writings
even lightly knows that we teach the contrary, and here we call upon the
consciences of our adversaries. For in evil works we attribute to GOD pure
punishment and the holy act of holy providence, since he is superior to every
law. To the human being subject to law, however, we attribute perverse acts
worthy of blame and punishment, and, as concerns the morality of the work, acts
contrary to GOD’s action in all four causes—as contrary as fire and water,
light and darkness. 4. The Libertines taught that human beings are wrongly
judged or condemned for evils because God governs them by his providence, and
that sin is good for the sinners themselves and issues in good. Consequently,
they said that those who judge others and condemn them for sin are hypocrites,
abusing that saying of the Savior in Matthew 7:5: *Hypocrite, first remove the
beam from your own eye,* and so forth. No one ascribes any such thing to us.
5. The Libertines handed down that no one is deterred from sin by a duty of
conscience, for they hold GOD’s irresistible will to take the place of
conscience for everyone, and that nothing displeases God when people commit all
evils by GOD’s will (for by his will they do all good things and, equally, all
evil things). We urge, press, and inculcate the consciences of sinners as the
single support of religion. 6. The followers of Florinus said that sin is mere
opinion and imagination and is nothing to GOD. We teach that sinners subject
themselves, because they sin, to GOD’s wrath and eternal condemnation. 7. The
Libertines professed that it is wisdom

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 481; IA PDF page 538; BSB canvas 533]

to indulge the flesh and one’s appetite, and to live lavishly and merrily after casting
away every care for sin, conscience, and the final judgment. Far be it from us
that any such thing should enter our mind. Let the reader see more about these
things in Eusebius, Calvin, and Theodore Beza. 8. While I was living at
Aberdeen, I encountered a certain scoundrel most foully polluted by the
Antinomian heresy. He asserted that the regenerate cannot sin, are subject to
no law, are no longer bound by the consciousness of sin, and are covered by
the imputed righteousness of CHRIST; and that only those who believe such
things are true Protestants and genuine disciples of Luther. Yet although the
bishops of both kingdoms cruelly persecute all defenders of the purer religion
with prison, exile, and removal from office, to this day they have never taken
action against such people, who are sufficiently well known to them. Indeed, I
hear that among the English bishops and followers of the Arminian sect, all of
us who stand with Calvin and Beza for GOD’s active providence in sins are
called Antinomians and Libertines.

[Marginal reference: Eusebius, *History*, book 5, chapter 19.]

[Marginal reference: Calvin, *Against the Libertines*, books 3 and 4.]

[Marginal reference: Beza, against Sebastian Castellio, accusation 3, chapters
13, 14, and 15.]

3. Those who stand neither with us nor with the adversaries argue: *The one who
is the cause of a cause is also the cause of what is caused; but according to
our doctrine GOD is the cause of the cause. Therefore he is the cause of what
is caused, namely, sin.* They call the major premise a Logical Maxim. They
prove the minor: *The one who knowingly and willingly is the cause of an action
to which malice is attached is the cause of the malice caused by that action;
but GOD knowingly and willingly is the cause—the first cause—of the action.
Therefore he is the cause of the malice.* More than one response is made to
this argument. For Theodore Smising reinforces it thus: *The one who is the
cause of something is also the cause of what is attached to it, whether in
itself* (if it is essentially attached) *or accidentally, even though he does
not intend it but causes it beyond his intention and incidentally. For example,
someone digging is truly the accidental cause of finding the treasure which he
did not know lay there.*

[Marginal summary: The claim “the one who is the cause of a cause is the cause
of what is caused; God is the cause of the cause of sin,” and so forth, is
discussed.]

[Marginal reference: Smising, volume 1, tractate 3, disputation 3, *On the
Divine Will*, figure 62.]

Alfonso Curiel speaks thus: *GOD is said to be unable to fail in acting: 1.
because he cannot fail in the second act; 2. because he cannot have a defect,
as it were, in the first act, by reason of which the defect of the second act
is attributed to him. As is commonly said, a defect in an action presupposes
some defect in its principle; therefore sin always presupposes a defect in the
intellect. Although the latter sense suffices to infer from these things that
God is not the cause in itself of the defect, because he has no defect in his
active power and the defect is reduced only to a defect of the principle, it
nevertheless does not seem sufficient to prove that GOD is not the accidental
cause of sin. Lameness is not reduced to GOD as to a cause in itself, that is*

[Marginal reference: Curiel, I–II, question 79, article 1, section 7.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 482; IA PDF page 539; BSB canvas 534]

*as to one who has in himself the power to fail; yet it is properly reduced to
him as an accidental cause because he is the cause of the positive action upon
which the defect follows.*

Duvallius says: *If GOD created a blind human being, the defect of blindness
would be referred to GOD, although he himself is not defective. Therefore, the
reason why the defect of sin could not be referred to GOD is not precisely
that he is not liable to defect.* But one can answer the Jesuit Smising that,
taking the word broadly, God is the fatuous cause of sin, as they say, and the
cause without which it would not occur. This is not in dispute between us, nor,
I believe, would the adversaries deny that in this highly improper sense God is
the cause of sin, since sin would never have existed among human beings unless
God had created the human being who sins. 2. Curiel and Andrew Duvallius are
answered by saying that an inference from one species to another is invalid.
Their arguments prove, namely, that God is the cause of a physical privation,
such as lameness and blindness; but it does not follow from this that he is the
cause of a moral privation, for that would be unworthy of God.

[Marginal reference: Duvallius, on Thomas II, question 10, article 1, treatise
on sins.]

2. Richard and Gabriel say that the maxim, *The one who is the cause of a cause
or act is the cause of what is caused by it, if the cause can exist without
what is caused,* does not hold, because *GOD can produce the entitative act of
hatred of himself without malice.* Not badly answered, in my judgment, if an
example other than hatred had been used. We answer thus: the one who is the
cause of a cause or act through physical influence and causation is also a
cause—namely, a moral cause—of what is caused, such as malice, which has no
cause except a moral one. Taken in this way, the maxim does not hold. Otherwise,
the one who physically influences the cause is also one who physically
influences what is caused, namely, the physical privation. Nor do Mæratius and
Vásquez justly attack this answer: *Although,* they say, *there are no works of
sin which in some way could not occur without malice, nevertheless, if GOD is
the cause here and now of the act of hatred when malice cannot but arise from
it, why will he not be the cause of the malice?*

[Marginal references: Richard, book 2, distinction 37, article 3, question 1,
article 2; Gabriel, book, sole question, article 3, doubt 3.]

[Marginal reference: Mæratius, volume 1, tractate *On Sin*, disputation 11,
section 5.]

[Marginal reference: Vásquez, on I–II, volume 1, disputation 129, chapter 5.]

I answer: The “why not” is easily removed, because the physical causation of an
act by one who is above the law is not immediately the causation of the moral
entity and the privation which follows the act.

4. Scotus also answers that *the malice of sin is therefore not from GOD,
because he is not bound to give rectitude to the act; but malice is ascribed to
the human being because he owes rectitude to the act.* This is our answer. In
concurring God is not the cause of sin, because he is above every law; but the
human being is the cause of sin because he is bound to abstain. And

[Marginal reference: Scotus, book 2, distinction 37, question 2, section 1.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 483; IA PDF page 540; BSB canvas 535]

the relation of subjection to law here makes a human being a culpable moral
cause, while the absence of this relation is the reason why God is not a moral
cause in this sense but is called by Soto, Cano, and Vásquez a quasi-physical
cause, as though non-necessary and non-free. Nor does GOD deny every rectitude
to the act, because, as Faber Faventinus says, *GOD gives the rectitude owed to
the act privatively, because he would not will it to exist; but the created will
does not give this rectitude privatively because it wills to elicit the act
even if it knows that it is contrary to GOD’s will.* Nor does Vásquez justly
criticize this answer: *A human being,* he says, *does not sin because he owes
rectitude to the act, since that act*—hatred of GOD, for example—*is not capable
of rectitude, but because it occurs contrary to the rule of reason.*

[Marginal reference: Faber Faventinus, book 1, *Sentences*, distinction 39,
disputation 54, sole question, section 61.]

[Marginal reference: Vásquez, ibid.]

I answer: The entitative act which is in hatred of GOD is a truly human act and
in itself indifferent. Therefore, it is capable of rectitude. 2. If a human
being sins in the act of hatred of GOD because that act is contrary to the rule
of reason, we obtain our point: the human being sins because he denies to the
entitative act underlying hatred of GOD the rectitude which the rule of reason
requires to inhere in that act. This is what we say.

3. Ockham, and with him the Cameracensis, answers that *the creature is the
cause of malice because it produces an act deformed from the law, and malice is
something more than a deformed act. For it always expresses incongruity with
the law and supposes that the agent is subject to the law, but the act as act
expresses no moral subjection.* This answer coincides with ours, because by
producing only the act God cannot be considered the cause of malice. For malice
can have no cause other than a moral one; but production of the bare act
requires only physical, not moral, causation.

[Marginal reference: Ockham, *Sentences*, book 3, distinction 12.]

[Marginal reference: Cameracensis, book 1, question 13, article 1.]

4. Molina says: *Just as the crimes committed by those who use swords wrongly
are not imputed to the smith, because swords are indifferent toward good and
evil use, so, since GOD’s concurrence is indifferent toward good or evil acts,
they must be attributed not to GOD but to the choice which abuses GOD’s
indifferent concurrence.* This is the adversaries’ common answer, for in this
way they deny the universal validity of the maxim, *The one who is the cause of
the cause is also the cause of what is caused.* They hold that it applies to
particular and determinate causes, not to universal and indifferent ones.

[Marginal reference: Molina, *On Concord*, question 14, article 13,
disputation 32.]

I answer: Because God, as a universal cause, raises the arm in the act of
homicide by a concurrence which is indeed indifferent—that is, not at all
joined to the crime—yet truly causative, he contributes far more causality
toward causing the sinful act in the class of cause than the smith contributes
toward the act of homicide. For the smith only fashions the sword and does not
physically reach the murderer’s hand and mind by any causation; but God
causatively, in the manner of a physical cause, concurs with both the murderer’s
mind and his hand. 2. Because an indifferent cause truly is a cause, just as it
is the cause of the cause, it is in its own way the cause of what is caused.
Just as the sun is the cause of a human being, it is also, by a universal mode
of causing, the cause of a human being born blind or two-headed.

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 484; IA PDF page 541; BSB canvas 536]

Molina objects in the same place: *Just as blame is assigned to the smith when
he hands a sword to one whom he probably knows will abuse it, so GOD, concurring
with one whom he certainly knows will abuse GOD’s concurrence, will be at
fault.* Molina answers: *GOD is Lord of all; but human beings, who are subject
to GOD and the divine laws, are bound by natural law to prevent the sins of
their neighbors.* But what is this except to flee to us? For unless God were
absolute Lord and superior to every law, his concurrence toward sinful acts
would implicate him in the causality of the crime. Therefore, according to this
answer, although God concurs toward the entitative act and is even the cause of
the cause, he is not the cause of what is caused, namely, sin, because he is not
subject to law. 2. According to the adversaries’ doctrine, this is to beg the
question, as they object against us, for GOD’s essential holiness is a rule for
God by which he would be bound neither to permit sin nor to contribute
causative aid to the sinful act, just as GOD’s law obliges human beings neither
to permit their neighbors’ sins insofar as they can prevent them nor to concur
causatively with the acts.

[Marginal reference: Molina, ibid.]

5. Richard Field, an Englishman, answers that *GOD cannot will the act without
necessarily willing the deformity inseparably and necessarily attached to it.*
He attempts to prove this against the Reformed Churches from Durandus and
Suárez, poorly understood. Thus he teaches on p. 126 that *the universal mover
moves and impels all creatures, good or evil, toward actions fitting the
condition and state of the nature in which they are; and if this is a state of
aversion from GOD, it is because his will is that nothing remain idle and
without action, as befits the nature of an agent. The example is a limping horse
driven by its rider, which has from itself and not from its driver the fact
that it limps.*

[Marginal reference: Richard Field, in his English work *Of the Church*, book
3, chapter 3, p. 121.]

[Marginal reference: Durandus, book 2, distinction 37, question 1.]

[Marginal reference: Suárez, *Metaphysics*, volume 1, disputation 22, section
4, p. 522 and p. 126.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 485; IA PDF page 542; BSB canvas 537]

I answer: Field, though otherwise learned, reveals that he is wholly
unpracticed in these controversies. His answer involves a contradiction. For
if GOD cannot will the act without necessarily willing the deformity
inseparably attached to it, then God also cannot will his own impulse and
motion, by which as universal mover he moves evil creatures in evil acts
fitting their natures, without necessarily willing the deformity inseparably
attached to such an impulse. But Field would not admit this. Indeed, expressly
from Cumel—whom he also does not sufficiently understand, since he was not
sufficiently versed in the Arminian controversies and wrote when he had a Court
which favored Arminius more than was right—he says that *it involves a
contradiction for GOD to cause our will to perform such acts as to their
material element but not also as to their inseparable formal element, namely,
malice.* He gives hatred of GOD as his instance. But in that place Cumel reports
not his own opinion but the opinion of others. For Cumel repeats and refutes
the arguments of Suárez and, in fact, Bellarmine and the Jesuits, in which he
himself is by no means content. 2. *If God physically moves an acting creature,
he also moves it to malice, for he wills the efficiency of the act and the
efficiency of the malice to be so inseparably joined that they cannot be
separated.* These are Field’s words. Nor does Durandus speak about anything
other than positive acts of will. He does not speak about privations of acts.
Field badly and ineptly cites Suárez—our adversary and most contradictory to
himself and to the truth in this cause—on our behalf. For Suárez, adhering to
Jesuit doctrine, is an enemy of the orthodox doctrine of Protestants.

6. Alvarez says that some answer that *GOD influences the sinful act not
positively and properly but negatively, because he does not prevent the sin
when he could prevent it but permits it.* This is Julian’s opinion, which
Augustine refutes; and certainly the Jesuits and Arminians say with Julian that
*human beings are hardened not through GOD’s power but through his patience,
or through bare permission.* Therefore, because God is only negatively the
cause of the cause, by not preventing the act, it does not follow that he is
the cause of the deformity. But this opinion concerning GOD’s idle permission
inflicts no slight injury upon his providence.

[Marginal reference: Alvarez, *Epitome on Aids*, book 2, chapter 9.]

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *Against Julian*, book 5, chapter 3.]

7. The Jesuit Mæratius says that this maxim—*The one who is the cause of the
cause is also the cause of what is caused*—does not hold in free causes. And in
response to the argument, *The one who is the cause*

[Marginal reference: Mæratius, volume 1, tractate *On Sin*, disputation 11,
section 5.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 486; IA PDF page 543; BSB canvas 538]

*Of a cause is also the cause of what is caused; but God is the cause of the
cause, namely, of the sinful act; therefore he is the cause of sin,* he says
that there are four terms in the syllogism. The one who is the physical cause
of the act is the cause of the moral act: this is false. But God is the physical
cause of the act. It does not follow that he is the moral cause of the malice or
evil act. Gibieuf says that *the will’s failing is from itself, because it is
constituted from nothing, and this is not to be reduced to GOD.* Bañez,
Alvarez, Thomas, Capreolus, and Ferrariensis say that sin or *malice follows
from the act insofar as it flows from free choice, not from GOD.* Therefore,
the maxim is not universally true.

[Marginal reference: Gibieuf, *On Human Freedom*, book 2, chapter 25, figure
15.]

[Marginal reference: Bañez, volume 1, question 23.]

[Marginal reference: Alvarez, *On Aids*, book 2, chapter 9.]

[Marginal reference: Thomas, question 3 *On Evil*, article 2, reply 2.]

[Marginal reference: Capreolus, book 2 of the *Sentences*, distinction 37,
article 2.]

[Marginal reference: Ferrariensis, *Against the Gentiles*, book 3, chapter 71.]

Bellarmine’s assertion, *For someone to sin it is not required that he will
malice as such or evil as evil,* is also easily dissolved. If the reduplication
refers to the sinner’s will, it is true that the sinner does not choose evil
insofar as it appears evil to his will, but insofar as it appears pleasant or
useful to the flesh. But if the reduplication refers to sin itself, it is false,
because the sinner chooses what is essentially and in itself evil rather than
to please GOD.

[Marginal reference: Bellarmine.]

Soncinas also rightly says: *A privation follows an act either in itself and
from the essence and nature of the act—in this way, the one who is author of
the form is author of what follows the form, and malice does not follow the act
in this way—or a privation follows an act not from the nature of the act but
from its relation to the agent, insofar as it issues from an agent to whom a law
has been prescribed. And thus whoever is the cause of the act is not the cause
of the privation which follows the act.* So too Raynaudus, Cano, Soto,
Mæratius, and Vásquez. So say we.

[Marginal reference: Soncinas, epitome on book 2 of the *Sentences*,
distinction 37, question 1.]

[Marginal reference: Raynaudus, *Natural Theology*, distinction 1, question 3,
article 1, section 52.]

[Marginal reference: Cano, *Theological Topics*, book 2, chapter 4.]

[Marginal reference: Soto, *On Nature and Grace*, book 1, chapter 18.]

[Marginal reference: Mæratius, cited place, section 12.]

[Marginal reference: Vásquez, on I–II, volume 1, disputation 129.]

8. They object against our theologians: 2. *The one who efficaciously procures
that sin occur is the author of sin; but according to the dogmas of Calvin,
Beza, and so forth, GOD efficaciously procures that sin occur. Therefore,
according to the dogmas of Calvin, Beza, and so forth, GOD is the author of
sin.* Thus the Jesuits and their wretched offspring—the Arminians,
Pseudo-Lutherans, and Socinians—argue against us.

I answer: 1. *The one who efficaciously procures sin is the author of sin.* This
major is true of procuring sin through efficient causation and an approving
volition of sin. But taken thus, the minor is false. If, however, the major
means that one who efficaciously procures sin through determination of the will
to the act, not to the malice, and through infallible permission of the malice,
is the author of sin, the major is false. 2. The keen Jesuit Hurtado de Mendoza
says: *Sin as sin reduplicates the mode by which it arises sinfully from its
cause, just as vitality reduplicates the mode by which it arises from its vital
cause. But sin does not arise*—is not procured—*from*

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 487; IA PDF page 544; BSB canvas 539]

*GOD in a sinful mode, just as it does not arise from him in a vital mode,
because the physical act arises*—is procured—*from GOD according to the laws of
his wisdom and of our free choice, which demands to be completed in order to
operate freely.* 3. Nor do our theologians speak thus, if you except Piscator,
who says that *GOD procures that sins occur,* but in the sound sense in which
Scripture says that God deceives, hardens, gives people over to the efficacy of
error, and removes Job’s goods through sheer robberies by which they were
stolen from him.

9. 3. Arminius speaks thus: *According to Calvin, the permission of sin is the
cause of sin. Therefore, if GOD is the cause of permission, he will be the cause
of sin. For* Perkins says: *What GOD does not prevent therefore occurs because
GOD does not prevent it; and what does not exist therefore does not exist
because GOD wills it not to exist. I conclude,* says Arminius, *that GOD’s
non-prevention and the event are joined to each other as cause and effect, and
indeed immediately. Thus non-prevention has the character of energetic
efficiency.*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, accusation 3, pp. 694,
695.]

I answer: 1. Permission is formally something other than non-prevention, as I
taught above, for it is not a mere negation. 2. God’s not preventing things
from occurring does not properly bring about the event, since GOD’s positive
action must concur in order to posit the existence of positive things. 3. The
particle “therefore” is taken either inferentially or causally. Taken in the
former way, because God does not prevent sin from occurring by the gracious
prevention which is the bestowal of efficacious grace, by which one holyly
abstains from sin, sin therefore occurs. But by no art of logic—even if
Arminius were logic itself—does he establish from this that GOD’s
non-prevention is the cause of sin, because God is not obligated to prevent
sin. Taken in the latter way, it is false that Perkins means that sin occurs
“therefore,” that is, from this cause, *because GOD does not prevent sin.* 4.
If “therefore,” taken causally, is taken negatively by way of not removing what
would prevent—which is a cause only in the most improper sense in moral effects
and is reduced to a physical cause—then, apart from the Arminians, no one denies
that a human being sins “therefore” because he is deprived of efficacious
grace, by which, had it been given, he would holyly have abstained from sin.
Otherwise no praise for abstinence from sin and no glory, either owed or
rendered, for God’s grace would exist; but this is Pelagian. Yet does Arminius
rightly infer from this that GOD is the cause of sin? Certainly not, because
from this there follows only the physical nonexistence of abstinence from sin,
not its moral nonexistence, just as from a physician’s not treating the diseases
of many

[Marginal summary: In what way sin therefore occurs because God does not
prevent it through efficacious grace, against Arminius.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 488; IA PDF page 545; BSB canvas 540]

poor people in a city whom he is not obligated to treat, the death of
many poor people follows by a certain physical, not moral, consequence. For sin
as sin reduplicates a moral cause, as Mendoza rightly says, just as the vital
cause of an act reduplicates a vital cause. Yet in this way Arminius certainly
makes God the author of sin. For he defines *permission as the suspension of
impediments which, were they posited, would necessarily prevent the creature
from sinning. Likewise, none of those arguments must necessarily persuade the
will not to will* sin; *otherwise it will not have been permission.* Thus
Arminius certainly says that Judas’s sin exists because God suspended the
impediments which, if posited, would necessarily have prevented Judas from
ending his life by the noose. Otherwise Judas, when permitted by God, would not
have brought death upon himself.

[Marginal reference: James Arminius, disputation 10, thesis 4.]

10. Objection 4. *What GOD hates, prohibits, and punishes,* say Arminius and
Bellarmine, *he in no way wills; but GOD hates, prohibits, and punishes sin.
Therefore GOD in no way wills sin.* Psalm 5: “God does not will iniquity.”
Scripture abundantly proves the minor. They prove the major because for God to
hate sin is for God to will that sin not occur; therefore, he cannot by another
will in any way will that sin occur, since the wills in GOD would thus be
contradictory.

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 701.]

[Marginal reference: Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*, book 2, chapter 8.]

I answer: 1. I say nothing yet about the punishment of sin, because GOD’s
hatred toward sin and its punishment do not have the same rationale. But the
statement, *What God hates, he in no way wills to occur,* can be denied without
qualification. I concede that what God hates with respect to its “occurring,”
or existence, he in no way wills to occur. I assume: but God hates the
`τὸ fieri`, the occurring and existence, of sin. This is false. Arminius indeed
strenuously asserts it, but proves it poorly—or rather, never proves it. 2. I
deny the major if it means that one who hates sin—that is, who does not approve
it but disapproves its morality as something iniquitous—cannot will, that is,
decree, that sin exist with himself permitting and the creature effecting it.
But I concede the major if it means that one who hates it altogether, so that
he cannot even by a decreeing will will the existence of sin for the declaration
of the glory of his name, can in no way will sin. I assume: but God so completely
hates sin that he cannot decree its existence even by a decreeing will. This is
altogether false. The Arminians badly and altogether most ineptly expound GOD’s
hatred toward sin, for God’s hating sin is not God’s having decreed that sin
never exist in the nature of

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 489; IA PDF page 546; BSB canvas 541]

things. God hates the Savior’s crucifixion, but did not decree
that this crucifixion should never exist for the redemption of the human race.
The adversaries always err miserably by not understanding the distinction
between the will of sign and the will of good pleasure. For the approving will,
as such, approves or disapproves the morality of acts, whether those acts are
to exist in the nature of things or are never to exist. But the decreeing will
does not regard a thing’s good or evil morality; rather, it posits that the thing
will or will not exist in its appointed time, whether the things are good or
evil. Therefore, first, the decreeing will is the creative cause of things’
existence or nonexistence and is consequently properly called GOD’s will. The
will of sign or approval is not creative of things’ existence and is less
properly a will, because it is not their cause and creator. 2. Natural and
efficacious permission is properly opposed to the decreeing will and properly
deserves the name of permission because it is efficacious prevention, just as
that will of GOD which is efficacious is properly a will. Romans 9: “Who resists
his will?” Psalm 115:3: “Whatever GOD willed, he did.” But moral permission,
which is improperly called permission, is opposed to the approving will. Thus
an earthly judge prevents all his subjects from sinning—that is, does not
permit them to sin—in the sense that he forbids them by law to sin. Opposed to
this will is the improper permission which is release from legal obligation.
Therefore, the approving will is improperly called a will. It by no means
follows that these wills are contrary.

[Marginal summary: The noteworthy difference between the will of sign and the
will of good pleasure.]

11. *But GOD necessarily loves his own goodness and perfection. Therefore, he
necessarily hates every sin which is repugnant to that perfection, not as form
to form but as something distorted from its rule.* Thus Lessius.

[Marginal reference: Lessius, *On the Divine Perfections*, book 8, chapter 1.]

I answer: I do not deny that God necessarily hates sin by his approving will.
Does it therefore follow that by his decreeing will he determined that sin
would never exist? I deny the consequence. 2. It deserves examination whether
God hates sin by necessity of nature, and whether sin is opposed to GOD’s
essential goodness. With the judgment of learned doctors preserved, I modestly
deny this until clearer light shines, for sin conflicts with God’s free
approval, not with God’s nature or natural goodness. What? Does the eating of
the forbidden fruit conflict with GOD’s natural goodness, so that God hates it
by necessity of nature? This is false to me, because God could, if he had freely
willed it so, have commanded the eating of such a tree. Indeed, it is not
repugnant

[Marginal summary: To what extent GOD hates sin by necessity of nature.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 490; IA PDF page 547; BSB canvas 542]

to GOD’s nature that he command all physical acts insofar as they are abstracted
from malice. For law is the free volition of his subjects’ duty and the free
limitation by the supreme Legislator by which, according to his free will, he
either commands or forbids every act indifferent in itself; and according to
his free will he can forbid what he commands and command what he forbids.
Therefore, on the hypothesis that God freely forbids this act, he necessarily
hates it.

It can be objected: *If he hates sin not naturally but freely, then it is not
repugnant for GOD to will and love sin.* I answer that this by no means follows,
because once it is posited that God has disapproved these acts, it conflicts
with GOD’s constancy and immutability for him to be able to love them. In this
sense it is essential to God to hate malice as such. But it can be objected:
Thus God hates sin, or does not will sin, in the way that he does not will the
non-creation of the world or the non-coming of CHRIST to judgment before
Antichrist is revealed. For on the hypothesis of his decree God cannot fail to
will these things, and yet, absolutely speaking, it is not repugnant for God to
will the non-creation of the world. Indeed, in this way God, considered
absolutely and apart from his free decree, is indifferent to willing or not
willing sin just as he is indifferent to raining or not raining. This is absurd.

I answer: This consequence does not hold. Malice essentially and formally
includes a nilling and disapproval of malice as a morally iniquitous thing which
obligates a rational creature, and malice quidditatively expresses repugnance
to GOD’s holy will. But the non-creation of the world, raining, or not raining
do not essentially include either GOD’s willing or nilling, for GOD’s will is
accidental to each. The rationale of these things and of sin is therefore far
different. But if the discussion concerns sinful acts, God does not will them
in the same way he does not will the non-creation of the world—I mean, with
exactly the same necessity. For by necessity of nature God neither wills nor
does not will the non-creation of the world; rather, he wills it by a merely
free will. And by his mere freedom, with no natural necessity, he wills or does
not will all sinful acts.

Thus the great Bradwardine says: *There is no sin and deformity in the world
absolutely, in relation to the first cause, with whose wisdom all things
conspire, but only in relation to creatures. And every act is just in relation
to GOD as its author.* Nor do I think it absurd that God is indifferent toward
willing or not willing all acts which terminate upon a creature and outside
himself, just as he is

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 34.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 491; IA PDF page 548; BSB canvas 543]

indifferent toward willing or not willing the non-creation of the world or
toward willing rain or no rain. This is the sense in which I deny that sin,
considered materially, is an object of hatred and abhorrence to GOD by necessity
of nature; rather, God hates it only freely.

Objection: *Because GOD is essentially holy, he therefore commands these acts
and forbids those. Therefore, GOD naturally loves some acts and hates others,
not freely.* I answer: 1. “Natural” is taken in two ways. First, for what depends
merely upon the principles of nature, as burning is natural to fire. Second,
something is called “natural” toward which nature inclines but by means of free
choice. In this way laughing is natural to a human being, yet with free choice
intervening. Because God is naturally holy, his holy nature inclines him to
command or forbid these or those acts not in the former but in the latter way,
with GOD’s free choice intervening, which wisely determines him in the exercise.
Thus justice is essential to GOD, as are mercy, liberality, and kindness; but
the exercise of justice in punishing, of mercy in bringing aid, and of kindness
in dealing kindly with his creatures is altogether free.

[Marginal note: Objection.]

*The divine will,* says our compatriot John Scotus, *is not so determinately
inclined toward any secondary object by anything in itself that it would be
repugnant for it to be justly inclined toward the opposite of that object.*
GOD’s holiness is also twofold. One is absolute and internal, as when God loves
himself and his own goodness, justice, and rectitude as an honorable object.
The other is relative and quasi-external, as when God loves and acts holyly
toward rational creatures outside himself in whatever way seems good to his
wise and free will; and if the opposite seemed good to God’s will, God would
also holyly love and do it. The quasi-rule of this relative holiness is GOD’s
most free will. Sin is formally opposed to GOD’s relative, not his absolute,
holiness, and therefore, strictly speaking, sin cannot be said formally to be
opposed to GOD’s nature.

[Marginal reference: Scotus, book 4, distinction 46, question 1.]

[Marginal summary: GOD’s twofold holiness.]

12. Objection 5. Calvin, says Arminius, *says that GOD wills that evil exist,
but nevertheless does not will evil; yet the distinction has no soundness. For
GOD hates evil and hates the existence of evil; and since evil consists in an
action, its coming to be is its being, and its being is its nature. Because GOD
hates the essence of evil, to speak somewhat improperly, he consequently
forbids evil to occur; and the reason why he wills that sin not exist is that he
hates sin itself. Yet he does not so hate the existence of evil or evil itself
that he does not permit evil to be done by a free cause—not because it is
better*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Examination of Perkins*, p. 701.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 492; IA PDF page 549; BSB canvas 544]

*That evils exist than that they do not, but because it is better, first, not
to impede free obedience, and second, to do well from evil rather than not to
allow evil to exist. This is the sum.* The Jesuit Fasolus says: *The evil of
fault has from itself no character of honorable good. Therefore, neither its
coming to be nor its being can have any character of honorable good, because
the being and coming to be of a thing have the same malice and goodness as the
thing itself, with which they are essentially identified. Therefore, GOD’s will
cannot be borne toward sin, either toward its being or toward its coming to be.*
And Augustine’s *It is good that evils exist* is, he says, to be understood not
of the existence of sin but of the permission of sin, because permission is
good. And Arminius says: *The one who wills the fall to occur wills the event of
the fall; the one who wills the event of the fall wills the fall, for the event
of the fall is the fall, just as the event of the action is the action itself.*

[Marginal reference: Fasolus, on Thomas I, volume 2, question 19, article 9,
doubt 3, sections 9, 10.]

[Marginal reference: *Enchiridion*, 96.]

[Marginal reference: *Examination of Perkins*, p. 647.]

I answer: 1. Arminius shows himself to be a shapeshifting sophist when he
recites our distinctions. He is a bad advocate for our good cause. Our
theologians do not employ the distinction of sin according to essence and
existence unless the distinction of the will of sign and good pleasure is
employed together with it. Who among us denies that God hates and disapproves
the existence of sin? Who teaches that GOD loves and approves the existence of
sin? For this would be for God to approve existing sin, and thus God could
commend and command to us the existence of sin. Scarcely Florinus—even with
difficulty that unclean dog Quintinus—could digest this. But we teach that by
his decreeing will God wills that, with himself permitting, sin should exist
for the manifestation of the glory of his Name, while he abhors, detests, and
disapproves sin itself according to its essence as a dishonorable evil.

[Marginal summary: None of our theologians says that GOD approves existing sin
or the existence of sin.]

2. It is false; it is `ἀθεολόγον`, godless, to say that *sin’s coming to be or
existence is the nature of sin.* What? Is the rose’s existence the rose’s nature
and essence? Then a rose in winter, when it has lost its existence, also loses
its essence. One who philosophized in this way would be `ἄλογον`, irrational,
to Aristotle and Plato. Certainly the essence of sin, according to John’s
definition, is transgression of the law, whether the sin exists or not. God
never refuses the existence of sin, but only its essence. Joseph hated the
violation of his mistress, which never actually existed. David hated the killing
of the Lord’s anointed which he might have committed but never actually did.
Cato the Elder, as Doctor Twisse observes, willed Carthage to be or exist
insofar as it was a whetstone and material for the valor of Roman youth, and
yet he did not will, love, or approve Carthage. Peter ought to have willed that
his Savior

[Marginal summary: GOD’s willing that sin exist is not God’s willing or
approving sin.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 493; IA PDF page 550; BSB canvas 545]

should not spare himself but should be killed by sinners; and
because he did not will this, he heard from the Savior, “Satan, you do not
savor the things which are GOD’s.” Yet he ought to have refused and disapproved
the criminal and unjust killing of the Savior. It is therefore false that either
GOD or a human being can will and not will sin according to its essence and
according to its existence in the same way. Arminius’s argument runs aground.

3. It is false that *the reason GOD does not will sin to exist is that he hates
sin itself.* A good logician ought to have proved by some solid argument that
GOD does not will sin to exist. This appears nowhere in all Scripture. That GOD
does not will any sin to exist in the world is, evidently, proved validly by the
mere breath of Arminius’s mouth or a bare stroke of his pen! GOD’s forbidding
sin to occur is not God’s willing and decreeing that sin not occur, which is
the very point in question.

4. Arminius denies that God permits sin *because it is better that sins exist
than that they not exist, but because it would be better for free obedience to
exist, and it is better that GOD should do well from evil than not allow evil to
exist.* That is, because it is better that sin exist than that it not exist.
For God’s doing well from evil—that is, God’s manifesting the glory of punitive
justice and pardoning mercy—rather than not allowing sin to exist, is in truth
God’s procuring, with himself permitting and the creature effecting it, that sin
exist rather than not procuring its existence. For without the actual existence
of sin the manifestation of the glory of punitive justice and pardoning mercy
is impossible. Thus Arminius says: GOD permits sin not because it is better that
sin exist than that it not exist, but because it is better that sin exist than
that sin not exist. Behold the sophist’s vertigo.

5. Fasolus errs with Arminius when he holds that Augustine must be understood
to speak about the permission of sin, not sin itself; for on that reading
Augustine’s sense would be null: GOD permitted sin to exist because it is better
that it be permitted than that it not be permitted. But Augustine’s very
question was why it is better for sin to be permitted than not permitted. Does
he solve the question well by answering the same thing with itself—namely,
because permission is better than non-permission? Yet that was the question the
Pelagians drove Augustine to solve. Further, nothing could be fashioned more
contrary to Augustine’s words, for he says, *It is good that evils exist,* not,
“It is good that permission exist.” I will not add what is also most true: if

[Marginal summary: In what way the saying “It is better that sin occur or exist
than not occur” is true; and that Augustine in that saying speaks not about
permission but permitted sin.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 494; IA PDF page 551; BSB canvas 546]

the existence of sin is evil and God cannot, with his holiness safe and
intact, will its existence, then it is not even good that sin be permitted. For
God does not do well from evil through permission itself: permission is not the
means by which the glory of punitive justice is manifested; only permitted sin
is such a means. God does not show himself just by punishing permission, or by
freely pardoning permission, but by punishing sins and graciously pardoning
sins in CHRIST.

[Marginal summary: How GOD wills sin with respect to its existence and does not
will it with respect to its essence.]

6. Sin’s coming to be or existing is occasionally good, or it is good in the
sense that, though not honorable, it is nevertheless useful. For matter does
not “matter,” as they say, or exercise the act of mattering or material
causality, except insofar as it actually exists. (For sin considered potentially
and according to its essence would be punished by GOD with no justice.)
Actually existing sin is suitable material for glorifying God through the
manifestation of divine power, severity, justice, longsuffering, and gracious
clemency in CHRIST the Savior. But sin itself, considered according to its
essence, is a dishonorable evil and a disgrace to the glory of the legislator.
Therefore, if sin according to its essence is evil, but according to its
existence is good, it can very well terminate different acts in God—and
sometimes even in a human being—namely, nilling and willing. God can therefore
properly refuse sin according to its essence and will sin according to its
existence.

7. To say without qualification or addition that GOD wills sin—as the
Arminians say to kindle hatred against us and ascribe to us—means that God
effects or approvingly wills sin. But in this sense God wills sin neither in
itself nor for the sake of his glory, for God does not do evil so that good may
come from it. We therefore speak more correctly when we say that God wills or
decrees that sin occur or exist in the nature of things, with himself
permitting, not approving, and the creature effecting it.

8. According to our adversaries’ dogma, for GOD to hate the existence and event
of sin is for GOD to have decreed that sin would never exist in the nature of
things, which the adversaries do not dare say without blushing. For it
contradicts Scripture to say that GOD decreed that the Son of GOD would never
be crucified, Joseph never sold, the spirit of discord between Abimelech and the
Shechemites never exist, the ten tribes never defect from David’s family, GOD’s
people never be devastated by the Chaldeans, or the Chaldeans by the Medes.
Indeed, Vorstius himself says that *there is a rationale of good in sins,*

[Marginal reference: Vorstius, *On GOD*, disputation 6, thesis 6.]

[Chapter XXIX, Part A; printed page 495; IA PDF page 552; BSB canvas 547]

*Not only insofar as they are motions but also insofar as they are sins.* And
who does not know that God hates the wicked, yet wills the existence of the
wicked to endure eternally so that they may be punished eternally?

9. Nor do we agree with Fasolus when he says that *because the evil of fault
has no rationale of honorable good in itself, and because a thing’s being and
coming to be have the same malice and goodness as the thing with which they are
essentially identified, GOD’s will cannot be borne either toward the being or
toward the coming to be or existence of sin.* For 1. Although the evil of fault
has no rationale of honorable good in itself, this is not a sufficient reason
why God—who is by no means bound by the law of created honorableness (for what
God wills is honorable for God because he wills it, not because he prescribes
it as honorable for his creature)—cannot will it. For if it has the rationale
of useful good, God can will it. Sin, however, is a useful good. 2. Although the
being and coming to be of a thing have the same malice and goodness in the
order of honorable good and dishonorable evil, nevertheless, in the order of
useful good and useless evil they do not have the same goodness. The existence
of sin is useful because sin cannot be punished unless it exists, whereas sin
considered according to its essence but never existing is justly punished by
no one. Therefore, sin and existing sin do not have the same goodness. For the
cause and the cause in act do not have the same and equal causation.

[Marginal summary: Sin and existing sin do not have the same goodness and
malice.]

Finally, Arminius falsely says that *the event of the fall is the fall, and the
event of the action is the action itself.* If “event” is taken for an effect
caused or occasioned by sin, this is altogether false: the punishment of sin
is not sin; the redemption of the world is not the crucifixion of the Savior.
But if “the event of the fall” is taken for the fall as occurring and existing,
it is true that existing sin is predicated of sin, for *existing sin is sin.*
Yet sin and existing sin have different goodnesses and terminate GOD’s will in
different ways.

[Marginal summary: The event of sin is not the same thing as sin.]

11. From the prohibition and punishment of sin, it does not follow that God
does not will by the will of sign that sin exist. Indeed, from the will of sign
we cannot infer either the existence or nonexistence of a thing, but only its
morality—namely, what our duty is, whether we obey or not.
