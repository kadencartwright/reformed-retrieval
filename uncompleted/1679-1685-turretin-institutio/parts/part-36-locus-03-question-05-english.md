QUESTION V

Are the divine properties really distinguished from the divine essence?
Negative against the Socinians.

[Marginal heading: What the divine attributes are.]

I. To understand the question, certain things about the divine attributes
must be set out beforehand. Divine attributes are the essential properties by
which God makes himself known to us in our weakness and is distinguished from
creatures, or which are attributed to him according to the measure of our
capacity in order to explain his nature.

II. Attributes are not properly attributed to God as something additional
(ἐπουσιῶδες) that is accidental to a subject, perfects it, and is really
distinct from it. Rather, they are attributed improperly and figuratively,
insofar as they denote essential perfections of the divine nature which are
conceived by us in the manner of properties.

III. Although each attribute represents the most fruitful and most simple
nature of God, the attributes can nevertheless represent it only inadequately:
that is, not according to its whole account, but now under this perfection and
now under another. For what we cannot attain with one adequate concept, since
it is finite, we divide into various inadequate concepts so that we may have
some knowledge of it. This testifies not to error in the intellect, but only
to imperfection. Thus omnipotence is the divine essence itself apprehended as
lacking every obstacle in acting; eternity is the essence of God as lacking
every limit in duration; and so with the rest.

IV. Those inadequate concepts of God's essence are presented to us by
precisive abstraction, or by simple and negative precision, as when I think
about goodness without thinking about power. But they are not presented by
exclusive or privative precision, as if, for example, I were to assert that
someone is omnipotent who is neither merciful nor just.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 170 | IA leaf 224 | PDF p. 224]

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

V. The question about the distinction of the attributes from the divine
essence is raised against us by the Socinians. In order to prove more easily
that the Holy Spirit is not God or a divine person, even though he is called
the power of God, they maintain that God's attributes are really distinguished
from his essence. The orthodox teach that the attributes are really the same
as God's essence itself, but are distinguished from it virtually and
eminently.

VI. Things are said to differ *really* when they are distinguished as diverse
things according to essence, whether or not they belong to the same subject.
To be distinguished *virtually*, however, is nothing other than either to
contain distinct effects virtually, to possess unitedly in oneself things
which are distinct in others, or to possess an eminent power that can be the
principle of diverse actions. Thus the basis of such a distinction can be not
intrinsic but extrinsic—not on the side of the principle or subject, but by
reason of the terminus and object—on account of the diversity of operations
and effects which arise from the properties and according to which different
formal concepts of them are formed.

VII. God's attributes cannot really differ from his essence or from one
another as one thing from another, because God is supremely simple and
perfect. Now a real distinction presupposes things diverse in essence, which
supreme simplicity excludes; and things that are really diverse become one
only through aggregation, which is opposed to absolute perfection. Moreover,
if they differed really, the essence would be perfected by something really
distinct from itself and so would not be supremely perfect in itself. Third,
it would follow that God is not altogether immutable, because he would have
in himself passive potency, the root of mutability, through which the
attributes would either be elicited from the essence or added to it. But since
God is the first and independent being, who is whatever he can be, nothing can
accrue to him or be taken away from him.

VIII. Nevertheless, it is clear from the diversity of concepts that God's
attributes differ both from his essence and from one another. For where there
is a basis for grounding distinct formal concepts of some thing, even though
it is one and simple on the side of the thing itself, some virtual and eminent
distinction must exist. Therefore, since in the most simple divine essence
there is a basis for grounding different formal concepts of the divine
perfections—as is clear from their distinct definition and explanation—the
attributes that ground such concepts are very properly said to be
distinguished both from the essence and from one another virtually.

IX. Although the attributes are essentially and intrinsically one in God,
they are nevertheless rightly said to be distinguished both conceptually
(νοηματικῶς), with respect to a different formal concept, and objectively and
effectively, with respect to various external objects and effects. Hence it is
clear that this distinction is neither simply a real distinction between one
thing and another, nor a formal distinction that exists only by reason of our
mode of conceiving, but an eminent distinction. Although it does not exist on
the side of the thing as between one thing and another, it nevertheless has a
basis in the thing on account of the diversity of objects and effects.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

X. Although our formal concepts of God's essence and his properties are
various, they cannot be called false, because just as many objective concepts
correspond to them. These are indeed actually undivided in God because of his
supreme simplicity, but nevertheless virtually and eminently distinct.

XI. The attributes can be predicated of one another in an identical sense,
insofar as they are considered as having unity and identity with one another,
as when I say, “The divine intellect is the same thing as the will,” or,
“Justice is the same thing as mercy.” But they cannot be so predicated in a
formal sense, because they are formally distinguished by our concepts and by
their own objects. Thus I cannot say, “Justice is mercy,” because the formal
concept of justice differs from the formal concept of mercy.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 171 | IA leaf 225 | PDF p. 225]

XII. Whoever conceives what is actually and really one and simple in God as
actually and really diverse conceives falsely. But whoever conceives what is
one in actuality in itself as many virtually and extrinsically or objectively
does not conceive falsely; rather, because of the weakness of the human
intellect and the eminence and perfection of the divine nature, he conceives
the thing imperfectly and inadequately.

XIII. The divine attributes are considered either absolutely and subjectively
in themselves and on the side of God, or relationally with respect to effects
upon creatures and on the side of the object. I admit that in the latter way
punishing justice and sparing mercy are opposed, but not in the former way,
which is the matter in question.

XIV. The properties are many on the side of the object and terminus, or of
operations and effects, but not on the side of the subject or principle, which
is one and supremely simple.

XV. Where there is prior and posterior with respect to absolute and real
being, there is a real difference; but not where there is only prior and
posterior with respect to known and intelligible being, as occurs in God and
his attributes.

XVI. The definition of a thing in itself is one thing; the definition of our
concepts of the thing is another. The former, not the latter, implies a real
distinction. The definitions of the divine properties, however, are definitions
of our concepts, which conceive God under this and that aspect, rather than of
the thing itself, which is one and most simple.
