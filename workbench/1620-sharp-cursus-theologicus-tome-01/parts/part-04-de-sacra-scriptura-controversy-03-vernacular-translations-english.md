Third Controversy: Translating Scripture into Vernacular Languages

The Roman Catholics do not agree among themselves about this question.
Sanderus, in book 7 of *The Visible Monarchy*, says that it is heretical to
affirm that the Holy Scriptures must necessarily

[1620 Tome I | col. 36 | BSB frame 00032]

be translated into vernacular languages. Bellarmine, however, in book 2,
chapter 15 of *On the Word of God*, says that the catholic church does not
entirely prohibit vernacular translations, but does prohibit their reading
from being granted indiscriminately to everyone and Scripture from being read
in vernacular languages in the public and common use of the church, as is
also established at the Council of Trent. Yet in the same chapter he attempts
to prove that translations are not necessary. We, however, say that Holy
Scripture must be translated into vernacular languages. The state of the
controversy, therefore, will be this: whether it is permissible—indeed,
whether it is necessary—for Holy Scripture to be translated into vernacular
languages. They deny it; we affirm it, relying on these arguments.

1. Argument. If it belongs to every individual to search the Scriptures and
to examine by the touchstone of Holy Scripture the things said in sermons,
then Scripture must necessarily be translated into vernacular languages. But
the former is true (John 5:39; Acts 17:11). Therefore.

2. Argument. If Scripture is to be read by everyone, then it must be
translated into everyone’s vernacular languages. But the former will be
proved in the next controversy. Therefore.

3. Argument.

God himself gave the law in the church’s vernacular language, and he willed
both the Old and the New Testaments to be written in the language most
familiar to the church. Therefore, even today Scripture must be translated
into vernacular languages for the church’s use and benefit.

4. Argument. If Scripture is not to be translated into vernacular languages,
then this is so—as the Roman Catholics claim—lest this sacred doctrine be
contaminated. But this reason is not valid. Therefore. The assumption is
proved: this sacred doctrine is not contaminated when it is taught in a
vernacular language; therefore, neither is it contaminated when translated
into one. It is contaminated more when it is cor—

[1620 Tome I | col. 37 | BSB frame 00033]

—rupted by depraved interpretations in the course of teaching; yet the
adversaries will not therefore say that it ought not to be taught, because the
abuse of a good thing ought not to take away its legitimate use.

5. Argument. If Holy Scripture was translated into Latin for the necessary
use of the Latin church, then by equal reasoning it ought to be translated
into other languages, unless they teach that Scripture is more necessary for
the Latin church than for others. But the former is true, as the adversaries
themselves testify. Therefore, the latter is also true.

6. Argument. The end for which Scripture was given is that we may be saved by
hearing, reading, and understanding it. But the church can neither read nor
understand Scripture unless it is translated into the languages known to each
nation. Therefore.

7. Argument. If bodily food is denied to no one, much less should food for the
soul be denied to anyone. But Holy Scripture is food for the soul. Therefore.
Meanwhile, if it is not translated into the language that all ordinary people
understand, then it is certainly denied to them.

8. Argument. The weapons of Christian warfare must not be taken away from the
faithful; instead, they must always and everywhere be equipped with them. But
since Holy Scripture is precisely these weapons—as is clear from Christ’s
example in Matthew 4, Ephesians 6:16, and Hebrews 4:12—if it is not translated
into vernacular languages, it is taken away from Christians, because they are
armed with these weapons by reading, meditating, and so forth. Therefore.

9. Argument. Scripture is like a burning lamp, given so that it may shine
before everyone and be set forth (Matt. 5:15). Therefore, so that it may shine
for everyone and be seen by everyone, it must necessarily be translated.

10. Argument. In many places there are frequently no ministers of the sacred
word, and if there are, they preach rarely. Therefore, since Christians always
need the light of the word and this medicine for the soul, Scripture must
necessarily be translated so that they can draw these things from it.

Testimonies

Chrysostom says in homily 9 on the Epistle to the Colossians: “Listen, I beg
you, all you laypeople. Obtain Bibles for yourselves, medicines for the soul.
If you desire nothing else, at least acquire the New Testament.” Jerome, even
by Hosius’s own testimony in his book *On Reading Sacred Things in the
Vernacular*, translated Scripture not only into Latin but also into Dalmatian.

Alphonsus de Castro says in book 1, chapter 13: “We acknowledge that the
sacred books were once translated into the vernacular language.” Cornelius
Agrippa says in his book *On the Vanity of the Sciences* that the Nicene Synod
decreed that no Christian should lack a Bible in his own home.

Socrates testifies in book 4, chapter 33 that Ulphilas, the Gothic bishop who
attended the Nicene Council, translated the Scriptures into the Gothic
language.

Eck says in his *Enchiridion*, in the chapter on saying Mass in Latin, that
Armenians, Russians, Ethiopians, Dalmatians, and Muscovites were accustomed to
read the Scriptures in the vernacular language. Harding says the same in
article 3, section 38.

Bede says in book 1, chapter 1 of his *History* that in his time the Scriptures
were read in five British languages.

Objections

Objection 1. Scripture ought to exist only in those languages that Christ
honored by the inscription on the cross. But he honored only three in this
way: Hebrew, Greek, and Latin. Therefore.

Response 1. The proposition is denied. For Christ willed the cause of his
death to appear in various languages, especially familiar ones, so that it
might be understood by everyone. Hence it follows instead that Scripture is
to be translated into every language. 2. If this argument is valid, then the
councils and Fathers who translated the Scriptures erred; and wrongly

[1620 Tome I | col. 39 | BSB frame 00034]

did the pope permit the Moravians to translate Scripture into their own
language, as Bellarmine acknowledges in book 1, chapter 16 of *On the Word of
God*.

Objection 2.

These three languages surpass the rest in antiquity, dignity, and extent.
Therefore, Holy Scripture ought to exist only in them.

Response 1.

Other languages are equal in antiquity and more extensive in common use.

2. We deny the inference, because all languages, just like all nations, have
been sanctified in Christ (Mark 16:17; Acts 2:4, 6; Rom. 14:11; Phil. 2:11).

Objection 3.

The Scriptures ought to exist only in the languages in which they were
originally written by their authors. But they were written in these three:
Hebrew, Greek, and Latin. Therefore.

Response 1.

The proposition is denied, for it was proved above that they are to be
translated.

2. The assumption is denied, for they were not written in Latin by their
authors. Therefore, if the argument is valid, they ought not even to exist in
Latin.

Objection 4.

The apostles preached the gospel throughout the whole world, but they did not
write in the languages of the nations to which they preached. Therefore.

Response 1.

It was not necessary for the apostles to translate their writings into every
language, because this would have been an endless labor; consequently, they
left this work for others to perform. It was enough for them to write in the
most widely shared language, which could then be understood by more people and
from which Scripture could more easily be translated into other languages.
Moreover, the church could for a time do without translations, just as for a
time it did without the writings of the New Testament.

Counterinstance. But Peter, as well as James, wrote to the Jews in Greek. Yet
Greek, for them,

[1620 Tome I | col. 40 | BSB frame 00034]

was not their mother tongue. Therefore.

Response.

They wrote these epistles in Greek because the Greek language was familiar to
the Jews dispersed throughout Greece, and because they knew that the epistles
would benefit not only the Jews, but still more others.

Objection 5.

Whatever the church has observed for a long time must be believed to be done
rightly—and indeed to have taken its beginning from the apostles—unless some
other beginning for it can be shown. But for a long time the church has used
Scripture only in these languages. Therefore.

Response 1. The proposition is denied, because one must ask not what the
church has done, but how rightly it has done it.

2. It was proved above that the church also had other translations; therefore,
the assumption is false.

Objection 6. If there is no reason why Scripture ought to be translated, then
it is not to be translated. But there is no reason. Therefore. The assumption
is proved: if there is any reason, it will be this alone—that the people may
understand Scripture. But they do not understand it; indeed, not even the
learned understand it unless they read commentaries and hear expositors.
Therefore.

Response 1. The assumption is denied. For although the people do not
understand everything as soon as they are read, they can nevertheless understand
many things, as did the eunuch in Acts 8:31. 2. By this reasoning, not even the
learned ought to read the Scriptures, since they themselves do not understand
everything.

Finally, the argument, when reversed, concludes well against the adversaries:
they do not understand; therefore, they ought to read so that they may
understand.

Objection 7. Nothing in Scripture is to be changed (Deut. 4:2; Rev.
22:18–19). But Scripture cannot be translated unless many things are changed.
Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because although Scripture is translated,
Scripture nevertheless remains unchanged in the authentic editions.
Furthermore, in the cited passages the Holy Spi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 41 | BSB frame 00035]

—rit is speaking not about a change of words but of things. Thus in
translations, although words and phrases are changed, the things are not
changed. Finally, if Scripture were changed because its words and phrasing
were changed, then Christ and the apostles, by citing words that were not the
very words of the Old Testament—or at least by citing them in another
language—changed Scripture and transgressed that law, which is unthinkable to
say. It
would also follow from this that Scripture ought not to be translated into
Latin, because Hebrew and Greek words and phrases cannot exist in a Latin
edition.
