QUESTION XVIII

Is God’s will the first rule of justice? A distinction is necessary.

I. This question has not only been raised by the scholastics, who have divided
into parties here, but is also debated among the orthodox themselves. Some of
them stand for the affirmative, holding that all moral goodness and evil
depend upon God’s free will and that nothing is good and just except because
God wills it. Others, on the contrary, stand for the negative and acknowledge
some goodness and justice in moral actions as essential prior to God’s will,
so that those things are not good and just because God wills them, but God
wills them because they are good and just.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. We follow the latter opinion, with the following distinctions laid down.
1. The will can be called the first rule of justice either intrinsically or
extrinsically—that is, either with respect to God or with respect to all
things outside God. In the former sense, God’s will is regulated by his
justice; in the latter, justice in us is regulated by nothing other than his
will. 2. Just as one kind of God’s right is natural and indispensable, founded
upon God’s very nature and holiness, while another is free and positive,
depending only upon his will, so the goodness or evil of things can be related
either to natural right or to positive right.

III. This being established, I say that the will can be called, and really is,
the first rule of justice extrinsically and with respect to us, but not
intrinsically and with respect to God. With respect to us, the source of all
justice must be sought nowhere other than in God’s will. Just as that will is
most perfectly just in itself, so it is the rule of all rectitude and justice,
for the first in any genus is the rule of the rest. In this sense theologians
say that God’s will is the highest rule of justice, and therefore that
whatever God wills is just and good precisely because God wills it. But with
respect to God, the will cannot always be called the first rule of justice. It
is the rule in those things that have only free and positive goodness, but not
in those that have essential goodness—that is, in ceremonial matters, not in
moral matters. For in the latter God’s will is regulated, not extrinsically
but intrinsically, namely, by

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 211 | IA leaf 265 | PDF p. 265]

his most holy nature. Hence it has been well said that some things are good
because God wills them, such as the observance of ceremonies and all positive
commands, while God wills other things because they are just and good, such
as love of God and neighbor, because they are such in themselves and by their
nature. Even if, by an impossible supposition, God did not command them, they
would not cease to be just and therefore to be performed by us.

IV. The reasons are: 1. Just as there is in God natural justice and holiness
prior to his free will, so moral goodness and justice, which are founded upon
God’s justice and holiness and bear their image, cannot fail to be natural.
And just as God cannot deny himself or fail to love his own holiness, so he
cannot fail to love its image and to prescribe it to the creature as the most
perfect rule of justice and holiness.

V. 2. There is in God an eternal and indispensable right prior to every free
act of the will, by which, as Creator, he has dominion over the creature.
Correspondingly, the creature must depend upon God not only in the order of
being with respect to preservation, but also in the moral order with respect
to worship and obedience. That obligation is of such a kind and so strict
that its opposite is incoherent (ἀσύστατον) and cannot be posited without
contradiction. For it is no more possible to conceive that God exempts the
creature from this duty than that he denies himself. Since, therefore, such a
right exists in God prior to his will, some rule of justice exists
independently of the will.

VI. 3. If God’s will were the first rule of justice even intrinsically, so
that nothing were good and just except because God wills it, it would follow
that there was no atheism, magic, Epicureanism, hatred or blasphemy of God, or
other crime of that kind, which God could not command and thereby render
morally good as commanded. For whatever can fall under God’s command can also
be morally sanctioned and rendered just. Thus God could dispense with all the
precepts of the Decalogue and command the creature to believe that God is not
good and holy, that he is not to be worshiped but rather pursued with hatred,
and even that he does not exist—which the mind shudders to relate. Thus, if
God willed, he could be the author and approver of man’s disobedience, for he
could command man not to obey him in anything he commands or forbids. On this
view no obligation in man, and no right of imposing obligation in God, is
presupposed prior to God’s will. Thus there would be no essential vindicatory
justice in God, nor would any necessity of satisfaction against Socinus have
to be urged—something that those who embrace this opinion nevertheless do.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. God is subject to no external moral duty, because he is debtor to no one
and there is no cause outside God that can obligate him. But he can be bound
internally, because he is debtor to himself and cannot deny himself, just as
in divine matters the Son is obligated by the Father to act and the Father is
obligated to love the Son. So also in external actions, once it is posited
that the creature has been produced, God cannot fail to command it and give it
just and holy precepts.

VIII. The law is called the norm of all good and evil actions with respect to
us and our knowledge, because the ground of goodness and evil becomes known
to us from no source other than the law. But with respect to God and the very
right to obligate or command, a ground exists prior to God’s will, because it
is founded upon God’s very majesty and holiness.

IX. It is absurd to say that God depends upon something outside himself, but
it is not absurd to say that God depends upon himself, provided this is
understood in a manner worthy of God (θεοπρεπῶς), namely, that God wills
nothing except in accordance with his holiness and justice.

X. God is exempt from the law that he establishes for man, formally speaking,
that is,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 212 | IA leaf 266 | PDF p. 266]

in taking the law as law. But he is not free and absolved from all the matter
of the law, so that he could either command or himself do its opposite—for
example, believe that he is not God and command others to believe this, which
pious ears cannot hear without horror.

XI. Although the divine will is free without qualification in relation to
things outside God, nevertheless, upon the supposition of one free act it can
be necessitated to another. Thus, if God wills to promise absolutely, he
cannot fail to fulfill what was promised; if he wills to speak or reveal
himself, he must necessarily reveal the truth; if he wills to govern, he must
govern justly. Accordingly, the divine will cannot of itself be determined to
will that something exist or not exist, but it can indeed be determined, with
respect to complex states of affairs, toward the rectitude of things that
exist or that it wills to exist.

XII. Man sins immediately against God’s revealed law, but he also sins
mediately and consequently against God, the author of the law and the supreme
Lord who fixed the law. Thus sin always remains lawlessness (ἀνομία), both
with respect to the external revealed law and to the eternal law founded upon
God’s holiness.
