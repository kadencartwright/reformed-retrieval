BOOK III.
ON CLERICS.

CHAPTER I. On the name “Clerics.”

The first question is whether the ministers of the Church are fittingly and
with just reason distinguished from the people of God by the designation
“Clerics.” The Pontificians affirm it; we deny it.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. *Drawn from the divine writings, this designation has been in the
mouth of all councils, all the Fathers, the whole Church, and the whole world,
in every age and century.*

Protestants. I. It could not have been drawn from the Scriptures, since it is
not contained in the Scriptures as a designation proper to the ministers of
either the New or even the Old Testament. II. To no one except God himself and
Christ belong the authority and right to impose names by which the ministers

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n276 | printed p. 59]

of God are distinguished from others. For the imposition of names belongs to
parents and masters: masters are accustomed to give names to slaves and
possessions, and parents to their offspring, at their own discretion, as is
clear from the Scriptures. From this it has come about that the imposition of
names denotes right, authority, and dominion, Genesis 2:19–20; Psalm 147:4;
Isaiah 40:26. If, therefore, councils or the Fathers imposed this name upon the
ministers of God, they rashly arrogated to themselves authority over another's
servants. III. That what Bellarmine affirms concerning *all the Fathers, the
whole Church, and the whole world* is false and fabricated is sufficiently
clear from the fact that he produces no instance or testimony from the first
two centuries after Christ in which a designation of this kind is appropriated
to ministers.

2. Bellarmine. *Tertullian speaks in this manner in* On Monogamy: *When we are
exalted and puffed up against the clergy, then we are all one; then we are all
priests, and so forth.*

Protestants. I. Tertullian flourished at the beginning of the third century.
We do not deny that this designation was in use around that time. But we say
that then other unsuitable phrases also crept into the Church—concerning
*priests, altars, merits,* and so forth—which departed from the style of
Scripture, to the detriment of truth and peril of the Church. II. When
Tertullian was writing this book, he had defected from the Church to the heresy
of Montanus. Those very people whom he says were puffed up against the clergy
and had less approved the designations *clergy* and *priesthood* were true and
orthodox Christians. Thus, from this very passage it can be gathered that in
the beginning the Church admitted new titles of this kind only reluctantly,
titles introduced by certain innovators with an affectation not to be
approved. III. There is another designation drawn from the Scriptures and used
by Tertullian which the Pontificians now spurn, *Apology*, chapter 39:
*Approved elders preside.*

3. Bellarmine. *In Jeremiah 12 the Holy Spirit speaks thus: “Their clerics will
not profit them.” Origen, Epiphanius, and Jerome interpret these words of
ecclesiastical orders.*

Protestants. The Holy Spirit says nothing of the kind; rather, some Greek
translator does so mistakenly. Origen and others after him, proceeding
allegorically, sometimes seized an opportunity for wordplay from an error of
translation.

4. Bellarmine. *Dathan and Abiram formerly cried out the same thing against
the priests of the Old Testament, Numbers 16.*

Protestants. It was not the same. For I. there was no dispute or question there
about a name, but about the priestly office itself, verses 10 and 12. II. They
were raising sedition against priests instituted and sanctified by God. We,
however, contend for such priests against the ambition and conspiracy of

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n277 | printed p. 60]

those who by unjust force have occupied their place and the privileges of the
people of God.

5. Protestants. The whole Church is called the *clerus* in the Scriptures, 1
Peter 5:3.

Bellarmine I. *It is probable that the designation* cleri *used in this passage
ought to be referred not to the universal Church but only to those who are
commonly called Clerics in the proper sense. Thus bishops are warned, when
feeding the laity, to beware of shameful gain, but when governing clerics (who
were the bishops' own ministers), to flee the desire to dominate.*

Protestants. I. If dominion over ministers is here forbidden to bishops, then
the practice of the Pope, the papalists, and the bishops is expressly
condemned. They arrogate to themselves the right to assign inferior ministers
at their own discretion to this or that use, laying laws upon them and imposing
fines, which are the proper acts of masters. II. Bellarmine himself professes
the same thing when he says that clerics are *the bishops' own ministers.* Yet
this is so false that it cannot be affirmed even of the Apostles themselves
that they had presbyters as their ministers. Peter shows this plainly when he
writes to the presbyters as a colleague, a *fellow-presbyter*, verse 1, and
acknowledges Christ as the one *Chief Shepherd*, verse 4. Ecclesiastical
ministers belong to the Church insofar as the Church is the object about which
they minister. They belong to Christ insofar as he is the principal cause and
the Lord upon whom the ministers depend. But in no way do they belong to the
bishops, except in the manner in which all things are said to belong to their
possessor, whether by right, that is, or by wrong. III. No mention is made by
Peter of lordly bishops, but of bishops of the Lord, that is, presbyters, verse
1: *I exhort the presbyters who are among you.* IV. Two duties of a pastor are
not set out, as Bellarmine dreams—*to feed the laity and to govern the
clerics*—but the common duty of feeding and its one common object, verse 2:
*Feed the flock of God which is among you.* This is clear from the connection
of the manner prescribed in verse 3 with what precedes, as all pertaining to
one act of feeding: *Feed, not by compulsion, not shamefully, nor as
those who dominate.* V. That *clerus* and flock denote one and the same body as
the object of that act is clear from the same connection. It is also clear
from the opposition between domination and being set forth as examples, which
are not opposed except with respect to the same thing: *not as those who
dominate the* cleri, *but as examples to the flock.* IV. Cajetan, in his
explanation of this passage, thus concludes with us: *Do not understand by the*
cleri *those whom we call Clerics, but all Christians chosen for the divine
lot.*

6. Bellarmine. *Without doubt, St. Peter alluded to the Jewish Levites, who in
Numbers 18 and Deuteronomy 18 are said to be of the Lord's portion, and the
Lord*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n278 | printed p. 61]

*is called their portion. But the Levites now correspond only to priests and
their ministers.*

Protestants. I. It is not beyond doubt that Peter had the Levites in view. For
it is not the Levites but the Church, or the people of God, that is everywhere
in the Old Testament called the *portion, inheritance, and possession of God.*
II. It is the Church, not the ministers of the Church, that is called by Peter
in chapter 2, verse 9 *a priesthood and a people whom God claims for himself as
his own*, that is, a portion. III. Neither the Levites nor the ministers of the
New Testament are anywhere called *portions or clerics.* Indeed, they are never
called the Lord's portion, although the Lord is called their portion; and the
office of ministry which fell to them by selection is also designated by the
name of a lot.

7. Bellarmine. *The same designation of “clergy” can most fittingly apply, in
different senses, both to the universal Church and to the ministers of the
temple alone.*

Protestants. It can indeed be granted that it is used specifically of
ministers, but on this condition and proviso: I. no style or solemn title for
ministers is to be fashioned from it without the authority of God. II. No
division of estates is to be introduced from it, as though the people of God
were base in comparison with clerics. III. Only incidentally, as occasion
arises and with the concept thus explained, are the phrases and designations
of Scripture—by which the ordinances of God are best and most safely
explained—to be regularly employed and observed in common usage. IV. It must
not, however,
be granted that *most fittingly* is in any way said of ministers, that is, more
fittingly than of the universal Church, because it is attributed to the Church
by God, but to ministers by human beings alone.

Bellarmine. *The Church is Christ's portion and inheritance because he
acquired her by his blood. Yet at the same time a certain definite part of the
Church, consecrated to the ministry of the altar, can in a more proper sense be
called Christ's portion and inheritance. So the Jewish synagogue was commonly
called the Lord's inheritance, and yet the Lord called the tribe of Levi alone
such in a special sense.*

Protestants. I. The Lord nowhere so called the tribe of Levi in a special
sense. II. There can be no more proper reason for this designation than that
redemption which pertains to the whole Church. III. After Christ's sacrifice
has been offered, the ministry of an external altar in no way pertains to
Christ's inheritance, any more than darkness pertains to light. IV. Unless the
ministers of the altar are more redeemed than the Church, this designation
cannot, except equivocally, apply to them more than to the whole Church.
