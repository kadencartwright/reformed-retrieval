### QUESTION VIII

Whether GOD exercises providence over all things by necessity of nature or
freely.

There is that saying of Lactantius: *Certainly he is provident, as GOD; nor can
divinity otherwise be attributed to him unless he both grasps past things,
knows present things, and foresees future things.* And there is the saying of
others: *GOD exists; therefore providence exists.* This may be called into
examination without impiety, for *God exists* and *GOD’s providence exists* do
not hold by an equal necessity of nature. 1. God exists by absolute necessity,
and *that God does not exist* implies a contradiction. But GOD’s providence
toward things outside himself is not by absolute necessity, just as creation is
not by any necessity of nature. For if God did not exist, God would be not-God;
but if no providence had been exercised outwardly toward created things, I doubt
whether God would thereby be God and not-GOD at once. At least, from eternity
God was not not-God. Yet he would have been blessed from eternity even if
neither creation nor actual providence had existed.

*Providence toward external things exists on the supposition* (you will say)
*that the creation of all things occurs; by necessity of nature GOD exercises
providence over the presupposed created things.* Certainly God would violate his
own nature if he had decreed to create and yet either did not care for or did
not know the things created. But GOD’s knowledge concerning created things is
an act of providence; and thus (I say) he would create the world by necessity of
nature, and God would not exist unless he had created the world. For on the
supposition that he decreed to create the world, he cannot fail to create the
world unless he were mutable; and certainly a mutable God is not God. Can a
hypothetical necessity in GOD therefore become an absolute necessity and a
necessity of nature (as they call it)? It appears, therefore, that GOD somehow
(πῶς), and in a certain sense, acts toward external things by necessity of
nature, because GOD’s nature obliges

and binds him to act outwardly immutably, omnisciently, and agreeably to his own
nature, on the supposition that he acts outwardly. But, simply speaking, he does
not act outwardly by necessity of nature, but only in a qualified respect.
Necessity of nature therefore belongs to GOD in another and far more eminent
manner than it belongs to creatures. For a twofold necessity of nature belongs
to GOD. One concerns the nature of the thing predicated and the substance of
the act. Thus by necessity of nature God is just, good, merciful, immense, and
omnipotent, and by necessity of nature he loves himself. In this sense God does
not by necessity of nature either create the world or govern the world by his
providence, because such acts do not inhere in GOD essentially, but are done
freely.

The other necessity of nature in GOD does not concern the nature of the thing
or the manner of acting: on the supposition that God decreed the world and that
the world is created, God creates the world and provides for the created world
by the necessity of his immutable nature and infinite wisdom. For he would
violate his immutable nature and wisdom; and consequently God would not be God
if he did not create and govern the things which he had decreed at once to
create and govern. But
this latter necessity agrees perfectly with GOD’s freedom, because, on the
supposition that God acts, he necessarily acts in a manner agreeable to his
immutable, wise, just, good, and omniscient nature. This is what Lactantius
intended, and Gregory of Nyssa likewise: *Because one who is wise and good
exercises providence wisely and in the best way over the things that exist. For
if he does not provide, he is not good; but if he does not provide well, he is
by no means wise.*

They are therefore greatly mistaken who teach that GOD cannot punish sin and
cannot fail, by his law, to command every good and forbid every evil. Hence
Cameron and others infer that by necessity of nature in the former sense, *and
with an affection wholly like that with which GOD loves himself,* he punishes
sin, commands every good by his law, and forbids evil. But in reality and
strictly speaking, God would not cease to be God if he did not punish or if he
did nothing outwardly. Rather, it is only by hypothetical necessity that he
would not be God if he did not act in this manner, since he can simply refrain
from acting and acts in this way altogether freely.
