We come at last to the third question, the crediting of Christ's
righteousness for our justification. Three further questions may be asked:

1. In what sense do we say that Christ's righteousness is credited for our
justification?

2. Is it truly credited to us as righteousness in that sense?

3. Which righteousness of Christ is credited to us for this purpose?

The first question—how Christ's righteousness should be said to be credited
for our justification—can regard either the crediting or the justification.

If it regards crediting, since we have enumerated several meanings and
kinds of crediting, the question is: by which of them do we say that
Christ's righteousness is credited to us for justification?

If it regards justification, the question is under what notion or relation
we want it to contribute to and be credited for our justification: as an
efficient, material, formal, or some other kind of cause.

To explain the first, namely the kind of crediting by which Christ's
righteousness is credited to us, we must recall and apply the previously
enumerated distinctions.

The first distinguished the crediting of a good or evil thing. It is hardly
necessary to say that this is the crediting of the very best thing, since it
is Christ's righteousness. It is goodness in itself, belonging to the one
who is universally good, Matthew 19:17, and it makes us blessed, Romans
4:6–8.

The second distinction can be no more controversial. Every crediting is
either righteous and right or unrighteous and unjust; if God performs it,
it cannot be unjust. “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?” Genesis
18:25. God himself does this according to Romans 4:6: God “credits
righteousness”—Christ's righteousness, as will subsequently appear—“apart
from works.” It does not detract from God's justice that this crediting is
said to be made apart from works and even, in verse 5, to the ungodly. We
have already shown that justification consists in forgiving offenses and,
from that forgiveness, accepting a person as righteous. Although we are
without works, indeed ungodly, guilty, and liable under every designation,
God's regarding even such persons as righteous gives no one a charge to
bring against his divine justice. In a human court, however, Solomon's
statement ob-

[1679 printed p. 218 | IA leaf 226]

tains: “He who acquits the wicked and condemns the righteous is an
abomination to the Lord,” Proverbs 17:15. This applies when a case is offered
to an inferior judge in which there is no satisfaction either by the guilty
person or his surety. The judge is inferior, not the supreme ruler, and thus
lacks authority to grant a free pardon. But if he is the supreme ruler,
then, although the guilty person considered in himself stands accused, the
ruler may do either of two things:

If a surety has made full satisfaction which satisfies justice, he may
acquit the guilty person and regard him as righteous without any stain of
injustice, especially when this occurs by a covenant freely made between the
ruler and the surety;

Or, as supreme ruler, he may in his mercy freely forgive the guilty person's
offense without any appearance of injustice, just as a creditor may in
either manner refrain from condemning a debtor.

All this applies most fully to God.

First, he is essentially and infinitely just and holy in himself. He
therefore hates sin, and condemns and punishes the sinner considered in
himself and remaining such, Psalm 5:4; Romans 1:18.

Second, according to his eternal decree and the covenant freely made with
Christ our surety, full satisfaction has been rendered to his justice in
our place by this surety, who was made a curse and sin. Thus our
justification is admitted without injury to divine justice, and God can be
“just and the justifier,” Romans 3:26. The one who justifies a sinner who
believes in Christ is, by this very fact, truly and properly just according
to the word's primary and most proper sense. I see no reason why that sense
should not obtain there.

Third, some perhaps contend too insistently that “just” there means kind or
merciful: God is just—that is, kind and propitious—in justifying the sinner,
while his justice properly so called remains intact. Because he is the
supreme ruler, as Lactantius and many others thought, it lies in his power
to grant pardon freely without prejudice to his justice. As far as we and
our merits are concerned, this occurs by grace alone. Yet as far as our
surety's satisfaction is concerned—whether God's decree necessarily
requires it, as all affirm, or his nature also requires it, as some
maintain—justice itself is preserved and the matter is transacted by the
highest right. Thus, though in different respects, righteousness is at once
credited to the believing sinner from pure grace and by supreme justice.

Third, the previous distinction between crediting according to debt and
according to grace creates no difficulty here. The crediting of Christ's
righteousness to us, for whom and in whose place he rendered full
satisfaction, is owed to Christ as the one who so satisfied, and in part to
us in Christ by the covenant. Yet considered in ourselves, where nothing of
this kind but its contrary is deservedly found, it is entirely and wholly
gracious. Thus believers are justified and saved according to both the
covenant of works and the covenant of grace. Christ fulfilled the former
covenant for us: “Do this and live.” The law is therefore satisfied, and in
Christ life falls to us according to the law itself. Paul thus says in
Romans 8:4 that “the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in
us.” Beza rightly explains this as “the very thing the law requires, namely
both the full payment of the penalty owed by us, that we may be freed from
condemna-

[1679 printed p. 219 | IA leaf 227]

tion, and the full performance of the law, that we may be found entitled to
eternal life.” Let Satan turn this promise, “Do this and live,” in every
direction: we are righteous before God even according to the law's strictest
and most absolute formula.

As for ourselves, in the state of nature we are wholly dead in sins and
therefore children of wrath, Ephesians 2:1, 3; and in the state of grace we
are not yet fully emancipated from sin, 1 John 3:8. Both with respect to
infused righteousness and with respect to justification properly so called
from this gracious crediting, from the foundation's lowest point to the
highest stone we acclaim to him, as those of old, “Grace, grace,” Zechariah
4:7. Therefore:

Fourth, since crediting is either essential or by thought, the crediting of
Christ's righteousness to us cannot be the essential kind. As we showed
before, that rests upon the real existence and inherence of the thing
credited in the person to whom it is credited. The matter is far otherwise
in our justification, which regards us not as already inherently righteous
but as antecedently ungodly. It is therefore crediting by thought, by which
we are graciously regarded as righteous. Yet it is not imaginary or without
any foundation. It rests upon God's eternal election and love, his decree,
and Christ's full satisfaction as its causes. It also has supremely real
effects: not only inherent righteousness begun here and eventually
perfected in heaven, but, with respect to justification itself, a most true
and blessed change of our state for the better. We are no longer guilty and
liable to condemnation, Romans 8:1, but are actually given forgiveness of
sins, Romans 4:6–8, and truly constituted righteous, Romans 5:19. We have
peace with God, Romans 5:1, and access through faith into grace, verse 2. Not
only so, but we glory in tribulations, verse 3, and beyond that glory in God,
verse 11.

Fifth and finally, since this crediting by thought is either of a person or
of a thing, both occur in our justification. Here there is a twofold
transfer—or, so to speak, exchange or substitution.

There is a transfer of persons: as our sins are transferred to Christ, so
Christ's righteousness is transferred to us, 2 Corinthians 5:21.

Second, there is a transfer of things, when one thing enters another's place,
supplies its office, and is reckoned for it. Thus Romans 2:26 says that
uncircumcision is reckoned for circumcision. Some likewise say that faith is
reckoned to Abraham here, and to the other believers in verse 5, as
righteousness, as though it supplied the office of legal righteousness and
were accepted for it by God through the grace of the gospel. With God's
help, we will later show this to be utterly false. It is, however, entirely
true of Christ's righteousness credited to us. By a most blessed exchange,
Christ's perfect righteousness is substituted for the righteousness owed by
us to the law which we lacked. His righteousness is satisfaction of the law
for us with interest and infinite superabundance. God therefore accepts it
for our justification insofar as it is carried into our account through
crediting.

We have thus established in which meaning of crediting Christ's
righteousness is credited to us for justification.

Second, if one asks in what sense it is credited in relation to our
justification itself, I answer:

[1679 printed p. 220 | IA leaf 228]

First, as a meritorious cause. By both acting and suffering, Christ
perfectly satisfied the law for us and fulfilled all righteousness, Matthew
3:15. His righteousness is credited to us who believe so that it merits
righteousness and life for us, according to Romans 3:24: “justified freely
by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus”; and Romans
5:18: “through one righteous deed to all people unto justification of
life”—that is, as the procuring and meritorious cause of the right.

As far as I understand, even the Socinians will not deny this. Although they
blasphemously deny every satisfaction made by Christ in our place, they
affirm that Christ died for us—that is, for our good—so that if we believe
him we shall most certainly obtain justification and eternal life, and that
deservedly. Certainly the Roman writers, although they make credited
righteousness a laughingstock and receive it with jeers, do not dare deny
that Christ's righteousness and merit are credited to us for salvation in
this sense. Bellarmine says: “Inherent righteousness, together with the
remission of guilt and eternal punishment, is the effect of Christ's
satisfaction, which is conferred and applied to us in justification. In this
way it would not be absurd if someone said that Christ's righteousness is
credited to us,” and so forth. He says this here and similar things
elsewhere, as we shall subsequently see. They do not deny, I say, that
Christ's righteousness is credited to us as the meritoriously efficient
cause of our justification. They deny only that it is its formal cause.

Second, therefore, we ask whether the crediting of Christ's righteousness is
the formal cause or reason of our justification, and whether the same thing
can be both the meritoriously efficient and formal cause of the same thing.

I answer:

First, the Roman party understands justification as the infusion of
inherent righteousness and thus confounds justification with
sanctification. If that were the true nature and definition of
justification, they would rightly deny that crediting Christ's righteousness
is the formal cause or reason of such a justification—that is, of
sanctification. We are not so foolish or blasphemous as ever to say or think
that Christ's righteousness credited to us makes us formally righteous with
respect to inherence, as though we were inherently and formally righteous
by Christ's righteousness. If so, as they vainly object, we could be
righteous by Christ's righteousness just as we can be wise and upright by
another person's wisdom and virtue; indeed, we could be as righteous as
Christ himself, and why not be Christ himself? Christ's righteousness is
proper to him, inseparable from him, and incommunicable to others, just as
any proper accident or the very essence of another thing. We assert that it
inheres subjectively in Christ alone and is communicated to us in
justification only by crediting.

Second, then, when it is asked whether Christ's righteousness, thus
credited, makes us formally righteous with respect to justification as the
formal cause of our justification, our theologians—the Methodists and
others—affirm it, and rightly. Justification may be considered actively in
God as the agent or originating term, or passively in the justi-

[1679 printed p. 221 | IA leaf 229]

fied person as the receiving subject or terminating point, denoting his
qualification or state.

In the former sense justification is the act of God who justifies. Matter
and form are not predicated of actions as properly as of substances, and we
need not split hairs. Yet an action is commonly called formal either
antecedently, because it constitutes a form, or consequently, because it
flows from a form. If the question is whether crediting Christ's
righteousness is God's formal act by which he formally justifies us or
regards us as righteous, I answer that according to many theologians
justification contains two things: forgiveness of sins and the crediting of
Christ's righteousness. Paul joins both in this business: “Blessed is the
person to whom God credits righteousness apart from works,” and, “Blessed
is the one to whom God does not credit sin,” Romans 4:6–8. If they are
regarded as two constituent parts of justification, a matter to be discussed
later, the crediting of Christ's righteousness contributes only partially
to the complete formal act of justification. But if, as some think, these
two really signify the same thing under different manners of considering
it—as the starting and terminating points—then the whole formality of
justification is contained, at least synecdochically, under this crediting
of righteousness. This crediting may then rightly be called the formal act
of justifying, insofar as it constitutes a person righteous with respect to
justification in Paul's forensic sense. Immediately and consequently from
this crediting a person is justified, that is, regarded as righteous by God,
just as the crediting of a payment made to a creditor by a surety frees the
debtor from the bond of debt and constitutes him free.

The same applies to justification taken passively. Since Christ's
righteousness is the matter of our justification, its being credited to us
may rightly be called its form. Christ's righteousness, I say, is the matter
of our justification, the thing from which the whole of our righteousness
with respect to justification consists. Its crediting to us makes us
formally righteous with respect to justification; hence Philippians 3:9 says
we are found in it. Just as the ransom paid by a surety is the thing from
which a debtor's liberation consists, when it is carried into his account
he is actually liberated.

Thus Christ's righteousness is the meritoriously efficient cause for which
God justifies us, and the matter from which our righteousness with respect
to justification consists. By its crediting, God on his part formally and
actively justifies us, and we on our part are passively justified. It is,
as the Leiden theologians say most correctly, “that on account of which, in
which, and through which we are justified.” Paul therefore fittingly says
that Christ is “all in all,” Colossians 3:11, since on his part he confers
all things upon us, and that we are “complete in him,” Colossians 2:10,
since on our part we draw everything from his fullness. All glory and
thanksgiving must therefore be returned to him alone.

So much for the first question: in what sense Christ's righteousness is
said to be credited for our justification, both according to the several
meanings of crediting and the various kinds of causality in justification.
