VERSE 3. For just as a dream comes forth from much occupation, so the voice of
a fool comes from a multitude of words.

He proves this caution concerning conciseness of speech from the folly that
accompanies much speaking. For since a slip of the tongue is so easy and ready
that only the man who does not sin with his tongue is to be considered
perfect—James 3:2—it is surely impossible for one who pours out many words not
to go astray. Premeditation precedes well-considered speech, and the mind can
scarcely, if at all, remain intent for a long time. It therefore happens that
amid a multitude of words some things fall out rashly and without
consideration.

Hence Solomon says that the end and final part of a foolish man's speech is
madness. Conversely, if a fool keeps silent, he will be regarded as wise. This
is argued from a comparison of equal cases: deranged and foolish speech arises
from much speaking just as many dreams arise from many occupations. The things
which the senses transmit to the understanding during the day, once impressed
and fixed more deeply, are sent back to the imagination, the common sense. If
our mind were wholly fixed upon godly exercises on the Lord's Day, those same
things would recur while we slept. Nor should we despise what
