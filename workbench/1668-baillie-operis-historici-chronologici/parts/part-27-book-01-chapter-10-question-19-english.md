### QUESTION XIX

#### What should be determined about the fasts of four months mentioned by Zechariah in 7:3 and 8:19?

Answer. I do not intend to digress here into questions about Hebrew months,
years, and days. We reserve those, together with the other rough and thorny
problems of chronology, to be weighed in their proper place with the good aid
of God. Nor shall we discuss the persons who raised this question about the
fasts: whether Sherezer and Regem-melech were Persian princes and satraps and
courtiers of Darius, as the Rabbis maintain; whether they were leading men
among the Samaritans, as Cyril of Alexandria and others of the Fathers hold;
or whether they were Jews

[Printed p. 192 | IA leaf 222 | access PDF 222 | OCR object 221]

still remaining in Babylon after the captivity had been released, as Calvin
and a host of interpreters hold. We are not now investigating these matters.
Our question concerns only the Prophet’s answer to the delegates’ inquiry.
They raised only one fast, in the fifth month, which they customarily observed
every year in Babylon. The Prophet answers concerning all the fasts, or at
least the four in greatest use during the period of the captivity. What,
therefore, were the fasts at these four appointed times?

Jerome testifies on the passage that Christian interpreters of the Prophet
say nothing at all about these fasts. Certainly Theodoret, a contemporary of
Jerome, passes over the fasts without touching them in his commentary. Cyril
of Alexandria, however, a writer of the same century, acknowledges that so
many before him had written on the Minor Prophets—although almost all of them
appear to have perished long ago—that in his prologue he writes a defense of
his own labor after so many others as not altogether useless.

Cyril indeed brings forth clearly and plainly, from the Scriptures themselves,
the following points about these four fasts, in which we rest: the fast of the
fourth month was instituted because Jerusalem was taken by Nebuchadnezzar, and
the fast of the fifth because the temple was burned; the fast of the seventh
month was because Gedaliah was killed, and that of the tenth because the city
was besieged by Nebuchadnezzar. Clear testimonies of Scripture show that these
mournful events, which supplied the Jews with the most just cause for great
grief and fasting, occurred in the stated months. Jeremiah 52:4 records that
Jerusalem began to be surrounded by a siege on the tenth day of the tenth
month. Verses 6 and 7 of the same chapter record that the wall was breached
and the city taken on the ninth day of the fourth month; verse 12, that the
city and temple were burned on the tenth day of the fifth month. And from 2
Kings 25:25 we have that Gedaliah was killed in the seventh month. Since Cyril,
the common opinion of interpreters of Scripture and chronologists has also
been that fasts were observed by the Jewish captives in Babylon during those
four months for the reasons named in these passages of Scripture.

> Marginalia, printed p. 192: *Cyril of Alexandria correctly explains the
> fasts of the captivity*.

From the Jewish calendar Jerome adduces many other fasts during those four
months and for other reasons: on the tenth day of the fourth month, because
Moses broke the tablets of the law that day; on the first day of the fifth
month, because of Moses’s death; on the sixth, because the virgins were
carried off by the Benjamites; on the ninth, because of the sedition stirred
up by the spies of the land of Canaan; and on the eighteenth, because the lamp
in the temple was extinguished in the days of Ahaz. In the seventh month, the
third day was because Gedaliah was killed; the fifth, because the Israelites
coming to Gedaliah were killed; and the tenth, because of the people’s solemn
annual atonement. In the tenth month, the fifth day was because the sad news
of the burned city came to Ezekiel; the eighth, because the law had been
translated into Greek by the seventy interpreters.

> Marginalia, printed p. 192: *Jerome does not explain them correctly*.

It is asked whether the Prophet’s words should be understood of all these
fasts. Certainly the fast because of the work of the seventy interpreters,
which occurred long after Zechariah’s days, cannot be meant. Following Jerome,
the Jesuit Ribera defends most of the others at length, but the remaining
teachers, including the Roman Catholics Lyranus, Vatablus, Sanctius, and
others, confine themselves to the four named by Cyril.

Indeed, we are not investigating the one point Ribera attempts to prove:
whether Moses’s death, the spies’ sedition, and the other events occurred on
the days of those months mentioned above. The question is whether, if this is
granted, it follows that public annual fasts were instituted by the people for
these reasons and observed even in the Babylonian captivity. Ribera offers no
solid argument for this belief, nor can anyone offer one. The Jewish
calendars, especially the one published by Genebrardus, teem with fables.

But since the serious difficulty commonly raised here concerning the right to
institute fasts is theological, not chronological, we shall touch upon it in
only a word or two. We assert that Grotius had no ground to complain in this
passage that our writers raise a question about the Israelite people’s right
to institute public fasts. Not one of us ever called this into doubt.
Certainly Calvin, Gualter, Danaeus, and every Protestant whom I remember
reading on this passage uphold that right.

> Marginalia, printed p. 192: *Hugo Grotius’s slander*; *The Reformed affirm
> the Church’s power to institute fasts*.

Why should the Jewish Church not have possessed the right, while eminent
Prophets—Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, and others—still lived in it, to institute
solemn humiliations for the most mournful reasons, such as the burning of
Jerusalem and the temple, the murder of Gedaliah, and the people’s captivity,
and to continue them until God turned from the severity of his wrath to have
mercy on his afflicted people?

But suppose the Jews infer from this that their profane and perverse synagogue
may lawfully impose upon the people everything that we read in their calendar:
observances of either feasts or fasts to be perpetuated through every age for
whatever reasons happened to please the minds of a few witless Rabbis. And
suppose the Jesuits Sanctius, Ribera, Cornelius, and others likewise conclude
from this that the Roman Church’s practice should be approved after the Jews’
example: fasts in its four seasons, with Lent in spring and others in summer,
autumn, and winter, and feast days on almost every day of the year in honor of
more saints than the Roman calendar can contain. Despite all this, sensible
theologians will abstain from such Jewish and Antichristian superstitions,
although

> Marginalia, printed p. 192: *But they refuse to agree with Grotius*.

Hugo Grotius may have formed conclusions of this kind and many others that
overturn the Reformed churches from their very foundations. They impose upon
Protestants not only the necessity of returning to the bosom of the Roman
Church, even as it sits today beneath the umbrella of the Tridentine faith,
but also of heartily embracing as true and pious most of the loathsome
doctrines and practices of that great Whore.

Although, I say, divine justice punished the very learned Hugo Grotius’s
apostasy from the Reformed churches—small in a few matters at first—with great
and utterly perverse blindness in very many, all other Protestants nevertheless
carefully guard, and will continue to guard, against every concession that
opens a broad gate for Roman Catholics to bring in cartloads crammed with
their superstitions and idolatries.

> Marginalia, printed p. 193: *Embracing cartloads of Roman superstitions*.
