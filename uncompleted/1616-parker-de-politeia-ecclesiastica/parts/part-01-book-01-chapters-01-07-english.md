ON ECCLESIASTICAL POLITY.

BOOK ONE.

On Its Nature and Excellence.

CHAPTER I.

On Its Definition.

Reason requires that, when we are going to speak about ecclesiastical polity,
we begin with its definition. We may thereby satisfy Sutcliffe, who complains
that it is very often omitted in the writings of our party. What that
definition ought to be can most easily be elicited by analogy with the other
forms of order by which human and Christian societies are adorned.

[Marginal reference: *On Ecclesiastical Discipline*, Sutcliffe, chapter 2.]

What, then, is household government? It is that order by which a household is
directed, through domestic means, to attain its domestic happiness, 1 Kings
10:5. What is civil polity? It is that order by which a commonwealth is
advanced, through civil means, toward civil happiness, Deuteronomy 4:5. This
is what Aristotle thought: “A polity is the ordering,” he says, “of those who
inhabit a city.” Why, then, should ecclesiastical polity not be defined after
their pattern—as that order by which the congregation of the Church is
directed through ecclesiastical means toward its public happiness in the
House and City of God and in the communion of saints? Certainly Paul himself
seems to have defined it so. In Colossians 2:5 he calls it “order,” and in 1
Timothy 3:15 that order by which all ranks of the faithful are taught how they
ought to conduct themselves in the Church, which is the house of the living
God.

[Marginal reference: Aristotle, *Politics*, book 3.]

A clear analysis of each of these definitions will be obtained only when
three things are distinguished in every one: first, the subject; next, the
end; and last, the means by which that end is introduced into the subject. In
household government the subject is the household; the end is holy and happy
domestic activity; the means are ruling and obeying as befits domestic
society. In civil polity the subject is the commonwealth; the end is holy and
happy civil activity; the means are ruling—

[1638 control | printed p. 2 | BSB canvas 10 | bsb10507786_00010]

—and obeying in the manner best fitted to complete civil happiness in every
respect. In ecclesiastical polity the subject is the congregation of the
Church; the end is holy and happy public activity in the communion of saints;
the means are ruling and obeying as Christ, the prince of the whole Church,
has both described and prescribed them for the supreme blessedness of the
congregations of saints.

There is nothing about the subject that needs to be said here. Concerning the
end, perhaps someone will ask why no mention has been made of the salvation of
souls, which appears to be the ultimate end of this polity. But this should
detain no one. That salvation is indeed the final and ultimate end of this
polity, but it seemed proper to omit it from the definition because it is not
peculiar to this polity; it is common both to Christian household government
and to civil polity, since both strive toward this, that every householder and
citizen may be blessed not only in this life but especially in the life to
come.

Perhaps a scruple will remain about the end peculiar to ecclesiastical
polity: only happy public activity in the Church is mentioned, while ethical,
domestic, and civil activity are passed over, even though all of them appear
to be directed, formed, corrected, and finally perfected by ecclesiastical
polity. This is true, but it does not appear to belong to ecclesiastical
polity first and in itself. Rather, it accrues to it from that eminent rank by
which it excels every practical discipline and exercises authority over them,
as will be taught more fully in its place.

As to the third point, the means of ecclesiastical polity is not only one,
namely ruling, but rather two—ruling and obeying. If we wished to be as
captious as Matthew Sutcliffe is when he gnaws at almost every definition
offered by our writers, we certainly would have something to charge against
Thomas Bilson because, when about to write a book on ecclesiastical polity, he
called it only “government” in his title. But far be it from us to be unfair
to him on that account. Most political writers customarily deliver precepts
only about government and say little about the manner of obedience, because
its precepts are ordinarily learned without much trouble from the precepts of
its correlative.

Matthew Sutcliffe, however, is wholly lacking in this fairness. After
spitting with black bile upon every definition supplied by our people, he
finally substituted a new one of his own, limping on this foot: “We say that
the discipline of the Church is an external order prescribed partly in the
word of God and partly by the Christian magistrate—or, if he is alien to
religion, by the chief ministers of the Church according to the rules of the
divine word—for the government of the whole Church and of each member insofar
as he belongs to the Church.” To let the other faults of this definition rest
for the present, it omits the second means of ecclesiastical polity now before
us, namely obedience. Even Hadrian Saravia could have warned him: when Saravia
wrote about civil polity, he was advised to prefix the title not only *On the
Authority of Commanding* but also—

[1638 control | printed p. 3 | BSB canvas 11 | bsb10507786_00011]

—*On Christian Obedience*. This, then, is the first controversy concerning
ecclesiastical polity debated among us Englishmen: its definition.

CHAPTER II.

Ecclesiastical Polity Is a Part of Christ’s Kingdom.

The second question concerns its excellence. This contains three heads: the
first asks about its dignity, the second contends for its utility, and the
third and last disputes about its necessity. This question belongs to dignity:
should ecclesiastical polity be regarded as part of Christ’s kingdom? “But
who,” you will ask, “doubts this?” Who doubts? Indeed, there are those who
receive it with mockery. Richard Bancroft, until recently Archbishop of
Canterbury, speaks thus: “I dare say you would smile if it happened that all
the consistorial ministers were said to be Christ’s substitutes in that
office in which he is King of the Church.” I am certainly compelled to say
that this very thing is done by them. But please, friends, restrain your
laughter until you have heard our position, which we state as follows.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, *Survey of the Pretended Holy Discipline*,
chapter 24, page 278.]

Christ’s prophetic office is that part of his mediation by which he teaches
the Church; his priesthood, that by which he redeems and reconciles her; his
kingdom, that part by which, effectually applying to the elect the results
(ἀποτελέσματα) of prophecy and priesthood, he spiritually governs his Church
to the destruction of every enemy and the perfect glory of his own.

It has two parts: spiritual administration in the world, and the last
judgment at the consummation of the world. Spiritual administration gathers
the Church in the world and preserves her for the last judgment. This in turn
has two parts: the inward working, which is the Spirit’s activity (ἐνέργεια),
acting inwardly through the outward means of polity (πολιτείας); and the
administration of outward ecclesiastical polity itself, by which outward
means are directed and blessed so as to effect the inward operation.

In this administration Christ alone is Lord, exercising absolute dominion in
the administration of divine things. The superior administrators in the
Church are those who, according to the rule prescribed by their Lord, are
stewards (οἰκονόμοι) of divine things, with the authority of a servant, not
the dominion of a lord. Reader, unless you are altogether godless (ἄθεος),
what will you say here deserves derisive laughter rather than reverence?

Bancroft himself must be asked under which of Christ’s offices he will place
the ministers of ecclesiastical polity. Will he call them vicars of Christ as
Prophet? By no means. To the words “Ministers are Christ’s vicars only insofar
as he is Prophet,” he replies, “Did anyone ever say that? Certainly, so far as
I remember, no one.” But if they depend neither upon Christ the King nor upon
Christ the Prophet, it must be feared that they belong to Christ in no way.
“Not at all,” he replies: “The common and received opinion of former times is
that Christ joined the Apostles—”

[Marginal references: Parker, *De polit.*, book 1, page 68; Bancroft, page
278.]

[1638 control | printed p. 4 | BSB canvas 12 | bsb10507786_00012]

—“and disciples to himself in the priestly office, to which preaching and
teaching belong. Hence it has always been argued against the Papists that
every minister is as much Christ’s vicar as the Pope himself is when he
preaches, administers the sacraments, and prays for the people; he must be
received as Christ’s ambassador, as if Christ himself, present on earth,
performed all these things.”

Thomas Bilson’s opinion is unlike this in its own form, though its substance
is similar: “Christ,” he says, “who is the fullness and head of the Church,
governs and administers her as Prophet, Priest, and King. In his image all
public government in the Church is prophetic, priestly, and royal: Doctors
hold the prophetic, Pastors the priestly, and Magistrates the royal.”

[Marginal reference: Bilson, *On the Perpetual Government of Christ’s
Church*, chapter 10, page 198.]

I say that Bilson’s thesis contains first an open contradiction and then an
inextricable absurdity. Bancroft says it is unheard of that ministers of the
Church are vicars of Christ the Prophet, yet Bilson subjects them to Christ as
Prophet insofar as they teach. In doing this he thinks that only the remaining
functions Bancroft names—the administration of the sacraments and the
recitation of public prayers—depend upon Christ’s priesthood.

Hence arises an inextricable difficulty: under which office of Jesus Christ
is ecclesiastical jurisdiction to be placed? It is found in ordination and in
excommunication, upon which turns the controversy debated among us about
episcopal presidency (προστασίᾳ). Would you not say these men had labored
beautifully if you saw that jurisdiction for which they fight so fiercely
completely shut out, the door barred, and wholly expelled? So far as I can
discern, this Tamar whom they so ardently love lives in exile, if not scorned
then at least neglected by her own lovers. Bancroft did not dare to say that
jurisdiction depends upon Christ’s priesthood; certainly he did not list it
among the ministerial functions that depend upon that priesthood. Nor do I
see how Bilson can transfer it wholly from ministers to the magistrate.

C. Scultingius writes: “In the *Commonplaces*, chapter 10, on the duties of
ministers, Musculus neglects the office of Elders; but in the chapter on
magistrates he refutes it at greater length. Gualther of Zurich attacks it
sharply in his commentary on 1 Corinthians 5. The Anglo-Calvinists, however,
maintain that the civil magistrate succeeded in place of these Elders.”
Gualther’s error is plain: he removed the Elders in such a way that he removed
excommunication and ecclesiastical jurisdiction along with them. It is
therefore a wonder that the Hierarchists glory so much in Gualther, who
transferred to the magistrate the episcopal jurisdiction by which they differ
from other ministers and in which they glory so much. Hadrian Saravia saw
this clearly enough: “There are those who think that all discipline of morals
should be committed to the magistrate, and who confine the ministry of the
Gospel to the bare preaching of God’s word and administration of the
sacraments. Since this can be taught neither by God’s word nor by any example
of the ancients, it is astonishing that such an opinion could enter the minds
of theologians.”

[Marginal references: Scultingius, *Hierarchica Anacrisis*, book 10; Saravia,
tract 7, *Theology in the epistle to the reader*.]

[1638 control | printed p. 5 | BSB canvas 13 | bsb10507786_00013]

Indeed, Bancroft himself dislikes this very thing: “There are,” he says, “in
the lands across the sea, as it seems, some who affirm that the Church’s power
is only spiritual and does not include any outward exercise of jurisdiction,”
by which Lambert Danaeus is justly disturbed. But what if this happens on our
side of the sea? What if it is done by the Hierarchists themselves—indeed, by
Bancroft himself? Scultingius says the Anglo-Calvinists maintain that the
magistrate succeeded in place of the Elders. He means Whitgift, who by this
very expression transferred to the Magistrate the ecclesiastical jurisdiction
exercised by these Elders in the primitive Church. One cannot say that the
Magistrate succeeded in place of the Elders unless he also succeeded to the
Elders’ power.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 26, page 299.]

But suppose Whitgift spoke conditionally—“if there were any Elders in the
ancient Church”—although Scultingius does not interpret him that way, nor
should he, as will be demonstrated in its place. By what path can Bilson avoid
the rock of Gualther and Erastus? I do not see. He says there is no place for
Elders because Doctors hold Christ’s prophetic office, Pastors his priestly
office, and Magistrates his royal office. Why not add from Gualther and
Erastus: therefore no place at all remains for episcopal ecclesiastical
jurisdiction? It is not under Christ’s prophecy, because it does not teach in
the manner in which the Church’s Doctors teach; it is not under Christ’s
priesthood, because today that does nothing but intercede. Therefore it either
belongs under Christ’s kingdom or suffers banishment.

Bancroft sticks in the same mud: “The power of the Church is not only
spiritual,” he says, “but includes an outward exercise of jurisdiction”; and
therefore, because that kingdom is spiritual, this exercise does not belong
under Christ’s kingdom. As though Christ’s priesthood were not just as
spiritual as his kingdom! Thus, if the exercise of jurisdiction is so outward
that it is not spiritual, it will belong neither under Christ’s priesthood nor
anywhere at all. However that may be, while the Hierarchists assign wholly to
the Magistrate Christ’s royal authority, upon which all ecclesiastical
jurisdiction depends, they take that jurisdiction away from the Bishops
themselves just as much as from the other ministers.

Lous follows, explaining his opinion thus: “Christ, by the breath of his
Spirit, so instructs those in error that he entrusts the outward function of
teaching to others; he so feeds that he wills others to labor in feeding; and
he so governs that he commands others to be appointed governors of
ecclesiastical affairs.” Since he brings this forward to support archiepiscopal
authority over ministers, he appears to distinguish the office of Doctors and
Pastors from episcopal government: ministers in teaching and feeding are
subject to Christ as Prophet and Priest, whereas Bishops in the government of
the Church depend upon him as King and Governor. If so, he contradicts both
Bancroft and Bilson. They assert that Christ rules because as Prophet he
teaches and as Priest feeds, thereby uttering something never heard before.
From whom does government come if not from a king? “But ecclesiastical
ruling,” he says, “is nothing but teaching and feeding.” I reply first that in
the teaching and feeding of the Church two things—

[Marginal reference: Lous, *Complaint of the Church*, page 148.]

[1638 control | printed p. 6 | BSB canvas 14 | bsb10507786_00014]

—must be considered: the word itself that teaches and feeds, and the
administration of that word, which introduces not only teaching but ruling.
From the word considered simply in itself flows only the act of teaching,
which proceeds from Christ as Prophet; but from the administration of the
word there also arises the act of ruling, and this comes from Christ the King.

Second, I say that ecclesiastical jurisdiction, which is occupied with the
censure of morals, is something different from the manner of teaching and
feeding proper to the Doctor and Pastor. Do not our adversaries teach the same
thing? On this basis they argue that Acts 20:28 does not mean presbyters who
only govern, because presbyterial government does not feed the Church in the
same manner as preaching presbyters feed her. They must say this if they wish
to preserve episcopal government, since, even by their concession, in the
office of teaching and feeding one minister does not differ from another.

I shall add more. The authority of their government over other ministers must
be borrowed not only from Christ the King but also as a government of a
kingdom, since the stewardship and servitude of a household manager does not
extend its borders so far. The same reasoning applies to the lawful presidency
(προστασίας) among ministers and to that which Peter is said to possess among
the other Apostles. To transform this into an unlawful primacy, Sixtus of
Siena rejected the superiority of apostolate and episcopate, in which Jerome
teaches that the Apostles were equal, and invented a third superiority, which
he calls that of a Kingdom. By this he says Jerome affirms that Peter was
chosen above the other Apostles and made their head. Thus Sixtus must either
invent a government of a kingdom from Christ the King, or episcopal government
over equal ministers falls, or at least archiepiscopal government over
Bishops, whom even they confess to be equal.

[Marginal reference: Sixtus of Siena, *Bibliotheca*, book 6, annotations 269,
271.]

CHAPTER III.

In Which the Adversaries’ Position Is Examined, and First Their Disguise Is
Stripped Away.

Thus far we have spoken about both the contradiction and the absurdity of our
Hierarchists in their explanation of their own position. Now that very
position must be examined. First their disguise must be exposed and stripped
away, the disguise by which they customarily mask their cause and burden ours.
Second, their hidden motives must be laid bare, by which they were led to
embrace it. Third, the sophistry by which they defend it must be refuted.

As to the first: Bancroft adorns his dependence of ecclesiastical polity upon
Christ as Priest by saying that it is especially hostile to the Papists,
because it teaches that all ministers—

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, *Survey*, chapter 24, page 277.]

[1638 control | printed p. 7 | BSB canvas 15 | bsb10507786_00015]

—are as much vicars of Christ as the Pope himself. First, if this follows, why
will our conclusion not equally follow: all ministers are as much Christ’s
vicars as the Lord Archbishop or Bishop himself? Second, the Hierarchists do
not dissent from the Papists in this dependence without giving them the
greatest satisfaction. “They defend the Pope’s primacy from Christ the King,”
you will say. But do they not also, and chiefly, defend it from Christ the
Priest? Read Harding: “As to Christ’s kingdom, it was contained in his
priesthood; and therefore when we speak of Christ’s kingdom, we say that it
was even on the cross: God reigned from the tree.” In the same sense, what
Moses calls a priestly kingdom Peter, with the words reversed, calls a royal
priesthood.

[Marginal reference: Harding, *Apology*, part 6, chapter 11.]

So far is Bancroft from dissenting from the Papists that he rolls exactly the
same stone with them. Both contend that the government of the Church is drawn
from Christ the Priest, so that it may all be derived only to priests, with
the Elders excluded. Both also detract from Christ’s kingdom, though in
different ways: the Papists subordinate it to the priesthood, while the
Hierarchists banish it entirely from the outward government of the Church.

This is the disguise by which they adorn their own cause. Now follows that by
which they defame ours. First they invert the state of the question and invent
masks with which they may safely fight. Bilson especially does this: “By no
means ought we to esteem those consistories—I know not what they are—as half
of Christ’s most just and glorious kingdom.” But who, I ask, does so? I know
no one who has called presbyterial discipline half of Christ’s kingdom. It is
enough for us to affirm that it is some part of that kingdom, for we understand
well that the inward part of Christ’s kingdom is too great and august to allow
this outward part to be called half of it.

[Marginal reference: Bilson, chapter 3, page 50.]

“Come now,” you say, “you nevertheless proclaim that it is great, which cannot
be said without rash and inconsiderate fervor.” But take care, Bilson, not to
turn our fervor into your own madness if you think so meanly even of the
lowest part of the kingdom that you do not consider it great. In Isaiah,
Christ’s kingdom is magnified as great even because all the furniture of the
house is entrusted to it, down to the smallest vessels, chapter 22, verse 24.

Second, Bancroft, Bilson, and Downam try to make our cause hateful, if not
ridiculous, because we adorn our presbyteries with the heavenly insignia of a
tribunal, throne, and scepter; call ecclesiastical polity the Kingdom of God,
the Scepter of Christ, and his Tribunal; and call the execution of the
presbytery the restoration of Christ’s kingdom. We have no reason to be
ashamed of these things, nor do they occur so rarely in Scripture that a
sharp-eyed theologian should regard them as monstrous: “As a glorious throne
exalted from the beginning, so is the house of our sanctuary,” Jeremiah
17:12. “In those days Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the Lord,”
Jeremiah 3:17. “The Lamb who is in the midst of the throne feeds them,”
Revelation 7:17. “The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of justice; your
throne endures forever,” Psalm 45:6. “He shall send the scepter of your power
out of Zion,” Psalm 110:2. “He shall strike the earth with the scepter of his
mouth,” Isaiah 11:4.

[Marginal references: Bancroft, chapter 33, pages 423, 428; Bilson, chapter 3,
page 48; Downam, book 1, page 129.]

[1638 control | printed p. 8 | BSB canvas 16 | bsb10507786_00016]

“But what do these have to do with presbyteries?” they will say. Everything.
Since Christ reigns in the visible Church as upon a throne and wields his
scepter through the administration of the word, there is no reason why
presbyterial discipline should be deprived of every share in this honor. It
is a sanction of the word, a part of Christ’s outward kingdom, and exercised
through the Church’s administrators in the very name of Christ the King.
Indeed, a key is equivalent to a throne and scepter, since the kingdom of
Jesus Christ himself is represented by a key, Isaiah 22:22–24, and
presbyterial discipline is expressly honored by Christ himself with the name
“key,” Matthew 16:19.

Since this is so, how unfair are these words of Richard Bancroft? “I remember
Beza’s saying that every presbytery is the tribunal of Christ himself. That is
almost the same as the flattery of certain papal parasites that the Pope and
Christ have one and the same consistory.” First, I do not see what this aims
at except the discharge of bile, since no one is ignorant of the distance
between Beza’s meaning and that of the canonists. Second, Beza does not even
name a “tribunal,” which Bancroft wished to make hateful here, but a
“consistory.” The words therefore touch an episcopal consistory more nearly
than the tribunal of a reformed presbytery. Third, the Pope’s consistory is
Satan’s throne, Revelation 2:13; the reformed Presbytery is Christ’s throne,
Revelation 4:6. Bancroft was not ashamed to mix them together.

[Marginal references: Beza, *On the Presbytery*, page 124; Bancroft, chapter
24, page 280.]

He cannot escape the words “tribunal” and “throne” in this passage of
Revelation by any device, since the visible Church is described in that
chapter and her visible administration under Christ the King is portrayed.
The farther Bancroft flees from it, the nearer he comes to Satan’s own throne,
that is, to a papal consistory whose image I wish did not survive anywhere in
England today. So much for the Hierarchists’ disguise, by which they strive to
adorn their cause and dishonor ours.

CHAPTER IV.

The Adversaries’ Hidden Motives Are Laid Bare.

I now come to those hidden motives which led them to think that this dependence
of polity upon Christ as Priest should chiefly be embraced. The reader may
wonder why the Hierarchists so anxiously flee from the dependence of
ecclesiastical polity upon Christ’s kingdom as from a rock, especially—

[1638 control | printed p. 9 | BSB canvas 17 | bsb10507786_00017]

—when Richard Bancroft acknowledges that a very great prerogative is thereby
derived to every minister of Christ’s Church. He displays enough desire for
this prerogative that in rejecting it he seems to follow nothing but love of
truth. But, as they say, a snake lies underneath; something else is at issue.
He foresaw certain wounds that would befall his cause and required preventive
medicine (φυλακτικῇ).

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 24, page 280.]

First, I think he foresaw that if Polity depends upon Christ as King, a
definite form of it was commanded by Christ himself in the Gospel. It is in no
way probable either that Christ surrendered his authority in making laws to
the Church’s judgment or that he was negligent in this royal office. To cut
this conclusion off, Christ must lose his outward royal right in governing
the Church, and Thomas Cartwright must be declared guilty for asserting that
Christ as King prescribed in his word an exact form of discipline.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 24, page 249.]

I ask whether Bancroft will concede that Christ enacted at least some outward
laws for Polity. If some—as many admit, since otherwise the episcopal
authority over their fellow initiates and colleagues, their Helen, would not
rest on divine right—will he say that Christ did this not as King but as
Priest? Let him consider whether laws that are enacted can really be enacted
by Christ as Prophet or Priest rather than King.

Hadrian Saravia opposes him, citing Philo to prove that “it belongs to a
prince to make laws.” Matthew Sutcliffe opposes him: “It belongs to the king,
the supreme prince and governor, to make laws; the Church, then, is governed
in a far different way from a kingdom and commonwealth.” The whole doctrine
of political writers opposes him: the power of making laws is so peculiar to
a monarch that it is numbered among his royal rights. Why say more? Richard
Bancroft himself, as well as Downam, admits that the Genevan commonwealth is
democratic because the power to make laws belongs among the royal rights and
there resides with the people. I shall judge you out of your own mouth. In
saying this Bancroft convicts himself of despoiling Christ’s kingdom of one of
its royal rights, since he has stolen from Christ the power to make laws.

Homer customarily calls the ancient princes lawgivers. “Legislation,” says
Aristotle, “belongs to royal dignity.” Hence the princes of God’s people are
called lawgivers in Scripture itself, Genesis 49:10, a name never given to the
priests throughout the Law. Finally, if laws proceed from Christ as Priest,
why was Aaron, his type as priest, not the lawgiver of the Israelites, while
Moses—who was not a priest but only a civil prince—was? George Carleton proves
this fully against Bozius. When Bozius objected from Psalm 99:6, “Moses and
Aaron among his priests,” Carleton answers that *cohanim* is common to civil
magistrates and priests. Thus David’s sons are called *cohanim*—

[Marginal references: Saravia, *On the Pontiff*, 46, book 6, §1; Aristotle,
*Politics*, book 3, chapter 11; Carleton, *On Jurisdiction*, chapter 3, §21.]

[1638 control | printed p. 10 | BSB canvas 18 | bsb10507786_00018]

—in 2 Samuel 8:18, though they were not priests but civil magistrates, 1
Chronicles 18:17. But if Moses is called a priest here, it is only in this
respect: like a priest, he prayed for the people, as John Piscator noted in his
scholia.

Second, Bancroft foresaw that if ecclesiastical polity were administered
under Christ the King, our Elders and Deacons could fittingly be applied to it
even though they are not priests. Therefore, to exclude them and keep the sum
of affairs with the priests, so that priests alone—as holy Ambrose puts it—may
appear to be something, discipline will be administered not by Christ the
King but by Christ the Priest. Bancroft himself betrays this secret by a clear
enough indication when he argues that Elders have no place in Christ’s
ecclesiastical polity because they are neither under Christ the Prophet,
since they are not prophets, nor under Christ’s priesthood, since they are not
priests.

But, I say, by this argument he also cuts off the Magistrate’s ecclesiastical
government. For if the government of the Church comes from Christ’s priesthood,
and no one has a place in any part of it unless he is similarly a priest, and
Magistrates no more than Elders are priests, it certainly follows that
Magistrates who govern ecclesiastical affairs thrust their sickle into another’s
harvest no less than Elders do.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 24, page 280.]

Bancroft’s third secret is this: he wants the ministers of ecclesiastical
polity properly to be priests. Polity must therefore be under Christ the
Priest, not the King, because in Christ’s outward kingdom there is room only
for ministers and servants, not for priests properly so called. But our
Whitaker argues that in the New Testament the name “priest” is not given to
ministers of the Gospel. When the Scot Duraeus objected that the Fathers did
call them priests, Whitaker answered that this arose from custom and was an
improper way of speaking. Since the Apostles abstained from it, we should do
so too. Moreover, did not the Fathers long abstain from this name—even by
Bellarmine’s admission—while it was necessary to distinguish Gospel ministers
from the then-flourishing Jewish priests? Thus the custom of the Fathers does
nothing for Bancroft, since the same necessity to abolish the name now exists:
Gospel ministers must be distinguished from papist priests. These, then, are
the hidden motives for which the Hierarchists exclude ecclesiastical polity
from Christ’s kingdom.

[Marginal references: Whitaker, *Against Campian*, reason 9; *Against
Duraeus*, page [unclear]; Bellarmine, *On the Sacrament of Orders*, chapter
[unclear].]

CHAPTER V.

The Adversaries’ Sophistry Is Refuted: First, Three Arguments of Bancroft.

In the third place we must now consider the adversaries’ arguments. Perhaps
this can scarcely be done without nausea, but we must not allow the truth to
appear defeated by our silence.

Richard Bancroft first argues that Christ as King does not govern the Church
at all in outward matters but rules only the elect in inward matters. For,
according to our consistorial Pezelius, “Christ is head only of the elect, and
just as the Church is called Christ’s body, so it signifies only the elect.”
The answer is easy and ready: “body” and “head” are said in two ways—properly,
of a natural head and body, and figuratively, of a political head and body.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 24, page 276.]

What follows? Insofar as Christ bears the likeness of a natural head and the
Church of a natural body, Christ is King and Head only of the elect, because
only into them does he pour the Spirit’s vital power. Conversely, the elect
alone are Christ’s body because they alone are spiritually enlivened by
Christ as natural head. But insofar as he bears the likeness of a political
head, he outwardly governs everyone in the Church, not only the elect. Why
not? In this sense the Father appointed Christ head even of the nations,
Psalm 18:44, and of the angels, who are not of the redeemed Church, Colossians
2:10—indeed, head of every individual whether or not of the Church, 1
Corinthians 11:3.

Unless this distinction is observed, statements that occur in authors will
appear to conflict although they agree. For example, Whitaker says, “It does
not belong to Christ as head to rule the Church in outward things”; and again,
“Christ is head in no other way than by giving life to the Church.” Who would
not think Whitaker here supports the Hierarchists? Yet he does not. Christ
does not outwardly rule the Church *as head*: that is, he does not rule
outwardly in his own person. Christ is no other kind of head—namely, natural
head—than one who gives the Church life. Elsewhere the same writer shows that
Christ, as political head and King, rules the Church in outward things:
“Christ is always so truly present in his kingdom that no king can be more
truly present in his. He does precisely what kings customarily do, cares for
every part of his kingdom, and diligently performs whatever is required of a
present king. What do kings do that Christ does not? They defend their
subjects and govern them by laws. Does Christ not do the same?”

[Marginal references: Whitaker, *Against* [Bellarmine], book 4, question 1,
chapter 3, §§8–9; ibid., chapter 2, §1.]

Bancroft’s second argument is this: “By making the outward government of the
Church a part of Christ’s kingdom—”

[1638 control | printed p. 12 | BSB canvas 20 | bsb10507786_00020]

—“you introduce a Genevan papacy and royalty into the churches; indeed, you
make every presbyterial Elder of yours a petty king.” We say this follows by
no connection. “Does Christ not make kings of all those over whom he presides
as King?” he asks. “I demand a reason why Christ’s kingdom would not make
kings just as his prophecy makes prophets and his priesthood makes priests.”

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 24, pages 279–280.]

I answer that Christ’s prophecy does not make worldly and political prophets,
nor his priesthood worldly priests and every kind of political priest. Yet
you assume our position would make Christ’s kingdom produce worldly political
kings when you argue that Church polity does not depend upon the kingdom
because political and worldly petty kings would then arise as a stumbling
block to the magistrates’ authority.

“But remove that obstacle about worldliness,” he says; “how then will you
answer?” Another obstacle, concerning political office, remains. I say that
Christ’s prophecy makes only ethical prophets and his priesthood only ethical
priests. In the same way Christ’s inward kingdom makes every believer a king,
but only ethically, not politically. Christ’s prophecy is communicated to
every believer, yet not all should exercise the public office of prophesying
in Church polity. Likewise his priesthood flows into every believer, yet not
all should put their hands to the public priesthood in the Church.

“But Christ’s prophecy produces some public and political prophets, and his
priesthood some such priests. Why would the kingdom not also produce petty
kings of this kind—those called to govern the Church?” I deny that prophecy
makes any public prophets or priesthood any political priests. Those who
discharge what you call the public office of prophets and priests in the
Church depend entirely upon Christ’s outward kingdom, by which he is author of
Polity, not upon prophecy or priesthood.

“Grant this,” you say. “Since Christ’s inward kingdom makes ethical kings, why
will his outward and political kingdom not likewise supply outward and
political kings?” Because the nature of that outward kingdom opposes it. This
principality approaches the nature of monarchy, whose force cannot be shared.
It ceases to be a monarchy unless the monarch alone is King: private subjects
are left only to obey, while public administrators of his kingdom serve in
the manner of servants, without any title of kings.

Lous himself observes this. He concedes to Theodore Beza that no one in the
Church should be called king except Christ alone and adds: “If he called
himself king, he would claim the supreme dignity; if he called himself a
secondary king, by that very fact he would confess he was no king.” Whoever
the poet was, he rightly said: “It is enough to be a servant; now I refuse to
be a vicar, for one who is king especially has no king.” Thus Bancroft’s
reasoning is so far from harming our cause—

[Marginal reference: Lous, *Complaint of the Church*, page 247.]

[1638 control | printed p. 13 | BSB canvas 21 | bsb10507786_00021]

—that it especially confirms it. You detest petty kings in ecclesiastical
polity. Rightly so: Lous says that Christ’s monarchical royalty in the outward
Church is wholly incommunicable. Where, then, does your wretched monarchical
episcopal jurisdiction stand? By what right do Prelates dominate the churches
like petty kings, as you say? Finally, with what face does this author contend
against Beza that the name “Chief Shepherd” (ἀρχιποίμενος) can be shared with
Archbishops? Monarchical power is incommunicable, but monarchical names
communicable? Anyone who does not see that “Chief Shepherd” signifies Christ’s
monarchical authority should, I suggest, sail for Anticyra.

Bancroft’s third argument comes from our Lord’s speech to Pilate: “My kingdom
is not of this world.” “Since you press this against the Bishops,” he says,
“why will it not equally hold against those consistorial petty kings of ours?”

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, chapter 24, page [unclear].]

As to the comparison of Bishops and Elders, blind is the man who cannot see
that Elders govern only spiritually, whereas we call episcopal power worldly,
and rightly: it was invented by the world and human beings, savors of the
world’s pomp, and resembles worldly monarchy. For these reasons alone Christ,
when Pilate raised the subject of worldly Roman rule, denied that his kingdom
was of this world.

Thus Bancroft’s argument has no substance: that Christ’s kingdom cannot extend
to the outward government of the Church because then, being outward, it would
be worldly. My lord, I ask: will you call Christ’s priesthood, upon which you
say the outward government of the Church depends, worldly because it is
outward? Will you also concede that your episcopal government is worldly,
since it is outward? I do not see what he can answer except perhaps this: the
episcopal office may be called worldly while Christ’s kingdom is not worldly,
because that office depends upon his priesthood, not his kingdom. The matter
is thus safe, and the Lord Bishop sails into harbor: he keeps his government
worldly, deriving it not from Christ’s kingdom, which is not worldly, but from
Christ’s priesthood, which he thinks can be called worldly because it is
outward.

The first error of this argument is that it confuses outward and worldly as
though they were one. The second lies in supposing that Christ himself would
be an outward King of the Church if, as King, he rules the Church in outward
things. I deny that this follows. If it did, then from the adversaries’
priestly dependence it would equally follow that Christ himself is an outward
Priest of the Church, because, according to them, as Priest he governs the
Church outwardly. In both kingdom and priesthood one must distinguish Christ
himself from his administrators. Priests under the Old Testament and Pastors
under the New are not called priests or pastors in the same way that he is
Priest and Pastor, as Whitaker rightly says. Because the Romanists—

[1638 control | printed p. 14 | BSB canvas 22 | bsb10507786_00022]

—do not understand this, they wish the Pope to be called monarch in the same
way Christ is Monarch; and because Christ is now absent in person, they demand
a visible monarch in the Pope. Answering them, Whitaker denies that outward
government belongs to Christ, but affirms spiritual and invisible government.
In explanation he denies that Christ was a visible monarch with respect to
outward government while on earth or would now be one if he were on earth:
“He would not manage these outward things: he would not create Bishops, confer
orders, convene synods, or do the other things of that kind.”

[Marginal reference: Whitaker, *Against* [Bellarmine], book 4, question 1,
chapter 2 §3; chapter 3 §8.]

Whitaker might appear to contradict us when he denies that Christ is monarch
with respect to outward government. But he likewise denies that Christ is
Bishop and Pastor with respect to outward government in the way Bishops and
Pastors are. Whatever the dependence of Polity upon Christ, it does not follow
that Christ in his own person is an outward King or Pastor, although he
governs the Church outwardly through Pastors. We must retain the distinction:
Christ and ministers are called rulers in different ways; ministers are
outward rulers, but Christ is not.

CHAPTER VI.

Two Arguments of Bilson Are Answered.

The fourth argument is Thomas Bilson’s. After premising that the Church’s
government is twofold, spiritual and outward, he infers that outward Church
government does not belong to Christ’s kingdom because that kingdom is
heavenly and spiritual. Thus, as for Bancroft every outward government of the
Church was worldly, for Bilson it is now neither heavenly nor spiritual. In
Whitgift and Bancroft, Christ’s spiritual kingdom and the Church’s outward
government are everywhere opposed.

Yet on the preceding page Bilson himself confessed that the Church’s outward
aspect is called heavenly: “The outward form of the Church, in which the good
and wicked are gathered and mingled through word and Sacraments, is sometimes
called the kingdom of heaven.” Very well. If the visible Church, though
outward, is heavenly and belongs to Christ’s heavenly kingdom, why will her
Polity, though outward, not also be heavenly and belong to it? Was not John’s
ministry outward, yet from heaven and therefore heavenly, Matthew 21:25? Is
not the visible Church governed by her rulers called “heaven” throughout
Revelation, and particularly in 4:2?

[Marginal references: Bilson, chapter 3, pages 47–48.]

[1638 control | printed p. 15 | BSB canvas 23 | bsb10507786_00023]

We never hear “word of the priesthood” or “gospel of the priesthood,” but
“word of the kingdom” and “gospel of the kingdom,” Matthew 4:23; Mark 1:14;
Matthew 13:19. Nor is the outward preaching and ordered administration of the
Gospel ever called the heavenly priesthood, but the kingdom of heaven and the
heavenly kingdom, Matthew 3:2; 11:12; 13:24, and so forth. Finally, the
administrators of ecclesiastical polity are never called ministers of the
priesthood but ministers of the kingdom—indeed, ministers of Christ in the
kingdom of heaven, Matthew 11:11.

Just as the Church’s outward government is heavenly and belongs to Christ’s
heavenly kingdom, so it is spiritual and belongs to his spiritual kingdom.
With respect to its efficient cause: its administration proceeds from the
Spirit and is perfected by the Spirit’s gifts, 1 Corinthians 12:1; therefore
it is spiritual. Its end: it is given for spiritual edification, Ephesians
4:12; therefore spiritual. Its matter: the word and Sacraments that it
administers are spiritual, 1 Corinthians 10:3–4; therefore spiritual. Its
manner of acting: it acts through the demonstration of the Spirit, 1
Corinthians 2:4, 13; therefore spiritual. Its object: it regards human spirits
and souls, Hebrews 13:17; therefore spiritual. Its means: it works through
spiritual weapons, 2 Corinthians 10:4; therefore spiritual. Its effect: it is
the ministry of the Spirit, 2 Corinthians 3:6; therefore it is spiritual.

Bilson’s fifth argument is that, even if our presbyteries were conceded to be
instituted by Christ, their tribunals nevertheless cannot belong to Christ’s
kingdom because it is proper to his majesty. I would scarcely have touched
the force of this argument had I not remembered John Whitgift’s statement that
the government of the Church is twofold, spiritual and outward: in the former
Christ alone is Pastor, but in the latter he has subordinate Pastors under
him. From this comparison it is easy to understand that, in Bilson’s judgment,
Christ’s kingdom is so proper to his majesty that it has no substitutes.

This perhaps receives light from William Whitaker: “In the priesthood Christ
left many vicars, not one; in the kingdom he left no vicar. If we wish to
substitute any vicars for Christ in the kingdom, they should rather be kings
and princes who defend the Church with laws and arms than the Pope who
destroys her.” I will not now dispute in what sense or how truly this is said.
For the present it is enough for my purpose to call those whom Whitgift calls
substitutes and Whitaker vicars “servants and administrators.” I therefore say
that although Christ’s kingdom is proper to him, it nevertheless has servants
and administrators under it through whom he administers the kingdom outwardly.
If Bilson does not concede this, he will exclude the Magistrate himself, whom
elsewhere he affirms to hold Christ’s royal authority. Will Christ’s kingdom
have servants under it in the commonwealth through whom it rules outwardly,
but none in the Church? In my judgment, it will have them much more in the
Church: Christ’s royal efficacy is more present to the Church’s ad—

[Marginal references: Whitgift, *Tract*, chapter 1, division [unclear];
Whitaker, *Against* [Bellarmine], book 4, chapter 2, §6.]

[1638 control | printed p. 16 | BSB canvas 28 | bsb10507786_00028]

—ministrators than to the princes of this age, and his inward kingdom is more
closely joined to his servants’ ministry in the Church than in the
commonwealth. Since this is so, Bilson should have heard Christ’s saying,
“What God has joined together, let no man separate,” Matthew 19:6, rather than
write: “The rash and inconsiderate fervor of some in our age causes us to
linger in separating the Church’s outward discipline until we have carefully
and exactly severed it from that inward and true kingdom of Christ.”

[Marginal reference: Bilson, chapter 3, page 48.]

I understand: you institute this separation so that the Church’s inward
government belongs to Christ’s kingdom, but the outward belongs only to his
priesthood and not his kingdom. If I am not mistaken, the separation made by
Stapleton and other Papists is somewhat akin to this: “A head has two offices.
The first is inward, to be the principle of vital influence, and thus Christ
is head. The other is outward, to direct the outward functions of the body by
his command, and thus the Pope is head.”

[Marginal reference: Stapleton, *Doctrinal Principles*, book 6, chapter 16.]

John Reynolds answers: “First, we must not forge two heads from the two
offices of one head.” Bilson must be answered similarly: from the two offices,
inward and outward, of the one head Christ, we may not form two heads of
government—a kingdom that enlivens inwardly and a priesthood that directs
outwardly. “Second,” Reynolds says, “to govern the body’s outward actions is
the principal office of the head and belongs only to the one who supplies the
body’s inward influence.” Bilson must again be answered similarly: to govern
the Church outwardly is the principal work of that office in Christ by which
he is the Church’s Head and King. Outward direction flows from the same source
as inward enlivening; indeed, by one and the same act and office the Church is
both directed outwardly and enlivened inwardly.

“Third,” Reynolds says, “by separating inward influence and outward government
you greatly detract from God’s house, because you thereby appoint Pastors and
Doctors only to direct the body’s outward functions, although they were given
by Christ for the full perfecting of the saints,” Ephesians 4:12. The same
answer applies exactly to Bilson. By separating Pastors’ outward government
from Christ’s inward and, as he calls it, spiritual government, he leaves them
only an outward administration over bodies. Yet their ministry penetrates
into the inmost marrow of spirits while Christ’s royal power works with it.
Otherwise Christ would not have acknowledged them as his fellow workers, as
Bilson himself admits, 1 Corinthians 3:9.

[Marginal reference: Reynolds, *Conference with Hart*, chapter 1, division
2.]

These, so far as I can perceive, are all the adversaries’ arguments. Good God,
how frivolous they are! Certainly weaker than ours, which must now be added as
briefly as possible.

CHAPTER VII.

Our Arguments Are Proposed.

It is fitting that ecclesiastical polity be founded upon that office of Jesus
Christ which has authority to send, power to bless, efficacy to work, might
to supply strength, and finally glory to reward. This is neither Christ’s
prophetic office, which only reveals, nor his priesthood, which now only
intercedes, but his royal office, by which all the things named are performed
both most powerfully and most gloriously.

I. My first argument is from the promise to constitute polity, Matthew 16:19:
“I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven.”

First, ecclesiastical jurisdiction is expressly called here not the key of
the priesthood but the key of the kingdom.

Second, Christ does not allude to some priestly key—what key, I ask, would
that be?—but to the key of a head of household and prefect of a city, which is
customarily entrusted to the steward and gatekeeper.

Third, elsewhere it is called not the key of Aaron the priest but the key of
David the king, Revelation 3:7.

Fourth, Eliakim, who bore this key as a type, was not a priest but a prince,
Isaiah 22:22.

Fifth, by this key the administrators of polity judge with great weight, as
Tertullian speaks, and Gregory the Great agrees: “The Doctors of the Church
are called the heavens because the Lord presides in them when he exercises
his judgments through them. He judges through those to whom he has given the
keys of the kingdom of heaven.” Judgment is so proper to a king and prince
that the princes of the Jewish people were consequently also called Judges.

[Marginal reference: Gregory the Great, *On the Penitential Psalms*.]

II. My second argument is from the general institution of ecclesiastical
polity, Matthew 28:19: “Go and preach,” and so forth. Does Christ here allege
his priesthood so that polity should be considered founded upon it? Not at
all. He brings forward his royal authority as the foundation, prefacing the
command, “All authority in heaven and earth has been given to me,” and
afterward concluding with his royal efficacy, “I shall be with you until the
consummation of the age.”

III. My third argument is from the particular narrative of constituted polity,
Ephesians 4:11: “He gave some Apostles, some Prophets, some Evangelists, some
Pastors and Doctors.” The Apostle relates that Christ did this when he ascended
on high, leading captivity captive and giving gifts to men, verse 8. There is
no room for evasion. Polity was constituted not when Christ’s priesthood
prevailed over his royal office, but when the priesthood was subjected to the
royal office. For Christ ascended primarily to exercise a new kingdom, and
only secondarily for the priesthood, insofar as its one remaining part—

[1638 control | printed p. 18 | BSB canvas 30 | bsb10507786_00030]

—is intercession, which has nothing to do with establishing polity, and is
exercised in and adorned by the glory of his royal office.

IV. My fourth argument is from the particular and precise institution of
polity, Acts 1:3: “He was seen by them for forty days and spoke to them about
the things pertaining to the Kingdom of God.” Why should I not say that,
among other things, Christ then gave precepts about the whole polity of the
Church and prescribed its exact form, which he also calls his kingdom?

“But by this interpretation of yours,” says Bancroft, “you agree with the
Jesuits, who interpret this passage of their Antichristian kingdom, just as
you do of your Genevan royalty.” Our Genevan royalty is nothing. Your
episcopal royalty is all the more hypocritical because, although it approaches
the Antichristian more closely than it ought, it nevertheless evades Christ’s
royal office so that it may hide under his priesthood from every suspicion of
royalty.

[Marginal reference: Bancroft, *Survey of Discipline*, chapter 24, page 279.]

Against the Jesuits I oppose Augustine: “Since the Lord himself kept silent
about these things, which of us may say, ‘They are this or that’? Who is so
vain and rash as to affirm without any divine testimony that they are what the
Lord then wished to say?” As these words slay the Jesuits, so they refute
Downam. Augustine says it is vain and rash to think that Christ here intended
a certain Pope’s primacy, or the superiority of Bishops, since no polity of
that sort can be proved from any other place of Scripture. Is it not equally
vain and rash for George Downam to argue that the Apostles intended
metropolitans although they did not say so, when nowhere in their writings
does it appear that they intended it? But to interpret these words of Christ
of Genevan polity, since that polity is afterward narrated in the rest of
Acts, is neither vain nor rash in Augustine’s judgment but, I add, altogether
necessary and fitting.

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *Tractates on John*, tractate 96.]

The adversaries object that even we do not know the place and time at which
our presbyterial Elders were first instituted by Christ. I answer that Saravia
likewise finds it uncertain where and when the presbyters who labor in the
word were first instituted: “They are first mentioned in Acts 11:30; but it is
fitting that they were instituted earlier, though I do not know when.” Luke
gives you the time: those forty days. Then Christ prescribed teaching
presbyters, governing Elders, and ministering Deacons when he spoke about the
whole constitution and polity of his kingdom. The Apostles would not have
ordained Presbyters, Elders, or Deacons from their own brains unless Christ
himself had specifically named them as administrators of his kingdom at this
time. We are therefore led by clear reason to say that Christ the Lord here
provided for his polity; otherwise the chief administrators of the kingdom,
namely preaching pres—

[1638 control | printed p. 19 | BSB canvas 31 | bsb10507786_00031]

—byters, will labor under an unknown origin. No reason can be given why Luke
omitted the history of their first ordination except that he thought it enough
to have first described their institution in these words at the beginning of
the book. So G. Whitaker notes that Luke said nothing in Acts 15 about the
Apostles’ convocation of the Church because he thought it enough to have said
once at the beginning that the Apostles customarily called the Church
together, Acts 6:2.

[Marginal reference: Whitaker, *On Councils*, [book/chapter unclear].]

V. My fifth argument is from the preservation of ecclesiastical polity, once
instituted, until the last judgment. The formula of prayer “Your kingdom
come,” Luke 11:2, shows this to be the preservation of Christ’s kingdom. One
cannot say how loudly the Hierarchists cry out because we contend that in this
petition, among other things, we must pray for ecclesiastical discipline.
Hence R. Bancroft mocks a certain Kingston, and George Downam scourges the
prefacer of the *Demonstration of Discipline*, because they judged that in
this clause of the Lord’s Prayer we ought, among the other parts of Christ’s
kingdom, to pray for the coming of ecclesiastical discipline.

[Marginal references: Downam, chapter 31, page 399; Bancroft, book 2, page
129.]

But John Bridges, Bishop of Oxford, acquits us by his own vote. Speaking of
the polity both of the English Church and of the reformed churches, he adds:
“May God grant that in our own state and that of our elder and neighboring
brethren, and in all other parts of the true Church of God—according as we all
say in the Lord’s Prayer, ‘Your kingdom come’—these blessings of God may
increase more and more each day.”

[Marginal reference: Bridges, *Defense of English Government*, book 1, page
87.]

VI. My sixth argument is from the cessation of ecclesiastical polity at the
last judgment. The words of 1 Corinthians 15:24, “Then will be the end, when
he shall have delivered the kingdom to God,” and so forth, show this to be the
cessation of Christ’s kingdom. Francis Turrianus ineptly interprets Luke
1:32–33—“God shall give him the throne of his father David, and he shall reign
over the house of Jacob forever, and of his kingdom there shall be no end”—of
the visible government of the Church, because the passage concerns the
kingdom of Christ that will have no end. In return, the adversaries ought to
grant me that 1 Corinthians 15:24 concerns the visible government of the
Church, because, contrariwise, it treats a kingdom that will finally end.

[Marginal reference: Turrianus, *Against Sadeel*, book 2, page 97.]

“Rather,” says Bilson, “all of this must be understood of Christ’s inward
kingdom against his enemies—sin, death, and hell—which is said to be eternal
in Luke but here in Paul not eternal: eternal as to possession and enjoyment,
joined with wonderful sweetness and happiness; not eternal as to
administration, because once death, sin, and hell have been conquered there
will be no use for that administration.” But this is exactly what we say: we
expect the possession and enjoyment you mention to be eternal, and understand
the cessation of Christ’s kingdom narrated by Paul only of its administration.

[Marginal reference: Bilson, chapter 3, page 47.]

[1638 control | printed p. 20 | BSB canvas 32 | bsb10507786_00032]

“But,” he says, “this administration is specifically against Christ’s
spiritual enemies. Paul therefore speaks only of the spiritual administration
belonging to Christ’s inward kingdom, not the outward government of the
Church.” Bilson always goes wrong on this string: he thinks the outward
government of the Church is not a spiritual administration of Christ’s
kingdom, especially though it destroys Christ’s spiritual enemies—sin, death,
hell, and the Devil. Paul expressly says the contrary in 2 Corinthians 10:4:
“The weapons of our warfare are not carnal but divinely powerful for pulling
down strongholds, overturning reasonings and every height raised against the
knowledge of God,” and so forth.

What then? By Bilson’s concession, Paul here speaks of that spiritual
administration of Christ’s kingdom by which he conquers spiritual enemies.
Therefore he speaks at least in part of outward ecclesiastical administration,
because Christ conquers his enemies not only by an inward administration
acting within, but also by an outward one, apart from which he ordinarily
exercises no inward administration. In this sense the Fathers must be
received: they do not exclude the administration of the outward kingdom
against enemies although they say nothing specifically about it. More recent
writers do speak of it. I shall name only Bartholomew Keckermann, one of the
latest:

“When it is said in 1 Corinthians 15:28, ‘After all things have been subjected
to him, then the Son himself shall be subject to him who subjected all things
to him, that God may be all in all,’ this must be understood with respect to
the outward government, not the inward or the divine majesty Christ possesses.
The Apostle, as the context shows, does not speak of the deprivation or laying
down of majesty or royal power. With respect to it Christ is God blessed
forever and the King of glory whose kingdom will have no end. He treats the
change of that outward and ministerial government which flourishes in the
Church militant, whom Christ governs mediately through his ministers in this
life. At the consummation of the age that ministerial government over which
he presided as Mediator will be removed, and he will deliver the kingdom to
the Father; that is, he will govern the Church immediately with the Father, so
that God may be all in all and there be no further need of outward means by
which God governs his people.”

[Marginal reference: Keckermann, *System of Theology*, book 3, chapter 6,
page 401.]

Thus Keckermann means only the outward administration here, not the inward,
because the latter will always endure, though without outward means. There is
certainly substance in what he says. One can easily see how badly Bilson errs
when he says only Christ’s inward kingdom will cease, although it will truly
remain—“as to enjoyment,” he says. I say it will remain not only as to
enjoyment but also as to inward administration itself. This is seen in love,
which always remains, 1 Corinthians 13:8, supplied to all the elect even in
the age to come by the efficacy of Christ’s inward kingdom. Yet it may be said
to cease in three ways. First, with respect to its object: it will not then
fight enemies, because there will be no enemies—

[1638 control | printed p. 21 | BSB canvas 33 | bsb10507786_00033]

—then. Second, with respect to its matter: it will not then administer certain
graces that it now administers, such as hope and patience. Third, with respect
to the form of administration, which will then be immediate, 1 Corinthians
13:8, when God will be all in all, whereas in this age he ordinarily works
only through outward means.

VII. My seventh argument is from the judgment of theologians. Although their
number is infinite, I shall be content with one, Martin Bucer. Writing to
Edward VI about ecclesiastical polity, he prefixed to his book the title *On
the Kingdom of Christ*. He certainly would not have done so unless he judged
ecclesiastical polity to be Christ’s kingdom. In the book itself he gives a
description of Christ’s kingdom that comprehends the outward government of
the Church:

“It is useful to add a definition of Christ’s kingdom, since so few know it.
It is the administration and procuring of the eternal salvation of God’s
elect, by which the only-begotten Son of God, sending them his Gospel through
ministers chosen by him for this purpose and breathing upon them by his
Spirit, gathers them from the world to himself. Having gathered them, he
incorporates them into his holy Church; and having incorporated them, he
himself rules and governs them, using for this purpose the dispensation of his
word and Sacraments through suitable ministers, and also the vigilant
administration of his discipline.”

[Marginal reference: Bucer, *On the Kingdom of Christ*, book 1, chapter 5.]

I come to the Hierarchists themselves. Matthew Sutcliffe argues that Christ
governs his Church as King, although the means of government differs from that
by which earthly kings rule their commonwealths. John Bridges allows

for Bishops: “Christ’s Monarchy can remain intact while someone possesses

Hadrian Saravia affirms that Christ as King prescribed in the Gospel an exact
form for governing the Church and commanded that it remain as it were
unchangeable until the end of the age.
