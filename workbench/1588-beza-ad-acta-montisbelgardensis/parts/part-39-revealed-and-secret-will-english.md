## Part XXXIX: Revealed and Secret Will

[Part II | PDF page 206 | logical source scan 205 | printed page 204 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

What, then? Sometimes “God’s will” designates law and gospel. In the law he reveals what he approves as well and rightly done and what he condemns as badly and perversely done, in the form of commands and prohibitions. This does not mean human beings can by nature will to obey his commands when heard or avoid what he prohibits, much less actually perform them. Rather, by understanding more expressly than from natural law what we owe both to God and neighbor, we may be held increasingly convicted. The gospel declares that counsel of God promised through the prophets and displayed at the proper time by Christ himself, by which alone he determined to save human beings—namely in Christ himself, apprehended through true faith. Both these wills have long been revealed and are daily revealed, yet not universally—that is, without exception to every people, much less every person—but promiscuously and indefinitely to these or those, as he pleases to appear to those seated in death’s shadow, as Isaiah speaks.

But when by “God’s will” we understand that part of God’s providence by which he brings all human affairs and every individual—with all secondary causes and their motions and effects subordinated to the execution of this will—to that final end, his own glory, as Solomon testifies, some by the way of mercy and others by the way of judgment, we customarily call it hidden and secret for two chief reasons. First, it proceeds from his mere good pleasure, unsearchable and adorable even to all angels. Second, it is recognized only *a posteriori*, as the schools speak—that is, from its effects. This pre—

—destination is not affected by secondary causes, chief among which is the human will, as the Stoics dreamed and those still imagine who make foreseen faith and its fruits the cause of the decree of election and, conversely, maintain that foreseen unbelief and impenitence cause the decree of reprobation. Rather, predestination moves and affects them in two different ways, yet never coerces the human will, because where there is coercion no will properly so called exists.

Accordingly, when those upon whom God wills to have mercy are in view, since no one can share this benefit except by believing God’s promises, while God finds the human will not merely indisposed to believe but entirely opposed to the gospel, in his singular goodness he prepares the mind to admit the gospel’s light, softens the stony heart, and finally renews the entire inner person. He forms in that person a new willing and acting in Christ, who is apprehended through faith likewise freely formed in him. For, as Augustine excellently says, “It is certain that we will when we will, but he causes us to will. It is certain that we act when we act, but he causes us to act,” by supplying the most efficacious powers to the will he made. God therefore does not find the elect but makes them, as Augustine says elsewhere.

As for the others, God finds them corrupt and, by nature depraved through that voluntary fall of the first parents, already inclined and fully disposed toward final destruction. Why would he coerce their will to do the evil toward which they move of their own accord? Why would he instill new malice in them, since he who is goodness itself cannot author any evil and native corruption alone provides the most just cause of everlasting destruction? Yet as he pleases, he not only governs and restrains that evil will but also moves and inclines it—both to harden itself and by employing it sometimes to exercise his people, sometimes to chastise them, and sometimes to set evil people against one another—always justly and wisely.

Since the one who is omnipotent and immutable can neither be deceived nor changed in either case, and since “necessary” is defined as what cannot be otherwise, it follows altogether that those destined to salvation are necessarily saved through mercy and those destined to destruction necessarily perish through their own fault. But this necessity of the outcome of divine predestination in both groups brings no force against either will. The will of the good, insofar as it is freed from sin’s slavery by God’s living grace, voluntarily tends toward good; and the will of the rest, remaining and growing in its viciousness, likewise voluntarily plunges toward evil. How, then, can the former complain about that most free necessity of doing good, or the latter about the necessity of sinning in which they were born slaves? The former, which we called the most free necessity, is true freedom and nothing—

—other than the beginning of God’s image restored in the elect. Thus we say God himself is necessarily good and cannot lie or die. Nothing is thereby detracted from his will; it is rather affirmed. Conversely, the necessity of sinning does not remove but demonstrates the voluntary motion of an evil will. For how could an evil will move itself otherwise than evilly?
