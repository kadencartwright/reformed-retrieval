### Chapter X

#### On the testimonies of Scripture produced by the Remonstrants to support
their thesis

The first is taken from John 3:16: “God so loved the world that he gave his
only-begotten Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have
eternal life.” Our men reply that in Scripture the name “world” does not always
denote all and each mortal, but sometimes those whose head is Christ (2 Cor.
5:16), and sometimes also those whose prince is Satan (John 12:31), for whom
Christ denies that he prays (John 17:9). The Remonstrants attack this
prescription too in many ways. 1. They deny that throughout all Scripture
“world” anywhere signifies only the elect. Even if this were true of all the
other passages where the word “world” is used, it would nevertheless not
overturn the very thing which our men intend, because many phrases occur in
Scripture which have plainly the same force as this one and cannot be referred
to any but the elect faithful: “all families” and “all nations” (Gen. 12:3;
18:18; 22:18; Acts 3:25; Gal. 3:8); “the borders” or “the ends of the earth”
(Ps. 2:8); “all the ends of the world” and “all the families of the nations”
(Ps. 22:27); “the habitable part of the world and the sons of men” (Prov.
8:31); “all nations” (Isa. 2:2; Hag. 2:7); “all flesh” (Joel 2:28; Acts 2:16;
Isa. 44:3); and even “all men” (John 12:32). But not only phrases of equivalent
force: the very name “world” is also used in Scripture to denote the faithful
or elect, so openly that a reader who studies the truth freely and candidly
cannot extend it further. For thus we read in John 6:33: “That bread of God is
he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.” But if anyone

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 165 | printed p. 155]

doubts whether the faithful or elect alone are those to whom he gives life,
let him consult John 10:27–28, where this is taught to be the peculiar
privilege of the sheep, whom the Remonstrants themselves have already quite
often acknowledged to be the faithful elect. Christ’s words in John 17:21, 23
also have the same meaning—even with Toledo himself, though a Jesuit and
cardinal, as interpreter—“that the world may believe,” “that the world may know
Christ”; unless someone should say that Christ’s prayers are ineffectual, or
could show another world truly believing and knowing Christ through faith
besides the world of the faithful. Entirely parallel to this passage is the
other one, Romans 11:12: “Their fall is the riches of the world”; and 1 Timothy
3:16: “Christ was believed on in the world”; and also Romans 4:11–13, where
“father of the faithful” and “heir of the world” plainly mean the same thing in
the promise made to Abraham. The proper reason for these expressions seems to
be explained in those words of the apostle in Hebrews 2:5: it was not to
angels, but to Christ, that he subjected “that world to come.” For into this
world, which is the kingdom of Christ as he distributes the grace and gifts of
the Spirit according to his will (v. 4), all and only the faithful have been
transferred (1 John 3:14). Not a few similar passages occur, but there is no
reason for me to depart from the footsteps of the participants in the
Colloquy on this matter. Our men press the apostle’s words in 2 Corinthians
5:19: “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not imputing their
sins to them.” Here those to whom God does not impute their sins are called
“the world,” that is, the elect, the faithful, and the truly blessed (Rom.
4:8). In order to weaken this confirmation the Remonstrants object to many
things, but they will vanish at the slightest touch. 1. From verses 14 and 15
they observe that this world contains “all those who died in sins”; and they
certainly rightly note that all and only those who are called dead in verse 14
are understood by “the world” in verse 19. But in my judgment it is not right
that they refer that death to the state common to all outside Christ and
remaining in

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 166 | printed p. 156]

sins. For the same apostle, treating the same argument in Romans 6, teaches
that this death is another kind: namely, the one which is *to sin*, not *in
sin* (vv. 2, 11), which occurs through coalescence into the likeness of
Christ’s death (v. 5), and which, finally, brings justification from sin (v.
7). Nor can it be taken otherwise in this passage unless we say that the
inference from Christ’s death to our death in verse 14 does not agree with the
apostle’s purpose. For Paul’s purpose there is not to prove that all who are
outside Christ had died in sins, but that all those who are in Christ (as he
himself speaks in v. 17) have died to sin and themselves so that they may live
to Christ, not themselves (v. 15). 2. They note that these words (“not imputing
their sins to them”) do not refer to real remission, but to the ministry of
reconciliation, whose form is that God does not will to impute sins to the
world, that is, to human beings in general, if they believe. But they ought to
have noticed that the apostle relates reconciliation and remission as already
accomplished, not foretells them as future on a condition to be fulfilled:
“God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not imputing their
offenses to them”—not *will be*, but *was*. Nor ought they avert their eyes
from the order which the apostle establishes between the work of
reconciliation and its ministry. This ministry was not set first so that
reconciliation might perhaps follow from it; rather, after reconciliation had
first been accomplished before God, its ministry or word was then committed
to the apostles, as Hugo Grotius rightly observes in his *Defense against
Socinus*. 3. They argue: “If by the reconciled world the elect are signified,
then that same reconciliation was most certainly to be applied to them, and
therefore neither Paul nor God through Paul would have entreated them so
earnestly and fervently to be willing to be reconciled to God.” The contrary
will be a most solid argument: because God prepared a most certain
reconciliation for his elect, for that reason with all urgency, both
externally and internally, he

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 167 | printed p. 157]

moves their minds, as is fitting, so that they may receive that kindness of
his. Nor does certainty of the outcome either take away the sincerity of
prayer and entreaty or extinguish their fervor, as is manifest from Christ’s
prayers (John 11:42; Heb. 5:7) and Paul’s entreaties (Acts 27:31) in matters
which they held to be most certain. 4. So that they may appear to answer not
only what has been said but also what can be said, they state that we can most
fitly take this “world” as the Gentiles together with the Jews; but not all
Gentiles and Jews are elect, and so not even in this way is this passage
understood only of the elect. As though a certain universality of the elect
from Gentiles and Jews could not be constituted unless all Gentiles and Jews
were held to be elect! Or as though God chose all and each foolish person
because he chose the foolish of the world (1 Cor. 1:26). Either they formed a
decidedly inept objection for themselves or they themselves gave a most inept
answer. 5. They also employ a distinction by which the non-imputation of sins
can be considered either universally, insofar as God, for Christ’s sake,
presents himself on his part as ready and willing to pardon all sins; or
particularly, insofar as that reconciliation which was procured is applied to
this or that person. By this reasoning they can distribute not only
reconciliation but also justification, adoption, sanctification, and
glorification itself into universal and particular, since they teach that God
on his part is ready and willing to grant all these things to all; and with
the aid of this distinction they can easily defend that all are not only
reconciled but also justified, sanctified, and glorified. And, armed with this
distinction, they need not fear to defend anything, since by its benefit they
know how to demonstrate that sins are universally not imputed and yet are
particularly imputed, that all are universally reconciled and yet many are
particularly held among hated enemies. But in truth both the words which they
use and the

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 168 | printed p. 158]

meaning subjected to them are contrary not only to the truth but also to
themselves. For properly speaking, nothing is universal which does not exist
in all the particulars of the same kind, nor does the universal exist
otherwise or elsewhere than in them. Therefore, if the reconciliation of all
is universal, it cannot but be applied particularly to this and that person.
It must also at last be applied to all if God on his part is ready and wills
to do this—unless something further on the human being’s part is said to be
required and furnished, and that before every application of Christ or his
grace, from which both this application and its effects would depend as upon
their proper cause. But if it is in no way applied, then it ought not to be
called reconciliation, because it is not a restoration of friendship, nor is
any change made on the human being’s part; rather, his sins are imputed to him,
and divine wrath altogether remains upon him.

After that skirmish concerning the apostle’s meaning, the Remonstrants approach
those words of Christ in John 3:16, where they contend with various arguments
that the beloved world does not consist only of the elect. All of them must be
examined briefly. 1. They say that in their judgment “world” is nowhere taken
in this way, especially when reconciliation is treated apart from respect to
application. What it is in their judgment does not matter much, but I have
proved by manifest evidence that the matter itself stands otherwise. Nor
could they deny this as confidently as they freely wished, as is clear from
the qualification subjoined (“especially when reconciliation is treated apart
from respect to application”), though it is fictitious and empty. I call it
fictitious and imaginary because no passage of Scripture is given where
reconciliation is treated apart from respect to application. I call it empty
because it conflicts with reason that a means, as such, should be treated
without any respect to its end. They themselves acknowledge on the very next
page (206) that in the very procurement of reconciliation Christ had the
intention of actually

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 169 | printed p. 159]

and really saving human beings. If this is true, it in no way allows every
respect to application to be separated from reconciliation. 2. They deny that
even if “world” were taken elsewhere for the elect alone, it can be so taken
here, because in verses 17–19 it is divided into two parts, the faithful and
the unbelieving. Otherwise it would absurdly be said that “God loved the elect
so that all the elect who believe in him should be saved,” and that “light came
into the elect, and the elect loved darkness rather than light.” I answer: 1.
It cannot be proved from the context that the same “world” is properly
distributed into the faithful and unbelieving as into its parts. I
acknowledge a certain distinction of the word or its varied use. But this
ought not to be said to occur in parts, but in various meanings. 2. Many
authors employ a mode of speech in which the same word is indeed repeated,
but with a plainly different or even contrary meaning (it is called
ἀνάκλασις καὶ ἀντίστασις). It is also frequent in Scripture and is sometimes used by
John, as in 1 John 2:7–8: “I do not write a new commandment to you,” and, on the
contrary, “I write a new commandment to you.” In this way “world” can denote
both the faithful and the unbelieving in the same sequence of discourse, yet
in verse 16 designate only the faithful or certain chosen persons from the
world. 3. I say that “world” is taken here collectively, not generally or
universally, so that with respect to its various parts it is truly said both
to be loved and to be hated, both to be saved and to be condemned—in precisely
the same way that the Israelites collectively are called both enemies and
beloved in Romans 11:28: “enemies according to the gospel, but beloved
according to election.” In this way that absurdity with which these men strive
to burden our interpretation is sufficiently removed. For God was so disposed
toward the lost condition of the world that he would not allow all mortals to
perish, but willed to save those who would believe. This also is the
condemnation of the world with respect to a great part of it: light came to
them and they did not receive it. 3.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 170 | printed p. 160]

They propose certain questions which must be dispatched in a few words. How
did the Lord truly say that this is the condemnation, that human beings loved
darkness, when they could not but love darkness? He nevertheless truly said
this by the very fact that, being afflicted with a lethal disease, they did
not take the medicine, although they could not take it, because they had
contracted that inability by their own fault and neither acknowledged that it
had been contracted nor truly desired to be restored. How will that hatred of
the light result in condemnation if the light did not come to illuminate
them? In the very same way that both the lack of medicine and the fault of
aversion result in the destruction of one who has sufficient medicine offered
to him, whatever the offerer’s internal counsel may have been. Would they not
rather have sinned if they had wished to embrace a light not kindled for their
benefit? Nothing less; for we must always pronounce on duties and sins not
from God’s internal intention but from his precepts. 4. On this reasoning they
say that in order God first loved unto salvation and chose out of love before
he determined to give his Son Christ, although Christ is the foundation of
election (Eph. 1; the old Confession, art. 16). The order of these things has
been sufficiently treated in the first article, as have the apostle’s words
and those of the Confession. Nevertheless, these things can further be added
here. 1. On the very next page the Remonstrants themselves acknowledge a
certain saving love prior to the gift of Christ and the cause of it, where
they say that “with saving love God so far loved the whole world that he willed
to give it his Son.” 2. No mention of a foundation is made either in the
apostle’s words or even in those of the Catechesis. 3. In the Remonstrants’
judgment the word “foundation” is used imprudently when actions are under
discussion. These are their own words on p. 406, and therefore the term is
ineptly applied here by them to election, which Nicolaus Grevincovius
expressly defines as an action of God on p. 103. 4. Christ’s testament was

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 171 | printed p. 161]

made before he laid down his life and was confirmed through his death (Heb.
9:17). But in a testament the institution of heirs is the foundation of the
whole act, as the jurists say. It is therefore necessary that the heirs were
instituted by Christ before he could seal anything to them through his death.
5. In the last place they meet our men’s objection from verse 17, where Christ
is said to have come “that he might save the world.” They answer that this
cannot be understood otherwise than of Christ’s intention, which was actually
and really to save all human beings. They press and twist this at greater
length, but it is not necessary to follow their footsteps in this passage,
for from Nicolaus Grevincovius’s more candid confession it is sufficiently
clear that in this whole answer we are being given mere words. For on pp. 8–9,
etc., he denies and utterly denies that anyone’s actual salvation was either
intended or could properly be intended in the matter of redemption. Indeed,
on pp. 170 and 188 he contends that contradictory wills are attributed to God
by those who say that God wills and intends the salvation of human beings
before he sees them believe (which must necessarily be held if he intends
actually and really to save them before he gave Christ to them), and yet does
not will to confer salvation upon them before they believe. This is also the
customary refrain of all the Remonstrants. If they say that those statements
are to be understood of the will of election, then they ought to state the
reason—which they will never do—why a decree to save the faithful cannot stand
equally well with a certain will to elect as with an intention actually to
gather all before foreseen faith, and show some distinction—which is
nonexistent—between the will of election and that intention actually or really
to save human beings.

Let these things therefore suffice to have been said about the first
prescription. The second is that the text does not say that God gave the Son
for both those worlds; on the contrary, it says that he was given unto
salvation only for all or each who believe.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 172 | printed p. 162]

The Remonstrants oppose almost nothing to this except repetitions of what was
examined before. They say that the word “only” is not present, which they truly
say of the letters and syllables of this little word; but since it denotes
nothing other than separation, and in the text those who are to be saved are
separated from the rest through faith, it is truly said that “only” is found
there as to the sense. 2. They acknowledge that it is expressed that “whoever
believes will be saved,” but by no means that “the Son was given only for
them.” Nor was it stated in the prescription that he was given only for them,
but that only for them was he given *unto salvation*. This is expressed in the
very words which they relate. 3. They say that concerning salvation procured
they can assent to this assertion, but this does not pertain to the matter,
because the controversy concerns salvation *to be procured*. The present
controversy concerns the sense of this passage. But if in this passage Christ
is said to be given unto salvation only for the faithful—which is an assertion
to which they say they can assent as true—then the proper end of his death was
the salvation of the faithful, and the love of the world consists in giving
Christ to the faithful unto salvation. For this gift is for the purpose of
being lifted up (v. 14), that is, for the death of the cross; and in verse 16
that love of God toward the world is shown to shine in this gift. 4. They say
that “world” is understood universally. This was discussed in the former
prescription. 5. They deny that those words (“that whoever believes in him,”
etc.) are properly a restriction, but say they are an indication of the
Father’s aim and intention in giving the Son—namely, that he gave him to the
world with this law, that the world should not be saved through him except by
faith. Here, 1. they seem to contradict themselves by saying that no
restriction is properly contained in words which they acknowledge denote the
end and law of this action. For every ordered action has its limits and bounds
from the end and purpose of the agent and ordainer; and it belongs to the
nature of a law to place a restriction and rule upon the things about which it
is enacted. 2. They seem to hand this whole testimony over to us, for either a
restriction is contained in these words, “whoever

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 173 | printed p. 162 (sic; repeated; intended p. 163)]

believes,” etc., or the company of believers extends as widely as that world
loved by God which was mentioned before. Whichever of these they choose makes
against them. And certainly, if all things are weighed rightly and seriously,
it will be clear that hardly any passage of Scripture is more hostile to the
Remonstrants in this cause than this one, in whose favor they are accustomed
to glory so greatly before the unskilled. For 1. this is one of their tenets:
God did not will or was not able to will the salvation of human beings before
Christ had satisfied his justice (Nicolaus Grevincovius, p. 9). But here it is
expressly said that God first loved human beings—that is, willed them well unto
salvation—before he gave his Son to them or for them. 2. They deny that this
love denotes an act of will (the same author, p. 20). Yet here it is set down
as the immediate cause of the gift of Christ, and it would be insane to deny
that this gift depends upon the divine will. 3. All the Remonstrants deny that
this love is absolutely supreme (p. 193), although the highest degree is
openly designated by the prefixed expression “so . . . that” and is clearly
confirmed from parallel passages, as was proved above in answer to the sixth
argument. 4. They say that God gave Christ before he had decreed anything
within himself about faith, salvation, the faithful, or those to be saved
(Arminius, *Declaration of His Sentiments*, p. 47). But here it is manifestly
taught—as the Remonstrants themselves imprudently concede on pp. 206 and
208—that when God destined his Son for death, his aim and intention was
actually and really to save human beings, and that through faith. Thus some
antecedent decree concerning salvation and faith must necessarily be
conceived, since otherwise they could not have entered into the intention of
this work, that is, its first moment. All these weapons are supplied to us
from the quiver of this testimony, with which we can assail the Remonstrants’
cause. But nothing in it, not even in appearance, favors them except the bare
sound of “world.” They add,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 174 | printed p. 164]

in the last place, that their position is confirmed by the exposition of
leading Doctors of the Reformed churches: Luther, in a sermon on the Feast of
Philip; Calvin, on this passage; Zanchi, *Miscellanies*, p. 282; Bullinger and
Musculus on this passage; and Rodolphus Gualterus, homily 5 on 1 John. But they
ought to be smitten by their own hearts when they acknowledge these men as
leading Doctors of the Reformed churches. In the past, with their toothed pens
they have not merely cut off a wing of their cloak, as David did Saul’s (1 Sam.
24), but, as the Ammonites did David’s envoys (2 Sam. 10), they have, as it
were, shaved off half their beards and cut their garments through the middle
and sent them away in this fashion—not merely to Jericho and Jerusalem, but
also to Gath and Ashkelon. This is what makes them stink to those Reformed
churches among which the names of these Doctors still remain a blessing. Yet
they do not truthfully relate that their position is confirmed by them. I have
not yet found Luther’s homily on Philip in his works. Although Calvin, on this
passage, observes that universal terms were attached with respect to the
indiscriminate offering of this benefit, he nevertheless judges this love to
be the same as that mentioned in Ephesians 1:5 and to be peculiar to the
elect. Zanchi’s sole purpose in the treatise which they cite is to prove that
Christ is the advocate and ἱλασμὸν, or propitiation, only of the elect; and he
judges that in John 3 “world” can denote all and each in no other sense than
with respect to the offering, which is in some way common to all: “God so loved
the world, for he offered to all,” he says. On John 3 Musculus says that
“world” is opposed to the Israelite people, and he explains his meaning more
openly on 1 Timothy 2, where he teaches that by “all men” are understood not all
individuals but all kinds. He has the same position on Romans 9 and in his
commonplaces on election and on the will of God. Gualtherus, in homily 5 on 1

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 175 | printed p. 165]

John, has nothing which pertains to this. In homily 7 he says that Christ is
the universal Savior of all, but explains himself in these words: “Although
the promises were formerly given to the Jews, and Christ had to be born from
them, nevertheless his merit pertains to all who receive him.”

The second testimony by which the Remonstrants’ thesis was supported was taken
from 1 John 2:2: “He is the propitiation for our sins, and not only for ours,
but also for the sins of the whole world.” Here, 1. our men require the same
proof as in the former passage: namely, that “world” signifies all and each
mortal. The Remonstrants reply, 1. by referring the reader to their former
defense; 2. that not only “world” but also “whole world” occurs in the text, a
phrase by which they think the faithful alone are nowhere designated. But I
gladly appeal with them to the former discussion, where I showed that in
Scripture the faithful are denoted not only by the bare word “world,” but also
by “all nations,” “all families of the nations,” “all flesh,” “all men,” and
“all the ends of the world,” that is, the whole world. And indeed, since “the
whole world” denotes only the worse part of humanity in 1 John 5:19 and
Revelation 12:9, I see no reason why it cannot also be applied to the better
part. Nor do I see in what other sense the apostle’s statement in Colossians
1:6 can rightly be understood: “the gospel reached the whole world and brought
forth fruit.” For the word did not so reach any as to bring forth fruit in them
except the faithful. 2. Our men note that the propitiation of Christ’s blood is
a propitiation through faith (Rom. 3:25), and therefore pertains only to the
faithful, not to all and each human being. The Remonstrants reply: “This
propitiation is through faith with respect to application.” From this, then, I
say that it is proved that John’s words concern only the faithful, for he
speaks of propitiation under the aspect in which it is joined with
intercession for the faithful and is contained within the same

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 176 | printed p. 166]

limits. But the Remonstrants themselves do not deny that the intercession
mentioned in verse 1 pertains only to the faithful. 3. Our men think that it
does not agree with the apostle’s purpose for the apostle to speak here of all
and each mortal, since his purpose was to console the faithful and exhort them
not to despair if at any time they should fall into sin. Therefore, he brings
a universal consolation, but one universal to all the faithful, of all
conditions and places, and no further. The Remonstrants reply, 1. that the
preceding exhortation, “that you do not sin,” is common to all; therefore so is
the consolation that follows. Here they play upon an ambiguity, for that
exhortation is common to all with respect to the kind of teaching contained
in it, but with respect to the apostle’s immediate aim, it too pertained to a
finite company of human beings. 2. They say that no consolation arises from
the redemption of the faithful for an infirm person who doubts his faith and
is so oppressed by the burden of sins that he does not feel the power of the
Spirit in himself. But from the doctrine of universal redemption a foundation
of consolation has been laid, although the conscience is not made altogether
tranquil; for one who hears this proceeds with confidence, because God is able
and willing to forgive the sins of every repentant sinner without distinction.
1. As to our doctrine concerning redemption and what is annexed to it, I say
that it brings the highest consolation to all the faithful, since it
establishes an inseparable bond between true faith and the eternal benefit of
eternal redemption. If, therefore, they speak of a person who formerly had a
genuine sense of faith, although at present he suffers a certain fainting, we
will nevertheless say that in his former experience is deposited a foundation
of hope not to be despised, by the recollection of which he ought to be
animated and awakened to renewed repentance and faith. Indeed, if even a true
and sincere desire for faith accompanies that infirmity—without which it
should not be called infirmity—we believe this very thing to be

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 177 | printed p. 167]

an argument that some seed of faith and fruit of election lie beneath it. But
if no faith
at any degree could ever be observed to be present, we teach that such a
person as yet has no business with election and predestination, since election
has no use and ought not to be regarded by human beings except where it shines
forth; and it shines forth chiefly through its efficacy and application in
the faithful through faith. For all the rest, the apostle prescribes another
rule of conduct for us in 2 Timothy 2:25: they are to be instructed with all
gentleness; their misery is to be set before their eyes through the law; the
sufficiency of the redemption which is in Christ is to be presented to them
from the gospel; and they are to be taught what is required of them, namely,
that they repent and rest upon Christ. All these things must be done diligently
by us as we try whether perhaps God will grant them repentance through the
knowledge of the truth. 2. They acknowledge that their doctrine does not make
human consciences altogether tranquil, and this cannot be denied. Nor ought
this to be understood only of the doctrine of redemption which they deliver,
but also of the chain of causes of salvation which they posit. It grants no
one among the faithful certain peace beyond the present moment, for they hold
that all future things which concern human salvation are as contingent as the
inclination of a will in no way predetermined, which in a brief moment is
borne this way and that. 3. They acknowledge that John here establishes the
consolation of the faithful, but say this is done by an argument from the less
to the greater: he notes that this propitiation was made not only for the
faithful, which is less, but also for all human beings, which is greater. But a
benefit is not always greater because it is more common. That saying of Seneca
more often holds: “What is given to anyone is pleasing to no one; make rare
what you wish to be pleasing.” And in truth nothing would be added to the
consolation of the faithful if Christ had been given equally for all; indeed,
not a little

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 178 | printed p. 168]

would be taken away, for then they could recognize no singular love or care
for themselves in Christ’s death more than for those who had already taken
their place in hell.
But grant that there is a progression from fewer redeemed to more, provided it
is understood in this way: that it is not only for those faithful whom John
addresses, but also for all the faithful or elect who have lived or will live.
The Remonstrants object here, 1. that only the faithful are not understood by
“the whole world.” I have sufficiently shown that the matter stands
otherwise. 2. They say that the preceding part of this comparison fits the
faithful of all times, as in chapter 1, verses 8–9. Therefore, in the following
part others distinct from the faithful are indicated by “the whole world”;
otherwise there would be no comparison. It undoubtedly fits all the faithful
with respect to the truth of the matter, for otherwise a comparison within
the same kind could not be established between certain of the faithful and
all the faithful. Nevertheless, because in the former part the apostle had not
spoken purposely of all, by adding the rest in that accession he indicates
that the matter is so. Let the same answer suffice for those other instances
by which they teach that common benefits are mentioned in private writings.
For the benefits are common, but from the writer’s intention they often have a
private application, and therefore are sometimes extended by a certain
accession to others not previously addressed by name. Thus in Ephesians 2:17,
the peace proclaimed through Christ is common to all the faithful;
nevertheless the apostle distinguishes, “you who were far off and those who
were near.” Thus in Colossians 1:6 the gospel pertained to all the faithful,
but the apostle distinguishes between those to whom he writes and the rest of
the world, in which it brought forth similar fruit: “it reached you, as also
the whole world, and it brings forth fruit.” These words alone can suffice to
explain the position now under discussion. For the gospel had not reached

understood. For the truth of such expressions it is sufficient if no kind of
human being is excluded before the rest, although there are many among human
beings who were never inscribed on this roll.

The fourth and last prescription of our men concerning this passage is that
it can be taken in this sense: this propitiation is said to be not only for
the Jews but also for the Gentiles. For John was a Jew and wrote this epistle
especially to the Jews, as appears from the following verse 7; and the
Gentiles set over against the Jews are called “the world” (Rom. 11:12). There
is less need to labor over this because our men are not so much stating their
own position here as relating what can probably be said. Nevertheless, the
things said on the other side must be weighed briefly. They say it is
uncertain that John wrote this epistle especially to the Jews; Augustine
thinks it was sent to the Parthians; others consider it Catholic, written to
all the faithful; and John himself says in the last chapter, verse 13, that in
general he wrote it to those who believe in the name of the Son of God. Yet
these things do not prevent what our men said from being true. For 1. certain
Jews are called Parthians (Acts 2:5, 9). 2. This epistle of John is no more
Catholic than those of James and Peter. But their superscriptions expressly
testify that those epistles were written to the twelve tribes and the
scattered sojourners (James 1:1; 1 Pet. 1:2). It is also probable that since
those three—James, Cephas, and John—were charged especially to discharge the
apostolate among the Jews (Gal. 2:9), and the first two wrote especially to
them by reason of their office, John, walking in the same footsteps, also had
them chiefly in view in this writing. Yet this in no way prevents them from
having written to those who believed in the name of the Son of God (add also

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 180 | printed p. 170]

all such people in some manner). Nor was this interpretation taken from Beza
alone, as the Remonstrants object, for among the most ancient this opinion
openly seems to have been Cyril’s. For he says in book 11, chapter 19: “John,
because he was a Jew by nation, lest he be thought to have taught that Christ
was a propitiation for the Jews alone when he said, ‘He is the propitiation for
our sins,’ added, ‘also for the sin of the whole world,’ in order to signify the
Gentiles also.”

I will not contend concerning the sense of the words brought here from chapter
2, verse 7. The argument which they form—“If both the Jewish and Gentile
faithful to whom John wrote are comprehended in the former words (‘for our
sins’), then the unbelieving are understood in the latter words (‘for the sins
of the whole world’)”—this argument, I say, is of no weight at all. For 1. he
perhaps comprehended all in the former part, but, lest he seem to have
understood only the Jews, added an explanation in the latter, as Cyril has it.
2. He could comprehend all to whom he wrote directly, yet not all who were in
the world. 3. If he comprehended all who were then in the world, it
nevertheless does not follow that when he set down those words, he had before
his eyes all who had lived before and would live afterward. The latter
statement could therefore comprehend others distinct from the former who were
not unbelievers. Concerning the apostle’s words in Romans 11:12, only one point
is brought forward which was not discussed before: namely, that when the
apostle says that the offense of the Jews is the riches of the world and their
diminution the riches of the Gentiles, by “the world” he understands not only
all the faithful among the Gentiles, but all peoples and nations without
distinction between faithful and unbelieving. For although no one becomes a
participant in those riches except by faith, nevertheless God’s riches are
ready for all and the treasure of grace is opened to all, since after the
offense of the Jews the apostles went to preach the gospel

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 181 | printed p. 171]

to all, which they also performed without making any distinction between the
faithful or elect and the rest. In this answer they seem to err grievously in
more than one way. For 1. the text concerns the riches of God, although God’s
riches do not belong to any human being unless they are granted to him as a
possession. 2. The expansion and opening of the treasure of grace through
external proclamation does not immediately make those to whom it is presented
in this way rich in grace. For then, according to the Remonstrants’ position,
all would be rich, since they contend that this is done for all; but this very
passage testifies that the Jews are now diminished, not rich, and the Savior
confirms that some store up treasures for themselves who are not rich toward
God (Luke 12:21). 3. Only those to whom grace has been given in Christ have
been enriched in God (1 Cor. 1:5), and it is plain that the riches of the
Gentiles consist in that for which the apostles and brothers are said to have
glorified God (Acts 11:18), namely, that “God also gave the Gentiles repentance
unto life.” 4. The apostle himself in the same chapter, verse 17, expressly
explains those riches and to whom they belong: namely, those who were grafted
into the olive tree and made participants in its root and fatness, that is,
the faithful. 5. Although the apostles—acting in a human manner—endeavored to
enrich any among the Gentiles with the treasure of the gospel, nevertheless,
according to the divine counsel, their ministry properly had in view the
saints who belonged to Christ’s body (Eph. 4:12), and their promise properly
belonged to those whom God was going to gather into that body (Acts 2:39) or
had gathered long ago (Heb. 9:15).

Let these things suffice concerning that celebrated passage, which the
Socinians—and among them their standard-bearer Ostorodius—confidently proclaim
to be lethal to our predestination and press with the same efforts as the
Remonstrants, as is manifestly seen in Jacobus, *At the Harbor of the Orthodox
Faith, Defense*, chapter 29.
