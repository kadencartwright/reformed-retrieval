# John Brown, *Libri duo contra Wolzogium et Velthusium*, Volume II (1670)

> **Working edition in progress:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,
> unreviewed diplomatic-normalized Latin transcription and independent close
> English translation. It is not a critical edition or publication-ready text;
> every reading and translation should be checked by a qualified human editor
> against the cited ONB facsimile.

## Work and volume identity

- **Author:** John Brown of Wamphray (c. 1610–1679), printed as Johannes Broun
  in the ONB catalog record
- **Collective work:** *Libri duo. In priori, Wolzogium ... In posteriori,
  Lamberti Velthusii sententia Libertino-Erastiana ...*
- **Volume used here:** Volume II only, with the divisional title
  *Libertino-Erastianæ Lamberti Velthusii Sententiæ, De Ministerio, Regimine, &
  Disciplinâ Ecclesiasticâ ... Confutatio*
- **Date:** 1670
- **Cataloged collective imprint:** Amsterdam, Henricus ab Aquisgrano
- **Subject of Volume II:** Lambert van Velthuysen’s position on ecclesiastical
  ministry, government, and discipline

The divisional title leaf says that the refutation was written “by the same
author” but does not repeat Brown’s name or the collective imprint. Those data
come from the ONB record for the bound two-volume work. No text from Volume I,
Brown’s work against Wolzogen, is transcribed or translated here. Wolzogen is
mentioned once within the selected Volume II text because Brown compares his
treatment of orthodox theologians with Velthuysen’s.

## Part 01 boundary and source map

Part 01 is Brown’s first complete argumentative unit in the body: his statement
and synopsis of Velthuysen’s position, the first of the three steps Brown
announces on printed p. 4.

- It begins with the body heading and `Narus Satanas quàm necessarium...` on
  printed p. 1 / ONB canvas 9.
- It carries Brown’s introduction and his detailed extraction of Velthuysen’s
  claims concerning church power, excommunication, ministry, ordination,
  preaching, sacraments, and the secular magistrate.
- It includes Brown’s final compact synopsis and ends with the complete sentence
  `Plus potest unus aliquis ... quàm plures docti multarum horarum, imò, &
  dierum spacio, confirmare ac demonstrare possunt.` at the top of printed p. 20
  / ONB canvas 28.
- The next paragraph, `Nos tamen compendiosam aliquam inibimus rationem...`,
  begins Brown’s transition to his second announced step, comparison with the
  Reformed confessions, on the same page and is excluded.

The unit therefore spans exactly twenty printed pages, pp. 1–20 / ONB canvases
9–28, with only the upper portion of p. 20 included. The unnumbered divisional
title on canvas 7 and its verso on canvas 8 are mapped here as preliminary
matter but are not part of the twenty-page body unit.

## Part 02 boundary and source map

Part 02 begins with `Nos tamen compendiosam aliquam inibimus rationem...` in
the lower portion of printed p. 20 / ONB canvas 28. It starts Brown’s comparison

## Part 03 boundary and source map

Part 03 completes the confessional survey and then contains Brown’s first two
assertions concerning Christ’s mediatorial kingdom.

- It begins with `Confess. Wirtemberg cap. de ordine` in the lower portion of
  printed p. 37 / ONB canvas 45.
- It completes the Württemberg and Bohemian material, cites Polish synods and
  the Basel Confession, and concludes the survey with chapters 23, 30, and 31
  of the British Confession.
- `ASSERTIO I` proves that Christ as mediator is king and possesses a kingdom;
  `ASSERTIO II` distinguishes that mediatorial kingdom from his universal
  kingdom as God.
- It ends with the complete sentence `De secundâ assertione jam satis.` in the
  upper portion of printed p. 57 / ONB canvas 65. The following heading,
  `ASSERTIO III`, and its text on the same page are excluded.

The unit has twenty-one synchronized markers for printed pp. 37–57. Because
only the lower portion of p. 37 and upper portion of p. 57 belong to it, the
unit comprises approximately twenty full pages of text without splitting
Assertion II.

## Part 09 boundary and source map

Part 09 is Brown’s complete `ASSERTIO IX`, arguing that the people’s election

## Part 11 boundary and source map

Part 11 begins with `Secundo, Illi tantummodo potestatem ordinandi habent...`

## Part 12 boundary and source map

Part 12 begins with `Dico ergo II. Si data fuerit à Christo potestas hæc

## Part 13 boundary and source map

Part 13 begins with `Assertionis altera pars, quòd scil. jus ac potestas

## Part 14 boundary and source map

Part 14 is Brown’s complete `ASSERTIO XI`, arguing that the proper performance

objections, and closes with an extended discussion of the ordination,
permanence, and material support of elders and deacons. The part begins in the
lower portion of printed p. 193 / ONB canvas 201 and ends immediately before
`ASSERTIO XII` in the upper portion of printed p. 205 / ONB canvas 213. Its

Ecclesiâ Christi necessaria est, & in finem usque duratura.`

Part 15 is Brown’s complete `ASSERTIO XII`, arguing that the pastoral ministry

## Part 16 boundary and source map

Part 16 is Brown’s complete `ASSERTIO XIII`, arguing that lawfully called, sent,

## Part 17 boundary and source map

Part 17 is Brown’s complete `ASSERTIO XIV`, arguing that the conversion of
human beings to God and their reconciliation with God rank among the greatest
subordinate and intermediate ends and effects of the pastoral ministry of the
word. Brown carefully defines preaching as God’s principal ordinary moral
instrument rather than the principal or physically efficient cause of
regeneration, then gives twenty-six arguments from Scripture. He answers
Velthuysen’s readings of Romans 10, 2 Corinthians 5, and Acts 6 and concludes
that Velthuysen’s merely human, temporary, order-preserving conception of
ministry agrees substantially with the Socinians. The part begins at the exact
requested join in the lower portion of printed p. 258 / ONB canvas 266 and ends
with `De hâc Assertione satis. Sequitur,` immediately before `ASSERTIO XV` on
printed p. 280 / ONB canvas 288. The paired transcription and translation below

approximately twenty-two full pages of text. The exact next untranslated

annunciando, tenentur omnes Pastores fideliter se gerere, secundum canonem Dei
Verbi; ac nullam aliam primam, primariam ac principalem, agnoscere regulam,
normam aut amussim.`

## Editorial and translation method

Long *s*, ligatures, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v` are normalized.
Obvious typographic contractions are expanded, while common reference
abbreviations are retained. Substantive spelling, wording, names, numbering,
quotations, and foreign-language matter are retained. Running heads,
bare page numbers, signatures, catchwords, ornaments, and non-textual bleed from
adjacent pages are omitted. Page markers identify the volume, printed page,
and one-based ONB canvas. The duplicate ampersand printed on p. 1 is retained.

The short Greek expression on p. 9 is retained in the Latin and explained in a
translator’s note. Brown’s quotations from Velthuysen are distinguished
typographically, but their wording remains Brown’s Latin presentation rather
than a new transcription of Velthuysen’s Dutch books.

The English is a full translation of the accompanying Latin, not a summary.
It preserves Brown’s polemical attributions as historical claims rather than
endorsing them. Biblical quotations are translated directly from Brown’s
printed Latin wording. No modern translation has been used as a wording source.

## Part 17: conversion through the ministry of the word

### Latin source

[Volume II; printed p. 258; ONB canvas 266 (lower portion)]

ASSERTIO XIV.

Inter alios hujus Ministerii, verbi pastoribus à Christo commissi, fines &
effectus, subordinatos Dei gloriæ & intermedios, hic; qui est hominum ad Deum
conversio, & cum Deo reconciliatio, inter maximos numerandus est.

Non dicimus, ministerium verbi per pastores administratum, esse causam
conversionis aut regenerationis principalem, absit. Hanc prærogativam soli
Deo, & gratiæ efficaciæ ascribendam in solidum esse, constanter affirmamus:
Sed tantum esse causam instrumentalem, seu medium, quo Deo visum est uti, ad
hunc effectum producendum. Nec tamen volumus, quando instrumentum appellamus,
vim aliquam naturalem ei ascribere; ac si physicè producere posset effectum:
sed tantum medium morale à Deo institutum volumus, quo talis à Deo producitur
effectus. Nec in hoc negotio verbi administrationem ab efficaciâ Spiritus
separamus: nam sine eâ prorsus inefficax erit. Instrumentum seu medium, licet à
principali agente distinguatur, effectum tamen nunquam producere potest nisi
quando à principali agente applicatur & adhibetur, & tum effectus principaliter
à principali causâ pendet. Denique non dicimus hoc esse unicum ac solitarium
Dei, in hoc opere, medium, absit. Limites Omnipotenti non præfigimus. Novimus,
Eum non modo posse, sed & re verâ nonnunquam actu uti aliis, ad suos
convertendos,

[Volume II; printed p. 259; ONB canvas 267]

mediis. Sed dicimus hoc esse medium præcipuum ac maximè ordinarium, quod, ad
fidem ingenerandam, adhibet. Seu, in hunc finem specialiter à Deo institutum
esse hoc medium, ministerium scil. & verbi administrationem; ac propterea non
modo non contemnendam esse hanc Christi ordinantiam, sed & magni semper
faciendam esse dicimus. Hoc autem ex variis Scripturæ testimoniis confirmabimus,
tam contra *Velthusium,* quàm Enthusiastas, Anabaptistas & Swencfeldianos.

1. *Marc.* XVI. 15, 16. *Et dixit eis, profecti in mundum universum, prædicate
Euangelium omni orbi condito. Qui crediderit, & baptizatus fuerit servabitur.*
Ubi liquet Christum discipulis suis potestatem dedisse Euangelium annunciandi,
& eos ad prædicandum misisse, idque in hunc finem, ut crederent electi: fidei
ergo generatio est finis annunciati Euangelii.

2. *Joan.* XVII. 20. *Non tantum autem pro istis rogo, sed & pro iis, qui per
sermonem eorum* (id est Apostolorum) *credituri sunt.* Hinc sequitur quosdam
esse credituros; & per sermonem Apostolorum, id est, per annunciationem
Euangelii, seu administrationem verbi per Apostolos, & Apostolorum, quoad hoc,
successores, in finem usque factam, id factum iri. Ergo supponit Christus
sermones Ministrorum medium esse ordinarium, quo ad fidem perducantur ii, pro
quibus preces ad Deum fudit.

3. Idem liquet ex parabolâ illâ Christi *Mat.* XIII. de semine, quod seminavit
sator. Illud enim semen Christus ipse interpretatur, & dicit esse sermonem de
regno v. 19, 20, 22, 23, & terram bonam, eum esse dicit, *qui sermonem audit &
intelligentiam intendit, qui videlicet fructum fert editque, alius quidem
centena, alius sexagena, alius vero trecena.* Hoc est, qui convertitur ad fidem,
& bona edit opera. Hæc autem conversio fit per verbum regni auditum, & à Spiritu

[Volume II; printed p. 260; ONB canvas 268]

benedictum. Ergo hoc sermone salvantur quidam, quando alii, qui eundem audiunt,
manent professores inertes, hypocritæ & temporarii ad summum, non conversi.

4. *Rom.* I. 15, 16. *Itaque quicquid in me situm est, promptum est ad vobis
quoque, qui Romæ estis Euangelizandum. Non enim me pudet Euangelii Christi,
potentia siquidem est Dei ad salutem cuivis credenti,* &c. Statuerat Apostolus ac
sæpius proposuerat, ut præcedentia ostendunt, Romam ire ad Euangelium
annunciandum, ut inter eos fructum aliquem haberet, quia debitorem se esse
reputârat, tum Græcis tum Barbaris, tum sapientibus, tum insipientibus. Et
etiamsi fortè, multi eum, præterea quod talem prædicaret doctrinam, sannis
exciperent, dicit tamen se eo non pudefieri, propterea quòd, sit potentia Dei
ad salutem. [*Observa autem* (inquit *Calvinus* in loc.) *quantum verbi
ministerio tribuat Paulus, quum testatur Deum illic suam ad salvandum virtutem
exercere: Non enim de arcana aliquâ revelatione, sed vocali prædicatione hîc
loquitur. Unde sequitur eos quasi datâ operâ respuere Dei virtutem, &
liberatricem ejus manum procul à se repellere, qui se prædicationis auditu
subducunt.*] Consule *An. Belgic.*

5. *Rom.* X. 14, 15, 17. *Quomodo igitur invocabunt eum in quem non
crediderint? quomodo autem credent ei de quo non audierint? quomodo autem
audient absque prædicante? Quomodo autem prædicabunt nisi missi fuerint? Ergo
fides ex auditu est, auditus autem per verbum Dei.* Hoc textu clarior nullus
afferri potest ad thesin nostram confirmandam: Nam apertè ostendit Apostolus,
pulcherrimâ gradatione, ordinem usitatum, quem Deus in convertendis hominibus
observat. Requiritur fides, inquit, ad rectam Dei invocationem, fides ordinariè
non producitur, nisi per verbum

auditum, seu verbum Dei à prædicante, hoc est, à prædicante misso, annunciatum.
Ergo, concludit Apostolus, fidem esse ex auditu, & auditum per verbum Dei. Quid
clarius dici potuit? Quæ replicat *Velthusius* postea examinabuntur, vide
*Annot. Belgic.* in loc.

6. 1 *Cor.* I. 21. *Nam postquam in Dei sapientiâ, mundus non cognovit Deum

ASSERTION XIV.

Among the other ends and effects of this ministry of the word committed to
Pastors by Christ—ends subordinate to God’s glory and intermediate in
character—this one, namely the conversion of human beings to God and their
reconciliation with God, must be numbered among the greatest.

We do not say that the ministry of the word administered through Pastors is
the principal cause of conversion or regeneration—far from it. We constantly
affirm that this prerogative must be ascribed wholly to God alone and to the
efficacy of grace. We say only that it is an instrumental cause, or a means
which God has seen fit to use to produce this effect. Yet when we call it an
instrument, we do not mean to ascribe any natural power to it, as though it
could physically produce the effect. We mean only a moral means instituted by
God, through which such an effect is produced by God. Nor in this business do
we separate the administration of the word from the efficacy of the Spirit,
for without it the administration will be wholly ineffectual. Although an
instrument or means is distinguished from the principal agent, it can never
produce an effect unless it is applied and employed by the principal agent;
and then the effect depends principally upon the principal cause. Finally, we
do not say that this is God’s sole and solitary means in this work—far from it.
We do not set bounds for the Almighty. We know that he not only can use other
means for converting his own, but sometimes actually does so in very fact.

[Volume II; printed p. 259; ONB canvas 267]

But we say that this is the chief and most ordinary means which he employs to
engender faith. That is, this means—namely, the ministry and administration of
the word—was specially instituted by God for this end. We therefore say not
only that this ordinance of Christ must not be despised, but that it must
always be highly esteemed. We shall confirm this from various testimonies of
Scripture, against Velthuysen as well as the Enthusiasts, Anabaptists, and
Schwenckfeldians.

1. Mark 16:15–16: “And he said to them, Go into the whole world and preach the
Gospel to every created being. He who believes and is baptized shall be
saved.” It is evident here that Christ gave his disciples the power to
announce the Gospel and sent them to preach, and this for the end that the
elect should believe. The generation of faith, therefore, is an end of the
announced Gospel.

2. John 17:20: “But I pray not only for these, but also for those who will
believe through their word”—that is, the word of the Apostles. It follows that
certain people will believe, and that this will be brought about through the
word of the Apostles: that is, through the announcement of the Gospel, or the
administration of the word by the Apostles and, in this respect, by their
successors, continued until the end. Christ therefore presupposes that the
words of Ministers are the ordinary means by which those for whom he poured
out prayers to God are brought to faith.

3. The same is evident from Christ’s parable in Matthew 13 about the seed which
the sower sowed. Christ himself interprets that seed and says that it is the
word of the Kingdom in verses 19, 20, 22, and 23; and he says that the good
soil is the person “who hears the word and applies his understanding, who then
bears and yields fruit—one indeed a hundredfold, another sixtyfold, and another
thirtyfold.” That is, it is the person who is converted to faith and produces
good works. This conversion takes place through the word of the Kingdom heard
and blessed by the Spirit.

[Volume II; printed p. 260; ONB canvas 268]

Therefore some are saved through this word, while others who hear the same
word remain idle professors, hypocrites, and at most temporary believers, not
converted.

follows that those who withdraw themselves from hearing preaching deliberately

5. Romans 10:14–15, 17: “How then will they call upon him in whom they have not

they have been sent? Therefore faith is from hearing, and hearing through the
word of God.” No text clearer than this can be brought forward to confirm our
thesis. For by a most elegant progression the Apostle openly shows the usual
order which God observes in converting human beings. Faith, he says, is
required for the proper invocation of God, and faith is not ordinarily
produced except through the word

The Apostle therefore concludes that faith is from hearing and hearing through
the word of God. What could have been said more clearly? Velthuysen’s replies

6. 1 Corinthians 1:21: “For since, in the wisdom of God, the world did not know
God through that wisdom, it pleased God through the foolishness of preaching
to save those who believe.” God therefore instituted preaching—which many in
former times, and some now, among whom Velthuysen must be numbered first,
regarded and still regard as foolishness—so that through it, as his primary and
ordinary means, he might save his own. “He expressly mentions preaching,”
Pareus says on the passage, “because the Gospel is the power of God insofar as
it is preached and heard. For faith is from hearing, and hearing through the
word of God, namely the word preached, as is said there: This is the word which
we preach. This declares the dignity and efficacy of the Ecclesiastical
ministry.” Moreover, it is evident from the preceding verses that the Apostle
is speaking here of preaching itself, or the administration of the word. “For
Christ did not send me,” he says in verse 17, “to baptize, but to preach the
Gospel.” And in verse 18 he says, “The word of the cross is foolishness indeed
to those who perish, but to those who are saved, that is, to us, it is the
power of God.” From this we can draw another argument. For the word of the
cross—that is, the preaching of the Gospel—is called the power of God, meaning
God’s efficacious means for converting his own and bringing them to salvation.
Thus the phrase “the foolishness of preaching” means “foolish preaching,” by a
Hebraism. In the same way Christ is afterward called foolishness to the
Gentiles in verse 23, insofar, namely, as he is preached in the Gospel.

God effected this remarkable change.

18. Ephesians 4:11–12: “He therefore gave some to be Apostles; others,

this effect to it. For what is more excellent than that Christ’s Church should
be constituted so as to stand in its proper and perfect integrity? Yet the
Apostle here proclaims that so splendid and divine a work is accomplished by
the external ministry of the word. Hence it is plain that those are mad who,
bypassing this means, hope to become perfect in Christ—

such as the fanatics, who invent secret revelations of the Spirit for

alone, it certainly also belongs to him to prescribe the manner in which he
wishes it to be built. But Paul openly testifies here that, according to

than through external preaching, when we allow ourselves to be ruled and
taught through human beings. This is a universal rule, extending to the

Mother of all the godly, who begets in the Lord, nourishes, and governs Kings

woe to their pride. We do not indeed deny that we can be perfected by God’s

pleased God and what Christ has ordained, not what God has the power to do.”

which elsewhere he calls the doctrine of faith. See the Belgic Annotations.

“why,” Calvin says on Acts 10:5, 8, “does the Angel not fulfill the part of a

to him. Meanwhile I confess that the spirits must be tested, lest we hear
without discrimination everyone who pretends to be a Minister of Christ. But
because faith is from hearing, no one will attain it who rejects and spurns

24. The same is confirmed by that other instance in Acts 9:6, where Christ
sends Paul away to Ananias. “Although,” Calvin says on the passage, “it might
seem absurd that Christ, the eternal wisdom of God, sends elsewhere to learn a
disciple who is already attentive to hear and, as it were, eagerly gapes to
receive doctrine, leaving him in suspense. But I answer that this was not done
without reason. For in this manner the Lord wished to test Paul’s modesty, when
he handed him over to be taught by one of his disciples, as though he did not
yet deign to address him familiarly, but sent him away to his servants,

[Volume II; printed p. 269; ONB canvas 277]

of whom he had recently been both so proud a despiser and so savage a
persecutor. Under his person we too are instructed in humility. For if Christ
subjected Paul to the ministry of an ordinary disciple, which of us should
disdain to hear any teacher whatever, provided he has been ordained by Christ,
that is, provided he proves by the reality itself that he is Christ’s Minister?
Let us therefore know that Paul is sent to Ananias to honor the ministry of
the Church. This is certainly no ordinary honor with which God dignifies the
human race, when he chooses our brothers from our midst to be the interpreters
of his will, and causes his sacred oracles to resound in the mouth of a person
who is naturally profane and given to lies and vanity. But here once again the
world’s foul ingratitude displays itself, because no one can bear to hear God
speaking through a human mouth. They would all desire Angels to fly to them,
or heaven to be repeatedly torn open and the visible glory of God to come
forth from there. Since this perverse curiosity arises from pride and impious
contempt for the word, it opens the door to many delusions and dissolves the
bond of mutual agreement among the faithful. Therefore the Lord not only
testifies that he is pleased for us to be taught through human beings, but
commends and sanctions the order established by him. Those commendations also
belong here: He who hears you hears me—so that Christ may obtain proper
reverence for his word.”

From this it is sufficiently apparent how ridiculous Velthuysen is when he
says on pages 339–340: “The opposing party supplies no text from which it may
appear that God especially wills to regenerate human beings through the
preaching of the word performed by Pastors.” For the Reader now sees from how
many passages of Scripture it can be clearly demonstrated that this is God’s
principal and ordinary way of converting human beings and bringing them to
Christ. What does he say to Romans 10? “The Apostle wishes only to prove,” he
says, “that the calling of the Gentiles to the fellowship of the Gospel cannot
take place unless God wills and commands it, and therefore that no one can
invite them unless God orders it, expressing this in these words: How will
they preach unless they are sent?” Reply. Pareus says on the passage that it
has been disputed whether these things are to be understood of the Jews or of
the Gentiles; preserving the judgment of others, he refers them to both—that
is, to “all who call upon him” from verse 12—because “he speaks indefinitely of
everyone: no one can call upon one in whom he does not believe, and so cannot
call upon God or Christ unless he believes in him, whether he is a Jew or a
Greek. The inferential particle *therefore,*” he says, “depends upon what
precedes: since with the heart one believes unto righteousness and with the
mouth confession is made unto salvation, and the mouth speaks from the
abundance of the heart, how will they call upon him in whom they do not
believe?” And so forth. 2. But what if we suppose him to be speaking of the
Gen-

[Volume II; printed p. 271; ONB canvas 279]

tiles? This always remains true: they cannot believe in God or Christ unless
they hear of him; they cannot hear unless there are people to preach; and no
one can preach unless he is sent. And so it will always follow that hearing
the word announced by a preacher is necessary as the external instrument by
which faith is ordinarily obtained. “For the Apostle is speaking of the
ordinary way of salvation,” Pareus says. “Reading the divine word is useful,
but belongs to a few; hearing belongs to everyone. Therefore, just as the
invocation of God is necessary for salvation, and faith in Christ is necessary
for the invocation of God, so hearing the word is necessary for faith. He is
speaking of the hearing which takes place with the ears, not that which takes
place with the heart, although both are required for faith, as is evident from
the following member.” And a little later: “For hearing the word, therefore,
the preaching of the word is needed. Heralds and Teachers are needed to make
the word resound in people’s ears. — And here the necessity of the
Ecclesiastical ministry is founded, since God does not now will to speak to the
Church from heaven, but through Heralds of the word.”

Finally, I ask, how does this reply differ from the Socinians’ reply?
Volckel, *On the Sending of Ministers,* page 99, says: “The meaning of these
words is that no one can preach in any manner—that is, announce a new
matter—unless he is sent, that is, unless this duty is imposed upon him by
God.” What other escape does Velthuysen devise? “Or one must say that the
passage speaks of the sending of the Apostles, with which the calling of
Pastors has nothing in common. And since God willed the Gentiles to become
participants in Christ’s grace, that will and the Gospel had to be announced
to them if they were to believe, and God predestined them from eternity to this
faith. The Apostle expresses this in these words: How will they believe unless
it has been preached to them? By preaching he understands the announcement of
the Gospel, whether made orally, in writing, by private exhortation, or in a
pub-

[Volume II; printed p. 272; ONB canvas 280]

lic assembly.” Reply. 1. Velthuysen certainly does not know what he ought to
say. This must be answered, he says, or that—which is the mark of a doubtful
and wavering mind. 2. By what argument, or even probable showing, does
Velthuysen prove that this must be understood only of the Apostles and their
sending? Does not the statement, “How will they call upon him in whom they have
not believed?” have its truth now no less than formerly? Is not the statement,
“And how will they believe in him of whom they have not heard?” also of eternal
truth? Is not the statement, “How will they hear without one who preaches?” as
true now as formerly? With what appearance of probability, therefore, can
Velthuysen say that this sending is to be understood solely of the sending of
the Apostles? Was that oracle in Isaiah 52:7 a prophecy only about the sending
of the Apostles, or to be applied only to their sending? Although it can
rightly and deservedly be applied to them in the first place, all others must
not therefore be excluded. “From this,” Pareus says on the passage, “we must
learn how great a treasure is offered in the Gospel, how highly the preaching
of the Gospel must be valued, and finally with how much honor, love, and
gratitude the Heralds of the Gospel are to be embraced by us.” Thus that
conclusion in verse 17 always remains true in every age: “Therefore faith is
from hearing, and hearing through the word of God.” Accordingly, if this
sending is to be restricted to the Apostles, it would follow that faith is not
from every hearing of God’s word, but only from hearing the word through the
preaching of the Apostles. For all these questions have the force of denials,
and the whole progression goes from the denial of causes to the denial of
effects. The progression proceeds thus: How will they call upon him in whom
they have not believed? And how will they believe in him of whom they have not
heard? And so forth. Thus there is no invocation now, no faith, unless the
Apostles have been sent. Is no faith now to be expected among unconverted
Gentiles unless Apostles are sent anew? We too are Gentiles,

[Volume II; printed p. 273; ONB canvas 281]

and many among us do not believe; must Apostles therefore be sent? Does
Velthuysen, with certain others, expect an age of the Holy Spirit? 3. Many who
now live have also been predestined to faith. Therefore, for them to believe,
they must hear the Gospel announced. Therefore there must also be people who
preach. Therefore these people too must be sent. Consequently, the preaching
of those who are sent is the ordinary means by which human beings are brought
to faith. But those sent must be people other than the Apostles. 4. Although
God can bless the announcement of the Gospel made privately or through
letters, this is nevertheless not God’s ordinary or principal way. I readily
concede, however, that it has its use and sometimes—in a time of persecution,
for instance—very great fruit. Nevertheless, public preaching, or preaching in
assemblies, is more ordinary and is to be preferred when it can be obtained.

To the passage in 2 Corinthians 5:20 he replies on page 341 that this is either
something peculiar to the Apostles or common to all Christians. Reply. A third
option is given: namely, something common to all Pastors, as we proved above.
Where, when, or in what respect are all Christians said, or can they be said,
to be God’s ambassadors or to serve as ambassadors in Christ’s name? Does every
private person have the word of reconciliation committed to him? He indeed can
and ought, in his own manner, to instruct and teach his neighbor, but
Velthuysen’s conclusion does not follow from that.

He replies to Acts 6:4, “But we will continue in prayers and the administration
of the word,” which I do not know by whom it was alleged. It is easy to invent
empty or frivolous objections, but anyone who acts this way acts with little
candor. Let us nevertheless hear what he replies. “It is not denied,” he says
on pages 341–342, “that reason and order require that one who is fit to teach
should devote himself to this work and not allow himself to be distracted by
other things which are not so necessary. But how does this prove that there is
any particular power, authority, or character in the pastoral

[Volume II; printed p. 274; ONB canvas 282]

office which has nothing in common with those things found in individual
Christians and does not flow from the members of the Church, who nevertheless
elect the Pastor, give him this character, and, so far as order can permit,
also reserve it for themselves?” Reply. I do not know who adduces this passage
for this purpose, but in any case these things are clear from the text. 1.
Distinct Ecclesiastical offices are given. 2. The office of preaching is
distinct from the office of Deacons. 3. Different offices or functions thus
require different and distinct administrators. 4. Every Minister must
discharge his own office faithfully and give himself wholly to it. 5. The
office of preaching so occupies the whole person that he is not permitted to
devote himself to other matters. 6. These duties cannot be performed by
private members of the Church; otherwise public Ministers would be elected in
vain. But now to his reply. I answer: Let him tell me whether the power,
authority, or character of the Apostles—about whom that text speaks—had anything
in common with the things which belong to individual Christians. Did they
receive their power from members of the Church? Were they elected by the
people? Because Pastors in the present day are elected by members of the
Church, does it therefore follow that Pastors receive all their power,
authority, and character from them? What, I ask, is that power or authority,
and what is that character which the members of the Church reserve for
themselves? What else are these things than the dreams of a diseased brain?

From all these statements of Velthuysen which we have now refuted, it can be
obvious to anyone how highly he values the Ecclesiastical Ministry and that he
can rightly be charged with abolishing the entire ministry of the Gospel.
Indeed, he scarcely acknowledges any ministry instituted by Christ. Whatever
he may say, this is more than sufficiently evident from the points already
refuted. It is true that he says on page 344: “The pastoral office is to be
regarded as an institution of God, just as

[Volume II; printed p. 275; ONB canvas 283]

the office of the Magistrate is.” But I do not see how he can reconcile this
with the preceding points unless he says that he acknowledges this much: for
the sake of order, it is necessary that some suitable person be nominated to
perform those public duties for a certain time, and that he then return to his
former condition; another is then to be nominated to perform the same public
duties, and another after him, for the sake of order and to avoid confusion.
In the same way, in ecclesiastical assemblies, for the sake of order now one
person and now another is chosen to preside, who returns to his former
condition when his allotted time has passed. Thus, to avoid confusion in the
public exercises of Religion—lest everyone, namely, speak, teach, and pray
publicly, and so forth—perhaps he wishes one person to be nominated who would
discharge these duties on this occasion or for some fixed and designated
period. When that time established by the Electors has elapsed, he would
return to his former, that is, private condition. Then another nominated
person would succeed him, or one elected by those to whom this power belongs,
and would be occupied in those exercises for a time. After him another would
be elected, and another by turns. For the whole assembly is now holy, and
therefore every member possesses the immediate potential to perform all these
duties; and when the electors will it and order permits it, he will actually
discharge this office. Unless Velthuysen thinks this, it is impossible for him
to be reconciled with himself. And if he does think this, it is abundantly
clear what sort of ministry he acknowledges. Moreover, the statements which
follow must be observed. In the same place he says: “This office is especially
necessary for those who feel that they are edified more by hearing preaching
than by reading the Bible or a good Author.” What, is this office necessary
only because of them? Was this office instituted by Christ because of such
people, whom Velthuysen judges superstitious? Or can that be regarded as an
office instituted by Christ which is necessary only, or principally, for
superstitious people of that kind? Did

[Volume II; printed p. 276; ONB canvas 284]

Christ will to institute some office because of superstitious people and to
nourish their superstition? Can one who says this rightly say that the
ministry is an institution of God no less than the office of the Magistrate?
Or should he not rather be considered to speak contradictions? Furthermore,
if this office was instituted by Christ, it was instituted for some end and
for the performance of some particular actions. For every public office is
instituted for particular actions; a particular office is not instituted for
actions common to everyone or to be performed by everyone. I therefore ask:
What actions are proper and particular to this function or office? Not the
preaching of the word, not the administration of the Sacraments, not the
exercise of discipline. What then? Did Christ institute the ministerial office
in vain? If everyone were permitted to exercise all the functions of the
Magistracy, to what end would the office of the Magistrate be instituted? We
have already seen that, according to Velthuysen, everyone is permitted to
perform all the things which we judge to belong to the pastoral office. This
is further evident from pages 325–326, where he has the following: “It must be
noted that in Preachers, whom we judge to be instituted only for the sake of
order, there is nothing more than in other Christians who know Christ, have
been knit together in love, and have the full riches of complete assurance of
understanding unto the knowledge of the mystery of God and of the Father and
of Christ, Colossians 2:3. Therefore they have no need to submit themselves to
the discipline of others and are no less holy or participants in the Holy
Spirit. Accordingly, it is not only false but also incomprehensible that there
resides in the office of the preacher any authority, special holiness, more
efficacious operation, or special administration which is not found in other
Christians. Those who set up such a preacher introduce the papacy again
through the back door and impose upon us the yoke under which the Jews were in
the Old Testament and

[Volume II; printed p. 277; ONB canvas 285]

our Fathers under the Papacy.” Hence it is evident that he acknowledges the
ministry to have been instituted in no other way than the Socinians themselves
can and will acknowledge it. For Theophilus Nicolaides speaks thus in chapter
10: “It is therefore apparent that this calling or appointment in no way
belongs to the nature of the teaching office. But if it was formerly employed,
or is now employed, this is done in a well-constituted Church for the sake of
preserving order and decorum.” And nowhere, so far as I know, does Velthuysen
understand a divine order or decorum, that is, one founded upon a divine
command or practice. Moreover: 2. What kind of Ministers or Pastors are those
in whom there is nothing other than what exists in private members of the
Church? Must Christ be said to have instituted Ministers of that sort? 3. What
kind of public pastoral office is it whose holder has no more authority over
private members of the Church than they have over him? 4. No Ecclesiastical
office was instituted by Christ which does not render the person invested with
it specially sanctified and set apart for Christ. 5. Can that be regarded as
an institution of Christ which ordinarily has no greater efficacy to produce
an effect than anything else? Such a thing can be said of a human institution,
but it is impious even to suppose it of a divine institution. 6. Did Christ
institute an office to which no particular action is annexed? Can that be said
to be an office instituted by Christ which has no administration proper or
particular to it? 7. Neither the ceremonial yoke nor the papal yoke consisted
in this: that in the Church there were Ministers distinct from others,
instituted by God and set over the Church, possessing authority granted by God
and exercising it according to God’s prescription. Nor do those who now
acknowledge such Ministers introduce the Papacy or the ceremonial yoke; they
desire Christ’s institutions to be observed in holiness. Finally, from this it

[Volume II; printed p. 278; ONB canvas 286]

is evident what sort of ministry Velthuysen acknowledges and how he
acknowledges it to have been instituted by Christ, so that there is no need to
add more.

I do not deny that Velthuysen sometimes says that election is required in the
person who discharges that office. But 1. nowhere, so far as I know, does he
say that this Election is of divine right, was instituted by Christ, or is
necessary by reason of a divine command or practice. 2. Indeed, in the preface,
page 63, and often elsewhere, he says that it is done only to avoid confusion
and for the sake of order. For by right, namely, everyone without exception
can perform all these things, just as in a private gathering of Christians
everyone who is suitable can pray. But because there would be no order, only
sheer confusion, if everyone prayed at once, they elect or nominate the person
who will pray first. As long as he prays, no one else will be permitted to do
so, otherwise confusion will not be avoided; after him comes another, and so
on. In the same way, according to Velthuysen, anyone can be elected to the
sacred ministry. And because the greatest confusion would result if everyone
discharged it at once, someone must be elected; while he discharges it, another
will not be permitted to undertake that office, since order does not require
it of one who has not been called or nominated. And this is the sole foundation
of Velthuysen’s Election: namely, an order which is merely human and a human
institution for avoiding confusion. 3. Beyond this election Velthuysen
requires nothing else. He holds that there is no sending, no solemn setting
apart,
consecration, or appointment of persons to perform this office. Indeed, on page
30 of his preface he expressly says “that every such thing is nothing other
than the invention of certain canons from Christian prudence,” and that now
under the New Testament there is no sending or consecration to an
Ecclesiastical office. Indeed, he holds that there is no spiritual power or
authority in the Church, nor any spiritual, sanctified,

[Volume II; printed p. 279; ONB canvas 287]

set-apart, or divinely instituted person who, by reason of that institution
and sanctification, is qualified to administer some part of divine worship
which others are not permitted to undertake. On the contrary, he holds that
the whole congregation is holy and that each person, insofar as the
aforementioned order and personal fitness permit, is allowed to administer
every part of divine worship; and that each Church possesses the power to
commit this administration to whomever it wishes. Who that says these things
and wishes to speak consistently can say that the Ecclesiastical ministry is
an institution of Christ? He holds that everything is civil and purely
political, for he acknowledges nothing Ecclesiastical or spiritual distinct
from the political. The reasoning which Velthuysen employs to prove this must
also be noted. Because we do not acknowledge papal consecration to be
necessary, therefore, he says, there is no sending of persons or solemn
setting apart for this office according to Christ’s institution. Because we do
not acknowledge that this right of sending and consecrating Pastors belongs
solely to the Roman Church, he therefore thinks it must be said that no Church
has the right to send and ministerially to give power for discharging the
Ecclesiastical office, or that there is absolutely no authoritative sending,
appointment, setting apart, or ordination by divine right. Rather, if there is
anything of that sort, it is all a human invention for observing order and
avoiding confusion. 4. In what respect, I ask, does he differ from the
Socinians? Smalcius, *Against Frantzius on Ecclesiastical Order,* disputation
4, says: “Nor are we so dull and obtuse that we administer anything in our
Churches without legitimate order. Indeed, whatever truly belongs to order
and decorum is observed much more properly and purely by our people than by
the Lutherans.” And Socinus, Letter 2 to Radecke: “Furthermore, to elect some
one person in each assembly to explain God’s word to the others and from it to
exhort them to true piety and holiness of life

[Volume II; printed p. 280; ONB canvas 288 (upper portion)]

is Apostolic; and, as it seems to me, no reason can be brought forward why it
ought not to be done today.” Theophilus Nicolaides, chapter 10, page 106: “For
Socinus is not speaking of what belongs to the very nature of a Minister and
must necessarily be done, but of what pertains to preserving a certain
honorable order and can merely be done, tacitly assuming in the meantime that
the person approved by others is worthy of that office.” This is more than
Velthuysen concedes. Let Andreas Radecke also be heard in *Notes Refuting the
Gordian Knot,* chapter 4, folio 8, as cited by Peltius: “We concede that no one
can discharge any office without the approval of others. But by this reasoning
a sending is destroyed”—pay attention, Velthuysen—“for just as in a free
Commonwealth it is permissible to elect a person fit to discharge an office,
so in Christ’s Church, which is most free, it will also be permissible to elect
a person fit to teach others to that office and thus set him over themselves.”
We could add more, but these few points can make clear to anyone not blinded by
prejudice how well Velthuysen and the Socinians agree in this matter, whatever
he may say to deceive the unwary and mislead all those who do not investigate
everything precisely enough or weigh it seriously. Enough concerning this
