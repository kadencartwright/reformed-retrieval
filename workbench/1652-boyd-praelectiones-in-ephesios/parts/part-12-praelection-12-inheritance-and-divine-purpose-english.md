On verses 11 and 12. “In whom, in whom also,” and so forth.

[Marginalia: Lecture XII.]

When we set out and traced the summary of the first part of this first
chapter, we said at the proper place something which must now be recalled to
your minds and memories at an opportune time, hearers. Our Apostle first
explains that notable general proposition concerning God’s saving benefits in
Christ, which he laid down above in verse 3 in the form of a doxology, by
enumerating its parts—that is, certain particular blessings—and then applies
it. Thus far, then, came that enumeration or explanation, through a list of
the principal benefits with which divine grace and kindness has favored us in
Christ—indeed, enriched us and overflowed toward us in every abundance. Among
these, eternal election and predestination, both to the end and to the means,
rightly held first place as the fountain and foundation of the rest which
flow from it. From this followed our redemption and liberation through the
blood of Christ. Under that heading we rightly included everything Christ
obtained for us by the merit of his death: the forgiveness of sins,
reconciliation with God, righteousness, adoption, and eternal life itself.
Finally, in the third place, he mentioned our effectual calling. Through its
benefit those former things begin actually to be conferred upon and applied to
us by the faith aroused in our hearts by the Holy Spirit. Through the preaching
of the gospel the Spirit richly illuminated our minds with that heavenly and
spiritual wisdom and understanding—the gospel, I say, through which that
mystery or secret of divine truth and will was disclosed to us: that at the
fullness of time all the elect were to be gathered under the same head, Christ
our mediator and redeemer, into the same body and the same communion of the
catholic Church. This was a synopsis, or summary comprehension, of that
preceding enumeration.

[Marginalia: Transition.]

Now, therefore, in the third place, there follows in the next four verses a
more particular application of that same doctrine and those same benefits. In
it he applies that effectual calling especially—for it was mentioned just
before—first to the Jews, among whom he also numbers himself, and then to the
Gentiles, specifically the Ephesians to whom he was writing. The first part of
this application is contained in these two verses, 11 and 12, whose exposition
is now before us.

[Marginalia: Proposition.]

“In him,” I say, namely in Christ, whom he named in the preceding verse. Thus,
by a kind of recapitulation or resumption, the Apostle connects this verse with
the former one. There are indeed some who attach this particle to the end of
the preceding verse and allege a Hebrew pleonasm, in which the relative is
often redundant and is added to its own antecedent as something superfluous,
following Hebrew idiom. Yet it seems far more elegant to begin the next verse
with it resumptively: “in whom also we were allotted a portion,” that is, were
admitted, received, or called into the inheritance—as will be explained more
fully below—“when we were predestined,” or because we had been predestined,
“according to the purpose of him who effects all things,” or who effectually
works all these things, for this is the force of the word *energountos*, “from
the counsel of his will”; that is, with supreme wisdom and with supreme freedom
and authority, by which he can—

[Marginalia: Exegesis.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 81 | Caput I p. 39]

—ordain concerning all things just as he wills. He does this to the end “that
we should be to the praise,” or “for the praise, of his glory”; that is, that
our salvation, and indeed we ourselves, and all that beneficence with which he
has richly heaped us in Christ, should redound to the praise of his glory—of
God himself, glorious and shining with infinite majesty. Then he explains who
the “we” are whom he mentioned: “we,” he says, “who were the first to hope in
Christ”; that is, we who first believed in him, in comparison with you
Gentiles, before you had been imbued with knowledge of him through the
preaching of the gospel and converted to faith in him, as is evident from the
verse immediately following.

Now, so that we may understand this first part of the application more
precisely, five things in order must be discussed in the Apostle’s words.
First, how he designates the benefit of calling as applied to the believing
Jews: namely, that in Christ “we also were allotted a portion.” Second, the
cause from which he teaches that this calling depends: namely, that we were
“predestined according to the purpose of God the Father.” Third, how he
describes God the Father, the author of our predestination, by calling him “the
one who effectually works all things from the counsel of his will.” Fourth, the
end of our calling into the inheritance of God’s children: “that we should be
for the praise of his glory.” Finally, how he describes the Jews who were
admitted to this blessed inheritance earlier: they are those who had first
hoped in Christ, or placed their hope and trust in Christ. These are the five
members which, under divine guidance, must first be explained in order and
then, after gathering certain observations from them, applied more closely to
our use.

[Marginalia: Division.]

As to the first member, the Apostle uses the expression “to be allotted”
metaphorically, but with great emphasis and in a way especially suited to his
purpose: designating a calling that is entirely gratuitous and came to those
called through no merit of their own. Everyone knows that the noun *klēros*,
from which the verb *klēroō* comes, signifies among other things sometimes a
lot, sometimes a part, portion, or inheritance, and sometimes even a ministry,
office, or function that has fallen to someone by lot. The verb *klēroun* in
the active voice means to cast lots or to choose by lot. It is used with
particular propriety of magistrates created by lot. Aristotle throughout the
*Politics* says that such persons are chosen by lot, and calls magistrates of
this kind “allotted,” that is, created and appointed not by election but by
lot—not established from the aristocracy but constituted by lot, as he says
there.

[Marginalia: Member 1. What “to be allotted” means. Aristotle in the
*Politics*. Application.]

Therefore, when the faithful who are called to faith in Christ, whether Jews
or Gentiles, are said here by the Apostle “to be allotted,” this admits at
least two senses and interpretations. First, the faithful themselves have
been admitted passively as God’s allotted possession: that is, they themselves
become his portion and the cord of his inheritance, chosen out of all the
others, as sacred Scripture customarily speaks of the people of Israel in
comparison with the other nations. In this sense the Apostle undoubtedly
alludes to the expression customary among the Prophets, specifically that
notable passage in Moses’ song in Deuteronomy 32: “For the Lord’s portion is
his people,” he says, “Jacob the cord of his possession.” This is the more
proper interpretation of the verb in this passage. Second, they have been
admitted actively into the inheritance, so that they possess that inheritance
of the saints and hold their own part and portion in it. This latter
interpretation depends on the former and is necessarily joined to it. For all
whom God has chosen out of the rest as his portion—that is, his inheritance,
possession, and peculiar treasure—certainly have their part in the lot of the
saints; that is, they necessarily share in his eternal inheritance. Thus they
are said to have been allotted, that is, admitted into that portion and
inheritance of the saints.

[Marginalia: First interpretation. Deuteronomy 32:9. Second interpretation.]

The Apostle often designates the grace of adoption and the heavenly
inheritance by the name “lot” or “portion.” For example, in Colossians 1:
“Giving thanks to the Father, who has qualified us to share in the portion of
the saints.” He immediately explains where this portion is situated when he
adds, “in the light”: both in the light of the gospel, that is, the kingdom of
grace in this life, and especially in heavenly light, that is, the kingdom of
glory in the life to come. In Scripture the heavenly kingdom and glory are
customarily designated by the name of light. In this sense, whenever the
Apostle mentions the portion, or the calling of the faithful that comes as by
lot, he undoubtedly alludes to the portions of the land of Canaan which were
divided by lot among the twelve tribes of Israel. Those typological lots—the
portions of the promised land which fell to each by lot—were so many types and
figures of the true portion and inheritance which came to the elect, the true
Israel of God, through Jesus Christ, the true Joshua. They were admitted to it
as if by lot, through God’s gratuitous and unexpected calling. For the term
“allot,” which the Apostle uses so often in the matter of our calling, and
“lot,” by which he designates that heavenly inheritance, signifies a benefit
that is not only entirely gratuitous with respect to us, toward the obtaining
of which nothing has been brought or contributed on our part, but also one
that came to us as unexpected and unanticipated. The very best lot can fall
not only to those who do not deserve it but also to people who are absent,
indeed asleep and thinking nothing of the kind. Thus the Apostle indicates
that this most blessed fellowship or partnership with Christ and his
inheritance came to the believing Jews.

[Marginalia: Colossians 1:12. It should be noted that there is a metonymy of
the adjunct for the subject, or of the sign for the thing signified, since in
expressions of this kind the lot is taken for the actual thing represented by
such a lot. Note the force of the word.]

There is also another interpretation of Ambrose on this passage. He
understands the word as the lot of the apostolic calling: namely, that the
Apostles, who were the first of the Jews to believe in Christ, were admitted
and chosen by Christ above the rest for this eminent office of apostleship,
and this through no merit of their own but from his mere grace. It is as if
Paul alluded to the account given in Acts 1 concerning the appointment of
another in the place of Judas the traitor. For there the Apostles speak of
Judas in this way: “He was numbered among us and obtained a portion in this
ministry.” Later, in prayer: “You, Lord, knower of hearts, show which of these
two you have chosen to receive the portion of this ministry and apostleship,”
and so forth. This interpretation agrees very well with the nature of the verb
itself, which is used with particular propriety of those who are created by lot
for some office or ministry, such as holding a magistracy, as we taught above.
Yet it seems too restricted to agree with the Apostle’s meaning here, as is
clear to anyone who considers both the reason added from God’s eternal
predestination and the things which precede and follow. It is evident there
that the Apostle speaks of the calling of all the faithful, Jews as well as
Gentiles, into the portion of the saints—that is, fellowship in the heavenly
inheritance—and does not speak of the special calling of the Apostles to some—

[Marginalia: Ambrose’s third interpretation. Acts 1:17 and 25.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 82 | Caput I p. 40]

—particular office. We therefore retain the former interpretation.

The Apostle’s statement that in Christ we have been admitted to this
inheritance has already been repeated several times above, and once again we
briefly impress the same point along with him. All blessings have been placed
and stored by God the Father in Christ as in a treasury. In him is
righteousness; in him redemption; in him forgiveness of sins; in him the
grace of adoption; in him the Spirit of sanctification; in him, to put it in
one word, is that spiritual and eternal life, since by the merit of his blood
and death he obtained everything for us. Therefore, everything we need for
life and salvation the Father has richly stored in him as in a treasury—not,
however, for the purpose that these things should reside in him alone and in
his person. Indeed, many of those benefits do not pertain to him and cannot in
any way belong to him: the forgiveness of sins, the imputation of
righteousness, redemption, reconciliation, adoption, regeneration, and all
the other things he obtained by the merit of his death. What, I ask, has he to
do with redemption, adoption, regeneration, forgiveness of sins, and so
forth? He therefore merited these for us alone, not for himself; by his death
he procured and obtained them for us alone, not for himself. So, to resume what
we began to say, with respect to all these things he is not so much their
steward and distributor—although he is indeed steward and distributor with
respect to his Father and by reason of his office as mediator—as their very
storehouse and repository. They do not remain in him, but flow and are derived
from him as head into all who are implanted in him as true and living members
and joined to him through faith. They flow into them, I say, and are
necessarily communicated to them by the power and efficacy of the Holy Spirit.
Therefore, among other things, they are rightly said to have been admitted or
received in him, through effectual and gratuitous calling, into the portion of
that eternal inheritance. They are admitted in him who alone is the natural
heir of all things; but because we are sons in him and through him, we also are
heirs in him and through him—that is, his coheirs through the grace of
adoption.

[Marginalia: In him. Note carefully.]

Hence it is evident that the right of inheritance, insofar as it is conferred
upon us, always necessarily presupposes the grace of adoption. None are heirs
unless they are first children. In the order of nature, therefore, we are
first admitted into the portion of the children—that is, into the grace of
adoption—and then, by an inseparable consequence, into the portion of the
saints, that is, fellowship in the same inheritance with all the saints. I
believe that this is what the apostolic expression signifies in one word.

[Marginalia: A corollary in passing.]

Let us now proceed to the second member. Here the Apostle again returns to the
fountain of this benefit: that eternal decree of predestination which flowed
from God’s mere purpose and depends upon it. He seems to append mention of it
directly to this allotment of the Jews, that is, their admission to the
inheritance. First, he does so to exclude every merit of theirs from this
matter, lest the Jews attribute anything here to themselves as though they had
obtained this prerogative above the rest by any merit of their own, namely,
that they had been called and admitted first to fellowship in the heavenly
inheritance. Nor were they to attribute it in any degree to the merits, faith,
and piety of their fathers, especially the first patriarchs—something which
they would not have dared to ascribe to themselves, since they were
sufficiently conscious of their own unworthiness and depravity. The Apostle
therefore wishes to show that the benefit is entirely gratuitous. He speaks of
the same thing in 2 Timothy 1: “Who saved us,” he says, “and called us with a
holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and
grace, which was indeed given to us in Christ Jesus before the ages began, but
has now been manifested through the glorious appearing of our Savior Jesus
Christ, who abolished death and brought life and incorruptibility to light
through the gospel.”

[Marginalia: Member 2. Reason 1. 2 Timothy 1:9.]

Second, he does so to restrain the profane curiosity of those who might have
asked not only why the Jews were called to such great grace earlier, but why
they rather than any other nation were called. The obstinacy of the human mind
is always accustomed to speak against the dispensation of divine grace and to
answer back to its counsel, to which by its own nature it cannot submit.
Therefore, to suppress murmurs of this kind, the Apostle does not stop at the
promise made to the fathers—that is, at the gratuitous covenant struck with
Abraham and his seed and afterward repeated to the other patriarchs. That
covenant consequently had to be confirmed first of all with them through
Christ’s advent and to exert its power toward them, according to the remnant
from the election of grace. Rather, he ascends higher, to the fountain of that
promise and covenant: God’s predestination according to his purpose, which he
had purposed within himself, moved or impelled by no cause outside himself.
Consequently, to wish to require and investigate a further and higher cause
of the divine purpose is not only vain but impious, since nothing higher than
it can be conceived.

[Marginalia: Reason 2.]

Finally, because a lot has an uncertain outcome, as Chrysostom says, and
things that come by lot seem to happen by chance, immediately after mentioning
the inheritance with the preceding word, the Apostle very deliberately added
mention of the divine decree and purpose so that no one would think mere lot
or chance ruled here. Although this portion came to them from God when they
neither hoped for it nor merited anything of the kind—so that they became the
people of God, believed in Christ first, and were called to the heavenly
inheritance—nevertheless, on the part of God who called and received them,
nothing here was done randomly or by chance, but by his settled counsel,
according to the purpose which he had purposed within himself from eternity.
Indeed, with respect to ourselves it could be said that we stumbled as if by
chance upon that immense and ineffable grace as an unexpected windfall. Yet as
soon as we lift the eyes of our minds to our God and his fatherly care and
providence, we know certainly, believe, and confess with grateful hearts that
it flowed to us from his eternal decree and immutable purpose. God willing, we
will now consider from the words that follow what kind of purpose this was.

[Marginalia: Reason 3. Note carefully.]

Let us now consider in the third place that third member proposed to us at the
beginning. By it he describes with a notable periphrasis God the Father, by
whom he had said the Jewish believers were predestined: “the one who works all
things, from the counsel of his will.” These words certainly must not, as they
say, be passed over with a dry foot. First, they contain a reason for the
preceding assertion concerning the calling of the Jews, drawn from the genus
itself, as they say—that is, from a maxim or general proposition which
contains under it as an inferior species the conclusion to be proved—in this
way: God effectually works all things from the counsel of his will; therefore,
it is no wonder if from the same counsel or purpose he calls whomever he calls,
and—

[Marginalia: Member 3. Note 1. Argument from the genus itself.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 83 | Caput I p. 41]

—from the same purpose admitted the Jews into the portion of his children
before the Gentiles. For if God works all things in this way, then especially
the things which concern the care, government, gathering, and preservation of
his Church, since he exercises particular care for it above all the other
parts and created things of the world. Second, from these same words we learn
more clearly what that purpose of God just mentioned is: nothing other than
“the counsel of his will.” From it he wills all his works without exception to
depend and flow.

[Marginalia: Note 2. Exegesis.]

By these words two things in particular are ascribed to our God—as principles
of all his works and actions which are said to be external—and are common to
all three persons of the Holy Trinity. First, supreme wisdom, signified by the
word *boulē*, or counsel. Second, supreme freedom, and so supreme power and the
highest authority in acting, signified and conveyed by the word “will.” For he
who does all things from his own will is certainly supremely free and not
answerable—that is, not bound to give anyone a reason for his deeds, subject
to no necessity, and therefore endowed with the highest right, supreme
authority, and fullness of power over all things. To say that God works all
things from the counsel of his will is therefore to assert that he does all
things with supreme wisdom, supreme freedom, and from his full and absolute
power over every creature. This manner of acting belongs to God alone and
cannot belong to any creature. Consequently, as to the very manner of working,
this phrase conveys the excellence and most absolute perfection of the divine
actions.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the exegesis.]

The very position and order of these words further suggests this excellence
of acting to us. We will therefore bring it somewhat more precisely into the
light. The Apostle does not say “from the will of his counsel” but “from the
counsel of his will,” and the distinction between these two must be noted in
passing. When “counsel” is grammatically subordinated to “will,” in the phrase
“from the will of counsel,” we understand that counsel—that is,
deliberation—came first, and only afterward followed the will and choice by
which a person wills and selects above the rest the things which, after taking
counsel, had seemed best and preferable to the others. Prudent people conduct
their actions in this way, especially the weightier ones and those of some
importance: deliberation comes first, by which they investigate what is best
to do; then from the counsel taken arises the will by which they wish and
decide to do what they judged best after deliberation. But to do something
solely “from the counsel of the will,” where “will” is grammatically
subordinated to “counsel,” indicates that the will alone of the one who works
in this manner stands in the place of counsel and performs the function of
counsel. For him, to consult or deliberate about anything is nothing other
than simply to will. His will alone contains in itself all wisdom, holiness,
justice, and rectitude; it alone is the rule of all justice and wisdom and has
no need of any deliberation. Indeed, properly speaking, he cannot deliberate
about things to be done, because deliberation carries with it some ignorance,
some depravity of the human will, and some weakness of the human mind, which
generally cannot foresee and attain things agreeable to right reason and
suitable to its end unless counsel is taken. Consultation is therefore used
as a guard and precaution against error. This cannot befall an omniscient God
who is utterly incapable of error in acting, nor can it be attributed to him
except by speaking of him in human terms.

[Marginalia: Elaboration on the counsel of God’s will. Response.]

Therefore, the former manner of speaking can also apply to wise or prudent
human beings: namely, to plan and carry out what they must do “from the will of
their counsel,” or, as the Romans used to say while their commonwealth was
still free, *D.S.C.*, by decree of the senate. Otherwise, that saying belongs
to tyrants and to foolish and wicked people:

Thus I will, thus I command: let my will stand in place of reason.

It is a saying full of audacity, pride, recklessness, and indeed folly. But the
latter expression, which the Apostle uses—“to do all things from the counsel
of his will”—can be rightly used only of one whose will is the reason and rule
of all wisdom and justice, indeed the supreme reason and standard, by which
all holiness and justice, wisdom and rectitude are measured. All actions are
ultimately resolved into his will as the supreme cause; to wish to require or
investigate any higher cause beyond it would be no less impiously curious than
empty and foolish. Thus this manner of acting can be attributed to God most
good and great alone, and to no creature. The Apostle therefore said very
deliberately here that he alone is the one who works all things from the
counsel of his will, in order to indicate his supreme excellence and
preeminence in acting and his utterly divine manner of action.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the elaboration.]

But before we leave this notable passage and pass to the next verse, one
difficulty still presents itself for resolution concerning the true and
genuine sense of these words in which the Apostle says that God does all
things, and so forth. Thus far we have seen the manner of acting which is
signified by “the counsel of the divine will.” We must next see the very things
which God effects and works from the counsel of his will. The Apostle expresses
these by the words “all things.” What, then, are these things designated for
us here by the name “all things”? I see that not everyone holds the same
opinion on the question. Some, after weighing the emphasis and force of the
article, render *ta panta* not simply “all things” but “all these things” about
which the Apostle was speaking—that is, everything that pertains to our
salvation. In this passage he discusses those things alone: the elect to
eternal life and salvation, and God’s benefits conferred upon them in Christ
from mere grace. It is clear that these have been his subject from the
beginning of this chapter. This is the most restrictive interpretation of all.

[Marginalia: A difficulty to note. “All things.” First interpretation.
Piscator.]

The second interpretation, held by Jerome and very many more recent writers
and also approved by Bellarmine in book 2, chapter 15, *On the Loss of Grace
and the State of Sin*, is that God does not work from the counsel of his will
absolutely all things which happen in the world. For then, they say, it would
follow that he works both evil and good, an absurd and impious assertion.
Rather, the Apostle’s meaning is that whatever God most good and great does,
he does not rashly or unwillingly but freely and prudently. This, they say, is
to work according to the counsel of his will. In the same way prudent men are
said to do all things deliberately—not because they do absolutely everything,
but because whatever they do, they do prudently and with counsel. Thus
Bellarmine quotes Jerome’s opinion on this passage, saying that Jerome wrote
as though he had foreseen Beza’s commentary:—

[Marginalia: Second interpretation. Bellarmine. Instructive example.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 84 | Caput I p. 42]

—“God works all things according to the counsel of his will, not because all
things which happen in the world are carried out by God’s will and counsel,
for otherwise even evil things could be imputed to God, but because everything
he does, he does with counsel and will, since the one who acts is full of
reason and power.”

But certainly—whatever is finally to be determined concerning the passage,
whether we extend it according to the Apostle’s meaning to absolutely
everything that happens in the world, or with some restrict it to the things
he works effectually in the elect and faithful by the grace of his
Spirit—Bellarmine himself answers in the following words the argument of
Bellarmine, or rather of Jerome, drawn from sin and the evil actions of wicked
people. “Although,” he says, “we grant Beza that these words of the Apostle
signify that absolutely everything which happens in the world is done by God,
it would not therefore follow that sins done by human beings are also done by
God; for to commit sin is not to act but to fail. Nor is sin some thing, but
the corruption of something.” He asserts that he both taught before and will
teach afterward how God does whatever of reality and goodness exists in an
evil action and yet is not the author of the corruption and defect discerned
in that action.

[Marginalia: Answer.]

From these words of Bellarmine we clearly infer two things. First, by his own
judgment the argument from evil actions can easily be answered. He admits that
it does not follow that God commits sin or is the author of sin, even if we say
that everything which happens in the world is done by God—that is, that God
works all things absolutely and without restriction. On this side, therefore,
nothing prevents us from extending these words of the Apostle to absolutely
everything which happens in the world, provided that we preserve the
distinction among actions and observe the difference between the ways in which
God works something through the reprobate, executing the secret decree of his
providence, and in the elect through the grace of his Spirit. This distinction
and difference must be maintained in this doctrine. It is therefore evident
that they bring forward in bad faith and with a bad conscience arguments which
seem to oppose an opinion or interpretation of ours, though they themselves
know the solution very well.

[Marginalia: First corollary.]

Second, everyone can see from this, unless I am mistaken, how shamelessly and
maliciously this sophist attributes to Beza and Calvin the impiety of making
God the author of sin. Bellarmine takes it as confessed that this was their
opinion when he says, “Let us grant that this was the Apostle’s meaning; yet it
would not therefore follow that sins committed by human beings are also done
by God.” It is as though he said, “As you wish it, Beza, and as your opinion
holds.” But what is it to be a mere slanderer if this is not? Did either Beza
of blessed memory, Calvin, or any orthodox writer assert anything but this
very thing? The action itself, insofar as it is a thing, a motion or action,
and consequently something good, is also from God as first cause and author,
“for in him we live, and move, and have our being,” as the Apostle says in Acts
17. He is the author of every good and therefore of all things insofar as they
are things and consequently possess the character of some good. But the
lawlessness, depravity, evil, deformity, crookedness, and departure of an evil
action from God’s law—which is the rule of all that is right and the standard
of both straight and crooked—is not from God, but from the perverse and
corrupt instruments themselves. Therefore, since the very nature and form of
sin, so to speak—for what, properly speaking, can be the form of a
deformity?—that is, what they call its formal element, consists in that
lawlessness and depravity of which God, as supremely good, can in no way be the
author, it follows that God is not thereby made the author of sin itself when
he is said to work everything that happens in the world. We nevertheless
confess that he works something through the devil and wicked people in a very
different manner from that in which he exercises his efficacy in the hearts of
the faithful, and that the power of the first cause which sustains, moves, and
directs all things concurs differently in the actions of the one group and the
other.

[Marginalia: Corollary 2. Rebuttal of the slander. Acts 17:28.]

If it is further urged that the things which happen from the counsel of the
divine will are good only, and therefore this saying must be understood not so
much of evil actions as of good ones, nor extended absolutely to all things,
but restricted to the things which happen in the world in such a way that they
are approved by God as conformable to his revealed will and law, the answer is
readily at hand. Absolutely nothing happens in the world except from the
counsel of the divine will—that is, from God’s eternal decree. Far be it from
us to introduce into the world such disorder and anarchy, indeed sheer
atheism, as to say that anything is done or happens in the world without any
decree of God—that is, anything which God did not in any way determine should
be done and happen in the world in his eternal counsel, which extends to
absolutely all things and embraces all things as widely as providence itself
extends. We nevertheless confess that he did not decree all things in the same
manner. He determined to permit some things, such as the fall of human beings
and reprobate angels, but to effect other things: either by effectual
providence, as all things insofar as they are things and possess the character
of good, or by the grace of the Holy Spirit, as the salvation of the elect.

[Marginalia: Objection. Answer.]

Let this, then, remain firm and unshaken: everything that happens in the world
comes about from the counsel of the divine will; and everything done by him,
insofar as it is such and is referred to God himself, possesses the character
of good. Yet nothing prevents these things, insofar as they are done by evil
instruments—that is, considered in respect of their lawlessness and evil which
departs from God’s law—from being evil and impious and worthy of eternal
punishment.

Here above all the goodness and wisdom of the divine counsel shines forth. It
not only directs in the best way and disposes toward its own end the things
which are in themselves and by their nature wholly right, good, and holy—a
task of lesser skill and labor—but also directs the very darkness of human
beings, that is, their worst actions, toward light: it bends and converts them
to his own glory and the salvation of his people. Thus the worst works of
human beings, insofar not as they are from human beings but as they are
permitted, decreed, directed, and ordered by God, are changed by this
incomprehensible goodness and wisdom into glorious means of displaying the
divine glory.

[Marginalia: A serious note.]

Now, therefore, to conclude this passage, I leave it to the judgment of the
student of theology to embrace either interpretation. He may prefer to
restrict this statement of the Apostle to the things which concern the
salvation of the faithful and consequently interpret *ta panta* as “all these
things,” namely, those things about which the Apostle was speaking, an
interpretation toward—

—which the word “working” seems to invite us, since later in this same chapter
the Apostle will use it of the efficacy of God’s Spirit in Christ and the
faithful. Or perhaps it seems better to extend the statement to everything to
which the counsel of the divine will—that is, God’s providence and decree—
extends: namely, to absolutely everything which happens in the world insofar
as it possesses the character of good or of being, but not insofar as it
assumes the name, form, and character of sin on account of the lawlessness
arising from secondary causes and evil instruments. In this way the Apostle’s
statement and reasoning seem to have greater weight and importance. For my own
part, if a choice must be made, I would gladly come down in favor of this
latter opinion, so that by this general and notable assertion of the Apostle
the efficacy of divine providence extending to all things may be defended and
illustrated more fully. Yet in passages of this kind I would rather remain in
doubt for a while without blame than form a judgment with any danger to the
truth.
