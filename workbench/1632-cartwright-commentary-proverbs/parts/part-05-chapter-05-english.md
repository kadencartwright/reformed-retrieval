CHAPTER FIVE.

Verse 1. My son, attend to my wisdom; incline your ear to my understanding.

Verse 2. That you may observe every kind of prudence, and that your lips may
guard knowledge.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 49 | printed columns 72–73]

Adapting his general doctrine concerning the study of divine wisdom to the
particular case of the seventh commandment, he surveys in it the advantages
and disadvantages both of the present life and of the future. From verse 1 to
verse 7, in exhorting to the study and practice of wisdom, he teaches that its
use is exceedingly fruitful in avoiding the precipices of the prostitute.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - an exhortation to the study and practice of wisdom;
  - a reason drawn from the accompanying danger posed by an impure prostitute.

In the former, besides the things that have been repeated more often, it must
be observed that the study of true wisdom makes a person prudent in every
matter and cautious against every danger, lest he be ensnared by the deceitful
traps of Satan and his attendants. Thus in Psalm 119 David, through the study
of true wisdom, surpassed princes, elders, and teachers in prudence. Hence Saul
pronounced him subtle. Other counsels, however ingenious, are very bad for the
one who consults them; these alone, which godliness supplies, rescue their
masters.

Next he wishes knowledge of these things to be kept upon the lips; that is, to
be so loved that through frequent conversation and speech about them they may
come to our aid opportunely and at the right time. For just as the lips of
priests preserve knowledge because they instruct others, so the lips of each
person ought to preserve that knowledge by which he may deliver himself and
his people from error. For this reason we are commanded to conduct
conversations about sacred things on the road and at home.

Verse 3. Although the lips of the strange woman drip honeycomb, and her palate
drips more softly than oil;

Verse 4. Nevertheless, her latter end is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a
two-edged sword.

There follows a reason drawn from the accompanying danger against which wisdom
will fortify a person. Here it must first be observed that neither stricter
education, nor austerity of life, nor the pursuit of solitude and cloisters
can rescue us from the infection and snares of prostitutes apart from the
study of sacred Scripture and divine truth. Honorable and legitimate methods
both of education and of fasting are indeed not to be despised; but their
reins are too loose to restrain the wild disposition of man. By the fear of
God alone, as by a bridle and curb, are untamed lusts so constrained that they
cannot stir themselves. For true knowledge of God is like an antidote and an
amulet by which we are kept immune from this infection.

Next it must be observed that the words which the Spirit supplies from the
study of sacred Scripture provide medicine against the flattering and
seductive little words of the prostitute. However shrewd someone may otherwise
be, however strong in eloquence, unless true knowledge of God shapes and forms
his tongue, it is all over. Many, trusting in their intellect and speech, dare
to converse with prostitutes and do not doubt that they can easily avoid the
darts she casts at them by what seems a slight movement of the body. But they
are very greatly deceived. For a prostitute’s words are honeyed, and with the
choicest honey, which drips spontaneously from the comb (in English, *life
hony*). Therefore the word of God is needed, which is sweeter than honey and
the honeycomb, so that we may avoid that evil and become weary of her
sweetness in comparison with that which is found in the word of God. For just
as all things are sordid in comparison with Christ and must be counted as
dung, so the prostitute’s words are wholly tasteless in comparison with God’s
speech. The prostitute’s words are sweet only in opinion, whereas God’s words
are most pleasant in themselves and by their own nature. Moreover, the
prostitute’s words are smoother than oil: they appear to have no harshness in
them, nothing to offend or cause trouble, but rather something to soothe and
soften. Therefore the word of God is needed,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 50 | printed columns 74–75]

so that we may see and avoid these deceits. David says that he would have
perished had God’s word not sustained him, Psalm 119. See an excellent example
of this in Genesis 39:8. By contrast, when the word of God had fallen out of
Solomon’s mouth, he was immediately conquered by women. This also happened to
Samson.

But what she and her honeyed words bring is bitter as wormwood and sharp as a
two-edged sword. This double comparison suggests both grief and the difficulty
of a cure. For he opposes wormwood to honey dripping from the comb and a
two-edged sword to oil. Thus many poisons are sweet when drunk but create
intolerable pains in the stomach. The brief and momentary taste indeed flies
away, but punishment and its sting remain, as though enduring in the stomach
of the conscience.

Verse 5. Her feet descend to death; her steps reach the grave.

Her feet, or her ways—that is, her pursuits and endeavors—descend, or plunge
down, as those who travel from a mountain into a valley, to present death. And
so all who cling to her hasten with her toward death. Her tracks reach the
grave, so that there is no space between the grave and her pursuits. This is
not to be understood only of the death which by God’s law is owed to all
adulterers, but also of the fact that God, even when the magistrate does
nothing, afflicts them with lethal diseases by which they die.

Verse 6. Do not weigh the path of life; her tracks wander without your knowing.

By this verse he indicates that the prostitute’s disposition is so variable
and changeable, and that she transforms herself like Proteus into every shape
and form, that if you should wish to weigh her ways on a balance and bring her
manner of life back to the calculation of reason, you would waste your effort.
For she so twists herself and her ways that all your labor in this respect
will be fruitless. See Proverbs 30:18–19. Therefore it is useful never even to
take counsel with yourself about embracing her, but immediately to turn from
her, abhor her, and not admit even the slightest interaction a single time.
An excellent example appears in Joseph, who immediately withdrew from
conversation with her after her first appeal.

Verse 7. Now therefore, sons, listen to me, and do not depart from the words of
my mouth.

Having exhorted his hearers to obey his doctrine by fleeing the prostitute, he
pursues more particularly through verse 15 the reasons for the exhortation
which he had earlier touched upon in general.

[Analytical diagram]

- Its parts are:
  - an exhortation to obey his doctrine by fleeing the prostitute;
  - a more particular explanation of the foregoing reasons.

In the former it must be observed—as was said above—that we must repeatedly be
aroused to attention, which otherwise cools.

[Analytical diagram]

- The exhortation is:
  - general;
  - particular.

The general exhortation is that the prophet must be obeyed. This is clarified
by negating its contrary: one must not depart from the words of his mouth. For
it will not have been enough to hear, understand, and remember unless we also
express the words in deeds.

Verse 8. Make your path far from her, and do not approach the door of her house.

He now teaches what the words are from which one must not depart even the
breadth of a fingernail.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 51 | printed columns 76–77]

First, one must move himself and his ways far away from the prostitute, so
that he neither touches nor looks at her, nor converses with her, nor receives
any messenger from her, nor sends anyone to her. For straw or a lamp-wick
easily catches fire. We must therefore think of her as though infected with
leprosy. Just as it was not lawful for a clean person to approach a leper, but
he addressed him from the greatest distance possible, so the student of
spiritual purity must not mix in any matter with her; indeed, he ought not
even in thought to come near her.

What follows contains the same matter, but not without an increase, for he
forbids approaching the doorway of her house. Her contagion is so great that
she makes the very house pestilential, as it were. See the notes above on 4:15.
The same is intended by 2:18, where such houses are compared to ruinous
buildings which threaten a collapse by which anyone who approaches will be
crushed.

Verse 9. Lest you give your honor to others, and your years to the cruel one.

There follows the other part, a more particular explanation of the foregoing
reasons. Here first are enumerated the outward disadvantages which befall him
in verses 9 and 10, and then the pangs of mind arising from those disadvantages
in the remaining verses. In the former part first comes the loss and forfeiture
of his honor and dignity through adultery, for this sin brings contempt. This
is taught by an argument from the lesser, Proverbs 6:30, 33. Moreover, if a
mark of infamy is cast upon the one who suffers this wrong, how much more upon
the one who commits it. To this seems referable the apostle’s statement that
the one who fornicates sins against his own body, while other sins are outside
the body. Evidence of this is sought from David, who, immediately after he had
violated Uriah’s wife, began to be despised by his own sons and servants; and
also from Solomon, against whom his own servant Jeroboam rose and treated him,
as it were, with kicks. Therefore, when you devote yourself to adultery, it is
as though, taking the crown from your head, you handed it to others inferior
to you and removed the bridle or curb from the mouths of those who otherwise
would reverence you.

Second, he teaches that in this way your years, or your life, are exposed to
the wrath of the cruel avenger of the adultery you committed, who will avenge
that injury by shedding your blood. See Proverbs 6:26, 34–35. See also the
history of Jacob’s sons, who avenged the violation of their sister with the
slaughter of an entire city. This also often occurs through rivals, sometimes
even through the prostitute herself, who kills her lovers by poison; and it
ought to be carried out by the magistrate. How foolish, therefore, to betray
one’s life for the sake of a brief little pleasure! David refused to drink the
water of Bethlehem because it had been acquired at the peril of his servants;
how much less ought you to pursue these stolen waters at the peril not of
another’s blood but of your own!

Verse 10. Lest strangers be filled with your wealth, and your labors remain in
the house of a foreigner.

The third disadvantage is the loss of wealth. This is also amplified by the
accompanying circumstances: the acquisition of the goods, your strength and
grief, and the foreign person to whom they will be transferred. See 6:26 and
Luke 13:30. It is altogether well known that anyone who devotes himself to
supporting prostitutes incurs the greatest expenses. For a prostitute, like a
chasm, whirlpool, or grave, is never satisfied. She neither sees nor desires
anything which she does not immediately demand, and nothing is denied to her
when she demands it. Many who are otherwise rather attentive to property are
found to be exceedingly lavish toward prostitutes. Madams and pimps also make
profit from it; maidservants too, and others to whom knowledge of the affair
will come, must be fed with gifts and bribes.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 52 | printed columns 78–79]

See an example of this in Solomon, who, though the richest of all men, was so
drained by the women whom he foolishly loved that he was compelled to burden
his subjects with excessive taxation. From this it is clear how greatly those
men deceive themselves who, in order to become rich more easily and preserve
their household property, pursue prostitution rather than marriage. Next one
must note the foolishness of people who, for the momentary satisfaction of
lust, squander the wealth they acquired with great labor and grief.
They spend it upon people who will return no gratitude to them, while passing
over their relatives or even their children.

Verse 11. And at last you will roar when your flesh and your body have been
consumed.

The outward disadvantages have been treated; anguish of mind follows. This is
marked by its effect, a cry, which is illustrated by a metaphor drawn from the
roaring of lions or of the sea, whose sound is most fearsome. He will not
merely groan or sigh; he will not merely weep or lament, but like a lion or the
sea will shout aloud because of pain and torment, so that he seems in some way
to have reached the vestibule of hell. Here we see that the pleasure which
adulterers take in their shame does not equal even one hundredth part of the
pain they often experience in this age. It is therefore no wonder if the
adulteress’s end is compared to wormwood and a two-edged sword.

Next in this passage is added the fourth disadvantage which commonly follows
these men: the wasting of flesh and body. Even if a person escapes death
inflicted by the magistrate or some other avenger—something not imposed on
fornicators even by the law—this wasting nevertheless hands over to old age a
feeble and exhausted body, if he reaches old age at all. For many kinds of
disease, such as the Neapolitan disease and other most shameful diseases of
that sort, take their origin from this.

Verse 12. And you say, “How I hated instruction, and my soul despised
correction!”

Verse 13. “I did not listen to the voice of my teachers, nor incline my ear to
those teaching me.”

In these verses and the following one is contained the vain and useless
complaint of the adulterer or fornicator, which the disadvantages brought upon
him by prostitution extort and force out. To affect the godly more powerfully,
it is presented in the person of the fornicator himself. He had
previously cared little if someone called him back to a more exact observance
of the commandments and pressed him with the goads of exhortation to live
honorably. Now, by contrast, he is astonished that he was so insane as to hate
the doctrine concerning flight from the prostitute and despise it in his soul,
however much he had not dared for shame to express this in words. Something
similar preceded at 1:28.

It must be observed that a time commonly comes when the lustful are compelled
to confess their very great guilt. For such is the power of truth that even
enemies are not only convicted in conscience but are often compelled to bear
witness to it. Those who did not yield when reasons
were presented nevertheless confess the matter as it is when punishments are
brought near and imposed to sting and press them. See Pharaoh and Judas. We
see the same thing done by robbers about to be fastened to the cross, who then
at last confess and openly proclaim their stubbornness toward their parents,
their adulteries, and so forth. But the godly ought especially to be led to
hatred of sin by the word and the word’s persuasion.

It must next be observed how, not without astonishment,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 53 | printed columns 80–81]

they confess their crimes, as though someone said of himself, “How brutish and
alien to common sense I was! How exceedingly foolish, for I could neither
advise myself nor obey those who advised me well!” This indeed presents a
great appearance of repentance, although we see that it is set forth here
under the name of punishment, and a very severe one. Repentance, however, is
not a punishment of sin but an excellent gift of God proceeding from faith in
Christ. For it would be a statement very foreign to the Holy Spirit to say,
“If you commit adultery, at last you will be led to true and saving
repentance.” Here, therefore, we must observe how disgracefully they err who,
from a sinner’s few words condemning himself and criticizing his former life
before everyone, immediately conclude that he has repented. See the notes
above on 1:28, “Then they will call upon me,” and so forth. They not only call
upon God themselves, but invite others to call upon God for them, Acts 5,
although they remain very far from true and saving repentance.

It must further be observed that the cause of the sin was that he hated rebuke
and correction in his soul, although at the time he did not dare to profess it
openly. A person would have to possess abandoned audacity to stand openly as a
patron of adultery. It is therefore not enough to exempt anyone from the
number of these reprobates that he openly honors chastity and an honorable
life in words, while in the meantime he does not submit to instruction and
correction.

In verse 13 he proves the hatred and contempt from the accompanying refusal to
hear those whose duty it is to instruct, whether at home or outside, privately
or publicly. By this he indicates that medicine for this evil must be sought
from the preaching of the word, and specifically from those whom God places
over us as instructors.

Verse 14. I was almost in every evil in the midst of the congregation and
assembly.

To the vain and fruitless confession which he makes concerning his sin, he
adds confession of the punishment and calamity springing from it. First, he
affirms that he has been involved in almost every evil. He magnifies its
severity from the place joined to it, namely, the church, in which many who
have been made blessed by embracing doctrine behold and gaze upon his misery,
and in which he himself could have been fortified against these evils.

Third, he argues from witnesses, employing a particle of undoubted assertion
to confirm the entire matter. In the first point it must be observed that
deplorable sinners in this life are often very nearly standing, as it were, on
the threshold and track of hell because of the severe torments they endure.
The lust which was once a delight to the soul is now like a fracture of the
heart. What was once pleasant in use is now more bitter than wormwood even in
mere recollection. Finally, the person who earlier laughed at threats uttered
against shamefulness now confesses that he suffers more than he actually does,
crying that he is being exercised by nearly the gravest torments, although he
does not yet feel one hundredth part of what awaits him after death. Second,
the misery is amplified because he contracted it in such a place. For the
church of God is the kingdom of heaven; therefore, to live in heaven and yet
be as though in hell is grievous. Next, to be where salvation overflows yet be
unable even to taste it is most miserable. Finally, to be surrounded by a
circle of the godly who will witness his condition is lamentable. Thus the
wicked in Job are said to be crushed in the gate. And this is often what
torments criminals most of all: so many witnesses of their calamity are
admitted. In this way the punishment of the damned is magnified in Revelation
14, because their smoke rises before the angels and before the Lamb.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 54 | printed columns 82–83]

Finally, it is clear that the wicked who once denied all credence to God’s
threats and refused to believe anything announced to them afterward change
their judgment and confess the truth of all the threats. Thus the wicked often
believe to their destruction what they earlier refused to believe for their
caution and salvation. Those who earlier believed less than was right
afterward believe more than is right, for they are often brought to believe
that their wound can never be healed, although repentance would heal it.

Verse 15. Drink water from your cistern, and streams from the midst of your
well.

Prescribing a remedy against the foregoing evil of shameful lust, he commands
each person to have his own wife and sets forth through verse 20 the excellent
fruits of the commandment.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - the command to obtain one’s own wife, verse 15;
  - the excellent fruits of lawful marriage in the remaining verses.

The comparison is elegant and fitting. Since the fire of lust burns a person,
water is needed to extinguish the blaze; and since heat produces thirst, he
most fittingly compares the remedy to a cistern and well. Just as everyone
desires a spring inside his own premises for every domestic use rather than
seeking water outdoors and elsewhere, so it is worthwhile to have your own
wife, from whom you may seek a remedy for the evils with which lust assails
you. When he adds flowing, living waters and says that clear water must be
sought from one’s own well, he suggests that the remedy sought from
prostitutes is like muddy, turbid water trampled by many feet, and therefore
does not quench thirst as conveniently and wholesomely. This is what the
apostle urges: to avoid fornication, let each woman have her own husband and
each man his wife. This convicts those who repeat a proverb in defense of
prostitution: calves which wander through various meadows are fatter than
those tied to one. Yes, they are fattened earlier for the day of slaughter!
But have we not learned that strength is exhausted and health undermined? Is
it not far better to drink from one’s own cistern than another’s, from flowing
rather than stagnant water, from clear rather than muddy water? How absurd are
those who, to save expense and be thrifty, flee to a prostitute has been said
before.

This also argues against the papists, who establish brothels—as they say—in
the church of God, although God’s law does not permit a prostitute in Israel.
They also so highly proclaim their priests’ celibacy that they utterly condemn
their marriage.

Verse 16. Let your springs spread abroad, streams of waters in the streets.

There was the command; the fruit of obedience follows. The first is
*polyteknia*, abundance of children. Children are like springs and streams
flowing from the fountain of a lawful wife. Fruitfulness especially accompanies
lawful marriage. Therefore a lawful wife is compared to a vine planted by the
sides of the house, Psalm 128:3, which prospers more happily both from continual
inspection and from the sun’s reflection off the wall. First, moderate use of
intercourse is suited to secure fruitfulness, whereas wandering and immoderate
intercourse greatly hinders it. Next, since children come from God, Psalm
127:3, who doubts that God will bestow them upon lawful

[1632 witness | PDF p. 55 | printed columns 84–85]

spouses rather than adulterers? Although a godly wife is sometimes barren and
a prostitute, especially a young one, bears a child, it nevertheless remains
undoubted that marriage obtains the blessing of posterity for the most part
(ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ). Although prostitutes have children, those children are not
long-lived; or if long-lived, they are nevertheless wicked. Here Solomon
supplies a memorable example: though he had so many wives, his posterity was
extinguished in Coniah. See Ahab’s seventy sons. By contrast, lawful and
genuine children are compared to olive plants which flourish and grow. To this
is added that nearly all the wicked perish by hanging and other punishments of
infamous people; finally, they are commonly dishonored and so, while they
live, are as though dead.

Verse 17. Let them be yours alone, and not shared with strangers.

He notes another advantage of lawful marriage: a man will have his own
children, whereas adulterers’ children are called children of the people.
Those born from a prostitute belong partly to this man and partly to that, as
though the foot belonged to one, the hand to another, the shin to one, and the
arm to another. First, it will always be doubtful whether they are his own;
besides the fact that no conscience binds a prostitute to speak truthfully in
this matter, she herself does not always know whose the child is. Next,
although a child is conceived by one act of intercourse, those who have
intercourse with the prostitute after conception are one flesh with her.
Hence it disgracefully happens that there are in some manner as many fathers
as adulterers. For this reason it was always judged dishonorable if a widow
pregnant by her deceased husband married another before giving birth. It is
counted an advantage if a man has his estate as his own possession, separated
from others’ estates; how much more useful to have so precious an inheritance
of posterity as his alone! This is clearer if one considers the weak and slack
mutual affections between illegitimate children and their parents. As commonly
happens, things shared by many receive less care than things belonging to one.

Verse 18. Your fountain will be blessed; therefore rejoice with the wife of
your youth.

What he earlier said elegantly but rather obscurely in allegorical speech, he
now explains plainly, saying that a man should rejoice with the wife he married
when young. What he earlier said, “Let your springs spread,” and so forth, he
now expounds by saying, “Your stream,” or your spring, “will be blessed.” He
teaches that God’s blessing is summoned into marriage if a man rests content
with his own wife and delights in her. If the wife is not fruitful, she is
immediately accused as barren, while the husband does not consider whether he
himself, having been an adulterer or fornicator, brought this curse into his
family. However righteous a husband may be within marriage, if his wife is not
fruitful, he must not immediately suppose he has permission to flee to
wandering lusts. See Zechariah’s example. Nor should he seize upon Abraham’s.
First, there is no similarity; next, Abraham acted at his wife’s instigation;
finally, this displeased God, who duly avenged it.

Verse 19. Let her be a most beloved deer and a graceful wild goat; let her
breasts satisfy you at every time; wander continually in her love.

He illustrates what he said with two apt comparisons: a stag which ardently
loves a doe, and a male goat which ardently loves and pursues a she-goat,
wandering here and there. By this comparison the church, Christ’s bride, marks
his intimate love toward her. Thus Solomon adorns the chaste yet intense love
which ought to exist in marriage and keep the husband wholly in love with his
wife. At the same time he sends adulterers to the school of beasts, to shame
those who in this respect are worse and more intemperate than beasts.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 56 | printed columns 86–87]

When he commands the man to wander in his own wife, he alludes to the custom
of those wild animals. He does not approve intemperance in marriage, but by
this manner of speech grants a freer use within marriage, since there is a
certain chastity even in marriage, as the apostle speaks. As moderation is
commanded in food, so also in intercourse, which ought not occur without
prayers.

When he says, “Let her breasts delight and intoxicate you at every time,” he
seems to mark a distinction between intercourse with a wife and with a
prostitute: a man can use a prostitute only secretly or by turns, because he
does not possess authority over her. Yet this must not be taken as though it
were always lawful to use one’s wife, including when she is in the customary
condition of women.

Verse 20. And why, my son, would you wander with a strange woman and embrace
the breast of a foreign woman?

In the rest of this chapter, concluding from the preceding things that there
is no cause for adultery, not even the slightest, he presents certain
anticipated objections and answers them abundantly.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - a conclusion from the preceding things, removing every hope of benefit
    from the adulterer;
  - further confirmation of the same conclusion, answering subsequent
    objections.

Since adultery is attended by so many disadvantages, and marriage by so many
advantages and defenses of life, what madness, my son, can entice or invite
you to a prostitute’s embraces? If there were nothing evil in adultery,
nevertheless, since so many advantages are found in marriage, desire for them
ought to keep you free and untouched by adultery so you may enjoy them. If
there were nothing good in marriage, the evils following adultery ought to
deter you from it. But since so great a heap of good things exists in marriage
and of evil things in adultery, no one except a madman will reject marriage
and devote himself to adultery. Everyone sets before himself something
good—useful, pleasant, or honorable. Here there is nothing but shame and
shipwreck of wealth. Although it may seem pleasant for a moment, afterward it
is bitter as wormwood; though it appears smooth as oil, afterward it pricks and
cuts like a two-edged sword. How foolish to pursue this pleasure while passing
by one joined to no bitterness!

Verse 21. Since each person’s ways are before the eyes of Jehovah, and he
weighs all his paths.

He further confirms the same thing with general arguments by which not only
this sin but all others are beaten back and broken. For to be exposed to God’s
eyes and to drag the sinner to eternal death belong to every sin. Here is
something most worthy of observation: two kinds of missiles are required to
defeat sin. The first is particular, taken from the very inward parts of the
particular sin; the other is general, deduced from the general nature of sin.
Just as in curing a more serious disease not only particular medicines which
attack it directly are applied, but also general ones, such as purging the
whole body,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 57 | printed columns 88–89 | ends above the ruled beginning of Chapter VI]

opening a vein, and so forth, the same must be done in spiritual disease. The
evil of sin is so fierce and swollen that, unless pressed down and restrained
by weights of every kind, it will burst out completely. This must especially
be done with sins which cling to us by natural impulse or by pursuit and
custom. Among these is adultery, toward which depraved nature inclines us.

Next he answers a tacit objection. It may enter the adulterer’s mind that he
will conduct his affairs so secretly and cautiously that they will never
become public. Suppose, he says, that you have escaped human eyes; what profit
do you gain when God’s ever-watchful eye gazes upon you? You seek night and
dark places, but to God darkness is as light, Psalm 139. See Zechariah 4:10 and
Jeremiah 23:24. To keep people in their duty, pagan nations admonished each
person always to set before his eyes some grave and honored man—Cato, Socrates,
or another—whose fear and reverence would restrain him from wickedness. How
much more ought meditation upon the most watchful and holy divine presence
prevail among us! If we dread a prince’s gaze, how much more that of the King
of kings. Nor do you escape the eyes of the angels, who behold nearly all that
is done.

Verse 22. His own iniquities will catch him, that wicked man, and he will be
held by the cords of his own sin.

Verse 23. He will die without instruction, and in the abundance of his folly
he will wander.

He answers another objection: “But,” the adulterer will say, “if I am caught
in adultery, I will rely upon friends and defenses, or art and cunning, so I
may easily free myself from the penalties owed to the sin, from prison, and
death.” Solomon replies: although you do this, the sins you have tied together
are like nets and snares. They will hold you bound so that you cannot free
yourself until the day of death and the execution of divine judgment arrives.
In it you will certainly perish because you admitted no discipline. See
Proverbs 1:31; Genesis 4; Job 20:11. This is the reward of adulterers,
especially those who, though warned and instructed, did not take care for
themselves. Although someone may be saved through repentance, since this
occurs rather rarely and only by the free forgiveness of sins, it is no wonder
if he announces here a death owed to every adulterer and escaped by hardly
anyone.
