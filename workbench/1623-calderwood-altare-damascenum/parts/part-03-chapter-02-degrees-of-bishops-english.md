### Chapter II

#### The Degrees of Bishops

By divine law there is no inequality of degrees among the church's pastors.
The distinction introduced into the church between bishop and presbyter was
that slight beginning from which the pope's monarchy arose; and the degrees of
bishops were the steps by which that wicked and lawless Roman Antichrist
ascended to the summit of dignity and tyranny. “Antichrist,” says Wycliffe,
“has under the appearance of clergy twelve agents who plot against Christ's
church. Among them are commonly placed popes and cardinals, patriarchs,
archprelates, bishops, archdeacons, officials, deans, monks, forked canons,
pseudofriars recently introduced, and questors. All these twelve—and especially
the imperial prelates and the friars introduced without foundation—are
manifest disciples of Antichrist, because they take away Christ's liberty,
burden the holy church, and hinder the law of the gospel from running freely as
it once did.” He likewise maintains that “imperial pride devised these degrees
and orders. I similarly say,” he continues, “that in Paul's time a presbyter
and a bishop were the same, as is evident from 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1. That
profound theologian Jerome testifies to the same, as is evident from
Distinction 87, chapter *Olim*. At that time the distinction between pope and
cardinals, patriarchs and archbishops, bishops and archdeacons, officials and
deans, together with the other officeholders and private religious orders—of
which there is neither number nor order—had not yet been invented.” Although
presbyters abuse their own order, he does not

[Marginalia: *Dialogue*, book 4, chapter 26.]

[1623 printed p. 37 | IA leaf 49 | PDF p. 49]

number them among Antichrist's disciples and the degrees brought into the church by
imperial pride, as he does bishops. But since in chapter 4 we shall speak a
little more fully about the distinction between bishop and presbyter, for the
moment let us grant our adversaries that by divine law bishops are superior
and greater than presbyters in degree of power. We nevertheless utterly deny
that the differences of degree among bishops themselves were established by
that same law. Some want Timothy to have been an archbishop, properly so
called, of Asia properly so called, and Titus an archbishop of Crete. Let it be
granted, not conceded, that they were diocesan bishops; our adversaries will
not prove that they were also superior to other bishops in archiepiscopal
degree. We read of no diocesan bishops subordinate to them in Asia or Crete.
Paul commands Titus to appoint presbyters town by town. And lest perhaps they
think that under the word *presbyter* lies concealed one who was later called
“bishop” by preeminence, because the terms were then common, it must be noted
that the apostle does not say “a presbyter city by city,” nor “presbyters in
the cities,” but “presbyters city by city”; just as Luke reports in Acts 14:23
that Paul and Barnabas appointed “presbyters church by church.” Thus Titus
established presbyteries, not a diocesan bishop, in the individual towns or
cities. Paul commands Timothy not to receive an accusation against a
presbyter; he says nothing about receiving an accusation against a diocesan
bishop. He gives precepts concerning presbyters, deacons, and widows. He
instructs him by no commands concerning diocesan bishops, either under the name
of bishop or of presbyter. Apparently the apostle was to write three whole
letters to archbishops or metropolitans and, amid so many varied commands, not
insert even one that was peculiar to an archbishop or metropolitan. The
apostle gives no admonition about convening synods of bishops, consecrating
bishops, correcting the excesses and defects of prelates, or receiving appeals
lodged in bishops' consistories, although the hierarchists consider these the
chief duties of archbishops. Ephesus was indeed established as a metropolitan
see in later ages, as was Gortyna in Crete. Bilson ventures to affirm nothing
certain and definite about whether it gained this prerogative from the
distinction of the place or by succession from Timothy. They imagine that the
apostle James was a bishop and fasten him to Jerusalem. But the Jerusalem see
was not metropolitan; in later ages it was subject to the bishop of Cæsarea.
That church had great preeminence on account of the gospel's first spread,
Christ's passion, and the ancient city's distinction. For these reasons it was
eventually raised to patriarchal dignity. If the see had been metropolitan
while James occupied the chair, it would not later have been reduced from the
same dignity and subjected to Cæsarea.

The apostle James, then, will be only a diocesan bishop, while the evangelists
Timothy and Titus will be archbishops. Will they transform apostles into
bishops, but evangelists into archbishops? Was there one archangel among the
angels of the seven Asian churches? Were they not all equal to one another,
like equal branches proceeding from the same stem of the golden lampstand?
What singular thing does Christ praise or blame in the angel of Ephesus that
savors of archiepiscopal jurisdiction over the other angels, although some of
them also had their seats in Asia Minor? Moreover, Timothy and Titus could by
virtue of office exercise the pastoral ministry in any part of their
prefecture—Titus in any town of Crete, Timothy in any city of Asia—if nearly
the whole Asian nation was indeed entrusted to Timothy, as Chrysostom holds.
But an archbishop ought not discharge a pastor's office in every diocese of his
province. They were therefore not archbishops, but either bishops or
evangelists. It should be noted in passing that episcopal dioceses did not
extend to the breadth of provinces, islands, or regions. Thus they were bound
to evangelize throughout their whole prefecture not insofar as they were
bishops, but insofar as they were evangelists. I hold this principle most
certain: no ordinary minister was placed by the apostles over multiple
churches—for example, over the churches of Ephesus and Pergamum, or Pergamum
and Laodicea—but only over one. We read of no single provincial church, but in
the plural of several churches belonging to one province or region. Finally,
if the chief parts of the archiepiscopal office were not assigned to
metropolitans until after the apostolic age, they were not instituted by the
apostles. But the antecedent is true: the consecration of bishops, convening of
synods, confirmation of elections, decision of appeals, visitation of the
province, and presidency in councils are the principal parts of the
archiepiscopal office. Yet all these were transferred to the metropolitans
after the apostles' age, as we shall show a little below. Whitgift of Canterbury
maintains that the use of archbishops in the church is sometimes necessary and
sometimes useless, and is alterable at the pleasure of princes. They are
therefore not of divine institution. Princes cannot abolish what Christ
instituted, and the church's ordinary ministries, which are of divine
institution, do not ebb and flow like the Euripus. Since Downham could not
teach that archbishops were actually established by the apostles, he retreats
to the apostles' hidden intentions. He claims that the apostles intended a
metropolitan to be set over all the various churches established in a
province, although he can demonstrate this by no certain evidence. From such
presumed intentions the hierarchists can also forge an ecumenical bishop and
greater patriarchs, if

[Marginalia: On 1 Timothy 5; *Reply*, pp. 310, 311, 316.]

[1623 printed p. 39 | IA leaf 51 | PDF p. 51]

such fictions please them so greatly. You will say that the apostles generally
preached the gospel in the most celebrated cities so that from there it might
be spread more quickly and easily to the smaller towns. It does not follow,
however, that jurisdiction over the converted churches was granted to the
churches of those cities. On that reasoning the church of Jerusalem, the
mother of the faith and the Christian religion, ought to have been set not
only over Cæsarea, to which it was later subjected, but also over Rome,
Alexandria, and indeed the churches of the whole world. Moreover, although the
apostles, as prudent workers, put the sickle into a larger harvest and placed a
little leaven into a great mass so that the whole might be leavened more
quickly, they did not pass over lesser places negligently. They scattered the
gospel's seed not at random, but as they were led by the Holy Spirit, whether
to cities, towns, villages, or rural districts. The Lord himself went through
all the cities and villages (Matthew 9:35), and by his example thus showed in
advance what the apostles who were to be sent into the whole world would do.
The apostles lacked no opportunity to establish archbishops even in their own
age, as in the Asian churches, yet we read that they actually established none.
If, as Downham holds, the apostles actually set bishops in the individual
dioceses not only over people already converted but also over those yet to be
converted, why did they not likewise actually set a provincial bishop over
provinces that were to be converted, even though they had not yet actually
been converted? Let that notional archbishop, not yet born in the apostles'
age, go and, as an illegitimate child, seek other fathers than the apostles.
The Nicene fathers refer the origin of the metropolitans not to apostolic
institution but to “the ancient customs.” It is evident that they speak not so
much of the sees of Rome, Alexandria, and Antioch as of the degrees of the
metropolitans themselves, from the addition of “the other provinces,” while no
particular sees are named. Moreover, we read of no permanent consecrators and
governors of bishops before the metropolitans—before the Roman at Rome, the
Antiochene at Antioch, the Alexandrian at Alexandria, and so forth. Let them
show us even one example of a permanent president before a metropolitan who
was set over the other bishops of the province to consecrate, convene, and
govern them. What was his name? He could not be designated *metropolitan* if he
was not bishop of a metropolitan church. The ostentatious title *archbishop*
was not yet used in the church. But it is no wonder that one who did not exist
was anonymous. Bilson's conjecture is therefore empty. “But when those
wondrous gifts of the Holy Spirit failed among men—gifts which perhaps the
earliest church regarded in choosing certain leading governors of the other
bishops—the following ages, just as they placed the most celebrated men over
churches, also wished the bishops of those churches to have the prerogative of
precedence among their brethren.” I pass over the fact that Nazianzen
complains that episcopal thrones belonged not to

[Marginalia: *On Government*, chapter 16, pp. 487–488.]

[1623 printed p. 40 | IA leaf 52 | PDF p. 52]

worthier men but to more powerful ones. I add that they belonged even to
monstrosities.
Who were those men endowed with wondrous gifts, distinct from the apostles or
evangelists? What was their office, and by what name was it adorned? What
Saravia brings forward from the Apostolic Canons is also frivolous. “The
bishops of the individual nations must know who is first among them and is to
be regarded as a head, without whose judgment they are to do nothing of greater
importance.” From this he dreams up the claim that this responsibility was
entrusted to men other than metropolitans, because the Apostolic Canon does
not mention a metropolitan. But that canon was taken, with the wording slightly
changed, from the Council of Antioch, where the metropolitan is also named and
a reason is given why ecclesiastical controversies are to be referred to the
metropolitan rather than to another. This fact alone proves that the falsely
named Apostolic Canons are later than the Council of Antioch: the canons
published in Greek under Clement's name command the Lord's nativity to be
celebrated on December 25, something wholly unknown in the eastern church
before the age of Epiphanius and Chrysostom, and yet the Apostolic Canons
mention these canons. “Since these canons cannot defend their own antiquity,”
says Scaliger, “how much less can those which mention them defend themselves
under the apostles' name, especially when they say certain things worthy of
censure?” Tilenus, Saravia's follower, nevertheless defends the antiquity of
metropolitans by means of these later canons. But even if it were granted that
the Apostolic Canons were older than the Council of Antioch, it does not follow
that they were older than the metropolitans' authority; and so the Antiochene
canon will merely be an exposition of the apostolic one.

Their own audacious conjecture also exposes the vanity of Downham's fantasy
about the apostles' intention. For if the apostles intended metropolitans to
be set over the other bishops of the province, why did the fathers establish
inspectors of those bishops who were not metropolitans? It follows either that
those hidden intentions were not yet known to the fathers or that they
despised the apostles' institutions. The Nicene fathers therefore refer the
metropolitan's degree, not merely his see, to ancient custom. The word
*ancient* in the Nicene canon does not necessarily imply many centuries going
back. In the Third Council of Carthage, canon 54, according to Zonaras, “the
ancient form” means a form instituted seventy years earlier at the Council of
Nicæa. In Acts 15:7 Peter says that God chose him “from ancient days,” that is,
long ago—literally, “from days of old”—“so that through his mouth the Gentiles
might hear the word of the gospel.” We say that what existed in our
grandfathers' age, not our own, existed from ancient times, although it did not
reach the times of our great-grandfathers or more remote ancestors. But let us
consider the antiquity and beginnings of the thing itself. Justin Martyr and
Irenæus say nothing about the degrees of bishops; among the writers of that
age there is profound

*Introductory Canon*, book 3, pp. 301–302; *Paraenesis*, p. 19.]

silence about them. At the beginning of the third century Tertullian, Clement
of Alexandria, and Origen are also silent. In the middle of the third century
Cyprian writes that bishops are equal to one another. “The episcopate is one,
spread abroad in the harmonious multitude of many bishops.” Likewise, “The
episcopate is one, a part of which is held in its entirety by each.” Spalato
comments as follows on these words of Cyprian: “Cyprian does not reduce all
the bishops to any unity through subjections and prelacies up to one supreme
head—which is unity of subordination—but they all govern that whole church
jointly and in its entirety. If all govern in its entirety and together, there
is certainly neither prelacy nor subjection among them. And,” he says, “there
is one episcopate belonging to all the bishops; that is, all bishops taken
together and jointly hold one episcopate in its entirety, and all have one and
the same office and duty, although it is dispersed among many.” In the Council
of Carthage the same Cyprian has these words: “For none of us constitutes
himself bishop of bishops or drives his colleagues to the necessity of
obedience by tyrannical terror, since every bishop has, according to the
latitude of his liberty and power, his own judgment, as one who cannot be
judged by another when he himself cannot judge another.” If someone were to say
that bishops could thus do whatever they pleased with impunity, Cyprian answers
elsewhere: “For this reason, dearest brother, the abundant body of priests is
bound together by the mutual glue of concord and the bond of unity, so that if
anyone from our college should make a heresy and try to tear and ravage
Christ's flock, the others may come to its aid and, as helpful and merciful
shepherds, gather the Lord's sheep and flock.” To reduce these many bishops to
one, says Spalato, “he makes them cohere with one another by the glue of
concord. This coherence presupposes equality and does not admit the
subordination of one person to one person; it admits the subordination of one
person to several, or to a college, as the *body of priests*, as Cyprian calls
it.” In his letter to Evagrius, Jerome infers that bishops are equal to one
another from the fact that all are the apostles' successors. “Wherever a
bishop may be—whether at Rome or Gubbio, Constantinople or Reggio, Alexandria
or Tanis—he has the same merit and the same priesthood. The power of riches and
the lowliness of poverty do not make a bishop higher or lower. Besides, all are
the apostles' successors.” Whitgift of Canterbury says that bishops and
archbishops are equal with respect to ministry, not jurisdiction, and are of
the same order, not the same degree. “I do not deny,” says Downham, “that
metropolitans are superior to the other bishops of the province in power of
jurisdiction, however much absolutely all bishops are equal in power of
order.” Bellarmine says the same: “What Jerome says, that bishops have the same
merit and priesthood, is true, but it must be understood of the episcopate with
respect to order, not jurisdiction. For Jerome did not intend to deny that the
bishop of Alexandria had a greater dominion than the bishop of Tanis, since it
is agreed that the former presided over three great

is not to explain Jerome, but to mock him. *Priesthood*, they answer, signifies
the order under discussion; but what can *merit* signify other than dignity,
power, authority, and jurisdiction? It is as if Jerome said that all bishops
are equal not only in priesthood and order, but also in merit, power, honor,
and authority.” He shows from Jerome's words that the word *merit* bears this
meaning, for Jerome first said, “If authority is sought,” and then adds, “all
have the same merit and priesthood.” They may be richer or poorer, but this
unequal condition of wealth or poverty does not take away equality of dignity
and authority. As for the objection about the greater dominion of the bishop
of Alexandria than the bishop of Fano—or, as others have it, Tanis—he answers
that Jerome spoke about right, not human custom or constitution. “Equality of
order is from God,” says Junius, “but the inequality of jurisdiction of which
Bellarmine speaks here is from positive and human law.” Tilenus says nearly the
same in the very same words. “Did Jerome not know,” says Spalato, “to
distinguish jurisdiction from order? Was he unaware that the bishops whom he
made equal in order were equal in jurisdiction? Under the title of priesthood,
of course, he included whatever order and power a bishop possessed, and as the
one unanswerable reason for this equality he posited succession from the
apostles.” Bishop and presbyter are of the same order, although according to
the hierarchists' opinion they are unequal in jurisdiction. Does an archbishop
then stand in relation to a bishop as a bishop stands in relation to a
presbyter? Who is so witless that he does not perceive that Cyprian's
testimonies must be understood of equal power of jurisdiction? The convening
of synods and presidency in them during the second century prove that the
metropolitans' power was supported not by apostolic institution but by human
constitution. Near the end of the second century Eusebius reports that the
faithful met more than once, examined Montanus's doctrine, rejected his heresy,
and expelled it from the church. He does not report that metropolitans convened
those synods. In the same book he relates that various synods were convened
because of the controversy over Easter, and that Theophilus of Cæsarea and
Narcissus of Jerusalem presided over a synod in Palestine. Thus no one presided
over the synod by metropolitan right. Palmas was placed over the synod of the
bishops of Pontus “as the most ancient,” that is, the oldest. He therefore did
not preside as metropolitan; nor would they have conferred this prerogative on
Palmas because of his old age if it had been owed to the metropolitan by
divine law, some ecclesiastical law, or custom. If the metropolitan did not
preside, neither should it be thought that he convened the synod. Who in his
right mind would dare affirm that men who neither presided by virtue of office
in synodical meetings of bishops nor convened those meetings consecrated
bishops, visited provinces, received appeals, and corrected the excesses and
defects

of their fellow bishops in the province? In his letter to Evagrius, Jerome
records that the bishops in the Alexandrian church were ordained neither by
bishops nor metropolitans, but were merely elected by the presbyters and placed
in a higher degree. “For at Alexandria,” he says, “from Mark the Evangelist
until the bishops Heraclas and Dionysius, the presbyters chose one of
themselves, placed him in a higher degree, and called him bishop.” The word
*consecration* had not yet arisen in the ordinations of bishops—indeed, neither
had *ordination*—for the ordination of bishops and presbyters was one and the
same. Thus this elevation to the episcopate was merely an election and a
placement in a permanent presidency. In the third century we read that certain
councils were convened in Africa over which the bishops of Carthage did not
preside—for example, the Council of Lambaesis, at which Privatus was condemned
by ninety bishops, and the Council of Capsa, convened for the ordination of the
bishop of Capsa. Near the end of the third century, the Antiochene synod was
convened not by the Samosatene bishop of Antioch but against the Samosatene.
For nearly three centuries bishops assembled in synods by mutual consent,
sending letters to one another; the perpetual prerogative and official charge
of convening and presiding was not attached to one man in every province.
Cyprian's testimonies cited by us above abundantly attest that Cyprian was not
an archbishop or a provincial bishop superior to the other bishops of his
province. From them Musculus also concludes that Cyprian did not even think of
metropolitans and patriarchs. Cyprian does indeed write as follows: “But
because our province extends more widely, it also has Numidia and the two
Mauritanias adhering to it.” Yet he writes in the name of his colleagues, as
the preceding words indicate: “But when several of us colleagues who had come
together had determined.” That broader province did not belong to Cyprian
alone, but was common on equal terms to all the bishops of the province.
Nazianzen has these words about a certain prefecture of Cyprian: “He is set not
only over the church at Carthage or Africa, but also over all the western
regions, nearly over the East itself, and also over the southern and northern
quarter.” Would anyone be sufficiently sane who inferred from these words that
Cyprian had jurisdiction in these widely extended provinces and asserted that
he was some ecumenical pontiff? Because Cyprian aided the church wherever it
labored through his writings or counsel, because by the light of his doctrine
he shone in the church like a star of greater magnitude among lesser stars,
and because the care of the universal church—which rested upon all bishops in
common—made him more solicitous, Nazianzen uses the verb *to set over*
rhetorically rather than dialectically. Nearly the same care shone in Cyprian
as in the prefects of those provinces, though he wished not to preside over
them but to benefit them. What Nazianzen writes about Cyprian was personal

and must by no means be applied to the bishops of Carthage. If similar phrases
about other bishops of that century should occur somewhere, commendations of
persons—not a prefecture belonging to the see or dignity—must be inferred from
them, unless our adversaries can prove from writers of that age that the same
phrases apply to the other bishops of the same see. I am confident they cannot.

At nature's urging, with the apostolic church leading by its example and
necessity demanding it, neighboring bishops of the same province, region, or
civil prefecture assembled to frame canons, suppress heresies and budding
errors, and attend to other common affairs of their churches. The prerogative
of convening or presiding did not belong to one man. By agreement and common
consent, or by the synod's decree and summons, they called provincial synods
and chose a president, whether the man was most suitable by reason of age—as
Palmas was in the synod of Pontus—or by reason of gifts, and whether he was
bishop of the metropolis or someone else. Later the prerogative both of
convening and presiding was attached to the metropolitan, but without
jurisdiction. It was a mere presidency, although a permanent one; but because
it was permanent it was also dangerous and was afterward very easily changed
into jurisdictional power. By the worst counsel they attached prerogatives to
sees and places, as if the Holy Spirit were also attached to the sees
themselves, since “the worst men are very often the first, and they are
customarily as insolent in administering,” says Bilson, “as they were shameless
in obtaining.” Violent and tyrannical domination arose from the prerogative of
the throne. Hence Nazianzen's tears and wish: “Would that there were neither
precedence nor any place of preference and occasion for tyranny, so that we
might be known by virtue alone.” Precedence and prerogative of place therefore
leveled the road for tyrannical and violent domination. The ancients,
unwisely and without knowing who or what sort of person Antichrist would be,
diligently advanced the mystery of iniquity which was at work in the apostles'
times. Nevertheless, they knowingly and deliberately conformed the church's
polity to the type and form of the Roman Empire. The three most
celebrated metropolitans were placed over the other bishops in the civil
prefectures of their sees, first by custom and then by law and decree of the
Nicene Synod. The prefect of the city had his seat at Rome, and his
jurisdiction ran to the hundredth milestone from the city. His prefecture
embraced four regions: Old and New Latium and portions of Tuscany and Picenum.
The same prefecture was the province of the Roman metropolitan. Rufinus, who
wrote not long after the Nicene Synod, renders the words of the sixth canon as
follows: “Let the ancient custom be preserved at Alexandria and in the city of
Rome, so that the former may exercise care for Egypt and the latter for the
suburbicarian churches.” By *suburbicarian churches* he understands the
churches

in the urbicarian or suburbicarian regions that extended to the hundredth
milestone from the city, as the anonymous
conjecturer learnedly demonstrates in his dissertation on the regions of Rome
and the suburbicarian churches, and in its defense. The metropolitan of
Alexandria had the same prefecture as the prefect of Alexandria, who was also
called the prefect of Egypt and the Augustal prefect—that is, Egypt, Libya,
and the Pentapolis. The count of the East had his seat at Antioch, and the
Antiochene metropolitan, sometimes called the bishop of the East, was the
metropolitan of his prefecture. Although the provinces of the Alexandrian and
Antiochene extended more widely than the Roman's, the Roman metropolitan was
regarded as more dignified because of the dignity of the city and its prefect;
yet he had no power over other metropolitans. At the time of the Nicene Council
there were no more than two degrees of bishops: bishop and metropolitan. Those
who suppose that the patriarchs were established by the Nicene fathers are
mistaken. Neither the names of archbishop and patriarch had yet been heard in
the church, nor was there any subordination of metropolitans to greater
archbishops or patriarchs, even in those more celebrated provinces and
prefectures of Alexandria, Antioch, and Rome. The prefectures of the
Alexandrian and Antiochene, and perhaps of certain others, certainly extended
widely before the Nicene Council, although no metropolitans were subject to
them. Let them bring forward even one example of any metropolitan under the
Antiochene or Alexandrian at the time of the council, however vast their
prefectures were. It is not a broad expanse of territory but the subordination
and relation of metropolitans that constitutes a patriarch. And in the Nicene
canon the term *metropolitan*, no other, is used. The ancients did not stop at
these degrees, but after the Nicene Council fabricated a higher degree when
the organization of the civil polity changed. The anonymous author in the
*Vindiciæ* reasons as follows: “After emperors—Constantine in particular—were
seized with the desire to multiply administrations and magistrates, whether
to diminish the power of the magistrates, to admit more men to public office,
or for some other reason, a single diocese was eventually made from one simple
administration or province. This was done in such a way that individual
magistrates were set over individual provinces, while the highest
administration of nearly all of them, and thus of the whole diocese, was left
to the former magistrate. Thus Egypt, which acknowledged the Augustal prefect,
had originally been one province or administration; after it was divided into
three, a diocese was made from a province. Similarly, what had originally been
one administration extending to the hundredth milestone was afterward divided
into several provinces called suburbicarian and made the diocese of the city's
prefect, although the care of the individual provinces throughout this diocese
was entrusted to individual governors.”

Thus when the larger—or, as Spalato calls them, very extensive—prefectures were
subdivided, other metropolitans were established under the older
metropolitans, and the latter were elevated to a higher degree. A celebrated
passage of Scaliger in the *Description of Gaul* illustrates the hierarchical
ladder of degrees: “After Constantine the Great established four prætorian
prefects, two in the East and the same number in the West, he placed one
western prefect in the city of Rome and the other in the city of Trier. The
latter was called the prætorian prefect of the Gauls. And because the demands
of the empire did not permit them to remain in those cities continually,
several vicars were assigned to them to administer justice to their provinces
with the same power. Three vicars were therefore assigned to the prætorian
prefect of the Gauls: the seat of the first was placed at Trier, the second at
Lyons, and the third at Vienne. Just as the authority of these vicars was
great, so the distinction of the cities in which they had their seats was also
very great. The first and second Germanies, Belgica, Britain, and so forth were
subject to the vicar of Trier. I do not know how the Latins designated all the
territory subject to the jurisdiction of one vicar, but the Greeks call it an
exarchate, diocese, or patriarchate. Changes of kingdoms,” he says, “altered
many things in this division, but the ecclesiastical estate has until now
retained its traces intact in both East and West. For they call the cities that
gave their names to provinces *metropolises*. If the seat of a vicar, whom they
called an exarch, was in them, they call them not only metropolises but also
exarchates and patriarchates; they call their prelates exarchs and patriarchs,
but the rest metropolitans and archbishops, who have only bishops under them.
For exarchs have under them not only bishops but also metropolitans, while
metropolitans have only bishops. In the Latin church the name and right of
archbishops and their canonical jurisdiction over their suffragans remain, but
no traces of exarchates remain, unless perhaps there was some shadow of it in
the fact that the metropolitan or archbishop of Vienne calls himself Primate
of Primates.” You see, therefore, that a third degree—that of patriarchs—was
introduced in imitation of the civil estate. They were called exarchs and were
superior to metropolitans, and they held the same seats as the vicar of the
prætorian prefect. Wherever there was also the seat of the empire and of a
prætorian prefect, they were considered more dignified than the rest. Those
two patriarchs of Rome and Constantinople repeatedly contended with one
another until at last the Roman emerged superior. As the dignity of a province
or city increased, the authority or dignity of the bishops in the same city or
province likewise increased; when the former fell, the dignity of the
episcopal sees also fell. “If any city,” decreed the Chalcedonians, “has been
renewed by imperial authority or is hereafter renewed,

let the order of ecclesiastical things also follow the civil and public
forms.” The Roman bishop was regarded as more dignified than the rest “because
that city reigned,” or ruled the others, as is said in the same council.
Although the Constantinopolitan arose after the Alexandrian and Antiochene, he
was preferred to them because Constantinople was, as it were, a new Rome,
honored by the senate and imperial power and enjoying privileges equal to old
Rome. When the seat of the prætorian prefect of Illyricum was transferred from
Sirmium to Thessalonica, the ecclesiastical dignity of that see was also
transferred, and then priestly honor followed that very prefecture. Thus
Justinian says that the bishop of Thessalonica “gained a certain prerogative
under the shadow of the prefecture, not by his own merit.” When Cappadocia was
divided, Anthimus of Tyana contended that ecclesiastical things ought to be
divided together with civil things, and prevailed over Basil of Cæsarea in
that contest. We see, therefore, that the ancients, not only in establishing,
dividing, transferring, and elevating sees but also in establishing the
degrees themselves, fashioned the form of ecclesiastical polity according to
the pattern of the civil empire with ambitious and dangerous emulation. They
themselves reject the fantasy about flamens and archflamens. In his letter
prefixed to the *Description of the Patriarchates*, Miræus acknowledges this
emulation of the empire. “Next to him, namely the Roman pontiff,” he says,
“formerly stood the four principal patriarchs, but next at a long interval.
The metropolitans, or archbishops, follow, and then a long succession of
bishops. Their own dioceses and provinces are attributed and committed to each.
The church borrowed the patterns and forms of this division in large part from
the Roman commonwealth, so that even the old ecclesiastical provinces largely
correspond to the civil ones.” Although ecclesiastical divisions could not
correspond exactly to civil ones in every respect, whether because of peoples
not yet converted or other impediments, they nevertheless cohered for the most
part, and the ancients strove to make them correspond completely. “It is
enough to say,” says Filesac, “that those ancient fathers of the nascent church
derived the establishment of the supreme dignities in the church by a certain
imitation of the commonwealth, not merely according to the dignity of cities
as it then existed, but even more according to the division of provinces as it
was discerned in that age.” We showed above that they also regarded the dignity
of cities. At the Council of Turin the dispute between Arles and Vienne over
the primacy was settled as follows: whoever proved that his city was the
metropolis in temporal matters would have the primacy of the whole province
and the power of ordinations. “Thus under Boniface IV the metropolitan right
of the church of Toledo is defended,” says Spalato, “by King Gundemar, because
the city of Toledo had been exalted by the imperial throne and was the only
province in temporal affairs, whose metropolis was Toledo.”

In the same place: “That this entire hierarchical subordination among the
bishops of the catholic church was established by its own human ecclesiastical
law is especially evident from the fact that in councils every controversy
about it is settled only from ecclesiastical statutes and the secular patterns
of cities.” Downham also acknowledges that the church borrowed its form of
polity from the commonwealth. “The true origin,” he says, “of the superiority
of metropolitans and patriarchs in their own territories was the pattern of
civil polity in the Roman Empire.” But Saravia presses against us and declares
that all these things are of no importance. “It is a commonplace, or rather
childish, argument,” he says, “that is brought forward concerning
metropolitans, dioceses, and parishes, since the empire bears the form of the
Persian, Babylonian, Turkish, or Abyssinian polity just as much as it does the
Roman.” It is indeed true that it also resembles other empires; Peucer most
elegantly depicted in his book on the kinds of divination how it resembles the
Turkish. Empires and polities have certain things in common, just as the lion,
leopard, and tiger do. Yet in forming ecclesiastical polity the ancients did
not set before themselves the Persian, Turkish, or Babylonian empire as a
pattern, but the Roman. They formed the beast in the image of that beast, and
as the empire grew, declined, or changed, they emulated the polity of the
Roman commonwealth both in its degrees and its sees. And although the Nicene
fathers who established metropolitans and the Constantinopolitan fathers who,
according to Socrates, established patriarchs when the provinces were divided
did not, as I said above, introduce Antichrist knowingly, they nevertheless
acted unwisely. They established degrees of dignity, led not so much by human
prudence as either by a spirit of ambition or by error in discerning the
nature of Christian polity. Cyprian complains that “ambition slept in the
bosom of priests, reclined there beneath the shade, and fraudulently concealed
itself there in the bedchamber's secrecy.” Irenæus says, “You complain of the
losses of the Lord's inheritance, while you alone wish to exercise dominion in
the church.” Eusebius reports that the persecution of Diocletian was brought
about by the vices of ecclesiastics. The presidents and pastors of the
churches, having cast away the rule of godliness, burned with division and
contention among themselves and “strove to obtain rule in the manner of
tyrants.” I conjecture that those petitions offered to Constantine at the
Nicene Council were contentious disputes about the boundaries of provinces or
dioceses. They would have introduced the doctrine of demons had Paphnutius
alone not opposed them. Since they were so prone to fall and err in other
matters, could they not have gone astray in establishing the church's
discipline and polity? Antichrist could ascend to his throne only through a
corrupted polity of the church, and that iniquity was being worked in a
mystery, both in its origin and in its growth.
