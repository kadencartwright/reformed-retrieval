CHAPTER VI.

VERSE 1. There is an evil that I have seen under the Sun, and it is very great
upon mankind.

VERSE 2. A man to whom God himself has given riches, resources, and honor, and
whose soul lacks nothing in which it might delight, yet God himself does not
grant this man the ability to enjoy that thing, but a stranger enjoys it: this
is vanity and the worst affliction.

VERSE 3. If a man should beget a hundred children and live many years, however
numerous the days of his years may be, if his soul is not satisfied with that
good, and moreover he has no burial, I say that a stillborn child is better
than he.

VERSE 4. For it comes in vanity and walks in darkness, and its name is covered
in darkness.

VERSE 5. This one has neither seen the Sun nor experienced it; its condition
is more tolerable than the other's.

VERSE 6. Even if he should live two thousand years and not enjoy good, do not
all these go to the same place?

The summary is this: miserable and unhappy is the condition of those who live
amid the greatest abundance of all the conveniences of life, but to whom God
has not graciously granted the use of those same things.

There are two parts to this evil: 1. Its magnitude is briefly indicated in
verse 1. 2. It is explained more fully in the remaining verses.

VERSE 1. There is an evil that I have seen under the Sun, and it is very great
upon mankind.

Since in verses 13 and 14 of Chapter 5 Solomon taught that there are two evils
in avarice, he now returns to the former one, concerning riches stored up to
the ruin of their possessor. Here the first evil is identified from the place
that is its subject, namely, because it

prevails everywhere, in all the regions where the Sun rises and sets—that is,
throughout the whole habitable earth. If someone should object that Solomon
was not able, either by himself or through his people, to know what was being
done everywhere, I reply that Solomon's empire was extended and propagated
widely and that he received many reports through his ministers and
messengers. Moreover, since this evil was rampant among the people of God,
there can be no doubt that it prevailed among other nations. But this is
sufficient to uphold the truth of the statement: Solomon wrote it by the
inspiration of the Holy Spirit, who searches all things.

From this it is established that the spread of an evil does not diminish it.
For although this evil flourishes everywhere in all regions and at the same
time in every age, far from thereby losing any of its depravity, it instead
acquires something more. This refutes those who, when reproved for their sins,
immediately retort that they were neither the first nor will be the last to do
such things. Yet you are blameworthy for this very reason: you stumble against
the same stone when the fall of others ought to have made you more cautious;
and then others will strike it by following your example.

Solomon next aggravates this evil by its attendant magnitude, something he
had by no means said of the former evil. By these words he plainly intends to
declare that the present evil outweighs the former, as will also be perceived
more fully and clearly from what follows. By this preface, therefore, the Holy
Spirit endeavors to instill in us a horror of this evil so that we may take
careful precautions against it. For although people, in order to cause fear,
are accustomed to exaggerate the greatness of a danger in language more
abundant and dreadful than the truth allows, the Holy Spirit, who is also the
Spirit of truth, portrays each matter in vivid colors according to its true
weight and as its dignity or indignity requires. Thus far concerning the
former part.
