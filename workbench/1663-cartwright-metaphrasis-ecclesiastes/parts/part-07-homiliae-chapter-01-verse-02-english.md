VERSE 2. Vanity of vanities, says Ecclesiastes; vanity of vanities: all these
things are vanity.

The Book follows, whose summary is this: human happiness in this life, which
consists in none of the delights or advantages that are under the Sun, is
contained in the true and unfeigned fear of God alone. And this is concluded
by a syllogism of distribution: Human happiness consists either in some one
of all the things that are under the Sun, or in the fear of God governing the
rest. But not in the former (because all things are vain). Therefore in the
latter.

The parts of the Book are: 1. All things that are under the Sun are vain and
therefore do not make a human being happy. 2. This happiness is found in the
true fear of God.

These parts, however, are not treated in a certain continuous discourse
without interruption; instead, they are explained in turns repeatedly broken
off and interspersed.

The first part of the assumption—that human happiness does not consist in the
things that are under the Sun—is proved from the attendant vanity of
everything done here on earth, if you except the fear of God. But nothing vain

makes a human being blessed. Therefore, and so forth.

This vanity, however, is understood in more than one way. First, all things
are subject to vanity because they do not possess any stable firmness. Romans
8:20. In this sense all creatures are said to be subjected to vanity. Next,
things are vain, and are customarily called so, when they flaunt a certain
appearance of a good but do not exhibit or furnish that good in reality. Thus
riches and honors promise people comfort and security in living, but supply
neither. What is more, they often take away life and cast people into dangers,
for which reason they are also said to lie. See Psalm 43. Furthermore, they
are vain because, even when we enjoy them in abundance, they still do not
satisfy us. Solomon treats all these matters later at greater length. He
heightens this vanity by rhetorical ἀναδιπλώσει, reduplication, and likewise
by the trope of Adjunct for Subject, by which he marks the greatest emphasis;
finally, he heightens it by the grammatical comparison of the superlative
degree, which the Hebrews express periphrastically with the singular
nominative and the plural genitive of one and the same word. It is as if all
speech were meager in describing the vanity of these things about which he
has begun to speak, and as if words failed. All of this he confirms by his own
testimony—that is, by divine testimony.

The discourse, then, repeated so often in this very effective form of speech—a
discourse to whose confirmation the whole book is devoted, besides the other
arguments from the Old and New Testaments sprinkled here and there, and that
with so choice a Scribe employed for this task—plainly indicates how deep the
love of these most vain things has driven its roots in us. We permit that love
to be snatched and torn out of us only by great force and, as it were, by a
drawn-up battle line of arguments. We therefore deceive ourselves if we
believe that by some light skirmishing and cold effort we can disentangle
ourselves from this evil so deeply implanted, especially since the perverse
zeal of corrupt nature has acquired strength through long dwelling among and
habituation to these things. It is just as if, to cure a desperate disease,
not only an entire college of the most skillful Physicians were employed, but
also some man of the greatest genius were selected to devote all his labor and
zeal to curing that one disease. In that case the obstinacy will be more
loathsome (and consequently the judgment more severe) if we do not permit
ourselves to be healed.
