## Part XL: Providence and Contingent Causes

[Part II | PDF page 208 | logical source scan 207 | printed page 206 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

To make this still plainer, the following demonstrates that even contingency [τὸ ἐνδεχόμενον] is not removed by the necessary firmness and certainty of the divine decree. God’s providence, by which he governs every single thing without exception, does not remove but establishes secondary causes. It therefore does not change the natures of things but determines each according to its nature. For God is creator both of all things that exist and the modes by which they exist. Since there are two such modes, necessity and contingency, he determines both so that what he decreed to occur by necessity occurs not merely necessarily but from a necessary cause which by its nature cannot be otherwise. Conversely, what he so determined necessarily occurs because he can never be deceived, yet not from a necessary cause but a contingent one, since he decreed that it should occur contingently.

Let us demonstrate this distinctly first in Adam and then in all his descendants. There is no doubt that before the fall Adam possessed a good will, but—as the event showed—a mutable one. He could therefore have resisted Satan’s suggestion by his natural powers, since lusts had not yet been introduced into the human will. Although this will was flexible toward either part with respect to the power [δύναμιν] and faculty of willing, it could not choose both but only one. Uncoerced, but of its own accord, the will inclined not necessarily but contingently toward the part determined by God. Its outcome was therefore necessary from the remote cause, God’s predestination, but contingent from the proximate contingent cause. Since the latter’s movement was voluntary, all fault remained in the one who voluntarily fell. It is well known that the voluntary [τὸ ἑκούσιον] is opposed not to the necessary [τῷ ἀναγκαίῳ] but to the involuntary [τῷ ἀκουσίῳ]. Although Adam’s power [δύναμις], considered as the faculty was in itself, was flexible toward the other part, the act itself could not be directed indiscriminately but necessarily only toward the one part he voluntarily chose. He chose against conscience what he ought not to have chosen.

As for Adam’s descendants—

—, their corrupted will—even when their mind, wandering from truth, beholds some mere likeness of good and true (for I speak here of things concerning conscience’s duty toward God and neighbor)—never deliberates between what is truly good and evil. Instead it deliberates either among things having the appearance of good, such as all philosophical moral virtues, but which are not truly good, although some are farther and others nearer to true good; or between those virtues and vices truly and wholly evil; or finally between these or those vices. Whichever part it chooses, it can do nothing but sin. Yet since the will possesses toward either part that servile freedom flexible in both directions and enslaved to sin, it always voluntarily inclines toward the part God determined from eternity, as God also saw fit to cause them to move voluntarily by granting efficacy to the spirit of error and their lusts. It follows that the infallible outcome of predestination by no means removes the will’s contingency.

To make this still clearer, let us show by definite examples that God’s providence, although certain and immovable in its determination, does not change the nature of intermediate causes. Who denies that Christ could have died earlier than he did if one considers in themselves the power and malice of his adversaries? At the hour determined by the Father he died only contingently with respect to the authors of his death, since even when they determined to kill him it lay within their power, as previously, to postpone his death. But with respect to God’s determination, he could be killed neither earlier nor later, neither elsewhere nor by another kind of death. Only when his hour arrived did Herod and Pilate assemble to do whatever God’s hand and counsel had predestined to occur (Acts 4:28). Similarly, if you consider the nature of Christ’s bones and what executioners such as they were could do, who denies those bones could have been broken? Yet because the executioners could choose only one alternative and voluntarily, though unknowingly, inclined to what God had determined and foretold before all ages, by God’s ordering they could not be broken and therefore necessarily remained whole. But from the intermediate cause—the executioners’ will—they were contingently not broken.

Augustine therefore rightly and most learnedly discusses this matter against Cicero in book 5, chapter 9 of *The City of God*. After refuting both the Stoic fate which removes all contingency and the insanity of the Epicureans who remove all providential certainty, he speaks in words with which I wish to conclude this entire discussion: “It does not follow that because the order of all causes is certain to God, nothing therefore lies within our will’s discretion. For our wills themselves belong to the order of causes, which is certain to God and contained within his foreknowledge, since human wills are causes of human works.” Again: “Our wills possess only as much power as God willed and foreknew them to possess”—behold foreknowledge always joined with will—“and therefore whatever power they possess, they possess most certainly; and what they will altogether do, they will do because he whose foreknowledge cannot be deceived foreknew that they would will and do it.” Again: “If necessity is defined as that according to which we say something is or occurs in a certain way, I do not know why we should fear that it removes our will’s freedom.” Aquinas, most learned of the scholastics, thought no differently—

— about this matter in I, q. 22, art. 2, reply 1 and q. 23, art. 4, reply 1.

The gift of conversion certainly pertains nothing to the reprobate. But if it is proper to those destined for salvation, it is utterly false and ridiculous to say God wills anyone besides them to be converted, or to deny that God does not will the rest destined to condemnation to be converted. How much more ridiculous is it to dare affirm that the gift of conversion is offered to all—that is, in Andreae’s opinion, every individual? As though any besides those whom God draws could ask for, much less obtain, the grace of conversion which Andreae himself rightly testifies cannot be produced by human powers.
