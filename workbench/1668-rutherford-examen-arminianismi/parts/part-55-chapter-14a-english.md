CHAPTER XIV.

ON

THE CERTAINTY OF SALVATION.

Romans 8:35.

Who shall separate us from the love of God?

He understands chiefly God's love actively considered, though without
altogether excluding the love of God passively considered. Hence:

It is asked whether anyone can be certain that he is truly justified, is in
the grace of God, and will be saved. To determine the state of the question,
certain matters must be noted beforehand.

One kind of certainty is that of the entity and object, or entitative and
objective certainty, by which matters of faith are most certain in themselves
when general propositions and truths are under consideration. Our opponents,
both the Roman Catholics and the Arminians, grant that this certainty is of
such a kind that no falsehood can lie beneath it, except that the Arminians
assert that many truths concerning future contingents, even when predicted by
God, are doubtful and indifferent to either of two opposites. Thus, in the Old
Testament, these things were not certain in themselves: Joseph will take Mary
as his wife; Antichrist will

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 626 | PDF p. 675 | split word joined: venturus]

come; Christ's bones will by no means be broken. Another kind is certainty of
the mind or subject, or subjective certainty, by which a matter is certain to
me and to my apprehension.

Every subjective certainty has some objective certainty upon which it is
founded. For no one knows with certainty that a thing exists unless it is
certain that the thing is certain in itself. The Arminians maintain that no
person's final and complete election is certain, either with the certainty of
the entity or with certainty of mind, because it is not certain in itself that
anyone is finally elected until he expires in faith and repentance. Thus the
vacillation and uncertainty of the mind readily follows from uncertainty in
the entity.

Subjective certainty is in turn twofold. One kind is intellectual certainty
in the mind, by which I am certain concerning all the truths in God's Word.
The other is fiducial adherence, or, as they say in the schools, certainty of
affection. This belongs to the will and the affections, and by it we rest and
hope in God.

Roman Catholics, Arminians, and Socinians hold different opinions here. Some
Roman Catholics are earlier and others more recent. The earlier are sounder.
Cajetan, Scotus, Medina, Bañez, and Ambrosius Catharinus, in his *Apology
against Dominicus de Soto*, maintain that people are certain that they are in
grace. On this account Catharinus is beaten about by Franciscus de Sancta
Clara in *On Nature and Grace*, problem

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 627 | PDF p. 676]

1, problem 27. “Hence, with regard to the point of certainty,” he says, “I
would suppose that he”—Catharinus—“fell into Calvin's error, for Calvin
acknowledges that this certainty comes from special grace, though one granted
to every individual.” But the more recent theologians, especially those of
Trent, Session 6, chapter 6, teach shamelessly and heterodoxly that no one can
know that he is in grace with a certainty of faith beneath which no falsehood
can lie.

The Roman Catholics teach that fiducial certainty is not common and constant
among wayfarers. Franciscus de Sancta Clara nevertheless softens this opinion
concerning uncertainty while expounding the words in Romans 8, “The Spirit
himself bears witness,” and so forth. “I said,” he writes, “that there is a
certainty of inclination or affection which arises from hope. Hence the
ecclesiastical doctors more often, and rightly, call it certainty of hope.
Although this can coexist with some fear, it often excludes every actual fear
of the opposite and, for a time, establishes in the soul a certain holy peace
and security and great confidence.” We grant that certainty of hope does not
always and at every point in time exclude all actual fear, though habitual
certainty always remains. But the Roman Catholics maintain that it does not
exclude all habitual and actual fear, so that a person can, and indeed
ordinarily ought to, fear and doubt whether he is in grace, as Gregorius de
Valentia teaches in volume 2, disputation 8, question 4, point 4. He attributes
the certainty that excludes both the capacity and the act of fearing to the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 628 | PDF p. 677 | split word joined: confirmatis]

confirmed apostles and to the Virgin Mary. He attributes another certainty,
which excludes the act but not the capacity of fearing, to those who are very
intimate with God and his love, although such people will rarely entertain
doubt so judiciously that they cannot discover the signs of their salvation.
The third certainty, namely a probable and conjectural knowledge of grace and
salvation, he commonly attributes to believers.

The Arminians and Socinians, first, do not attribute even certainty of hope to
anyone if it excludes every fear of finally falling away. Many Roman
Catholics deny certainty of faith but admit the certainty of hope or trust;
the Arminians deny both. Second, they admit a temporary certainty by which a
person can know, and does know, without any fear that he is justified. But
first, they teach that no one in this life can know whether he is finally
elected to glory. Second, one who can finally fall away cannot know whether he
has full remission of all sins and full justification. Third, they loudly
declare that no mortal possesses a full certainty which excludes either the
capacity or the act of fearing the opposite and by which he is certain that
he will persevere and be glorified. Fourth, they teach a hypothetical
certainty of salvation and perseverance, by which a person is certain that he
will persevere in grace if, through free choice, he does not fail God's grace;
but they deny absolute certainty altogether. Indeed, in opposition to the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 629 | PDF p. 678 | split word joined: Jesuitas]

Jesuits, they deny that it can be known whether any mortals at all will be
saved or whether there will be any future bride of Christ in heaven, because
they teach that every mortal can fall away totally and finally. Thus the
Arminians deny certainty of faith in general, and not merely certainty of
faith applied to individual believers, especially on the point of the
apostasy of the saints. Fifth, they teach that it is probable that some who
are confirmed in grace possess an absolute certainty of their perseverance
and salvation which excludes every fear.

The question, therefore, first, is not about extraordinary certainty through
extraordinary revelation. Second, neither is it about the certainty of the
object and entity, though the Remonstrants even shake that certainty, since
they maintain it to be possible that no one is actually redeemed and saved
through Christ. The Jesuits are more moderate here: they grant a fitting and
indefinite necessity of the perseverance and salvation of the elect, while
the Arminians deny every necessity. Third, the question is not about
conjectural and moral certainty, but about an infallibility and quasi-physical
necessity which, by its proper character, excludes both the act and the
capacity of fearing final defection. Fourth, it does not concern certainty
through natural revelation, but that which comes through spiritual grace and
the inward testimony of the Holy Spirit. Fifth, it is not asked whether
certainty concerning one's perseverance and salvation inheres in a believer
at all times and at every point in

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 630 | PDF p. 679 | split word joined: temporis]

time, but whether it ought to inhere by right. Sixth, the question does not
concern the certainty of those who are in a state of sin or who do not use the
means of persevering in the fear of the Lord. Seventh, it does not concern
experiential certainty or certainty of sense, but certainty of faith. Eighth,
the question is not about certainty in the present, which the Arminians grant,
but certainty concerning the future, namely whether people can be certain
that they will never fall away totally and finally. Ninth, it does not concern
the certainty supported by acts of free choice for which the Remonstrants
contend, but the certainty which rests upon Christ's merits and grace. Tenth
and finally, the question is not about that certainty which generally
includes confidence and excludes fear. The Jesuits grant such certainty to
certain people long and greatly exercised in piety and faith, and the
Arminians grant it to some who are confirmed in grace.

The question, therefore, is whether adult believers can be certain—not only
in the present but also concerning their future state—with intellectual
certainty and supernatural confidence which excludes every fear of the
opposite, not indeed at every point in time and as a constant matter of fact,
but as something properly due, concerning their final perseverance and their
eternal salvation as absolutely future, through that special grace which is
proper to the elect. The Roman Catholics and Arminians deny this; we affirm
it.

1. Romans 5:1 says, “Therefore, having been justified by faith, we have peace
with God through our Lord Jesus Christ”; and verse 2, “Through whom we also
have been brought

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 631 | PDF p. 680]

by faith into this grace in which we stand, and we glory under the hope of the
glory of God.” The regenerate have the certainty of faith and hope that they
will be glorified; therefore they have such certainty. Romans 8:38 says, “For
I am persuaded that neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities
nor powers, neither things present nor things to come”; and verse 39, “Neither
height nor depth, nor any other created thing, can separate us from the love
of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.” Colossians 2:2 says, “That their
hearts may receive consolation, being knit together in love and in all the
riches of the full assurance of understanding, unto the knowledge of the
mystery of God the Father and of Christ.” Hence, if believers are persuaded
by faith and have from faith the consolation that they will be saved, then
they ought not to fear final apostasy.

2. A promise has been made to them that it cannot happen that the truly
regenerate fall totally and finally from grace. This is because of God's
eternal predestination, Romans 8:30; 2 Timothy 2:19; Romans 11:7, 29; because
of the Son's intercession, Luke 22:31; Hebrews 7:25; John 17; and because of
the Holy Spirit's sealing unto the day of redemption, Ephesians 4:30. They
ought, and therefore are able, to believe that these promises are true and to
be certain of them. But promises concerning their final perseverance have
been made to all believers, as was proved above. The Arminians' reply to this
has already been refuted. The Roman Catholic evasion—namely, that believers
cannot know with the certainty of faith that they

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 632 | PDF p. 681]

believe savingly—is, first, a begging of the question, and second, contrary to
Scripture. First John 2:3 says, “By this we know that we know him, if we keep
his commandments”; and chapter 3:14, “We know that we have been transferred
from death to life because we love the brothers.”

3. Whatever the Spirit of God testifies to us as true, we are duty-bound to
believe as true. But the Spirit testifies to every believer that he, first,
believes; second, will finally persevere; and third, will be saved. Therefore,
we are duty-bound to believe these things. The minor premise is proved from
Romans 8:16: “The Spirit himself bears witness together with our spirit that
we are children of God”; and verse 17, “And if children, then heirs: heirs of
God and joint heirs with Christ, if only we suffer with him so that we may
also be glorified with him.” It is vain for Bellarmine to retreat here to
conjectural knowledge because, as he says, “It is not established by divine
faith whether it is the Spirit of God who gives such testimony.” For just as,
when God speaks in the Word, we must believe not merely that what he says is
true but also that it is God who speaks, so, when the Spirit of God speaks
within the conscience, we must believe that his testimony is true and that it
is God himself who testifies. Others say that a certainty of hope is meant,
which often excludes every actual fear of the opposite for a time. That this
is vain is plain from 1 Corinthians 2:12: “But we have received not the spirit
of the world, but the Spirit who is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 633 | PDF p. 682]

from God, that we might know the things which God has freely given us.” First
John 4:13 says, “By this we know that we dwell in him, and he in us: because
he has given us of his Spirit.” It is therefore established by divine faith
whenever the Spirit bears witness together with our spirit. Ephesians
4:18, 30: “We have been sealed by the Spirit unto the day of redemption.”
Second Corinthians 1:21–22 says further, “He who confirms us with you in
Christ and who has anointed us is God, who has also sealed us and placed the
earnest of the Spirit in our hearts.” If, therefore, the Spirit seals
believers unto the day of redemption, they can and ought to be assured of this
sealing.

4. The saints were persuaded with the firmest certainty concerning their own
perseverance and eternal salvation. Job 19:25 says, “Indeed I know that my
Redeemer lives, and that he, the latter one, will rise upon the dust”; and
verse 26, “And after worms have devoured this, when I awake, then in my flesh
I shall see God.” Psalm 23:6 says, “Nothing but goodness and kindness will
follow me all the days of my life,” and so forth. Second Timothy 1:12 says,
“For I know whom I have believed, and I am persuaded that he is able to keep
my deposit until that day.” First John 3:16 says, “By this we have known the
love of God: that he laid down his life for us.” This cannot be attributed to
a special revelation, for Scripture speaks of all those for whom Christ laid
down his life.

5. It follows from God's command. Luke 12:32 says, “Do not fear, little flock,
because it has pleased the Father to give you the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 634 | PDF p. 683]

kingdom.” In Matthew 8:26 doubt is opposed to faith and rebuked by Christ:
“Why do you doubt, O you of little faith?”

6. No consolation, no peace, no hope that does not put to shame, and no joy of
the Holy Spirit could come to the saints. Instead, the fear of hell, perpetual
agitation and vacillation of conscience, trembling, terror, and the spirit of
slavery would necessarily occupy them continually if they were ignorant,
first, whether they are in God's grace and a state of justification; second,
whether they will finally persevere or perhaps fall totally from grace and
faith; and third, whether they are immutably elected to glory or instead were
finally destined from eternity to eternal torments with the devil and his
angels. Against these conclusions there are clear testimonies to the contrary:
Romans 5:1–4; 1 John 3:2; Romans 14:17; Colossians 2:2; Romans 8:35–38;
Romans 15:4; 1 John 2:1–2; 1 Peter 1:3–4; 2 Corinthians 4:18; 2 Corinthians
5:1–3; 2 Timothy 1:12; 1 Peter 1:8; John 16:22.

7. The saints can know with certainty of faith that they will obtain what
they pray for according to God's will in Christ the Mediator. But the saints
pray for the testimony of the Spirit, final perseverance, and eternal glory.
Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is from John 14:13; Mark 11:24;
1 John 5:14; John 9:31. The minor is established from Matthew 6:13;
1 Thessalonians 5:23; Luke 22:31; Psalm 119:34–36.

Finally, Tertullian says in *Apology*, chapter 39, “We are

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 635 | PDF p. 684 | split word joined: Certi]

certain in the sight of God; an unimpaired faith is secure concerning
salvation.” Cyprian says in *Against Demetrius*, in the sermon *On Mortality*,
“If you truly believe in God, why are you not secure? God speaks with you, and
do you, faithless, waver? This is not to have faith in God's house. Let him
plainly fear to die; but the person who is not reborn is consigned to the
fires of hell.” Chrysostom says in Homily 2 on 2 Corinthians, “If God, after
first giving us this pledge, does not add the remaining possession of the
inheritance, he himself would suffer the loss of a forfeited pledge.”
Augustine says in Tract 5 on the Epistle of John, “Therefore, let no one ask
another person; let him return to his own heart. If he finds love there, let
him be secure, because he has passed from death to life.”
