As for the treatment of this doctrine itself, now that the preliminary lesson
proposed above has been completed, it follows that we should approach it with
such religious caution, such godly fear, and such sobriety of a reverent mind
as it is right to employ in every mystery of our salvation and article of
heavenly doctrine. This is especially so here, where the subject is the secret
and eternal judgment and counsel of our God. How dim, indeed how blind and
worthless, are the judgments of even the most acute and keen-sighted people in
this matter is taught us abundantly not only by the perception of everyone
who is conscious of his own weakness, but is also attested by the experience
of other merely natural people. It will therefore be worthwhile for us, at
the very threshold, to set down these pious maxims like cautions and wholesome
admonitions. By them we may be guided as by a thread and recalled to the
bounds of modesty if at any point we should wish either to turn aside from the
right path or to advance and break forth into the depths of that unsearchable
abyss beyond what is right and lawful for us—

[Marginalia: Lecture XVII.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 101 | De Prædestinatione p. 59]

—mortals.

First, God cannot be understood except through God, and what is to be
understood about God must be learned from God himself, because he is known
only with himself as the author.

Second, this doctrine concerning predestination must be handed down in
accordance with those principles and common notions about God which are known
to us by nature. Such are these: God does nothing except justly, wisely, and
in the best manner; he acts and decrees all things with the best end proposed,
even if the reason for his counsel and works is not evident to people and
their equity and rectitude do not immediately appear. As though it were fair
or right for divine judgments to be weighed and assessed with us as judges!

Likewise, nothing can be past or future to God. By his immeasurable eternity
and eternal immensity he reaches in one stroke across every distance of place
and time and, in one most simple act, across every diversity and difference
of created things. Rather, all things are present, naked, and exposed to his
eyes. Consequently, nothing can be unknown to him, nor can anything be
decreed by him anew, that is, in time, since he himself is not in time.

Likewise, he does not idly observe what will happen or is happening, but
orders all things for his glory, precisely as he had decreed from eternity.
Consequently, whatever things change, even when their nature is contingent in
the highest degree, nevertheless depend upon his immutable decree. This does
no injury to their native freedom.

Likewise, nothing good can come to pass unless God both wills and effects it
unconditionally, and nothing evil can be averted unless God prevents it. It
is averted to a greater or lesser extent as God prevents and removes it to a
greater or lesser extent. Thus a person does not even perform the good which
by grace he is able to perform unless God effects in him that he actually
performs it, just as God effected that he should be able to perform it if he
willed.

Likewise, God knows how and is able to make good use not only of good things,
instruments, and actions, but also of evil ones, and he alone can draw light
out of darkness. Yet this infinite goodness of God in ordering even evil
actions most wisely in no way excuses their wickedness; nor, in turn, does
their wickedness cast any stain upon his goodness. Finally, not only a part
but the whole government of the world and every execution of justice and
judgment must be ascribed to him as author.

[Marginalia: 1. Hilary, book 1 *On the Trinity*. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.]

Having now been thus forewarned and fortified, we should not spend time here
proving that predestination exists, although the first question in an orderly
treatment of any subject is, “Does it exist?” We have already proved
predestination above at such length from so many very clear testimonies of
Holy Scripture.

[Marginalia: Rejection.]

The next question, then, is: What is the thing itself? For the meaning of the
name was discussed in its proper place when the Apostle’s own words were
expounded. Likewise, how many forms are there, or what are its parts or
species? What are its causes, effects, adjuncts or properties, subjects,
comparisons, and opposites—that is, what opinions are opposed to it, and what
objections are customarily brought against the truth? I think it better first
of all to propose and establish at greater length the true judgment concerning
each of these matters, and then to answer the contrary objections and
arguments and refute corrupt and erroneous opinions. God favoring us, we will
strive to accomplish this here with as much brevity as we can, lest that work
of exegesis remain interrupted and suspended for too long before we take up
again the divine fabric of the Apostle’s discourse which, in accordance with
our plan, must be unwoven by us.

[Marginalia: Divisional proposition.]

In order, then, that we may understand more distinctly what predestination
is, it must be noted that some use this word more broadly and other orthodox
teachers more narrowly. Some understand the name *proorismos*, or
predestination, not only of the preordination of human beings and angels to
certain ends, but also extend it to other things which are also said in Holy
Scripture to be predestined. Thus in Acts 4, “Herod and Pilate assembled with
the Gentiles and the people of Israel to do all things which God’s hand and
counsel predestined to come to pass.” And in 1 Corinthians 2 the Apostle
speaks of “the wisdom which God predestined before the ages for our glory.”
Thus, speaking generally in this broadest sense, predestination is that holy,
wise, and immovable counsel of God by which he preordained all things within
himself from eternity to those ends through which he would be glorified.

[Marginalia: Threefold sense. So Polanus. Acts 4:27. 1 Corinthians 2:7.]

Others, on the contrary, take it in the strictest sense for election alone,
so that this word *predestination* does not properly include reprobation but
does so only catachrestically and improperly. They are led by this reason:
since predestination is properly the establishment of an end and the ordering
of means to the end, while eternal damnation is not the end of a person but
only *to eschaton*, or the final state—for an end is something best and the
perfection of a thing, whereas damnation is the ultimate evil and the highest
imperfection—it follows that God cannot properly be said to have predestined
some people to eternal damnation, and consequently that reprobation cannot
properly be called predestination. From this they further infer that,
speaking precisely, predestination and election to eternal life are entirely
equivalent and are, as they say, grammatical synonyms.

[Marginalia: So Keckermann.]

Finally, others, with whom we rather agree, approve a middle sense in which
this word embraces under itself both of those parts: election to life and
reprobation. As for the reason just given, drawn from the nature of an end, we
grant that damnation is not something good with respect to the damned
creature itself. But we utterly deny that it is good in no way, and therefore
cannot in any way have the character of an end, insofar as it is the execution
and glorious manifestation of divine justice. Then the same men grant that
the name *proorismos* extends more broadly than the word *predestination*,
because the former is derived from *horos*, which properly signifies a
boundary. Anything *eschaton*, or final, can have the character of a boundary,
even if it is not properly a *telos*, or end. Yet everyone is accustomed to
render the Greek *proorismos* in Latin by the name *predestination*. There is
therefore no need to quarrel about words. Leaving everyone free to his own
judgment in a matter of no great importance, we will regard *proorismos*, or
predestination, taken absolutely and without an added qualification, as a
kind of common genus for each of the two aforesaid parts or species. Yet
whenever we speak of the faithful or name the predestination of the saints,
with this qualification added, we will regard it as equivalent to election
itself and therefore as grammatically synonymous with it.

[Marginalia: So Junius and Trelcatius. The chosen usage.]

Augustine himself seems to have had this in view when he entitled his treatise
*On the Predestination of the Saints*. For it is otherwise evident that in
other places he extends this word, taken absolutely, to reprobation as well.
For example, in book 15, chapter 1, of *The City of God*, he says that he
“mystically calls two cities,” that is, “two societies of human beings, one of
which is predestined to reign eternally with God, while the other is
predestined to undergo eternal punishment with the devil.” Likewise, in the
*Enchiridion*—

[Marginalia: Augustine, *City of God*, book 15, chapter 1.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 102 | De Prædestinatione p. 60]

—and the *Enchiridion* to Laurentius, chapter 100, it is piously and subtly
said that God, “even by
the evil will of a creature through which what he did not will was done,
nevertheless fulfilled what he willed. As the supremely good, he made good use
even of evil people for the damnation of those whom he justly predestined to
punishment and for the salvation of those whom he graciously predestined to
grace.” Nor is Augustine alone. Other ancient writers use the word in the
same sense. For example, Fulgentius says in book 1 *To Monimus*: “Therefore,
the wicked were not predestined to the first death of the soul,” that is, sin,
“but they were predestined to the second,” that is, the state of fire and
brimstone. And in book 2 he says, “God’s predestination is nothing other than
the preparation of his works, which in his eternal disposition he foreknew
that he would perform either mercifully or justly.” Likewise in book 1: “By
God’s predestination either the gracious forgiveness of sins or the just
punishment of sins was prepared.” So too Anselm, in his book *On the Harmony
of Foreknowledge and Free Choice*, says, “There is predestination not only of
good things but also of evil things.” The scholastics likewise treat under
this same name both the election of the saints and the reprobation of the
ungodly. Those among them who are more orthodox commonly say that, so that
God’s perfection might be represented more fully, he predestined some by
representing his goodness in the mode of mercy and damned others by
representing his perfection in the mode of justice.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *Enchiridion* to Laurentius, chapter 100. Fulgentius,
book 1 *To Monimus*, chapter 27, and book 2.]

Predestination taken generally, then, is that eternal decree of God by which
he most wisely, justly, freely, and immutably purposed and decreed within
himself what he willed to be done concerning each rational creature, so that
he might be glorified in them. Or, to restrict it to human beings alone, it
is that most simple decree by which he determined from eternity, according to
the good pleasure of his will, to grant the grace of adoption as children to
some but by no means to others, in order most wisely to display his glory both
in mercy and in justice. Or, if you prefer it more briefly: it is God’s counsel
concerning the final end or state of a human being beyond this temporal and
animal life, in respect of which we are all equal. In God’s counsel, this kind
of life is nothing other than a stage and preparation for that future and
heavenly life. In this respect predestination differs from providence: not
only because providence extends to all things while predestination extends
only to rational creatures, and especially to human beings, but also because
providence directs things to their natural ends, whereas predestination
directs them to supernatural and eternal ends through spiritual and divine
means as well.

[Marginalia: Definition. So Perkins.]

Moreover, since this counsel includes within itself not only the end but also
the means which lead to the end, as will be clear from the doctrine of
election itself, by ordering and arranging those means toward the end, before
we descend to election specifically it will be worthwhile to touch briefly
upon and merely indicate what means are given in common to each part—that is,
which serve the execution of both election and reprobation. There are two.
First is the creation of humanity, male and female, in an upright and indeed
whole condition, that is, in the image of God, yet in a mutable condition and
endowed with animal life. For God alone is properly immutable, and hence even
the angels themselves were created mutable by nature. Second is the
permission of the fall, which we can extend also to the ruin of the evil
angels, although we must speak about this more soberly and cautiously, since
it is not properly part of our undertaking and is not set forth so clearly
and distinctly in Holy Scripture. Under this permission, insofar as human
beings are concerned, I also include the propagation to all Adam’s descendants
of the guilt and punishment of that first sin, so that in Adam all are said
to have fallen according to God’s most wise ordering.

[Marginalia: Explanation. First common means. Second means.]

God accordingly decreed justly to permit this by not preventing it although
he could, since he was obligated to no one to prevent it. He is said not to
prevent evil when he in some manner ceases from his operation, neither
illuminating the mind nor inclining the will to obey his voice. He does this
not without foreknowledge and a prior will, but on account of a greater good
of the universe which would have been prevented unless God permitted even the
evil of guilt to occur. For what place would there otherwise have been for
either mercy or judgment unless God had confined all under sin? What place
for the coming of Christ the Savior? What place for the patience of the
martyrs, or for the saints’ victory and triumph over the devil, the flesh,
sin, the world, death, the grave, and hell? Finally, what place for Christ’s
own cross and sacrifice, whose merit and worth by itself infinitely surpasses
even all the evil of guilt in the whole world? What place, I ask, would there
have been for all these things unless, by divine permission, there had been a
place for sin in the world?

[Marginalia: Explanation.]

Hence Augustine rightly says in the *Enchiridion*, “He judged it better to
bring good out of evil things than to permit no evils to exist.” Gregory also
says many excellent things on this subject in his exposition of 1 Samuel,
chapter 4: “If Adam had not sinned, our Redeemer would not have needed to
assume our flesh. Great are the evils which we suffer through the desert of
the first fault; but what believer would not prefer to suffer worse things
than not to have so great a Redeemer?” Diogenes the Cynic once said, “For
happiness one must go even through fire”; that is, “For happiness, one must
go even through fire itself.” What faithful soul would not say the
same of Christ with far better right—that one must go even through hell itself
to reach him? Hence the same Gregory elsewhere splendidly exclaims a saying
which, as I have heard, is still customarily sung by the papists at the Easter
Vigil: “O happy fault, which merited to have so great a Redeemer!”

[Marginalia: Augustine, *Enchiridion* to Laurentius, chapter 27. Gregory on 1
Samuel, chapter 4. Crates in his letters. In the blessing of the Paschal
candle.]

Now what has been said about the permission of the fall must also be
understood of the human fall permitted by God, but with this distinction
maintained: permission is indeed in and of its own nature a means of the
divine decree, since in every respect it has the character of good, being a
certain mode and degree of the divine will. The fall itself is not so. It is
a means only by God’s ordering, as he draws good from evil by wise power and
powerful wisdom. It has the character of good only in this respect: as it is
ordered to a greater good by God who permits it.

[Marginalia: Note the distinction.]

Although this fall did not occur except with God willing, and did not happen
in any other manner or to any greater extent than the manner and extent in
which God permitted it to happen and occur, in accordance with those
threshold axioms already laid beneath our discussion at the outset, it does
not follow that God or God’s will was the cause of that permitted fall.
Rather, the cause was solely the will of the person falling, left to itself by
God and driven by Satan’s suggestion. The person himself chose to obey that
rather than God’s will and command, and thus, enticed by the devil’s snares,
nevertheless fell of his own accord into the pit dug for him by the enemy.

[Marginalia: Anticipation of an objection.]

I will try to illustrate this with a comparison. I plant a tree in a sunny
and suitable place, a kind which in the course of time could put down deep
roots, bear fruit for many years, and reach its proper size if I either
fortified and supported it with hedges and props against the violence of
gusts and winds, or entirely warded off the force of the winds and the
assault—

[Marginalia: A comparison to be noted.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 103 | De Prædestinatione p. 61 (through Lecture XVII close)]

—of storms so that they did not strike or assail this young plant. But since,
knowingly and willingly, I saw fit neither to put props or a fence around it
nor to turn aside the blasts of the raging winds, the result was that the tree
was immediately overturned—not by me, although I could have prevented its
fall if I had willed, but by the winds themselves tearing it out by the roots.
Therefore, I must not be called the cause of its fall, even though I both
foresaw it and willed it to the extent that I permitted it to happen by not
preventing it when I could have prevented it. For one thing, I was not bound
to prevent it, since I was under obligation to no one; for another, for just
reasons I had determined within myself to allow that event to occur.

[Marginalia: Application of the comparison.]

In this way our God left Adam to himself so that he might be tested by
temptation and it might be made manifest what a creature can do when the
Creator’s assistance and governance cease for a time. Augustine piously
teaches us this in Sermon 11 *On the Words of the Apostle*: “He who made him
governed him,” he says. “The one who had been made wished to forsake him by
whom he had been made. God permitted it, as though saying: Let him forsake me
and discover himself; let him prove by his misery that he can do nothing
without me. In this way, therefore, God wished to show humanity what free
choice is worth without God. O evil free choice without God!” and so forth.
Yet God must not be called the cause of that defection, since he neither
enticed the human being into the fall but, on the contrary, deterred him from
it both by admonishing and threatening, nor inclined his mind to sin, nor
introduced wickedness or any corrupt desire into him. Indeed, he did not even
withdraw any gift bestowed upon him in creation. It only seemed good to God
to deny or not confer confirming grace, which he was by no means bound to
confer. For who, I ask, has bound God to us so that he should always grasp and
support us with his gracious hand, lest at some point in our course we stumble
or fall?

[Marginalia: Augustine, volume 10, Sermon 11 *On the Words of the Apostle*.]

Let us therefore conclude that the cause of this fall was nothing other than,
on the one hand, Satan, who contrived our fall and whom we can call the
preliminary or inciting cause by his persuasion and suggestion, and, on the
other hand, Adam’s own will. When it began to be tested by the enemy’s
temptation, it did not seek God’s aid but willingly inclined itself to its
defection and thus entangled itself in the devil’s snares. Let this suffice
concerning predestination in general and the means subordinated to it.
