The adversaries refer ordination to the power of Order, and they contend that
this act of the power of Order is especially proper and peculiar to bishops;
whereas most other things have been reserved to bishops more for the honor of
the priesthood than from necessity of law, such as the dedication of churches,
the reconciliation of penitents, and the like. But they maintain that the
ordinatory laying on of hands is a sacrament, and therefore is to be
administered by its proper minister, and that its proper minister is the
bishop, not the presbyter. In defining the power of the episcopal order,
Dounamus says: *The power of the episcopal order is, in addition to that which
it has in common with presbyters, the power of conferring grace through the
laying on of hands, as the ordinary instrument of the Holy Spirit, either upon
the baptized for their confirmation, or upon penitents for their
reconciliation, or upon those designated for the ministry for their
ordination*—1 Tim. 4:14; 2 Tim. 1:6. From this definition it follows that not
only the ordinatory laying on of hands, but also the reconciling and confirming
layings on of hands are sacraments. For, in his judgment, these layings on of
hands are sacred actions of spiritual efficacy, and confer grace upon the
baptized and upon penitents. Thus we have three sacraments added to baptism and
the Lord's Supper. He defines the ordinatory laying on of hands specifically
as follows: *It is*

[Marginalia: Lib. 3. ca. 4. pag. 105.]

[1623 printed p. 216 | IA leaf 228 | PDF p. 228]

*a sacred action of spiritual efficacy which consecrates a man to the ministry
of divine worship and confers upon him the power of that order in which he is
constituted, which brings it about that the ordained man can perform sacred
acts of spiritual and supernatural efficacy.* The scholastics and papists
ascribe a twofold effect to the ordinatory laying on of hands: grace making one
acceptable, and that power of ministering spiritually which they call a
character. Dounamus says that grace and power are conferred. Bilson speaks more
plainly: *To create ministers by laying hands upon them contains not only this,
that power is given them to dispense the word and sacraments, but also that the
grace of the Holy Spirit is imparted, which makes both parts of the office holy
and fruitful.* First, we shall grant that it is a sacrament; nevertheless, it
will not follow that its proper minister is the bishop. Fildus admits that the
most learned of the scholastics held this opinion: that bishops were not
greater than presbyters in the power of consecration or Order, but only in its
exercise. In the Scriptures we do not read that the minister of baptism and
the Supper was distinct from the minister of the other sacraments; nor is it
likely that, by divine ordinance, any consecration was greater than the
consecration of the Eucharist. *A distinction must be made concerning power,*
says Durandus, *because one kind is of jurisdiction and another of
consecration. The power of jurisdiction is for governing the people, but the
power of consecration is for the consecration and administration of the
sacraments. Of these two powers, if each is considered precisely and compared
with the other, the second is more worthy than the first, for the first is
ordered to the second.* By this reasoning, the consecration of the Eucharist
also is more worthy than ordinatory consecration, for the latter is ordered to
the former. It is therefore unjust to exclude a man from the inferior
consecration when the more worthy and noble one belongs to him. Hence some
among the ancients argued as follows: he can baptize and make the Lord's body;
therefore he can also lay on hands and complete all the sacraments. Let us hear
Jerome—or whoever was the author of the letter to Rusticus of Narbonne which
was inserted in Gratian's *Decretum*: *But because it is written, “Let the
presbyters be counted worthy of double honor, especially those who labor in the
word of the Lord,” it is fitting for them to preach; it is useful to bless,
proper to confirm, appropriate to restore communion, necessary to visit the
sick, to pray for the infirm, and to complete all the sacraments of God.* This
letter stings Bilson so sharply that he cannot restrain himself from reviling
its author, whom he calls a counterfeit Jerome. Bellarmine, however, in his
book on ecclesiastical writers, did not strike this letter from the number of
the genuine writings. But grant that it is not Jerome's: in the judgment of
the critics it is nevertheless the letter of a learned author. They also
object with that statement of Jerome: *What does a bishop do, ordination
excepted, that a presbyter does not do?* Therefore, they say, ordination at
least was peculiar to the bishop. In that letter Jerome contends by many
arguments that bishop and presbyter are the same by divine right,

[Marginalia: Pag. 106.; *De gubern. eccl.* cap. 9. pag. 158.; *De eccles.* l. 5. c. 27.; l. 4. sent. di. 24. quaest. 1.; Dist. 95. ca. *Ecce*.; Epist. ad Euag.]

and he asserts that traces of this remained even in his own age—namely, that
in his century a bishop did nothing that a presbyter did not do, except
ordination. For jurisdiction was common to the bishop and the senate of
presbyters; but ordination, or rather the rite of ordination, namely
χειροθεσία, was proper to the bishop alone, although the act of ordination
itself was also common. Nor was the administration of the rite always and
everywhere reserved to the bishop alone, as we shall teach later. *He speaks
of the custom of those times: even then, when by the custom of the church a
bishop was greater than a presbyter, the bishop nevertheless had no more power
than a presbyter in anything except ordination. Indeed, elsewhere Jerome
ascribes ordination itself to presbyters,* says Whitaker. And Junius says:
*namely, at the time when Jerome wrote these things, according to the later
institution; but Bellarmine confuses the times so that he may deceive the less
skilled through their ignorance of the refutation.* And elsewhere: *The bishop
has the act of ordination in common with the whole presbytery,* 1 Tim. 4, *but
the rite of ordaining through the laying on of hands belonged to the bishop
alone, that is, to the man who presided over the presbytery, whether he
presided by a perpetual institution—as afterward happened with bishops—or by
a temporary, annual, monthly, weekly, and so forth, arrangement. Jerome was
speaking about this rite, which he calls ordination συνεδριακῶς.* After Jerome
had proved from testimonies drawn from Scripture that bishop and presbyter
were the same, he adds: *But as to the fact that afterward one was chosen* who
would be set over the others, and so forth; and then he adds: *For what does a
bishop do, ordination excepted, that a presbyter does not do?* From this
sequence of words it is plain that Jerome is to be understood of his own
century and of the age after that in which afterward one was chosen:
“afterward,” that is, after all the testimonies brought from Scripture. He
also adduces evidence from the letters of John, who lived until the time of
Trajan. But if this concerns Jerome's use of *afterward*, we shall say more
later. Chrysostom is also objected to the same effect in his commentary on 1
Timothy 3: *There is almost no difference between a bishop and a presbyter,
since the care of the church has also been entrusted to presbyters, and the
things that he said about bishops also fit presbyters. For they are superior
by ordination alone, and in this alone they seem to have more than
presbyters.* Thus Bellarmine, Bilson, Dounamus, and others follow the Latin
translator. But the final words, τὴν γὰρ χειροτονίαν μόνην αὐτῶν
ἀναβεβήκασι, καὶ τούτῳ μόνῳ δοκοῦσι πλεονεκτεῖν τοὺς πρεσβυτέρους,
are translated by more learned interpreters thus: *For by their election
alone they have ascended, and in this alone they seem to do an injury to the
presbyters.* Thus Junius. Χειροτονία *here is said of the presbyters, not
of the bishops; but if χειροτονία is ordination, then ordination belongs
to the presbyters. Moreover, who interprets the verb πλεονεκτεῖν in this
way with the accusative case? I appeal to the Suda, which says
πλεονεκτῶ, τὸ πλέον, ἔχω, γενικῇ· καὶ*

[Marginalia: Whit. de pontif. quaest. 1. c. 3. s. 29.; *De cleric.* ca. 15. not. 7.; ca. 3. not. 22.; in c. 3. prioris ad Tim. hom. 11.; *De cleric.* cap. 7. no. 63.]

*τῶν Θουκυδίδου λόγων πλεονεκτεῖ. πλεονεκτῶ δὲ, τὸ ἀδικῶ, αἰτιατικῇ· ὡς
τὸ πλεονεκτεῖ ἡμᾶς ὁ Διάβολος.* Chrysostom therefore does not establish the
difference in this, that bishops have the right to ordain and presbyters do
not, but in this, that they ascend above the presbyters to the rank of
Ἐπισκοπῆς. Yet because they ascend by the votes of the presbytery, they seem
to injure the presbyters whenever they assume any power to themselves,
although they were appointed by the votes of the presbyters for the sake of
order, not of power. In the same place Chrysostom says: *The middle between
bishops and deacons, if there is any, is very small, since bishops and
presbyters are equal in function and office and, finally, are one in fact and
principle. They differ only by human ordination, insofar as bishops have from
the votes of their colleagues that they preside over the presbytery in order;
and when those who go before in order alone preside in power, they seem to
injure the presbyters and diminish the power of the presbytery.* Among
ecclesiastical writers, Χειροτονία also is not always taken for ordination,
but sometimes for election by votes, as Balsamon and Zonaras observed on
Apostolic Canon 1. Theophylact also interprets Chrysostom in this way. *In
this passage,* Saravia admits, *he has the same thing as Chrysostom, with the
words changed a little. For Theophylact is an epitome of Chrysostom. These are
his words: The things he said about bishops also apply to presbyters. For the
office of teaching and the government of the church have also been committed
to them; they are inferior only in election.* But let it be granted that by
χειροτονίαν Chrysostom understood ordination taken actively, and that he spoke
barbarously and even solecistically; nevertheless, it does not follow that the
act of ordination was proper to the bishop. *Yet grant that the noun
ordination is taken actively,* says Junius, *as Bellarmine takes it; still the
equivocation remains, because the bishop indeed ordained alone in sign and
speech, declaring the sacred institution of the man who was ordained, and yet
he was not alone in the true ordination which the sign represented.* But I
add: if they say Chrysostom took ordination actively, he did not comment
correctly on Paul's passage. He says that the apostle passed from bishops to
deacons, omitting the order of presbyters, because the interval between a
bishop and a presbyter is very small. But there the apostle says nothing about
bishops who afterward were so called by preeminence, but about presbyters who
were also called bishops, as is clear from comparison with the wholly similar
passage Titus 1:5–8. Therefore, if Chrysostom understood the apostle as they
wish, because the inveterate and received difference between bishop and
presbyter lay like a cloud between them, he could not see the truth clearly
and conceived two things in place of one. Bishop and presbyter were
ὁμοβάθμιοι, of the same rank, and there is no need to imagine the apostle
leaping over and passing by the τάγμα, or order, of the presbyters. Let us
return to Jerome. Durandus gathers from Jerome's statements that he thought
there was no power of Order or consecration greater than the priestly power,

[Marginalia: *De triplici Episp.* pag. 37.; *De cleric.* ca. 65. not. 29.]

[1623 printed p. 219 | IA leaf 231 | PDF p. 231]

saying: *Jerome seems to have agreed that the highest power of consecration or
Order was the priestly power, so that every priest, insofar as priestly power
is concerned, can administer all the sacraments—confirmation, all orders, all
blessings—and perform all consecrations. But because of the danger of schism
it was ordained that the priests should choose one from among themselves who
would be called Bishop, as one attending over them, whom they would obey and
to whom the making of orders is reserved; and none but bishops do this. A
bishop therefore is greater than a priest with respect to the power of
jurisdiction, but not with respect to the power of Order or consecration; they
are equal, although by ordinance of the church some things have been reserved
to bishops and forbidden to presbyters. That this is so is proved from the
statements of Jerome, who shows in many ways that Scripture,* and so forth.
And below: *Thus Jerome says that Bishop and Presbyter formerly were
synonymous names, and their administrations also, because the churches were
governed by the common counsel of the priests. But as a remedy for schism,
lest each man draw the church to himself and tear it apart, it was ordained
that one should preside: with respect to the name, so that he alone would be
called Bishop; and with respect to the administration, so that some sacraments
and sacramentals would be reserved to him by the custom of the church.* And
below: *The custom and institution of the church can give jurisdiction, but
not the power of Order or consecration.* Durandus does not say that Jerome
thought this change was introduced by the apostles to avoid schism, as Fildus
falsely attributes to him. Jerome therefore thought, as Durandus says in the
same place, that the power of Order or consecration was greater in a bishop
than in a simple priest by ordinance of the church, not by the nature of Order
and consecration according to divine institution. Panormitanus indeed says
that the apostles restricted the exercise of this power in presbyters.
*Immediately after the death of Christ,* he says, *all the presbyters governed
the church in common, nor were there bishops among them in this sense, but a
presbyter was the same as a bishop, and they alike consecrated all the
sacraments, as in canon “Olim,” Distinction 95, and canon “Legimus,”
Distinction 93. Afterward, to settle schisms, the apostles made or ordained
that bishops should be created, and they reserved certain sacraments to them
by forbidding them to simple presbyters.* What he grants we accept: that at
the beginning presbyters conferred all the sacraments. What he adds about a
reservation instituted by the apostles we deny, and he confirms it by no
testimony of Scripture. In the passage quoted above, Durandus reports not only
the opinion of Jerome but also that of other doctors. *Some also say,* he
states, *that at that time both powers were conferred upon all who were set
over congregations, so that congregations might have readily at hand an
ecclesiastical ruler who could administer all the sacraments and institute
ministers.* But this is a principle of perpetual equity: namely, that
congregations should always have readily at hand someone who can administer
all the sacraments. Their tale that the exercise of the power was removed by
an institution of the apostles

[Marginalia: *De eccles.* l. 5. c. 27.; in Decret. l. 1. titul. de Consuetud. cap. *Quanto*.]

[1623 printed p. 220 | IA leaf 232 | PDF p. 232]

must be understood in one of two ways. Either they mean that the exercise of
the power was removed so that every ordination of a presbyter by a presbyter
is merely unlawful; or that it is not only unlawful but also invalid and null.
That is, a man not only sins by ordaining a presbyter, but also effects
nothing, and what is done is not valid. Let us illustrate by an example. When
a presbyter baptizes or celebrates the Supper in another parish without
permission, he sins, although with respect to the kind of action the
sacraments he administered are true, because these, they say, are acts of the
power of the presbyteral order. They maintain that the exercise of the power
of consecration was so removed by the apostles that if presbyters attempt
anything by themselves in ordination, they effect nothing. Thus Panormitanus
says in the passage cited: *Observe likewise that the practice of the apostles
concerning the sacraments has power to prevent the impression of the
character.* Likewise: *And you see here that such an ordinance*—that is, the
institution of the apostles of which he spoke shortly before—*has power also
to prevent the impression of the character, because if presbyters confer
those things in fact, they confer nothing.* The adversaries also hold that no
ordination by presbyters is valid except in a case of necessity—that is, when
bishops are entirely lacking or fall away from the faith—however much they
pretend otherwise in words. For, as Johnson relates in the first treatise on
the ministry of the English church, there was no need of a new ordination for
the papist priests ordained in Mary's reign, whether in England, Rome, Rheims,
or elsewhere. Rather, Marian priests have been retained to this very day:
Tirellus, Titherus, Nicolaus, and others who were made priests at Rome or
Rheims were inducted into benefices by the prelates without any new
ordination. By contrast, men who were lawful ministers in the churches across
the sea are not eligible for benefices until they have first been made deacons
and presbyters by the prelates. Johnson shows this by the examples of
Whittingham, Travers, and Wright, who, although he had previously been a
minister in Belgium, was nevertheless not instituted and inducted into a
benefice until he had been made a presbyter by Scambler of Norwich. It seems
to me not only absurd but also impious to assert that the apostles rendered
invalid and null an act of the power of consecration which presbyters have by
virtue of their order from Christ's institution. This is the same as if one
were to say that the apostles could decree that a Supper celebrated by a
presbyter was not a true sacrament. Moreover, it is absurd to say that the
power of consecration remains, and yet an act of that power is not only
irregular and sinful against an apostolic canon but also null; for this would
remove not only the exercise but the power itself. But if the exercise was
removed by the apostles only to the extent that presbyters who ordain
presbyters sin, the power itself nevertheless remains and the acts of that
power are valid. Yet this absurdity also ought not rashly to be attributed to
the apostles. For what Suarez affirms concerning the sacrament of
Confirmation may likewise be affirmed

[Marginalia: pag. 96.]

[1623 printed p. 221 | IA leaf 233 | PDF p. 233]

concerning the sacrament of ordination. Thus he says: *If presbyters by virtue
of their ordination had sufficient power of Order to administer this
sacrament, they would be forbidden without cause to confer it altogether, and
if they attempted to give this sacrament, they would be said to exceed their
measure and to usurp the things proper to the height of the episcopate,* as
the Gloss says. In the same way, if presbyters by virtue of their ordination
had sufficient power of Order to administer this sacrament of ordination,
they would be forbidden without cause to confer it altogether, and if they
attempted to give this sacrament of Order, they would be said to exceed their
measure and usurp things proper to bishops. They are forbidden altogether
when they are not ordinary ministers, or when their ordination is null and
entirely invalid: thus Suarez understands the phrase *they are forbidden
altogether* in the sacrament of Confirmation. It is therefore unlikely that
the apostles altogether prohibited presbyters from exercising the power of
their own order which they had by virtue of their ordination. Therefore,
whether they say the exercise of the power to ordain was removed by the
apostles in the former or the latter manner, they ought to produce the canon.

That the presbyter is by divine right an ordinary minister of this sacrament
is proved. Those who belong to one and the same order can administer the same
sacraments. Presbyter and bishop belong to one and the same order. Therefore,
they can administer the same sacraments. Lombard says: *And although all the
orders are spiritual and distinct, nevertheless the canons judge that only
two are preeminently called holy orders, namely the diaconate and the
presbyterate, because the primitive church is read to have had these alone,
and concerning these alone we have a command of the apostle.* When the
scholastics enumerate only seven orders, they count the episcopate and the
presbyterate as one. Peter Soto said at the Council of Trent that *the
canonists, going beyond the bounds of their profession, added two others—the
first tonsure and the episcopate—whose opinion could introduce many other more
capital errors.* But Saravia says it matters little whether they are of one
order: *Hence there are those who contend that a bishop is a priest not only
by so great a grace as presbyters, but also by order. I judge that this matters
little, provided that this action is conceded, with antiquity, to be proper to
the bishop.* Dounamus says that the scholastics determined bishop and
presbyter to be of one order in relation to the Eucharist, and to have the
same function, because every priest has as much power in the consecration of
the Eucharist as the pope himself. But the ancients, he says, establish three
ranks—of bishops, presbyters, and deacons—and consequently three orders of
ministry. For *every order,* he says, *is nothing other than the position
which a person or thing obtains among persons or things subordinated to one
another, some being superior and others inferior.* But since “order” is taken
in two ways,

[Marginalia: Quaest. 27. disp. 36. sect. 1. in 3. Tom.; Lib. 4. dist. 24.; Petri Suavis *historia conc. Trid.* l. 7. p. 659. edit. Francof.; Sar. *defens. de grad.* cap. 24. in append. p. 288.; D. s. l. 3. cap. 4. sect. 23.; In 4. sent. dist. 24. quaest. 1.]

[1623 printed p. 222 | IA leaf 234 | PDF p. 234]

as Durandus says—*in one way for the relation or disposition of things put in
order; and in this way Augustine speaks of it when he says that order is the
arrangement of equals assigning to each its own places. In another way it is
taken for the rank which produces order taken in the first way. In a third
way order is taken for that which is outwardly and perceptibly done about
someone so that he may be constituted in some rank; and this should more
properly be called ordination than order. Taken in the second way, it is a
spiritual power or deputation for the dispensation of the sacraments.* From
order taken in the third way, that is, ordination, I prove that bishop and
presbyter belong to one and the same order taken in the second way, that is,
to one and the same rank, as they speak, namely with respect to the power of
dispensing the sacraments. They say that the order and rank of power which a
bishop has for confirming and conferring orders comes through ordination and
in a sacramental manner. *But the ordination of presbyters and bishops is one
and the same,* says Ambrose. Since the ordination of presbyter and bishop is
one and the same, therefore one and the same power to dispense the sacraments
is conferred upon bishop and presbyter. Where one and the same power is
conferred, there will be one and the same rank of power for dispensing the
sacraments. Indeed, Bellarmine admits that the old scholastics denied that
episcopal ordination is truly a sacrament, although they concede this of
presbyteral ordination. Bonaventure says that in the episcopate no new
character and no new power is impressed. The sign and perceptible thing of
both ordinations, episcopal and presbyteral, is the laying on of hands. As for
the bestowal of the staff, it is not the sign or perceptible thing of this
sacrament, nor is the anointing. *Hence, just as the bestowal of the pallium
does not give a new order to an archbishop beyond the bishop, so neither does
the bestowal of the staff, ring, miter, or anything similar give one beyond
the priest; for such things are sacramentals rather than sacraments,* says
Bonaventure. And in the same place: *Hence, just as he does not receive other
keys, neither does he receive another order.* A bishop is anointed not from
necessity but for solemnity. *Just as the term and meaning of “order” is
extended, so also is the verb when one is said to be ordained as a bishop.
Hence, someone is not so properly said to be ordained when he is promoted to
be a bishop as when he is promoted to be a priest; rather, he is more properly
said to be consecrated.* If episcopal ordination is not a sacrament, it will
follow that bishops do not have the power of ordaining conferred upon them
through episcopal ordination, but through presbyteral ordination, and
therefore that they consecrate insofar as they are presbyters, not insofar as
they are bishops. For sacramentals, such as the bestowal of the staff and ring
and anointing, cannot confer upon the bishop either grace making one
acceptable or a new character, nor can they enlarge or extend the presbyteral
character. They do not confer a new character, as those scholastics admit,
nor do they extend the presbyteral one. *For the same efficacy,* says
Bellarmine, *is required actually to extend a character as to impress a new
one.* Durandus indeed says that the episcopate or episcopal ordination is
truly a sacrament, yet one not precisely distinct from the simple priesthood,
but is

[Marginalia: Ex 1. Tim. 3.; *De sacr. ord.* cap. 5.; In 4. sent. distinct. 25. quaest. P.; *De sacr. ord.* cap. 6.; In 4. sent. distinct. 24. quaest. 5.]

[1623 printed p. 223 | IA leaf 235 | PDF p. 235]

*one sacrament with it,* he says, *as the perfect and the imperfect. Since the
perfect is that which can make something like itself, that priesthood by
which a priest does not receive the power of ordaining another priest is
therefore incomplete, and such is the simple priesthood. But the high
priesthood, that is, the episcopate, is the perfect priesthood, because
through it the power is received of ordaining another as a high or simple
priest; and thus the ordination of a simple priest and episcopal ordination
constitute one sacrament.* These things are ridiculous. Did Christ institute
imperfect and incomplete sacraments which conferred imperfect and incomplete
power? All the works of Christ are perfect works. Bellarmine says that
episcopal ordination is a new sacrament distinct from the presbyterate and
impresses a new character, because a bishop can confer two sacraments,
namely Confirmation and holy orders, which no other lower orders can confer;
and if they attempt to do so, they effect absolutely nothing. Yet the same
Bellarmine says concerning the sacrament of Confirmation that *to confirm is
an act of Order, and that Order is also in the presbyter, at least begun and
imperfect.* And whether the episcopal character is different from the
presbyteral character or the same character made broader and greater, he says
that it *is the absolute, perfect, and independent power of conferring the
sacraments of Confirmation and Order, while the presbyteral character indeed
is an absolute, perfect,* and independent power with respect to the
sacraments of baptism and the Eucharist, but a power begun, imperfect, and
dependent upon the will of a superior *with respect to the sacrament of
Confirmation.* He should have added “and of ordination,” so that what follows
would cohere with what precedes. Does he not appear here to hold the same
opinion as Durandus and fall into the same inventions about incomplete and
imperfect sacraments? That episcopal ordination is not a sacrament distinct
from the presbyterate, as he tries to prove in this treatment of the sacrament
of Order, is plain first from this: no one can be consecrated as a bishop who
either was not already a presbyter or does not then receive both ordinations
at the same time. If it were a distinct and new sacrament, it could also be
separated from the presbyterate; and thus a bishop could ordain although he
could not consecrate the Eucharist, which is absurd. Someone can be ordained
a presbyter who was not previously a deacon. They answer that the
presbyterate does not include the diaconate essentially as the episcopate
includes the presbyterate. But this very reason proves that episcopate and
presbyterate are not two sacraments, but that episcopal ordination includes
presbyteral ordination as the intellectual includes the sensitive: either by
conferring both powers, presbyteral and episcopal Order, simultaneously, in
which case it will properly be one sacrament; or by presupposing presbyteral
ordination and a power already begun, in which case it will also be one as
the perfect and imperfect, the begun and completed—although to call it one in
that manner

[Marginalia: *De sacr. ordinis* cap. 5.; Cap. 15.]

[1623 printed p. 224 | IA leaf 236 | PDF p. 236]

is absurd. Moreover, the perceptible thing is the same in both presbyteral
ordination and episcopal consecration. Indeed, John of Paris, a doctor of the
Sorbonne, in his book on royal and papal power approved by the theological
faculty, proves that bishops are equal to presbyters with respect to the
essential dignity of the ministry, *because their ordination consists of the
same words as that of bishops and apostles, namely, “Receive the Holy Spirit,”
and “Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven.”* See Plessy on the
church and the Eucharist. If the necessary and essential rite is the same,
namely the laying on of hands—for we do not pause over staffs, rings, miters,
and anointings—and the words are the same, why is the sacrament not also the
same? The reason which moves Bellarmine to maintain that they are two distinct
sacraments is that otherwise he cannot maintain that bishops are greater than
presbyters in ordination and jurisdiction. *Second,* he says, *because if the
episcopate is a sacrament distinct from the presbyterate, it will be easy to
defend that by divine right a bishop is greater than a presbyter both in
Order and jurisdiction, which all heretics at this time deny; otherwise this
could scarcely be defended.* But of the scholastics, some do not even regard
episcopal ordination as a sacrament; others regard it as not precisely
distinct from the presbyterate, but as the same sacrament as the
presbyterate; and we have proved from other sources that it is wholly the
same. Therefore it follows that it cannot be defended that a bishop is
greater than a presbyter in Order and jurisdiction by divine right.
*Inextricable and insoluble difficulties arise if Order is held to be a true
sacrament*—which the Roman and English hierarchists do—*and episcopate and
presbyterate are asserted to be two different orders, one superior and the
other inferior. Almost all the canonists on canon “Cleros,” Distinction 21,
and canon “Perlectis,” Distinction 25, and with them not a few of the older
scholastics, and lastly Bellarmine, make the episcopate new and distinct from
the presbyterate. And certainly, once it is posited that Order is properly a
sacrament, since sacramental operation consists in something indivisible,
they are altogether compelled to this if they also steadfastly assert that
by divine right bishops are superior to presbyters. Otherwise, if the
ordination too is not distinct, they can by no reasoning defend that claim,
as Bellarmine expressly admits.* When the ancients enumerate three ranks,
namely bishops, presbyters, and deacons, they enumerate the ranks prevailing
in the church in their own centuries. Nor in all centuries after the time of
the apostles was the distance between the first two the same. At first the
rank of bishops was only one of priority and honor. Then it was a rank of
power with respect to external jurisdiction, which was smaller or greater
according to the breadth of the times. But with respect to the rank of power
for dispensing all the sacraments, bishop and presbyter were always, even in
the more corrupt centuries, of the same rank. And this rank of power and
Order are the same. For Order in the second sense, as it is taken for a rank
of power, is said by Durandus to be a spiritual power

dispensing the sacraments; and the power of Order is conferred by sacramental
ordination; and the ordination of bishops and presbyters is one and the same,
and the sacrament the same, it necessarily follows that they are of one and
the same Order or rank, insofar as rank is taken for the rank of the power of
dispensing the sacraments. For we shall deal with the power of jurisdiction
later. From the same passages from which they gather that the ordinatory
laying on of hands is a sacrament, we gather that its ordinary ministers were
presbyters—1 Tim. 4:14. For the apostle says that the presbytery, that is, the
senate of presbyters, laid hands upon Timothy. If the senate of presbyters
laid hands together with Paul, an extraordinary minister, in promoting him to
an extraordinary function, there is no doubt that, once extraordinary and
temporary ministers and functions were removed, the apostles entrusted the
ordinary ordinations of ordinary ministers to presbyters as the ordinary
ministers. We proved above that by the term *presbytery* the senate of
presbyters is understood. The compilers of that fictitious Fourth Council of
Carthage seem to have looked to this passage when they decreed in this way:
*When a presbyter is ordained, while the bishop blesses him and holds a hand
upon his head, all the presbyters who are present should also hold their hands
beside the bishop's hands upon his head.* Whatever we may think of the
authority of that council, nevertheless it is sufficiently clear from the
Roman Pontifical and the Roman Order that the custom was ancient. Bonaventure
calls this custom law. *But as to what is said concerning the laying on of the
presbyter's hands,* he says, *it must be said that “presbyter” there is called
an elder, and that the apostle understood himself by the laying on of the
presbyter's hand; the gloss calls the presbyter a bishop. Or “of the
presbyter,” that is, “of the presbyters,” because, as the law says, at least
three priests ought to lay on hands.* You see how he calls the laying on of
presbyters' hands law, and moreover in Paul's passage interprets “of the
presbyter” synecdochically as the assembly of presbyters. What if he had read
*presbytery*? With one simple interpretation he would have said, “presbytery,”
that is, the assembly or senate of presbyters. Nevertheless Bonaventure adds
in the same place: *But they do not lay on hands as giving the power or the
Order, which can and ought to be given by one alone. Hence they do not bless,
but the bishop alone does; rather, this is a sign of his consecration.* The
same author elsewhere says: *It must be said that this laying on of hands is
not for the conferral of ordination but for signification. For it signifies

laying on of hands flows from the power of Order, and by virtue of their Order
they have power to lay on hands. If they had laid on hands only for the sake

the words of consecration, they attained their rank, although he acted against

country-bishops did, and this without any permission or letters of the bishop;
and their ordination was valid, although they had ordained in another's
parish. Much more, therefore, was an ordination valid if celebrated in their

presbyter to ordain, it is an indubitable sign that a presbyter has the same

ecclesiastical canon, not removed by divine right. Dounamus, perhaps having
forgotten that hackneyed axiom, or rather conquered elsewhere by the force of
truth, admits that bishops in the primitive church communicated this power of
ordination to others. We proved this quite clearly from the aforesaid canon of
the Council of Ancyra, where we also observed that before the canon was
enacted it is indicated that city presbyters ordained presbyters without the
letters and permission of the bishop, and that even after the canon was
enacted they were not forbidden to ordain presbyters in their own parish
without authority from episcopal letters. The things Durandus asserts about
the minister of Confirmation can much more be affirmed about the minister of
ordination too. *Supposing that confirmation or ordination is a true
sacrament instituted by Christ, and by Christ's institution the bishop alone
is the minister of Confirmation or Ordination, in no way can a simple priest,
by commission of the pope*—much less of an inferior bishop—*confer this
sacrament. The first reason: of the elements of every sacrament there are the
matter, form, and minister. But the pope or bishop can change nothing about
the form or matter of the sacraments; therefore neither can he change anything
about the minister. Thus either a simple priest by divine institution is a
minister of confirmation*—or ordination—*and then he can confer it without a
commission of the pope* or bishop, *or he is not a minister, but the bishop
alone is, and then by any commission whatever he can do nothing. It does not
matter that the minister stands externally to the sacrament while the matter
and form stand internally; for just as the minister conferring it stands
externally to the sacrament, so does the person receiving it. But when the
person receiving a sacrament is determined, as a male is in Order, this cannot
be changed by the pope or bishop; and if it is done, the sacrament is null, as
if a woman were ordained. Therefore absolutely nothing about the minister can
be changed without emptying the sacrament, although he stands externally to
the sacrament. Second, dispensing sacraments whose acts regard the mystical
body belongs to the pope and bishops either by reason of jurisdiction or by
reason of consecration. Not by reason of jurisdiction, because when an
elected pope and a confirmed bishop have full jurisdiction before they are
consecrated, they could then dispense or confer this sacrament, which is
false. Therefore it belongs to them not by reason of jurisdiction but of
consecration. But things which require consecration cannot be committed to an
unconsecrated man. Therefore a pope or bishop cannot commit to a non-bishop
the ability to confer such sacraments. When administering sacraments or even
sacramentals belongs to someone by reason of Order, no one can dispense so
that another not so ordained may administer them, unless he is the institutor

concerning the minister, but one less than the pope, namely any other bishop,

Finally, to reduce the preceding discussion to a few points: even granting
that the ordinatory laying on of hands is a sacrament, Scripture makes it
plain that by divine ordinance its power belongs to every presbyter, as even
the adversaries admit; that its exercise was never removed by the apostles,
nor by the church immediately after the apostles, but ordinations celebrated
even by presbyters were valid; and that when they were regarded as invalid,
this did not arise absolutely from the nature of the thing but from the rigor
of the canons, as happened in other ordinations which in these most recent
centuries are not only valid but also usual in the Roman and English church.

devolved upon presbyters in a case of both avoidable and unavoidable necessity

Bede's history that abbots consecrated even bishops outside a case of
unavoidable necessity; and that they can do so in a case of unavoidable
necessity is testified by Auxerre and those doctors of whom Alexander of
Hales spoke above. Nevertheless, the supreme injury inflicted upon the divine
institution in the more corrupt centuries by declaring ordinations by
presbyters invalid must not be concealed. For as to Durandus's statement that
*the church can restrict the minister by its statute, but cannot invalidate a
sacrament when the things necessary to the sacrament concur,* the first part
is partly true: the power can be regulated and directed, provided that its
ordinary exercise is not removed, so that all things are done orderly and
decently for the edification of the church. The latter part is true: the
church cannot decree invalid and null the sacrament of the Eucharist
celebrated by a lawful and true presbyter who has this power from divine
institution. So it is with the other sacraments, and consequently with
ordination if it is a sacrament, as we have thus far granted. It now follows
to prove that ordination is not a sacrament, and consequently that presbyters
can much more lay on hands and consecrate the ordinands. For no consecration
is greater than a sacramental one. Therefore, if a presbyter can consecrate in
a sacrament, he can also consecrate outside a sacrament. That ordination is
not a sacrament is proved as follows. If ordination is a sacrament which

Bridegroom and Bride is common, and its common ministers are instituted by
Christ and the church, whom we call bishops, presbyters, or pastors. Thus
Christ has the whole power of ordination jointly and severally, which he
shares with his church, each according to its own right. The Bridegroom, I
say, and the Bride deliver it to the presbytery by divine right. Afterward,
however, by human right the presbytery conferred this power upon those
specifically called bishops. From this followed a usurpation, indeed very
ancient but not for that reason more just, in which tyranny has long
entrenched itself.* Since no one ought to be ordained to ministry
ἀπολελυμένως, without attachment, but specifically to the ministry of this
or that church, it is equitable that he be ordained by that particular church
in its own, ἰδίᾳ, ministry, in the common name of that church. In ordination there
are various acts and rites: blessings and prayers; dedication and assignment
to the ministry; authoritative mission, as Spalatensis calls it, or rather
induction into possession of the office and confirmation of the preceding
election; and also the laying on of hands, whether as a gesture of one praying
and blessing or also of one ordaining and inducting into possession of the
office, as in those promoted to the rank of Master in the schools of the
rabbis. All these things are done by the authority of God and the church. And
when one man prays and lays on hands, he does it in the name of the church and
presbytery. *Thus God ordains through the church; the church ordains through
the presbytery; the presbytery ordains through its bishops and pastors: each
confers what belongs to it.* When bishops and teaching presbyters fail, the
hierarchists themselves admit that elders can lay on hands, and that one of
them can pray, bless, and as it were present the ordinand to God in the name
of the church. I add that when elders too fail, the church can appoint one
from its own number to lay on hands and induct, in the name of the church, the
man designated by election into possession of the office. For the laying on
of hands, as we said, is not a sacrament which by divine institution requires
its own proper minister. And if Dounamus conceded this right of ordaining to
the body of the church even on the assumption that ordination is a sacrament,
as we showed above from Dounamus, how much more must it be conceded if it is
not a sacrament? Thus the church will never perish, because in a case of
necessity godly and faithful people gathered in Christ's name can elect and
induct the elect man into ministry. *There is no assembly of the faithful so
small, associated in the true faith, which, if it is placed in that extreme
necessity, does not have as much power in ecclesiastical matters as theologians
maintain was given and left by the universal Lord*

*of the church for its edification, both in the doctrine of faith and in the
discipline of morals. The common opinion of the theologians is that every
ecclesiastical power is signified by the name of the keys and that they were
given not only to persons but also to the universal church,* says Saravia.
Does not Bucer say the same thing in other words? *God delivered the power of
ordaining not simply to individual persons but to the church, and ordinarily
through it consecrates ministers to himself.* Therefore, radically and
originally, the power of ordaining is in the church always, even outside a
case of necessity. The church's ordinary instrument for administering the
rites of initiation is the pastor, who is constituted for men in the things
to be done before God. Outside the regular order, in constituting a church or
restoring one wholly collapsed, if a pastor is lacking, the instrument is the
man whom the assembly of the godly elects to celebrate that act of ordination
in its name. From what has been said it is plain that the presbytery no less
than the bishop can initiate by the rites of ordination in the name of the
church, since it is constituted by divine right for men in the things to be
done before God. And when the bishop, who presides over the presbytery and
church by human right alone, claims the entire act of ordination for himself
alone, presbyters ought to reclaim and recover the ordinary possession of
their right. For positive laws, when they first begin to corrupt their own
way, as Panormitanus speaks, are to be made obsolete. And this is enough
concerning the power of ordination, which they refer to the power of Order
because they thought ordination was a sacrament. But since it is not a
sacrament, and since, in addition to the blessings and prayers which
Spalatensis says are not absolutely essential to holy Order, there is the
authoritative mission, as he calls it, or induction into possession of the
office for which the ordinand was previously designated by election, in this
respect it ought rather to be referred to the power of jurisdiction. For to
promote the ordinand to some office or function and to confirm the preceding
election belongs to jurisdiction, as does the election itself which is
confirmed. Therefore, the consecration and sanctification of a man to the
ministry must be distinguished from the conferral of power or confirmation
of election, although they are performed at the same time.
