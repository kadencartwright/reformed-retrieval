ON

THE CAUSE OF GOD AGAINST THE ANTI-SABBATARIANS

BOOK I

On DIVINE LAWS, especially the NATURAL, MORAL, MORAL-POSITIVE, and CEREMONIAL
LAW.

Because the adversaries with whom we are dealing here—namely, the
Anti-Sabbatarians, or those who deny that the precept concerning the weekly
Sabbath binds us under the gospel, or that by divine precept we are bound to
consecrate one whole day in the cycle of seven to God—rely chiefly upon this
foundation: that the precept concerning observance of the weekly Sabbath,
which is the fourth commandment of the Decalogue, was not moral. And in order
to make this foundation firmer, they fashion for us such a definition of the
moral law that it appears to be the same as the natural law, and explain both
to us in such a way that it is more than certain that the fourth precept of the
Decalogue can by no means be called natural or moral. Furthermore, they laugh
at us when we make any mention of a moral-positive law. Here, as is well
known, they constantly take refuge and resort as though to an asylum. I have
therefore thought it worth the effort to weigh their doctrine on this matter
more carefully and bring it to the anvil, and to do so first, so that the
things to be said in the course of this disputation may become clearer.

But in order that we may explain the nature of the Natural and Moral law more
distinctly, and at the same time make it manifest that there are divine laws
which may quite properly be called moral-positive, several other matters must
also be explained here: namely, what a properly ceremonial law is (an
explanation of which will also serve our principal aim very greatly); what a
judicial law is; what right is natural to God; what right is natural and
created for us; and similar matters. We also calmly examine and reject the
marks or characteristics—whether of the Moral or of the Natural law—which the
adversaries propose; while we present and vindicate the true and accepted
ones.

Therefore in this Book I we explain more fully the things we judge necessary
to know for discussing this controversy: the nature of laws in general, and
of all Divine laws—Natu—

[1674 logical printed p. 2 | IA leaf 20 | PDF p. 20]

—ral, Moral, Ceremonial, Judicial, and positive, as well as moral-positive
laws—and we proceed in this order.

Chapter I. We first set down certain matters concerning the nature of law in
general.

Chapter II. We explain the nature of Divine law.

Chapter III. We inquire briefly what should be said about this question:
whether all moral goodness and evil in our actions consist in the will and
constitution of God, who commands some things and prohibits others.

Chapter IV. We treat God's natural and positive right.

Chapter V. We explain more fully what created Natural right is.

Chapter VI. We treat the law of nature, which we explain, and briefly examine
various questions raised about it.

Chapter VII. We vindicate the commonly accepted division of Divine laws into
Moral, Ceremonial, and Forensic laws against the exceptions of Louis de
Moulin.

Chapter VIII. We explain the nature of Ceremonial law.

Chapter IX. We explain the nature of Forensic or judicial law.

Chapter X. In expounding the nature of Moral law, we treat various questions
about it and compare the differing conceptions even of orthodox men.

Chapter XI. We examine and show that the false marks of Moral law which the
adversaries offer us and for which they contend sharply must be rejected.

Chapter XII. We propose, explain, and vindicate some true marks of Moral law
from which it may be abundantly manifest that the precept concerning the
weekly Sabbath is moral.

Chapter XIII. We carefully examine the opinion of Dr. Burman concerning moral
law, which he sets out more fully in chapter 1, page 4 and following of his
*Apology for the Vindication of the Inquiry into the Morality of the Weekly
Sabbath*.

Chapter XIV. We show that no Moral law should be called purely positive on
account of some positive determination attached to it; and we calmly examine
what Dr. Burman says to the contrary in the cited book.

Chapter XV. We treat the moral-positive precept more fully and bring Dr.
Burman's doctrine on the matter in the said book to the anvil.

CHAPTER I

On Law in General

According to some, *lex* derives from *lego*, because, that is, it is read
publicly (see Varro, book 5). But according to Isidore, book 2, chapter 10, it
is called *lex* from *legendo*, because it is written; or rather, as Perotti
holds (according to Martinius in the *Philological Lexicon*, page 1486),
because it is read publicly, so that it may be known to all and observed by
all. Cicero, however, in book 1 of *On the Laws*, appears to derive it from
*lego* as meaning the same as *deligo* or *eligo*, because a selection is made
in a law, or because a law is some selected opinion. “Every institution,” he
says, “which we follow when we choose what we are to do and avoid its contrary
may be called a law by the common designation.” Others, however, hold, not
improbably, that *lex* is derived from *ligando*, because it binds subjects to
obedience or binds human freedom and restricts it to certain things. Martinius,
at the place cited, says that no other origin or rationale of the word should
be sought than that which was set down at the beginning: *lex* from *lego*, as
*Rex* is from *Rego*. He also says that λόγος means law among the Greeks, as
δεκάλογος means ten words—that is, ten precepts or laws—and that the Septuagint
rendered the Hebrew דבר by λόγοι at Leviticus 7:19 and Jeremiah 19:15, and
also by προστάγματα at Deuteronomy 15:1 and 19:4, Joshua 6:4, and 1 Chronicles
26:32, and by νόμοι at Psalm 75:1, 9, 104. Moreover, λόγος in Plato signifies
law. And finally, *lex* is the same as λέξις, that is, a spoken thing: “The use
of speaking,” he says, “is older and more frequent than that of writing; for
hearing belongs to everyone, reading to few.” We leave this controversy for
others to decide, since it will make no difference to us from where the word
is derived.

Among the Greeks law was formerly called θεσμός, because it was something
posited, established, or instituted. Now, however, it is commonly called νόμος,
a word unknown to Homer and the other most ancient writers, according to
Josephus in book 2 *Against Apion*, where, speaking of Moses, he says: “I say
that our legislator is more ancient than all the legislators remembered
anywhere. For Lycurgus and Solon and Zaleucus of the Locrians, and finally all
those admired among the Greeks, are proved to have been quite recent if they
are compared with him; indeed, the very name for law (νόμος) was formerly
unknown among the Greeks, as Homer too bears witness, since he nowhere uses it
in his poem. For in his time there was no law; rather, the people were ruled by
indeterminate opinions and the commands of kings. Hence for a long time they
used unwritten customs and were accustomed to alter many things as
circumstances arose.” Now νόμος is said to derive ἀπὸ τοῦ νέμειν, from
distributing—

[1674 logical printed p. 4 | IA leaf 22 | PDF p. 22]

—for it signifies “I assign,” namely because law assigns to each his own. For
law ought to be an indicator of justice, which is the virtue by whose habit we
are inclined to give and render to each person what we owe. See the lexicons of
Martinius and Calvinus.

Among the Hebrews it is called תורה, doctrine or instruction, from ירה, “he
cast” or “projected,” which in the Hiphil sometimes means “he taught,
instructed, or educated.” This indicates that law is doctrine which educates
and teaches us what the legislator's will is, or how we ought to live. That
this is indeed the nature of law is indicated by Cicero in book 1 of *On the
Laws*: “The fact is,” he says, “that since law ought to correct vices and
commend virtues, the doctrine of living should be sought from it.” There are
other words in Scripture signifying divine law, which we shall discuss in the
next chapter.

The word *law* is taken in various senses. Among jurists, in contracts, it is
taken for an *agreement*, according to Calvinus in the *Legal Lexicon*.
Sometimes it denotes some *condition*, as Plautus says in the *Asses*: “Come,
show the bond you drew up, and read through the terms between me, my mistress,
and the procurer.” Hence we frequently say, “on this term,” that is, on this
condition. For a condition is like a command or prohibition, since the action
to which it is attached will not be valid without it. Terence says in the
*Eunuch*: “But see here: bind my fidelity to you by this term.” It also
sometimes denotes a condition or state, as Cicero says in the third *Tusculan
Disputation*: “We were born on this condition, that no one can remain forever
free from evil.” Sometimes it denotes a rule, norm, and custom, as Cicero says
*For Murena*: “A certain prosecutorial rule, rather than a true freedom to
revile, has compelled those men to say something about the life of Lucius
Murena.” The same author says in *On Friendship*: “Let this therefore be
established as the first law in friendship: that we neither ask for shameful
things nor do them when asked.” And in book 1 of *On the Laws*, chapter 4, he
says that one set of laws is to be observed in history and another in poetry.
Elsewhere he says: “In verse there is a certain and definite law which must be
followed”; and elsewhere, “The speech must be directed and formed according to
this law.”

Under the designation “laws” (says Calepinus in his *Vocabulary*) are included
the laws of the Twelve Tables, decrees of the senate, plebiscites, edicts of
the praetors, decrees of the emperors, legal actions, interpretations of the
jurists, and other things of that kind. Sometimes, moreover, it means a
*privilege*, which is not a general command concerning all citizens but one
framed concerning particular persons; for what we call *singula*, the ancients
called *priva*. Thus Cicero says *For His House*: “By what right, what custom,
what precedent did you carry a law expressly concerning the life of an
uncondemned citizen? The sacred laws forbid it; the Twelve Tables forbid laws
to be imposed on private persons—for that is a privilege.” It likewise
sometimes denotes *command*, as Cicero says in book 3 of *On the Laws*, chapter
3: “When I say ‘law,’ I wish nothing else to be understood than command,
without which neither any household, nor city, nor nation, nor the whole human
race can stand, nor the whole nature of things, nor the world itself.” It also
sometimes signifies the end intended by legislators, as in that familiar
maxim, “Let the people's welfare be the supreme law.” Sometimes it expresses
the legislator's internal intention or conception; hence the expression
*eternal law*, of which later.

The same word sometimes denotes Reason itself, Judgment, or a commanding Will.
Thus Cicero in book 1 of *On the Laws*: “Law is the force of nature, the mind
and reason of the prudent; it is the rule of right and wrong.” And in the same
place: “If, as nature is, people were humane in judgment and—as the poet
says—regarded nothing human as alien to them, right would be cultivated equally
by everyone. For those to whom reason was given by nature were also given right
reason; therefore they were also given law, which is right reason in commanding
and forbidding. If law, then right too was given to all.” Thus he distinguishes
law from right. Again in the same place: “Law is the highest reason implanted
in nature, which commands the things that ought to be done and forbids their—

[1674 logical printed p. 5 | IA leaf 23 | PDF p. 23]

—contraries. When that same reason has been confirmed and completed in the
human mind, it is law. Therefore they judge prudence to be law, whose force is
to command right action and forbid transgression. They named the matter with a
Greek term derived from assigning to each his own; I derive it from choosing,
for as they place the force of equity in law, so we place in it the force of
selection—and yet both are properties of law. Law is nothing other than right
reason proceeding from the divine power, commanding honorable things and
forbidding their contraries.” And in book 2 of *On the Laws*: “I see that the
wisest men held this opinion: that law was neither contrived by human intellects
nor some ordinance of peoples, but something eternal which ruled the whole
world by the wisdom of commanding and forbidding. Thus they said that the first
and ultimate law was the mind of God, compelling or forbidding all things by
reason; from it the law which the gods gave to the human race is rightly
praised.”

[Marginalia: Law. The force of law.]

Furthermore, the word is sometimes taken more broadly for the *connection of
physical causes*, or for that natural order established among all creatures,
even inanimate things and brute animals. Sometimes it is taken more strictly,
when it denotes the same thing as *natural right*, common to human and brute;
but this is said improperly, since (according to P. Voet in his *Commentary on
the Institutes*, book 1, title 2, page 51) it is nothing other than a certain
divine power implanted by the Creator himself in created things, or a certain
instinct of nature common to all animals endowed with sensory life, which
merits neither praise nor blame and is not determined by moral circumstances.
Hence Cicero's statement *For Milo*: “For this, judges, is not a written but an
inborn law, which we did not learn, receive, or read, but seized, drew, and
expressed from nature herself—a law for which we were not taught but made, not
instructed but imbued.” When taken more strictly and referred only to humans,
it is sometimes used so broadly as to include even admonitions and counsels.
Although these may perhaps contain some power of binding on the part of the
one who ought to do something, as P. Voet says at the place cited, they
nevertheless properly denote no authority in the one admonishing and
counselling, or any power to give laws to others.

Hence there are various and distinct definitions or descriptions of law. We
have already seen several given by Cicero. He also says that “law is that which
establishes in writing what it wills, either by commanding or forbidding, as
the common people put it” (*On the Laws*, book 1, section 19). Elsewhere he
describes it thus: “Law is the distinction between just and unjust things,
expressed according to the most ancient and primary nature of all things; human
laws are directed by it, punishing the wicked and defending and protecting the
good.” Some jurists call law “a common precept; the counsel of prudent men; the
restraint of offenses committed voluntarily or in ignorance; the common pledge
of the commonwealth.” Demosthenes said that law is “that which all people ought
to obey, for many reasons, but especially because every law is a certain
invention and gift of the gods.” Seneca, in book 4 of *On Benefits*, chapter
12, holds it to be “the rule of justice and injustice,” namely of doing what is
just, avoiding what is unjust, and judging both. In book 1 of *On Clemency* he
describes it thus: “It is the bond by which the commonwealth coheres and the
vital spirit which the polity draws, so that without law the commonwealth will
be nothing but a burden and prey.”

Keckermann, *System of Politics*, book 1, chapter 6, defines law insofar as it
is the instrument of government in general as follows: “It is the rule of
those things that pertain to the wholesome administration of a kingdom”—that
is, a Rule of things to be done or omitted. And he says that the Law for
governing a Kingdom is “the political norm by which a Prince governs his
subjects and administers the kingdom.” Hoenonius, *Political Disputation* 1,
thesis 9, calls political law “an opinion concerning the duty of a citizen.”
Grotius, *On the Law of War and Peace*, book 1, chapter 1, section 9, defines
law taken in the broadest sense as “a rule of moral acts binding one to what is
right.” But here—

[1674 logical printed p. 6 | IA leaf 24 | PDF p. 24]

—no mention is made of the legislator, who ought not merely to be included but
also expressed, so that by this rationale a political rule might be
distinguished from others. Aquinas, I–II, question 90, article 1, defines law
thus: “Law is a certain rule and measure of acts, according to which someone is
induced to act or restrained from acting.” But as Suarez rightly says, this
definition applies to natural things (as it also does to artificial things),
which have their own rules and measures of operation. Suarez himself defines
it thus: “It is a certain measure of moral acts, so that through conformity to
it they possess moral rectitude, and if they disagree with it they are
crooked.”

Amid so great a variety of definitions or descriptions, let me too propose and
explain the one which most commends itself to me. I therefore think that law
may be defined well in this way: that it is “a sign or signification of the
will of a Ruler or legislator, constituting the duty owed by subjects and
determining rewards for the obedient but punishments for the disobedient.” Or,
that it is “an authoritative constitution concerning the duty, reward, and
punishment owed, for the ends of government.”

For this definition to be rendered clearer, certain points must be noted and
explained.

1. By a *Ruler or legislator* we may understand all who are endowed with
authority, so that under this definition may be comprehended both divine laws
and human laws—all human laws, whether from supreme political Magistrates,
Parents, Masters or Owners, Guardians, or governors of other associations,
whether civil or military. For in all these and similar relations there is true
government, and the laws by which the respective Subjects are governed are
also true and properly so called.

2. I hold that law is a sign of the *Ruler's will*, because it belongs to the
Ruler alone to make laws; and laws are properly authoritative constitutions
flowing from his authority.

3. I say *will*, not because law flows exclusively from the will with the
intellect excluded, provided it is given with order and deliberation, but
because in the making of laws the will—which is the commanding faculty and
therefore shines forth chiefly in commands—presupposes or includes an act of
the intellect and is more directly regarded and most nearly comes into
consideration. This is especially so in divine laws, which we are bound to obey
promptly even if we do not grasp their reason distinctly and even if their
right and equity do not appear to us at first sight.

4. I hold that law is a *sign or signification*, because law can be taken
either actively or passively. Taken passively, it is the effect of legislation,
or the constitution itself when effected and produced. In this way, although
it may also be called a *signification* without impropriety, it is most
properly called a *sign*. Taken actively, it connotes or comprehends in itself
the legislation itself, or the act of the legislator producing such an
effect. Thus this definition can be applied whichever way the term is taken.

5. I say *sign* because it is necessary that this will of the Ruler be known,
and it can become known to Subjects only through signs. But by what particular
sign this will of the Ruler is declared—whether in writing, by a living voice,
or in any other way; whether he reveals his mind to the mind through the common
and evident dictate of natural reason or through singular inspiration (which
can be said of God alone), to the ear through spoken instruction, or to the
eyes through writing—it comes to the same thing, provided that sign, whatever
it may be, is a sufficient and suitable indication of the will. Indeed, it is
not worth our effort to inquire whether promulgation of a law, that is, its
public and solemn declaration, is necessarily required for the very essence of
law, or only as a condition without which obligation cannot arise.

[1674 logical printed p. 7 | IA leaf 25 | PDF p. 25]

6. I say “a *sign constituting*,” and so forth, because legislation is thus
distinguished from a judgment or judicial sentence pronounced according to an
existing law. The effect of this judgment is not some constitution, but rather
the application and execution of a constitution already made. Here, however,
we are speaking about the effect of legislation, not the effect of a judgment
or of a sentence pronounced by a judge.

7. I say “*constituting what is owed*,” and so forth, because from this
constitution there arises a duty owed by Subjects; that is, the constitution
determines what the subjects' duty is in this or that case. And, provided the
subject is obedient, it determines what reward he may expect by right, or what
punishment if he violates the law. Nevertheless, I do not decide here the
question whether this debt of punishment or reward is necessarily included in
every law. I know that some assert that human laws exist which establish only
a right to have or possess, without any immediate and direct obligation to
obedience or punishment, and also that purely reward-conferring laws exist
without any obligation to punishment. Here, however, I am speaking of law as
commonly understood. And it is certain that such law establishes what is owed,
both of punishment and reward, because it establishes the duty antecedently
owed.

The jurists have sufficiently indicated how *law* is distinguished from a
*plebiscite*, a *decree of the senate*, an *edict of the praetors*, and other
such things. It is not fitting to say anything about this here.

It is clear from what has been said:

1. A law is properly enough called a law even if it was given to very few. For
the essence of law does not require that it be given to absolutely all
subjects. It is enough if it is a sign of the legislator's will establishing
the duty owed by the Subject or Subjects. A law established by a Ruler for one
person partakes of the nature of law no less than one given to many.

2. A law as such does not need to bind forever or for some long period. A law
established for only a day is no less a law than one possessing binding force
for several years. For a Legislator or Ruler can signify his will for a short
time just as for a longer one, and by that sign establish a duty owed by the
Subject or Subjects, or declare what one subject to the Ruler ought to do or
omit for the prescribed time.

3. A law in which a punishment is expressly attached, or in which mention is
made of punishment owed for transgression, determines nothing about the event.
For by establishing that punishment is owed, the law does not say that the
transgressor will in fact be punished, but only that by right someone who
transgresses may and ought to undergo the penalties imposed by law—unless, of
course, some dispensation, remission, satisfaction, or something of that kind
intervenes.

The modifications of laws are various, namely:

1. *Proposal*. Among the Romans no law was carried unless a Magistrate proposed
it. Hence Festus says that *rogat* means “he consults the people, or asks them
to resolve what he is proposing.” Gellius reports these words from Ateius
Capito: “A law is a general command of the people or plebs when a Magistrate
proposes it; a plebiscite is a law which the plebs, not the people as a whole,
accepted. But the very head, origin, and fountain, as it were, of this entire
matter and right—whether the people or plebs is asked, or whether it pertains
to all—is the proposal itself. For all these terms are classified and contained
under the principal kind and name of proposal; unless the people or plebs is
asked, no command of plebs or people can be made.” It is true that the word
*proposal* sometimes signifies the legislation itself or the law—

[1674 logical printed p. 8 | IA leaf 26 | PDF p. 26]

—as when it is said that a proposal is a kind of law, and that it cannot fail
to be a law provided it was proposed in a lawful assembly.

[Editorial note: The errata leaf at PDF p. 774 directs that printed p. 7 line
39, `sciscat`, be read as `sciat`; the corrected Latin reading underlies the
translation above.]

2. *Promulgation*. Laws are said to be promulgated when they are first issued
among the people, as though published abroad.

3. *Abrogation*. By this, after opinions have been solicited, a law is wholly
removed; that is, because of a contrary constitution or custom, the cessation
of its cause, a difference of place or time, its severity, some greater evil
arising from it, or similar factors (see Livy, book 9, *From the Founding of the
City*), the law is completely removed and made obsolete. This was formerly
done by a Magistrate when the people were asked whether they willed and
commanded that law to be removed and gave their assent, just as the law had
been enacted and promulgated when the same people were asked. Thus formerly to
abrogate a law was nothing other than to ask the people that some law no
longer have force and be entirely removed.

4. *Derogation*. By this something is taken away from an old law through a new
proposal; that is, the law is diminished or removed in part. It occurs properly
when a new law ordains that something in an old law is not to be done. Thus to
derogate is the same as to except, that is, to exempt something from an old law
through a new law. Formerly a law was derogated when the people were asked to
remove certain provisions from an old law.

5. *Subrogation* occurs when something is added to an earlier law.

6. A law was said to be *excepted* when something was exempted from an old law
through a new law.

7. *Obrogation* is when something in the first law is altered, or when another
law is carried for the purpose of weakening the earlier one.

8. *Dispensation* is when, in some case in which a law truly has force and
binds, its obligation is removed from one particular person while the rest
remain bound.

9. *Interpretation* is either the same as a relaxation of the law, when strict
right is not enforced, or the same as declaring that a law does not bind in
some case.

10. *Invalidation* is when a law is abolished before it binds fully; for to
invalidate is to will, show, or make something not ratified. So much for these
matters.

CHAPTER II

On the Law of God

The nature of a human being, who is a rational and social animal subject to
error, declares that he must be ruled by moral means, that is, by laws, to the
glory of God. For if a human being, constituted and existing as he now is,
were not governed morally in this way, he would soon be undone. “Laws were
made,” says Isidore, book 2, chapter 10, “so that human audacity might be
restrained by fear of them, innocence might be safe among the wicked, and in
the wicked themselves the capacity to harm might be curbed by dread of
punishment.” But if laws were abrogated, says Demosthenes in his oration
*Against Aristogeiton*, “and everyone were given license to do whatever he
wished, not only would the commonwealth be ruined, but there would be no
difference between our life and that of wild beasts.” This would follow all the
more if there were no Divine laws. For since there are as many opinions as
people, and everyone is naturally prone to evil, everything would be full of—

[1674 logical printed p. 9 | IA leaf 27 | PDF p. 27]

—slaughter, butchery, and barbarity; everyone would pursue and devour another
out of hostility. Thus the world would be a mere slaughterhouse, so that it
would be safer to live among wild animals than among human beings; for nothing
would restrain anyone from evil except greater force. And thus all virtue and
vice would be abolished. Just as no virtue flourishes and no vice reigns among
brute animals, among whom laws have no place, so if stronger people trampled,
destroyed, and swallowed up the weaker after the manner of the fish of the sea
(Habakkuk 1:13–14), they would be marked by no vice and would be free from all
fault. For “where there is no law, there is no transgression”; nor would a
human being then do anything worthy of praise. “If right confirmed by nature,”
says Cicero in book 1 of *On the Laws*, “does not exist, all the virtues will
be abolished. For where will liberality be? Where love of country? Where piety?
Where will either the will to deserve well of another or to render thanks be
able to exist? These things arise from the fact that we are naturally inclined
to love human beings, which is the foundation of right. Not only duties toward
humans but also ceremonies and religious duties toward the gods would be
abolished; and I judge that these should be preserved, not through fear, but
through that connection which a human being has with God.”

Since nature herself dictates this even to those who do not enjoy a clearer
light, what person merely Christian in name, but in reality more atheistic than
any atheist, would not be ashamed to assert that God does not govern this world
by laws, but only by physical premotions, or in the way people govern automata
and a shipmaster governs a ship? Would not all difference between humans and
beasts be removed on this account? Do not those who are not ashamed to assert
such things set out to abolish all foundations of right, all commonwealths, all
order among humans, all human laws, and all religion? For once God's government
is removed, every human government is removed. Yet who does not know that some
government flourishes even among the most savage nations? Indeed, those who
assert this consequently deny that God is governor, possesses a kingdom, or
exercises distributive justice, contrary to almost innumerable passages of
Scripture. They will also deny that we are guilty of sin, that God forgives
sins or punishes anyone on account of sins, and that Christ made satisfaction
to God for sins. They must also deny eternal punishments and rewards, and thus
must overturn absolutely all religion. Indeed, so far as they can, they banish
all humanity whatever.

But not only all Scripture and God's works among humans and Angels testify
that God is the supreme governor of all and actually governs rational creatures
by laws; the very light of nature teaches this to everyone. What else does the
human conscience, excusing and accusing, declare except that people are subject
to God's laws and liable to punishments when they violate them? Who would deny
the authority and power of Parents, Husbands, Masters, and Magistrates over
children, wives, servants, and subjects, or say that the latter are by no means
bound to submit or obey? Indeed, whoever denies this government of God is
consequently bound also to deny that the human heart is subject to any
government. The reason is plain: no human laws reach the heart and inward
thoughts or prescribe a manner for them. But who will say that the outward and
not also the inward person is subject to vices? Who will deny that inward
lusts and other impieties, not yet carried into outward act, are vices? Such a
person will even deny that hatred of God itself is sin. Indeed, against plain
experience, he will deny that there is now any government in the world. For
from where would people have this power to govern others except from God, the
highest and supreme governor of all? Indeed, human laws must presuppose some
supreme right which binds the legislators themselves—

[1674 logical printed p. 10 | IA leaf 28 | PDF p. 28]

—“For if,” says Cicero in book 1 of *On the Laws*, “rights were constituted by
the commands of peoples, decrees of rulers, or sentences of judges, then there
would be a right to robbery, adultery, and forging wills if these were approved
by the votes or ordinances of the multitude. If the opinions and commands of
fools have so much power that the nature of things is changed by their votes,
why do they not ordain that things which are evil and destructive should be
regarded as good and beneficial? Or since a law can make right out of injury,
why can the same law not make good out of evil? But we can distinguish a good
law from an evil one by no norm other than the natural.” Thus it is clear that
those men must also assert that nothing was good or evil, virtue or vice,
before their human laws concerning all things; that what is regarded as good
today can be pronounced evil tomorrow; and that whatever is commanded by
people is good and just. Cicero judged this most foolish in the same place,
pages 224–225 in my edition: “It is now most foolish,” he says, “to suppose
that everything enacted in the institutions or laws of peoples is just. Even
if these were the laws of tyrants? If the Thirty had wished to impose laws on
Athens, or if all the Athenians delighted in tyrannical laws, would these laws
therefore be regarded as just? No more, I think, than the law our interrex
carried, that the dictator could kill whatever citizen he wished with impunity
and without trial. For there is one right by which human society is bound and
which one law establishes. That law is right reason in commanding and
forbidding; whoever is ignorant of it is unjust, whether it is written anywhere
or nowhere.” Besides, even if this most foolish point were granted, from where,
I ask, will those who are lawmakers have this power? Is it not from God, from
whom they also have all things? And if it is from God, will God not also be the
supreme legislator and governor? Moreover, if God's supreme dominion were
removed and it were conceded—impossibly—that people would still possess power
to govern others and set laws for them, it would follow quite justly, provided
it could be done safely, that people could violate all those laws; for every
higher law binding the conscience to obedience would be removed. “But if,”
says Cicero a little later at the same place, “justice is obedience to written
laws and the institutions of peoples, and if, as the same men say, all things
are to be measured by utility, then whoever thinks it will be profitable to him
will neglect the laws and break through them if he can. Thus there is no justice
at all if it does not exist by nature, and if what is established on account of
utility is overturned by that same utility.”

I do not deny that God also governs human beings physically and disposes all
things as it has seemed good to him. Far be it from me to deny God's dominion
over our souls, intellect, and will, his predetermination of the will, or his
natural and gracious concurrence and influences. I maintain here only that God
rules rational people, even as rational, in a manner most suitable to a
rational creature: that is, by laws, which are arguments and means through
which a rational creature can be moved rationally. Indeed, those atheists who
deny this do not consider what kind of creature a rational human is and how far
he differs from brute animals, which are not urged by such motives but in a
very different manner suited to their natures. For if they considered human
nature itself better, they would say something else even at Cicero's warning
and instruction. Thus he reasons admirably at the place cited, page 219,
against those who said that God cared for nothing, whether his own affairs or
those of others: “This animal,” he says, “provident, sagacious, manifold,
acute, endowed with memory, full of reason and counsel, whom we call human,
was generated by the supreme God in a certain distinguished condition. For
alone among so many kinds and natures of animate beings, he partakes of reason
and thought, while all the rest are devoid of them. But what—not merely in the
human, but in all heaven and earth—is more divine than reason? When it—

[1674 logical printed p. 11 | IA leaf 29 | PDF p. 29]

—has matured and been perfected, it is rightly called wisdom. Since nothing is
better than reason, and it is both in the human and in God, the first fellowship
between human and God is one of reason. But those who share reason also share
right reason. Since this is law, humans must be considered associated with the
gods by law as well. Furthermore, those among whom there is a communion of law
also have a communion of right. Those who share these things must be regarded
as members of the same polity. But if they submit to the same commands and
powers, they submit much more to this heavenly order and to the divine mind and
supremely powerful God. Hence this whole world must be regarded as one polity
common to gods and humans.” And a little later, page 220: “Thus among so many
kinds, no animal except the human has any knowledge of God; and among humans
there is no nation so untamed or savage that, although it does not know what
kind of God it ought to have, it does not nevertheless know that one must be
had.” And afterward: “Nature herself adorned the human not only with agility of
mind but also assigned the senses as attendants and messengers. She disclosed
obscure and necessary understandings of many things, as though foundations of
knowledge, and gave a bodily form serviceable and suited to the human
intellect. For while she had cast the other animals down to their food, she
raised the human alone to the sight of heaven, as though to the recognition of
his kindred and former dwelling. Then she so formed the appearance of the face
that it might portray deeply hidden characters. For the remarkably expressive
eyes tell how we are affected in mind; and what is called the countenance,
which can exist in no animal except the human, indicates character. The Greeks
know its force, though they have no word for it at all. I pass over the other
conveniences and aptitudes of the body, the modulation of the voice, and the
power of speech, which is above all the conciliator of human society—Now since
God generated and adorned the human, whom he willed to be the first principle
of the remaining things, this is evident: lest everything be pursued at length,
nature herself advances farther by herself; proceeding without any teacher
from those matters whose kinds she recognized from a first and inchoate
understanding, she confirms and perfects reason by herself.”

Why say more? Those who err in this way abolish, so far as they can, the moral
virtues which everyone experiences; and so they abolish every foundation for
the exercise of justice among humans. For when this government is denied,
every right and obligation is a mere fiction. From where could any right or
obligation come except from a law constituting it? If it is said that this
right comes from human constitutions, one may ask from where comes the right to
impose such constitutions upon others, and why others are bound to submit to
them. Furthermore, before those laws were carried, no one could do any injury
to another, for on this opinion no right had yet been constituted. Thus every
foundation of commonwealths is removed. For to what end are societies and
commonwealths and any political government established except that each
person's right may be preserved safe and sound, and that no one may injure
others with impunity? On this opinion the force of all covenants entered into
between equal parties is also removed, and whoever excels in strength, craft,
and power could lawfully do whatever he wished. Parricide, matricide, homicide,
and the like would not be unlawful for him. All these and similar consequences
conflict not only with Scripture—which those atheists utterly reject and whose
authority they repudiate—but also with right reason and with the experience of
all people of every nation. For how few, I ask, do not believe that others can
injure them? Even the authors of this opinion would readily acknowledge it if
someone attacked them by force, beat them with clubs, or wounded them with a
sword. Who does not perceive that, by apprehending an intended end, he is
strongly moved toward using the means by which he can attain it, and so is
moved rationally? And because reason grows weak in many ways, who does not
perceive that he needs the spurs of laws—

[1674 logical printed p. 12 | IA leaf 30 | PDF p. 30]

—by which he may be urged to perform the proposed duty?

With far more divine reasoning, Cicero argued in book 3 of *The Republic*, as
quoted by Lactantius in book 6 of the *Institutes*: “True law is indeed right
reason, congruent with nature, diffused among all, constant and everlasting,
which calls to duty by commanding and deters from wrongdoing by forbidding.
Yet it neither commands nor forbids the upright in vain, while it does not move
the wicked by commanding or forbidding. It is not permissible to counteract
this law, nor may anything be taken from it, nor can it be wholly abolished. We
cannot be released from this law by the Senate or the people; nor is any other
expositor or interpreter of it to be sought. There will not be one law at Rome,
another at Athens, one now and another hereafter, but one everlasting and
immutable law will contain all nations at all times. And there will be one
common master and ruler of all, as it were—God, the inventor, adjudicator, and
giver of this law. Whoever does not obey him will flee from himself and,
despising human nature, will by this very fact suffer the greatest penalties,
even if he escapes the other things regarded as punishments.”

Finally, those who abolish this Dominion of God must at once abolish every
right and rectitude. “Thus it is right and just,” says the most learned
Martinius at the place cited, page 1490 in my edition, “that God is in every
way most worthy to be acknowledged and worshiped by us, so much so that this
cannot be omitted by us without the greatest crime, and those who do not care
for it are worthy of destruction. For God is the truest and highest good. God
both knows this right of his and has enabled us to know it by creating us as
humans endowed with sound reason. Among other things, he inscribed in our mind
that judgment which can be read clearly there, provided that by thinking and
judging we open the eye of the mind. Indeed, we who do not wish to suppress
God's truth in injustice read it clearly there: it belongs to justice to incline
and move us toward doing what we have understood to be right and to turn away
from its contrary. Lest we think this optional for us, there is a law that
commands it. God impressed these notions upon the nature which he created
upright. For example, in Adam's mind, as upon a certain tablet by God's finger,
these rules were inscribed at the very moment he existed: I, who now begin to
exist when before this time I did not exist, was created by another. He is an
eternal, most wise, best, and omnipotent mind—in one word, God. He is in every
way worthy that I acknowledge, love, and fear him: in truthfulness as the
truest, in confidence as the best, in humility as the highest and greatest. As
omnipotent he can reduce me again to nothing; as a lover of piety he can
preserve me in blessedness and even render me more blessed; and as a hater of
impiety he can make me wretched by a just judgment. I also perceive him
addressing me in my mind as follows: I am the Lord your God, who created you
and brought forth your body from the clod of earth—earth which I had previously
made from nothing—and breathed into you a soul produced by me, through which
you live, perceive, and understand, and in which I inscribed the aforesaid
judgments of mine concerning my benefit and dominion and concerning the duty
of law. You shall acknowledge and love me alone as God above all things; and
in gestures, words, or deeds, you shall present yourself to me in no other way
than will accord with the commanded acknowledgment and love. Likewise, through
your thoughts, gestures, speeches, and actions you shall render to every other
person and thing what belongs to each, and shall love your neighbor as
yourself.” So far Martinius, learnedly and clearly. Now that this impious error
which seeks to overthrow God's supreme dominion has been routed, let us say
something about those laws of God by which he governs human beings.

[1674 logical printed p. 13 | IA leaf 31 | PDF p. 31]

Divine Law enjoys various designations in Scripture by which its nature, use,
or benefit is set before us, who are ignorant and quite unfit to understand it;
by which either the whole law or certain principal parts of it are indicated;
and by which, finally, its force, obligation, promulgation, end, and similar
matters are also indicated with no obscurity.

1. It is frequently called תורה, which, although it extends more broadly than
the name *law*, especially throughout Leviticus, so that it may be rendered in
Latin as *rationale* or *prescription*, nevertheless sometimes specifically
signifies the *doctrine* by which we are taught concerning God's will toward us
and the formation of our life and morals. Sometimes it means any Divine
Doctrine, whether precept or promise. It comes from ירה, “he cast, projected,”
and in the Hiphil “he taught,” as though he projected his will or doctrine. For
(as Mercer says in Pagninus) the metaphor is taken from drops of rain, after
whose manner doctrine and instruction teach and make fruitful people who are
otherwise ignorant, as rain does the earth. Hosea 10:12. Others hold that it is
the same as תורת, “constitution, arrangement, institution, doctrine,” from תור,
“arrangement, form, condition, Rationale, order,” which derives from תור, “he
explored, searched, inquired, investigated.”

2. דרך, ἡ ὁδός, “Way, Custom, Practice, Manner”; also (according to Mercer)
“Study, Work” in which someone is engaged, “Institution, Worship, Religion,
Doctrine, Dogma,” whether good or evil, from דרך, “he trod, went, advanced,
journeyed,” because it shows us the way in which we ought to tend toward God
and in which, by advancing, we may at last come to him.

3. מצוה, ἐντολή, “Precept, command, injunction, mandate,” which binds one to
observing something, from צוה in the Piel: “he instructed, commanded, ordered.”
Sometimes, “he decreed, ordained.” Thus this word denotes the obligation to
render obedience to a law, inasmuch as it was given by one endowed with
authority. Sometimes it denotes a *promise*, Psalm 113:3.

4. אמרה, “Utterance, speech, word,” τὸ λόγιον; likewise אמר, the same; and אמר,
which also signifies “a pronouncement, statement, promise.” All derive from
אמר, “he said, proclaimed, foretold, appointed, narrated, preached, spoke.”
When it is said of God (according to Mercer in Pagninus), it frequently
signifies something stronger, so as to include a command or ordinance. It
differs from דבר, “to speak,” because the latter signifies the act itself of
speaking, while דבר signifies the ordering of the thing or cause, like the
Latin distinction between *loqui* and *dicere*. Thus this word denotes the
authority impressed upon a law while also connoting its pronouncement or solemn
promulgation.

5. דבר, “Word, speech, narration of a matter,” whence it is used for the matter
spoken or narrated; and with ב prefixed, בדבר means “by persuasion, prompting,
counsel, exhortation, command, or mandate” to do something, from דבר, “he spoke,
uttered, said.”

6. פקודים, “Commands, trusts, commissions,” as though *precepts deposited* in
the heart, from פקד, “he visited, commanded, constituted, deposited, appointed.”
This word intimates that we will render an account of this sacred deposit
entrusted to us and that God will at last visit its violators.

7. עדות, “Testimonies.” The law and commandments of the Lord are called by the
name “testimony” (Kimchi says, according to Mercer in Pagninus) because they
were for a testimony to the Israelites concerning the matter which they had
received. Hence “the ark of the testimony,” and indeed the testimony itself is
so called, on account of the tab—

[1674 logical printed p. 14 | IA leaf 32 | PDF p. 32]

—lets which were inside it, which were indeed a testimony or for a testimony to
the Israelites. For the same reason, the place where the ark was is called the
Tabernacle of the testimony, and so forth. Universally, it is taken for God's
word, by which he testifies his will to us, from עוד in the Hiphil: “he bore
witness, testified, called to witness, protested, warned, admonished.” For the
law reminds us of our duty and makes us more certain of God's will by declaring
and confirming it to us.

8. משפטים, τὰ κρίματα, “judgments, rights”; likewise in the singular, “measure,
norm, rule, precept, law, arrangement.” Sometimes, according to Mercer, it
signifies “the lawful and just consideration and adjudication of causes which
is done by right.” In the plural it means “rights, laws,” according to which
judges pronounce. Judgments are often precepts concerning one's neighbor. And
frequently, in a general sense, God's decrees, pronouncements, or decisions are
understood by this name, from שפט: “he judged, exercised judgment, administered
right according to the prescription of the laws, justly adjudicated causes,
duly decided controversies, ruled, administered the commonwealth, prescribed
laws and rights, exercised command.” Thus it denotes God's prescription and
sentence, or his judgment concerning what our actions ought to be.

9. חקים or חקות, “Statutes.” For חק signifies “Precept, right, statute, law,
what is right for someone to receive, the rationale of Scripture, law, or
custom.” Specifically in the Scriptures it denotes *rites and ceremonies*
which refer to and represent something, and thus pertain to religion and the
worship of God. In this way, according to Mercer, it differs from משפטים, by
which “political rites and constitutions which pertain to preserving society”
are understood. Thus it is the same as δικαιώματα: “prescribed statutes,
decrees, constitutions,” from חקק, “he established, wrote, inscribed, decreed.”

10. צדקות, singular צדק, “justice,” both political and public as well as
private. Theologically and spiritually (says Mercer), it may be referred to the
proclamation of the law, which commands that evil be avoided and God's
commandments kept, from צדק, “he was justified, had a just cause.” Hence it is
clear how just and equitable God's laws are and that they prescribe a rule of
justice for us.

That one Psalm 119 presents all these designations of law to us. There are also
other words which some expound as law, such as: (1) תושיה, Isaiah 28:29. Mercer
says it signifies “rite and custom,” according to which it is fitting that a
person conduct himself, and also “Rationale or prescription” and whatever is
solid or stable. This word may denote both the stability of law and wisdom. (2)
לקח, which according to Rabbi David is “knowledge” and “sweetness of speech.”
Others hold that it signifies “law and doctrine,” as though doctrine received
or to be received, by which one is captivated. In this way some render this
word at Isaiah 29:24 and Proverbs 4:2, from לקח, “he took, received.” (3) משמרת,
“Observances, ordinances.” Thus Leviticus 18:30; Numbers 18:3. (4) דת, as at
Deuteronomy 32:2. אש דת, “fiery Law,” or a law of fire. It signifies “Statute,
Decree, Mandate.”

It is also used in Holy Scripture in various ways and distinct senses.
Sometimes it is taken generally for the whole doctrine divinely revealed, as
at Psalm 1:2 and 19:8–9. Sometimes it means all the books of the Old Testament,
as at John 10:34: “It is written in your law,” understanding Psalm 82:6; so
also John 15:25: “It is written in the law, They hated me—

[1674 logical printed p. 15 | IA leaf 33 | PDF p. 33]

—without cause,” indicating Psalm 35:19. So also 1 Corinthians 14:21: “It is
written in the law, Therefore through men of a different tongue and other lips
I will speak to this people,” words found at Isaiah 28:11. Sometimes it is taken for the
books of Moses alone, or for the Law strictly so called, namely when it is
distinguished from the Prophets and Psalms, as at Luke 24:44 and Romans 3:21.
Sometimes it is taken more properly: either for the *forensic* or judicial law,
as at John 19:7; for the *ceremonial* law, as at Luke 2:22–24, compared with
Leviticus 12:6, Exodus 13:2, and Numbers 8:18; so also Hebrews 10:1; or
for the *moral* law or Decalogue, as is clear from Matthew 5:17 and 22:40, Luke
10:26, Romans 3:20, 5:20, 7:7, 12, 14, 16, 22, and 13:15, and so forth;
Galatians 3:13; 1 Timothy 1:8–9; James 2:10–11. Sometimes it is taken for the
Old Testament dispensation when this is opposed to the New Testament
dispensation, Hebrews 7:12, and thus comprehends that whole Economy. So Matthew
12:13. Conversely, it is sometimes taken for the Doctrine of the Gospel,
called the law of faith, Romans 3:27; so Jeremiah 31:33. Sometimes it signifies
the curse and rigor of the law, Romans 6:14; Galatians 5:18. Furthermore, it
denotes natural law, or the rational and naturally equitable perception,
Romans 2:14. It is taken in another way when it is called “holy, good,
spiritual, just,” and “unto life,” as at Romans 7:10, 12; 1 Timothy 1:8; Acts
7:38. But it is taken differently when it is called “a prison enclosing,”
Galatians 3:24; “a yoke of bondage,” Galatians 5:1; “the power of sin,” 1
Corinthians 15:56; “the operation of wrath and death,” Romans 6:15 and 7:5; “the
ministry of death and condemnation, and the letter that kills,” 2 Corinthians
3:6–7, 9; “the handwriting against us,” Colossians 2:14; and “a testament
engendering bondage,” Galatians 6:24. Sometimes it denotes the force of inward
corruption; hence the law of sin, and so forth.

Passing over the metaphorical and metonymical senses of law, we consider it
here as taken properly, namely insofar as it denotes that Norm of things to be
done and avoided which God prescribed to rational Creatures. Theologians,
however, define it in different ways.

Zanchi, *On the Law of God*, chapter 10, thesis 5, defines it thus: “It is the
disclosure of the divine and eternal will, by which it both teaches what God
wills human beings to do or avoid and institutes that it be done and avoided,
to his own glory and to the good of human beings, both private and especially
public.” Apollonius, *On the Law of God*, page 13, says: “It is a certain Holy
institution or prescription of God, commanding honorable things, binding
rational nature to obedience with a promise for obedience and a threat of
punishment if it is violated.”

We, however, according to what was previously said about law in general, judge
that it may be defined in this way: Divine law “is a sign of the will of God,
our supreme Ruler and legislator, constituting the duty owed by rational
creatures, as well as the punishment owed if they violate it and the reward if
they observe it, to the glory of God himself.”

Among the essential good offices of law it is commonly counted that it teaches
what is to be done and what avoided. We express this directive or declarative
act of law sufficiently in this definition when we say that it is a “sign of
the will.” For by law as a sign God reveals and makes known to us what he wills
to be done or not done, or what we must do or avoid.

When we say that law is a “sign constituting the duty owed,” we indicate quite
distinctly that other essential office of law by which it obliges and binds us
to avoid prohibited things and perform commanded things. For it concerns the
duty of human beings and prescribes things to be done and avoided. And that
duty owed is the same as the obligation to do what is commanded and avoid what
is forbidden.

[1674 logical printed p. 16 | IA leaf 34 | PDF p. 34]

There are also other acts of law which, as Apollonius says at the place cited,
pertain more to the law's well-being—that is, to its use and end—than to its
being. Such are moving or impelling to the performance of obedience, and
absolving and condemning according as obedience is rendered or refused. And we
indicate both here. For the law urges and impels more strongly because promises
of reward and threats of punishment are attached to it; and we made mention of
reward and punishment owed because, although something to which no promises or
threats are attached may be regarded as a law, divine laws nevertheless have
them joined to them. As for acts of absolving and condemning, they cannot be
said of law except improperly. For properly they are acts of a judge who
pronounces sentence against the accused according to the law, and thus acquits
the obedient person who was falsely accused and condemns the transgressor.
Likewise, the acts of conferring reward upon the aforesaid obedient people and
inflicting punishment upon the disobedient are not properly acts of law, but
follow upon a law observed or violated. The law nevertheless lays the
foundation: for the judge must pass sentence according to law, and because of
the law the obedient person has a right to reward and the disobedient deserves
to be punished. Thus we have expressed this abundantly when we said that law
constitutes what is owed of reward and punishment.

I call law a sign without naming any particular sign because it has seemed
good to God not always to reveal his will to his creatures in one and the same
way. We do not know in what manner he prescribed law to Angels; nevertheless,
it is certain that he made his will known to them by some sign. As for human
beings, it is evident that he declared his will in one way to our first parents
and in another to their descendants; in one way to the Church and in another
to those outside the church. And within the Church he declared it in one way
to Adam and other fathers, to whom he made it known by himself, through a
living voice or other signs, and in another way to others, to whom he
prescribed their duty through others, whether Angels or humans. So too he
revealed his will in one way before Moses, another through Moses, and another
after those times. But whatever sign God employs to signify his will concerning
the duty owed by creatures makes no difference to the nature of law. Hence it
is evident that our definition can be fitted to all those modes of revelation
and can be applied both to that law whose knowledge was innate and inscribed
upon the heart of our first parents and to the law which ever became known by
revelation, whether through a living voice, dreams, writing, or any other
means. Even now it becomes or can become known, whether through the works of
creation and natural light considered objectively, through the works of
providence and the book of nature, or finally through the Scriptures.

When I call God's law a *sign*, I understand a sign by nature fitted and given
by God for this end: to make known his will concerning the duty owed, even if
it does not actually make known to us what God prohibits or commands. For it
can happen through our supine negligence and culpable inattention that we
actually understand little or nothing concerning God's will through those
sufficient signs. And our natural ignorance is now so great, and the eyes of
our mind are covered by so many and such great shadows—to say nothing of our
congenital wickedness and perverse hostility—that we grasp quite little of what
the law of nature itself prescribes and can understand nothing spiritually and
savingly of the things Scripture reveals to us without the help of the divine
Spirit. Nevertheless, the signs of the divine will prescribing duty to human
beings remain: both the law of nature for those, namely, whose ears have not
yet heard the report of the gospel, and the law of Scripture, together with
the law of nature, for those who are in the Church.

[1674 logical printed p. 17 | IA leaf 35 | PDF p. 35]

When I call God's law “a *sign of the divine will*,” I indicate plainly enough
that the form of Reason which exists in the divine conception as in an
archetype is not properly a law, although many call it the *eternal law*. For a
sign of the divine will is something other than that form: it is that form's
impression and expressed image, or radiance, which represents it through
external signs as by certain lineaments and sets it before creatures. And
indeed, if that law called eternal is that holiness, justice, and perfection
of the divine essence conceived in God after the manner of an attribute, as
some hold but Apollonius denies in *On the Law of God*, page 27; or, as that
same Apollonius says, if it is the idea of good resulting from an act of the
divine will by which he determined to prescribe these rules of holiness and
justice and to bind rational creatures to observe them; or if it is nothing
other than the idea of the will of sign, insofar as that will is God's eternal
and immanent vital act and is abstracted from every external sign by which it
is revealed in time; or if it is that will by which God from eternity decreed
and determined the moral rectitude of human acts that would be pleasing and
acceptable to God, constituting what would and would not be the human duty—it
is plainly evident that it can be called *law* only improperly. For it existed
from eternity, but from eternity there were no subjects or creatures capable
of any obligation. Yet we cannot conceive of law without at the same time
necessarily conceiving this obligation actually arising from it. And no one
will say that the internal conceptions of a legislator, not yet made known by
some external sign, have the force of law or can properly be called laws. Law
is an instrument of government used by a Ruler to govern subjects; therefore,
where there is no government, law can have no place. But there was no
government from eternity. Finally, that so-called eternal law is no
signification of the divine will, nor is it God's will insofar as it assumes
the character or relation of governor; for just as he was not Creator from
eternity, neither was he governor. Government is by nature posterior to
creation and presupposes creatures about whom it must be exercised.

Furthermore, the given definition can be applied to all God's laws properly
so called, whether they were established to induce a perpetual obligation or
to constitute a duty owed only for some fixed time. For that law against eating
the forbidden fruit was no less a sign of God's will constituting duty,
punishment, and reward than any other law binding Adam's descendants to render
obedience down to this day.

It is evident from the definition that I do not number among laws properly so
called that mode or rationale by which the Lord governs all creatures, whether
inanimate or animate but irrational, and which is the same as providence. For
I speak only of law which is given to rational creatures and to those to whom
punishments and rewards can properly be said to be proposed. This cannot be
said of inanimate things and brute animals; nor can a duty owed properly be
prescribed to them, for in them there is no will or choice by which, from a
consideration of reward or punishment, they may voluntarily choose this rather

Nevertheless, the definition can be applied no less to that law which God set
before angels than to the law given to human beings. For those intelligences,
or intellectual creatures, are rational creatures and capable of punishments
and rewards; a duty can also be prescribed to them. And it is certain that the
angels were subject to God's law and that some sign constituted for them a
duty which they could and ought to perform; and because—

—some of them transgressed this law, they have suffered punishments and even
now undergo them, while others, persisting in obedience, have already obtained
the reward.

In the definition I say “*to the glory of God*,” because this legislation is
an action of God himself, and God does all things for his own sake; for he can
set no further end before himself. And just as human legislators make laws for
the good of the whole society, so God sets forth laws that he himself may be
glorified; for the good of the universe consists most properly in his glory.
Nevertheless, I do not exclude the creature's good from an inferior and
subordinate consideration.

I said that divine law “*is a sign of God's will*,” because it depends upon
God's will that this and not that is my duty. If God's will were set aside, no
duty whatever would be constituted; and once it is posited and signified, this
obligation to render obedience cannot fail to follow. Therefore this obligation
flows properly and immediately from the will. It is the will which
authoritatively constitutes this duty. This will, however, presupposes—or
rather includes—God's most perfect wisdom and goodness. For whatever he wills,
he wills most wisely; and he wills nothing, nor can he will anything, which is
not consistent with his infinite wisdom, goodness, justice, and perfection—yes,
which is not most fitting when everything that ought to be considered is
considered.

Finally, I said “*constituting the duty owed*,” and not, as some do, “commanding
just and honorable things and prohibiting their contraries,” because this would
imply that God commands nothing which was not just and right antecedently and
prior to his will, and prohibits nothing which was not shameful, unjust, and
iniquitous antecedently to the act of his will. Thus nothing at all would be
good, just, and equitable precisely because God commands it, or evil, unjust,
and iniquitous because he forbids it. No one will say this, and in the next
chapter we shall show more fully that it cannot be said.
