3. Third, we come to Castellio's second argument, by which he attempts to
prove that sin cannot be removed through faith at the point of death. He
draws it from an absurdity, and one so great that, in his judgment, it
should never enter anyone's mind. But what, after all, is the thing that he
thinks must be regarded as such an inconceivable absurdity? It is this:
“Through the power of faith, something would occur in one instant of time
that the same faith could not accomplish throughout a person's whole life.”

But we pay no attention to this Claudian thunder. There is no absurdity
here for us to fear. For:

From philosophy we know that by its natural inclination a natural motion
becomes swifter at its end.

We have also learned, both from Holy Scripture and from God's ordering and
government of human affairs, that a work makes greater progress the nearer
it approaches its end. A runner in a stadium, the nearer he approaches the
finish, presses on and strives more sharply—“stretching forward,” to speak
with the Apostle. Thus the Israelites wandering in the wilderness covered
greater distances in the final, that is, the fortieth year of their
pilgrimage than in all the previous thirty-nine. Likewise, of the four
hundred years following the promise made to Abraham, throughout the whole
first half the holy seed had grown to no more than seventy or seventy-five
souls, Genesis 46:27 and Acts 7:14. But after the second half of those years
had elapsed, it had increased to six hundred thousand men besides children,
Exodus 12:37.

Moreover, a greater thing is accomplished in a short time when the agent
acts more powerfully; but this most commonly happens at the

[1679 printed p. 285 | IA leaf 293]

end. Just as “the devil has great wrath, knowing that he has little time,”
Revelation 12:12—and hence, as death approaches, he customarily presses his
temptations more fiercely—so, on the other side, our merciful and faithful
high priest Jesus Christ deigns to bring us the greatest assistance in the
final struggle, so that his power may be perfected in our weakness. Thus,
when Samson, already near death, once bent with all his strength, the house
fell upon the Philistines, so that he killed more in dying than he had
previously killed while alive. Here, if ever, the grace of the Holy Spirit
knows no slow endeavors: aided by its power, our runner is carried with
greater speed toward the prize he is now reaching.

This is all the more true at the end because the runner has already been
relieved of more of sin's former burden. Through the continual mortification
of the flesh in his preceding life, the old man has become weaker and, as
the Apostle says, is “near to vanishing away.” Wounds quickly finish an old,
feeble, and sickly man from which robust and vigorous young men readily
recover. Sour fruit is scarcely shaken down by a greater blow, while once
it is fully ripe or withered it falls “into the eater's mouth,” as the
prophet speaks, at the slightest shaking of the tree. Believers enter this
final conflict against an enemy frequently struck and triumphed over in
earlier battles. It is no wonder if at last they gain a complete victory:
his strength has already been diminished, his sinews cut. The material is
now prepared: the stubble has dried and is ready to burn; iniquity has
become like “a breach in a falling wall, bulging in a high wall, whose
collapse comes suddenly, in an instant,” to speak with the prophet. Thus
the final drop hollows the stone; nor is it surprising that after many
earlier blows from the woodcutter, the entire tree at last falls with the
final one.

In the same way, Antichrist is first consumed by the Spirit of the Lord's
mouth and then utterly abolished by the brightness of his coming,
2 Thessalonians 2:8.

Anyone not satisfied by these examples should explain to us the manner by
which habits, previously diminished through frequent contrary acts,
finally vanish altogether. Once instructed by him, we will show him more
clearly how the habit of sin, first weakened through the continual course
of repentance and godliness, at last ceases to exist beyond death: its
tinder is gradually removed beforehand and then completely extinguished.
We have thus disposed of the arguments by which Castellio attempted to
prove that sin cannot be extinguished at death through the power of faith.
