### CHAPTER I

#### On the Knowledge of Christ's Soul

##### THESIS I

In the first three books of Bellarmine's second controversy, no part of our
confession is presented for attack, except in book 2, chapter 19, where it is
charged as heresy that some of our writers are accustomed to call Christ
αὐτόθεον—that is, God existing from himself. Yet even in this matter one
finds malicious wrangling on Bellarmine's part rather than any weighty
controversy. For, first, Bellarmine himself accuses our writers hesitantly: *I
do not know,* he says, *whether this heresy lies in the substance or only in
the words.* Second, he admits and proves that Calvin's view is *that the Son is
from himself with respect to essence, not with respect to person.* Third, he
also adds that both the Father and the Son can be called αὐτόθεον—that is,
most truly God—and that Epiphanius calls them so. He censures only the manner
of speaking, although he himself shows that this very manner of speaking was
necessary to refute Valentino Gentile, who continually boasted that the
Father alone was αὐτόθεον. Against this accusation, therefore, it should
suffice to set Calvin's brief defense, Letter 35, section 1: *When Christ's
divinity is under discussion, the attributes proper to God are rightly
ascribed to him also, because one then has regard to the essence of the one
God, leaving aside mention of the distinction between the Father and the Son.
In this respect Christ is truly said to be*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 93 | printed p. 83 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*the one and eternal God existing from himself. Nor is this view opposed by
what has also truly been handed down by ecclesiastical writers: that the Word,
or Son of God, is from the Father even with respect to his essence. Indeed,
this designation of the persons has its place where the distinction of the
Father and the Son is mentioned. The name Jehovah, however, is a title of
divinity which embraces the Father and the Spirit no less than the Son. Thus
Cyril, who is accustomed to call the Father the Son's principle more
frequently, nevertheless regards it as a great absurdity, in dialogue 3 of On
the Trinity, unless the Son is believed to have life and immortality from
himself. He likewise teaches that if it is proper to the ineffable nature to
be from itself, this rightly belongs to the Son. Indeed, in Treasury, book 10,
he reasons that the Father has nothing from himself unless the Son also has it
from himself.*

2. In the fourth book there first occurs a question concerning the knowledge
of Christ's soul—not one of the greatest necessity—which must be dealt with
briefly: namely, whether Christ's soul was so filled with all knowledge from
the moment of its creation that he advanced in knowledge in no way. The Roman
Catholics affirm it; we deny it.

##### Bellarmine's Arguments

3. Bell.: *Isaiah 11: The Spirit of wisdom will rest upon him. Psalm 44: God
has anointed you. Likewise Isaiah 61, Acts 4 and 10.* Prot. 1. We grant that
Christ was anointed with the Spirit of wisdom from the moment of conception.
(For Bellarmine needlessly occupies himself with a sophistical denial of this
time, as though it were our defense.) But just as the divine nature in him
somehow restrained and concealed its majesty in Christ's humiliation so as
not to exercise that dignity which afterward appeared in his exaltation, so
also that Spirit of wisdom was held back, so that it might exercise itself not
at once in perfection but gradually, according to the condition of Christ's
state. 2. The Spirit of wisdom is present where there is an ability to
perceive easily everything necessary to know, as occasion requires, even
though every particular thing is not actually known. 3. Wisdom which is
perfect intensively and in first act can increase in second act through its
extension to new objects.

4. Bell.: *Christ received grace without measure, John 1, Ephesians 4, the
last chapter of John, and Colossians 2.* Prot. 1. The answer is given by

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 94 | printed p. 84 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Bartholomew Medina on Thomas, part 3, question 7, article 12: *In Christ grace
is infinite, but the created knowledge in Christ's soul is not infinite. The
reason for this difference is that the infinite dignity of the person causes
infinite grace, but the dignity of the person contributes little or nothing
to the nature of knowledge. For knowledge is measured together with the light
that illuminates the truths of knowledge; and because the light of knowledge
in Christ's soul is created and has a measure, it also follows from this that
his knowledge is not infinite.* 2. Those very people who attribute to Christ
some knowledge that was full and perfect from the beginning nevertheless
admit concerning acquired knowledge that Christ advanced in it not only with
respect to its effect, and not only with respect to experience, but even with
respect to the essence or habit of that knowledge. In the *Summa*, part 3,
question 12, article 2, in his reply to objection 2, Thomas even answers that
*Christ's knowledge was always perfect according to the time*—that is, in
proportion to his age—*but not absolutely and according to nature.*

5. Bell.: *Our position is proved from the teaching of the Fathers.* Prot. 1.
Medina admits, on Thomas, part 3, question 10, article 2, that Ambrose, Denis
the Carthusian, Epiphanius, Euthymius, and Bede taught otherwise. 2. Most of
those whom Bellarmine cites either speak of the person of Christ, whose
operations and perfections his soul shares in its own manner, or attest things
which we do not deny.

6. Bell.: *It is not credible that the Word should have personally become man
and should not at the same time, in the incarnation itself, have poured into
that human nature all the gifts of which it was capable.* Prot. 1. Those gifts
under discussion did not follow naturally and necessarily from the union, but
from free communication. It is therefore credible that they could have been
communicated gradually by a definite counsel. It is just as credible that
certain gifts, or degrees of gifts, were not poured into Christ's soul in the
incarnation itself as that the glory which his body received at the
resurrection was not poured into it at once.

7. Bell.: *Wisdom and grace were natural to Christ as man.* Prot. 1. This was
not because it flowed from the natural principles of human nature, or was
communicated naturally and necessarily by the divine nature, but because, by
reason of the person in whom

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 95 | printed p. 85 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

that nature subsisted and of the office he had undertaken, it was like a
nature to him. 2. Natural gifts are not accustomed to be present in people
together and all at once in the highest degree.

8. Bell.: *Christ was the Son of God from the incarnation and heir of all his
Father's goods, nor did he have to await an inheritance arising from the
Father's death; therefore he had to be filled with every gift at that time.*
Prot. 1. Then from the beginning of the incarnation he had to be glorified in
body as well as soul, for this glory belongs to his Father's goods. 2. We
admit that Christ had a right to all these things from the beginning, but by a
voluntary dispensation he lacked the possession of some of them for a time.

9. Bell.: *In the incarnation itself he was made head of men and angels; but
the head ought not to be less adorned or less wise than the members.* Prot. 1.
By this same reasoning one may conclude that Christ's body was glorified from
the beginning, because the good angels, however many there are, are adorned
with glory. 2. He was the head in both natures conjointly, as the God-man; and
in this way he surpassed all the angels in wisdom.

10. *Adam was created most wise; much more so was the second Adam.* Prot. 1.
Why then was Christ not also born at the perfect stature of a man, since Adam
was created so? 2. The second Adam, in order to raise us up from that ruin by
which the first Adam crushed us, willed to descend into the same state in
which Adam left us, except for sin.

##### Our Arguments

11. Prot. Hebrews 2:17: *He had to be made like his brothers in all things.*
And 4:15: *Tempted in all things in like manner, without sin.* Bell.: *When sin
is excepted, not only sins are understood to be excepted, but also all those
defects which were not useful for redemption. And when he is said to be made
like them in all things, by “all things” are understood all things which
pertain to the perfection of nature or contribute to redemption.* Prot. 1. In
a general respect even those defects assumed by Christ whose particular
usefulness we do not perceive contributed to redemption as parts of his
humiliation.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 96 | printed p. 86 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

2. Progress in knowledge contributed to redemption just as much as progress
in stature. 3. The application of redemption is included under redemption,
and it is particularly conducive to that application to know that Christ
experienced infirmities of this kind which are ours.

12. Prot. Luke 2:40: *He grew and was strengthened in spirit, and he was
filled with wisdom, and God's grace was upon him*; and verse 52: *He advanced
in wisdom and stature and in favor with God and men.* Bell. 1. *The Greek word
πληρόμενον in verse 40 sometimes means overflowing.* Prot. 1. This is
not the genuine or proper meaning of this word. 2. It does not fit this passage
because of the interpretation that follows in verse 52. 3. This overflowing
is contained in the fact that he is said to be filled or full, but something
else is also signified. Bell. 2. *The meaning, then, is that his bodily organs
were increasingly filled with wisdom day by day and adapted to more excellent
works.* Prot. This is something, but it is not the whole. For the Evangelist
affirms that he was strengthened in spirit, not in body alone, insofar as he
was filled with wisdom: ἐκραταιοῦτο πνεύματι, πληρόμενον σοφίας.
Bell. 3. *He was filled with acquired wisdom through his own practical
experience of those things which he did not theoretically fail to know.*
Prot. 1. Neither in the Scriptures nor anywhere else is this expression found
in such a sense that someone is said to be filled with wisdom because he
experiences things which he formerly knew perfectly. 2. On this reasoning he
also grew in wisdom after the resurrection no less than in childhood, for
only then did he first experience what it is to ascend into heaven, to sit at
the Father's right hand, and other such things. Bell. 4. *Christ advanced in
wisdom and grace in the opinion of the people to whom day by day he revealed
his wisdom more fully.* Prot. 1. This is true of the person of Christ, but his
human nature also advanced in a human manner. 2. He is said to have advanced
in wisdom in the same sense in which he advanced in stature, for Luke joins
them; but he grew in stature in reality, not only in opinion. 3. In the
*Summa*, part 3, question 12, article 2, Thomas himself affirms and proves from
this passage that the very habit of acquired knowledge received increases
together with the body's age and stature. Many of the ancients also agree, as
Medina shows in his comments on that passage.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 97 | printed p. 87 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

13. Prot. Mark 13:32: *Concerning that day and hour, not even the Son himself
knows.* Bell.: *The Son is said not to know because he did not know it in order
to tell others, and because he made them not to know it.* Prot. 1. By this
reasoning it will also be permissible to say that God himself does not know
the day of judgment. Nor could Christ have said that the Father alone knew it,
for in this invented sense the Father is more ignorant of that day than
various people who strive in every way to disclose it to others. 2. By this
exposition Christ is established as the author and approver of that most
shameful and deceptive equivocation which the Jesuits habitually use. For in
this way a person who knows something to be most certain can, when asked, deny
simply and absolutely in his words that he knows it, provided that in his mind
he understands himself to deny only that he knows it so as to tell it. Thus
almost every distinction between true testimony and a false lie is removed.
3. *Many ancient Fathers*—as Maldonatus admits concerning these words—
*Athanasius, Gregory Nazianzen, Theodoret, Cyril, and the author of the
unfinished work on Matthew, taught that Christ, insofar as he was man, did not
know the day of judgment.*

What Bellarmine mixes into this question concerning Christ's prayer needing
correction and having been corrected is a futile and malicious calumny leveled
against Calvin, not a controversy to be seriously treated.
