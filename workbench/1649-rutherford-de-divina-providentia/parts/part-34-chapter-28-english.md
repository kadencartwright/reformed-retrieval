CHAPTER XXVIII

The special providence of GOD which determines and individuates sinful acts is
shown forth. 2. The general concurrence of the Arminians and Jesuits is shown,
from their own doctrine, to implicate GOD in the bond of crimes. 3. The
distinction by which the malice of actions is shown to arise from the created
will, not from GOD, is defended.

The twentieth argument is this. If God particularly decrees and determines
this entitative act of sin with respect to the individuation of the act, he
cannot thus permit sin idly; for such individuation and indifferent permission
involve a manifest contradiction to me. But it is certain that the act of sin
is determinately individuated by God. 1. For if no sparrow falls to the earth,
no hair falls from the head, and this lot rather than that one does not come
forth from the lap without GOD’s determining will, neither does a human being
fall into this pit of sin rather than that one without it. 2. GOD created all
things by number, weight, and measure. Therefore from eternity he determined
so many free acts—neither more nor fewer. The adversaries’ refuge here is
wretched: that God determined the number of free acts, not absolutely, but on
the hypothesis of the created will’s free determination. For: (1) in that case
the created will antecedently and primarily determines and individuates the
number of free acts, while God merely approves afterward, in the manner of a
scribe, what the creature has already established; (2) in that case God could
not bring about twenty-eight or twenty-four free acts, because the will had
already determined twenty-five; (3) Scripture ascribes to GOD this numerically
same removal of goods, not another one, in Job 1. It must not therefore be
imputed to the free determination of the Chaldeans or Sabeans. The saints
ascribe to GOD the numerically same affliction which they suffer here and now
from free agents. Eli piously submitted to GOD’s will in the Philistines’
killing of his sons. David refers Shimei’s most accursed tongue to GOD as its
author. 4. If the individuation of free acts were not determined by GOD—

[Marginal argument XX.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 468; IA PDF page 525; BSB canvas 520]

—then neither abundance of children, crops, herds, and money, nor poverty,
would be from GOD, contrary to Deuteronomy 28. For these things come to human
beings through the ministry of free acts. 5. Nor would it be from God’s
determination in the individual case that Ahithophel’s counsel was rejected;
that Solomon, not Adonijah, was heir to David’s kingdom; that Joseph was sold
and not killed by the Patriarchs who boiled with envy against him; that Jacob,
not Esau, obtained the blessing; or that the soldiers broke the robbers’ legs
but not CHRIST’s. 6. Nor would GOD retain dominion over free acts if their
individuation were not from God. For if it is not from God’s determination that
this free act rather than another is performed here and now, GOD does not rule
over free acts. 7. If the will itself, and not God, individuated its acts, it
would follow that God not only depends upon the created will but depends upon
it so completely that God could not produce these rather than those acts unless
the will had first voted in favor of GOD’s willing; nor could God draw this lot
rather than that one from the lap unless the lot itself—or rather Fortune—had
first nodded assent to God; nor would it be from God that these rather than
those enemies inflicted this rather than that calamity upon GOD’s holy servants.

[Marginal summary: It is demonstrated that the entitative acts of sin are from
GOD’s care and special providence, even in the smallest and most particular
things. Deuteronomy 28.]

2. Raynaud replies: *As regards simultaneity in the order of reason, it is true
that the second cause brings about that formal determination prior in reason to
GOD, because the reason why this action is performed here and now is sought not
from GOD’s side but from the side of the free cause.* Elsewhere he says there
is no dependence, but a gentle disposition and accommodation of the first cause
to the requirement of the second cause. Fasolus states it this way: *I say that
the will and operation of the first cause, as prepared to concur toward several
opposites and as conditionally and physically decreeing to concur and actually
concurring toward precisely that with which it in fact concurs, is prior to the
human will and its operation. But insofar as it is absolute and morally
determinate—that is, insofar as it decrees to produce this and produces this,
so that it does not decree to produce or actually produce something else—it is
consequent and posterior, not by posteriority of real causality and intrinsic
dependence, but only by extrinsic dependence*—elsewhere he calls it simple
presupposition—*and therefore the will should not be called a handmaid, because
that word signifies dependence and need.* Alfonso Curiel likewise speaks as
follows: *The reason why GOD determines himself to concur in execution toward a
sinful act is his internal volition by which GOD resolves to concur with the
created will—

[Marginal references: Raynaud, *Natural Theology*, distinction 8, article 5,
sole question, no. 369; distinction 8, question 5, article 5, no. 379.]

[Marginal reference: Fasolus, on Thomas I–II, question 14, article 13, doubt 17,
point 7, no. 417.]

[Marginal reference: Alfonso Curiel, I–II, question 79, article 3, section 4.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 469; IA PDF page 526; BSB canvas 521]

—according to its requirement; nevertheless, the required condition for him to
determine himself in this manner is the concurrence or vision of the created
will.* Duvallius says likewise: *The second cause determines the first, not as
an efficient cause, but only as a motivating object.*

[Marginal reference: Duvallius, on Thomas I–II, *On Grace*, question 5, “On
Concurrence,” article 2.]

I answer: The sophists thus attempt to evade, through the fog of petty
distinctions, the dependence of God upon the created will which they plainly
enough establish. But everyone sees that Raynaud contradicts himself, for he
attributes the numerical unity of the material act of sin to God. Yet if the
reason why this free act is performed here and now is sought not from GOD’s
side but from the side of the free cause, then the individuation of the free
act is from the created will and not from God. But then the dominion of divine
Providence over acts collapses. The Christian consolation of the Church
oppressed by free agents is empty; trust in God, who neither procures nor
prevents these particular free acts, is nothing and a bare word besides. 2. It
is no gentle but a bitter disposition of providence if, whatever our JEHOVAH
wills or decrees to the contrary, it lies with the created will that these and
not other evil free acts, and these and not other good ones, are performed here
and now. 3. I do not dispute that God’s will and concurrence cannot exist
without the created free will’s operation. But it directly contradicts GOD’s
special providence to say that this free act rather than that one is performed
primarily from the created will, while it comes from God only secondarily,
subordinately, and dependently at the nod of the created will. This is sheer
dependence of the Creator upon the creature. For if not willing rather than
willing, and not willing A rather than not willing B, is prior because of the
created will’s determination rather than God’s, then the created and morally
determined will exercises physical influence prior to God. Fasolus’s
distinction is therefore empty. 4. Nor does Duvallius’s distinction avail,
namely that the second cause as a motivating object determines GOD. For then
the created will is the sole cause moving God to supply all the concurrences
which he actually supplies to second causes. The number of free acts, and the
fixed abundance or scarcity of every punishment God inflicts through the free
agency of all the elect and reprobate, the converted and unconverted, those
persevering in faith and those falling away from it, turns upon this hinge—the
unstable determination of the created will. Thus the created will will inscribe—

[Marginal summary: Through distinctions the Jesuits obscure the concurrence of
special providence which determines this free act rather than that one.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 470; IA PDF page 527; BSB canvas 522]

—the book of life and its names primarily, while God does so only secondarily
and by way of approval.

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, *New Discussion of Liberty*, part 2, chapter 2,
no. 7.]

3. Raynaud says: *Although GOD is absolutely independent of creatures, yet if
he is considered precisely as the first efficient cause together with second
causes, he can be said in that mode of efficiency to depend upon the second
cause. For then the word “depend” means nothing other than that GOD cannot
apply his power to acting any more than he can apply it to exercising influence
with a second cause and produce an action of this kind without the second
cause.* This is said most truly. Sirenius agrees, saying that a
subdependence—or counterdependence—of the divine will upon the human will
intervenes, just as a prince depends upon his cook with respect to certain
services the cook performs for him, although the cook depends upon the prince
in a very different way under many other titles. Bellarmine more prudently
thinks the word “dependence” should be avoided to escape odium.

[Marginal references: Raynaud, ibid.; Sirenius, *On Fate*, book 8, chapter 21;
Bellarmine, book 4, *On Grace*, chapter 15, response to argument 2.]

I answer: 1. The Jesuit distorts the state of the question. We do not deny the
relational dependence between the first and second cause in acting. Francisco
de Arriba, the reconciler of the controversies concerning divine grace between
the Jesuits and Dominicans, observes that in this sense GOD depends upon vital
causes, because it is repugnant that God should produce vital effects except
through vital causes. But the question concerns causal dependence. They teach
that God cannot produce a free act unless the created will has first by nature
determined the first cause’s concurrence. 2. The prince does not depend upon
the cook as to whether such services occur. The prince can prevent what the
cook does, command that such services be rendered, and effect his will through
the cook. But God cannot procure or prevent through the created will these
rather than those free acts, because it lies with the created will—and in no
way with GOD—that these and no others be free acts. But this is Pelagius’s
cause, which Bradwardine refuted long ago: *If the created will acts naturally
before GOD, because, as you Pelagians say, GOD wills it to act because it acts,
otherwise the created will would act unfreely; therefore GOD unfreely causes
his own act.*

[Marginal reference: Francisco de Arriba, *Theological Mirror*, book 1, chapter
5, no. 3, proposition 2.]

[Marginal summary: The Jesuits imagine GOD dependent upon the created will.]

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *The Cause of God*, book 3, chapter 31.]

4. If the individuation of a free act is from the created will and not from God—

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 471; IA PDF page 528; BSB canvas 523]

—then the created will will be the first operator and first determiner, which
Raynaud admits. He says: *I say that the determination to exercise a free act
arises from the second cause, which can to that extent be called the first
principle of the action, since no other cause can be assigned for why this act
is exercised—a fact especially evident in a sinful act.* Thus no other cause
will exist—not even God’s providence or divine grace—for an act of conversion
to God rather than nonconversion; indeed, I say that no other cause besides
free choice alone can be given. 2. He says this is especially evident in a
sinful act. But why this subterfuge? Why is it not especially evident in the
act of believing, repenting, and loving God above all things? For the will is
equally free with the freedom of contradiction in good and supernatural acts
as in sinful acts—and indeed all the freer, the holier and sounder it is.

[Marginal summary: On the Jesuit position the created will will be the first
determiner.]

Raynaud adds: *Before the will determines itself to act, divine concurrence is
not actual but exists only in preparation. I say that GOD is determined in his
own manner, that is, objectively and materially. For although the action is
foreseen, it has not yet been exercised, and so the free action does not act by
the concurrence of GOD actually supplied; nor does it possess absolute being in
the nature of things after the manner of an impression, but it moves GOD as an
object and passes from conditional to absolute as soon as the condition on the
part of the created will is posited. This is not for the creature to be prior
to GOD, but is GOD’s goodness, which follows his covenant and leaves merit to
the creature.*

I answer: 1. Although divine concurrence is not actual but only in preparation
and potency before the will determines itself to act, nevertheless the created
will’s determination is truly foreknown as really and infallibly future—and yet
it is possible for GOD’s concurrence either not to be posited or to be posited.
The will is thus the first agent without GOD. 2. This is a magnificent
distinction: the will, he says, determines GOD to act and concur only materially
and objectively, not by way of impression. But neither does the devil solicit
and drive people into the most atrocious crimes and into Tartarus except
objectively and persuasively, not by way of impression or efficacious physical
traction. Thus the created will in this same manner determines God’s will to
concur with sin; by such objective and material causation it drives him toward
every positive act, just as the devil drives people toward sins. The devil,
however, truly concurs causally toward sins. In the same way that a servant is
made by his master, or a maid by her mistress, to do this rather than what the
master or mistress commands, so—

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, ibid.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 472; IA PDF page 529; BSB canvas 524]

—God depends upon a free cause. For God is compelled by the office of the first
cause to furnish concurrence toward acts of final disobedience, where he
himself longs and most earnestly desires to furnish his concurrence toward acts
of final obedience. 3. The will is foreknown as about to act without needing
GOD’s concurrence actually supplied, and yet in this sign God has not yet
decreed to give his concurrence, which is a principle essentially prerequisite
to the will’s operation. But this is for God to foreknow the operation as
future while that operation is a nonbeing, since the concurrence necessary for
that operation is a nonbeing. 4. How God can foreknow that operation as future
while it is as yet unexercised is ἀμαλαλησίον—an incomprehensible thing. 5. It
matters little how the created will determines God to concur, whether
objectively or through an effective impression. On the adversaries’ opinion,
the individuation of the free act is from the created will, not God. 6. God’s
goodness in concurring is twofold. One kind presupposes a causative and
subjective dependence, in the manner that a servant is good when he obeys his
master; the adversaries ascribe this sort of goodness to God, which is absurd.
The other is the goodness of free good pleasure, by which God concurs with no
subjective dependence upon the created will but because he wills to concur in
this way according to his eternal decree. We acknowledge such goodness in God
as he concurs with second causes. The adversaries, however, do not tolerate it,
which is to make God a servant dependent upon the nod of our will and to make
our will the Lord.

9. Ruiz: *On the supposition that GOD sees that the created will is going to
determine itself to the material action of sin, the divine volition still
remains wholly indifferent with respect to its entire intrinsic and real mode
of willing, and is determined by extrinsic denomination alone. For he wills
only to concur toward every action insofar as it participates in the common
character of action, willing nothing else except what is signified by the
aforementioned precision. This is proved as follows. If GOD willed a particular
sinful action, on the supposition that he saw that it would be future, it would
follow that this action was absolutely loved by GOD and pleasing to GOD’s will,
and thus the inseparably connected shamefulness would also please GOD. Hence
GOD does not place the will in such circumstances because he wills it to elicit
those actions, but only because he wills to permit them, although GOD delights
in his general concurrence, holily given, and in the effect which follows from
it.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, volume *On God’s Knowledge*, disputation 50, section
2.]

[Marginal summary: God wills a free action in common, but GOD does not will this
action individually.]

I answer: 1. The claim that God wills being and action in common but not this
action—

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 473; IA PDF page 530; BSB canvas 525]

—is refuted by Raynaud, who inveighs against Álvarez and `Vestonus` because they
ascribed to Molina the position that none of the metaphysical grades of
concurrence come from God, but only the general grade does. 2. It is a
contradiction for GOD’s will to be indifferent toward this act while
determinately willing the common character of an act. 3. If God wills being in
common and general entity in sinful acts and causes it as the author of nature,
then as the author of nature he also causes this determinate being and this
positive material act which is performed here and now. But this latter
conclusion is absurd to the adversaries. Therefore the former is also absurd.
The major premise is proved as follows. God cannot will an act of hatred of GOD
in particular without also willing its inseparably connected malice, as the
adversaries so often argue against us. I assume this. Yet being in common and
action in common have this being and this material act of hatred of GOD much
more inseparably connected with them than an act has malice connected with it.
Therefore God cannot will action in common without necessarily willing this
material action, namely hatred of GOD. The reason is that a genus, although not
purely equivocal—as being is—coexists more inseparably with its species than a
subject does with its accident. A genus cannot subsist separately outside its
species, although a subject can subsist without this or that common accident.
4. If God, following the will’s determination, wills only to concur toward an
action insofar as it participates in the common character of being and action,
then God does not will to concur toward this positive action here and now.
Therefore either this positive action is in no way brought about by God, which
is absurd, or God makes an entitative act which he wills to make neither
absolutely nor conditionally. This is for God to make a positive being while
plainly unwilling to do so, which is absurd. Suárez himself would not tolerate
this opinion, for he says that *GOD determinately and definitely wills to concur
with the will toward an act in particular and individually.* 5. Ruiz himself
says elsewhere: *GOD permitted Saul’s sins in order to punish the people
through the sins Saul committed in governing them. Therefore,* he says, *the
inefficacious intention to permit Saul’s sins was prior to Saul’s sins being
foreseen as absolutely future.* It is therefore astonishing that GOD,
concurring with Saul’s sinful acts, should will only the common character of an
action, since he efficaciously willed Saul’s sins to be the punishments of the
rebellious people. For not action in common, but action insofar as unjust, was
the people’s just punishment.—

[Marginal reference: Raynaud, *Moral Discipline*, distinction 4, no. 358.]

[Marginal reference: Suárez, *On the Aids of Grace*, book, chapter 15, no. 3.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 474; IA PDF page 531; BSB canvas 526]

6. If concurrence is holily given toward an action insofar as it participates
in the common character of action, then it is also holily given toward the
action insofar as it is this action. Therefore a holy will can will this act
insofar as it is an act, for GOD’s will is just as holy as his concurrence, and
his concurrence is just as holy as his will. Otherwise God could be the author
of sin by concurring but not its author by willing, which is absurd. 8. If
action in common abstracts from malice and God wills that act under that
abstraction, then, because this material action abstracts from malice, God can
will this action even if he does not will the malice connected with it. 9. If
God does not place the will in these circumstances because he wills it to
elicit the material act of sin, but only because he permits it, then God wills
this act insofar as it is being in common and permits it insofar as it is this
being. By equal reasoning he can will this act insofar as it is this act and
permit its malice, as we teach.

10. Ruiz: *A determinate act requires determinate concurrence and a determinate
command toward this act. Therefore it is not sufficient that GOD will the act
insofar as it is being in common.* Thus Ruiz raises the objection against
himself. He replies that *one kind is GOD’s concurrence in first act, which is
concurrence inwardly and preparation to concur; another is concurrence
outwardly, which is an external act received in the will. For outward
concurrence, an indifferent volition and an indifferent command inclining more
toward good than evil are sufficient, because GOD’s outward concurrence
proceeds from the divine will, not as a determining cause but as an indifferent
cause, though one inclining more toward good. Therefore the divine will need
not be determined; it is sufficient that the divine will universally decreed
to concur on the hypothesis that the creature wills.*

[Marginal reference: disputation 50, section 3.]

I answer: The Jesuit does not solve the argument in this way. For the inward
will, as it comes to an outward act, is determined toward outward concurrence;
otherwise God concurs although he did not determinately decree his concurrence
from eternity, which is absurd. 2. Nor can a determinate act flow from an
indifferent cause, for an act flows from such a cause no more than a non-act
does. 3. The doubt still remains: namely, that God causes this act and does not
will this act. Thus there will be some positive being effected by God which
falls under neither GOD’s absolute nor his conditional will. 4. A general
decree—

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 475; IA PDF page 532; BSB canvas 527]

—of GOD to concur toward no determinate act makes GOD ignorant, doubtful, and
confused according to the Jesuits’ principles.

11. Ruiz’s objection in the same place: *Concurrence determined toward a
material action contrary to the divine law is evil, but GOD’s concurrence is
of this kind. Therefore…* Ruiz replies by distinguishing the major premise:
*Concurrence which is determined by itself toward such actions is essentially
evil. But concurrence which essentially moves first toward good and is not
determined toward such actions except through the creature’s concurrence is
not evil. The real identification of GOD’s concurrence—good in itself and
worthy of GOD—with an action contrary in its quiddity to GOD’s Law, on account
of the office of the first being, does not stain but rather commends the purity
of that concurrence.*

I answer: 1. If a human or angelic volition, pure in itself, willed what is evil
in its quiddity, it would become stained accidentally, as the adversaries
argue. Why should the same not be said of GOD’s concurrence, unless immunity
from law—as we teach—preserved God’s concurrence? 2. If the identification of
God’s concurrence with an evil action does not stain that concurrence because
of the office of the first cause, neither will GOD’s volition be stained by
God’s willing the material act of sin. For if it is GOD’s office as first cause
to concur toward all physical acts, it is likewise his office to will those
same acts. It is impious for God not to will what he effects and for all GOD’s
volitions not to be as holy as his concurrences and co-efficiencies with second
causes. 3. The Jesuit’s reasoning is empty, and the distinction is nothing. The
person who, determined by another’s persuasion, supplies assistance to a crime
is just as much a joint cause of the crime as the person who contributes aid to
it from himself alone. 4. Unless the office of the first cause is the same as
immunity from law and God’s good pleasure by which he concurs with evil acts
and remains innocent because he is above the law, the conclusion is shamefully
begged here. For the question is whether it belongs to the office of the first
cause jointly to cause evil acts. Those who, with Durandus, deny GOD’s immediate
concurrence with second causes would deny it precisely so that God would not
become a joint cause of sin. 5. I refer here to what Hurtado de Mendoza says on
the matter. Since the reader perhaps does not have the book at hand, he objects
to himself as follows: *You will say, then, that GOD is the cause of sin,
because he is the cause why this sin rather than that one arises, and this is a
sound causal proposition. I deny the consequence “this malice arises because
GOD wills it.” To the proof I answer that GOD, by the law of the created free
cause, has general concurrence ready—

[Marginal summary: Ruiz badly exempts God from participation in wickedness by
saying that he concurs as determined by another.]

[Marginal reference: Hurtado de Mendoza, *Physical Disputations* 10, section 4,
no. 42.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 476; IA PDF page 533; BSB canvas 528]

—toward some evil action of this species. Once this is posited, what difference
does it make that it is readied toward this rather than that action? For it is
always readied for it indifferently. Malice, however, is taken according to the
relation to our will.* And why, I ask, according to the relation to our will?
Certainly because our will is subject to law, while neither GOD’s will nor his
concurrence is circumscribed by any bounds of laws. Yet it is sufficiently clear
that the adversaries bear this unworthily. 2. If God holds general concurrence
in readiness for some specifically evil action, whether this one or that one,
he is the cause of this evil act in the same manner that he is the cause of
this good act. Whoever is the cause of this evil act is the cause of an evil act.

12. The adversaries say that the law of GOD’s goodness binds him not to
predetermine evil acts. Pennottus and Arriaga say that hatred of GOD is not
prohibited with respect to GOD but is prohibited to the creature. They thus
prove that GOD does not sin, but do not prove that GOD is not the cause of sin.
Furthermore, if absence of prohibition were sufficient to free GOD from
causality of hatred of GOD, then even if malice were the very positive entity
of hatred of GOD, GOD would not be its cause. But that absence is not
sufficient. Therefore, just as GOD, notwithstanding the absence of
prohibition, is properly the cause of the act of hatred of GOD, he will also be
the cause of the privation—namely, malice.

[Marginal reference: Pennottus, *Defense*, book 8, chapter 4, no. 6.]

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, *Philosophical Course*, disputation, section 1,
subject 4, no. 38, number 10, article 2.]

I answer: 1. Bartolomé de Medina says that GOD is the cause of the act and the
human being of the privation, because the privation does not follow from GOD’s
mode of acting but from the creature’s mode of acting, for GOD concurs as a
rule to himself. 2. No one doubts that GOD’s essential goodness is an eternal
law to God; but the question is whether predetermining the material acts of sin
violates that law. We deny it. 3. The distinction between sinning and being the
cause of sin is also empty. Whoever is in any way a cause of moral sin—for it
has no other kind of cause—in some way sins, since the sin is imputed to him to
that extent. 4. The claim that absence of prohibition is sufficient to free
GOD from causality of hatred of GOD has two senses. Absence of prohibition
frees God from moral and culpable causality of the act itself; that is true.
Yet if a human being were prohibited from concurring physically toward an act
and nevertheless concurred, he would truly be a moral cause of sin and a
culpable cause. The other sense is that absence of prohibition suffices to
free GOD from culpable causality of hatred of himself even if he caused malice
itself. Arriaga ascribes this sense to us—

[Marginal reference: Medina, I–II, question 79.]

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 477; IA PDF page 534; BSB canvas 529]

—but undeservedly. For hatred of GOD is formally something prohibited by God.
Thus it does not follow that, if malice were a positive being—which is to say,
if malice were not malice, since it is formally a privative being—God could
cause malice because he is not subject to law. If it remained malice and were
formally prohibited, it would conflict with GOD’s constancy and immutability
for him to cause it, since then malice would be forbidden by God and not
forbidden. But if malice were a positive being and not sin, it would not be
absurd for God to be its cause. These propositions must therefore be noted:
“because GOD is above the law, he can by his own right concur toward a sinful
act and cause the act to exist in the nature of things”—this is true. But
“because GOD is above the law, he can be a joint cause of sin”—this is false,
although taught by the adversaries, for they teach that God’s causing the act
of sin is the causing of the malice connected with the act. Alfonso Curiel
illustrates the matter thus: *A sick person eating meat during Lent does not
sin; a healthy person sins by eating it. Likewise the shipwreck is imputed to
the helmsman, not the passengers.* This comes over to our opinion: obligation
to law is the true cause why an act is morally imputed.

[Marginal reference: Curiel, I–II, question 79, article 1, section 5.]

13. Ruiz: *Nor does it avail to say that an action, insofar as it proceeds from
the will, is from the deficient creature and not from GOD. For not only is the
action from the will and from GOD, but so also is the relation to the created
will, which is the most formal character of the action, through which the
quality that is hatred of GOD receives its being from the created will. But
this mode is caused by GOD as the author who determines and applies the created
will so that it produces such a quality of hatred of GOD through the
concurrence proper to that will. Therefore hatred, insofar as it is from the
created will, is from GOD once predetermination is posited.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 37, section 7.]

I answer: The Salmanticenses saw the matter differently. They hold that the
material act is considered as the formal foundation of malice. Thus an agent
reaching the foundation of malice precisely as foundation cannot reach it
without reaching malice through its own malice. But if the created will willed
precisely that in hatred of GOD which God predefines and predetermines—namely,
the act only in the physical order—it would not sin. If it sins, this is
because it extends beyond that and elicits the hatred as discordant with GOD’s
Law. Thus the act’s relation to the will is twofold: the physical relation is
from God, while the moral relation is from the deviating creature.—

[Chapter XXVIII; printed page 478; IA PDF page 535; BSB canvas 530]

Ruiz: *The formal character of natural merit follows an act only insofar as it
comes from the created will. Nevertheless GOD is the author and cause why a
human being gains something through his natural merits. Therefore, although
malice follows an act insofar as it comes from the created will, this will not
prevent GOD from being the author of malice unless a distinction is shown from
some other source.*

I answer: Merit—if the word as used by the Fathers is taken in a sound
sense—is good in itself, effectually procured by God, and procured by his grace
which determines the will both to the work and to the work’s merit. Malice,
however, follows the act not insofar as the act is from God, but insofar as it
is from us as we deviate from the rules of morals. Thus merit and malice are in
no way parallel. Pesantius agrees with us: *GOD concurs by willing—not with a
will of delight and approval that the sin occur, but with the will by which he
wills himself to concur toward that entity. And that entity, insofar as it
proceeds from GOD as the first cause, is not evil, but is evil insofar as it
freely proceeds from the created will.* Thus Curiel, Soto, Vega, and Duvallius.
