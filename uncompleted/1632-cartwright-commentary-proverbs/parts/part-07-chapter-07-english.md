CHAPTER SEVEN.

Verse 1. My son, observe my words and store my commandments with you.

Verse 2. Live by observing my commandments, and keep my doctrine as the pupil
of your eyes.

Verse 3. Bind them upon your fingers; inscribe them upon the tablet of your
heart.

In these and the next two verses a general exhortation to embrace the following
doctrine is presented with an accompanying promise.

[Analytical diagram]

- The discourse is:
  - simple and open in verse 1 and the first half of verse 2;
  - more adorned with tropes and comparisons.
- In both parts are:
  - exhortation;
  - promise.

Observe throughout what preparation and fortification must precede hearing the
word. As princes prepare attention by trumpets or another solemnity before
publishing edicts, so does the prophet here. See how people in Daniel are
prepared for idol worship

[1632 witness | PDF p. 73 | printed columns 120–121]

by many instruments; much more must they be stirred to worship the true God.
This is more necessary in hearing the word because nature makes us deaf, with
blocked and uncircumcised ears for these mysteries, and because God’s word
infinitely excels human words and his commands princely edicts. Thus he
emphatically commands observing his words and precepts proposed in God’s name:
whatever others say or command against them, hear mine, for obeying God is
better than men. It is praiseworthy to violate a prince’s command for God’s,
while woe is announced not only to makers of unjust laws but their ministers,
Isaiah 10.

Having urged reception of his words in the first half, he commands storing the
precepts in the second. People eagerly seize promises containing God’s
generosity but neglect precepts urging their obedience, and approve only
precepts pleasing them. Yet what God joins no one must separate. All God’s
words are purified sevenfold. He also explains how to preserve them: store
them in the mind. If left on the heart’s surface, negligence or forgetfulness
may make them fall, or the devil remove them, unless meditation sets them
deeper. We must act like those hiding treasures (κειμήλια) in deepest places
against thieves’ fraud and violence.

The promise of life follows in verse 2. Its imperative formula—enjoy life at
once—shows how prompt and certain is the blessedness of those devoted to God.
Human sowing brings no immediate or certain harvest, servants receive no
immediate reward, and much falls between cup and lip. In divine things the
beginning of doing good is itself life; we truly enter obedience’s path no
sooner than we partly share life. See Psalm 119. This does not open a door to
merit: commandments include believing God, so we are saved by faith’s
obedience; and life in Christ is freely promised to obedience.

The other part first warns us to preserve God’s doctrine like the eye’s pupil,
solicitously and diligently. The eye is noble in use and price. The apostle
depicts the Galatians’ love by saying they would have plucked out their eyes
for him. Every part is tender and carefully guarded, especially the pupil,
easily harmed and itself the eye and light directing the body. Eyelids, lashes,
and brows fortify it. God could not more effectively declare love and care for
his people than by saying whoever attacks them attacks his eye’s pupil.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 74 | printed columns 122–123]

Thus not the smallest thing opposing law and doctrine may be admitted, for
nothing is too fine or light to trouble the eye, especially its pupil. Note
this against those finding exact adherence to divine law strange and absurd,
and those who will not openly violate it but readily tolerate others doing so.
The prophet asks God to treat him like his eye’s pupil, Psalm 17:8. If we desire
the same, we must promptly obey God commanding observation of his precepts.

For verse 3 see above. Fingers are mentioned to teach how continually precepts
must stand before our eyes: no naked body part is so visible to us as hands
and fingers, since our face, though bare, is seen more by others. It also
teaches their dearness: Scripture makes a finger-ring an emblem of the dearest
thing, Jeremiah 22.

Verse 4. Say to wisdom, “You are my sister,” and call prudence your kinswoman.

Solomon is never satisfied on this subject. Though he proposed excellent
comparisons indicating our due love for heavenly doctrine, as if he had said
nothing he pursues others seemingly less fitting. Comparing lifeless things
with precious lifeless things is suitable, but comparison with a rational
living thing seems bolder. Such comparisons were needed to rebuke our lethargy
and, attentively considered, nothing is more elegant: he establishes every
bond of blood and marriage with heavenly doctrine. He earlier made it our
counselor; now sister and kinswoman. If, young man, you delight in beautiful
women’s society, behold this far more beautiful wisdom. Unite her by kinship
and enter kinship with Christ himself. Christ seems to borrow from here in
Matthew 12.

Verse 5. That she may keep you from a strange woman, from the foreign woman
flattering with her words.

He relates the end and fruit of guarding commandments, subordinate to the
former end: heavenly wisdom fortifies against an adulteress’s fraud. No human
wisdom, whatever it is, can rescue from that stain, as Solomon clearly shows.

Verse 6. For through the window of my house I looked from my lattice.

Verse 7. I saw among the simple; I observed among the sons a senseless youth.

Verse 8. Passing along the street by her corner, he went toward her house.

Verse 9. At twilight, in the evening, in black and gloomy night, I observed.

Verse 10. Behold, a woman met him in a prostitute’s dress and with a crafty
heart.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 75 | printed columns 124–125]

Verse 11. She is noisy and defiant; her feet do not remain in her house.

Verse 12. Now outside, now in the streets, she lies in wait at every corner.

Verse 13. Seizing him, she kissed him and, hardening her face, spoke.

Verse 14. Peace offerings are with me; today I paid my vows.

Verse 15. Therefore I came to meet you, diligently seeking your face; I have
conveniently found you.

Verse 16. I furnished my varied bed with woven coverings and Egyptian linen.

Verse 17. I perfumed my chamber with myrrh, sandalwood, and cinnamon.

Verse 18. Come, let us abound in loves until morning and delight ourselves in
lovemaking.

Verse 19. For the man is not at home; he has gone on a long journey.

Verse 20. He took a purse in his hand; he will return at the appointed day.

Verse 21. She bent him by her many teachings and drove him by flattering lips.

Verse 22. He suddenly follows her, like an ox going to slaughter and one in
fetters to the correction of fools.

Verse 23. Until a blade pierces his liver, like a bird hastening to the snare,
not knowing it is against its life.

Here is an anatomy (ἀνατομία) of the preceding doctrine on adultery under a
fictional example which nevertheless can and commonly does happen.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - means by which the devil sets snares for the unwary and entangles them in
    adultery through verse 21;
  - effects of those means in the remaining verses.
- In the former are considered:
  - the unwary, incautious youth’s conduct;
  - the prostitute remarkably equipped with fraud.

First, the magistrate must not sleep in both ears, but be solicitous as city
guardian and watchman to discharge fidelity in administering the state.
Solomon says he looked through his window and lattice like a vigilant watchman
turning his eyes everywhere to discover evils raging among the people and
punish them. See Proverbs 20:8. Let the magistrate make his house a watchtower
to explore subjects’ conduct, not only by others’ eyes but his own. Though this
may not have happened exactly as narrated, it often does or ought to.

Concerning the youth, observe generally that he is called foolish and
senseless. Amnon’s sister said he would be called a fool in Israel if his
intended crime became known.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 76 | printed columns 126–127]

It is grievous to be deemed foolish in Israel, where were schools of wisdom
and a people judged prudent among nations. If being called foolish by wise
people is grievous, how much more by God, wisdom’s fountain!

Why is he foolish? First comes his youth, commonly sinning here and inclined
to every fall, especially this kind. Thus Paul urges Timothy to flee youthful
lusts. Labor must especially establish youth by wisdom’s words, which alone
purify their paths. Other ages, sexes, and orders must do so more earnestly in
proportion as they are especially liable to a particular sin.

Note his place: crossing the street toward the prostitute’s house, contrary to
4:15 and 5:8. Those loving danger fall into it. Verse 21 suggests he perhaps
went not intending pollution, but to converse, see her, test his disposition,
or talk and dine with her companions.

The time is night. He perverts God’s natural order, Psalm 104:22–23, and
confirms 4:16, “They do not sleep unless they have done evil.” What night and
blindness to flee human eyes without caring for God’s! He does not always
escape human eyes either. Relate Numbers 5 on diligent investigation of this
crime.

[Analytical diagram]

- Thus far the youth; in the prostitute consider:
  - God’s just providence directing her to meet him;
  - her equipment for deception.

Her meeting him occurs by God’s just judgment, so seekers of evil fall into it
and it comes to meet them, especially one hopelessly setting out to pursue it.
Those God purposes to save, though disposed to evil, he averts from their
designed crime. See Hosea 2:5–7. God restrained David from killing Nabal by
Abigail’s encounter, and struck Abimelech when ready to commit adultery with
Abraham’s wife. God sometimes, to test his people’s weakness or infused
virtue, presents means easily abused to sin, as David’s ministers and
attendants advanced his adultery and murder. Therefore pray not to be led into
temptation.

[Analytical diagram]

- In the prostitute note her:
  - equipment for deception:
    - outward;
    - inward;
  - effects.

The outward equipment is prostitute’s dress, perhaps twisted curled hair,
exposed neck and breasts, and affected gait.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 77 | printed columns 128–129]

Among Canaanites prostitutes’ clothing differed from matrons’, but this is
unlikely among God’s people where brothels were not tolerated. Since clothing
made for lust and luxury is Satan’s instrument, chaste matrons must wear what
befits professed godliness.

The inward equipment is a heart fortified by fraud lest anything escape that
impedes her criminal aim; from it flows fraud betrayed by dress, gesture, and
deeds. See Proverbs 30:18–19 and especially Ecclesiastes 7:26, nets and snares
in the adulteress’s heart. How can a woman, inferior to man in prudence,
acquire craft deceiving cautious men? We wrestle not against flesh and blood
but principalities, and nature is very ingenious for evil. Follow Solomon:
commit the heart to God and delight the eyes in his ways, Proverbs 23:26; see
5:1–2. Ecclesiastes 7 says only one accepted by God escapes; no wisdom remedies
this unless God has mercy.

The effects are deeds and words. First she is noisy and turbulent, so living
in a leaky or smoky house is better than with her in a splendid one. Awareness
of wickedness should tame her, yet she insults. This opposes what the apostles
require in modest godly women, 1 Peter 3:3–5; 1 Timothy 2:11–12.

Next she is defiant toward God, husband, and honest laws from pride and
restlessness. Godly women obey husbands, like Sarah calling hers lord, 1 Peter
3. Her feet do not remain home but run everywhere because she is idle. Compare
Titus 2:5; Judges 5:24; Psalm 68:13. Though these three do not make everyone
committing them a prostitute, they accompany prostitutes for the most part
(ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ), and must be avoided to preserve an honorable reputation.

Another effect is seizing the youth, shamelessness foreign to a woman. She
should resent being seized; seizing a young man is great impudence. Isaiah 4:1
marks universal disorder when seven women seize one man. See Potiphar’s wife,
Genesis 39:12. Ecclesiastes 7:26 says people are bound in her hands: better a
constable seize and imprison us than she lay hands upon us.

Greater impudence is kissing him. Womanly modesty requires even a wife await
her husband’s kiss rather than seize it. Offering a kiss to a youth is notable
shamelessness. If danger lies in touching hands, much more a kiss, when lips
like snares draw him into a pit.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 78 | printed columns 130–131]

Her shamelessness appears in going publicly with prostitute’s dress and
gesture, seizing and kissing him, but most in addressing him. Solomon says she
now hardens or rubs her forehead—not that she lacked impudence, but now
surpasses herself: formerly iron-faced, now bronze or adamant. Potiphar’s wife
first raised eyes, then seized Joseph’s cloak, finally invited him openly.
Among many arts and degrees of prostitution this is highest and clearest proof
of a worn-out prostitute: not waiting for lovers’ enticements but anticipating
them with impure speech.

Her speech persuades unlawful embraces. Its two heads are arguments for the
act’s convenience and answers to impediments. The first argues from a liberal
table: peace offerings returned largely to her, providing a feast. Prostitutes
prepare banquets to attract lovers, drive unwary youth into nets, and inflame
them by food and wine. Bread’s fullness spurred Sodom’s monstrous lusts,
Ezekiel 16. Avoid her table as net and trap; it inflames mind and removes or
bends judgment.

Payment of vows adds proof. She indirectly displays religion to the ignorant
youth: “Though indulging loves, do not think me alien to God. I perform not
only common worship, but zealously vow and faithfully pay.” This is Satan’s
deep method: putting holiest religion forward in the foulest matter. See 1
Samuel 2:22, women professing great godliness polluted with wicked priests.

The second argument is her love, claiming she sought him above others. She
lies: prostitutes with many lovers call each dearest, though only one can be,
or none is dear except for profit and lust. Having abandoned the husband of
her youth whom she should love most, her love for others is easily inferred.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 79 | printed columns 132–133]

The third argument is efficient cause: divine providence directed him to her,
as if God authored the crime. Saul similarly, hearing David could be captured,
said God enclosed him in his hand, 1 Samuel 23. Sinners whose wishes succeed
persuade themselves God approves their wicked vows, Zechariah 11:5.

The fourth is the chamber’s convenience: curtains, carved bedposts, finest
Egyptian linen, perfumes suffusing bed and room. She affects every sense—taste
by food, sight by ornaments, touch by soft linen, smell by incense—to draw him
into nets and lust. These stimuli blind even those otherwise alien to lust.
Their beauty averts minds from unchastity’s deformity, fragrances from its
stench. Remember Christ’s adorned bed in the Song: one departs from it as one
approaches hers. Such ornaments are not wholly condemned if used soberly
according to station without immoderately provoking lust.

She concludes: come, indulge loves all night. Note insatiable lust scarcely
satisfied by an entire night.

The first persuasion is done; the second removes scruples. Note her sophistry,
omitting no trap for the senseless youth. Preachers should be ashamed not to
use equal diligence and prudence in divine things. She anticipates fear of the
husband: he is away. She is silent about omnipresent God, whose thought should
cause greater fear. She calls him “the man,” not “my husband” or “lord” as
chaste women do, avoiding a name that might strike conscience. Abimelech
advised Sarah that her husband be a covering of eyes. She answers fear of an
unexpected return by the long journey, confirmed by his moneybag.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 80 | printed columns 134–135]

The devil’s means are exposed; effects follow in verses 21–23. The chief cause
of ensnaring is her speech. Dress and kiss did something, but speech was a peg
driven through his temples. Its accompaniments are many words (πολυλογία) and
sweetness (γλυκύτης): many arguments sprinkled with hope of highest delight.
Her lips drip honey and her tongue is like a lyre enchanting hearers. Evil
conversations corrupt morals. Imitate the serpent’s prudence in stopping its
ear against enchantments, and Joseph refusing to hear his mistress.

He marks degrees: she first bent, then drove him. Therefore hold Solomon’s
teaching on vigilant heart-guarding; keep it and the whole person is kept.

[Analytical diagram]

- Persuasion has two effects:
  - he followed her;
  - she affected him miserably.

Following suddenly shows inconsideration and haste. Even persuaded, had he
taken time to weigh her words and coming guilt and punishment, he would never
have stepped there. Joseph unfolded obligations toward master and God.

Three comparisons illustrate his going. Ox and bird suppose they go to food
while hastening to death, becoming food for others; the youth expects a merry
feast but drinks poison. Solomon rightly calls him senseless. Her speech
intoxicates him until, transformed (μετεσκευασμένος), from man he becomes brute
and block.

The second comparison makes him fettered toward fools’ punishment, teaching
how inextricable her traps are. See Proverbs 23:27; Ecclesiastes 7:26. Though
fettered from escaping ruin, he does not move slowly toward evil: stupid as an
ox, he hastens like a bird. He never returns to himself until the crime’s arrow
pierces liver—until he destroys soul and body.

Verse 24. Therefore now, sons, listen to me and attend to my mouth’s words.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 81 | printed columns 136–137 | ends above the ruled beginning of Chapter VIII]

Verse 25. Let not your soul incline to her ways; do not wander in her paths.

Verse 26. For she has cast down many pierced through, and all killed by her are
numerous.

Verse 27. Her house is the way to the grave, descending to death’s chambers.

From the example he exhorts sons, with reasons in verses 26–27.

[Analytical diagram]

- In the exhortation:
  - first he secures attention;
  - then proposes the matter.

Verse 24 says: warned by such an example, hear my faithful, saving speech, not
her deadly speech; prefer mine, though drier, to hers though honeyed.

Verse 25 contains two things: let the soul not incline to wickedness, nor walk
her paths.

[Analytical diagram]

- Two reasons:
  - one from those killed by her:
    - number;
    - quality;
  - another from inescapable danger.

All come from the preceding event, verse 21 and following.
