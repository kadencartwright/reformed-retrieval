Parts:
{ The subject of such generosity, the recipient.

The first part of the parable and proposition is illustrated by a likeness; such is Matthew 22 and Proverbs 23. The purpose is that the doctrine presented by the parable—so necessary and illustrious—may sink more deeply into the mind. And just as the preceding discourse in chapter 8 is set against the speech of the flattering and alluring harlot, so this parable concerning a banquet is set against her banquet as its opposite.

{ The insignia of the treasure which God first prepared for mortals, in two verses.

The manner of communicating it, in verse 3.

In the first part, God’s generosity is represented under the figure of a most noble woman, just as in Matthew 22 it is represented under the likeness of a most august king, so that something great and sumptuous may be understood—something presented with such majesty and exaltedness. At the same time, he continues the allegory of the preceding chapter, where he said that he delighted in the sons of men.

{ The structure of the palace, or building, in two verses.

By saying that wisdom, or the Son of God, built a palace with seven—that is, with innumerable—columns, he suggests that it is royal both in the form of the building and in its very vastness. Hence it is evident how foolish and frivolous the invention of the seven sacraments is, insofar as it is founded on these words. Next, the firmness of this palace is indicated by the seven columns, as in Matthew 16: “Upon this rock.”

The material of these buildings is celebrated by Solomon in Song of Songs 1:17. For the beams are said to be cedar, and the galleries or walkways made from beasts. By this magnificence of construction are indicated the excellence of the Church in this age, 1 Timothy 3, and the splendor of heaven, which awaits us in the age to come.

It is said to float, or to be carried away; see 2 Corinthians 5. And that this splendor may be his secure protection, it is noted under the likeness of fences and a sheepfold, Psalm 23. In the first place, it is compared to a fortified city, surrounded by a wall and a protective outwork. The baptism that now introduces one into the church is the pledge and token of this introduction, just as circumcision once was.

From this it is clear that at first we are by nature wanderers, vagrants, and fugitives from our father’s dwellings, from which we have withdrawn ourselves, Luke 15, wandering and running about here and there like wild creatures before we are gathered into the church. Therefore, all who are not gathered into the Church are exposed to the hail, storms, and lightning of divine wrath. As wanderers, they are likewise subject to divine justice, and every day we are in danger lest, seized together with the ungodly and unclean spirits, they should be carried away into eternal destruction.

Since, then, we rightly condemn those wanderers who settle nowhere—especially if they reject the habitation offered to them—we must take care that we do not pronounce the sentence against ourselves if we reject so great a manifestation of the generosity of our God. Moreover, it ought to seem strange that we should occupy ourselves entirely in other people’s houses and foreign dwellings, while meanwhile we disdain and contemptuously pass by homes so splendid, already built without any expense on our part. For who would not condemn as utterly foolish the man who wears himself out building a little hut while contemptuously rejecting the great houses prepared for him by his father?

The second part of the preparation follows: preparation at a sumptuous table, which is outlined in Matthew 24 by a royal feast prepared for the wedding of his son, and in Psalm 23 by the tender grass most pleasing to sheep. Here it is indicated by the slaughtering of fattened animals and by the tempering of wine—either reducing it to moderation or seasoning it with spices. The latter seems to fit better with Proverbs 23 and 25:6.

Defæcated, clarified wine is praised. In the Lord’s Supper we have a pledge and token of this poured-out generosity of God toward us, sealing for us our continuance in the Church by the nourishment supplied. Here, therefore, let us consider how naturally hungry we are and exhausted by famine, since, overwhelmed by the abundance of our sins, we are compelled to drink our own urine and feed on dung and excrement—as is falsely reproached to the people of God in 2 Kings 18—but it may truly be affirmed of us that, like the prodigal son, we eat pods with the pigs. Therefore our ingratitude and madness will be all the greater if we make light of such abundant generosity from God.

This is the subject of the Prophet’s complaint, Isaiah 33: “Come to the waters; come, buy milk and wine, and that without money. Why will you spend your money for food that is useless and harmful?”

But what these delicacies signify, see Isaiah 25:8, and also John 6, where these and similar parables are explained most clearly. They signify grace in this life, verse 7, and glory in the life to come, verse 8. So much for the preparation; what follows concerns communication. Let us now examine what taste and appetite we bring to so exquisite a table.

This communication is so necessary that without it the benefit already prepared is useless. For what good would it do to prepare a feast and build a royal house if they were not made known? Or what good would a treasure buried and hidden in the earth do? The means of communicating these benefits is the ministry and proclamation of the Word. Its ministers are called servants in Matthew 22, but here they are called maidservants—not because the ministry of the Church is granted to women, 1 Timothy 2:12, but so that the propriety of the allegory may be maintained. For since he had previously compared the Son of God to a most exalted woman, it was fitting that he should assign to her maidservants and attendants to wait upon her. Here, therefore, it must be considered of what importance this is.

Versf. 4. & 5. By the simple and open man he understands one who is so lacking in religion and wisdom that meanwhile he does not labor with any malice against the professors of truth: nor does he trust in himself concerning himself, as the derider is clear by contrast. This is not unlike that speech which Christ has, Math. 22., where the blind, deaf, &c. Jews are introduced to the king’s banquet, Johan. 9. 39. 41. Luc. 14. See Isa. 38. 9, & 14. where he prefers infants & fugitives to deriders in being instructed in the doctrine of God. This therefore is an incredible grace of God, that he not only delights and takes pleasure with men; but further, with foolish and senseless men, he himself, the wisest.

Versf. 6. By the fact that he says her, that is, wisdom, addressed them, not those, that is, the maidservants, we are taught that in the speech of ministers their authority is not so much regarded as God speaking in them. He who hears you hears me, &c. & 1. Thess. 1., that you received our speech, not as the speech of men, but, as it truly is, the speech of God.

It is not unlike what is said, Math. 22., where they are accused because they brought their own rags to the banquet; for this foolishness is regarded as a kind of rags. For most are found who approach the Evangelical banquet in such a way that meanwhile they do not cast off the old depraved customs and corrupt morals to which they became accustomed in the time of their misery; but remain just as greedy, disobedient to parents, devoted to extravagant amusements, as they formerly were. Here see how worthwhile it is for us to withdraw ourselves from the company of the impious. Nor is it merely required that fools bring themselves, if they wish to be participants in heavenly wisdom; but that they walk in the ways of understanding. For, having cast off their own rags, they must necessarily put on the wedding garment, if they wish to be acceptable to the Queen.

Vers. 7. He calls deriders those whom Christ the Savior, Joh. 9., in the latter part of v. 39, & 41., says are described as seeing clearly, although they are not; but in their own judgment they seem to themselves wonderfully wise. He warns that these are not to be reproved, since they are dogs & swine, Matth. 7. From this he argues by the effects of their own disgrace, which is illustrated by the likeness of a stain sprinkled upon the face. Thus Christ, lest they rise up against you: likewise, of inward hatred, as if he were saying, you will accomplish nothing for them; and you will greatly harm yourself: therefore reprove me, as can be established from the following opposites.

Vers. 8. & 9. This very thing is argued from opposites, namely, the rebuke of the wise man, who is likewise called just: whence effects will flow, in the one admonished and reproved, progress in wisdom, but in the reprover, love, Psal. 141. 5. Here the error of the common people is refuted, who attribute foolishness to someone if he patiently permits himself to be reproved and censured.

Here the wise man is shown to be the one who fears the Lord; and the understanding man, the one who is imbued with that knowledge which belongs properly to the saints. He confirms this as so completely true that whoever is not endowed with the fear of God, reverence, and the knowledge of the saints possesses not even the smallest part of true wisdom or true understanding. Thus, by comparison, he proves that the fear of God and the knowledge of the saints are true wisdom and understanding, Ps. 111, last verse; Jer. 4:22 and 8:9; Deut. 4:6. Wisdom of this kind is foolishness with God. And if anyone wishes to become wise, let him first become a fool: without this wisdom, the wisdom of the Chaldeans, Egyptians, indeed even of Solomon himself or Adam, while he stood in integrity, would now be folly.

Verse 11. By the accompanying reward of long—and indeed vigorous—life, she invites us to this feast. She does this very appropriately, since the fruit of food and of a banquet is that one, being healthily nourished, gathers strength for living. By this synecdoche she understands all good things, especially those that are future and those which are not perceived by the senses. And when she says “by me,” she indicates that no other way can be entered by which we may multiply our days: “I am the way, the truth, and the life.”

Verse 12. Then, by way of warning, he anticipates what might be objected: that some benefit, and a very great one, might seem to return to God if someone listens to her exhortation, or harm if someone refuses it. “Not at all,” he says. For if you are wise, you have been wise for your own benefit, not for God or Christ. A wise servant does not benefit his master in this way; so neither will our wisdom benefit God, Ps. 16. Hence that saying so often sung in the sacred writings: “that it may go well with you,” Prov. 3:5, 8.

Furthermore, even if you are a mocker, God is not mocked, Gal. 6; but you will bear evil, Job 35:6 and 8. Add here what is found in the Gospel: that the Pharisees rejected God’s counsel against themselves, Luke 7:30.

From this it is clear that people often turn this over in their minds: namely, whether the gain should return either to God or to the minister, since they press piety so earnestly; and, on the other hand, that the ungodly, by their ungodliness, imagine they will thoroughly punish God’s servants, whose reproofs they repel so proudly.

Next, the incredible goodness of God must be noted: he pleads with us, rebukes us, presses us, and urges us, in order to do us good, Matt. 22.

Third, when we have done everything that is commanded us, we are nevertheless to acknowledge ourselves useless servants.

**Effects.**

First, folly—or simplicity—is shown to be whatever is opposed to error, superstition, moral corruption, and, finally, to everything comprehended in the wisdom described above. He indicates its cause: that it knows nothing, but exists ignorant of everything. Ignorance, therefore, is not the mother of religion, but, on the contrary, the parent of every wickedness and impiety. Hence it is evident that folly, and the most foolish folly, is every matter that has wisdom previously described—or that belongs to every kind of wisdom.

Another adjunct is that it is noisy. For although it is ignorant, nothing is more talkative or more self-assured than it; a cloud without rain, fountains without water, Judg. 5:12, 16. They are likewise compared to frogs, Rev. 16, which continually cry out with their croaking that one thing, “No, no.” Hence it is that so often, after some I know not what frivolous condition has been proposed, they devote themselves to destruction and bind their own souls to eternal perdition. Likewise, they declare war, in a way, on all who do not put some morsel into their mouths. Then he compares it to a prostitute of shameless countenance, who does not sit at home like a matron, but comes out and markets herself at the door, as prostitutes are accustomed to do, or displays her beauty at the entrance or windows. By this is described the supreme shamelessness and lust of certain people who boldly mix themselves into and thrust themselves upon everyone; although equipped with no calling, they nevertheless claim to be, like apostles, settlers of the entire field of the Lord.

**Verse 14.** Further, she is described by pomp and display: she sits on a throne, in the most prominent places and on the highest points of the city’s buildings. For just as prostitutes, deprived of natural beauty, adorn themselves with cosmetics and allurements, so corrupt doctrine insinuates itself into people’s minds through pomp, outward display, and other delights of the senses; it pursues the pleasures of the ears and eyes. For when it is consistent with the form of doctrine, it is adorned precisely by neglecting all adornment. See the false teachers of Corinth; consider the practice of idolaters who sacrifice upon mountains, hills, and high places. Jer. 2. Likewise, consider the Babylonian prostitute shining with gold and purple, so that by these outward ornaments she may conceal inward filth.

**Verse 15.** Afterwards she invites those whom she summons—namely, those who have entered the right way. For this is Satan’s chief endeavor and effort: to lead the godly away from the straight path, whether of worship or of uprightness. Since he himself is destined for destruction, he strives to drag others into the same ruin. But concerning those who are immersed in the mire of sins, he is not very anxious and does not labor greatly; rather, he strains every nerve to draw back to himself those who have been transferred from the kingdom of darkness. When the strong man possesses the fortress, he holds everything peacefully; but when the stronger one comes, then at last the alarm is sounded and arms are taken up. Therefore we see how carefully those must proceed who have entered the way of truth, and how much they need a lawful and properly equipped ministry.

calls them; how he now calls the same persons dull and senseless? Answer: he introduces Folly speaking in her own dialect, and pronouncing judgment upon the godly from the foolish sense of her mind—as if you should say: “O you dull and demented people, who despise the delights, conveniences, and honors of life; who crucify your flesh; who incur the hatred of men for nothing. For piety and the wisdom of God are regarded by the world as folly, indeed as madness. Thus the Prophets were called mad.”

Verse 17. The deceptive argument by which she seeks to lead the godly from straight paths into crooked and winding tracks is this: things dishonestly obtained are sweeter and more pleasing to the palate. Thus it almost becomes a proverb: “To taste the meat taken in a frenzy is better than that which is lawfully caught during the day”; and by a diabolical proverb it is said that the variety of shepherds makes calves well-fattened. Similar is the saying in Proverbs 20: “The bread of deceit is sweet.” Thus some, abandoning public sermons, seek hiding places. And here we must note the remarkable depravity of our nature: we would rather obtain advantages through frenzy and wicked practices than honestly and lawfully; and we would rather use our possessions secretly and hidden away in storage places than freely in public and permitted locations. This indeed imitates the perversity of those who, though they are rich and abound in all things—and were even born in a broad and distinguished station—still cannot restrain themselves from secretly stealing spoons, buckles, pins, or other things of this kind.

Verse 18. The outcome follows, contrary to the one described above, concerning the guests at Wisdom’s banquet. For they were receiving a very long life from the feast; but these, from the banquet of Folly, often receive present death, and always death to come. Compare with this passage Proverbs 20:1, verse 7, the latter half of the verse, where it teaches that stones sometimes strike the mouth and injure the teeth.
