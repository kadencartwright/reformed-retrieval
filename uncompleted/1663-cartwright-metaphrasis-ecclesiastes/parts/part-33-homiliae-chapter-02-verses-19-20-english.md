VERSE 19. I also hated all my labor which I labor under the Sun, because I am
going to leave it with the man who will be after me.

VERSE 20. And who knows whether he will be wise or foolish, who will rule over
all my labor which I shall have performed and which I shall have wisely
acquired under the Sun? This too is vanity.

He presents another effect of the causes mentioned above: namely, that he held
in hatred all those works of his which, though accomplished with great trouble
and weariness, could not bring the tranquility he sought. This too would be a
judgment of corrupt flesh if this hatred were aimed directly at the works in
themselves. For since the works were lawful, and indeed—as regards the study
and investigation of wisdom—commanded by God for a certain special reason, so
that men might occupy themselves in inquiring into them, he could not hate
them without fault. Ecclesiastes 1:13. Next, it was decreed by God that each
person should eat his bread in the sweat of his face and sorrow of mind. To
this is added that by those works he provided for the life of himself and his
people, since through them sustenance and clothing were supplied.

Furthermore, to hate those works because they did not bring tranquility of
mind—which they could not supply by any power inherent in them even if
everything turned out successfully according to his wish—is altogether
absurd. Therefore this hatred should also have been directed against ourselves
and our sins. Yet it customarily happens to men, like the Israelites who
regretted leaving Egypt because they were pressed by hunger and thirst—even to
men more distinguished in piety—that they regret the most excellent works and
hate them because they do not attain the desired outcome. Numbers 11:4–5 and
20:5. This happened to Jonah, who was deeply distressed over the ministry he
had discharged among the Ninevites because Nineveh had not been overthrown
according to his wish. Jonah 4:1. Likewise Jeremiah, who, because he was held
in derision, resolved upon perpetual silence within himself. Jeremiah 28:9.

It happens no differently among us. If we are deprived of the expected reward
of our labors, weariness and disgust with the obedience and duty we performed
seize us, although we should instead rejoice in this: that God supplied us
with the will and strength to discharge what belonged to our duty, and that
we have answered for the fault, even though we cannot answer for the loss.
Solomon, however, does not condemn labor or foster idleness by these words;
rather, he wishes a limit to be set in these things and the mind not to be
held fixed upon them.

He next presents another reason for this hatred: the *inability*
(`ἀδυναμίαν`) attached to enjoying them forever. This is amplified by another
adjunct, ignorance—whether he will leave them to a fool or to a wise man. Here
too the corrupted disposition of our nature betrays itself. We are so fixed
upon the advantages of this world that we bear it painfully and unjustly to be
divorced from them, and not even then do we grant their use to others, when by
death we are prevented from using them any longer. And just as we brought
nothing into this world, so it is unfitting to bear it painfully that we take
nothing away from it. From this it is plain that we bear separation from goods
we ourselves acquired more painfully than separation from those we possess by
receiving them from our ancestors. Such is the corruption of our nature that,
although we receive from our ancestors everything pertaining to the
maintenance of this life, it makes us resent

handing those things down to posterity. And even if we acquired them by our
own labor and industry, nevertheless, if we weighed the matter properly, we
would recognize that we came into possession of them more by God's blessing
than by any diligence or power of our own. Having finished this life, we would
then much more equitably bid them farewell and hand them over for others to
use. The chief cause of this malignity is that the *natural man* (`ψυχικός`)—
whose disposition, empty of all piety, is described here—is uncertain of the
future life and happiness. He therefore clings tooth and nail to these
transitory and perishable goods and does not allow himself to be torn away
from them.

Therefore, so that we may aspire promptly and cheerfully to eternal things
while despising these transitory and momentary goods, let us fix all our hope
in heaven and in the sure expectation of heavenly goods. Above all, those who
have acquired great supports for this life by their own industry must keep
watch, lest, carried away by zeal and love for them, the desire for the
heavenly kingdom grow cold or dissolve within them. For things acquired by our
own industry please us as though they were our own children and are loved more
intensely than things acquired by our ancestors and passed down to us by
hereditary right.

This torment, which our depraved nature brings upon us—although Solomon was
not afflicted by it at the time when he wrote these things—is increased
because the wise man does not know whether the one who is to come will be wise
and able to preserve what he acquired, even if no increase is made, or a fool
who will dissipate by prodigality, immoderate largesse, litigation, or neglect
what was acquired by his labor and trouble. Yet the successor would free him
from this care and torment if he applied every diligence and effort to become
prudent, although Solomon could not yet determine, on account of the
successor's age, what sort of man he was going to be. In this respect it is
not altogether reprehensible that Solomon, a truly wise man, was troubled.

Everyone is concerned not to leave goods and a patrimony to a foolish and
unwise man, even if he is an only son, because he cannot preserve the estate
or transmit it onward to posterity. How much more must they take care not to
leave their goods and landed estates to a wicked man, whom Solomon calls a
fool in Proverbs. By means of them such men may inflict all the greater harm
upon the Church and Commonwealth, the more they have been increased and
burdened with riches and revenues.

Since, therefore, unless the Lord builds the house, they build in vain, we
must flee to God in prayer. Let well-born children learn to conduct themselves
prudently and modestly while their parents live, so that their parents may set
out for death more cheerfully in the hope and expectation that their children
will conduct life prudently. But as for what certain learned men say—that by
these words Solomon wished to intimate his suspicion of the foolishness of his
son Rehoboam—they stray far from the truth, as it certainly seems to me. For
if you except that one foolish deed, when he admitted the counsel of the young
men and rejected that of the old men, so that what had also been foretold by
God through the Prophet might be fulfilled, the rest of his administration,
both of the kingdom and of his household, will be found full of prudence and
foresight, especially in the first three years during which he persisted in
the fear of God. See 1 Kings 12:23–26 and 14:7 to the end of the chapter; 2
Chronicles 11:5–8 and verses 22–23; and 2 Chronicles 12:10–11, 15.

To this is added that Rehoboam was forty-one years old when Solomon died. At
that time it was easy for Solomon, a man of such acute discernment, to perceive
his character. Furthermore, Solomon should be understood as speaking not only
of his immediate heir but of one who would follow and succeed him even after
several intervening generations. This thought could be like a mandrake for
cooling that fever from which misers suffer: the fear that a prodigal will
dissipate the things they avariciously and rapaciously heaped up. For in this
way Solomon feared with good reason that things justly acquired by him might
come into the possession of a fool, not to mention those things which have
been unjustly amassed.

*Translator's notes:*

1. The p. 131–132 printed-page join divides `cade- / rent`, and the PA131–PA133
   image join divides `poste- / ris`. In accordance with the normalization
   policy, the Latin joins both words as `caderent` and `posteris`; at the
   image join, both source markers precede the complete joined word and the
   corresponding English phrase “handing those things down to posterity.”
2. The edition prints the grammatically irregular `cum homini` in the
   verse-19 lemma, `Aliud effectum` at the opening of the exposition, and the
   past-tense `dissipabat` within the later future alternative. The Latin
   retains each form; the English gives the contextual sense without silently
   emending the transcription.
3. The edition prints `quod culpam præstitimus, tametsi damnum præstare non
   possimus`. The translation preserves its contrast with the deliberately
   close wording “we have answered for the fault, even though we cannot answer
   for the loss”; the Latin has not been regularized.
4. The Greek `ἀδυναμίαν` means “inability,” and `ψυχικός` designates the
   “natural man.” Both expressions are retained in Greek script and translated
   in the English body.
5. The printed `Ier. 28. 9` does not contain Jeremiah's resolve to remain
   silent; that episode occurs at Jeremiah 20:9. The Latin and English retain
   the printed reference in the body. All other printed references—`Eccles.
   1. 13`, `Num. 11. 4. 5. & 20. 5`, `Ion. 4. 1`, `1. Reg. 12. à vers. 23. ad
   26. & 14. 7. ad finem capitis`, `2. Chron. 11. à vers. 5. ad 8. & vers. 22.
   23`, and `2. Chro. 12. vers. 10. 11. & 15`—are likewise retained rather
   than silently repaired.
6. In `Atque illum hac cura ... qualis futurus sit statuere`, the subject
   relations and the force of the counterfactual are compressed and uncertain.
   The English construes the successor as the subject of `liberaret` and
   `adhibens`, but supplies Solomon as the changing subject of `potuerit` and
   the successor as the referent of `futurus sit`; the Latin preserves the
   printed wording. In the following exhortation to children, the English
   supplies “their parents” as the contextual subject of `alacrius ad mortem
   proficiscantur`.
