### QUESTION XVIII

#### How are, first, Zechariah 1:12 and Jeremiah 25:11–12 to be reconciled; likewise 2 Chronicles 36:20 and the following verses to the end, and Ezra 1? The latter passages affirm that the seventy years of Babylonian captivity ended in the fall of the Chaldean monarchy and the rise of the Persian kingdom, in Cyrus’s first year; but the former says that the seventy years of captivity had not yet ended in the second year of Darius, which followed Cyrus’s reign.

Answer. I did not intend to treat this difficulty at greater length than I had
done in what I brought forward under Question III. But since I see the learned
Jesuits Ribera, Sanctius, Cornelius, and others greatly exaggerating the
difficulty of this question, I append the following points to my earlier ones.

First, Ribera had no reason, in seeking a solution to this question, to implore
not only divine aid but also that of Michael the Archangel and Saints Jeremiah,
Ezra, and Zechariah. For us, God’s grace in the mediation of Christ alone is
sufficient for every necessary solution of any difficulty. Besides, we had
thought that the Prophets of the Old Testament, not yet canonized by the Pope,
were also excluded by Roman Catholics from every mediation of intercession and
therefore from every cult of invocation. Yet right down to the present day,
the most learned Jesuits court popular favor through their works of
supererogation, even amid the grossest superstition and idolatry.

This can be seen in this very passage, in Cornelius’s commentary on Zechariah
1:12. There he not only reproaches the Reformed religion with the Babylonian
captivity—which most truly suits papal servitude—but also calls the very few
Jesuits in England and Scotland, and the rebels stirred up by Jesuits, who
justly suffered punishment, so many hundreds of martyrs. Even today, he says,
they pray importunately and wash away our iniquities with their own blood;
and, as sacrifices for sin, with their blood they cleanse the transgressions
of the English and Scottish nation. These are the beautiful fruits of Jesuit
doctrine. From this, how greatly every hope of their reform grows worse day by
day is plain to the eye even of a one-eyed man. In any event, since they attach
so much importance to the difficulty before us, we add the following to our
earlier points.

> Marginalia, printed p. 189: *Whether the seventy years of captivity are to be
> distinguished from the seventy years of desolation*; *The Jesuits are
> superstitious and rebellious*.

Three answers are commonly given to the question, each of which has eminent
authorities. The first is the answer of those who make Zechariah’s seventy
years different from Jeremiah’s seventy years, so that the two have different
limits both at their beginning and at their end. They begin Zechariah’s
seventy years, which they call the years of desolation, at the devastation of
Jerusalem and end them at the restoration of the temple. Jeremiah’s seventy
years, which they

> Jeremiah’s seventy years*.

call the years of captivity, they begin in Nebuchadnezzar’s first year and
Jehoiakim’s fourth, as we saw above, and end in Cyrus’s first year, when the
Babylonian empire was overthrown and the Jews’ captivity released.
Theodoret, Jerome, Augustine, and other ancients hold this opinion. Ribera
defends it at length, and the Jesuits Sanctius, Petavius, Cornelius, Henricus
Philippi, and others receive it on his authority.

Pererius rises against this opinion, for which he is beaten by his own
brothers, though he does not deserve it. He does indeed reject this opinion
for some reasons that ought by no means to be approved. For he says that many
more years of desolation elapsed between the temple’s destruction and its
restoration in the second year of Darius Hystaspes than the truth allows. He
counts more than forty years from Cyrus’s first year, when the captivity was
released, to Darius’s second. Here, with some of the ancients, he errs grossly
by confusing the first year of Cyrus’s Persian kingdom with the first year of
Cyrus’s monarchy and of his Babylonian empire, when the Jews were liberated.
For Cyrus ruled the Persians for more than twenty years before Babylon was
taken and the Assyrian monarchy overthrown.

Nevertheless, Pererius’s adversaries, his Jesuit brothers, do not altogether
censure this anachronism in him. And indeed, if you pass over this error in
him, he rightly attacks the first answer. He says that Jeremiah and the other
Prophets generally identify the Babylonian captivity with the Babylonian
desolation, and that it is not for men to separate things that God makes the
same. We add the following reasons as well.

> Marginalia, printed p. 190: *Pererius is defended against his Jesuit
> brothers*.

First. This distinction has no foundation, since it rests upon the claim that
the Prophet Zechariah called Darius’s second year the seventieth year. But in
Question III above we demonstrated that this is a manifest corruption of the
Hebrew text by the Latin translator, a point that both Petavius and Salianus
appear to concede.

Second. This Latin reading is contrary not only to the Prophet’s words but also
to his purpose. The chief ground for the Angel’s complaint before the Lord is
that the people’s calamities still continued after the limit God had set for
them—Jeremiah’s seventy years—had expired. There would be no ground for a
complaint here if the seventy years had not yet passed, but the very year in
which the Prophet spoke was the seventieth, as Calvin rightly observed on the
passage, followed by Gualter and the most recent English Annotators.

Third. The Jesuits admit that, provided the Latin words themselves are read as
a question—“Is this the seventieth year?”—the seventieth year is rightly
understood as past, not present. Now the words are indeed read as a question,
as Cornelius himself acknowledges in the Plantin edition. What is it to us
that the Roman edition lacks a question? Let them see to that. Certainly the
Hebrew truth is most plainly interrogative. The adverb “how long,” more
correctly placed at the beginning, as Arias Montanus rightly translates it,
cannot fail to be interrogative and to close the whole verse with a question
mark.

We shall leave this first opinion once we have noted the Jesuit Tornielli’s
error, which Salianus and others of his own party observed. He embraces the
distinction between two seventy-year periods rejected by Pererius and us, but
establishes a wholly singular and deviant method of reckoning. With the
others, he ends the seventy years of desolation in Darius’s second year, but
he alone begins them in Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-fourth year rather than his
nineteenth, when the city and temple were burned.

Grant that Egypt was defeated in Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-fourth year and that
some remnants of the Jews were then carried from Egypt to Babylon.
Nevertheless, no new devastation of Judea, the city, or the temple occurred
then; the final desolation of them all had been accomplished many years
before. Nor does any chronologist’s reckoning agree with Tornielli. From
Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-fourth year to the second year of Darius Hystaspes
there were no more than fifty-five years. As for the fifteen years he adds
from his miscalculated lives of the Chaldean kings, he must credit them to his
own dreams, not to any approved authority.

> Marginalia, printed p. 190: *Tornielli’s singular error*.

The second opinion is Scaliger’s, which departs farther from the truth than
the first. I do not speak of the singular error here of those eminent men,
Luther and Melanchthon. Deceived by the impostures of Annius of Viterbo, which
had not yet been sufficiently exposed in their age, they referred Darius’s
second year to Artaxerxes Longimanus and thus assigned new limits to
Zechariah’s seventy years. But since the nonsense of Annius has long since
been rejected everywhere by everyone, we shall not dignify it with a
refutation here.

> Marginalia, printed p. 190: *Luther and Melanchthon were deceived by Annius*.

As for Scaliger, he ends Zechariah’s seventy years in the second year not of
Darius Hystaspes but of Darius Nothus. Therefore, according to his reckoning
and that of his follower Emmius, he is forced to begin them in the thirtieth
year of Darius Hystaspes. Calvin and Gualter treat the authorities for the
first opinion quite sharply because they extend Zechariah’s seventy years to
the second year of Darius Hystaspes. With what spirit, then, would they have
received these later writers, who postpone those years all the way to the
second year of Darius Nothus? We argue briefly against them as follows.

> Marginalia, printed p. 190: *Scaliger’s error*.

First. This whole second opinion rests on a false foundation: namely, that the
Darius under whom the temple was built was Nothus, not Hystaspes. We dealt
sufficiently with this in

Question VIII. Second. The years of the fasts Zechariah records are wrongly
called seventy, for on this account they exceeded two hundred. In addition to
the two seventy-year periods conceded by Scaliger, there were almost seventy
intervening years. From Cyrus’s first year to the thirtieth of Darius
Hystaspes there are at least sixty-seven years. Joined to the following years
down to the second year of Darius Nothus, they make two hundred and seven; at
the beginning and midpoint of this period the Jews were fasting because the
temple had been burned.

Third. During Zechariah’s seventy years the Jews fasted and mourned because
the temple had been burned, Jerusalem devastated, and the people taken
captive. It is therefore reasonable that the people mourned during the
seventy years immediately following these calamities, not more than a hundred
years after the return from captivity, when the temple had been founded and
Jerusalem built, though it had not yet been surrounded by a wall. Indeed, what
reason can be imagined why the Jews, after neglecting the fast throughout all
seventy years of the captivity and also throughout the sixty-seven years
after the return, should at last have renewed these fasts in Darius’s
thirtieth year?

Fourth. Scaliger’s seventy years beginning in Darius’s thirtieth year must
have had some notable event for the Jewish people on account of which an epoch
of this kind could be established as a marker. But let us turn and turn again
all the sacred and profane writings of the ancients: we shall find nothing
notable in the final years of Darius Hystaspes that could suitably establish
the beginning of any new epoch.

To avoid this argument, Sethus Calvisius contrives to place the beginning of
this Scaligerian seventy-year period not in Darius’s thirtieth year but in
Xerxes’s first, when Xerxes—Ahasuerus, Esther’s husband—prevented the
construction of the temple. Calvisius maintains that the Jews reckoned
Zechariah’s seventy years from that calamity. But this is Calvisius’s own
invention, in which he has neither predecessor nor follower. The dates also do
not agree, for from Xerxes’s first year to Nothus’s second there are only
sixty-five years according to Calvisius’s own calculation. Third, who could
believe that throughout the entire time of Ahasuerus, Esther’s husband, and
the days of his son Artaxerxes, Esther and Mordecai obtained no decree
favorable to the Jews? And this is to pass over Scaliger’s refuted fiction
about Xerxes as Esther’s husband in Question VI.

> Marginalia, printed p. 191: *Calvisius’s own peculiar error*.

Despite these objections, Junius follows this opinion of Scaliger in his
annotations on Zechariah 1:12 and 7:4, but in a few words and almost timidly.
Calvisius and Emmius do so more plainly, though likewise very briefly. Diodati
merely presents it, determining nothing. Pererius presents it in such a way
that he does not reject it, although he disapproves of some of its parts. But
Danaeus and the English Annotators expressly refute it.

> Marginalia, printed p. 191: *The error of Junius, Diodati, and others*.

There remains the third opinion, which is our own. It is followed by Calvin,
Gualter, Danaeus, the first, intermediate, and final English Annotators,
Pererius, Salianus, and most other interpreters of Scripture, both Protestant
and Roman Catholic. It holds that Zechariah’s seventy years are not distinct
from Jeremiah’s seventy years, but that both end in Cyrus’s first year,
seventeen years before the second year of Darius Hystaspes in which the Angel
addressed Zechariah.

This opinion need not be confirmed by arguments other than those with which
we refuted the preceding ones and by inspection of Zechariah’s text itself,
which plainly indicates seventy years already past. This is so whether it is
read according to the Hebrew truth, according to the many translations of
learned men, both ancient and modern, or even according to the corrupt Latin
Vulgate itself, provided it is read as a question. As we saw above, both the
context of the verse itself and the interrogative adverb placed at the
beginning of the verse necessarily require that reading.

> Marginalia, printed p. 191: *The true opinion*.

If you object that the seventy-year period ending in Cyrus’s first year is
different from the one in which the temple was completed, I answer: no
seventy-year period had to end either with the temple’s construction or with
Jerusalem’s restoration, for no promise of such a seventy-year period was
made anywhere. Rather, every promise God made that was to be fulfilled after
seventy years was delivered by Jeremiah, applied to the single seventy-year
period of captivity, and fulfilled in Babylon’s fall to the Persians, the
release from captivity, and the Jews’ return. Nowhere did God promise that the
temple and city would be built at once, immediately after the seventy years of
captivity had ended. Those things were left to be accomplished by divine
providence according to its good pleasure.
