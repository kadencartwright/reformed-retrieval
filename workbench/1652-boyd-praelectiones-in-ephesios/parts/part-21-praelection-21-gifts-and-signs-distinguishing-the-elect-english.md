[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 119 | De Prædestinatione p. 77 (from Lecture XXI opening)]

It has seemed good to linger further in expressing and explaining these gifts
to you somewhat more distinctly. For this matter is, and ought to be, of such
weight and importance to every one of us that this whole life would rightly
be spent in confirming and sealing our election and adoption to us. We labor
in vain and devote ourselves fruitlessly to every other kind of knowledge
unless we learn this saving lesson, with the Holy Spirit himself, who alone
can teach it, teaching it to us inwardly.

[Marginalia: Exposition. Lecture XXI.]

First, then, let us pass over those gifts of the Holy Spirit which can be
shared by the reprobate with the elect. Such are, for example, remarkable
skill and excellence in any art, discipline, or human science, of the sort we
know was also given to many pagans. Another is the gift of understanding God’s
will revealed from the word. It must by no means be denied that many
unbelieving Jews also possessed this gift, since the Lord himself says in the
Gospel, Luke 12, that “the wicked servant who knew his lord’s will and did not
prepare himself to do it must be beaten with many blows.” Another is the gift
of prophecy and of interpreting Holy Scripture. Although it is an excellent
and splendid gift, Christ himself teaches us in Matthew 7 that it can also be
imparted to many of the reprobate: “Many will say to me on that day, ‘Lord,
Lord, did we not prophesy through your name, and cast out demons in your name,
and perform many mighty works through your name?’” Yet he will then profess
that he never knew them and will drive them away from him as workers of
iniquity. Therefore, no one should become insolent or puffed up by this gift
alone, even if they are pastors and teachers of the Church. Otherwise, those
who build that spiritual house even in this holy and glorious ministry of the
gospel, yet do not meanwhile build themselves by living faith and the practice
of the doctrine whose torch they carry before others, will certainly be like
those whose labor Noah the patriarch probably employed in building that vast
ark. I do not find it credible that he constructed it alone with his children.
Yet all those workers, as is clear, perished in the flood because they neither
believed nor obeyed Noah’s warnings and preaching.

[Marginalia: The first gift. Luke 12:47. Matthew 7:22. Analogy of the ark.]

Another is the gift of curbing and restraining certain vicious passions of
corrupt nature so that they do not break out into outward and manifest sins.
Thus we see in Esther 5 that the wicked Haman for a time concealed the insult
he thought Mordecai had done him, and checked and curbed his anger so that it
did not then break out into an outward act and open sin. So too Genesis 20 says
that God restrained Abimelech, king of Gerar, from committing the outrage he
had nonetheless purposed in his mind. He did not know that he had carried off
another man’s wife, but acted in ignorance because he thought Sarah really was
Abraham’s sister, not his wife. Hence God himself testifies about him that he
had done it in simplicity and integrity of heart. Likewise, we see from pagan
histories that in every age many have existed who were free from such outward
vices and conspicuous for a certain outward holiness, or at least its mask,
appearance, and shadow. Thus they were illustrious for the outward appearance
and praise of those civil virtues. By presenting themselves before others as
just, brave, generous, temperate, modest, and moderate, they dazzle their eyes
and both seek and obtain renown for their reputation and name among their
contemporaries and posterity alike.

[Marginalia: Esther 5:10. Genesis 20:6.]

But where does this difference come from when the condition of their nature
and their birth in sin is the same? Where does that distinction between
*psychical* or natural people come from, by which some, overwhelmed and
covered with crimes and wickedness, have passed an infamous and abominable
life, while others have lived free from the outward contagion and disgrace of
the same vices and grown renowned for distinguished virtues in the judgment
and estimation of people who measure everything by their carnal disposition?
Where, I say, does this come from if not from a certain general operation of
the Holy Spirit, restraining and curbing that common depravity and corruption
of nature in them and adorning and distinguishing them with those common
gifts—that is, with certain shadows and images of ethical virtues and with the
aids and ornaments of arts, prudence, wisdom, and human knowledge—for the
common good and for fostering and preserving civil society among them?

[Marginalia: Note the explanation of the cause.]

From this we are surely warned again and again to take earnest heed to
ourselves and beware lest we willingly deceive ourselves in this respect, as
though living a civilly honorable life, abstaining from outward vices,
restraining certain violent passions, and curbing the sinful desires of the
flesh on only one occasion or another were enough to make us true Christians,
true believers, and true members of Christ. We see that even an ungodly and
reprobate person can do these things. We, however, must labor and strive
continually throughout our whole life to feel Christ’s Spirit, that Spirit of
sanctification, mortifying and crucifying in us the bitter root of sin through
the efficacy of Christ’s cross and death, and not merely curbing and
restraining it. For the latter can happen even to the reprobate, but the former
belongs to the elect.

[Marginalia: A corollary to be noted.]

Finally, there is the gift of receiving and hearing God’s word, even with joy.
You know the parable of the sower and of seed falling upon stony and rocky
ground. It denotes those who receive God’s word with joy but have no roots;
therefore, they believe only for a time and fall away in a season of
temptation. This efficacy of the Holy Spirit upon hearts of this stony sort—by
which, even if they are not completely softened and changed, they are at least
breathed upon, affected, and illuminated at the surface—the Apostle calls in
Hebrews 6 “tasting the good word of God and the powers of the age to come.”
Everyone certainly knows how great the difference is between tasting some food
or drink and actually eating or drinking it. A person can taste something
that he afterward vomits up—or rather spits out, expectorates, and ejects—and
never digests or assimilates or turns into his own nourishment and blood. The
latter belongs to the elect, who are thoroughly imbued and renewed by God’s
regenerating Spirit. By a living and deeply rooted faith they do not merely
taste or sample Christ crucified, but eat and swallow him, while their soul
hungers and thirsts intensely for his righteousness and life. Consequently,
they are not merely lightly seized and affected by his peace and the sweetness
of heavenly joy,

[Marginalia: Luke 8:13. Hebrews 6:5.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 120 | De Prædestinatione p. 78]

but are filled and satisfied with them in a measure which even their hearts
cannot take in or contain, according to the royal psalmist’s words in Psalm
63: “My soul shall be satisfied as with marrow and fatness, and my mouth shall
praise you with lips of songs.” Lest anyone wholly endowed with carnal
understanding and sense, knowing nothing but the earth and earthly, bodily
things, should think that the prophet is promising and expecting only temporal
and perishable blessings for himself in that passage, and not speaking
especially about God’s paternal favor and mercy toward him in Jesus Christ,
the true peace of conscience with God, and spiritual joy through the Holy
Spirit as the sum of his hopes and prayers, please see how he explains himself
and his meaning more clearly in Psalm 65. After crying out there in a strongly
prophetic manner, “Blessed is the one whom you have chosen and brought near to
dwell in your courts,” he immediately adds what good and fruit he expects from
this, as one fully conscious and entirely persuaded that he belongs to the
number of the elect: “We shall be satisfied with the goodness of your house
and the holy things of your temple.” This therefore belongs properly and
peculiarly to the elect and regenerate. The other condition—merely tasting and
being lightly affected by heavenly things, namely Christ, his kingdom, and his
grace—can also be shared by the ungodly and reprobate.

[Marginalia: Psalm 63:6. Note carefully. Psalm 65:5.]

Let us return, then. Having passed over those common gifts which can fall to
both groups, let us at last approach those which properly and peculiarly
belong to the elect and the children of God, and which are therefore so many
testimonies and proofs of our predestination to eternal life. We can certainly
refer them all to this one head: the gracious indwelling of the Holy Spirit in
their hearts. By this indwelling they become his temples with respect to his
peculiar presence and gracious operation in them, as the Apostle teaches in
that passage known and familiar to everyone, 1 Corinthians 6. But because this
is too general, and because the Spirit becomes known and is clearly perceived
and observed by us only through his effects, gifts, and operations, let us now
explain and unfold more precisely that abiding, presence, and beatifying
indwelling of the Holy Spirit in the hearts of the elect from its effects and
fruits.

[Marginalia: Resumption of the subject. General proposition. 1 Corinthians
6:19.]

We shall establish as the first of these effects a certain knowledge and firm
persuasion of their reconciliation with God in and through Christ Jesus. Hence
the prophet, speaking of Christ in Isaiah 53, says, “By the knowledge of
himself my righteous servant will justify many.” And the Lord himself, in that
remarkable passage in John 17, established eternal life as “knowing the Father
to be the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom he sent.” He certainly does not
mean that general and bare knowledge without any trust, fullness of assurance
in the heart, and particular application, since even the devil himself can
have such knowledge. He undoubtedly means that special knowledge by which a
person recognizes both that God the Father is the true God and that he is his
own Father in Christ; that Christ the Redeemer is his own Redeemer and Savior;
and that the Holy Spirit is his own Sanctifier, so to speak, and Comforter,
given to him by the Father through and because of Christ. For this is the
peculiar work of the Holy Spirit in the elect. In this sense the Apostle
testifies in 1 Corinthians 2: “We have not received the spirit of the world,
but the Spirit who is from God, so that we may know the things God has freely
given us.” See what kind of saving knowledge that Spirit inscribes upon our
hearts!

[Marginalia: Effect 1. Isaiah 53:11. John 17:3. 1 Corinthians 2:12.]

The second effect is that regeneration by which he so reforms and renews us
that, after stripping off and putting away—that is, abolishing and erasing—the
devil’s image, which our corrupt nature reproduces and represents exactly, he
remolds and recasts us into the holy image of Christ our head. It is as though
he reheats a mass of metal in a furnace and cleanses and purges it of its dross
and refuse; or rather, he causes us to be born anew as new people and new
offspring, namely as God’s children born out of the devil’s accursed progeny,
which we were at our first birth. Hence our sanctification is compared to a
new birth, and so to a new generation, when the Lord instructs Nicodemus, as
we heard expounded yesterday. By “generation” I mean what comprises the whole
formation, growth, animation, fostering, and increase of offspring in the
womb, until it is perfectly produced or brought forth into the regions of
this light. We may extend the name of offspring and generation even to
egg-laying animals, and not only to those that bear live young.

[Marginalia: Effect 2. Note carefully. From Pastor Duncan.]

If someone should still ask and desire to know more distinctly how this
spiritual offspring is formed in us, and how the image of Christ is produced
in our souls by the Holy Spirit after that accursed likeness of the devil,
impressed upon Adam and all his posterity through sin and the first fall, has
been taken away and erased, he seeks and desires to know a splendid science
and a saving theorem. Come, then, let us try, with God’s gift and aid, to draw
and delineate some features of this divine work from the very analogy which
both Christ himself and Christ’s forerunner, John the Baptist, use in proposing
and explaining this mystery of our regeneration and, so to speak, new birth,
whose symbol, as you know, is baptism. Christ compares the Holy Spirit who
regenerates us to water, and the Baptist compares him to fire. Thus the Lord
says in John 3, “Unless someone is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot
enter the kingdom of heaven.” In the next chapter, on the occasion of the
discourse held at the well of Sychar, you see that he compares his Spirit’s
grace to water which quenches thirst and to a fountain of living water leaping
and springing up into eternal life. The Baptist, however, says that Christ
will baptize his disciples with the Holy Spirit and fire in place of that
outward and material water with which he himself baptized people.

[Marginalia: Question. John 3:5 and 4:14. Matthew 3:11.]

I shall now briefly adapt each analogy to the present purpose. We see that
fire has these natural effects, among many others which we pass over for
brevity’s sake. First, it heats and dissolves, softens, melts, and liquefies
things congealed and stiffened by cold. Second, it also cleanses and purifies
impure things, separates and consumes the dross from gold, and cooks, seasons,
and sweetens raw things, and so forth. So it is with the Holy Spirit. A human
being is not only stiffened in sin and congealed by a certain native hardness
and stupidity, but is altogether dead in offenses and destitute of every kind
of spiritual life and warmth. The Spirit heats him, dissolves and softens his
heart, and gradually animates and vivifies it with the love of God and the
desire for Christ and the spiritual life. In addition, he eats away and
consumes the foul rust and dross of sin which has adhered to his whole soul and
each of its parts. He burns away the stain and corruption hidden in the heart,
and causes the soul itself, illustrated and adorned to some degree with
heavenly light and purity,

[Marginalia: The first analogy. Application.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 121 | De Prædestinatione p. 79]

to shine and glow before God himself. The nature of water is nearly the same.
You know that it has the native power,
on the one hand, to water and saturate dry things destitute of every sap and
moisture, so that when moistened they bud and sprout, though when dried out
they lost their life and strength; and, on the other hand, to wash away filth
and cleanse and purify every unclean thing. So Christ’s sanctifying Spirit
surely purges that foul flood of sin and gradually pours, as it were, the sap
and perception of grace into a person’s parched heart. By it he makes us fit
to perceive, choose, think about, love, seek with a supreme and constant
affection, and apprehend spiritual things which concern God, Christ, and the
life to come.

[Marginalia: Analogy 2.]

See how the Holy Spirit, from the very word of the gospel as from a living and
immortal seed, forms, fosters, enlarges, and advances that spiritual offspring
of life and holiness—that is, Christ’s image—in us. If you still wish to know
more distinctly and to perceive whether he is undertaking this great and
divine work in your heart or not—and you certainly must know this in order to
be certain of your eternal election, which is the aim set before us here—then
please see and observe the identifying mark or sign which the Apostle proposes
in Romans 8: “Those who are according to the flesh,” he says, “mind the things
of the flesh, but those who are according to the Spirit mind the things of the
Spirit.” Examine yourself, then, and diligently test your own mind. Do you have
a heart and affection which values the things that are truly good—that is,
one affected and touched by them, thinking about them, seeking them, rejoicing
in and taking delight in some measure in the things that are God’s? And do you,
on the other hand, truly grieve before God and from the heart over the contrary
things which please the lust of the flesh and of the eyes and greatly please
and delight the disposition of this world? Do you reject them first in
yourself and then in others? Do you refuse to take pleasure in anything which
draws you away to any degree from God or Christ, but reject all such things
and struggle against your own flesh so that you may rest in God with a calm
and tranquil mind, obey his will sincerely and cheerfully rather than
reluctantly, and submit yourself to him not from a servile fear of punishment
but from filial love and reverence toward him? If, I say, you value and feel
these things, or even if you sincerely strive and aspire toward them, all is
well: you possess the earnest and seal of the Spirit, who has certainly
renewed your mind in some measure. But if you perceive and find nothing of
this kind in yourself, and never have perceived or found it in your life, but
have an affection devoted and enslaved to other things—vain, foolish, impure,
profane, and belonging to this life and body—speaking of, valuing, desiring,
and taking its only pleasure in them, and thus still alienated from God’s life
and either recoiling from the exercises of piety, temperance, and love or
treating them with indifference, then you surely still possess a carnal mind
unrenewed by God’s Spirit. Consequently, you can have no firm and undoubted
proof or sign of your eternal election or gratuitous adoption. This, then, is
the Holy Spirit’s second effect in the elect: that saving regeneration.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the exposition. Romans 8:5. Application. Member 1.
Member 2.]

The third effect, which follows and depends upon this one, is that spiritual
rule or government by which he rules and governs the hearts of the elect. You
know that one who occupies a house as its master or lord orders and rules it
as he sees fit and disposes everything done at home according to his own will.
So God’s Spirit surely rules those whose hearts he inhabits, for “the children
of God are led by the Spirit of God,” as the Apostle says in the same chapter
of Romans. There he exercises a gracious and blessed dominion and rule in
their souls, minds, affections, wills, and every appetite, and finally in their
members themselves and bodily motions. He does this in two ways. First, he
represses, checks, calms, and extinguishes depraved impulses which incite them
to sin, whether they arise from the flesh and corrupt nature itself, the
world’s enticements, example, and intercourse, or Satan’s suggestion. Second,
he arouses and kindles good impulses, holy thoughts, and affections from every
occasion offered. Hence Galatians 5 says that “the flesh desires against the
Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh” in the two ways just mentioned: by
both taming and subduing everything depraved and implanting and arousing
everything good.

[Marginalia: Effect 3. Romans 8:14. Galatians 5:17.]

Hence the Holy Spirit is also distinguished by those excellent titles in
Isaiah 11, where he is called “the Spirit of understanding and wisdom, the
Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and reverence for
Jehovah.” Why is this, unless it is because he kindles in the hearts of the
elect those holy movements and affections of spiritual wisdom and prudence,
counsel, might, and filial reverence toward God? So too, in the same chapter of
Galatians, the Apostle enumerates these fruits of the Holy Spirit: “love, joy,
peace, meekness,” or the restraint of anger, “kindness, goodness, faith,” or
faithfulness, “gentleness, self-control.” They are called such because, where
God’s Holy Spirit reigns and exercises dominion, he produces these holy
movements and affections, and finally these virtues and special gifts. Among
them the following are especially conspicuous and must be observed by us.

[Marginalia: Isaiah 11:2. Galatians 5:22.]

1. A sincere and intense hatred and rejection of sin because and insofar as it
is sin. This is known when someone is not so much intent upon the sins of
others as upon his own and judges, reproves, and corrects himself—except
insofar as his office and calling lay upon him the duty to expose and reprove
the sins of others in love and the Spirit of gentleness, with zeal for divine
glory and his neighbor’s salvation. It is also known when he flees and avoids
sin not so much from fear of punishment as because it is evil and displeases
God, whom he perceives to be so gracious and merciful to him in redeeming and
saving him through Jesus Christ. This movement and affection truly arises
from God’s indwelling Spirit.

2. The second is a hunger and thirst for righteousness, that is, an intense
and burning desire to return to favor with God, to be united and reconciled to
God in Christ, and to perceive him as peaceful and gracious toward oneself in
Christ, above all things which we can think or desire.

3. The third is the Spirit of prayers and supplications. God’s Spirit is
called this because he makes the heart fit and disposed to pray, as was said
above. It is wonderful and almost incredible how unfit by nature the heart of
a natural person is for the work of true prayers proceeding from a humble,
contrite, burdened, groaning spirit, yet one full of confidence and kindled by
the flame of desire.

[Marginalia: 1. 2. 3. Zechariah 12:10.]

From this work in particular a person must certainly examine whether he feels
God’s Spirit dwelling in him. I shall explain it to you by the clearest
analogy. You see a mother or nurse carrying a nursing infant in her arms. If
the child seeks his mother’s breast, cries out, and sucks, by this sign alone
he sufficiently displays and testifies that he is living and vigorous. But if
for several days he is observed not to

[Marginalia: An excellent analogy.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 122 | De Prædestinatione p. 80]

cry for or seek the breast, suck, move, or utter any sound, he thereby
certainly shows himself dead or near death. In the same way, therefore, God’s
true and living children undoubtedly cry out to their heavenly Father with
earnest prayers, pant for him with their hearts, and groan repeatedly to him.
But the one who feels no movement, affection, or
ardor of this kind expressing itself through living prayers should know that
he is a dead child, destitute of Christ’s life. This is the Spirit’s third
effect, spiritual dominion or government over us.

The fourth work, and so his fourth effect in the elect, is consolation in
distresses and oppression of every kind. Hence, when our Savior promises him
to the apostles and all believers, he is called the Paraclete or Comforter.
Elsewhere the royal psalmist calls him in Psalm 45 “the oil of gladness,” which
cheers the hearts of the elect even amid their afflictions. Two things chiefly
trouble, sadden, and torment the human mind. The first are outward miseries and
calamities—for example, the loss of life, health, possessions, reputation,
children, parents, friends, and so forth, or even the fear and danger of losing
them. The second is an afflicted and wounded conscience. “Who can bear it?”
Solomon exclaims, indicating that it is, as it were, the heaviest burden of all
and an intolerable weight. This is clear from Job’s complaints and from so many
of David’s psalms, particularly Psalms 6, 32, 38, 42, 51, 102, and 143 and
others with the same subject, but above all Psalm 88. Unless the Spirit of
consolation shone upon the elect in such distress to a greater or lesser
degree, they would utterly break and fail and would be swallowed up with many
of the ungodly in that deepest abyss of despair.

[Marginalia: Effect 4. Psalm 45:8.]

But here God’s mighty Spirit supports and sustains them in temptation and
conflict so that they are not utterly submerged and overwhelmed. As the
prophet says in Psalm 69, “Let not the deep swallow them, and let not its open
mouth close over them.” Indeed, even when he appears to withdraw every comfort
of his presence from them and hide himself for a time, he is nevertheless not
absent or wanting. Rather, he holds out and presents to them from a distance,
as it were, handles to the sweetest promises from God’s word. Although their
faith is afflicted, it avidly grasps and seizes them because he strengthens
it. Then he first grants them the strength to bear and endure in that dreadful
struggle and afterward to emerge from it. Finally, bringing back the face of
God in Christ as a gracious sun, dispersing those most sorrowful clouds and
wiping away the tears of his people, he not only kindles his inexpressible and
glorious joy and consolation in their hearts but redoubles it. Its perception
is the sweeter as their sorrow was the more bitter and the struggle they
endured was the more grievous. This is the fourth effect.

[Marginalia: Psalm 69:16.]

The last effect we have judged should be treated in this place is the
strengthening and fortification or equipment of the elect, by which he so
strengthens their hearts that they can discharge the most difficult duties in
their calling, whether by acting or suffering. Hence you heard him called
above by the prophet the Spirit of might and power. A Christian certainly has
many things to do, bear, and undergo which are far beyond his strength. For
example, first, amid an earnest and intense perception of his sins, by whose
weight he is pressed down as far as hell itself, to raise and stretch out the
hand, or at least the eye, of his faith to heaven itself and thus to behold
and apprehend God’s mercy in Christ is by far the most difficult work of all
for a sinful human being.

[Marginalia: Effect 5. 1.]

Second, in the heat and violence of temptation, when Satan assails with all
his strength and incites and drives one toward sin, nevertheless to resist and
conquer temptation is almost the same as keeping kindling or tow, or gunpowder,
from catching fire when flame is applied—something most difficult for a human
being to do. Third, when a choice is presented to someone, to rank life,
possessions, honors, and everything else beneath Christ, to deny himself
entirely, and to trample upon his life and everything as refuse in order to
preserve and gain Christ alone and adhere to him constantly with an
indissoluble bond both in life and death is certainly not a work of human
strength. Fourth, when every ordinary means and every instrument, aid, and
proof of this bodily life and safety fails, when no hope shines from anywhere,
no way out of danger and no path of escape presents itself, nevertheless to
rest upon God with a firm faith, depend upon his providence with a firm trust,
and commit oneself to him with a peaceful, secure, and tranquil mind just as
if every means and protection were at hand is surely a task no less difficult
for a human being than to shake this whole mass of earth or move it from its
place.

[Marginalia: 2. 3. 4.]

What, then? Whenever necessary, that same Spirit of God who is the Spirit of
might and fortitude performs all these things in the elect. Hence, although
the Apostle complains about his gravest affliction in 2 Corinthians 1 and says
that he was burdened beyond measure, indeed beyond his strength, in the same
place he professes that the consolation of the Holy Spirit overflowed beyond
his sufferings. Elsewhere, in Philippians 4, he testifies that he can do all
things in Christ who strengthens him. Likewise in Colossians 1 he testifies
that he strives with God in prayer for the believers he addresses, “that,
having been made mighty with all power, or strengthened according to his
glorious might, they may walk with all endurance and gentleness with joy, as
is fitting for the Lord, pleasing God in everything, and, bearing the fruit of
every good work, may increase in the knowledge of God.” He also exhorts
Timothy in 2 Timothy 1 to “share in afflictions for the gospel according to
God’s power.” He indicates that it is the peculiar work of God’s Spirit,
strengthening the hearts of the elect, to endure for Christ everything that
human or even diabolical violence and rage can inflict.

[Marginalia: 2 Corinthians 1:8. Philippians 4:13. Colossians 1:11. 2 Timothy
1:8.]

These are the special graces; these are the gifts proper to the elect. They
are therefore so many most certain testimonies by which God’s Spirit bears
witness to and seals to our spirit our eternal election and gratuitous
adoption in Christ. Consequently, our own spirit and conscience bear the same
witness to us together with him and establish our election within us from at
least some perception and application of these things in at least some degree
and measure. For our consciences can sincerely and from the heart assume that
we observe in ourselves at least certain beginnings and rudiments of these
effects and gifts of the Holy Spirit and from time to time perceive them by
God’s grace—or, at least, that we strive toward them and are earnestly carried
away and aspire toward them.

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

But suppose that, after an examination of this kind and an anatomy of our
hearts, we find very little holiness and grace and therefore very little
certainty about our election. Someone may ask what we should then do and what
counsel we should adopt in such a case. We answer briefly that we must at
least have recourse to the signs most readily apparent and conspicuous to us,
and most necessary for confirming us concerning our election.

[Marginalia: Question. Answer.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 123 | De Prædestinatione p. 81]

We must see whether we find in ourselves at least the smallest measure of
grace within which and with which certainty of our election can stand, and
without which we cannot be truly persuaded of it. To do this more easily, we
shall reduce to fewer points these same signs and testimonies which God’s own
Spirit shares with our conscience and propose them somewhat more simply, so
that even those who are less learned and less exercised in that spiritual
life may be able to comprehend them when God’s Spirit shines upon them. For we,
my hearers, ought neither to grow weary nor be reluctant to linger and dwell
longer upon a matter of such great importance. You see that the subject here
is by far the gravest of all: it is most joyful and blessed for us if we attain
it, but most disastrous and ruinous if we wander or fall away from it. We are
dealing not with hearth and home but with the full assurance and complete
persuasion of that eternal election—that is, with the foundation of eternal
salvation.

We therefore say that the conscience purified and sanctified by Christ’s
blood gives us attestation of our election and salvation by two kinds of
testimony: internal and external. The internal testimonies are certain gifts
and special graces produced by the Holy Spirit by which someone can recognize
his election. These can in turn be subdivided into two members according to
their objects: some concern our sins, while others concern God’s mercy in
Christ. Those which concern our sins in turn regard sins past, present, or
future.

[Marginalia: Division and subdivision.]

The sign in the soul concerned with past sins is that grief or sorrow which
the Apostle in 2 Corinthians 7 calls “according to God.” He describes it in
that place by its effect, saying that “the sorrow which is according to God
produces repentance leading to salvation, never to be regretted.” We may
rightly and deservedly call it the mother and nurse of many other virtues. It
is a certain grief conceived in a sinner’s heart from sin with respect to God
himself. Its nature will shine forth somewhat more clearly and distinctly to
us from its opposite species if we compare this grief with the grief which is
not according to God but rather according to the world, although the latter
also arises from sin. It is in fact nothing other than that horror of
conscience and apprehension of divine wrath because of sin which torments the
sinner’s soul. The pious grief which we call “according to God” with the
Apostle can certainly take its occasion from our sins and the perception and
apprehension of them, but it properly arises from the perception and
apprehension of God’s paternal kindness and mercy in Jesus Christ. For while
the grief of the world properly arises only from sin’s punishment, the sorrow
which is according to God laments, hates, and abhors the sin committed insofar
as it is sin, because it knows that divine goodness is offended and his Holy
Spirit grieved by it, even if it knew that no punishment whatever would
follow.

[Marginalia: Past sins. 2 Corinthians 7:10. Explanation.]

So that no one may willingly deceive himself in this respect, as we said
above, let us please see by what signs and marks the Holy Spirit defines this
grief for us in the following verse of the same passage. He proposes seven of
its fruits and effects in the faithful Corinthians to whom he writes, which
we can and ought to extend to everyone. The first is “earnestness,” an effort
or concern to cease from sins and leave and avoid them in the future. The
second is “a defense,” vindication, or excuse. The Apostle seems to have called
it this by synecdoche, using the name of a part for the whole, since by this
name he indicates the whole judicial proceeding in which the sinner, as
though placing himself before God’s tribunal, accuses, judges, reproves,
convicts, and condemns himself in God’s presence, but pronounces God just in
his judgments. He gives glory to God but assigns shame to himself; he
sincerely confesses himself guilty and culpable, but entreats that God’s wrath
be turned away and humbly and earnestly seeks pardon. The third is
“indignation,” by which someone is angry and indignant with himself because of
his sins committed against God, his most kind Father. Thus he is marvelously
inflamed against himself and his own filthiness and ingratitude as against the
most shameful and unworthy of people. The fourth is “fear,” lest he ever fall
back into the same mire, defile himself again with the same mud, stumble on the
same stone, and again offend and provoke his God. The fifth is “desire,” namely
to return to favor with God and be reconciled to him, and to be supplied by
God’s Spirit with new might and strength with which to resist sins in the
future so that he may never be conquered or entangled by them or fall into
Satan’s frauds and snares. The sixth is “zeal,” that is, fervor and striving
of soul to promote God’s glory in the remainder of his life and to help and
build up his neighbors in every way, particularly by performing those duties
which he knows to oppose his own particular sins. The seventh and last is
“vengeance,” by which he tames and subdues his body through spiritual
exercises and their aids—for example, constant prayers, weeping, fasting,
sackcloth, and other things of this kind, prudently employed with a humble and
sincere mind—so that it may not be an instrument or occasion of sinning in the
future, nor disturb, impede, or interrupt the pursuit and exercises of
repentance and true piety. These are the signs and companions of grief
according to God, which is the grief of true repentance. Whoever perceives
himself affected by them should know that he is affected by true grief, which
alone is saving to the sinner.

[Marginalia: Its exposition. 2 Corinthians 7:11.]

As for present sins—that is, the remnants and corruption of sin still
remaining in our nature—the sign is that spiritual struggle between the flesh
and the Spirit which is proper to the regenerate. It must be carefully
distinguished from that bite of conscience which can be common to the elect
and ungodly whenever they transgress God’s law. This is the battle and contest
of the understanding, will, desires, affections, and all internal movements by
which they draw the regenerate person, insofar as he is regenerate, in the
opposite direction, while the corruption of nature draws him elsewhere toward
sin. It is like what commonly happens to those afflicted by the disorder
called *Ephialtes*, the incubus or nightmare. This is a severe nocturnal
oppression and suffocation of the body which interrupts breathing and impairs
and hinders movement and speech. The sufferer feels himself so pressed down by
a mass lying upon him that he cannot move his limbs, cast off that mass which
assails him, raise and lift himself up, or even draw breath or freely utter a
sound.

[Marginalia: Present sins. Analogy of the incubus.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 124 | De Prædestinatione p. 82]

So too, insofar as God’s children are regenerate and made new creatures, they
feel the stain and corruption of their nature lying upon their heart like a
mass heavier than any Etna. By it they are pressed and dragged toward the
earth but called and drawn away from God and divine things. They therefore
continually try to lift themselves and shake it off by the strength implanted
in them from above by God’s Spirit, so that they may serve God in the freedom
of the Spirit and follow and perform his will with their whole heart and
affection. Yet they cannot do so as they wish. Instead, with the Apostle in
Romans 7 they are repeatedly compelled both to confess and cry out that they
do not do the good they will, but do the evil they do not will. In this way the
all-wise God exercises and subdues them in this afflicted life and valley of
miseries and prepares them through dejection and humility for the glory of his
kingdom.

[Marginalia: Application. Romans 7:19.]

As for future sins, the affection of a truly regenerate soul concerning them
is a care and concern to avoid not only the sins themselves but also their
occasions, enticements, and provocations; to close off every approach and
access to sin; and rather to withdraw and call himself away in the contrary
direction from that toward which he knows and feels himself inclined by the
vice of his nature. John teaches us in the fifth chapter of his first epistle
that this pious care and concern is a sign of the elect: “We know,” he says,
“that everyone born of God does not sin, but the one begotten of God keeps
himself, and that wicked one does not touch him.” This holy pursuit exerts
itself not only in rightly arranging and ordering one’s outward life and acts,
but first of all in restraining and controlling the mind itself and the
heart’s thoughts and affections. For the power of the gospel, as the Apostle
teaches us in 2 Corinthians 10, when it reigns in the heart and exercises its
efficacy, “is mighty and powerful even for overthrowing reasonings and every
lofty thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God and for taking
every thought captive to obey Christ.” Hence in Philippians 4 he exhorts the
faithful that “whatever things are true, whatever honorable, whatever just,
whatever pure, whatever lovely, whatever of good report, if there is any
virtue, if there is any praise, they should consider these things, think upon
them, and delight inwardly in them.” In these things they should exercise not
only their outward life but the internal faculties, movements, conceptions,
and thoughts of the soul. These, then, are the signs concerned with sin.

[Marginalia: Future sins. 1 John 5:18. 2 Corinthians 10:5. Philippians 4:8.]

As for the things which regard God’s mercy in Christ, we shall observe these
two signs. First, when someone is pressed and burdened by the weight of sins
and is distressed in his soul at having offended God, he must be touched by an
earnest desire for Christ. Indeed, he must feel inwardly what poverty and need
of him he suffers, how intensely he needs even the smallest drop of that
precious and saving blood, and so must hunger and thirst earnestly for his
righteousness and, through him, God’s peace and grace, beyond everything a
person can attain by his comprehension or thought. The Lord himself has given
and proclaimed the sweetest promises to those whose souls are so affected.
With regard to their fruit and effect, these can pertain only to the elect.
For example, John 7: “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. The one
who believes in me, as Scripture says, out of his belly will flow rivers of
living water.”

[Marginalia: God’s mercy in Christ. Sign 1. John 7:37 and 38.]

Likewise in chapter 4: “Whoever drinks from the water which I shall give him
will not thirst forever, but the water which I shall give him will become in
him a fountain of water springing up into eternal life.” So too Revelation
21:6: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end; to the one who thirsts
I will freely give from the fountain of living water.” Hence we see what
follows from drinking or taking a draught of this life-giving and saving
water: the one who drinks will no longer thirst but will have within himself
a perennial fountain constantly springing up with which to quench and calm his
thirst and to water and revive his heart, which would otherwise be breaking
and drying up.

[Marginalia: John 4:14. Revelation 21:6.]

The second is a certain wonderful affection aroused in the heart of an elect
person by God’s Spirit, by which he values and esteems Christ and his merit
and righteousness so highly and holds them so precious in his eyes that by
comparison he counts the entire remaining world mere refuse and dung and
tramples it underfoot in preference for communion with Christ and
participation in his death and resurrection. That chosen vessel sufficiently
indicates in the often-cited passage in Philippians 3 that he possessed this
affection, and our Lord and Savior expressed it excellently in Matthew 13 in
the parable in which he compares the kingdom of heaven to treasure hidden in a
field. When someone found it, he concealed it and withdrew out of the joy he
received from it; after selling everything he had, he bought the field. He
also compares it to an exceedingly precious pearl which a merchant found and
purchased after selling everything he possessed.

[Marginalia: Sign 2. Philippians 3:7–10. Matthew 13:44 and 46.]

It is true that everyone will say and verbally profess that they are so
affected toward Christ and his blood, when in fact most have the disposition
of Esau. They prefer the cheapest broth to the right of the heavenly
inheritance and, with the ancient Israelites, would rather sit beside the
fleshpots in Egypt and eat Egyptian onions and garlic than enjoy God’s
blessings in the wilderness or depend upon his kind hand and paternal care.
Therefore, lest anyone deceive himself here as well, we shall propose these
two signs by which everyone may test his affection. First, let each person see
whether he earnestly loves a godly Christian precisely as such and because he
is such, and whether he reveres Christ’s image in his living members. Whoever
truly loves Christ certainly also sincerely loves all who belong to Christ.
The Apostle John suggests this sign to us in the third chapter of his first
epistle: “We know,” he says, “that we have passed from death to life because
we love the brothers. The one who does not love his brother remains in death.”
And the Lord says in Matthew 10: “The one who receives a prophet in the name
of a prophet will receive a prophet’s reward; and the one who receives a
righteous person in the name of a righteous person will receive a righteous
person’s reward,” that is, one who receives Christ’s members insofar as they
are such. Second, let him see whether he earnestly loves and longs for Christ’s
coming, either so that he himself may depart to the Lord when released by
death or, rather, so that Christ may return at the consummation of all things
and our communion with Christ may thus be perfected. It certainly cannot
happen that someone should hunger and thirst for Christ without ardently
desiring full communion with him, whether it comes to the departing soul by
death or at Christ’s final coming for judgment. The Apostle indicates near
the end of 2 Timothy that this is not the least mark of adoption, when he
asserts that the crown of righteousness is laid up not for him alone but for
everyone who has longed for

[Marginalia: Numbers 11:5. Sign 1. 1 John 3:14. Matthew 10:41. Sign 2. 2
Timothy 4:8.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 125 | De Prædestinatione p. 83]

Christ’s glorious coming. These, then, are the internal signs.

As for the external signs, this one will suffice: that new obedience by which
someone examines and orders his life with the greatest care according to the
standard of God’s law. As John says in the second chapter of his first
epistle, “By this we know that we know him, if we observe his commandments.”
This obedience must not be examined according to the law’s rigor, for
otherwise it would certainly furnish us with no evidence of grace but rather
an occasion for despair and a testimony to condemnation rather than
salvation. Instead, it must be estimated from God’s paternal affection toward
us in Christ, by which he kindly spares us as children and graciously accepts
our pursuit and effort, although very weak and imperfect, as the work and
effect itself because of the merit and obedience of Christ his Son, in whom he
regards us as acceptable to himself.

[Marginalia: External sign. 1 John 2:3.]

Nevertheless, lest someone willfully deceive himself in this respect also and
mock at his own peril—for sin’s deceit is marvelous and the human heart’s
hypocrisy profound—let him diligently test his new obedience by these three
signs. First, it must be rendered not merely to some or even many of God’s
commandments, but to all without any exception. Otherwise, it certainly can
be not sincere but hypocritical, reserving for sin some hiding place as if in
a secluded recess. The willing hypocrite cannot gladly dismiss and deny this
sin, because he loves and delights in it above the rest. Thus we see that
Herod did many things for John the Baptist’s sake and changed many things for
the better, and that Judas left everything, followed Christ, and preached the
gospel with the others. Yet Judas could never induce himself sincerely to
dismiss his avarice, nor Herod his brother’s wife. Therefore, nothing in their
life was sincere, even when they appeared to others to act rightly. For the
sincere obedience of a truly regenerate person, although it is not yet perfect
in its degrees but, like twilight or dawn, is a mixture of light and
darkness—that is, of the Spirit and the flesh—nevertheless extends itself to
all the commandments of the divine law without any exception. Hence the royal
psalmist says very significantly in Psalm 119, “Then I shall not be ashamed
when I look to all your commandments,” as though saying: I show myself
obedient to your will with a sincere soul when I devote myself to fulfilling
your law without excepting any sin. The Apostle James says that “the one who
stumbles in one point is held guilty of all” for this same reason: even if
someone keeps most of the other commandments, if he knowingly and willingly
perseveres against conscience in violating one or more, and always retains
the purpose of not abstaining from some known sin in which he delights, God
will surely count all his obedience as nothing. For the person who truly
repents turns simultaneously from every sin, both generally and individually
and particularly.

[Marginalia: Condition 1. Analogy. Psalm 119:6. James 2:10.]

Second, this obedience must extend through the whole course of life. Judgment
must not be passed about anyone from one or two acts, but from the continual
tenor of life. Although that life may be spattered with one or another spot or
stain from some particular sin because of human corruption, this will by no
means impair the person’s gracious condition before God, provided that he
renews repentance after his falls, does not lie secure and obstinate in some
sin, but rather strives to walk blamelessly before God and others throughout
his whole life.

[Marginalia: Condition 2.]

Last, just as the regeneration which is the source and root of this new
obedience belongs to the whole person—soul, spirit, and body—so the obedience
itself must proceed from the whole regenerate person and not merely from one
or another of his faculties or parts. For obedience is the fruit and effect
of love. But “love,” the Apostle says, “is from a pure heart, a good
conscience, and an unfeigned faith.” From this it is clear what sort of newness
of life, or new obedience, is required. The Apostle indicates it as a mark of
our election in 2 Timothy 2, where he asserts that “God’s firm foundation”
remains, “having this seal: The Lord knows those who are his.” Someone could
have replied: it is indeed true that God knows his own, but how shall I know
that I belong to him? He therefore immediately adds: “Let everyone who calls
upon Christ’s name depart from iniquity.” It is as though he were saying: let
people implore with earnest prayers the grace, peace, and mercy of God in
Christ and everything necessary for salvation; let them give thanks in all
things at the same time; meanwhile, let them abstain from all their sins and
continually correct and cleanse their life. This will be a firm sign of their
election.

[Marginalia: Condition 3. 1 Timothy 1:5. 2 Timothy 2:19.]

Finally, let each person see at a minimum that he finds in himself at least
these two signs. Without them not even a spark of true faith can exist, and
therefore not even the smallest measure of grace and holiness by which one may
become certain of his election: namely, a hatred and rejection of sin insofar
as and because it is sin, and an earnest desire to be reconciled to God
through Jesus Christ. When these two at least have occupied the sinner’s soul,
they demonstrate that he is not entirely destitute of the Spirit of grace,
provided they are solid and sincere. No one should object that Balaam, the
false prophet, wished to die the death of the righteous and for his end to be
like theirs, yet remained ungodly. The ungodly would certainly like to reach
the goal and the gate but refuse to hold to the road leading there; they would
like to reach the end, but securely despise the means appointed to the end,
namely repentance, faith, sanctification, new obedience, and so forth. The
former can be a work of nature, but the latter is a work of grace proceeding
from God’s Spirit.

[Marginalia: What the minimum is. Numbers 23:10 and 14.]

Yet no one should place this as a cushion beneath his false security and, as
they say, sleep soundly upon both ears. Rather, he should remember that he can
indeed be an infant in Christ in this way and must therefore devote every
effort to growing and maturing. Otherwise, unless he advances, he must
necessarily fail. To this end, among other passages of Scripture, let him
diligently apply to himself Psalm 15 and John’s first epistle, dictated by the
Holy Spirit for this purpose, and carefully examine himself by them exactly.

[Marginalia: Caution.]

Finally, suppose that after testing, searching, and exploring every hiding
place and recess of the soul, a person finds no certainty about his salvation,
no comfort, no sign of election, and no testimony either of God’s Spirit or
his own spirit. Someone may ask what he should then do or what counsel he
should adopt. Where should he turn in so wretched a state of soul, and what
refuge remains for him? He certainly must not hasten to a dagger, pit, or rope
with Saul, Ahithophel, or Judas. Far be it, far be it! He must by no means

despair entirely, nor should he entirely lose heart in such a case, as Satan
would undoubtedly urge him to do. Instead, Satan’s attack must be resisted and
checked by the barrier of patience and hope, so that he does not rush
headlong. Let him
certainly persuade himself that by God’s grace it is possible eventually to
attain what, to his deepest grief and torment, he now feels himself to lack:
a firm and living persuasion of his salvation and eternal election. Indeed,
if he is wise, let him seize this very thing as a handle and opportunity for
seeking God while he may be found, with burning prayers and sighs, if not with
tears, from the word heard, read, and understood and from other exercises of
piety. With God graciously helping, let him not cease to struggle continually
in the pursuit of true repentance with patience, endurance, and perseverance
until he wrests, as it were, some blessing through Christ—that is, until he
feels his heart thoroughly softened and kindled and utterly parched and
burning with that thirst for Christ’s righteousness. Some drop of that living
water—that is, of peace, quiet, and an apprehension of God’s mercy in
Christ—will undoubtedly fall upon it and at last calm and quench the soul’s
thirst and burning.

[Marginalia: Note carefully.]

Let everyone also know that godly and faithful servants of God sometimes
struggle through this shadow of death and these straits of temptation not only
throughout their whole life but even in its final hour. They certainly could
never struggle free of them unless that gracious God and Father from time to
time sent down into their hearts, by the efficacy of his Holy Spirit, some ray
of his favor toward them in Christ, and repeatedly instilled some drops of
that oil of gladness into their lamps, which would otherwise be dying. On this
matter, among others, consult these three excellent passages: the final verse
of Psalm 27, the middle of Psalm 62, and the end of Isaiah 40. Let this suffice
concerning the marks and signs of eternal election, which was the first and
chief corollary to be deduced for us from the preceding doctrine.
