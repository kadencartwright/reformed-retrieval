2. These concern ecclesiastical canons or laws to be administered and executed,
in that the prince can:

1. Visit the ecclesiastical estate and its persons.

2. Reform them, set them in order, and proceed against them, as well as against
heresies of every kind, schisms, errors, abuses, crimes, contempts, and
enormities of every sort.

3. Appoint, nominate, and grant authority, whenever and for as long as he
pleases, to such native-born subjects as he judges suitable, to exercise and
execute jurisdictions, privileges, and preeminences of every kind concerning
ecclesiastical jurisdiction; to visit; and to reform, correct, and amend any
excesses or defects that could have been reformed, set in order, amended, or
restrained by any ecclesiastical power, authority, or jurisdiction.

The prince's power to visit the ecclesiastical estate and to reform and correct
heresies, errors, and abuses is displayed for the most part in the delegated
power to visit, reform, and correct. Delegated judges can judge a cause of
heresy and decree spiritual censures. The prince can therefore exercise the
same power personally. “The authority belongs to the king himself; its exercise
is entrusted to others,” says Mucket. Through the chancellor the prince
appoints delegated judges not only to settle suits brought by appeal from
inferior courts to his supreme judgment, but also without any intervening
process of appeals,

[Marginalia: *On Polity*, chapter 3, p. 304.]

[1623 printed p. 18 | IA leaf 30 | PDF p. 30]

and indeed when no suit at all has begun in the ecclesiastical courts. He
appoints judges to exercise every kind of jurisdiction and to correct, reform,
and amend errors, schisms, and abuses. The delegates are called the *King's
Ecclesiastical Commissioners*, and their power is a *commission of jurisdiction
in ecclesiastical causes*.

The prince customarily appoints several commissioners, some secular and some
ecclesiastical: archbishops and such other bishops as he selects, deans,
archdeacons, doctors of law, members of the Privy Council, chief justices,
masters of requests, and sometimes aldermen of London, the lieutenant of the
Tower, the master of the posts, and similar persons. It is not necessary,
however, that all the commissioners selected from various parts of the country
and inscribed in the list of the royal patent—perhaps forty or fifty in
number—should be present and constitute a full session. One archbishop,
together with two or three others, whether secular, ecclesiastical, or partly
secular and partly ecclesiastical, can decide any cause without the rest even
being forewarned or summoned. If an adulterer or some other criminal is to be
punished, one who could easily have been punished in the inferior courts,
councillors or other men of standing may be advised to attend. But if godly and
upright people who abhor the ceremonies and relics of the papacy are to be
examined and compelled to conform, they are not advised, lest perhaps they be
moved by the godly people's complaints and perceive the mysteries of the
episcopal kingdom, as Barrow relates. The prince can entrust the exercise of
this authority to only one person, provided that he is native-born, whether he
is secular or ecclesiastical. This is attested by the grievances presented in
the Parliament of the estates in 1610 and can be gathered from the statute.
Thus by virtue of the statute he can appoint some papist or courtier as judge
in all ecclesiastical causes.

[Marginalia: *Discovery*, p. 252.]

The said three commissioners—of whom the archbishop is always one and without
whom the others can do nothing, unless the prince should please to transfer the
same power to another by a new patent—can inquire into heresies, errors,
enormities, and schisms which could lawfully be reformed by ecclesiastical laws
according to the statute of the first year of Elizabeth, chapter 1; and into
absolutely all offenses against the statutes of the first year of Elizabeth,
chapter 2; the fifth year of Elizabeth, chapter 1; the thirteenth year of
Elizabeth, chapter 12; the thirty-fifth year of Elizabeth, chapter 1; and the
first year of James. They can also inquire into defamatory pamphlets,
unauthorized assemblies, and books published against the tyranny of the
bishops.

These three—the archbishop and perhaps the prefect of the capital and the
master of the posts—can authorize any drunken apparitor to break open studies,
desks, chests, and private rooms belonging to any citizen of the realm, and to
search whether they secretly possess books by Cartwright, Fenner, Udall,
Travers, Parker, and others on the discipline and reformation of the church, or
writings

[1623 printed p. 19 | IA leaf 31 | PDF p. 31]

published or to be published against the bishops' foul domination, the
prelates' excesses or defects, or other corruptions of the English church, as
if it were an investigation of the crime of treason; and they can plunder them
at will.

If even the slightest suspicion has arisen, or if they themselves have
invented grounds, the said three can, by an apparitor, summon anyone holding
any sacred or civil office, at any season of the year—even plowing or
harvest—from any part of the realm, however remote, for the slightest little
offense: for example, omitting the cross in baptism or kneeling in receiving
the Eucharist, or because he secretly let fall some remarks against the simony,
luxury, or other enormities of ecclesiastics.

The said three commissioners can compel the person appearing to take the
superstitious oath they call corporal, touching the Gospels with his hand or
three fingers and swearing by God and the things contained in the Gospels that
he will answer their questions truthfully, although no accuser or articles of
accusation have yet been disclosed. This is done not only in matrimonial and
testamentary causes, but also in penal causes whose penalty is forfeiture of
goods or a still more severe punishment—and sometimes when the accused can be
convicted by witnesses or certain proofs. Trajan, although an enemy of the
Christian faith, nevertheless wrote this to Pliny the Younger: *Charges
presented anonymously ought to have no place, for this sets the worst example
and does not belong to our age.* The Lord said to the adulteress in John 8:20,
“Woman, where are your accusers?” Natural law teaches that no one is bound to
betray himself, for nature preserves itself. Under Mosaic law, “Every matter
shall be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses” (Deuteronomy
19:15). Alexander II wrote as follows to Raynold, bishop of Como, concerning
the presbyter Guilandus, who was accused of killing a bishop: *If certain
accusers are lacking, then at the dictate of justice and without any dispute
let the presbyter receive whatever he unjustly lost on this account, together
with his priesthood and benefices intact.* The Second Council of Braga decreed
as follows: *If anyone charges one of the clergy with fornication, let two or
three testimonies be required from him according to Paul's precept. But if he
cannot prove what he said by the testimonies given, let the accuser receive the
excommunication that threatened the accused.* Justinian requires an accuser so
that the accused can tender the oath back to him: *For he ought not reject in
his own person what pleased him in the person of his adversary. By the same
reason for which no one is compelled to produce witnesses against himself, he
must not be compelled to provide testimony against himself.* This oath they
call *ex officio*—which should rather be called the “officious oath”—was
introduced into the English church under Henry IV at the prelates' demand to
detect those whom that age considered heretics, especially those they called
Lollards. Foxe calls it a bloody law in the *Acts and Monuments*. It is
certainly a law

of the dragon and the beast, not of the Lamb. Yet by the same law the present
prelates attack the defenders of purer discipline and worship by which the
papists attacked the Lollards, although several jurists maintain that it was
abolished in the twenty-fifth year of Henry VIII and that commissioners cannot,
by virtue of their commission, compel anyone to take the oath *ex officio*.
Fuller proved this in his clients' cause by several arguments. Weighty and
learned men—Cartwright, Fenner, Snapp, and others—were cast into prison because
they refused to take that officious oath. After they had been detained in
prison for some time, lest the commissioners appear to have punished innocent
men with imprisonment, they arranged for the queen's attorney to accuse them
in the Star Chamber. Yet their integrity was clearly demonstrated by witnesses
produced even through the commissioners' own agency. Udall, a godly and learned
pastor of the church at Newcastle, was cast into prison because he refused to
reveal that he was the author of the book entitled *A Demonstration of
Discipline*. There he breathed out his godly soul along with a flow of blood.

The said three commissioners can order sheriffs, urban magistrates, deputy
magistrates, and other officers of the law to apprehend a person who does not
appear, cast him into chains, bind him over by surety, or drag him forcibly to
court. They can also impose a monetary fine for contempt upon one who does not
appear voluntarily.

The said three commissioners can not only inquire but also judge in all
ecclesiastical causes, including therefore a cause of heresy, idolatry, and the
like. I admit that the statute of the first year of Elizabeth, chapter 1,
provides that they condemn nothing under the name of heresy and error unless a
determination has already been made in the canonical Scriptures or general
councils. But this limitation is added to no purpose, for all heresies and
errors are determined in the canonical Scriptures. Thus the said three
commissioners, and still more the prince himself, can judge in every cause of
faith and doctrine. Indeed, under the statute he can entrust this power to any
one person, provided that he is native-born.

The said three commissioners can punish with a monetary fine anyone they
condemn by their votes, or cast him into prison. At their command, prison
wardens and jailers in every province are bound to receive and keep the
accused for as long as the archbishop and his assessors please. The grievances
presented in Parliament imply that the commissioners could, by the same
reason, be invested by royal patent with the power of life and death for the
slightest offense subject to ecclesiastical jurisdiction, since by authority
of the same patent they can impose fines and send people to prison—something
the estates never intended to enact in the statute. But let the bishops
consider what they can do by divine law. *These are not the spiritual arms of
priests, but the carnal arms which in former ages the kings of the earth
delivered to the beast.* This is not the disciples' rod, which is

[1623 printed p. 21 | IA leaf 33 | PDF p. 33]

a rod of love, but an iron rod by which the Lord's vessels are shattered. *Did
the apostles take any dignity for themselves from the palace? The church
terrifies with exiles and prisons, and compels people to believe her—the church
which herself was entrusted to exiles and prisons*, says Hilary against
Auxentius. *The king's sword is the court's sentence; the priestly sword is the
rigor of ecclesiastical discipline*, says Hildebert of Le Mans. *The apostles
were forbidden the domineering manner of rule observed by the powers of this
age*, says Ockham. *As it is not fitting for kings to exercise what belongs to
the stole, so bishops ought not exercise the things that belong to the king*,
says Anselm. It does no good that the archbishop sometimes takes secular
delegates as his associates when he exercises coercive force against the goods
or bodies of the accused. It is the same whether he imposes temporal penalties
alone, with secular men, or with ecclesiastics alone, since pastors are simply
forbidden the power of the sword and it lies outside the sphere of
ecclesiastical jurisdiction. Moreover, he is not bound to take secular men as
associates. If the dean or archdeacon are royal commissioners, he can impose
the same penalties together with them. And if the associates are only secular,
they nevertheless can decide nothing without the archbishop.

The said three commissioners can decree spiritual penalties and censures of
excommunication, deposition, and suspension. They can decree that the accused
be excommunicated and either pronounce the sentence of excommunication
themselves or, as in Scotland, order any presbyter or parish minister to
pronounce the sentence. If someone should say that in the latter case the
presbyter, and not those commissioners, excommunicates, it is as if he said
that the officer who carries out the sentence or the executioner is the judge.
The commissioners summon, hear the cause, decide, and hand the presbyter the
written sentence of excommunication to be pronounced. Are the commissioners
or the presbyters therefore the judges? Indeed, our adversaries try to maintain
that presbyters are in no way permitted to judge in the external forum and
decree censures unless power has been delegated by the bishop. Yet here the
presbyter exercises neither ordinary power nor that of a delegated judge by
hearing, judging, and deciding. He is merely a bare herald and simple servant
who must obey from fear of deposition or prison. If someone should say that
the archbishop decides as judge, the answer is at hand: the archbishop decides
not by episcopal power and ordinary jurisdiction but by power delegated by the
king, and therefore royal power. The surest indication of this is that by this
delegated power the archbishop can decree a censure of excommunication against
any accused person, even outside the province of his suffragans, wherever he
resides in any parish of England or Ireland. According to the hierarchists'
principles, insofar as he is a bishop he cannot exercise an act of external
jurisdiction in another diocese. If he baptizes or celebrates the Holy Supper
in another diocese, even without the permission of the diocesan bishop and
despite his prohibition,

although he sins against the canons, the sacraments remain true because these
acts flow from the power of order. But if he performs acts of excommunication
or absolution, those acts, flowing from the power of jurisdiction, are null and
void, according to Field. Moreover, under the statute the prince can entrust
the same power to any native-born person. The archbishop therefore exercises
not ordinary power insofar as he is archbishop, but power delegated and derived
from the royal crown. In the *Assertion of Christian Polity* we have the
following example of a sentence of excommunication issued by the commissioners
against someone for contempt: *In the name of God, Amen. We, I. W., doctor of
sacred theology, and so forth, chancellor of the church, and so forth,
commissioners proceeding duly and lawfully, pronounce I. E. of the diocese of
B. L.—lawfully and peremptorily summoned to the said day and place, forewarned,
long awaited, and in no way appearing—to be contumacious; and as punishment
for such contempt we excommunicate him in these writings.* It agrees with the
royal T. They do not decree that a sentence is to be issued; by decreeing they
actually issue it. The prince himself can do what the royal commissioners can
do, for no one can transfer to another a greater right than he himself
possesses. The hierarchists assert that the prince can make laws under the
penalty of a sentence of excommunication, deprivation, and so forth, to be
issued. Some English laws are made under the penalty not only of a sentence of
deprivation to be issued but of a sentence already issued—that is, the penalty
of deprivation by the very fact. Field, however, maintains the contrary as a
matter of law: “To make a law,” he says, *is to prescribe under a penalty which
the prescriber can impose.* From this he concludes that princes can make laws
but cannot frame canons like bishops, who command or forbid under penalty of
excommunication. If, therefore, as legislator the prince can impose the
penalty of excommunication through a law, or as judge impose it by decree, and
as supreme governor entrust that judicial power to others, provided that they
are native-born, could he not also pronounce the sentence already issued if it
were necessary and decorum permitted? Bishops too seldom or never pronounce it
themselves, but entrust this task to others. The statute says that every kind
of spiritual or ecclesiastical jurisdiction formerly exercised in the church
is united to the royal crown, and that the prince can entrust its exercise to
others. I shall therefore not deny that English kings possess power of
censures and of jurisdiction in the external forum, as some hierarchists try
to do. They can also impose the penalty of deposition upon the accused. If the
commissioners can, the prince too can. “He can depose them,” says Mucket, *as
Solomon did Abiathar—not by some canonical degradation, as if depriving them
of the priestly power which he did not give them, but only by prohibiting the
external exercise of this power over their subjects within his dominions.* As
