QUESTION XVI

ON THE PERFECTION OF SCRIPTURE

Does Scripture so perfectly contain whatever is necessary for salvation that
there is no need after it for any unwritten traditions (ἀγράφοις)? We
affirm, against the Roman Catholics.

I. In order that the Roman Catholics may more easily evade the tribunal of
Scripture, which they

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 122 | IA leaf 176 | PDF p. 176]

recognize as opposing them, they try to assail not only its authenticity
(αὐθεντίαν) and integrity, but also its perfection and clarity. Hence the
question of the perfection of Scripture is disputed between us and them.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. To establish the state of the question, observe: 1. The question is not
whether Scripture contains everything whatever that was said or done by
Christ and the saints, or everything that can in any way pertain to religion.
For we do not deny that many things were done by Christ which were not
committed to writing, according to John 20:30. Likewise, many things occur
which are, as it were, appendages and aids of religion, such as matters
concerning rites and ecclesiastical polity. These are not individually handed
down in Scripture but are left to the prudence of the rulers of the church,
who must take care, according to Paul's command in 1 Corinthians 14:40, that
all things be done decently in the church. Rather, the question concerns only
things necessary for salvation, whether they pertain to faith or to morals:
whether Scripture contains all these things in such a way that it can be the
complete and adequate rule of faith and morals. We maintain this; our
opponents deny it.

III. The question is not whether all these things are delivered in Scripture
word for word (αὐτολεξεὶ), or immediately and expressly. For we acknowledge
that many things are rightly deduced from Scripture by legitimate consequence
and are to be regarded as the Word of God. Rather, the question is whether
they are contained in Scripture in such a way—either expressly present in it
or legitimately deduced from it—that there is no need for any other unwritten
(ἀγράφῳ) principle of faith from which the things of religion and salvation
must be sought.

IV. The question does not concern intensive or qualitative perfection, which
consists in the exquisite truth of doctrines and precepts and in the most
perfect manner of delivering them. Rather, it concerns extensive or
quantitative perfection, which extends to everything to be believed and done.
The former occurs in every part of Scripture; the latter is seen in the whole
body.

V. The question is not whether the perfection of Scripture was always the
same in degree. For we acknowledge that its revelation also varied according
to the various ages of the church, so that as the church grew, revelation
also grew—not with respect to the substance of the things to be believed,
which was always the same, but only with respect to their clearer
manifestation and application. Rather, the question is whether Scripture is
so full today that, without any supplement of traditions, it is the adequate
rule of faith and morals.

VI. The question is not whether there was never any place in the church for
unwritten traditions (ἀγράφοις). For we acknowledge that God at one time
taught the church by an unwritten word (ἀγράφῳ), as before Moses. Rather, the
question is whether, once the canon of Scripture had been committed to
writing, there are certain unwritten traditions (ἄγραφοι) which are to be
received with the same feeling of piety as Scripture. The Roman Catholics
affirm this; we deny it.

VII. The question is not whether absolutely all traditions are to be
rejected. For we grant that there can be historical traditions which contain
an account of deeds, and ritual traditions which concern rites and ceremonies
of free observance. Rather, the question concerns only dogmatic and ethical
traditions, that is, traditions which concern either faith or morals; we deny
that these exist outside Scripture.

VIII. The question is not whether divine and apostolic traditions are to be
admitted, that is, all the doctrines handed down either by Christ or by the
apostles, for everyone readily grants this. Rather, it is whether there are
any such traditions besides Scripture. Therefore, until our opponents prove
by a certain argument that their unwritten traditions (ἀγράφους) actually
proceeded from Christ and the apostles—which they will never do—we reject them
as human.

IX. The question therefore comes down to this: Does Holy Scripture perfectly
contain, not absolutely everything, but everything necessary for salvation,
not expressly and in so many words, but equivalently or by legitimate
consequence, so that there is no need

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 123 | IA leaf 177 | PDF p. 177]

to resort to some unwritten word (ἄγραφον) containing any dogmatic or
ethical traditions? Or, is Scripture the complete and adequate rule of faith
and morals, or only a partial and inadequate rule? We defend the former. The
Roman Catholics maintain the latter, for they hold that unwritten traditions
pertaining both to faith and to morals are to be received with the same
feeling of piety and reverence as Scripture. See the Council of Trent, session
4; Bellarmine, book 4 of *The Word of God*, chapters 2 and 3.

X. The Jews preceded the Roman Catholics in receiving traditions. They
divided the law into written and oral: Moses, they claimed, received the oral
law on Mount Sinai and handed it down to Joshua; Joshua handed it down to the
seventy elders; they handed it down to the prophets; the prophets to the men
of the Great Synagogue; and so on, until at last it was written and recorded
in the Talmud. Hence various repetitions or secondary traditions
(δευτερώσεις) gained force among them, as Christ reproaches them. This was
Satan's device for drawing the Jews away from the written law more easily by
means of this law. By the same art he brought it about that the Roman
Catholics invented a twofold Word of God, one written and the other unwritten,
as though Christ and the apostles had taught many things orally which they did
not hand down in writing. From this arose the traditions called unwritten
(ἄγραφοι), so named not because they are written nowhere, but because,
according to Bellarmine, they were not written by their first author, or
because they are not found written in any apostolic book.

XI. Yet, lest they seem on this account to charge Scripture with
insufficiency, some of them, such as Stapleton and Serarius, distinguish
between explicit and implicit sufficiency; others, such as Perron, distinguish
between mediated and immediate sufficiency. They admit that Scripture is not
sufficient immediately and explicitly, but say that it can nevertheless be
called sufficient mediately and implicitly because, in matters which it does
not contain in itself, it sends us to the church and tradition.

XII. We, however, ascribe to Scripture a sufficiency and perfection that is
immediate and explicit, so that it is unnecessary to resort outside Scripture
to any tradition, even if it is put forward for sale as divine and apostolic.

[Marginal heading: Scripture is proved to be perfect from 2 Timothy 3:15, 16.]

XIII. 1. Paul says that every Scripture (πᾶσαν γραφὴν) is God-inspired
(θεόπνευστον) and useful for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, and
for discipline in righteousness, that the man of God may be perfect,
thoroughly furnished for every good work, 2 Timothy 3:15, 16, 17. Here several
arguments for the perfection of Scripture lie concealed. First, the sacred
writings can make a person wise (σοφίσαι) for salvation, verse 15. What more
do we seek than that we should become possessors of salvation? 2. Scripture
is useful for every theoretical and practical use: for teaching faith and
forming morals. 3. It can render the man of God perfect for every good work;
but what suffices for shepherds can surely suffice for the sheep as well.

XIV. The Roman Catholics object in vain. First, they say that Scripture is
called *useful*, but not everything useful is sufficient: water is useful for
life and air for health, yet they are not sufficient. But that which is useful
not merely for certain things but universally for all things, with a complete
and adequate usefulness rather than a partial and incomplete one, necessarily
implies sufficiency. Scripture is declared to be such here, when it is said to
be able to make a person wise (σοφίσαι) for salvation and to be useful for
teaching (διδασκαλίαν) truth and reproving (ἔλεγχον) falsehood, as
well as for correcting (ἐπανόρθωσιν) evil and training (παιδείαν) in
good. Nothing else is required for perfection. Nor do the comparisons they
offer pertain here, because it is one thing to speak of a usefulness directed
only to a remote and accidental end, such as the usefulness of air for health
and water for nourishment. In that case, usefulness can denote only an aid,
not sufficiency. It is another thing to speak of a usefulness directed to its
proper, immediate, and natural end. In that case, usefulness necessarily
carries sufficiency with it, as when fire is called useful for heating. It is
in the latter manner

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 124 | IA leaf 178 | PDF p. 178]

that Scripture is clearly called useful. Second, they say that the passage
concerns only the Scripture of the Old
Testament. If that alone is called sufficient for all things, the New
Testament is condemned as superfluous; or, if the New could be added to the
Old Testament, nothing prevents something from being added to the New today
as well. But Paul speaks of all the Scripture then in existence, and by that
time not only the Old Testament but also various parts of the New had already
been written. 2. If the Old Testament was sufficient, then the Old and New
together are much more so. 3. If the Old was sufficient in its own time, the
New was not therefore superfluous. For as the age of the church varies, so the
degree of revelation varies—not so that it may be made more perfect with
respect to the substance of the doctrines, but only with respect to their
circumstances and clearer presentation. 4. If the New Testament was added to
the Old, it does not follow that something can now be added to the New,
because the canon of Scripture is now perfect in every way, not only with
respect to the substance of the things to be believed, but also with respect
to the manner and degree of revelation that can be possessed in this life.
Third, they say that Paul does not use ὅλην, “the whole,” but πᾶσαν γραφὴν,
“every Scripture.” But if the syncategorematic term is pressed, this
perfection would have to be found in every part of Scripture, which is absurd.
For the word πᾶσα is not to be understood distributively here of each part or
verse of Scripture, but collectively of the integral whole, as it is often
taken in Matthew 2:3 and 27:45; Acts 2:36; Luke 21:32; and Acts 20:24. Thus
Cornelius a Lapide, Estius, and the Tridentine Catechism understand it.

XV. 2. God expressly forbids adding anything to or taking anything away from
his Word. Deuteronomy 4:2 says, “Do not add to that word which I command you,
nor take away from it.” Galatians 1:8 says, “Even if we or an angel from heaven
should preach to you beyond what was preached, let him be accursed.”
Revelation 22:18 says, “If anyone adds to these things, God will impose upon
him the plagues written in this book; and if anyone takes anything away, God
will take away his part from the book of life.” Here it cannot be said that
only the law handed down orally by Moses is meant, as though it were more
extensive than the written law. For the unwritten (ἄγραφον) and written
(ἔγγραφον) word of Moses differ only in manner, not in substance, and he
did not teach orally anything other than what he wrote. Hence he was commanded
to write out the whole, unmutilated law for the perpetual use of the church,
and as a faithful servant he did so, Exodus 24:4; Deuteronomy 31:9, 11, 19,
etc. Thus throughout Scripture “the law” is understood to mean the book of the
law, Deuteronomy 28:58; Joshua 1:7. Nor can it be said that the passage
concerns only the integrity of obedience, because the integrity of obedience
is related to the integrity of the law, which is such that it is unlawful to
add anything to it. Nor can it be said that it concerns additions which
corrupt, not additions which perfect. For no tradition is given that perfects
what is already perfect; and it is not only corruption that is condemned, but
simple addition—placing something beside it, not only placing something
against it. Hence in Galatians 1:8 Paul does not say merely “against,” but
“above” or “beyond” (παρ' ὃ) what was preached, as the Vulgate Version renders
it. Here Theophylact rightly says: “He did not say that if they proclaim
contrary things, but even if they preach some small thing beyond what we
preached” (οὐκ εἶπεν ὅτι ἐὰν ἐναντία καταγγέλλωσι, ἀλλὰ κἂν
μικρόν τι εὐαγγελίζωνται παρ' ὃ εὐαγγελισάμεθα). Every addition in a
matter of faith is corrupting because it is added to the foundation, which
must be one. If anything is added to it, it is by that very fact overthrown,
just as a circle is destroyed if you add even the least amount to it, and a
weight will no longer be just if you place on it something beyond what is
required. Nor are the prophets and apostles therefore to be judged as having
sinned when they added so many things to the Mosaic canon, because the ages of
the church must be distinguished, according to which revelation had to grow,
not with respect to the substance of the doctrines, but with respect to their
manner and circumstances. Otherwise, Paul, who professes that he announced
the whole counsel of God to the faithful, Acts 20:20, 27, nevertheless
declares that he said nothing beyond what Moses and the prophets had said,
Acts 26:22. Finally, most of the things which the Roman Catholics add are not
only beyond the Word, but also contrary to it. As for John, he did not look
only to his own prophecy in forbidding its interpolation. Rather, since he was
the last of the sacred writers,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 125 | IA leaf 179 | PDF p. 179]

by his Apocalypse he closed the whole canon of Scripture and sealed it with
its final words, as it were, by certain curses. Add that the argument from
parity would always hold: what is said of this book ought also to have a place
in the others.

XVI. 3. The law of God is called perfect, restoring the soul and giving
wisdom to the simple, etc., Psalm 19:8, 9. But the conversion and restoration
of the soul cannot be obtained unless all things necessary for salvation are
known. Nor can it be said that this concerns only intensive perfection with
respect to quality, because the law is spotless and without any defect in its
individual parts, but not extensive perfection with respect to quantity and
scope. For both the primary meaning of the word תמים denotes a perfection
lacking nothing, and the nature of the matter itself requires this, because
the perfection in question can restore the soul and give wisdom to the
simple, which cannot be obtained without full sufficiency.

[Marginal heading: From the end of Scripture.]

XVII. 4. The end of Scripture requires this perfection. It was given so that
we might have salvation and life from it, John 20:31; 1 John 5:13; Romans
15:4. But how could this end be obtained unless Scripture were perfect and
contained everything necessary for salvation? It was given to be the canon
and rule of faith. But a rule which is not complete and adequate is by that
very fact not a rule, for such a rule is a measure from which nothing can be
taken and to which nothing can be added—“an inviolable law” (νόμος
ἀπαράβατος), as Phavorinus says, “and an infallible measure admitting no
addition or subtraction whatsoever” (καὶ μέτρον ἀδιάψευστον, πᾶσαν
πρόσθεσιν καὶ ἀφαίρεσιν μηδαμῶς ἐπιδεχόμενον). It was given to be the
testament of Christ. But if no one dares to add provisions to
(ἐπιδιατάσσεσθαι) a human testament, Galatians 3:15, how much less to a
divine one, in which the lawful heirs believe, with no less safety than
firmness, that the supreme will of the testator is contained entire. Finally,
it was given to be the contract of the covenant initiated by God with us. Who
would say that God either promised us more things, or things foreign to those
written in these tablets, or that more or other things were stipulated from
us?

XVIII. 5. All dogmatic traditions outside Scripture are rejected, Isaiah
29:13; Matthew 15:4, 9: “In vain they worship me, teaching the doctrines and
commandments of men.” Nor can it be answered that Pharisaic traditions are
rejected, not apostolic ones. For all doctrines taught by men and not handed
down in Scripture are rejected generally, while the existence of apostolic
traditions besides Scripture is assumed without proof. Thus in Isaiah 8:20
the faithful are recalled “to the law and to the testimony,” and destruction
is announced to those who do not speak according to it. Nor can “testimony”
mean traditions, which are everywhere rejected by God, but either the law
itself, which is often called the testimony—that is, by explanation, the law
which is God's testimony—or the other writings of the prophets which were
added to the law. In 1 Corinthians 4:6 Paul forbids thinking “beyond what is
written” (παρ' ὃ γέγραπται), not only with respect to morals, lest anyone
seem wise to himself contrary to Solomon's command in Proverbs 3:7, but also
with respect to doctrine, lest anyone, puffed up by the presumption of empty
wisdom, propose foreign doctrines besides Scripture in the church, as the
false apostles were doing among the Corinthians.

[Marginal heading: Because no reason can be offered for traditions.]

XIX. 6. No adequate reason can be offered why God would have willed to hand
down one part of his Word in writing and the other part only by the living
voice. He would have provided badly for his church if he had entrusted a
necessary part of doctrine to the uncertain tradition of men, since there is
no tradition which cannot easily be corrupted with the passage of time.
Furthermore, no rule is given for distinguishing the traditions which is not
ultimately reduced to the testimony and authority of the church, and this
very authority is especially in dispute. Since, therefore, their origin is
doubtful, their authority uncertain, their meaning generally perplexed and
ambiguous, and the means of distinguishing them impossible, everyone sees
that we rightly reject them and adhere to Scripture alone as the most perfect
rule of faith and morals.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 126 | IA leaf 180 | PDF p. 180]

[Marginal heading: From the Fathers.]

XX. 7. The Fathers most clearly agree with us here. Tertullian, *Against
Hermogenes*, chapter 21, says, “I adore the fullness of Scripture,” and shortly
afterward, “Let Hermogenes prove that it is written, or, if it is not written,
let him fear that woe appointed for those who add.” Elsewhere, in *The
Prescription Against Heretics*, he says, “We have no need of curiosity after
Christ, nor of inquiry after the gospel. When we believe, this is the first
thing we believe: that there is nothing further which we ought to believe.”
Jerome, on Matthew 23, says, “What does not have authority from the Scriptures
is rejected with the same ease with which it is proved.” Augustine, *On
Christian Doctrine*, book 2, chapter 29, says, “In those things which are set
down openly in the Scriptures are found all the things which contain faith
and the morals of life.” Basil, in his sermon *On Faith*, says, “It is an
argument of unbelief and a sign of pride either to weaken anything that is
written or to introduce any of the things that are not written,” etc.
Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, book 3, chapter 1, says, “We learned the plan of
our salvation through no others than those through whom the gospel came to
us. They first proclaimed it and afterward, by the will of God, handed it down
to us in the Scriptures, to be the pillar and foundation of our faith.”

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXI. Although everything is not written part by part (κατὰ μέρος) with
respect to every individual word and deed—since individuals can neither be
numbered nor made the object of science, and these are the things treated in
John 20:30—nevertheless, everything is written according to kind (κατ'
εἶδος) with respect to the very substance of necessary doctrine. Hence it is
one thing to say that many things said and done by Christ and the apostles are
not contained in the Scriptures, which we grant. It is another to say that
such words and deeds differ in substance from those which have been committed
to writing, which we deny.

XXII. Whatever the Roman Catholics claim must be received outside Scripture
either actually exists in Scripture—such as the Trinity, at least with
respect to the thing itself; infant baptism, which Bellarmine proves from
Scripture in his book *On Baptism*, chapter 8; the unrepeatability of baptism;
the number of the sacraments, at least as the things numbered; the admission
of women to the sacred synaxis from Acts 2:42 and 1 Corinthians 11:5; and the
transfer of the Sabbath to the Lord's Day from Revelation 1:10; 1 Corinthians
16:2; and Colossians 2:16, 17—or else they are not doctrines necessary for
salvation, such as Mary's perpetual virginity even after childbirth and the
claim that Easter must be celebrated on the Lord's Day; or they are false and
fictitious, such as Christ's local descent into hell, purgatory, the Mass, and
the return of Enoch and Elijah, etc.

XXIII. The deposit mentioned in 1 Timothy 6:20 and committed to Timothy
suggests anything but doctrine handed down orally and not written. Rather, it
is either the pattern of sound words which he mentions in 2 Timothy 1:13, in
opposition to profane novelties of words and the oppositions of knowledge
falsely so called (ψευδωνύμου γνώσεως), or the talent of gifts entrusted
to him. These have nothing in common with the medley of unwritten traditions.

XXIV. The many things which Christ's disciples could not bear, John 16:12, do
not imply either the insufficiency of Scripture or the necessity of
traditions. For they were not new doctrines differing in substance from the
earlier ones, John 14:26, but the same things to be more fully declared and
more certainly impressed through the Spirit; and, after they had later been
taught by the outpouring of the Spirit, they committed them to writing.

XXV. The apostolic passage in 2 Thessalonians 2:15 does not prove the
existence of unwritten traditions (ἀγράφους), but designates the twofold
manner of handing down the same doctrine, both orally and in writing. The
disjunctive particle εἴτε, which can nevertheless also be taken copulatively,
as in Romans 14:8; 1 Corinthians 15:11; and Colossians 1:20, marks a diversity
not of the thing but of the manner, which could have been one and another,
especially in those earliest times when the canon of New Testament Scripture
had not yet been committed to writing. Moreover, even if everything had not
been contained in the epistle which Paul wrote to the Thessalonians, it does
not follow that it is not contained elsewhere in Scripture.

XXVI. Tradition sometimes denotes any doctrine which

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 127 | IA leaf 181 | PDF p. 181]

is handed down to us either in writing or orally; at other times it denotes
doctrine which is handed down orally alone and not in writing. The question
does not concern tradition in the first sense, for in this sense all doctrines
contained in the Scriptures can be called traditions, as Paul calls the
institution of the Eucharist in 1 Corinthians 11:23. Rather, it concerns
unwritten doctrine.

XXVII. Perron's distinction between mediated and immediate sufficiency is
wrong. On his account Scripture is called sufficient not in the second sense
but in the first, because it sends us to the church, which afterward supplies
Scripture's defects. But in that case Scripture's true insufficiency would be
signified. For if it sends us to the church which possesses that sufficiency,
it declares by that very fact that it does not itself possess it. 2. In the
same way the law could be called perfect for salvation because it sends us to
Christ, in whom salvation is found. 3. Scripture does not send us to the
church so that the church may propose new doctrines to us, but so that it may
explain and apply those which are contained in Scripture. Nor should it be
answered here that we maintain this mediated sufficiency when we hold that
Scripture contains all things necessary for salvation, if not expressly, then
at least by consequence. For when Scripture teaches something by consequence,
it does not send us to another to teach it, but draws from its own bosom the
things which lay virtually hidden within it. Nor can Perron's comparison with
letters of credence be applied here. Such letters are called sufficient even
though they do not contain everything that the ambassador has in his
instructions. But Scripture is not only a letter of credence; it is also the
edict of the sovereign, containing all things to be believed and done in such
a way that nothing is to be added to or taken from it.

XXVIII. The perfection of Scripture which we assert excludes neither the
ecclesiastical ministry, which was instituted by God to set forth and apply
the Word, nor the power of the Holy Spirit, which is necessary for internal
conversion. It excludes only the necessity of another rule for external
direction, added to Scripture in order to perfect it. A rule is not therefore
imperfect because the architect's hand is required to apply it.

XXIX. Some doctrines are positive and affirmative, positively explaining
what is to be believed by us; others are negative, teaching what is to be
rejected. The question here, however, does not concern the sufficiency of
Scripture with respect to negative articles, as though it ought expressly to
contain the rejection of all errors and heresies which either arose long ago
or will yet arise. For just as the straight is the index of itself and of the
crooked, errors are easily refuted by the statement of truth. Rather, the
question concerns chiefly affirmative articles, which are the very food of
the soul.

XXX. Tradition is taken either formally for the act of handing down or
materially for the thing handed down. Here the question does not concern
tradition in the first sense, in which we admit it, because through it we have
the Scriptures, but in the second sense, in which we deny it.

XXXI. The Scripture of the Old Testament was perfect essentially and
absolutely, because it sufficiently contained the substance of the doctrine
necessary for salvation in a manner suited to that time. Nevertheless, it can
be called imperfect accidentally and comparatively in relation to the
Scripture of the New Testament because of its manner of manifestation,
although in relation to the Jewish church it was the age of maturity,
Galatians 4.

XXXII. That Jesus the son of Mary is the true Messiah, or that the Son of God
came in the flesh, is not a new article of faith, but an explanation and
application of the preceding articles, teaching in the hypothesis what had
already been handed down in the Old Testament in the thesis concerning the
Messiah. Thus, when Christ adds the copy to the bond, the fulfillment to the
prediction, and the body to the shadows, he does not propose new doctrine but
only expounds and illustrates the old.

XXXIII. Tradition concerning Scripture does not prove that traditions exist
outside

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 128 | IA leaf 182 | PDF p. 182]

Scripture, because the question properly concerns not the principles but the
things derived from the principles: given the Scripture which we possess, is
there a need for some unwritten traditions to supply its defects in matters
necessary for salvation? Furthermore, this tradition is formal and active,
which we grant, because the oracles of God have been committed to the church
so that it may be their herald and guardian. It is not material and passive,
which would denote some doctrine handed down outside Scripture, and which we
deny. Thus we have Scripture through tradition, not as a principle for
believing it, but as the means and instrument by which it comes into our
hands.

XXXIV. Scripture is called perfect, not always adequately in relation to the
object, as though it perfectly explained all the mysteries it hands down, for
many things cannot be adequately expressed in themselves, such as God, the
Trinity, etc. Rather, it is called perfect adequately in relation to the end,
because it proposes those mysteries in such a way that they can be apprehended
by us sufficiently for salvation.

XXXV. When we call Scripture perfect in its being as a rule, we understand
this with respect to the whole Scripture collectively, but not with respect
to the whole of Scripture distributively, that is, each of its parts. In the
same way, in a material rule not everything that belongs to the rule is itself
the rule.

XXXVI. Although the Fathers often mention traditions, it does not immediately
follow that they admitted the Roman Catholics' unwritten traditions
(ἀγράφους), because they speak about them in various ways. First, tradition
is sometimes used by them for the act of handing down, by which the sacred
books have been preserved by the church through the never-interrupted course
of time and perpetual succession and handed down to posterity. This is formal
tradition, in which sense Origen says that he learned through tradition that
the four Gospels are undisputed throughout the universal church. Second, it
is often taken for written doctrine which was first given orally and afterward
committed to writing. Thus Cyprian, epistle 74 to Pompeius: “If it is commanded
in the Gospel or found in the epistles of the apostles or in Acts, let this
holy tradition also be observed.” Third, it is taken for doctrine which does
not occur in so many words in Scripture, but is drawn from it by legitimate
and necessary consequence, against those who demanded the express words of
Scripture and would not admit “of the same substance” (ὁμοούσιον) because
it did not occur word for word in Scripture. Thus Basil, *On the Holy Spirit*,
chapter 27, denies that the very profession of faith by which we believe in
the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit can be obtained from the Scriptures, meaning
the Creed, although its articles with respect to their sense are contained in
the Scriptures. Fourth, it is taken for doctrine concerning rites and
ceremonies, which is called ritual tradition. Fifth, it is taken for the
agreement of the ancient teachers of the church in the exposition of some
passage of Scripture, which they received from their forebears and retained
with a modest veneration for antiquity because it harmonized with the
Scriptures. This can be called a tradition of meaning or an exegetical
tradition. Irenaeus speaks of it in book 3, chapter 3, and Tertullian often in
his book *On Prescription*. Sixth, they use the word “tradition” in an
argument *ad hominem* against heretics who pressed traditions, not because
the things they proved were less fully contained in Scripture, but because
the heretics with whom they disputed did not admit the Scriptures. “When they
perceive themselves convicted from the Scriptures, they turn to an accusation
of the Scriptures,” as Irenaeus says. They therefore argue from abundance
(ἐκ περιόντος), from the agreement of tradition with Scripture, just as
we now generally argue from the Fathers against our opponents. They do not do
