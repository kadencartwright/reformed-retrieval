It is asked whether God prohibits the act or the malice of the act in his law.
The Arminians say that the act itself is formally prohibited, in *Synodical
Writings*, article, page 235; Arminius, *Against Perkins*, page 730. They say
that God cannot even predetermine one to the act without necessarily
predetermining one to

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 219 | PDF p. 270]

the malice, because the act itself is formally prohibited by God in the law.
Arminius therefore says that the very eating of the fruit of knowledge is to
sin, “the whole disorder consisting in this: that this act of eating should
have a place in the number and order of human actions.” We, on the contrary,
contend that entitative acts as such are neither commanded nor prohibited.

1. For entitative acts neither morally please God nor are morally an object of
his hatred and displeasure, because simply to act is neither moral good nor
moral evil; only to act well or badly is such.

2. For God gives law to a human being insofar as he is an ethical agent and
capable of blessedness. But performing physical acts belongs to a human being
insofar as he is a physical, not an ethical, agent.

3. For those acts are formally commanded and forbidden which make us blessed
or miserable. But entitative acts are not such; only “walking in God's law,”
Psalm 119:1, or straying from it is such.

4. For God formally commands that for whose performance the saints implore
God's grace, and he forbids that which human beings perpetrate when destitute
of grace.

It is asked whether the act and ἀνομία, “lawlessness,” are distinguished in
all sins. The Arminians deny it in the same place. We prove that they are
always distinguished.

1. For no act is essentially disordered, since then the act would essentially
be the disorder itself, which is plainly Manichaean. For although

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 220 | PDF p. 271]

snow is white, it is not whiteness itself. And a form or privation is
predicated concretely of the acts in which it inheres, but by no means
abstractly.

2. Scripture distinguishes these things. Isaiah 1:16: “Remove the wickedness
of your actions.” So also Hosea 5:15; Isaiah 65:2; Galatians 5:19. Therefore,
the work is one thing and the flesh adhering to the work another.

3. Arminius admits that eating is in itself a natural act having no disorder
in itself. But the lawgiver's prohibition does not remove the nature of the
natural act or transubstantiate it into the disorder itself.

4. For the act is a real and positive being; disorder is a moral and privative
being.

5. The act is metaphysically good and the proper effect of the first cause;
the disorder, however, is essentially evil.

6. If the act were forbidden in itself, then the contrary act which underlies
supernatural goodness would also be commanded in itself. Thus our act would
physically be essentially obedience itself, which is absurd.

It is asked whether sin is essentially opposed to God. The Arminians affirm
it, for they teach that sin is opposed to God's nature. We answer that there
are two kinds of opposition. One is internal, such as that between two
accidents, heat and cold. The other is external, which is non-agreement, such
as that between things which are primarily diverse, substance and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 221 | PDF p. 272]

quantity. In the former manner there is no opposition between God and
creatures. Nor is sin privatively opposed to God, because there can be no
privation of the highest good, since that would be the destruction of God. Nor
is it contrarily opposed to God, because that opposition exists only between
accidents. Nor is it relatively opposed, because then, if sin were posited,
God would be removed, or if God were posited, sin would be removed, which is
absurd. Nor is there a contradictory opposition here, because in that way
every creature is opposed to God: for God is not-human, not-tree, and
not-angel. Hence it follows that sin removes neither God nor anything of the
divine nature. It therefore removes only something approved by God's free
will: namely, it removes the rectitude which God's free will requires to inhere
in the acts and powers of our soul. Nor through sin, or through sin's
existence, is anything posited which is contrary to God, God's nature, or
God's essential properties—indeed, not even to his will or decree: “For who
has resisted his will?” Romans 9:19.

It is asked whether God concurs toward the entitative acts of sin by an
indifferent, universal concurrence determinable by the second cause, so that
it is not from God but from the second cause that the will's effect is good or
evil, forbidden or commanded. The Jesuits and Arminians affirm it. We deny it
and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 222 | PDF p. 273]

call their opinion absurd and repugnant to sacred Scripture. For if it is not
from God, but only from the determining will, that the will's action is good or
evil, then:

1. We pray in vain that God would obtain favor and grace for us among human
beings, and prayers would have to be addressed to Fortune or free choice, with
which grace or hatred rests. We implore from God in vain the acts of believing
or repenting rather than not believing and not repenting.

2. For God would promise us goods or threaten evils which occur through free
agents in vain, because their character as goods or evils is from the created
will, not from God.

3. For we give thanks to God in vain for deliverance from evil or the
bestowal of good through free agents. We trust and hope in him in vain, fear
him in vain, and, when afflicted by enemies, patiently submit our wills to him
in vain so that a good which will freely come to us may be granted or a present
or impending evil averted.

4. Each person's sentence will not be from the Lord but from the judge who
determines God's concurrence toward a just or unjust sentence as he pleases.

5. For the acts of believing and repenting are no more from God concurring in
this manner than the act of resisting God when he calls.

6. For God cannot convert more people than he converts or leave more in

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 223 | PDF p. 274]

their natural blindness than he leaves there.

It is asked whether God predetermines second causes to all their actions. The
answer is that the Jesuits and Arminians deny this. But we teach that all
second causes are predetermined by God. Among them, free causes are then also
determined by themselves under God and determine other, lower causes. The
arguments cited above prove this; let the following be added to them.

1. For the first agent is also the first determiner.

2. For he uses the wills of good and evil human beings, his own angels, and
devils to bestow benefits or inflict punishments upon human beings. He uses
false prophets to seduce Ahab; the Assyrians as the rod of his fury; and
Nebuchadnezzar as his servant and hammer in all that he contrives against
God's people with a bloodthirsty mind, in order to chasten and humble his
church. He uses Herod, Pilate, and the Jews to crucify the Savior and “to
fulfill all things written concerning Christ,” Acts 13:29.

3. God will lose the praise and glory of particular providence and of a world
administered gloriously and wisely if the predetermination of their acts rests
with the wills of devils and human beings.

4. According to the adversaries, all acts of devils and the impious would
occur not only outside God's intention but even against God's will, on the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 224 | PDF p. 275]

assumption that God did not will any material acts of sin to exist. But if
predetermination rests with free creatures, they could perform all their acts
against God's will.

It is asked whether, because God predetermines the will to the material acts
of sin, he is therefore the author of sin. The Jesuits and Remonstrants say
that by this title we make God the author of sin. We deny it.

1. For God's concurrence is after the manner of a physical cause, subject to
no law given to rational creatures. But causing sin is a plainly moral act.

2. For the office of the first cause necessarily requires God to furnish
predetermining concurrence to all second causes. But such an office does not
require God to be the author of sin.

3. The cause of sin is the one who contributes a truly moral efficacy to sin.
But predetermining efficacy abstracts from all moral efficacy.

It is asked whether the obligation of the first and most general cause excuses
God so that an evil action is not imputed to him, although he furnishes a
general and indifferent concurrence toward it. For as far as he himself is
concerned, God does not will directly and expressly to produce such a kind of
action as is really contrary to God's law, but instead prefers that it should
not be produced and furnishes a concurrence inclined of itself toward good,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 225 | PDF p. 276]

which is twisted toward evil by the created will against God's intention. The
Jesuits—Fasolus, Ruiz, Arriaga, and Penottus—and the Arminians affirm it. We
deny it.

1. If the preference that an evil action should not occur frees God from
partnership in the fault, then our predetermination frees him much more. For
given predetermination, God does not merely prefer otherwise—which would be a
starved and sickly will unworthy of God—but by his lordly and legislative will
of sign prohibits, disapproves, and effectually detests the malice as an
abominable thing.

2. “If one human being,” Meratius says, *On the Goodness and Evil of a Human
Act*, disputation 11, figure 5, “were to furnish another with a concurrence
toward an evil action which was such and as determinable as that which God
furnishes, he would sin and be a cause of the sin. Thus someone who helps
another enter through windows, knowing that theft or homicide will follow from
that ascent, although non-theft could nevertheless follow from that ascent,
would be a cause of the theft.” And unless he says that God is therefore freed
from the crime because his concurrence is physical and superior to every law,
he will never refute this argument.

3. If the obligation of the first cause excuses God from partnership in the
crime, predetermination renders God still more excused. 1. For it is the office
of the first cause. 2. Nor does it overthrow liberty. 3. Predetermination of
itself does not incline toward malice, but only toward the bare act. 4. For
although God does not expressly

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 226 | PDF p. 277]

and directly will to produce such an action, nevertheless, according to them, he
wills it indirectly and virtually, compelled by the second cause. Thus he will
be a more servile cause of sin, because he is compelled to will that evil
action against his own optative will. 5. What is this obligation of the first
cause? It is certainly a certain fittingness by which it is fitting that the
first cause should concur with second causes, lest perhaps the second causes,
lulled to sleep forever, should be free of all action. But the question returns:
from where comes this necessary fittingness of concurring? In a human being or
angel subject to law, such causation would certainly be unfitting and vicious.
But if the agent were superior to law, it would be fitting. God therefore
fittingly concurs for this reason alone: he is superior to every law. 6.
Indifferent concurrence is truly causative, although it is determined to
cause. Therefore, this concurrence will be just as much a concause of the
crime as predetermination. For predetermination abstracts from malice as its
effect just as much as indifferent concurrence does. 7. Nor would God cease to
be a cause of the sinful act because he does not determinately effect the act
but is determined by the second cause—unless being superior to every law freed
him from this. For otherwise someone who kills a human being after being
determined by another's persuasion would not be a cause of the homicide.

It is asked whether God's good pleasure is the first cause of all moral
goodness in creatures. The adversaries deny it. We affirm it.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 227 | PDF p. 278]

1. For no entitative act is essentially, of itself, and by its own nature good
and just. Therefore, every act is just through God's free will.

2. For God freely lays this down as law for the rational creature: because he
wills a father's preserving his son alive to be a part of God's image. And if
it had seemed good to God, he could have determined this to be a part of God's
image: that Abraham the father should sacrifice his son Isaac to God.

3. For the cause of an entity is the cause of its goodness.

4. For there is no created good which is not from the first good in itself.

5. For no external good circumscribes and limits God's will so that God should
will it because it is good. He does nothing good externally because it is good,
for he can make every good thing better and always better to infinity. A thing
is therefore good because it is willed by God, not, on the contrary, willed by
God because it is good.

6. But where the good is complex and is specified as good by the object—as in
loving and worshiping God—there the whole complex is willed by God because it
is good. But this is the will of complacency, which is borne toward the complex
object because it is good.

It is asked whether God's will and predetermination is the fate of the Stoics
or a Manichaean fate.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 228 | PDF p. 279]

The adversaries teach that God's decree of predetermination is fate. We answer
to the contrary: if fate is God's will, we do not hesitate to assert that all
things occur by fate.

1. For our fate is, “Thus Jehovah has spoken.” And given this fate, things,
though most contingent, will necessarily occur, Isaiah 14:27; 46:10–11.
Jehovah has spoken the destruction of Babylon; therefore, it will necessarily
occur, though the Medes are free executors of that fate. Jehovah has spoken
the liberation of his people; therefore, Cyrus will necessarily and yet freely
liberate them. Jehovah has spoken the piercing of Christ's hands and feet and
the non-breaking of his bones; therefore, these things will necessarily occur.

2. For God absolutely decreed all beings which will be future and the modes of
being. Therefore, God absolutely decreed that the soldiers should freely mock
Christ and that the owner of the donkey should freely give it when Christ
requested it.

3. For future contingents could not be subject to the dominion of God's will
unless, once God's decree is posited, they necessarily occurred.

4. It would be all over with the glory of particular providence, which
determines the fixed number of good and evil actions, elect and reprobate,
converted and unconverted, temptations, and victories over temptations through
God's grace.

5. Nor would our faith, prayer, hope, gratitude, fear, desire, and patience
rest and rely upon God, who determines, numbers, procures, rules, governs, and
decrees all

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 229 | PDF p. 280]

our actions and those of enemies and friends who pursue us with grace and
favor, unless God had decreed from eternity that free acts should necessarily
occur.

6. For this is God's fate. Divine fate is the cause of the act, not the cause
of the malice annexed to the act. Augustine says in *Confessions*, book 4,
chapter 3: “From heaven comes your inevitable cause of sinning, and Venus did
this, or Saturn, or Mars.” But the pagans' fate was the cause of malice.

7. For the predetermining decree is just and good, since it determines only
positive beings. But the pagans called the stars themselves maleficent. Basil,
*Hexaemeron*, homily 6: “A maleficent star! They transfer the causes of their
own malignity to their maker; for if maleficence belongs to the stars by
nature, then surely the Creator himself will be the author of malignity.”

8. For God's predetermining will sweetly conspires with elective liberty and
assists liberty to elicit free acts, by free necessity and necessary liberty,
according to its connatural mode of acting. There is no greater necessitation
here than the necessity by which a free cause must act on the hypothesis that
it acts. But Cyril of Alexandria, book 5 on John, chapter 5, says that among
the pagans God is accused of having implanted in the heavens that force which
impels human beings toward evils. And Nemesius, book

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 230 | PDF p. 281]

*On Human Nature*, chapter 35, says that those who reason in this way remove
the nature of the contingent together with free will.

9. For by the predetermining decree things were created good in their nature.
But the fate of Simon Magus—as Vincent of Lérins says in *For the Antiquity of the
Catholic Faith against Heresies*, chapter 34—was that God created human nature
with his own hands in such a condition that by a certain motion of its own and
the necessary impulse of a certain will it could or would do nothing other
than sin.

ROMANS 8:32

He did not spare his own Son.

It is asked whether the same vicious action which is done by sinning
instruments is done by God as the principal agent. The adversaries
calumniously allege that we teach that the same sin is from God as principal
agent and from human beings and devils as instruments, and that God is
therefore the principal and chief cause of sin. Thus Bellarmine, Stapleton, and
the Arminians.

We, however, teach that with respect to material being the action of God and
of the human beings who sin is the same, but it is not the same with respect
to moral being. That it is the same action with respect to material being is
clear.

1. For the Sabeans took away Job's goods, and God took away those same goods
through the Sabeans. But Job was not despoiled of his goods twice, once by God
and once by the Sabeans.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 231 | PDF p. 282]

2. For when David's concubines are taken from him through incest, God chastens
David by that very act through Absalom's defiling his father's bed. Therefore,
the material action of God and Absalom is the same.

3. In Isaiah 10:12, God “completes his work in Zion” by punishing the church
through the Assyrians who oppress God's people, as through “the rod of his
fury,” verse 5.

4. God punishes his church with slaughter, sword, and captivity through the
king of Babylon as through “his servant and hammer.” But the action of God and
of human beings is not the same with respect to moral being, because God acts
justly with respect to all four causes when he punishes the church through
sinning instruments, but the instruments' action is morally unjust and
vicious. Because God is superior to every law given to human beings, he does
not sin. Human beings, however, would sin gravely if they used other sinning
human beings in the act of sinning in this way. Nor is God's holy nature a law
to him here by which God may not exercise a supreme, eminent, judicial act
through sinning instruments.

It is asked whether God is therefore not the cause of the caused thing,
namely, malice, although he is the cause of the cause, namely, the act, because
God concurs toward the act with a universal and indifferent concurrence, and
God's concurrence is not the cause why the act is supernatural and good
rather than an act forbidden by God's law. Thus the Jesuits and Arminians. We

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 232 | PDF p. 283]

teach, however, that God is not the cause of the malice although he is the
cause of the act.

1. God is not the cause of malice because he is not bound to give rectitude to
the act, while the human being is bound, as Scotus says, book 2, distinction
34, section 1.

2. For God's concurrence is quasi-physical and necessary, not moral, and God
is not subject to the law here.

It is asked whether our theologians agree with the Libertines in this
doctrine. The Remonstrants affirm it. We deny it.

1. For Quintinus, an impure pig, said that God himself steals in the thief,
fornicates in the fornicator, and lies in the perjurer. Envy itself does not
dare charge us with such things.

2. Quintinus said that fornications, thefts, and lies are God's holy works.

3. He said that they are not to be censured unless someone thinks that God is
to be censured.

4. The Libertines say that all evil works belong to God and human beings in
precisely the same way, both materially and morally.

5. The Libertines maintain that human beings are wrongly judged and condemned
and that there is no last judgment. We banish such doctrines to hell.

6. The Libertines said that those who accuse others of sin are hypocrites,
abusing Matthew 7:5: “Hypocrite, first cast the beam out of your eye.”

7. The Libertines

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 233 | PDF p. 284]

extinguished the consciousness of sin and regarded God's irresistible
providence, the author of all crimes, as their conscience. We teach nothing of
the kind.

8. The Florentines said that sin was mere opinion and imagination and was
nothing to God. We maintain that it is the violation of God's holy law.

9. The Libertines endeavored to indulge the flesh and to live lavishly and
festively. We have not so learned Christ.

10. The Libertines taught that human beings do not properly sin and are not
subjected to God's wrath. We hold, teach, and defend the contrary.

It is asked whether this distinction which we teach—that God hates sin and yet
wills its existence—is frivolous. Arminius says it is, *Against Perkins*, page
701. We deny it.

1. For God's hating sin is not God's intending that sin should never exist,
because it is false that God decreed that the Savior's crucifixion, Joseph's
sale, the carrying away of his people into captivity by the Assyrians and
Chaldeans, the defection of the ten tribes from David's family, and Job's
despoiling by the Sabeans should not exist.

2. For sin is dishonorable evil in itself and the object of displeasure and of
the disapproving will. But for sin to exist is a useful good conducive to
God's glory, and every good which exists in time was decreed by God from
eternity.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 234 | PDF p. 285]

3. For Arminius falsely imputes to us, in his explanation of our distinction,
that we teach that God wills and approves existing sin and that God does not
hate existing sin. For God hates sin and hates sin's existence in the genus of
a dishonorable thing, and yet decreed to permit it to exist in the genus of a
useful thing.

4. Twisse rightly says that Marcus Cato willed Carthage to exist insofar as it
was a whetstone and material for exercising the virtue of Roman youth, and yet
he did not will—that is, he hated—Carthage. Indeed, I rightly add that Peter
willed Christ to spare himself, lest he should be killed by impious enemies;
and because Peter did not will this, Christ rightly designated him Satan. Yet
Peter was bound to hate that killing insofar as it was sin, Matthew 16:21–23.

It is asked whether all good and evil acts are individuated and determined by
God with respect to the acts' singularity and numerical unity. The Jesuits and
Arminians deny it, for they maintain that it is from the created will that
these acts, and this many, exist—no others and no more—not from God determining
them. We affirm the contrary.

1. No sparrow falls to the ground, nor is this lot rather than that one,
without God. Therefore, neither does this act rather than that occur without
God determining it.

2. For in that case God could neither do more good through free creatures than
he does nor prevent more evil through them than he prevents.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 235 | PDF p. 286]

3. For this numerically particular taking of Job's goods, this killing of
Christ, and this evil of the people's devastation by the Chaldeans is ascribed
to God.

4. For the number of beings would not be from God. Nor would the abundance and
scarcity of grain, herds, money, acts of favor and hatred, injuries, and
benefits, which often proceed from free causes, be from God, contrary to
Deuteronomy 28.

5. For it would not be from God that Christ should be crucified rather than
stoned, that he rather than Barabbas should be killed, that Solomon rather
than Adonijah should be heir to David's kingdom, or that Ahithophel's counsel
should be rejected rather than received.

7. For the number of the converted and unconverted, elect and reprobate, would
not be from God, because conversion rather than non-conversion would be from
the created will, not from God.

8. For the created will would be the first determiner.

It is asked whether permission is bare non-efficiency and an abandonment of
the will to its natural indifference of acting or not acting. Thus the Jesuits
and Arminians. We deny it.

1. For permission is often an act of punishing. But it is entrusted to no
creature that it can, according to its own choice, be punished or not punished
even after sentence has been passed.

2. For permission in this sense, in good acts,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 236 | PDF p. 287]

would have a place; and the good angels, Christ, and all the saints would be
permitted by God finally to fall away, because their will is not violated
toward good.

3. For in devils, murderers of parents, and all the devil's slaves whose will
is not violated there would be the same permission as in the elect and
regenerate.

It is asked whether permission of sin occurs only by persuasion. Arminius
affirms it. We deny it.

1. In this way God would permit sin as Cicero and the devil do.

2. For permission would posit sin as non-future just as much as future, just
as dissuasion from sinning does.

3. For if God permits only by persuasions, there is no reason to thank God for
a sin prevented and no reason to pray that God would prevent it, because it is
in our power to believe the persuasions or not.

It is asked whether, given permission, sin necessarily follows by logical
necessity. The Arminians deny it: *Remonstrant Confession*, chapter 6, thesis
3; Arminius, *Against Perkins*, page 667. We affirm it.

1. For permission is the denial of efficacious grace required to avoid this
sin when temptation presses here and now. But given the denial of grace, sin
necessarily follows in the hour of temptation.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 237 | PDF p. 288 | stop before CAPUT TERTIUM]

2. For Scripture argues from the positing of permission to the positing of
sin. God permitted Pharaoh to detain his people and Sihon to deny passage to
the people. Therefore, the former detained them and the latter denied passage.
Again, God did not permit Abimelech to violate Sarah, Abraham's wife; therefore,
he did not violate her, Genesis 20:6. He did not permit his people to
perpetrate the abominations of the nations; therefore, they did not perpetrate
them, Deuteronomy 18:14. So also Genesis 31:29; 1 Samuel 25:32; Psalm 106:41;
Acts 17:30.

3. Permission of sin is often the punishment of sin. But it is not a
punishment except insofar as sin follows permission.

4. For a form or privation must inhere in a capable subject at the due time.
Therefore, when grace is denied, as occurs in permission, privation—namely,
sin—must be posited.

5. For to maintain that, given permission, the permitted sin is not posited
utterly uproots fear of God, trust, fervor in prayer, humility, and gratitude.
For I pray devoutly or fear in vain lest God permit me to sin or hand me over
to a reprobate mind if, given permission, I am able not to sin.
