## Part XXXVIII: Necessity and Contingency

[Part II | PDF page 204 | logical source scan 203 | printed page 202 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

That sins are excused neither by the natural necessity of sinning nor by the necessary outcome of eternal predestination in both the elect and the reprobate; and that contingency is not removed

Indeed, this doctrine is dreadful and abominable to hear in God’s church: that either something can occur which God eternally does not will, or what God wills to occur cannot occur. If piety and truth itself condemn this as impious, it follows that only what God wills to occur occurs, and what he does not will to occur never occurs. Anyone who excepts from this the destruction of those to be condemned or already condemned detracts equally from God. “But I do not say that,” Andreae will reply; “I deny that it occurs from that absolute decree you attribute to God.” As though we assigned to God any such absolute decree as he dreams, by which God condemns someone from his will alone! On the contrary, we say that from eternity he simultaneously decreed both whom he would destroy in time and through what causes—causes inhering not in God who decrees but in those to be destroyed. He decreed to destroy only by just judgment those who deserved destruction.

If Andreae imagines that everyone, indeed every individual, can will to believe—

[Part II | PDF page 205 | logical source scan 204 | printed page 203]

—, he must attribute this either wholly to nature, partly to nature and partly to grace, or to grace alone. If to nature alone, let him openly go over to the Pelagians. If partly to nature and partly to grace, let him go to the Semi-Pelagian sophists and make terms with the Jesuits, although he will not be admitted even by the sounder scholastics. But if to grace alone, let him henceforth be orthodox with us and cease making this grace universal. For since “to draw,” as Augustine rightly explains, means nothing other than making one who is unwilling by nature willing by grace, it would follow that all are drawn if it is given TO ALL to be able to will to believe. It would follow again that all not only can come to Christ but actually come, since Christ testifies that all who have heard and learned from the Father come to him. All to whom it is given to know the Son and to be able to will to believe in him will certainly have heard and learned from the Father. What, then, will become of Christ’s other words, in which he testifies that not all, much less every individual, know the Father, but only those to whom he has revealed him (Matthew 11:27; Philippians 1:5; and frequently elsewhere); that those to whom God’s arm has not been revealed not merely do not believe but cannot believe (John 12:39); and that those to whom it has not been given by the Father not merely do not come but cannot come (John 6:65)? To what sound-minded people will Andreae persuade the claim that God ever willed the world to be saved—that is, ever willed to draw or gift with faith those for whom Christ himself testifies that he does not pray to the Father?

But here I seem to hear some confronting me and shouting with the remnants of the Pelagians: “Why, then, are those who do not believe condemned if they cannot believe because God does not will to convert them?” As though this natural incapacity to believe and submit to God, curable by God alone through grace, arose either from the creation of nature or from God—who is debtor to none—not willing to gift them with the grace of conversion, rather than from that one voluntary and willing apostasy transmitted to us from our first parents, although not apart from God’s will. By this apostasy the apostle testifies that the mind is not merely not subject to God but cannot be subject (Romans 8:7). The sophists’ substitution of a mere weakness for this necessity does not even need refutation, since all Scripture says we were not merely wounded but killed through sin, and that the old human being must not be revived or helped as though infirm, but slaughtered and entirely abolished as a savage and ferocious beast.

“Yet,” some will again say, “it was God’s will that Adam should cast himself and all his descendants into this necessity, which nothing can resist. What cause does God have for complaint?” Let the apostle answer for me: “O the depth!” Whoever does not rest in that reply may torment himself with those delusions as long as he pleases; he will never find what exists nowhere, as we have already shown from Augustine and truth itself dictates, since nothing is higher than God’s will. If someone objects that the apostle there discusses not Adam’s fall but the cause of the reprobate’s condemnation, which they could not escape if God hardens whom he wills, he assumes something true but concludes nothing against us. For that first fall can no more be exempted from God’s will than the necessity which followed from it and enveloped Adam and all his descendants. That the apostle leads us thus far so that we learn to abhor humanity’s foolish wisdom is also manifest because he compares God with a potter and the human race with a lump—

[Part II | PDF page 206 | logical source scan 205 | printed page 204]

—not a lump already created, but one to be formed such that from it he makes, that is, commands to be born, some vessels for honor or eternal life and others for dishonor or everlasting destruction. No place could have existed for this distinction unless the voluntary change of those first two, created from that clay as the stock of the whole human race, had intervened.

Yet without entering that profound abyss—indeed, building upon it what is most true—the error of those who think God cannot be vindicated from injustice if anyone necessarily perishes whom he justly decreed from eternity to destroy can also be refuted by three arguments. First, if no divine decree is held to intervene here, God’s eternal providence is abolished, since it would begin only from humanity’s first fall. Second, unless, as Augustine rightly speaks, God’s will is held to be the necessity of all things in the composite sense, as the schools say, his omnipotence is abolished. Third, if the outcome of his will is suspended from any secondary cause as its foundation, the entire series of causes is inverted, so that secondary causes do not depend upon the first but the first upon secondary causes. All these consequences are most absurd, utterly false, and finally impious.
