To complete this controversy, certain arguments of our opponents finally
remain to be answered.

Objection 1. The first and most obvious to anyone is that throughout this
entire argument, both in the Epistle to the Romans and in that to the
Galatians, the apostle mentions circumcision so frequently and sets it down
as an example when he denies justification to the law. Thus Galatians 5:2,
4: “Look, I Paul say to you, If you are circumcised, Christ will profit you
nothing. You who are justified by the law have been made void of Christ and
have fallen from grace.” It is as though being circumcised and expecting
justification through the law were the same thing. Consequently, when he
denies justification to the law, he must mean the law of circumcision—that
is, the ceremonial law.

[Marginalia: See Calvin on Romans 3:20.]

Solution. I answer that this does not follow, because another and entirely
true reason both can and must be assigned for the fact just mentioned. In
this argument the apostle frequently mentions circumcision while excluding
the law and its works from justification, not because he wished to banish
from it only the law of circumcision, that is, the ceremonial law, but:

1. Because this dispute first arose from circumcision in Acts 15.

2. Because the Jews boasted especially in circumcision, as is evident from
Romans 2:23, 25 and other passages. Indeed, when the apostle himself shows in
Philippians 3:4–5 that, if anyone else had something in which to trust in the
flesh, he above all did, if only he wished,

[Marginalia: “We know, however, that they boast only in external masks.”
Calvin on Romans 3:20.]

[1679 printed p. 107 | IA leaf 115]

he puts this first: “circumcised on the eighth day.” Thus, after the Jewish
manner, circumcision was as it were the supreme and principal head of all
fleshly confidence and boasting.

3. Because the Jews and Judaizing pseudo-apostles, though careless violators
of the moral law, as is evident from Romans 2:23 and Galatians 6:13, pressed
circumcision more earnestly as necessary to salvation. Like amber, which
draws straw, not like a magnet which draws iron, they seized these lighter
scraps of ceremonies with the greatest vehemence while making nothing of the
weightier matters of the law. So men in desperate health, about to breathe
their last, are accustomed—as physicians say—to pluck at little tufts like
cancer patients. To apply a remedy to men sick unto death in this way, the
apostle tears off and snatches away this plaster of circumcision, now useless
and even harmful. He does so more frequently and sometimes more vigorously
the more he sees them boast in it, trust it, and not merely offer it to other
Jews and Gentiles as a πανφάρμακον, a universal cure, but force it upon them
more imperiously.

4. Finally, because circumcision was the entrance and first initiation into
all Judaism, as well as its continuing mark and token afterward. Once it had
been banished, the whole law, at least insofar as it was the Mosaic pedagogy,
was thereby commanded to depart together with it from the business of
evangelical justification.

[Marginalia: “Moreover, in their opinion circumcision was a kind of
initiation into the righteousness of the law. It therefore seemed at the
same time a work of primary dignity, indeed almost the foundation of
righteousness and works.” Calvin on Romans 3:20.]

These and perhaps other reasons of this kind explain why the apostle mentions
circumcision so frequently in his argument. It was not so that he might
exclude it alone, and with it only the ceremonial law, of which it was a
great and primary part, as they labor to prove from this. For in order more
fully to settle this question of justification, which arose from
circumcision, as I said earlier, the apostle does not rest in circumcision or
the ceremonial law alone. He recalls it to its source, as Melanchthon says,
or to its genus, as Peter Martyr speaks. In order to prove *a fortiori* that
we cannot be justified by legal ceremonies, he proves more generally that it
cannot be done by any works whatever—not by works of the moral law itself,
much less ceremonial works. Let this suffice for the first objection.

[Marginalia: See Hoornbeeck, *Summary of Controversies with the Greeks*,
p. 843; see Peter Martyr in the preface to Romans 4.]

Objection 2. The second is Alfonso de Castro's, under the word “Faith,” heresy
2, folio 210b. Bellarmine quotes it from him in *On Justification*, book 1,
chapter 19. It is this: Paul is concerned in those two epistles to show that
the Mosaic law, which had been given to the Jews alone, had now ceased, and
that Christ's new law is common to all. Therefore, immediately after the
words, “We conclude that a man is justified through faith without works of
the law,” he adds, “Is he the God of the Jews only, and not also of the
Gentiles?”

[Marginalia: “He does not confine himself within ceremonies or dispute
specifically what force they possess, but comprehends the whole law.” Calvin,
at the place cited above.]

Solution. I have the following answers.

1. Whatever force this objection may finally have, and however well adapted
it may be to the proposition Alfonso wishes to prove, Bellarmine seems to
acknowledge that it lacks sufficient strength for that purpose, since despite
it he goes over to the contrary opinion. We therefore have little to fear
from an argument to which the Roman Catholics themselves give less credence.
Let this be the answer *ad hominem*. As for the matter itself:

2. This argument appears exceedingly tangled and quite inept for proving what
he intends. It must be formulated as follows: after the apostle concluded
that a man is justified by faith without works of the law, he immediately
adds, “Is he the God of the Jews

[1679 printed p. 108 | IA leaf 116]

only, and not also of the Gentiles?” Therefore he excludes from justification
only works of the ceremonial law, not also of the moral. But what reason for
this inference is here, or even appears to be here? Is the connection between
clauses always such that, when the preceding verse has treated one matter,
the immediately following verse cannot treat another? In Paul's writings,
and especially in this epistle, the opposite commonly occurs. Learned men
have noted that he often passes abruptly from one matter to another because
of his more ardent disposition and because of the various things and persons
about which, or with whom, he wrote. Or is there such a connection between
the terms here that the consequent can be properly and necessarily elicited
from the antecedent? Can one directly conclude, merely because Paul says that
a man is justified by faith without works of the law and immediately adds,
“Is God only the God of the Jews?”, that he is therefore speaking of
ceremonial works alone?

It may be said that the inference is excellent because, if justification
were from works of the law, only those under the law would be justified, and
these were the Jews alone. The apostle commonly describes them by saying that
they were ἐν τῷ νόμῳ, “in the law” (Romans 3:19), and ὑπὸ νόμον, “under the
law” (Galatians 4:3–4), whereas the Gentiles are said to have been “without
the law” (Romans 2:12). Moreover, Alfonso says that since the apostle's aim
is to prove that the Mosaic ceremonial law would cease after Christ, we must
believe that he spoke of that law when he excluded works of the law from
justification. Since that ceremonial law was proper to the Jews, the
connection of the two verses is fitting enough: “We are justified by faith
without works of the law. Is he the God of the Jews only?”, and so forth.
These following words appear clearly to show the works of which law are
excluded from justification in the preceding words: namely, the law proper
to the Jews, which was the Mosaic and ceremonial law.

[Marginalia: Peter Martyr connects the passages in nearly this way: “He
proves what he had proposed, that no one is justified apart from works; for
if righteousness depended on them, God would appear to have been only the God
of the Hebrews,” and so forth. See him on the passage. Bellarmine himself
wants a twofold question to be treated in Galatians: the special question,
whether legal ceremonies pertain to Christians, and the general question,
whether justification is through law. *On Justification*, book 1, chapter
19.]

I answer:

1. I deny what forms the basis of Alfonso's argument: that the apostle's whole
purpose in these two epistles is to prove that the Mosaic ceremonial law was
now to cease. Let this be one proposition which he establishes, as Calvin
concedes, but it is not the only or even the principal one. The principal
proposition was that we are justified by faith alone, without works not only
of the ceremonial law but also of the moral law, as we sufficiently proved
earlier from the sequence of the apostolic argument. He proposes this for
proof; he pursues it in both epistles more fully and even more sharply; he
ζητεῖ, investigates it, and concludes it after the manner of disputants.
Almost everything in both epistles pertaining to doctrine can and ought to
be reduced to it. Among the rest is what Alfonso wants to be principal,
namely, that the Mosaic law was now to cease. For the Jews and pseudo-apostles
maintained that it must be continued and even imposed upon the Gentiles if
they wished to be saved. Against them, the apostle's whole purpose is to
prove that Gentiles as well as Jews are justified by faith alone, not from
works—even moral works, much less ceremonial works now to cease. Thus by one
and the same labor he refuted his opponents' πρῶτον ψεῦδος, their fundamental
falsehood, namely, the continuation of the Mosaic law, and at the same time
firmly established the primary gospel doctrine which he undertook to unfold:
justification by faith without works.

2. As for the connection between those two verses: after concluding in the
former that a man is justified by faith, not by works

[1679 printed p. 109 | IA leaf 117]

of the law, Paul immediately adds, “Is he the God of the Jews only, and not
also of the Gentiles?” The reason is not the one Alfonso imagines: that by
works of the law Paul means only ceremonial works, which alone he wishes to
exclude from justification. Rather, Paul had previously proved both Jews and
Gentiles guilty against the moral law in verse 9 of that chapter, and thus
that “no flesh,” whether Jewish or Gentile, could be justified by works of
the law which they had transgressed, in verse 20. Finally, he concluded that
ἄνθρωπον, “a man”—not “a Gentile,” as Ambrose explains it, but indefinitely
and universally, any and every man from among Gentiles and Jews—is justified
by faith without works of the law. With a further ἐπερώτησις, question, he
inculcates and explains this in the following verses: the Gentiles, whom the
Jews wished to exclude, were entangled in the same bond of guilt and would
therefore share with the Jews both the same justification and the same manner
of being justified, namely through faith without works. Our writers Piscator
and Pareus explain it this way, as does Aquinas among the Roman Catholics,
most aptly and in accord with the apostle's intention.

Alfonso therefore proves nothing from the connection of these two verses to
show that only works of the ceremonial law are excluded. But if connections
are to be pursued, the connection between the parts of the same clause,
namely verse 20, plainly establishes the contrary. After concluding that “no
flesh is justified by works of the law,” Paul immediately adds in the same
verse, “because through the law is the knowledge of sin.” Thus works of the
moral law are excluded, since knowledge of sin comes principally through
that law, as we showed earlier.

[Marginalia: Chrysostom on Romans 3:28.]
