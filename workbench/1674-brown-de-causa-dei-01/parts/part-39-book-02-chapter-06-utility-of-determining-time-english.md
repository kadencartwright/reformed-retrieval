CHAPTER VI

The General Usefulness or Necessity of Determining Time for the Worship of God
Is Demonstrated

Having premised these general points both about time considered abstractly and
about time applied to divine worship, we must now consider in general—for we
will speak later about particular determinations, both with respect to the
various aspects of time and the various kinds of determination—whether it is
necessary that some time be determined for performing sacred duties or
worshiping God.

In order that the necessity or usefulness of determining time in general may
appear in relation to religion or the worship of God, we must first consider
whether some determination of time is useful and necessary for carrying out
any other business—for example, for advancing one's studies—so that everything
may become clearer.

The following considerations can demonstrate the necessity or usefulness of
this determination of time for advancing studies or doing something similar.

1. Acquiring knowledge of human affairs is a matter of great importance.
Accordingly, it is highly useful, if not necessary, that some determinate time
be spent on this

[1674 logical printed p. 260 | IA leaf 278 | PDF p. 278]

business. A matter of some importance ought not to be neglected, but for that
very reason requires a determination of time so that it may be attained or
completed. No one with a sound head judges that time should be assigned to
chasing butterflies or performing some useless task; but more sensible people
acknowledge that time ought to be determined for carrying out an important
matter. Thus the necessity or usefulness of the business itself says that it
is useful or necessary that time be determined for doing it.

2. When we are pressed by another thing no less useful or necessary in its own
kind, it is impossible to carry out this important business at the same time
and pursue it with the same ardor and intensity of mind. For either one will
occupy the whole person and distract him from all other things. It is therefore
necessary that time be determined for pursuing either this, that, or both, if
both are equally useful or necessary, lest both be neglected, or at least the
more excellent one. Thus the impossibility of carrying out simultaneously two
important matters which demand the whole person demonstrates the necessity of
determining time.

3. Sometimes even those whom studies chiefly concern have minds too greatly
alienated from them. It is therefore necessary that some determination of time
be made to counteract this aversion. Young people, who have the greatest
interest in acquiring learning, are often averse to studies and neglect them.
It is therefore necessary that those who have the authority prescribe and
determine a suitable time for them to do this business. Thus this aversion of
mind in some people requires the determination of time for pursuing studies.

4. Those whose minds are not sufficiently fixed upon their studies are often
easily impeded in advancing them and are called away and distracted by slight
occasions. Therefore, to counteract those impediments and distractions, it is
necessary that the time to be spent on this business be determined.

5. Many people do not know, or at least pretend not to know, what time is most
opportune or suitable for this or that business, or when such a thing can be
attained more safely and easily without wholly neglecting other things which
are also necessary in their own kind. It is therefore necessary that time be
determined for performing that action of the greatest importance, so that,
every pretext having been removed, the person who neglects this necessary
business, or who has no desire to spend any time upon it, may be deprived of
every excuse.

6. Conversely, there are some who have their minds so fixed upon this single
business that, intent upon it alone, they wholly neglect other things necessary
and not to be despised in their own kind. Or at least they do not know how much
time they may safely spend on it and how much on other business; or perhaps
they are troubled and distracted every day by scruples and doubts about the
matter, so that they often become wholly unfit to perform any work properly. It
is consequently clear that time must be determined, so that the scrupulous or
doubting person may with a safe conscience spend a suitable time upon the most
necessary business without neglecting other things.

These reasons show clearly enough that it is useful, and indeed necessary, for
time to be determined for carrying out every important business. They likewise
demonstrate that it is highly useful

[1674 logical printed p. 261 | IA leaf 279 | PDF p. 279]

and necessary for time to be determined for celebrating the worship of God.

1. No one will deny that religion and the worship of God are a business of the
greatest importance, both as regards God himself and as regards us. For in that
exercise God's primacy, dominion, glory, wisdom, goodness, omnipotence,
omniscience, justice, holiness, and so forth are publicly declared and
acknowledged. Those who worship God declare him to be the one and only God,
and the only one to be adored, glorified, exalted, and so forth. The principal
end of a human being is to glorify God in all things (Romans 11:36; 1
Corinthians 10:31). Thus, with respect to God, it is a matter of great
importance. It is so also with respect to the person himself, because his whole
salvation turns, as it were, upon this hinge. Here the highest honor of the
creature shines forth, when he worships and adores his Creator and supreme
Lord. In this lies a person's salvation and blessedness, and he ought to prefer
this spiritual and everlasting happiness to every other worldly business.
Therefore, if earthly business of any importance necessarily requires some
determinate time in which it may be performed more certainly and easily, the
worship of God, which must be preferred far above them all, will much more
surely require it. No one ought to neglect his salvation or postpone the means
necessary for attaining it to earthly things. Rather, he ought to cast aside
and despise all things which may become an obstacle, however good and useful
they are in themselves, as is clear from Matthew 16:26 and Mark 8:37. Indeed,
even for Adam in the state of integrity, solemnly worshiping God was a matter
of the greatest importance and to be preferred to all his other affairs. How
much more necessary will it be for us now that we are fallen, since we have all
sinned and fall short of the glory of God (Romans 3:23)? This is clear and
denied by no Christian.

2. From what was said above about God's worship, and from common experience,
it is clear that no one can give himself wholly to divine worship and at the
same time pursue earthly business with an equal affection of heart. For the
worship of God ought to occupy the whole person; we must worship God, who is
Spirit, with the whole heart, the whole mind, and all our powers. Otherwise we
take his name in vain and profane his worship. While we are engaged in God's
worship, we ought not even in our thoughts to turn earthly things over in our
minds or be occupied among them. It is therefore clear that we cannot at one
and the same time both worship God solemnly—for we are not speaking about that
mediate worship by which we are said to worship God whenever we perform any
commanded duty that immediately concerns ourselves or our neighbor, but about
worship immediately directed toward God—and perform other things also
commanded us which concern our earthly life, the public good of the city or
commonwealth, and that of our other neighbors, and which must also be performed
diligently (Ecclesiastes 9:10; Romans 12:11). Indeed, some affairs concerning
this life at times scarcely admit even ejaculatory worship, much less solemn
worship. Therefore, since it is impossible both to pursue these earthly things
in the manner we ought and at the same time to worship God with the ardor of
mind we ought, and since it is acknowledged that we must also attend to these
transitory and earthly things properly and in the prescribed manner, it cannot
be denied that it is useful, indeed necessary, for time to be determined for
celebrating God's solemn worship.

3. It is sufficiently clear—for daily experience, alas, confirms it—

[1674 logical printed p. 262 | IA leaf 280 | PDF p. 280]

how averse human beings are from this spiritual worship of God because of
their natural corruption and perversity of mind; how little they esteem this
necessary duty of divine worship; and indeed how far no small number recoil
from this honorable work. Thus, if some time had not been determined for God's
worship, it would be feared that they would never or only rarely give
themselves to divine worship. Hence it is clear how necessary it is for time to
be determined for this business. Otherwise many would spend no time at all in
advancing God's glory and their own salvation, and so would wholly neglect
this supremely necessary work. Thus this alienation of mind in many people
sufficiently confirms both the usefulness and the necessity of this
determination of time.

4. No one can be ignorant how many impediments Satan is accustomed to place in
the way, how many excuses our corruption puts forward, and how many
distractions earthly business creates—to say nothing of the opposition made by
wicked people, who are Satan's instruments in this. Hence we are either
prevented from undertaking this work or from proceeding in the celebration of
God's worship, and those obstacles draw away, or at least distract, the mind
even when some inclination to worship God appears. Who will deny that these
distractions and impediments will be much more effective when someone
undertakes this spiritual work with a reluctant mind, as, alas, happens not
rarely? All these things abundantly demonstrate the usefulness and necessity
of determining time for worshiping God religiously.

5. Who will not admit that many people are ignorant of the fair and just
proportion of time which ought to be given to God and his worship and that
which ought to be given to these earthly affairs? Who can fail to recognize
that there are very many who would give more than a fair amount of time to the
affairs of this life, and less than a fair amount to religious business and to
things concerning God's glory and their own eternal salvation? Preoccupied by
prejudices and carried away by a disorderly love of earthly things, they are
blind in matters concerning the soul, gape after these transitory things, and
pursue them far too ardently while setting aside better things which ought far
more to be preferred. Who, then, will say that they will be fair judges in this
matter, will rightly divide time between divine and earthly things, and will
not indulge their carnal inclination more than is fair? With respect to such
people, therefore, all fair judges of affairs will concede that it is highly
useful, indeed necessary, that the time to be spent on divine worship be wisely
determined.

6. On the other side there are some, though far fewer, who, laboring under
ignorance and perhaps blinded by some superstition, scarcely think it safe to
give any notable time to earthly affairs, but wish to spend almost all of it
upon the immediate worship of God—as if the Lord did not also require us to
perform our duty toward ourselves and toward others, whether equals,
superiors, or inferiors, according to the nature of our particular calling. It
is also not improbable that there are some who, though they know well enough
that earthly things are not to be wholly neglected, may nevertheless be
continually troubled by scruples because they do not know the just and fair
proportion, and so often fear that they are seeking worldly things too much
and neglecting God's solemn worship. With respect to these and similar people,
therefore, every consideration of reason and equity requires that time be
determined for the solemn and immediate worship of God.

Since these reasons, even when considered individually, establish firmly
enough the necessity of determining time for celebrating God's worship, much

[1674 logical printed p. 263 | IA leaf 281 | PDF p. 281]

more will no one deny that this necessity follows when they are all taken
together. And it has already been abundantly shown, nor will anyone deny, that
all these reasons have a place in the business of religion.
