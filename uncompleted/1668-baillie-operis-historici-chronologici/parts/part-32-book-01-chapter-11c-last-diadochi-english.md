Polysperchon, an infamous old beggar, afterward lived and died in obscurity.
His son Alexander was murdered by the Sicyonians. Alexander’s wife
Cratesipolis, however, a prudent and courageous woman, avenged her husband’s
murder and held Corinth and Sicyon beneath her soldiers’ yoke; but in the end
she lost those cities too, along with the rest of the Peloponnese, as the price
of Heracles’s blood.

> Marginalia, printed p. 210: *Polysperchon is reduced to beggary*.

Under the pretext of avenging the murder of Alexander’s children and setting
the Greeks at liberty, Antigonus made war upon Cassander; in reality, he wished
to enlarge his own vast dominions. The eradication of Alexander’s offspring
had by no means occurred against the wishes of Antigonus, Ptolemy, or Seleucus:
from that time all of them, just like Cassander, placed royal diadems upon
their own heads. Antigonus also had Cleopatra, Alexander’s full sister,
strangled when he could not draw her away from her proposed marriage to
Ptolemy.

> Marginalia, printed p. 210: *Antigonus kills Cleopatra*.

Demetrius, sent by his father with a fleet to free the Greeks from Cassander’s
yoke, first sailed to Athens. After he had expelled Cassander’s garrisons from
the Piraeus and Munychia, the city’s citadels, the Athenian people plunged
into such abject flattery that they heaped upon Demetrius and Antigonus not
only every human honor but even most of the honors belonging to the greater
gods. So far had the spirits of the foremost Greeks then collapsed into
servility. When military necessity pressed, Demetrius was second to no man of
his age in wisdom, diligence, and courage; but in peace he always abandoned
himself to every luxury. After gorging himself at Athens for several months on
the most ruinous pleasures, he was roused by news of new wars and immediately
lifted himself from the couches of his vices.

> Marginalia, printed p. 210: *Demetrius is adored by the Athenians*.

Cassander, fearing Demetrius’s prowess, had sent to Antigonus to ask for peace.
Antigonus would grant it on no other condition than that Cassander surrender
himself and all he possessed to Antigonus’s discretion. Terrified by this very
harsh reply, Cassander worked so effectively through letters and envoys that
he drew Ptolemy, Lysimachus, and Seleucus into alliance with him against
Antigonus. Antigonus scorned this coalition of four, and both sides prepared
arms for two full years. Seleucus advanced upon Antigonus with the others’
forces. Antigonus pitched camp with his son Demetrius at Ipsus, not far from
Ephesus. The armies were nearly equal, each containing a little fewer than one
hundred thousand brave men; the empire of Asia was staked upon the hazard of
that battle. Demetrius made a violent cavalry attack upon Seleucus’s son
Antiochus and routed him. But by pursuing his victory far too long, he left his
infantry phalanx exposed to Seleucus, who

in Demetrius’s absence attacked and routed it. Antigonus was slain, and
Seleucus gained a complete
victory together with the kingdom of all Asia.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Seleucus kills the defeated Antigonus*.

Demetrius came with a few men to the sea and from there to Athens, but the
fickle and ungrateful people refused him admission. He soon recovered his
strength, however, besieged the city, and pressed it with famine. Although he
could have taken vengeance, he granted equitable terms of peace, contrary to
their expectation and desert. He then moved against the cities of the
Peloponnese, and the Lacedaemonians, out of craven fear, enclosed their city
with walls for the first time.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Demetrius chastises ungrateful Athens*.

While Demetrius was occupied with these things, Cassander died of the louse
disease; Philip, the eldest and successor among his sons, was soon afterward
consumed by wasting sickness. His younger sons Antipater and Alexander
contended with one another for the Macedonian kingdom. Antipater became
Lysimachus’s son-in-law and cruelly killed his mother Thessalonice,
Alexander the Great’s sister, on the pretext that she favored his brother
Alexander more than himself. Alexander called Demetrius to his aid, and
Antipater called Lysimachus; the result was that Demetrius killed Alexander
and Lysimachus killed Antipater, divided Macedonia between themselves, and
exterminated Cassander’s entire family.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Cassander perishes with all his family*.

Demetrius was a man of such industry that a short time afterward he assembled
one hundred thousand infantry, ten thousand cavalry, and a fleet of five
hundred ships. Seleucus, Ptolemy, and Lysimachus all immediately began to fear
Demetrius greatly for themselves.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Pyrrhus defeats Demetrius*.

Aeacides, king of the Epirotes and Molossians and a cousin of Olympias, had
been driven from his kingdom through Cassander’s efforts. To make himself more
acceptable to the Macedonians, Demetrius married Aeacides’s daughter
Deidamia. Her brother Pyrrhus had barely escaped Cassander’s cruel hands in
infancy. As a young man he received his first training in arms with his
brother-in-law Demetrius, and in the battle at Ipsus, in which Antigonus died,
he performed a most courageous service though still a youth. When peace was
made between Ptolemy and Demetrius, Pyrrhus was among those whom Demetrius sent
to Egypt as hostages for the peace. The young man’s character and courtesy so
pleased Ptolemy’s wife Berenice that he obtained her daughter by a former
husband as his wife and troops with which to recover his father’s kingdom. He
settled the kingdom of Epirus with Neoptolemus by agreement rather than force;
and from Alexander, in return for helping him against his brother Antipater,
he obtained many Macedonian cities. Later, after killing Alexander, Demetrius
gave Pyrrhus half the Macedonian kingdom so that Pyrrhus would make no move
behind his back while he was occupied in Asia. But after Ptolemy, Seleucus,
and Lysimachus had drawn Pyrrhus into their alliance against Demetrius, that
most courageous and prudent man attacked Demetrius in Macedonia with such
force and skill that he reduced Demetrius’s formidable army to almost nothing
at once and occupied all Macedonia. Broken by Pyrrhus, Demetrius fled with the
remnants of his army into Asia. Seleucus had recently married Demetrius’s
daughter Stratonice; when Seleucus’s son Antiochus fell desperately in love
with her, his father granted her to him as his wife. Demetrius planned to
break through to the upper provinces of Asia, lately his own, and raise
another army. Prevented by the great forces of Lysimachus and Seleucus, he
performed many very courageous deeds but finally fell into Seleucus’s hands.
He was detained for three full years in custody that was magnificent but
nonetheless secure. There he abandoned himself wholly to pleasures and at
last destroyed himself with his own gluttony and luxury, while his son
Antigonus remained alive in Greece with a small army.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Demetrius dies a captive*.

Ptolemy, more than eighty years old, had handed the kingdom of Egypt to his
younger son Philadelphus by his later wife Berenice, excluding his elder son
Ceraunus. For two years before his death he took great pleasure in serving his
royal son publicly in the manner of a satrap and attendant. Alone among all
Alexander’s commanders, he died in the highest peace and honor, just as he had
far surpassed them all in humanity and equity.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Ptolemy dies as his son’s attendant*.

By force and guile Lysimachus expelled Pyrrhus from Macedonia, then crossed
into Asia with a great, well-equipped army to wrest the empire from Seleucus.
Besides other vices, the Greeks had learned polygamy and incestuous marriages
from the Persians, practices that overthrew the Greek empire no less than the
Persian. Lysimachus had fathered a very courageous young man, Agathocles, by
Amastris, and through his leadership had emerged victorious from many battles
in Asia. Upon Amastris he brought in Arsinoe, daughter of Ptolemy Lagus, as a
second wife. Moved by stepmotherly hatred against Agathocles, Arsinoe so
embittered Lysimachus against his innocent son with her love potions that he
allowed Arsinoe to remove him with poison. This flame reduced Lysimachus’s
whole house to ashes. Of his fifteen sons, each met one fate or another before
his father. Agathocles’s wife Lysandra fled with her family to Seleucus and
made bitter complaints about her parricidal father-in-law and her poisoner of
a sister. Seleucus heard her with ears open enough and prepared forces against
Lysimachus. The two sides met with all their strength. Lysimachus fell in
battle in the seventy-fourth year of his life, run through by a common soldier;
his body was not found for burial until several days later, when the dog that
guarded it revealed its location.

> Marginalia, printed p. 211: *Lysimachus is exterminated*.

Seleucus, now the only survivor of all Alexander’s commanders, was not content
with all Asia. He and his descendants—the kings of the North in Daniel—waged
perpetual wars with Ptolemy’s descendants, the kings of the South. The Jews,
who lay between the two in Syria, were treated most cruelly, now by one side,
now by the other. Seleucus himself treated the Jews kindly enough throughout
his life and gave them privileges equal to the Greeks in his

[Printed p. 212 | IA leaf 242 | access PDF 242 | OCR object 241]

cities of Antioch, Seleucia, and Apamea, many of which he founded. The Jews
also found both Ptolemy Lagus and Ptolemy Philadelphus friendly; but under
their descendants, nothing except divine omnipotence kept them from being
entirely devoured.

Seleucus, seven years beyond seventy, had long since handed most of Asia to
his son Antiochus. Desiring to die in his native Macedonia and to occupy the
kingdoms of Lysimachus and Greece without a rival, he crossed the Hellespont.
Ptolemy Ceraunus, so named after the thunderbolt for his savagery and
rashness, accompanied him. Ceraunus had fled from his father because he had
preferred the younger brother Philadelphus to his elder brother in the
kingdom. He was brother to Lysandra and Arsinoe, the wives of Lysimachus and
Agathocles. The prophets had foretold that Seleucus would die at Argos. After
crossing the Hellespont, he saw a tall altar upon the European shore. When he
asked the inhabitants about it, he learned that it was called Argos. Struck
by the name, he anxiously questioned them about the altar’s founders. While
he was examining these matters, Ceraunus rushed upon him from behind, ran him
through, and escaped to Lysimachia upon a swift horse. Seleucus was the last
of Alexander’s commanders, those lavish shedders of human blood; like almost
all the others, he succumbed to a bloody and violent death. The descendants
of Ptolemy who afterward reigned in Egypt, of Antigonus in Greece, and of
Seleucus in Asia had greatly declined from their fathers’ courage and could
not defend their realms against the incursions of barbarous peoples. Thus,
broken partly by one another’s arms and partly by those of neighboring
barbarians, they all became easy prey for the Romans.

> Marginalia, printed p. 212: *Ceraunus kills Seleucus*.
