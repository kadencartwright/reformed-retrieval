There follows a methodical treatment of the theological commonplace
concerning the eternal predestination or election of the saints.

Now that the first part of the first chapter has been completed, before we
pass to the remainder of it, it follows that we should carry out what we
promised above in its proper place. Looking back at the things which the
Apostle has proposed thus far and which we have expounded while following in
his footsteps, let us see which commonplaces of theological doctrine present
themselves here for explanation, according to the method of teaching which I
adopted from the beginning for weighty reasons. Among these, moreover, no one,
unless I am mistaken, fails to see that the first to present itself is that
difficult and excellent commonplace concerning the eternal election or
predestination of the saints. Among these spiritual benefits with which the
faithful are blessed by God the Father in Christ, the Apostle rightly sets it
first of all, as the fountain and foundation of the rest which flow from it.
Indeed, he not only proposes it but treats and explains it expressly from its
causes, effects, and adjuncts, and from other conditions which he introduces
for the sake of illuminating it. We will therefore be granted pardon, I hope,
if we also remain a little longer in explaining it more fully.

[Marginalia: Lecture XVI. Preparation.]

Before we approach this doctrine more closely, however, we have determined
to lay down at once, upon the very threshold, this single preliminary lesson,
as it were, by which an approach to what follows will be opened for us. We
will first touch on and discuss a question which some are accustomed to raise:
whether this doctrine concerning eternal predestination should be publicly
set before the faithful, both in churches and in schools, or not. For on this
matter various judgments and opinions have existed in the past and remain
among people even today. Although no one, so far as I know, has ever openly,
directly, and expressly attacked God’s predestination—and people professing
to embrace the faith and truth of Christ have not dared to break forth into
such impiety and madness—many nevertheless have assaulted and attacked it by
covered tunnels and circumlocutions. These include both those who say and
teach, or formerly taught in their own day, things which cannot stand with
its truth disclosed in Holy Scripture, and also those who, although less
seriously and as if unwittingly, do not think it should be freely preached in
the Church of God. They think it should at least be handed down only to the
learned in the school and not presented to the Christian people in a sermon.
Indeed, some, proceeding a little more leniently, concede that some mention
of election to eternal life may be made even in the Church, that is, in the
assembly of the faithful, provided that this doctrine is not pressed too
strongly. But they allow no mention at all of reprobation, which is its
opposite, and say that it must be buried in deep silence.

[Marginalia: Statement of the preliminary lesson. The corrupt basis of the
question.]

Let us therefore examine first the opinion of the latter group, namely, those
who think that this doctrine should either not be preached to the Christian
people at all or at least not preached fully and freely. It is evident that
this controversy is neither new nor recently arisen but was already debated
among otherwise orthodox people in Augustine’s age. This is amply taught by
those two letters written on this matter to Augustine by Prosper, Augustine’s
disciple, and Hilary, Bishop of Arles, and prefixed to his books *On the
Predestination and Perseverance of the Saints* in volume 7 of his works. The
occasion was this. While writing many things against Pelagius and the
Pelagians concerning the grace of Christ, and defending the truth against
their impious dogmas, Augustine had also said much about predestination in
the same books and had discussed the matter at length. Most of the brothers
from the churches of Gaul, and by no means obscure ones, were greatly offended
and disturbed by this doctrine. They asserted that this doctrine presses
God’s purpose of calling, according to which a distinction is said to have
been made between those to be chosen and those to be rejected, either before
the beginning of the world or at the very creation of the human race, so that,
as it pleased the Creator, some were created vessels of honor and others
vessels of dishonor. First, they said that it takes away from the fallen all
care to rise and gives those who stand an occasion for lethargy, since effort
in either direction is superfluous if the rejected can enter by no diligence
and the elect can fall away through no negligence. Second, they said that
debate on this matter is pointless because it cannot be comprehended. For, as
it is written, “Who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his
counselor?” Who, then, would dare to probe or search that profound abyss of
eternal predestination? Third, they said that by the same doctrine every use
of preaching, correction, admonition, and exhortation is taken away. On this
view the number of the elect is certain and fixed, unable either to increase
or decrease, so that no one can pass and migrate from one group to the other,
and consequently every labor and effort of teachers is vain. Fourth, this
doctrine also seemed to be recent in the Church, something about which the
ancient Fathers handed down either nothing or very little. Consequently,
since until the time of Augustine the Church had guarded and defended the
truth firmly enough against heretics without it, the Church could also do
without it in the future. Finally, fifth, they said that assertions of this
kind bring people back to uncertainty—

4, 5.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 97 | De Prædestinatione p. 55]

—about the divine will. This is nothing other than driving them to despair,
or at least casting them into such straits and surges of doubt that they
cannot keep a steady course, since they are uncertain about the outcome of
that immutable divine counsel upon which this doctrine nevertheless makes
their salvation depend. They therefore thought it safer to follow another
path, namely, to teach that God in his infinite goodness wills all people to
be saved, yet that not all are saved because they do not wish to be saved;
that is, they neglect and reject the grace of God offered to them.

This is the sum of what was formerly objected to Augustine. If these
objections really held, we too would certainly have to rest content with them
today, and this treatment should not be undertaken rashly and pointlessly,
with no fruit and with great injury to souls.

But first, God favoring us, we will see from Augustine himself what he
answered in the past to these phantoms and appearances of arguments and what
we still ought to answer today. Then we will prove by the strongest reasons
that this doctrine should by no means be suppressed in silence, but should be
handed down fully and completely in the schools and also preached from God’s
word prudently, fruitfully, soberly, and seasonably in the Church and in
Christian assemblies. In this way we will bring today’s discourse to an end.

[Marginalia: Transition to the answers.]

As to the first objection, its antecedent must firmly be denied, namely, that
this doctrine takes away from the fallen all care to rise and gives those who
stand an occasion for lethargy—provided that it is handed down fittingly.
Augustine confirmed this abundantly by his own example and practice, both
when he disputed very acutely against the Pelagians, the enemies of divine
grace, and when he taught the same doctrine most profitably in homilies or
sermons delivered to the Christian people. For the faithful should not
immediately be told: “Whether you do this or do not do it, you cannot change
God’s determination; and if you are elect, whatever you do, you cannot fall
away from salvation,” and other things of this kind. For this is to abuse the
best and most wholesome doctrine in the worst manner, for the destruction of
souls rather than their edification in faith and holiness. It is to apply an
otherwise beneficial medicine so improperly that it injures health instead,
like an unskilled, indeed malicious, physician. But we will afterward show,
God favoring us, that this doctrine, when proposed correctly and skillfully,
is so far from producing effects of this kind in the elect and faithful that
it produces their complete opposites. If it is an occasion for stumbling and
ruin to unbelieving and profane people, that should seem no wonder, since the
gospel itself is an aroma of death unto death to those who perish, although
to believers it is the effective power and wisdom of God for their salvation.

[Marginalia: To the first. Whether it is not taught or is taught without
skill. A comparison to be noted.]

To the second we say briefly that the antecedent must be distinguished both
with respect to the things themselves which are sought and with respect to
the manner in which someone investigates them. As to the first, it must indeed
be confessed that the abyss is impenetrable if someone wishes to search out
all the reasons of God’s judgments and the secrets of his counsels—for
example, why he passed over these or those while choosing others, or, on the
other hand, chose these while passing over and neglecting those. Here that
notable saying of Augustine in Sermon 11 *On the Words of the Apostle* has its
place: “It troubles me, you say, that this one perishes and that one is
baptized; it troubles me as a man. If you wish to hear the truth, it troubles
me too, because I am a man. But if you are a man and I am a man, let us both
hear him who says, ‘O man!’ If we are troubled precisely because we are human,
the Apostle addresses human nature itself, weak and feeble, when he says, ‘O
man! Who are you who answers back to God? Will the thing formed say to him who
formed it, Why did you make me thus?’ If a beast could speak and say to God,
‘Why did you make that one a man and me a beast?’ would you not justly grow
angry and say, ‘O beast! Who are you?’ You too are a man, but in relation to
God you are a beast,” and so forth. But if someone desires to know only what
has been divinely revealed to us on this subject in Holy Scripture, these
things will not be so obscure that they cannot be comprehended by our faith.

[Marginalia: To the second. 1. Augustine, Sermon 11 *On the Words of the
Apostle*.]

Then, as to the manner of investigation, we must always hold that we should
not immediately rush into God’s secret counsel, nor begin from the highest
step, in order to know what we should decide about ourselves and to reach the
certainty of our salvation founded upon God’s eternal predestination. Rather,
we should follow the order revealed and prescribed for us in God’s word. We
ascend from effects to causes, from things posterior and better known to us
to those prior and more hidden, until by an order of analysis we reach the
eternal fountain itself. Accordingly, when something to be done or avoided is
set before us, we should by no means inquire what God once decreed within
himself. Instead, we should turn to his will prescribed and revealed in the
word, and apply ourselves to the things which he himself has earnestly
commanded us to do and observe.

[Marginalia: 2.]

To the third, Augustine wonders with what face these people dare to assert
that this doctrine removes the use of preaching, reproofs, and exhortations,
or even gives people a cause for negligence and sloth. For Paul himself, the
teacher and Apostle of the Gentiles, than whom no one handed down this
doctrine more clearly in every place—consider Romans, 1 Timothy, and this very
epistle—nevertheless does not cease earnestly to exhort the faithful to the
pursuit of love, godliness, good works, and every exercise of holiness. Indeed,
the Savior himself, who in several places makes mention of eternal election
and its immovable firmness even in his sermons, as we will afterward show,
nevertheless did not cease effectually to exhort people to faith and
repentance. That God’s definite counsel by no means conflicts with people’s
care, diligence, and solicitude to make their calling and election sure, as
Peter speaks in his second epistle, is evident from Paul’s own practice,
particularly in the epistle to the Philippians. Although he had said that God
works in us both to will and to accomplish according to his good pleasure,
and likewise that God, who had begun a good work in them, would also complete
it so that they might be blameless on the day of the Lord Jesus Christ—and
had thus ascribed to God the whole beginning, progress, and perseverance of
good works—in the same epistle he nevertheless does not cease to exhort them
with the greatest earnestness to holiness of life and to labor at their
salvation with fear and trembling. It is also evident from the practice of
Christ the Savior. Although he calls and invites everyone to himself without
distinction, he nevertheless warns that no one will come to him unless the
Father draws him, and that God will give his people eyes to see and ears to
hear, although Christ with his voice invites everyone who has ears to hear.
Likewise, in Matthew 6, after he had admonished and taught his disciples to
pray, he adds that the heavenly Father knows very well what things they need.
Yet he does not therefore wish them to grow sluggish in the pursuit of
prayer, because those things—

[Marginalia: To the third. 2 Peter 1:10. Philippians 2:12. Matthew 6:32.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 98 | De Prædestinatione p. 56]

—which he has decreed to give us as useful and necessary for salvation, he
has decreed to give through these and those means, namely, our care, diligence,
watchfulness, and prayers. We ought to apply ourselves to these just as if we
would obtain nothing from his hand without them, because he decreed the means
themselves from eternity no less than the end. Hence it is evident how weak is
that argument drawn from the certain and fixed number of the elect and
reprobate and the immutable condition of both groups in order to conclude
that all our labor would therefore be vain, whether in exhorting, admonishing,
and preaching or in laboring and applying ourselves to good works. We must
not suppose that by human ministry and likewise by the diligence and work of
others some are newly admitted and inscribed upon the roll of the elect who
had not been inscribed in the book of life from eternity—far be such blasphemy
against God from us. Rather, it happens only that the elect are brought by
the means revealed in God’s word—the ministry of the Word and their own care,
diligence, and solicitude to live devoutly and holily—to the end appointed for
them, that is, eternal salvation. In this way they are brought to the
certainty of their own eternal election, of which they could not otherwise
be assured by any means. The rest, however—the rebellious, unbelieving,
fickle, and deserters of the truth which accords with godliness—are blinded
and hardened more and more and become inexcusable.

[Marginalia: Corollary.]

To the fourth, the consequence must immediately be denied. For if some
doctrine was handed down less fully and clearly before the rise of a heresy
which tears up and overturns that doctrine’s foundations, it does not follow
that the same doctrine afterward is no more necessary in the Church than it
was before. Nor is it strange that the Fathers older than Augustine did not
speak on this matter so copiously and accurately. We know that new heresies
springing up from time to time in the Church gave the Fathers occasion to
weigh and examine one dogma or another more diligently than before. By his
infinite goodness and marvelous wisdom, God thus not only scatters and
renders fruitless the schemes and counsels which that most crafty deceiver
contrives to disturb the Church’s peace and purity, but turns them to an
entirely opposite end: namely, to make his heavenly truth clearer and more
illustrious through this occasion of heresies. Augustine nevertheless proves
in the same book *On the Good of Perseverance*, by adducing testimonies from
Ambrose and Gregory Nazianzen for this purpose, that the earlier Fathers did
not remain entirely silent about this same truth—for how could they have been
ignorant of what was set forth so openly in Holy Scripture?

[Marginalia: To the fourth.]

Finally, to the fifth, it must firmly be denied that people are brought back
to uncertainty when they are taught from Holy Scripture how they ought and
are able to be certain and persuaded of their salvation and eternal election,
namely, by judging it not *a priori* but *a posteriori*, as we advised above.
It must also be denied that hope is diminished or broken in them, much less
that they are driven to despair, when from God’s word they are taught by this
doctrine to place all hope of salvation and confidence in God alone and not
in themselves. On the contrary, this alone is a living and true hope and
therefore never confounds or puts to shame those who rely upon it. It teaches
that our life and highest good depend not upon ourselves or our choice—alas,
a foundation far too frail and unstable, or rather no foundation at all—but
upon God’s own constant will and are founded in his eternal and immutable
decree. It is as if it were not safer to entrust our salvation to God himself—

[Marginalia: To the fifth.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 99 | De Prædestinatione p. 57]

—than sacrilegiously to claim it for our own choice and election. We have a
notable demonstration of this from the Apostle’s own practice in that often
praised passage in Romans 8. After saying that all things work together for
good to those who love God, who are called according to his purpose, he adds
a reason for this statement drawn from the fountain itself: “For,” he says,
“those whom God foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of
his Son,” and so forth. Hence, toward the end, so far is his spirit from being
broken, his hope diminished, or his confidence shaken when he ascends to this
eternal counsel of God, that on the contrary, supported by so solid and
immovable a foundation, he bursts forth into that most magnificent boasting,
full of hope and confidence. By it he deservedly defies all the forces of
hostile enemies and triumphs, is lifted up, and exults in God’s gracious love
in Christ.

[Marginalia: Romans 8:28.]

From these things it is evident not only that it is permissible, but that it
is supremely expedient, for this doctrine from God’s word to be handed down
and set before all Christians. We will further assert both its necessity and
its supreme usefulness by the following three reasons—or rather invincible
demonstrations. First, we see that God proposed and commended it through the
Prophets, through Christ himself, and through the Apostles so often, so
earnestly, so clearly and distinctly, that no one acquainted with the Holy
Writings dares deny it. What then? God willed to reveal it with such care and
diligence and entrust it to his Church in Holy Scripture. What would it be to
erase it from Scripture or enshroud it in the darkness of silence except to
accuse God himself of rashness? What else would it be but to display
sacrilegious impiety in this respect and reveal insane audacity? Certainly in
the business of religion and faith it is no less a crime to reject or cut
away some things which God revealed to us in his word and commanded us to
teach to others, and to condemn them as useless and superfluous or even
harmful and damaging, than it is to sew human fabrications onto them and
thrust them upon the Christian Church as divine oracles.

[Marginalia: Transition. Reason 1.]

Second, since the very science of understanding and judging rightly—I mean
logic—teaches us that anything is truly known and understood by us when we
grasp its cause, when, if it can be done, we draw its reasons from the fountain
itself, we must certainly judge the same concerning those saving gifts and
blessings of God in Christ. We must know from what fountain they first flowed
and streamed down to us, namely, from that eternal counsel of God which we
call predestination. Our Apostle taught us this clearly enough at the
threshold of this epistle by his own practice. When he was about to expound
in turn to the Ephesians those spiritual blessings which he had proposed in
general, in the immediately following words he disclosed and indicated God’s
eternal predestination as the fountain of them all. Indeed, this same doctrine
is not only the fountain and foundation of the entire gospel but its first
and principal part. Without knowledge of it, our mind could not rest in all
the remaining things handed down concerning God’s grace in Christ and through
Christ. For how will we rightly grasp that love of God by which he so loved
the world that he sent his only-begotten Son for this end, that whoever
believes in him should not perish but obtain eternal life—which is the power
and sum of the gospel—unless we know that God’s love flowed from that eternal
and immutable purpose by which he not only embraced the elect themselves with
his gracious—

[Marginalia: Reason 2. Confirmation. John 3:16.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 100 | De Prædestinatione p. 58 (through Lecture XVI close)]

—benevolence and decreed to take them up into life and glory, but also
foreknew Christ himself from eternity as the Redeemer and Head of the elect,
as Peter teaches us in his first epistle? Thus, if this firm and immovable
foundation were removed, neither faith, hope, calling, adoption,
justification, regeneration, glory and the future life itself, nor, finally,
any part of our salvation would have anything certain and solid upon which to
rest firmly. Who can deny that it is even the chief part of that joyful
announcement of eternal salvation, which Christ indicates to his Apostles in
Luke 10:20: “Rather rejoice that your names are written in heaven”? Likewise,
in Mark 13 he says that the Lord shortened those days for the sake of the
elect whom he chose. In Matthew 24 he indicates that it is impossible for the
elect to be seduced by false prophets and false Christs, even by the lying
signs and wonders which they will perform to seduce the rest. Likewise, in
Mark 13:27 he says that he will send his angels and gather his elect from the
four winds, from the end of the earth to the end of heaven. Likewise, the
Apostle teaches us this in Romans 8 when weaving and explaining that golden
chain whose links follow one another in this order: foreknowledge,
predestination, calling, justification, glorification. In the following
chapter he says that God bore with great patience the vessels of wrath fitted
for destruction, so that he might make known the riches of his glory toward
the vessels of mercy which he prepared for glory. Likewise, in chapter 11 he
says that a remnant was made according to gracious election, and afterward
that the elect obtained righteousness by faith while the rest were hardened.
Likewise, in chapter 9 he says that before those twins were born or had done
anything good or evil, they were told that the elder would serve the younger,
so that God’s purpose according to election might stand firm, not from works
but from him who calls. Likewise, in 1 Corinthians 2 he says that he preaches
to the perfect that wisdom of God hidden in a mystery, which God had ordained
or predestined before all ages for our glory. Likewise, in 2 Timothy 1 he says
that God saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our
works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was indeed given to
us in Christ before the ages began and has now been revealed through the
appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ. Likewise, in the following chapter he
says that the firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: “The Lord knows
those who are his.” Likewise, in Titus 1 he styles himself an Apostle of Jesus
Christ according to the faith of God’s elect. Likewise, in Philippians 4 he
says that the names of his colleagues and fellow workers whom he commends to
the Philippians are inscribed in the book of life. Likewise, Revelation 13
says that all the other inhabitants of the earth whose names are not written
in the book of life of the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world will
worship that blasphemous Beast. For in this way the book of life is taken for
eternal election, from the very expression of the Prophets, as is evident from
that notable passage in Exodus 32 and Psalm 69: “Let them be blotted out,” he
says, “from the book of life, and not be written together with the righteous.”
Finally, we have the same thing in this most illustrious passage addressed to
the Ephesians, where that entire doctrine is clearly declared from its causes,
effects, and adjuncts. Thus, either to wish to call it into doubt or to remove
its use from the Church is nothing other than actually to deny God and his
Christ and, if not to draw down the very sun of the gospel from the heaven of
the Scriptures, at least to cover it with clouds and darkness so that it
cannot shine upon us for salvation and solid joy.

[Marginalia: 1 Peter 1:20. Luke 10:20. Mark 13:20. Matthew 24:24. Mark 13:27.
Romans 8:30; 9:23; 11:5, 7; 9:11–12. 1 Corinthians 2:7. 2 Timothy 1:9; 2:19.
Titus 1:1. Philippians 4:3. Revelation 13:8. Exodus 32:32. Psalm 69:29.
Ephesians 1:4.]

Third, this doctrine has many uses which are most necessary for us. Whoever
thinks that this most weighty commonplace concerning eternal predestination
should either be suppressed entirely or confined within the walls of the
schools attempts to deprive God’s Church of them. For from it arises, first
of all, true and solid confidence concerning the certainty of our salvation,
which is placed in God and depends upon God alone; a firm defense and
fortification against the distrust of our flesh and the whirlpool of despair;
and for afflicted consciences a powerful and invincible ground—indeed, the
foundation—of sincere joy and solid consolation both in life and in death.
Hence above in Luke 10 the Lord says to his faithful disciples, “Rejoice that
your names are written in heaven.” Likewise in Luke 12 he says, “Do not fear,
little flock, because it pleased your Father to give you that kingdom.” See
from where the Lord supplies his faithful flock with a ground of confidence:
namely, from that good pleasure of the heavenly Father according to which he
prepared that kingdom for the elect from eternity. Likewise in John 6: “All
that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will by no
means cast out.” And in chapter 10: “My sheep hear my voice, and I know them,
and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, nor
will anyone snatch them from my hand.” Finally, in this way Paul, having laid
this foundation beneath him in Romans 8, exults holily in this confidence of
his salvation against every power of darkness and the gates of hell, as was
observed above.

[Marginalia: Reason 3. Luke 10:20; 12:32. John 6:37; 10:28. Romans 8, toward
the end.]

From this arises, in the second place, a most necessary caution and defense
against pride and carnal security, when we are taught to ascribe all the glory
of our salvation to God alone as its sole author. He chose us freely from his
mere benevolence, regarding nothing that was in us and finding no cause of
such grace in us or anywhere outside himself. Our Savior teaches us this in
Matthew 11 when he gives thanks to his Father that he hid those saving
mysteries of his kingdom from the wise and intelligent and revealed them to
infants, from his mere and gracious good pleasure. Thus the Apostle in 1
Corinthians 1 teaches that God chose the foolish, weak, and ignoble things of
the world to confound and put to shame the wise, strong, and illustrious, so
that no flesh may glory in his sight, but, as it is written, “Let the one who
glories glory in the Lord.” He teaches the same in this very passage in
Ephesians when he says that we were chosen and predestined by him to life,
freely, from his mere benevolence, for the praise of his glorious grace.

[Marginalia: 2. Matthew 11:25. 1 Corinthians 1:31. Ephesians 1:6.]

From this arises, in the third place, the strongest arguments for convicting
and routing the Pelagians and all the enemies of divine grace, whether direct
or indirect, and the sacrilegious thieves of God’s glory, whether manifest
and open or covered and hidden. They will never be able to resist these
arguments, just as Father Augustine happily and skillfully applied this
doctrine to this use in his own age. From it there is also abundant material
for confounding the Arians themselves and stopping their mouths, since Christ
is said to have chosen us to eternal life in John 13 and to have known whom he
chose. For when he openly denies that he speaks about all those whom he was
nevertheless addressing, because he knew whom he had chosen, although he had
chosen all of them into the number of his disciples, it is certainly evident
that he speaks about another election: that of those whom he had taken up to
eternal life from eternity together with the Father. So too in Mark 13, where
he is said to be about to send his angels to gather his elect from the four
winds, and so forth. It follows that he is the eternal God with the Father,
who chose the elect from eternity together with himself and the Holy Spirit.

[Marginalia: 3, 4. John 13:18; 15:16. Mark 13:27.]

From this the magnitude of divine love and grace is truly assessed by us. We
will never otherwise measure it—not only in keeping with its immensity, but
even according to our duty—unless we consider that we were freely chosen and
loved by him in Christ even before this world was founded. From an inward
sense of that immeasurable love, a constant and ardent love of our God and a
zeal for his glory is kindled in our hearts which either consumes and drives
out every other affection or at least purifies, refines, and sanctifies it.

[Marginalia: 5.]

From this also arises the strongest bond of brotherly love, when we consider
that all true believers were destined from eternity, together with us, to the
same life and glory and to fellowship in the same inheritance. They are
therefore united to us by a closer bond than all those things which never
extend beyond the limits of this life. Thus we cannot look upon and embrace
true believers, in whom we see living testimonies of their election, in any
other way than as citizens who will be with us eternally in the same heavenly
city.

[Marginalia: 6.]

From this the pursuit of godliness, love, and every good work is marvelously
sharpened and stirred up in true believers. They know and have learned from
God’s word—indeed, have perceived and proved by experience itself—that the
strongest, safest, and only way set before them to confirm their election and
calling and make it assured in their mind is to apply themselves
wholeheartedly to holiness and repentance. Without doubt, everyone touched by
a serious concern for his salvation regards this as his chief care and most
intently sets this aim before himself from the glory of God; he can count
nothing more important than certainty of his election.

[Marginalia: 7.]

Finally, from this arises endurance and patience under the cross whenever
believers look up to that immovable foundation which no affliction, no
temptation, and no force, from wherever it may finally rush upon them, can
shake or weaken. The Apostle instructs us by his own example in Romans 8.
Behold how great a necessity, how many uses, and how much fruit flow to us
from a right understanding of this doctrine, whose treatment it now follows
that we should enter, after we briefly meet the following thought of some.

[Marginalia: 8. Romans 8:29. Anticipatory transition.]

Compelled by the great force of the preceding arguments, they will perhaps
concede that the doctrine of election will be both useful and necessary for
us. Yet, they say, nothing has therefore been concluded from all these things
in favor of its opposite, reprobation, which would prevent us from deciding

enclosure, indeed, outside the bounds of sacred theology.

But these people certainly do not, first, weigh carefully enough what they
say, nor do they see that they posit things which cannot stand together and
even involve a tacit contradiction. They contend that the one can be
separated from the other, or that the one can be established without the
other, indeed, taught and distinctly preached. Yet what election, I ask, can
there be—what setting apart or taking up of some to eternal life—unless the
rest are passed over and neglected? What else is that, I ask, but their
reprobation? Second, neither has the Holy Spirit kept it silent from us in the
Holy Scriptures of both Testaments. For example, the Apostle cites Isaiah 10
in Romans 9: “Even if the number of the children of Israel should be like the
sand of the sea, only a remnant will be saved.” In Amos 3 the Lord compares
those who were to be saved from the Israelite Samaritans to pieces of an
animal torn apart by a lion—such as legs or an ear—which the shepherd would
snatch from its mouth: “Thus,” he says, “the Israelites will be snatched away
who sit in Samaria on the side of a bed and on the corner of a couch.” What is
this but for the rest of the multitude to be abandoned in its destruction?
Likewise, in the same chapter of Romans, after the Apostle inferred,
“Therefore he has mercy on whom he wills,” he immediately adds the opposite
member, “but whom he wills he hardens.” He afterward calls the reprobate
“vessels of wrath fitted for destruction.” In his first epistle, Peter says
that those who stumble over that cornerstone through unbelief and obstinacy
were appointed or established for this very thing. Finally, the Apostle Jude
says that those ungodly people had long ago been designated for the
condemnation which they incur. Likewise, Peter says in his second epistle that
the condemnation of seducers and false teachers has not been idle for a long
time and that their destruction does not slumber. Not to mention at present
that truly notable passage in Isaiah 30, in which the Prophet describes the
place long ago appointed for the reprobate themselves: “For Topheth,” he says,
that is, the place of destruction, “was prepared long ago; it has also been
established for the king. Jehovah has made it very deep and broad; its pyre
has much fire and wood, and Jehovah’s breath kindles it like a torrent of
brimstone.”

And finally, I ask, what else is the doctrine of reprobation than an
illustration of the magnitude of the grace of election? That grace can never
shine into our minds with the force it ought to have unless we see both from
how great a condemnation it delivered us and how many thousands it left
eternally immersed in that same condemnation by its just judgment. God could
have drawn them out and saved them as readily as us, leaving us there, if he
had willed. Meanwhile, however, it seemed good to his immense goodness and
gracious mercy toward us to embrace us graciously while passing them over,
and to give us to his Christ to be saved and redeemed. Hence we are compelled
to be utterly astonished at the magnitude of this grace and to be caught up
and lifted beyond ourselves into the highest admiration, love, and praise of
our glorious God. To him be praise, honor, and blessing forever. Amen.
