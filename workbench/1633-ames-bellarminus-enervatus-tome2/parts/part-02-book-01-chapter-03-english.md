CHAPTER III. On the nature of a council’s judgment.

The third question is whether in a council the bishops are judges of such a
kind that their sentence must be followed because they themselves have so
determined, just as the sentence of a magistrate is followed in civil cases;
or whether there is merely a certain inquiry and a ministerial and limited
pronouncement of a sentence, so that a council’s decree has only as much force
as its reasoning has. The former is asserted by the Pontificians; the latter
by us.

Bellarmine’s arguments.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. Deuteronomy 17. *Whoever does not obey their sentences is to be
condemned to death.*

Protestants. I. Earlier, more than once—*On the Word of God*, book 4, chapter
4; *On the Roman Pontiff*, book 1, chapter 1; and elsewhere—this passage was
wrested by Bellarmine to refer to the Roman Pontiff. He is therefore
inconsistent with himself when he refers it here to a council. II. The passage
does not concern—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n232 | printed p. 15]

—a general council gathered exceptionally as occasion arises. III. It concerns
particular questions there, including matters of fact and not only of law. Yet
in such matters the Pontificians themselves grant that a council and the
Pontiff can err, and consequently that his sentence does not bind absolutely.
IV. A definite passage is specified there according to which judgment was to
be given: *and by this it is intimated* (says Cajetan) *that in ambiguities of
this sort God was to be consulted, and that one was to proceed according to
God’s counsel given there.* No such passage or consultations of God are now
available anywhere. V. *That condition must be weighed* (Cajetan also adds),
*whatever they teach you according to the law; for unless it agrees with the
utterance of the divine law, both what they teach and what they judge is not
supported by divine authority.* VI. That law was judicial. VII. By the equity
of that law, only disobedience arising from pride is condemned, not that which
arises from conscience toward God.

2. Bellarmine. Matthew 18. *If he does not hear the Church, let him be to you
as a heathen and a publican.*

Protestants. I. The Church must be heard when, from the word of God, it rebukes
a sinner and calls him to repentance; but it must not be heard when it rules
imperiously about matters of faith with no word of God going before. II. From
this passage—if Bellarmine’s interpretation is admitted—the magisterial power
of every particular church could be concluded. For the gathering under
discussion there is, most properly, one of that sort, not a general council to
be celebrated three hundred years later.

3. Bellarmine. Acts 15 and 16. *Paul commanded the churches to keep the decrees
of the Apostles. Concerning them it should be observed: 1. that they were not
defined from the Scriptures; 2. that they were not permitted to be examined
by the disciples; 3. that they bound the conscience as a true law.*

Protestants. I. The Apostles’ authority was too great for ordinary bishops to
be equated with them, as though decrees made by these men and by the Apostles
stood on equal grounds. II. It is false that those decrees were made
by the votes of men without the Scriptures. For the decrees had two principal
foundations. First, many of the Gentiles had believed and had received from
God a singular testimony of grace without observing the law of Moses; Peter
confirms this from experience and James from the Prophets. Second, Moses was
still preached town by town in those places, and many people, not evil men,
were moved by zeal for the law and were not to be rashly offended, as was
sufficiently evident from the Scriptures. From the former foundation it was
concluded that the yoke of the law was by no means to be imposed upon the
Gentiles. From the latter it was concluded that they should nevertheless
abstain from things which, though lawful in themselves, offended weak brothers
in their common fellowship. Both conclusions, however, came from the
Scriptures. III. They were divine com—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n233 | printed p. 16]

—mands necessarily connected with charity, Romans 14 and 15, and therefore
they could be commanded without qualification. IV. The Apostle himself
expressly testifies that the consciences of believers were bound regarding
the foods about which the decree was made on account of scandal, not on
account of the things themselves or of the apostolic decree, 1 Corinthians
10:27, 29.

4. Bellarmine. *All councils pronounce anathema upon those who do not obey.
They call their decrees canons, or ecclesiastical laws. Bishops subscribe when
rendering a decision. In the Council of Chalcedon, those who refused to
acquiesce were regarded as heretics.*

Protestants. I. In articles of fundamental doctrine, the ancients pronounced
anathema upon those who did not obey, not by their own authority but by the
authority of God and Scripture. In other matters they either did not act in
this way or allowed something human to enter into it. II. Among ancient
ecclesiastical writers, *canon*—that is, direction or ordering—means something
less than *law*. Those words denote direction, caution, and suitable ordering,
not coercion and absolute power. III. Bishops can render a decision as
ministers of God’s sentence, but not by their own proper power. IV. The things
related from the Council of Chalcedon are neither wholly true nor, even if
they were entirely true, would they prove any point of right, but only a point
of fact. In fact, those men were regarded as heretics because they opposed
declared truth, not because they denied the authority of those declaring it.

5. Bellarmine. *If business were not to be conducted in councils by voting, it
would be wrongly done that bishops alone should give their judgment. It would
also be pointless to summon bishops who were unlearned and simple, such as
some who were at the Council of Nicaea. Rufinus, book 1, chapter 3.*

Protestants. I. It was shown earlier that the former practice is indeed
wrongly done. II. Unlearned and simple bishops—such as many among the Roman
cardinals themselves and among those who now occupy the principal Pontifician
sees—are also summoned to councils in vain. But there were no such men at the
Council of Nicaea, namely, men unlearned and incapable in matters pertaining
to the truth of religion. Rufinus, *History*, book 1, chapter 3, does not say
that some simple bishops were there. Rather, he says of various bishops that
they were *men not inconsiderably learned even in the dialectical art*. Of
only one man (whom he calls *a confessor and elder*) he affirms that he was *a
man of a most simple nature, knowing nothing except Jesus Christ and him
crucified, who had converted a distinguished philosopher to the faith*.
Simple bishops of this kind are not summoned in vain to the holy inquiry into
that truth which is customarily revealed to the humble while it is hidden
from Angelic, Seraphic, Subtle, and Irrefragable Doctors.
