CHAPTER VIII.

VERSE 1. Observe my command, the king's command, but with regard for the oath
of God.

I wish to set forth an example of this wisdom, as I did before in divine
worship, but now in the mutual duties of human beings toward one another. In
these matters, this is the first point about which I wish to admonish you:
carefully observe the king's command and obey him promptly, but with this
precaution, that you do not depart even a nail's breadth from the obedience
owed to God, to which you have bound yourself by oath.

VERSE 2. Do not depart from his presence in agitation, nor persist in an evil
matter, for he will do whatever he wishes.

Therefore take care that you do not indulge the confused affections and
agitations of your mind and so decline from obedience to the king. But if,
through human frailty, you have suffered some lapse or departed even in
thought from the duty you owe him, the next step is to withdraw yourself from
it promptly, lest you persist in the defection you have begun. This is
especially so since you know that he is armed with great power
and will accomplish whatever he wishes, and that once his anger is stirred
against you, he will often punish you more severely than the measure of your
fault warrants, passing beyond laws and equity.

VERSE 3. Wherever the king's word is, there is dominion; who may say to him,
“What are you doing?”

For he is furnished with such great power and forces that wherever his
command reaches, even if the place is far distant from him, it is no sooner
said than done. Nor is there anyone who may call him to account and compel
him to give a reason for what he has done.

VERSE 4. The one who observes the command does not experience an evil matter;
the mind of the wise person knows the time and the method.

The person who carefully observes the king's command, even if he receives no
reward for his merit, will at least sail into port unharmed by danger. For
this purpose, a mind ready to obey is needed, but also a wise mind instructed
in the knowledge of the divine will, so that it may apply judgment and choose
the suitable and opportune time for conducting affairs.

VERSE 5. For every purpose has its time and method, but a person's evil
presses heavily upon him.

This is no wonder, since in everything we undertake and institute, it is
worthwhile to take account of the time when something is said or done and to
apply judgment. This is especially so because a person is exposed to many
great evils that must overwhelm him unless we exercise judgment and choice,
taking from God's Word an account of time, place, and persons.

VERSE 6. Since he does not know what will happen, who may tell him how it will
happen?

These evils bear down and press all the more heavily because they come upon
the ungodly suddenly and without warning. They are not permitted to learn
when those evils will invade them. For, once one has departed from God, who
alone foretells future things, who is there who knows how to tell him what is
going to happen?

Yet amid such a great variety of evils to which a person is exposed, although
nothing certain can be precisely defined concerning most of them, death—the
greatest of all evils, if you consider the ungodly—hangs over every single
person.

VERSE 7. No person rules over his own spirit so as to restrain it, nor is
there dominion in the day of death, nor any weapon in that battle; finally,
deliberation itself does not deliver the one who devises it.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA53 | printed pp. 53–54]

For no one, whatever authority or strength he possesses, can prevent his
spirit from being breathed out at its appointed time. Death, which holds
dominion over everyone, is subdued by no one. Nor in this struggle with death
will you find, however much you search for weapons, any with which to repel
it when it attacks, not even when you employ the greatest exertion—or rather,
combat—to subjugate it.

VERSE 8. In this whole matter I saw, when I applied my mind to every work done
under the Sun, that there is sometimes a time when one person rules over
another to his own harm.

I understood everything I am about to say while directing all the powers and
sinews of my mind toward comprehending the things done under the Sun. I
understood that there are times when human beings rule over other human
beings, not only to the harm of their subjects but also—which they least of
all suppose—to their own destruction.

VERSE 9. For I observed the ungodly who had ruled in this manner being buried
after they died and departed from the holy place, and being given over to
oblivion in the city where they had acted so. This also is vanity.

It is indeed true that I saw these tyrants, who had exercised their rule so
harshly, obtain burial made splendid and celebrated with great pomp and
display, after death compelled them to depart from the holy and august office
of magistracy. Yet by this splendid funeral they could not vindicate their
name from oblivion or the shame of infamy in the city where they had
practiced injustice. Therefore I concluded in my mind that this tyranny,
however much it bore the appearance of happiness, was utterly vain.

VERSE 10. Because no decree—the swift recompense of that evil—is carried out,
the heart of human beings therefore burns within them to do evil.

I certainly would not deny that when the punishment commanded by God in the
law is deferred for a long time, whether by human negligence or by God's
patience, others grow bolder in sinning, while human hearts are, as it were,
stuffed with wickedness by this delay.

VERSE 11. Although God should prolong the days of a sinner who does evil a
hundred times, nevertheless I also know that it will be well with those who
fear God, who fear before his face.

Indeed, I hold this as certain and established: although the slave and
bondservant of sin repeats the same crime a hundred times, and although God
even prolongs the life that he abuses to afflict the godly, I hold it, I say,
as certain and established that it will be well and blessed with those who
fear God—that is, those whom the mere sight of God leads to flee evil and
pursue good.

VERSE 12. But it will not be well with the ungodly, nor will he prolong his
days, which are like a shadow—the person who does not fear before the face of
God himself.

Conversely, I regard it as equally certain that it will not be well with
those ungodly people who, neither fearing God nor abstaining from evils
through fear of him, afflict the godly, however exceedingly blessed they may
be judged because of their present abundance, both by themselves and by
others whose judgment is insufficiently prudent.

VERSE 13. There is a vanity done upon the earth: there are righteous people
to whom it happens according to the work of the ungodly, and there are
ungodly people to whom it happens according to the work of the righteous. I
said that this also is vanity.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA55 | printed pp. 55–56 | Chapter IX begins on p. 55]

Just as the Holy Spirit observes and identifies the things that are vain in
themselves in reality and truth, so corrupted human reason invents its own
vanities, which are not truly vanities. Among these inventions is the claim
that this happens rather often on earth: evils and disadvantages justly and
deservedly owed to the ungodly befall righteous and godly people, while,
conversely, the goods and advantages due to righteous and godly people are
bestowed upon the ungodly. I declared that this administration, fashioned by
the human brain, is itself utterly vain.

VERSE 14. Therefore I had praised gladness: there is no good for a person
under the Sun except to eat, drink, and rejoice, for this remains with him
from his labor during the days of his life that God gives him under the Sun.

With the same sense of corrupted reason, from a badly laid foundation, I
wove an even worse conclusion with myself and in my mind. I approved that
kind of life which, with every sensation of pain removed far away, would be
most closely joined with the pleasure of both mind and body. For I judged
that, so long as a person draws the breath of this life, nothing is beneficial
to him except to devote himself completely to food, drink, play, every kind
of bodily luxury, relaxation of mind, and delights, and to consume in them
this fleeting life that will soon pass away. For I judged this the sole means
by which he may console and sustain himself against the labors and hardships
of this life.

CHAPTER IX.

VERSE 1. But when I applied my mind to knowing wisdom and perceiving the very
business done upon the earth, even if someone receives no sleep for his eyes
by day or night—

Having completed the treatment of Spirit and flesh concerning the rule and
government of human beings over other human beings, and having examined and
discussed the matter from both sides, it follows that we transfer our
discourse to that rule and government in which God holds the rudder and
exercises dominion. The subject around which each form of government turns is
not always different, though the rationale and mode of governing are
different in each. In treating it, according to my usual practice, I shall
first bring forward what the Holy Spirit dictated to me.
Therefore, when I directed all the sinews of my mind and thought toward
knowing this supremely wise administration and considering the affairs God
administers on earth—which are certainly so hidden and obscure that no one
engaged in investigating them by day and night attains them—

VERSE 2. I observed the whole work of God, and that a person cannot attain
the work done under the Sun. However laboriously a person seeks, he will not
attain it; indeed, even if the consummately wise person thinks he knows, he
nevertheless cannot attain it.

I discovered that all God's works are of such a kind that no human being can
find them by seeking. I mean those works set here upon earth, as it
were, before our feet and meeting everyone's eyes. This remains so even when
someone joins laborious work and singular diligence to long continuance in
time. I add that not even the person whose sharpness of intellect has been
joined by the light of divine truth in the Word can accurately comprehend
the deeply hidden knowledge in God's works, not even if he resolves in his
mind that he can attain it through time and study by lamplight.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA57 | printed pp. 57–58]

VERSE 3. For I placed all this in my mind in order to explain it all: the
righteous and the wise, and their deeds, are in the hand of God himself.
Human beings use neither the thing loved nor the thing hated; neither is laid
open to them.

Just as I searched with all my strength for a right understanding and
judgment concerning God's administration of the world, so I applied my mind
and thought completely to the judgment of the flesh, which has many deeply
hidden turns and recesses, so that after the matter had first become known
and understood by me, I might explain it to others. The subject around which
the flesh's understanding and opinion of God's administration turns is,
first, righteous and unrighteous people together, the wise and the foolish,
second, their deeds. Concerning these, I wish to infer this in passing, so as
to arm the minds of the godly more effectively against the flesh's poisonous
darts, which will be set forth afterward: both the people and their deeds do
not rest within their own power but are governed by God in such a way that,
whatever they determine and whatever effort and skill they apply, what God
wills shall nevertheless be accomplished. Human beings do not know whether
they will continue to pursue with the same love or hatred the things they
deeply love or hate; indeed, they are ignorant of the outcomes even of those
things that appear most of all to fall under their own decision and power.

VERSE 4. The same outcome happens to all these people, just as to all the
rest: to the righteous and the ungodly, to the good or pure and the impure,
to the one who sacrifices as to the one who does not sacrifice, to the good
as to the sinner, and to the one who swears as to the one who reveres an
oath.

Now, to turn to an explanation of the flesh's understanding of God's
administration: from the flesh's perspective, I declare that all the
righteous and wise, like everyone else, have the same chance and fortuitous
outcome. To speak at greater length, the righteous and the ungodly have the
same chance, as do the person whose heart is good or pure and the one whose
heart is defiled, the person who worships God according to the prescribed
rite and the person who spends neither effort nor expense in sacred worship,
the upright person and the slave and bondservant of sin. The same things
happen to the person accustomed to oaths, who pollutes God's most holy name,
as to the man who, restrained by reverence for God, abhors rash oaths.

VERSE 5. This is the worst thing in all that is done under the Sun: the same
outcome belongs to everyone. Indeed, the heart of the children of human
beings is filled with evil, and every madness resides in their heart as long
as they live; afterward they likewise go to the dead.

In all the things done under the circuit of heaven, the worst matter is that
one and the same fortuitous outcome belongs to everyone, the righteous and
the unrighteous alike. This is not only so for the unrighteous, but even for
those whose heart is filled with every kind of crime and disgrace, not for a
few months or years, but throughout the whole course of their life. Finally,
it is so because the unrighteous are equated with the righteous not only in
this life but also in death.

VERSE 6. For whichever is to be chosen, there is confidence throughout this
life; indeed, a living dog is better than a dead lion.

It can be doubted whether the condition of the dead or the living should be
chosen because of the various changes and misfortunes of this life. But for
those who weigh the matter maturely, the condition of the living is more to
be desired. For although they struggle with various misfortunes at present,
so long as they live they can cherish and console themselves with the hope
that their affairs will change for the better. From this arose the common
proverb worn smooth in conversation: the basest of all animals, a dog, if
only it is alive, is to be preferred to a lion, the noblest beast and, as it
were, king of all animals, if it is dead.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA59 | printed pp. 59–60]

VERSE 7. For the living know that they will die, but the dead know nothing,
nor do they have any further reward, since their memory has been given over
to oblivion.

For the living, even if they are immersed in complete ignorance of
everything—which kind of life is the shortest route to enjoying pleasures—
nevertheless know that they will die. But the dead, every sensation having
been removed, know absolutely nothing. So far are they from bearing the
reward of their works that their memory perishes together with them.

VERSE 8. Both the thing they loved and the thing they hated, and also the
thing they desired, have now perished for them, and they no longer have any
part ever again in anything done under the Sun.

Since after this life they are completely without part in every action, not
even this remains to them: that they may either embrace something or someone
dearly, pursue it in hatred, or desire it. Their understanding, will, and
affections have alike perished through the intervention of death. Finally,
to comprehend everything in one word, they share nothing with the things
done or customarily done upon earth. It is therefore readily evident that the
condition of those who conduct their lives is preferable, since the power to
do all these things has been granted to them.

VERSE 9. Come now, eat your food with gladness and drink your wine with a
cheerful heart whenever God favorably regards your deeds.

Since these things are so, come then, compose yourself to live cheerfully and
according to your heart's desire. Take your food joyfully and drink your wine
abundantly with an exultant heart. Do this not only at intervals and scattered
times of the year, as a meeting with friends, relatives, or guests may prompt
you, but as long as God, attending you with his gift of good things, kindly
supplies you with the means to make these expenditures.

VERSE 10. Let your garments be bright at all times, and let no ointment poured
upon your head be lacking.

So that you may indulge your appetite all the more, see to it that you lay
aside and cast away your cheaper clothes and dress yourself every day in
white and splendid garments; continually drench your whole body with costly
ointments and the most fragrant spices.

VERSE 11. Enjoy life with the wife you love throughout all the days of your
utterly vain life, the wife God gave you under the Sun, throughout all your
utterly vain days, since this is your portion in this life and in the labor
with which you labor under the Sun.

As long as you draw breath, despising the use of prayers and fasting, you
will continually delight yourself with your wife. You will look to nothing
beyond the gratification of your own heart—not even to the procreation of
children for the stability of the commonwealth or the increase of the
Church. You will pursue all these pleasures the more greedily and ardently
because the momentary brevity of the present life prevents you from doing so
for long, especially since this alone is the fruit perceived from all the
labor with which you weary yourself. Thus, unless you seize this life's
pleasure by throwing both hands around it, you will have wasted all your work
and oil.

VERSE 12. Whatever your hand attains to do, do according to your ability, for
there is no work, reasoning, knowledge, or wisdom in the grave to which you
are going.

Time would fail me if I enumerated every delight and pleasure of the flesh in
each of their kinds. Therefore, to comprehend everything briefly: whatever
you attain by any method, or by skill, intellect, strength, and industry, for
filling mind and body together with the sweetest delight, you will procure
to the best of your ability. For in the grave toward which you journey,
completing some part of the way every day, there is nothing for you to do,
especially since every use of reason, knowledge, and wisdom has perished
there.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA61 | printed pp. 61–62]

VERSE 13. Turning again, I observed under the Sun that the race does not
belong to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor food to the wise, nor
riches to the prudent, nor favor to the knowledgeable, but time and chance
happen to them all.

I turned from human beings to the outcome of actions and sufferings. Casting
the eyes of my mind here and there in every direction, I discovered these
things in human actions. Speed and swiftness in running a race, however
suitable, do not always attain the prize. Nor does the person better furnished
with forces and military skill carry off victory in battle. Wise people are
found, not altogether rarely, to whom their wisdom does not supply the means
to procure food and clothing. People powerful in prudence and practical
experience lack the riches they seek and for whose acquisition they usually
enter upon a good, short path. People endowed with knowledge of many and
various things do not always gain the favor of those to whom their manifold
knowledge could be useful. The same may be affirmed concerning the other
virtues of mind and body suited to conducting this life comfortably.
Conversely, I have also learned by experience that people bare and empty of
virtues for living this life prosperously nevertheless obtain things for
whose attainment they are judged, by every likelihood, unfit and unsuitable.
Therefore, from the perspective of Epicureans and atheists, I conclude that
time exercises dominion in these things, and that it makes a very great
difference on which day—Jupiter's, Mercury's, or another planet's—you
undertake something, and that individual affairs revolve and turn rashly by
chance and fortune. (Here sailors who imagine the Sabbath day to be fated for
destruction must be censured.)

VERSE 14. A person also does not know his own time, just as fish do not know
when they are caught in an evil net and birds do not know when they are
caught in a snare; in the same way, the children of human beings are ensnared
in an evil time when it falls suddenly upon them.

Concerning human sufferings, it is indeed true that for the most part people
do not know either what will happen to them or when it will happen. From this
it comes into the minds of Epicureans and atheists to determine that human
beings are treated no differently from brute fish and little birds. For just
as fish swallow the deadly hook as bait and birds, entangled by bird-catchers'
traps and allurements, are suddenly captured, so human beings are suddenly
and unexpectedly overwhelmed by evils, both death and other miseries in this
life. And indeed, when these evils fall unexpectedly upon Epicureans
themselves and other ungodly people, they crush and batter them. But when
godly people have meditated upon these evils long beforehand, they not only
do not overwhelm them, but do not even leave them astonished, because of
their preceding reflection upon these things.

VERSE 15. I also observed this wisdom under the Sun, and it is great in my
judgment.

To make it more clearly evident that everything turns rashly and by chance,
and that the things under the Sun are not governed by a certain, constant
rationale and direction, I wish to transfer my discourse to human counsels.
In addition to what was said above about human beings and their deeds, I also
observed that in common life there is a certain experience and wisdom in the
administration of matters pertaining to this life. Although a good portion
of humanity thinks little of this experience, in my judgment and that of all
who judge rightly it is to be highly esteemed.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA63 | printed pp. 63–64]

VERSE 16. There was a certain small city in which there were few people, when
a great king came against it, surrounded it, and built great fortifications
against it.

I shall bring forward one example in place of many. The city was narrow and
not difficult to capture, and the men suited to its defense were few in
number. A very august king attacked it, furnished with so numerous an army
that he surrounded it on every side. He also raised immense siegeworks from
which he could hurl missiles and other engines of war for the destruction of
its citizens.

VERSE 17. There was present in it a poor wise man who delivered that city by
his wisdom; yet no one remembered that poor man.

And, as sometimes happens, there was present a wise man, though poor and of
slender means. Although he delivered the city from siege by his wisdom,
nevertheless, because he was poor, he was universally given over to oblivion,
as though no merit of his in saving the city had existed. I confess that I
entirely fail to see how this can be consistent with the provident government
of God which our theologians profess.

VERSE 18. But I say that wisdom is better than strength, although the wisdom
of that poor man is despised and his words are not heard.

But looking more closely and deeply at the little city's deliverance through
the poor man's counsel and wisdom, I resolved completely in my mind that
wisdom has more power than bodily strength to preserve or recover the safety
of a commonwealth. I will never be driven from this judgment by seeing the
wisdom of a poor and humble person held in contempt and scarcely any ear
given to his sound counsels.

VERSE 19. The words of quiet wise people are to be heard rather than the
shouting of a ruler among his fools.

For although the speech of wise people is quiet and humble, as though
proceeding from a person of slender means, it is nevertheless valued more
highly by all who are not thoughtless than the loud voices of foolish rulers,
fortified by great authority, and of all who adhere to them.

VERSE 20. Wisdom is better than weapons of war, but one person who goes
astray destroys much good.

From the same deliverance of that little city, I further draw this
conclusion: wisdom is more excellent than weapons and the equipment of war.
And the more excellently the effects of wisdom shine, the more the outcome of
everything is proved fortuitous and rash, since we see all its splendid
achievements not only buried in oblivion, as was said above, but, even when
they are valued for a time, crushed by deliberate forgetfulness because of a
single and very slight error, as though because of a fall.

VERSE 21. As one dead fly makes the perfumer's ointment belch out a stench,
so a little foolishness makes foul a man precious in name for wisdom and
glory.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA65 | printed pp. 65–66 | ends on p. 65 before Chapter X on p. 66]

For just as a single base and abject little creature—a fly—that falls into
the perfumer's ointment, which it eagerly seeks, and dies because it cannot
extricate itself, not only takes the pleasing and sweet scent away from the
fragrant and aromatic ointment but also causes a foul odor, so a man famous
and ennobled by the name of wisdom and the glory he has acquired from wisdom
becomes inglorious through any smallest and slightest departure from wisdom's
prescription.

VERSE 22. The mind of the wise person is at his right hand, but the mind of
the fool is at his left hand.

To make all the more evident the indignity by which that God, whom our
theologians and those who submit themselves to their instruction so greatly
proclaim, permits wisdom to lie cast down and despised, I wish to illustrate
this matter by a comparison and contrast of opposites. Therefore, I say that
the wise person's mind continually and ceaselessly supplies counsel, so that
by its benefit each individual matter, whether you conduct something
privately or publicly, by yourself or in conference with another, is
administered skillfully and prosperously. It is just like the things done by
the right hand, which is very well suited and fitted for handling affairs.
Conversely, nothing succeeds for the fool who undertakes everything rashly
and confusedly, just as things done by the left hand are managed awkwardly
and unprofitably.

VERSE 23. For even on the very road the fool walks, his mind is senseless,
and he proclaims to everyone that he is a fool.

Even if he should conceal his foolishness as much as possible and wish to be
regarded as wise, his folly is nevertheless so great, and he so betrays this
disease of his mind by the entire bearing of his body—his forehead, eyes,
countenance, gait, and gesture—that he proclaims his foolishness to every
citizen who meets him.
