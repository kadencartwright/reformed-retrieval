QUESTION XIII

ON MIDDLE KNOWLEDGE

Besides natural and free knowledge, is there in God a certain middle
knowledge? Negative against the Jesuits, Socinians, and Remonstrants.

[Marginal heading: God’s knowledge is twofold, natural and free.]

I. Although God’s knowledge is no less one and simple intrinsically than his
essence, it can nevertheless be considered in different ways extrinsically by
reason of its objects. Theologians commonly distinguish it into the knowledge
of simple intelligence, or natural and indefinite knowledge, and free
knowledge, or the knowledge of vision and definite knowledge. The former
concerns merely possible things and is therefore called indefinite, because
nothing about them has been determined with God in either direction. The
latter concerns future things and is called definite, because future things
have been determined by God’s certain counsel. Hence they differ from each
other: 1. in object, because natural knowledge concerns possible things, but
free knowledge concerns future things; 2. in foundation, because natural
knowledge is founded upon God’s omnipotence, but free knowledge depends upon
his will and decree, through which things pass from the state of possibility
to the state of futurity; 3. in order, because natural knowledge precedes the
decree, but free knowledge follows it, since it beholds things as future, and
they are not future except through the decree.

[Marginal heading: What is the origin of middle knowledge?]

II. Besides these two kinds of divine knowledge, the Jesuits Fonseca, Lessius,
and Molina devised a third. For they do not agree among themselves which one
is the true father of this spurious offspring, since each claims it for
himself. They called it middle knowledge because it is midway between natural
and free knowledge and differs from each: from indefinite and natural
knowledge because it concerns future things rather than possible things, and
from free knowledge because it does not concern futures as certain but only
under a hypothesis. Its authors explain this middle knowledge by saying that
it is God’s foreknowledge concerning conditional futures whose truth does not
depend upon God’s free decree, since it precedes that decree, but upon the
creature’s freedom, which God certainly foresaw, either in itself or in the
thing, as to how it would determine itself if placed in such an order of
things.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 193 | IA leaf 247 | PDF p. 247]

III. The Jesuits’ purpose was to defend the Semipelagian heresy of foreseen
faith and good works in election and to uphold the idol of free choice, so
that they might more easily extricate themselves from the arguments of the
Dominicans who reject such foresight. The principal argument was that, since
there is no knowledge in God except either natural knowledge, which concerns
possibilities, or free knowledge, which concerns futures, every foreknowledge
of faith and of the good use of free choice must depend upon the decree and
cannot precede it. They thought they could evade this argument in no other way
than by devising this middle knowledge. We need not recount the disturbances
this question stirred up between the Jesuits and the Dominicans, while the
pope—to whose tribunal the whole matter was brought more than once—let his
infallible judgment sleep in a matter of such importance and did not dare to
determine anything. It is enough to note that this invention, upon which the
Jesuits dote as upon their Helen, was very fiercely attacked as utterly false
by the Dominicans, among whom Cumel, Ripa, Alvarez, Nugno, and others are
prominent, and whom the Jansenists join today.

IV. This invention was later adopted by the Socinians and Remonstrants, who
vigorously maintain it in order to place free choice upon the throne. See
Arminius, thesis on the Nature of God; Vorstius, Disputation on God; and
Grevinchovius in his theological dissertation against Ames.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

V. The question is not whether God knows conditional futures, for everyone
agrees that by some knowledge God knows from eternity not only the things
themselves but all their combinations and connections, whether present, past,
or future, and whether necessary or contingent. Rather, the question is
whether they belong to a certain middle knowledge distinct from natural and
free knowledge. We deny this.

VI. The question does not concern necessary conditional futures, which,
given this or that condition, cannot fail to occur—for example, if the sun
rises, it will be day, and if Peter sincerely repents, he will be saved. For
since these are necessarily connected with each other, either from the nature
of the thing or from the hypothesis of the divine decree, they fall under
God’s knowledge: under natural knowledge if the condition is only possible,
or under free knowledge if it is future and decreed by God. Rather, it
concerns contingent conditional futures which, given the condition, can occur
or not occur—for example, if John were in Paris, he would speak or sin, and so
forth. Can such things be certainly and determinately known prior to God’s
decree? We deny this.

VII. The question is not whether knowledge of conditional futures exists in
God prior to every decree. For our adversaries do not deny that some general
decree precedes by which he decreed to produce secondary causes and by which
he is prepared to furnish at least a general and indifferent concurrence to
the creature whenever it wills to determine itself to act. Rather, the
question is whether it precedes the special decree concerning the certain
futurity of this or that thing, so that God sees that thing prior to such a
decree, either in itself or in its causes. They contend this; we deny it.

VIII. The question, therefore, is whether, besides natural knowledge, which
concerns only possibilities, and the knowledge of vision, which concerns only
future things, there is in God a certain third or middle knowledge of
conditional futures by which God knows what human beings or angels would
freely do without a preceding special decree if they were placed in such an
order of things with these or those circumstances. The Jesuits, Socinians,
and Remonstrants affirm this; the orthodox deny it.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that there is no middle knowledge.]

IX. The reasons are: 1. Natural and free knowledge encompass all knowable
things, and entities must not be

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 194 | IA leaf 248 | PDF p. 248]

multiplied without necessity. For nothing exists in the nature of things that
is not either possible or future, nor can conditional futures constitute a
third order. Such things are either from a merely possible or potential
condition that nevertheless will never occur, or from a condition certainly
future and decreed. In the former way, they do not go beyond the nature of
possibilities and belong to natural knowledge; in the latter, they are future,
decreed by God, and belong to free knowledge.

X. 2. Things which are not true cannot be foreknown as true. But conditional
futures are not true when the determination of the divine will is excluded;
for example, that the Sidonians would repent if miracles had been performed
among them. For by their own nature and given those miracles, the Sidonians
stood indifferently disposed toward repenting or not repenting. Therefore, if
it was altogether true that they would repent given those miracles, the
reason for its truth had to come from elsewhere. Yet no cause of this thing
can be imagined besides God’s will, for from eternity there was nothing
besides God’s will that could be the cause determining an indifferent thing
to one side. It was not his essence or knowledge, for each of these apart from
the will effects nothing outside God. Therefore, just as no effect can be
understood as future, whether absolutely or under a hypothesis, without the
divine decree, because no creature can exist in the world without divine
causality, so no conditional future can be knowable before the decree.

XI. 3. If all acts of the created will are subject to divine providence, so
that none is independent and indeterminate, there can be no middle knowledge,
which is supposed to have as its object a free determination of the will that
depends upon no higher cause. The dependence between the first cause and
secondary causes, between the Creator and creatures, proves that there is
such a subjection of the created will. It cannot suffice for preserving this
dependence to say that the will is created and its freedom given by God. For
it will not cease to be the principle of its own determination if its acts do
not depend upon some decree. It will not indeed be the first being, but it
will nevertheless be the first agent, and it will no longer be a secondary
cause but the first, because although it depends upon God in its being, it
does not depend upon him in its acting.

XII. 4. No uncertain knowledge is to be attributed to God. But middle
knowledge can have no certainty, because it concerns an uncertain and
contingent object, namely the will’s indifference (ἀδιαφορίαν). I ask,
therefore, from where can God know certainly what will or will not be future?
This will be either from the nature of the things themselves when he beholds
them, whether in their causes or in themselves as free acts in a created
will, which is Bellarmine’s view—but how will an uncertain thing be able to
ground certain knowledge? Or it will be from the infinity of divine
knowledge, which certainly foreknows toward which side moral persuasion will
incline a will that is otherwise free toward the opposite, as Vasquez and
Suarez hold—but how will the infinity of knowledge be able to change the
nature of things and see as certainly going to occur a thing that is
contingent? Again, knowledge either makes the event certain or foresees it as
certain. If it makes it so, how can it foreknow it as such, and where is the
will’s indifference? If it foresees it as certain, how can there be certain
foresight of an uncertain and indifferent thing? Or it will be from the
things’ existence from eternity, through which they are said to be present to
God, as others think. But since they can have no real being from eternity,
but only intentional being, they cannot be said to exist from eternity except
by reason of the decree in which they obtain their futurity. Therefore, since
the certain necessity of the event cannot be founded upon a contingent
connection of terms or upon the knowledge itself, which knows the thing but
does not make it, it follows that it is founded only upon the effective
decree of the one connecting them. Hence Thomas says very well in part 1,
question 14, article 13: “One who knows a contingent effect only in its cause,
and not in some higher cause that certainly determines it, has only

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 195 | IA leaf 249 | PDF p. 249]

conjectural knowledge of it, because from an indifferent cause, insofar as it
is indifferent, no determinate act will arise; and for the same reason, from
a contingent antecedent, insofar as it is contingent, no necessary inference
will arise before the decree of the divine will.”

XIII. Fifth, middle knowledge removes God’s dominion over free acts, because,
given this knowledge, acts of the will are supposed to exist prior to the
decree and therefore have their futurity not from God but from themselves.
Indeed, in this way God seems rather to depend upon the creature, since he can
decree or arrange nothing except once the determination of the human will is
posited, which God has seen in such a connection of things. It must not be
answered that God’s dominion is not removed on this account because God can
remove that connection or some circumstance of it. For example, in the
foreknowledge by which God knows that Peter will deny Christ if placed in
such a condition, God can cause Peter not to deny Christ by removing some
foreseen circumstance, such as the fear of death, or by adding greater light
in the intellect and a stronger inclination in the will toward confession,
and so forth. For it does not suffice to establish God’s dominion that God can
cause Peter not to deny Christ. He could have called Peter out of this life
before Christ was seized, but that would be to have dominion over Peter’s
life, not over his free choice. Rather, Peter’s free acts of denying or not
denying Christ must depend upon God, and this is denied when that knowledge
is posited. Furthermore, if God can remove one foreseen circumstance, then he
can change the event of the thing. If he can change the event by a decree,
then producing it also belongs to the decree. For the same one who prevents
the event by removing some circumstance must cause it by positing the
circumstances.

XIV. Sixth, given this knowledge, a reason for predestination outside God
apart from his purpose and good pleasure (εὐδοκίαν) could be assigned,
because the foreseen consent of Jacob’s will when placed in such an
arrangement would be at least the condition without which God would not
predestine Jacob rather than Esau to salvation. But according to Paul, no
reason for this election can be assigned apart from God’s good pleasure
(εὐδοκίαν), Romans 9:11. “For when the children were not yet born and had
done nothing good or evil,” it was said that “the elder will serve the
younger,” not because of a foreseen good use of free choice through middle
knowledge but “in order that God’s purpose might remain firm, not from works
but from the one who calls.” Nor should it be said that this condition is
assigned on God’s side, since the knowledge is his, and not on the side of
human beings. For although it is God who foreknows, the object he foreknows is
held to be the good use of free choice foreseen before the decree, so that the
reason for the decree is placed not in God but in the human being. Thus grace
will more fittingly be called the attendant rather than the mistress of human
inclination, and its companion rather than its cause, which makes God depend
upon the human being rather than the human being upon God.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XV. The passage in 1 Samuel 23:11, 12 cannot establish middle knowledge,
because it is not so much a prediction of future things that would still be
in the process of occurring as a revelation of hidden things which already
existed at that time, namely the plans being discussed among the Keilites to
hand David over if he remained there. For since David was uncertain about
Saul’s plan and the disposition of the Keilites toward him, and therefore
asked the Lord whether Saul would come down against Keilah and whether the
Keilites would hand him into Saul’s hands if he remained among them, God
answered that Saul would come down and the Keilites would hand him over if he
remained there, so that David might withdraw from their fury and take flight.
For Saul was in fact preparing for the journey, and the Keilites were already
secretly discussing plans to hand David over to him. “For they will hand you
over,” that is, they have the

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 196 | IA leaf 250 | PDF p. 250]

will to hand you over, as the Interlinear Gloss has it. Thus τὸ (“to go down”
and “to hand over”) is understood not of the action itself as future under a
hypothesis, but, as often elsewhere, it is put for the plan and intention,
that is, to have it in mind to do this, as in Acts 12:6 and 16:27.

XVI. Christ’s words in Matthew 11:22, 23, “If the miracles performed among
you had been performed in Tyre and Sidon, they would long ago have repented in
sackcloth and ashes,” must not be pressed literally, as if they concerned
some thing that would determinately be future under this or that condition.
For this is a hyperbolic and proverbial manner of speaking in which, by a
comparison odious to the Jews, Christ wishes to magnify the stubbornness and
rebellion of those cities that had been made illustrious by his miracles,
which, as the knower of hearts (καρδιογνώστης), he knew to be greater and
more obstinate than the wickedness of the Tyrians and Sidonians. Thus Christ
does not speak from foreknowledge concerning any conditional futures, but by
a customary hyperbole he wishes to reproach the Jews for their ingratitude
and impenitence in comparison with the Tyrians and Sidonians. It is as if a
teacher addressing a slow and dull pupil were to say, “If I had taught a
donkey for so long, it would not be ignorant of this,” or one were to say of
an inexorable judge, “If I had struck rocks and crags for so long, I would
have broken them.” We do not mean that rocks can be softened or a donkey
taught, but only that the pupil’s slowness and the judge’s hardness are very
great. In the same way Christ says in Luke 19:40, “If these are silent, the
stones will cry out,” not as if stones could cry out, but to show that his
person, doctrine, and works are so clear and indisputable that they can no
longer be concealed. Ezekiel 3:6 is a similar passage: “If I had sent you to
peoples of an unknown language, would they not have listened to you?”

XVII. In 2 Samuel 12:8, the prophet enumerates God’s benefits toward
ungrateful David, to which he would have added greater ones if David had
continued in obedience, not from some conditional decree or from middle
knowledge, but according to the promise made to piety. Likewise Psalm 81:14,
15 indicates a conditional promise with a reproach for ingratitude, but it
contains nothing about foreknowledge of a conditional future before the
decree. The words of Elisha to Joash in 2 Kings 13:19 must be understood in
the same way: “If you had struck the ground five or six times, you would have
struck the Syrians to utter destruction.” It is not that he knew this through
middle knowledge, but that the prophet inferred it according to the import of
a divine revelation given indefinitely.

XVIII. It is one thing for God to foresee or know the connection of one thing
with another—for example, of sin with death and righteousness with life—and
another to know this connection as future in a particular subject placed in
this or that condition. The latter requires some decree by which it is
determined what ought to happen concerning that subject, but the former can
be founded solely upon the possibility and relation of the things to one
another.

XIX. It is one thing for God to know all connections of all things as
necessary, and prior to the decree to know the causes of the things that will
occur through them; it is another to know contingent connections of events
and of all things possibly future. If the former were granted, it could favor
middle knowledge. But it is false that prior to the decree, upon which all
futurity of things depends, God knows all connections of all things as
necessary and as infallibly producing the event of things, especially in free
acts. The latter, however, which we grant, does not imply that middle
knowledge exists, because contingent connections of this kind belong only to
natural knowledge when they are considered prior to the decree that
determines the certain futurity of those connections or means.

XX. What is conceived by God to be determinate can also be pronounced to be
determinate, but what is conceived to be merely possible is pronounced to be
merely possible. But we deny that the coexistence of a free act

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 197 | IA leaf 251 | PDF p. 251]

with the hypothesis is conceived to be determinate prior to the decree; we
grant that it is conceived to be possible. Thus it is true prior to the
decree that Peter would possibly sin if placed in such an order of things,
but not that he would determinately sin, so that it would be true that Peter
would actually and really sin if placed in such an order of things. For this
cannot be certain except from God’s permissive decree.

XXI. Necessity and contingency are related in one way in simple terms and in
another in complexes. In the first way, being is divided into necessary and
contingent, and these cannot belong to the same thing. But in the second,
insofar as they arise from the different relation of causes to effects, they
coincide in such a way that what is necessary with respect to the first cause
can be contingent with respect to the secondary cause, since the first cause
so arranges matters that it not only causes the thing to exist but to exist
in its own manner—that is, a necessary thing necessarily and a contingent
thing contingently. Yet this necessity with respect to the first cause does
not remove the freedom of free choice, because it is not a necessity of
coercion but of consequence or infallibility, which is entirely consistent
with freedom.

XXII. Although prior to the decree God can know from the various means that
can be employed to move the will that these or those means, if employed, can
persuade more effectively than others, he nevertheless cannot know prior to
the will to give those means and to move efficaciously to produce the effect
that they will actually persuade. Nor is the example offered from fire valid:
God knows that it has a heating power prior to the will to create fire that
will actually heat. For the case is different with natural agents, which by
their nature are determined to one thing, and with free agents, which can be
borne toward either of two opposites.

XXIII. It is one thing to speak of the cause of things’ existence and another
of the cause of their futurity. Secondary causes can concur with God in
causing the existence of some thing, because they exist together with God and
are active. But no secondary cause can concur with God in causing the
futurity of things, because futurity was established from eternity, whereas
all causes exist only in time. Hence it is clear that the futurity of things
depends upon nothing other than God’s decree, and therefore can be foreknown
from nothing other than the decree.
