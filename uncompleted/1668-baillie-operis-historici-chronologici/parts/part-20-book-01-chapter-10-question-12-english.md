### QUESTION XII

#### What is the end of Daniel’s weeks?

Answer. We are not investigating minutiae here—whether the weeks close at
Christ’s baptism, at his death, or three years after his death—nor the precise
year of the world in which Christ’s death occurred. We may deal with all
these matters in their proper place. At present we address only this
difficulty: Do the weeks extend beyond Christ’s passion to Jerusalem’s
destruction? Sulpicius Severus long ago, and in our age Joseph Scaliger after
him, Junius, Diodati, Polanus, Alsted, and others who place the beginning of
the weeks in the second year of Darius Nothus are compelled by this foundation
to fix their end, with most Jews, at Jerusalem’s ruin. But other Christians,
wherever they begin them, end them with Christ the ruler.

> Marginalia, printed p. 173: *Whether the weeks end at Christ’s death or
> Jerusalem’s destruction*.

The English annotators in their latest edition profess to establish nothing
certain about the end of the weeks. Yet they indicate their desire to place
the beginning of the weeks in Cyrus’s first year and their end in the city’s
destruction, although these positions are altogether ἀσύστατα, “inconsistent.”

We argue against Scaliger as follows. First, if the weeks end at the city’s
destruction, their beginning cannot be referred to any decree about restoring
either city or temple. For according to the discussion above, Darius Nothus
issued no such decree, since the building of both city and temple had been
completed before he was born. And the chronological sequence does not allow
us to count 490 years, or seventy weeks, backward from Jerusalem’s destruction
to Cyrus, Darius Hystaspes, Artaxerxes, or anyone else named by any author.
The Scaligerians admit that this calculation lacks at least forty or
thirty-seven years—the years that elapsed from Christ’s passion to the city’s
destruction.

> Marginalia, printed p. 173: *Arguments against Scaliger and the supporters
> of the city’s destruction. 1*.

Second, after Christ’s public advent only one week of years remained until the
end of the weeks. But from Christ’s public advent until the destruction of
the city there elapsed not only one week of years but five and more, namely,
thirty-seven or forty years. Therefore the city’s destruction was not the end
of the weeks.

[Printed p. 174 | IA leaf 204 | access PDF 204 | OCR object 203]

There is complete agreement on the major premise. We prove it from Daniel
9:25: *From the issuing of the word until Christ the ruler there are seven
weeks and sixty-two weeks*. Here sixty-nine weeks are counted until Christ,
not merely named or suffering, but publicly inaugurated into his mediatorial
office through baptism. After them only one remains within the seventieth.

To avoid this argument, Scaliger in both editions entangles the weeks most
wretchedly, breaks apart their portions, and tosses the one, seven, and
sixty-two weeks here and there, forward and backward, in inexplicable
confusion. But let him turn the portions of the weeks upward, downward, or
wherever he wishes, and he will not escape the argument’s knot. For the
prophet affirms in the clearest words that seven and sixty-two weeks elapsed
before Christ. Whether this means his birth, baptism, or passion is irrelevant
to the present matter, provided that after Christ only one of the seventy
weeks remains until the end of the period. Once this is granted—and it is
sufficiently clear in the words of the prophecy—the weeks cannot close later
than seven years after Christ. Yet they will close five seven-year periods
and more after Christ’s passion if we place their end at the city’s
destruction.

Scaliger offers the following arguments for his opinion. First, the weeks do
not end before the events mentioned by the angel in the history of the weeks
have been completed. But the angel distinctly treats the people’s calamities
and the city’s destruction in the history of the weeks.

> Marginalia, printed p. 174: *Response to Scaliger’s arguments*.

Answer. We must distinguish: some things are treated within the history of
the weeks, and others together with the history of the weeks. We admit that
the weeks do not end before the earlier things are completed, but it is not
necessary for the later things to be completed before the end of the weeks.
In this passage the angel indeed mentions the city’s destruction together
with Christ’s advent, but the angel expressly places the latter before the end
of the weeks and just as expressly places the former after it. Therefore, in
closing the weeks and their history and all that they contain long before the
city’s destruction, we follow the angel as he goes before us and carries the
torch.

> Marginalia, printed p. 174: *To 1*.

The second argument is this. The subject of the weeks was the people and city,
Daniel 9:25: *Seventy weeks are determined upon your people and upon your holy
city*. Therefore the weeks should not end before the people are destroyed and
the city overthrown.

Answer. We deny the consequence. The weeks were not determined upon the
people and city without qualification, for many things concerning the Jewish
people and the city of Jerusalem both preceded and followed the seventy
weeks. Rather, God determined seventy weeks for accomplishing for that people
and city the things the angel adds, namely, those pertaining to Christ the
ruler and the restoration of the desolate city. In the following verse the
angel fixes the beginning and end of these things in the issuing of the word
and Christ the ruler. Indeed, the angel’s purpose was to comfort Daniel and
the faithful among the Jews with the best news, not to increase their present
grief by announcing future calamities. Thus, as he began the weeks with a
matter of great consolation—the city’s rebuilding—so he wished to close them
with an occasion of greater joy: the abolition of all sin and wrath through
Christ’s death and the covenant sanctioned by his blood. But what he relates
about the city’s destruction as the penalty inflicted on those who crucified
Christ and spurned his covenant was less consoling and therefore less relevant
to his present purpose. He suitably placed it outside the boundaries of the
weeks.

> Marginalia, printed p. 174: *To 2*.

The third argument. The chronology fits Scaliger’s calculation well.

Answer. Not at all. Apart from the fact that, by a peculiar invention,
Scaliger must add three and a half years to the 490 contrary to Scripture in
order to carry the weeks down to the city’s destruction, he must also refer
Artaxerxes’s decree sending Ezra to his fifth rather than his seventh year,
contrary to Ezra himself and every interpreter. Even leaving these points
aside, far more than seventy weeks elapsed from the true beginning of the
weeks, the seventh year of Artaxerxes Longimanus, to the city’s destruction.
Thus the chronology does not fit Scaliger’s calculation at all unless we
grant his false principles: that the king who built the temple was Nothus and
the one who sent Ezra and Nehemiah to Jerusalem was Mnemon.

> Marginalia, printed p. 174: *To 3*.

Scaliger draws his fourth argument from Matthew 24:25. Christ says that
Jerusalem will be destroyed when the abomination of desolation of which
Daniel speaks is placed in the temple. Therefore the weeks must end with the
city’s destruction.

Answer. There is no consequence here. Christ indeed cites the passage in
Daniel and asserts that the city’s destruction was predicted by Daniel, but
he utters no word about the weeks, as if they extended either to the city’s
destruction or to the placement of that βδέλυγμα, “abomination,” in the holy
place.

> Marginalia, printed p. 174: *To 4*.

Scaliger’s final argument, which he calls his strongest weapon, is this. The
Darius who in the time of the prophet Zechariah granted the Jews authority to
build the temple must have been Nothus rather than Hystaspes,

> Marginalia, printed p. 174: *To 5*.

[Printed p. 175 | IA leaf 205 | access PDF 205 | OCR object 204]

because seventy years before this Darius’s fourth year the people had
returned from Babylon to Jerusalem. For that many years earlier they
instituted the fasts in Zechariah 7:5, which they could not institute in
Babylon or anywhere outside Jerusalem. But seventy years before Darius
Hystaspes the people were living in Babylon rather than Jerusalem.

Answer. This argument is far too subtle, and it requires many prior
concessions and many preliminary syllogisms before it concludes what is
denied. But for brevity’s sake I strike at the argument’s throat. It is false
that the Jewish people could not establish solemn observances outside
Jerusalem and without an altar. Scaliger’s proofs of this assertion are weak.
Grant that it was not lawful to celebrate Passover or any other sacrifices
except at the altar in Jerusalem; the same principle did not apply to fasts.
They could be observed everywhere, just like solemn prayers. Indeed, Mordecai
instituted the festival of Purim in Media outside Judea. As for the fasts
mentioned by Zechariah, they were evidently instituted in Babylon soon after
the city and temple were laid waste. For how strange it would have been if
those who had abstained from every fast throughout the seventy years of
captivity and for several years even after their return had decided only in
the twenty-eighth year of Darius Hystaspes to institute public fasts because
the temple and city had been devastated so many years earlier.
