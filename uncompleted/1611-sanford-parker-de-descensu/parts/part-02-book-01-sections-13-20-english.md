13. If *Evoi* is tested by the accepted rules of theology, we shall show in
this way that it becomes *Iehova*. Write in their native order the four letters
that we have by now nearly worn smooth: they will sound *Iehova*, or, if the
ancient form is preferred, *Ievo*. Then, by the Cadmean practice of writing
“marks traced by a backward-moving hand,” just as we saw *iah* become the *El*
of Apollo, we shall perceive *Ievo* transformed directly into *Evoi*. This is
not a subtler transposition of letters but an inversion of the whole name. Both
operations may likewise be tried on our *Iove*, in which we readily discover
both *Evoi* and *ievo*.

[1611 Book I, printed p. 20 | Google Books PA20]

Varro believed that Jove was the God of the Hebrews, and, if there is any sound
judgment in St. Augustine, he rightly believed it. If *Evoi* is Iehova, then
without doubt *Evoi Saboi* is Iehova Sabaoth. This name is one—and indeed the
last—of the ten with which we said Greece filled itself with deities. Had the
subject allowed, I would willingly have treated it in orderly fashion and by
itself. Yet its nature and usage are such that it appears degraded unless it
is attached to Iehovah, or at least to Elohim. If I assign it a place together
with Iehova, necessity will excuse the resulting confusion. Let us therefore
take up *Saboth*, an epithet of God—I scarcely dare call it a name—since it is
never read alone in the Law, the Prophets, or the Psalms, but only joined with
God or Lord. Our writers translate *tsevaoth* in various ways. The Greek
translators of the Prophets often retain the Hebrew word; following them, the
Apostles seem, whenever occasion required, to have said *kurion sabaoth*
without translating it. Sometimes, however, those same translators use
*pantocrator* and *theos Israel* where the Hebrew has *saboth*. Unless my
memory deceives me, in the Psalms we everywhere read Greek *dunameos*, Latin
“of powers,” for *saboth*. Elsewhere nothing is more familiar to the Latin
translator than to replace “Lord” or “God saboth” with “Lord” or “God of
hosts.” The singular *tsaba* means an army; its plural is *tsebaotha* or
*tsebaim*. By dropping the final *mem*, a very common Hebrew practice, the
Greeks could readily have formed the *Saboi* we seek. It greatly strengthens
the probability of the act that we have already detected them adapting nine
names to their language; they could have done so with the tenth as well. Yet
we need not proceed by conjecture. We have arguments and witnesses, and with
the wind of antiquity, which we have so far used with sufficient success and
which still blows more favorably,

14. What, then, is being investigated? I suppose it is whether *Saboi* agrees
in origin with *tsebai*, since their agreement in sound is certain. Once it is
established that *Saboi* is Jewish and the name of the Jewish God, nothing at
all prevents it from being identified with Sabaoth. This will be established
as follows. *Saboi* shares with most divine titles the circumstance that it
came from home with an irregular ending, and as if in an oblique case—more an
*anaphorema*, an acclamation, than an *onoma*, a name. When it finally reached
Greece, it took a Greek declension like the others: just as *Iaos* is inflected
from *Iao* and *Ieios* from *Ieie*, so *Sabos* is inflected from *Saboi*. What,
then, is *Sabus*? Look and marvel. Everything we offer harmonizes so well that,
when I see such deep darkness in the facts illuminated by such immense clarity
in the names, I sometimes stand amazed myself. For Hesychius, Suidas,
Eustathius, and all the grammarians, *Sabus* is either a name or surname of
Bacchus. Unless our whole effort in proving this is thought mere play, no doubt
about it can remain. Yet we have not played: the evidence we have brought is
firm, and we shall add still stronger evidence.

One of Plutarch's problems asks, “Who is the God of the Jews?” It is only
asked, not answered, for only the title now survives; the discussion is lost.
What remains is the material that occasioned the problem. Plutarch had said
that Evius, Bacchus, Adonis, and Sabus were the same God, and that the Jews
customarily celebrated a certain feast of Bacchus—not through riddles but, as
his word has it, *amiltrus*: evidently, plainly, and openly. Who does not
understand how far this extends? For my part, I would wish to add nothing more
or different to the point before us.

15. Evius and Bacchus are both discerned in *Iehovah*, while this very line of
reasoning makes Adonis *Adonai*. What reason have they for denying that
*Saboi* is Sabaoth? They must either claim those other names against the
Hebrews—something they will never be able to do—or grant what is true here.
Let them say that *Saboi* is *tsebaim* by pronunciation, or deny that *Evoi* is
Iehova by inversion. If they can do neither—or rather, since they can do
neither—let them not merely send these words back to their homeland with a
calm mind. Let them also allow the same right of return to the exiles of the
same fortune that Demosthenes employed: *Huis attes, attes Huis*.

They should do this all the more gladly because they have never been able to
agree among themselves which god *Huis* was. Some thought him Jupiter, the
same as *Huetios*. Without using that name they called him *ombrion*, whom we
might call the Rain-bringer. Others supposed that Bacchus was designated by
the word and that his nurses were therefore called the Hyades. Aristophanes is
said to have numbered this being, whoever he was, among the foreign gods. What
of the rest—shall I call them more shameless or more foolish? Having nothing
that addressed the matter, they trifled with the sound, changing *Huin* to
*Hun*, a pig, and Attes to a boy whom the pig killed. At such a noble feast of
learning for Minerva, I propose that we sacrifice their pig and suddenly bring
the boy, whom they seem to have mourned long enough, back to life for the
banquet.

If it is not too troublesome, let us also summon that Latin Aristarchus. He
undertook to recount the single combat in the Second Punic War between
Iubellius Taurea of Campania and the Roman knight Gaius Asellus. Yet since
neither the knights' names nor their famous military glory had so much as
reached his ears, the witty and ingenious man invented a charming tale of a
“reddish bull” that contended with a limping ass in the Roman field by some
unknown throw of Mars's dice.

[1611 Book I, printed p. 23 | Google Books PA23]

But let us not stray from serious matters. *Huis* is plainly the name written
in Hebrew as *Hhui* and rewritten as Iehovah. No one need seek in it Jupiter,
Bacchus, or an unknown God. Once this name is found and placed in the light,
hardly anything obscure remains in the rest. A singer of sacred hymns very
frequently encounters both *attah Iehovah* and *Iehovah attah*. Thus *Huis
attes, attes Huis* not only appears to have arisen from these formulas but to
have been doubled in accordance with them. Everyone knows that Hebrew *attah*
means “You.” I would say it also does in Greek, were the senate and people of
the grammarians not apparently ignorant of this. The word has lived in Greece
for many centuries under Homer's hospitality: scarcely anyone old enough to
be numbered among the aged—not even the swineherd Eumaeus—has failed to hear
*atta, geron*, “old father.” Yet by an extraordinary fate the grammarians
agreed that it has no origin and admits no interpretation. Whatever they may
say, *attes* is *attah*: “You.”

Nor does the oracle issued to the Rhodians stand in the way; it confirms Attes
as a great God:

> Appease great Attes, the God who is chaste Adonis;
> Evius, giver of riches, beautiful Dionysus.

The Hebrews likewise use pronouns in place of divine names. When God must be
named, they often call him *Hu*—“He”—according to ancient practice. Yet the
“He” whom these little verses call Attes is not Pythagoras, but Evius, Adonis,
and Dionysus: that is, Iehovah.

*Death, the final line of human affairs, caused this line to be the last of the
most learned author, who now lives with God in the eternal age. According to
our slender ability, we have gathered some of his sketches, which he left
scattered here and there, as though from a shipwreck most sorrowful not only

16. There remain rearrangements of *Iehovah* that begin with *He*: *Hoibe* and
*Hioh*, together with the anagrams descended from each, יהוה. No one can doubt
that the former, *Hoibe*, became *Phoibe*, from which the Greeks derived
*Phoibon* and the Latins *Phoebus*. The Aeolians are known to have prefixed a
digamma to words beginning with a vowel. Grammarians teach that *phi* has the
same force and value, and older Greeks are found to have written *F* in its
place, as *foinon* for *oinon*.

As for the other, *Hioh*, it is such that I would sooner believe knowledge of
Iehova's name had long ago penetrated even to the inhabitants of the New World
than that it was wholly obscure or unknown to any region traversed by the
ancients. Francis Drake—an everlasting ornament of our England and the first
man to sail happily around the whole globe, since Magellan died on the
journey—passed through the straits that bear Magellan's name and sailed the
American coasts across the immense ocean. At about 32 degrees north latitude,
he reported that he encountered a people greatly devoted to sacrifices. They
worshiped his companions as gods and greeted Drake himself as their chief
with a great cry of “Hioh.” Since that excellent man related this while I was
present, and those who wrote his deeds thought it proper to publish, I have not
feared to bring it forward here—especially since this barbarous religious cry
displays *Iehovah* by a perfect anagram.

Moreover, in that ocean one finds circumcised people who seem to have derived
their origin from the Jews. This also concerns Peru, which some who have
written about that coast hold to be Ophir. Be advised that I have not lacked
precedent in fishing for Hebrew mysteries among Atlantic waves by this method
of investigation. Before me it was done by

Goropius Becanus and Arias Montanus—what men, how skilled in ancient
literature! They were persuaded that Peru was Ophir because the names of the
regions agree under the rule of anagramming. If they were permitted to try
this with place-names, why should I not do so, and much more readily, with the
gods? How foolish I was to walk one by one around Greek temples when, if we
listen to Herodotus, almost the whole nation of heavenly beings was borrowed
from Egypt, which was fragrant with the religion of Hebrew affairs! The rule
of anagramming wonderfully multiplied these gods not only from Hebrew terms
but also from words of other languages. The Greeks numbered *Eran*, the earth,
among the heavenly beings. By what rite? Plainly by changing *Eran* to *Rean*
according to the rule of the ancient discipline. In the same way *Aer* became
*Hera*. We receive this derivation of Hera from Plato, and Athenagoras, the
advocate of the Christians, clearly had it in view. He says Hera is Air, as the
name itself declares when joined to itself.

17. More about these matters in their proper place. We must now finish the
remaining anagrams of Iehovah: הוהי, whence I say Iacchus arose, and והיה,
whence Bacchus. Plutarch attests that Bacchus is the same as Adonis, Evius, and
Sabbus. So no one will hesitate or stumble here, it must be known that the
Greeks call not only the god *bakchon* but also that god's priests *bakchous*;
they use Sabbus and Sabbi in the same sense.

With this premise, I no longer delay over ignorant people who are distressed
that the name of so obscene an idol and such impious idolaters should be
derived from Iehova, particularly since there is a ready answer for everyone
so tormented. When the ancients cried *Hie* and *Euvion* at banquets—both
certain names of Iehova, as shown above—they certainly did not understand the
author of all shamelessness, drunkenness, and debauchery, who alone now boasts
in those names. They meant the God who presides over modesty, honor, and
temperance, whose presence could warn people to spend feast days honorably,
soberly, and peaceably. Later, when the custom took root of paying divine
honors to men—perhaps abandoned and profligate men, but certainly men—and this
newer practice could not conveniently be accomplished without changing names,

Thebans—a colony of Sidonians and, as neighbors, not ignorant of the Jewish
religion—wished to number Semele's son, their fellow citizen, among the gods,
they judged it wisest to take from Jewish history not only cries and names but
also feast days, rites, and deeds. Consequently we can see most of the things
most worth knowing that sacred literature records about the worshipers of
Iehova heaped upon Bacchus, either confused or deflected into fable.

Lactantius therefore had a point when he rightly said that what the poets
relate is true but veiled under some covering and appearance. He himself shows
that this appearance lies chiefly in the gods' names: “The poets' falsehood is
not in the deed but in the name.” He rightly calls them falsehoods, since the
name was often invented even though the better-informed priests of the gods
understood the fabrication and kept it hidden from the common people.
Harpocrates declares this even in silence: placed beside the Egyptian gods, he
held a finger to his mouth to command silence. Macrobius teaches us what this
meant: the hidden explanations flowing from the spring of pure truth are not
permitted to be recounted even in the sacred rites; if anyone attains them, he
is ordered to keep them concealed within his own consciousness and to make
public only what is fabulous or physical.

Does anyone wish to investigate every truth in this theology of Bacchus? Let
him recognize Moses as the true Bacchus, or the true God of Moses. Let him
judge that the chief things related about Bacchus refer only fabulously to
Theban Bacchus, or even now to Osiris, whom the Egyptians call Bacchus,
according to Herodotus. No one should doubt that, on account of the supreme
God, Bacchus received what the Sicilian says of him: although people hold
various opinions about the other gods, everyone keeps firm in mind the
immortality of Dionysus, because no Greek or foreigner is without experience
of his benefits and favor.

What, then? Shall we therefore say that the Jews worshiped Bacchus, conqueror
of the East, or that the Levites were so called as though “Evites”? Far from
it. One of our writers reasoned backward when he noticed that *Alma* signifies
a virgin among other peoples and therefore concluded that the words of all
languages were used by the Hebrews; his argument should have brought him
punishment. Hebrew words spread themselves into every language,

spirit now before us. There survives a hymn by Orpheus celebrating *Mosen*. In

How does *Muses* differ from *Moses* except, you might say, in the pointing?
Orpheus came closer than Alexander Polyhistor, who claimed that the Hebrews
had a woman named Moso, author of the law the Jews still use. Perhaps those
ancient writers were similarly deceived by the final *o*, common in Greek
women's names, when they included several women among the gods who came from
Egypt and conquered the Giants.

Orpheus continues to celebrate his Bacchus's birth in such a way that one must

> There in the pure, fruit-bearing plains,

utterly fabulous ways and transferred to Bacchus. Even some Christians seem
to have known this. Lactantius says that the rites of Egyptian Isis concern

Yet those who changed Moses' Arabia into Bacchus's India did not proceed
without any reason. The ancient Europeans used “India” for that whole tract of
regions beyond the sea, as the single ancient story of Andromeda plainly
proclaims. Ovid says Perseus carried Andromeda away from the black Indians.

Andromeda story points the same way. Pliny says the bones of the sea monster to
which she was exposed were brought to Rome from the Jewish town of Joppa and
displayed among other marvels by Marcus Scaurus in his aedileship; they were
forty feet long. Eustathius adds in his commentary on Dionysius that Egypt was
called Aetia from an Indian eagle named *Aetos*, as Joseph Scaliger noted.

20. Since matters stand this way, perhaps someone will marvel that Joshua
found no favor among the poets, who appear wholly silent about him. The
opposite is true. With the exception of Moses himself, they invented more
about no one than Joshua. He is their true Hercules and also their true
Apollo, as will plainly appear when the true explanation comes into the light
of everything they so loudly sing about the war with the Giants.

It is clear that the poets drew nearly the whole story from Hebrew records and
that the war against the Canaanites was the spring from which Greece drew most
of what it babbles about its Giants defeated by the gods—if one prefers to
call them so. The fables agree that the gods who fought the Giants came from
Egypt; these are the twelve tribes. They appoint Father Liber commander of the
whole army: he is Moses. They relate that his power shone in that battle and
add little more. Because the entire weight of the war fell on Joshua, they
assign the chief part to him: first under the covering of Hercules, insofar as
they chose to present

[Marginalia: Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 16, p. 294.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 33 | Google Books PA33]

the Canaanites from Moses' books as Giants; then under the covering of Apollo,
insofar as they wished to present them as Dragons, imitating the Prophets, who
in several places compare the enemies of God's people to Leviathans, whales,
dragons, and serpents.

To establish first that Joshua was understood under Hercules, one must recall
the chief point: Egyptian Hercules and Dionysus are said to have campaigned
together against the Indians. We saw earlier that these Indians were the
Canaanites. I hope no one now doubts that this Dionysus was Moses. Will this
Hercules, then, not be Joshua? The time fits exactly. Augustine assigns the
ages of Hercules and Bacchus between Israel's departure from Egypt and the
death of Joshua son of Nun. Among recent writers, Possevino agrees, affirming
that Bacchus was at Mount Nysa about twelve hundred years before Alexander,
which corresponds to the time of Moses.

The place of Hercules' war against the Giants sheds still more light, once the
place itself is brought into the light. This was formerly difficult because of
Virgil's error and Politian's uncertain disputes. Now it is certainly open to
us as we return to sacred Scripture, whose brightness will dispel every error
and whose judgment will settle the controversies. Homer correctly designated
the place *en Arimois*. Virgil did not understand and rendered it with the
single word *Inarimes*. Thus in an instant the region of Bashan leapt into
Sicily across the Mediterranean with far too skipping a bound, because that
island was anciently called Inarime. Let us give Homer's whole passage:

> The earth groaned beneath Zeus who delights in thunder,
> when in anger he lashed the earth around Typhoeus
> among the Arimi, where they say Typhoeus has his bed.

Virgil interpreted the last words as:

> and hard Inarime, laid by Jove's command upon Typhoeus's bed.

This is manifest error. The prince of poets explicitly names the Arimi. Others
have placed them in Mysia, Maeonia, Cilicia, or the Pithecusae islands, but
those who locate them in Syria certainly put them in their proper place. Among
these is Posidonius, whom Strabo supports: “The Syrians were called Arimi—those
now called Aramaeans—and a certain Arimus reigned over them. Hence the Orontes
near Antioch was once called Typhon.”

[Marginalia: Ps. 74.13–14; Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius* 2.14, Latin and
Greek, pp. 25, 38; Augustine; Possevino; Homer, *Iliad* 2, p. 264.46.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 34 | Google Books PA34]

How beautifully this agrees with sacred literature! Aram first reigned in
Syria, and Ammon's sons came from Aram. It is therefore clearer than daylight
that the ancient war of the gods described by Homer was the very war fought
against the Ammonites under Joshua's fortunate leadership.

Before proceeding farther, however, it must be noted that some people added a
fourth line to Homer's verse:

> In a wooded place, in a rich land full of waters.

This does much to persuade us that Homer speaks about the region of Bashan,
especially since sacred literature establishes that no soil was richer or
better suited for grazing cattle. Yet where did Homer obtain the dreadful
quaking of the stricken earth and the crashing of the thundering, flaming
sky? He may have taken it from the event itself. Of all wars that had ever
occurred, this war in Bashan was the most terrifying, both for the immense
preparations and for God's dreadful judgment, powerfully exercised that day
against his most savage enemies. Yet it is also quite probable that the poet
mixed things kept distinct in Scripture and heaped into this passage material
from other places like the following words of David: “The clouds poured out
water; the upper clouds gave voice; your arrows also flew; the sound of your
thunder was in the circuit; lightning lit up the inhabited world; the earth
was moved and trembled.” It is uncertain whether David mingled the destruction
of the Egyptians and the Canaanites in these words. Yet if they concern the
Egyptians dying at the Red Sea, it is not unreasonable to judge that the poet
transferred them here.

We therefore see how exactly the time, place, and manner of Hercules' war
against the Giants agree with Joshua's war against the Canaanites. Let us now
consider the enemies. Several unquestionable arguments persuade me that their
leader Typhoeus was Og the Bashanite. The strongest is drawn from the bed
mentioned above. Once Virgil's error has been destroyed, could I hear from
Homer that the chief Giant who fought the gods had his bed among the Arimi—a
bed preserved through long ages and renowned far and wide—without seeming to
hear these words from Scripture? “Only Og king of Bashan was left from the
remnant of the Giants. Behold, his bed was an iron bed. Is it not in Rabbah of
the Ammonites? It was nine cubits long and four cubits wide, according to the
cubit of a man.” A second argument,

[Marginalia: Eusebius, *Iliad* 6.262; Strabo; Gen. 10.23; 2 Chron. 20.2;
Strabo 13, p. 597; Ps. 77.18; Deut. 3.11.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 35 | Google Books PA35]

neither weak nor insignificant, is supplied by the name. It is not formed by
the third of the rules proposed above, the rule of anagramming, but follows
the second rule, which follows the thing signified by the name. What does *Hog*
mean in Hebrew? “Burned.” What does *Tuphoeos* mean in Greek? Is it not exactly
the same? For Hesychius, *tuphein* means to raise a fiery flame and likewise
to be set ablaze or burned.

Once the poets had represented Typhoeus as “the burning one” in name, nothing
was more ready to hand than to make the omen answer the name: they introduced
him with a burning body, struck by a thunderbolt, fleeing from battle, then
submerged beneath the waves, and finally extinguished. Yet to what place did
he flee, or in what lake was he submerged? Once this is known, both who he was
and where he was defeated will be seen very clearly. Apollonius comes most
helpfully to our aid. Typhaon, as he calls him, struck by lightning and
burning, came to the Nysaean mountains and disappeared from sight there:

> He himself reached
> the mountains and the Nysaean plain, where still
> he lies beneath the depths of Lake Serbonis.

This testimony plainly settles the matter and defeats the whole question.
Lake Serbonis is on the border of Egypt where it was once joined to Syria;
Nysa is in Arabia. Although we brought forward enough about it above, it will
still be useful to add the scholiast's testimony here. He cites it from
Herodotus in the same verses as Homer:

> There is a certain Nysa, a lofty mountain flourishing with woods,
> near Phoenicia and the streams of Egypt.

Were I not persuaded that it is now established that Joshua was the Hercules
who defeated the Giants, especially Typhoeus, I would add that the fable in
which Hercules is similarly aided by stones falling from heaven could only
have arisen from Joshua, who received that aid. As monuments to this event,
stone-covered plains called Phlegraean survived in Gaul, Thessaly, and Italy.
