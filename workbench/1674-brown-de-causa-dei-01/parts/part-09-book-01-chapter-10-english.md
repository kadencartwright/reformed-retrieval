## CHAPTER X

### On the Moral Law

SINCE the dispute principally concerns this law, everything in this controversy
must be examined here more carefully, and one must consider both what its *name*
means and what the *thing* is.

As for the name, it is merely devised by human beings, as are the other terms,
namely, *ceremonial* and *judicial*. For nowhere in Scripture do we read of any
law called *moral* in express terms. Yet we do not think that all terms of this
kind devised by human beings to distinguish and explain some theological matter
must be rejected. And since usage—which possesses the authority, force, and
standard of speech—has prevailed so that this word denotes those laws which are
distinguished from the ones commonly called *ceremonial* and *forensic*, we may
not coin other terms or take customary terms in a sense other than the common
one, unless we wish to provoke a useless dispute about words.

The reason for this designation is taken from the fact that these laws were
enacted concerning matters which in themselves pertain to good morals, as
Thomas says in part 1 of the second part, question 100, article 1, in the body;
or because they prescribe to everyone, from God's most holy will, the morals by
which they are to live. Cicero himself attests that a law, or anything else
which has such a relation to morals, may fittingly enough be called *moral*. He
begins his book *On Fate* (as we now have it incomplete) thus: “Because it
pertains to morals, which the Greeks call ἤθη, we customarily call that part of
philosophy *On Morals*. But one who enriches the Latin language ought to name it
*Moral*.” Since, therefore, we quite properly name that part of philosophy which
treats morals *moral*, why should we not also call that part of the divine laws
which contains precepts concerning morals *moral*?

It is indeed true that the other divine laws, both Forensic and Ceremonial,
also pertain to morals. Nevertheless, this designation can be attributed in a
peculiar sense and κατ’ ἐξοχὴν, “by preeminence,” to those laws which are not
limited to any period but bind absolutely everyone, and do so always. “For the
ancients,” Calvin says in *Institutes*, book 4, chapter 20, section 14, “who
handed down this division, although they were not unaware that those latter two
parts”—that is, Judgments and Ceremonies—“were concerned with morals,
nevertheless did not call them *Moral*, since they could be changed and
abrogated while morals remained intact. They peculiarly applied that name to
the first part, apart from which true holiness of morals and an immutable
standard of right living cannot exist.” Dr. Hoornbeeck, *Practical Theology*,
book 9, chapter 20, says that something is called *Moral* when it is “derived
from the nature of uprightness, just and equitable in itself, and therefore
pertains to all human beings.”

This *Moral* law can be considered either insofar as it had been repeated and
enacted particularly for the Israelite people, or in itself. When considered
in the former way, it is usually asked whether it binds all Nations, and
particularly Christians now under the New Testament. Theologians divide into
various parties here. Some assert that the whole of that law had been given
only to the people of the Hebrews and did not bind other Nations which had not

[1674 logical printed p. 111 | IA leaf 129 | PDF p. 129]

submitted themselves to the Jewish economy, and that it does not now bind us
Christians except insofar as it agrees with the law of Nature and has been
confirmed by Christ. Others affirm that although the Moral law enacted through
Moses first and immediately bound the Israelite people to obedience, it also
binds all other Nations, including us Christians. Others distinguish between
this law considered with respect to the matter which is established by natural
right implanted in every human mind, and with respect to matter prescribed by
supernatural divine institution and the ground of obligation introduced
through the ministry of Moses. They say that the *Moral Law* taken in the former
sense binds all Nations, but taken in the latter pertains only to the Jews. We
will not decide this controversy here. Later, when we treat the Decalogue
specifically, it will be more appropriate to state our opinion on that
question, namely, in book 4, chapters 2 and 3.

Here we consider the *Moral Law* in itself; and no one will deny that, taken in
this way, it binds absolutely all Nations and was in force long before the law
was enacted through Moses. Indeed, everyone who acknowledges that the first
human beings were not without law but subject to God as Governor and Legislator
will confess that it bound them.

The very learned Essenius plainly demonstrates that this Moral law was in force
before the time of Moses in his *System of Theology*, disputation 3 under title
9, *On the Legal Covenant*, thesis 23 and following, by an induction of the
precepts pertaining both to the first and the second table. The reader may
consult him.

It is certain that the first human beings were created in God's image, in true
justice and holiness, and that the standard of this justice and holiness was
God's law, which had been prescribed by God to institute and regulate human
morals—that is, the *Moral Law*. We by no means judge that it was then actually
distinct from the *Law of Nature*, unless the Law of Nature is taken more
narrowly than is right, namely, for that law whose knowledge had been so
engraved upon the human being that it could readily be drawn out without any
preceding or concurrent divine revelation or inspiration. It is also certain
that human beings were governed by law before the time of Moses. For on account
of the violation of this law God destroyed the world by the flood. Hence, too,
all sounder Theologians charge the Patriarchs with violating the law on account
of polygamy and similar things committed against the Moral Law.

Leaving aside these matters, which cannot be called into doubt, let us proceed
to others.

It is asked whether and how this *Moral Law* differs from the *Natural Law*.
Thomas, in part 1 of the second part, question 100, article 1, explains Natural
things so as to refer every Moral precept to the *Law of Nature*: “Moral matters
are those which in themselves pertain to good morals. Since human morals are
spoken of in relation to reason, which is the proper principle of human acts,
those morals which agree with reason are called good, but those which conflict
with reason are called bad. Just as every judgment of speculative reason
proceeds from the natural knowledge of first principles, so also every judgment
of practical reason proceeds from certain naturally known principles, from
which one may proceed in various ways to judge diverse things. For some matters
in human acts are so evident that with a little consideration they can
immediately be approved or disapproved; these are the common and first
principles. But some matters require for their judgment much consideration of
diverse circumstances, which not everyone but the wise can consider diligently,
just as consideration of the particular conclusions of the sciences does not—

[1674 logical printed p. 112 | IA leaf 130 | PDF p. 130]

—pertain to everyone, but only to Philosophers. There are also some things for
judging which a human being needs the aid of divine instruction, as in matters
of belief. It is therefore plain that, since moral precepts concern matters
which pertain to good morals, and these are matters which agree with reason,
while every judgment of human reason is in some way derived from natural
reason, all moral precepts must pertain to the law of nature, though in diverse
ways. For some are things which every person's natural reason immediately
judges by itself ought or ought not to be done, such as ‘Honor your father and
mother’ and ‘You shall not kill’; things of this kind belong absolutely to the
law of nature. Some, however, are things which the wise judge ought to be
observed by a more subtle consideration of reason. These too belong to the law
of nature, yet require the training by which the less wise are instructed by
the wiser, such as ‘Rise before the gray head and honor the person of the old
man,’ and other things of this kind. Still others are matters for judging which
human reason needs divine instruction, through which we are taught concerning
divine things, such as ‘You shall not make for yourself a carved image,’ and so
forth.”

Thus he refers to the *Law of Nature* even matters of faith which surpass
Natural reason and are proposed for belief by the Moral law.

Everyone admits that all things commanded or forbidden by the *Law of Nature*
are also commanded or forbidden by the *Moral Law*. The question, however, is
whether nothing else pertains to the *Moral Law* beyond what is commanded or
forbidden in the law of nature. The solution to this question depends upon the
various senses and explanations of the Natural and Moral law.

For if the *Natural Law* is restricted only to the things which *Adam* was
actually and formally bound to do or avoid in the state of innocence, then it
is certain that some things are now Moral which cannot be referred to the
Natural law taken so strictly, such as faith in Christ and repentance. And even
if someone should say (Dr. Burmann indeed concedes it, as we will see later)
that these and similar things are not moral because all Nations are not bound
to them, there are nevertheless other things which all Nations are now Morally
bound to perform but which *Adam* was not bound to perform before the fall—for
example, covering nakedness, not to mention other things such as many matters
in *judgments, the punishment of guilty persons, and war*. On account of the
change introduced by the fall, these are now morally required, though they were
not before, or at least not denominatively and determinately as they are now.

Further, if the *Natural Law* is said to be that whose knowledge was implanted
and engraved in *Adam's* heart, so that it required no divine revelation,
inspiration, or institution, then surely marriage itself, conjugal love, and
the prohibition of wandering lusts, adulteries, polygamies, and rash divorces
will not pertain to the *Moral Law*. For it was not without some divine
inspiration or suggestion (as noted above) that *Adam* learned that *Eve* was
flesh of his flesh, and so forth, and that a man would therefore leave his
father and mother and cleave to his wife. On this depended marriage between one
man and one woman, as is plain from Malachi 2:14–15; the condemnation of divorce
for any frivolous cause, Matthew 19:4–6; the mutual love of spouses, Ephesians
5:28–29, 31; and likewise the authority and dignity of the man above the woman,
1 Timothy 2:13 and 1 Corinthians 11:7–9.

But if the *Law of Nature* is taken for that law which God himself can in no way
change, and which he was necessarily bound to enact by reason of his own essence,
so that—

[1674 logical printed p. 113 | IA leaf 131 | PDF p. 131]

—it would imply a contradiction if he had not enacted the same law or if he had
enacted its contrary—that is, if things now forbidden had been commanded, or
things now commanded forbidden—then, with respect to *Right Natural to God*, not
a few things now commanded in the second table of the Decalogue would fail to
pertain to the *Natural Law*. For it is evident from what has already been said
that many things which cannot pertain to the Natural Law taken in this way are
forbidden or commanded by the Moral Law. Such are some things now commanded to
us in the *fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth*, and *tenth* precepts of the Decalogue,
as will be further evident from what follows.

Moreover, if the *Natural Law* is said to be only that which forbids or commands
things necessarily to be omitted or done, and which human nature itself, even
now corrupted, recognizes or can recognize as such without Scripture by the
power of the principles still inhering in human hearts, then certain things
which without doubt are forbidden or commanded by the Moral Law will not be
reducible to the *Law of Nature*. For although practical principles remain
unchanged to some degree after the fall, the conclusions to be drawn from those
principles by reasoning waver miserably. Indeed, the common notions themselves
have been so obscured, partly by the fault of nature and partly by the depravity
of custom and education, that they appear almost extinguished. Hence, as we
heard Thomas say above, a person now in the corrupted state needs divine
instruction to understand many things.

But if the *law of nature* is taken as we described and explained it above, it
is abundantly plain that all Moral precepts pertain to the Law of Nature—that
is, that all Moral precepts are matters of Natural Right for us.

It is therefore plain that the controversy so often raised and debated on both
sides about this matter is merely a dispute about words. And when the question
concerns the *Sabbath*, it is better to formulate it thus: Does the fourth
precept of the Decalogue *concerning the Sabbath* bind perpetually or not? That
is, by reason of that precept are we bound, after six continuous working days,
to devote the seventh to God? Framing it this way would cut off the occasion for
disputing about the words *Moral* and *Natural*.

Yet it cannot be denied that our theologians explain clearly enough in this
controversy what they understand by *Moral*, namely, what binds perpetually.
Nevertheless, the adversaries, who anxiously seek hiding places, stir up an
enormous dispute about this word, as we will hear. They will allow nothing to be
called *Moral* unless it is Natural; and, so that they may fight more securely,
they acknowledge nothing as Natural unless it deserves to be called so in the
strictest sense, or is Natural to God himself, or at least is what they
themselves want it to be.

Some hold that, besides the things which were inscribed upon the human mind in
the state of innocence, the Moral Law also includes certain things which they
call *constitutive*. Their subject matter was not necessary from the Natural
Law but accrued solely from the legislator's will. Thus Apollonius speaks in
*On the Law of God*, chapter 6, thesis 3: “The moral law of Moses includes the
repetition of the natural law inscribed upon the mind of the first human being
in the primal state of innocence. For its precepts form morals and direct them
according to the prescription of right and holy reason. Enlightened and holy
reason informed the first human being and was the standard of human actions. It
possessed certain notions and principles engraved and implanted by God, the
author of Nature, from which homogeneous conclusions were drawn both in
practice and in theory.

[1674 logical printed p. 114 | IA leaf 132 | PDF p. 132]

The natural law therefore included those principles and the conclusions drawn
from them, and they are now proposed in the moral law of Moses.

“The Moral law also adds to the Law of Nature certain constitutive things whose
subject matter was not necessary from the natural law but accrued solely from
the legislator's will. Hence those positive precepts which had been added to the
natural law by God's supernatural revelation in the primal state of integrity,
namely, the observance of the sabbath and the proper honoring of marriage, are
included in the moral law of Moses. A peculiar sanction fortified by certain
special promises and threats, as well as the precept concerning instituted
worship, were proposed and imposed upon human beings in the moral law of Moses,
although they were not contained in the natural law.”

If anyone followed this account, he could defend himself safely enough against
the adversaries' exceptions and firmly enough maintain and establish the
principal cause. For the sense of these words is abundantly plain from what has
already been said.

Others follow this account, distinguishing between *Natural Moral* and
*Positive Moral*. They call that the *Natural Moral Law* which concerns
necessary acts or omissions, whether toward others or toward ourselves, and
which even now-depraved human nature recognizes without the light of Scripture,
or which it can be moved to admit as such by arguments drawn from principles
still implanted in the heart. They call that the *Positive Moral Law* which is
contained in Scripture either clearly and expressly or by firm consequence,
and which even corrupted nature cannot deny to be good, just, and therefore
fitted to bind perpetually according to the legislator's good pleasure, even
though, before the legislator's will was revealed by some sign, nature was
unwilling or unable to judge it altogether necessary in itself.

I agree sufficiently with these men on the substance of the matter, although
we explain the terms in different ways. And it is certain that not all Moral
precepts are equally necessary in themselves. Some are necessary as
*Principles*, others as *Immediate Conclusions*, and still others as *Mediate
Conclusions* drawn from them at a greater distance. I also readily acknowledge
that there are some moral precepts which so flow from God's mere good pleasure,
and are so positive to God, that God could have refrained from commanding or
forbidding them, or perhaps could have commanded what he forbade or forbidden
what he has commanded. Therefore, as far as the substance of the matter is
concerned, it makes no difference whether we call these *Positive Morals* or
even *Naturals for us*. For when we call these precepts *Natural for us*, we do
not deny that they are *Positive*—to God, that is—but affirm the following as
well.

Tilenus says in his *Theological Syntagma*, part 1, disputation 32, thesis 27:
“It is manifest that the Moral Law pertains to the Natural Law, because its
precepts form morals and direct them according to the prescription of right
reason. Just as reason is the form of a human being and the standard of human
actions, so it has certain notions and principles implanted and engraved by
nature, from which homogeneous conclusions are drawn both in πράξει, ‘practice,’
and in θεωρίᾳ, ‘theory.’ Yet their συγγένεια, ‘kinship,’ or connection with the
principles is not equally perceived by everyone, according as some have
διανοήματα, ‘understandings,’ more purified and exercised than others.”

When it is asked why this *Moral Law*, since it is the same as the Natural Law
and therefore in some way written in every heart, needed to be made known by
voice or writing, Ames answers in *On Conscience*, book 5, chapter 1, thesis 28:
“In an integral nature no promulgation of this kind would have been needed. But
after the corruption of nature, such was the blindness of the mind and the
perversity of the will—

[1674 logical printed p. 115 | IA leaf 133 | PDF p. 133]

—and the disturbance of the affections that only remnants of this law remained
in every heart, like traces of a faded picture. It therefore needed to be
renewed by the voice and
authority of God, as if with a new brush.” Apollonius explains the reasons more
fully in the place cited above, chapter 6, thesis 7. 1. “Through sin the
knowledge of the law implanted in human minds at their first creation had been
obliterated and obscured. For that full and perfect light of knowledge, a part
of the divine image, was lost and forfeited. Corrupted natural knowledge and
justice recognize that external and graver offenses are forbidden by the law,
but they do not recognize the inner corruption of nature and the first motions
of concupiscence as sins. God therefore willed, by that solemn promulgation of
the law, to restore its whole and genuine meaning.” 2. “Among the Egyptians and
other Nations, many shameful things had been received through evil customs and
confirmed by long use, and they were regarded as the law of nature. Therefore,
by that public repetition of the Moral law it had to be shown which natural
notions and received customs agree with the standard of justice prescribed by
God in the primal state. It also had to be shown that the standard of justice
pleasing to God is not the decrees of corrupted reason or the examples of the
Gentiles, but the precepts of the living God,” Luke 18:3–4. 3. “Since in human
nature, miserably corrupted and depraved through sin, there inheres an abundant
crop of wicked inclinations, by whose force human beings are carried headlong
into graver offenses, God therefore willed this public law to stand forth so
that the human mind might be pulled back by it as by a barrier and made
firmer in resisting those wicked desires, and so that a greater and stronger
spur and accusing force might be added to the conscience which restrains and
scourges.” 4. “Since human beings do not recognize the inner uncleanness of
nature but, enchanted by admiration for external discipline, strive to erect
their own justice before God,” Romans 10:3, “by that solemn promulgation of the
law it therefore had to be shown what God's judgment is against sins, by which
every mouth is stopped and the whole world is rendered more and more ὑπόδικος,
‘liable to judgment,’ before God,” Romans 3:19, “so that, recognizing our misery
by this means, we may hurry to Christ the propitiator, stripped of our own
justice.” To these we can add this reason: because of the fall, certain things
are now morally required explicitly and formally which were not required in
this way before the fall, such as faith in Christ and similar things.

This *Moral Law* requires the rectitude and holiness of our nature as the
foundation and right principle of universal justice, as well as right external
actions and works. In these it requires perfection both of *degrees*, when it
insists that we do the things commanded with the whole heart, the whole soul,
the whole mind, and all our strength, Matthew 22:37 and Luke 10:27; and of
*parts*, when it requires the whole inner and outer person and requires us to
obey God in everything, Deuteronomy 27:26 and James 2:10. And in all these
things it requires constancy and perseverance, Ezekiel 18 and 33. It further
requires that our affections be placed in God alone and that we regard every
creature as contemptible in comparison with God, Psalm 73:24; that we worship
God with intense love; that we perform all our duties from faith and sincere
love toward God; and that we do all our external works to God's glory, even the
very act of eating and drinking. See Romans 14:23; Hebrews 11:6; 1 Timothy 1:5;
Titus 1:15; 1 Corinthians 10:31; Colossians 3:17; Romans 14:7–9; and 1 Peter
3:2. Hence this law is called most perfect, Psalm 19:8. But it would be tedious
to enumerate all these things. From these and similar points it is evident that
this Moral Law requires many things of which scarcely any traces appear among
Nations not yet enlightened by the light of the Gospel or God's word.

[1674 logical printed p. 116 | IA leaf 134 | PDF p. 134]

It also requires justice in punishing guilty persons, deciding controversies,
and undertaking war, as well as the exercise of mercy toward the miserable,
oppressed, and afflicted, and similar things. The Primal Natural Law did not
actually and formally require these things, as such and taken denominatively
and determinately—or insofar as they are so applied and restricted—from *Adam*
in the state of innocence. The very learned Essenius explains this excellently
in the place cited above, disputation 6 under title 9, *On the Legal Covenant*,
thesis 49: “Secondary natural right regards human nature deprived by
the fall of its integrity and vitiated by the gravest corruption and very many
miseries. In this state the habitual perfection of wisdom, even concerning
things to be done, has been lost, both with respect to the possession of
principles and to the ease of drawing conclusions and recognizing them.——
Indeed, the various applications also had to be made differently in relation
to the sins, misery, and calamities which intervened, as, for example, in
political judgments; in the punishment of guilty persons, even by capital
punishment; in war; in servitude; in duties of mercy; and so forth. Knowledge
of this right existed virtually and eminently in the primal state. In the
secondary state itself, it ought to be drawn from the principles of right
remaining there and is, at least to some extent, formally drawn from them. But
what is lacking in secondary right under the state which is merely natural in
this sense is supplied, at least objectively, through divine revelation which
repeats and as it were restores the moral law, particularly as this revelation
was made through the ministry of Moses, delivered to the Israelites, and later
repeated and inculcated more frequently.”

The same can be said of saving and justifying faith in Christ and of
repentance. For Apollonius says in the place cited above, chapter 9, thesis 7:
“After the fall the moral law was so adapted in its precepts and commands to the
salvation of fallen and sinful human beings; and therefore it commanded and
enjoined all those virtues which are required by the law for the salvation of a
sinful person. Indeed, the law of God in the state of integrity also established
the same virtues which are virtues after the fall. Only the same designation
and determination attributed to them after the fall would not then have
obtained. Thus repentance, as to the thing itself, also existed in the state of
integrity. For nothing else is meant by the excellence of the law which is
called repentance when human beings return to it after the fall. Modesty also
existed in the state of integrity, for as to the thing itself it is nothing
other than the love of chastity opposed to every impurity. It is now called
modesty because of that particular determination after the fall which is averse
to the shameful uncovering of members that must be covered. Therefore there was
room for the virtue of repentance under the perfect state, even though the
condition of that state did not admit such a designation and determination.”
The very learned Essenius speaks in nearly the same way in *Dissertation on the
Subjection of Christ*, chapter 3, section 14: “By the force of the first precept,
a person is always bound to believe with divine faith whatever is divinely
revealed to him. The Gospel proposes Christ; consequently, Christ must be
believed with divine faith by those to whom and insofar as he is divinely
proposed. This is not now a new precept, but the act of faith is extended to a
new object under the formal rationale which the law has always embraced. In
almost the same way, love to be shown to one's neighbor as such is now extended
to other human beings than those to whom it extended in former ages, since
other human beings exist now than existed then; yet the precept itself cannot
on that account be called different.”

Thus, once a positive worship of God has been instituted, this *Moral Law*
requires us to observe that instituted worship in a holy manner; yet it did not
require this before the institution of that worship. Hence we are bound today
under the Gospel to worship God by a different instituted external worship than
Old Testament believers were. And after the fall the Church is bound to duties
of external worship different from those to which—

[1674 logical printed p. 117 | IA leaf 135 | PDF p. 135]

—*Adam* was bound in the state of integrity, because of the new institution.
Yet the *Moral Law*, which commands a person to worship God properly according
to the manner prescribed by him, always remains the same—unchanged and
unabrogated—and nothing is taken away from it. For the precept itself is one
thing, and the determinate object of the precept another.

This *Moral Law* was in force before it was promulgated by *Moses*, as was said
above. [For, as Apollonius says in the place cited above, chapter 6, thesis 6,
“It can easily be demonstrated through the individual precepts of the Decalogue
that in those times manifest and manifold traces of the moral law are found in
God's addresses, the Patriarchs' institutions, the obedience of the godly, and
pious exercises. For it must not be thought that the religion and law of the
sons of *Noah* consisted only in those seven precepts commonly called the
*precepts of the sons of Noah*. Rather, it consisted in the same religion and
law by which *Abraham* pleased God, with the exception of circumcision. For that
law commonly called the law of the sons of *Noah* does not embrace the
catechesis and divine institution of the spiritual law of God which is
necessarily required so that the law may be our pedagogue to Christ and thus
lead us to salvation. The sons of *Noah* could not have enjoyed common
salvation with us through Christ unless they had been joined to God by a common
law, religion, and faith. Therefore those who attribute only the law of Nature
to the first two millennia of the world are deluded, as if the Church had then
been so rudely instructed that it was led and taught only with the aid of the
Natural Law, while only certain Prophets and Patriarchs had God's supernaturally
given word in an extraordinary manner. For the Church restored after the fall
was taught through the word by effectual calling, by the same rule which was
later expressed in the Scriptures; and it possessed the same faith and practice
of piety as the other Jews after the time of *Moses*, with respect to the
substantial articles of faith and the moral precepts of the divine law.”

Thus now, after Christ's advent, it remains in force under the New Testament and
binds to obedience. For Christ himself solemnly testified in Matthew 5:17 that
he had not come to dissolve the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them. He
fulfilled the Ceremonial Law by substituting the truth for the typical shadows,
and the Moral Law by rendering perfect and in every respect complete obedience.
But here he further declares that he also fulfilled this Law by his teaching,
that is, that he proposed it perfectly and completely according to the
legislator's meaning, as the purpose and circumstances of the passage clearly
indicate. For he immediately attacks the Scribes and Pharisees who corrupted the
law by their teaching, refutes their corrupt glosses, and proposes and explains
the law's true meaning. In verse 19 he says: “Whoever therefore relaxes one of
these least commandments and so teaches human beings will be called least in
the kingdom of heaven; but whoever does and teaches them will be called great in
the kingdom of heaven.” Christ and the Apostles never use, when speaking of the
Moral Law, the terms they use when speaking of the Ceremonial Law. Christ had
said, as we have just heard, that he did not dissolve the Moral Law; yet he did
dissolve the dividing wall of partition, Ephesians 2:14, and abolished or
abrogated—καταργήσας—the law of commandments set in ordinances, verse 15 and 2
Corinthians 3:11. Thus Scripture uses the following words concerning the
abrogation of the Ceremonial Law: *to change*, μετατίθημι, Hebrews 7:12; *to
become obsolete*, παλαιόω; *to grow old*, γηράσκω; *to disappear*, ἀφανισμός,
Hebrews 7:18; and *abrogation*, ἀθέτησις, Hebrews 8:13.

[1674 logical printed p. 118 | IA leaf 136 | PDF p. 136]

It never uses these or any other terms of the same force when speaking of the
Moral Law. See also the end of Romans 3.

Indeed, it is also inculcated under the New Testament, as is evident from James
2:8, 10–12; Romans 13:7–10; and Ephesians 6:1–3. In these places we see every
command of the second table urged and proposed as part of the rule of morals,
and it would be easy to show the same concerning the precepts of the first table
of the Decalogue. See the very learned Essenius in the place cited above,
disputation 6 under title 9, theses 52–53.

Polyander confirms the same thing with four reasons in *Synopsis of Purer
Theology*, disputation 18, thesis 38. 1. “Because it is the external image of
the internal law of nature impressed upon our first parents before the fall
through their creation in God's image, which is situated in holiness and
justice. The Decalogue represents the duties of those virtues to every human
being, and the Gospel demands them from the household of faith, renewed in
God's image through the Spirit of God.” 2. “Because this law, revealed to our
first parents, repeated and restored through Moses, was expounded not only by
the Prophets but also by Christ and the Apostles, observed by them, and strictly
commended to others,” Matthew 5:17 and following; Luke 10:17; Romans 6:13; 7:25;
12; and 13. 3. “Because the children of God are described by their observance
of the commandments of this law, as by a τεκμηρίῳ, ‘proof,’ and infallible
testimony of genuine union with God,” 1 John 1:6–7 and 2:3–6. 4. “Because love
from unfeigned faith and a good conscience”—which is the end of the
Decalogue—“is set before Timothy as the goal to which he ought to refer all his
actions,” 1 Timothy 1:5.

It remains now to ask: What is this Moral law?

Some describe it thus: “It is that law which is not typically ceremonial and
therefore binds perpetually.” These men express truly enough what it is not,
for it is abundantly plain from what was said in the preceding chapter that the
Moral Law is distinguished from the Ceremonial Law and that the latter finally
fades away and is abolished, while the former is not. But because they do not
state positively what this moral law is, they cannot give complete satisfaction.

Others say: “The Moral Law is that which binds all rational creatures at every
time.” Thus they hold that the Moral Law is the law which is perpetual and
universal. This definition or description is plain enough and quite suitable
for properly explaining the principal question concerning the sabbath. For the
adversaries deny that the precept concerning the sabbath is Moral or binds
everyone perpetually, while we affirm that it is Moral and binds everyone
always. Everyone agrees on this much: the Moral law binds Everyone and always.
And the adversaries would concede that this precept is Moral if it were once
proved to be perpetual and universal. Or if we demonstrated that it is
perpetual and universal—that is, that it constantly binds the whole human race
in every age—they would readily grant that it is a moral precept. Nevertheless,
because perpetuity and universality appear to be attributes of the moral law
rather than anything expressing its true essence, it remains to inquire further:
What makes the Moral law perpetual and universal? It is true that our curiosity
can be sufficiently restrained by the fact that it seemed good to God to enact
such a law as would bind all the descendants of *Adam* through every age, and
that by this consideration our vain and swollen mind can safely be restrained
from further inquiry. Nevertheless, because God Most Good and Great does
nothing without the best reason, it is lawful to inquire soberly whether—

[1674 logical printed p. 119 | IA leaf 137 | PDF p. 137]

—anything else can be assigned as the foundation of this perpetuity and
universality, in which the essence of the Moral law consists.

Those who hold that the Moral Law is the same as the Natural law, and that the
Natural law is nothing other than what right reason dictates or can dictate
without any external admonisher from internal principles of uprightness—or
what the light of nature, now corrupted, can prove to be just and necessary
without the Scriptures through the principles still implanted in human
minds—constrict the nature of the Moral law too closely and limit it more
narrowly than is right. For it would follow that this natural light, now quite
imperfect and blind, would be the competent, indeed the supreme or principal,
judge of the Moral Law, and that nothing pertains to this law except what our
very imperfect light perceives, or is able to perceive, as equitable and
necessary. It would also follow that no constraining necessity required this
law to be repeated through the ministry of *Moses*. It would further follow
that nothing now learned through training or teaching is Moral, but only what
is apprehended at nature's dictation. Indeed, many things would not now be
called Moral which were nevertheless natural to *Adam* in the state of
integrity and were vivid and effective impressions of the Law of Nature.

Walaeus rightly distinguishes, in *Dissertation on the Fourth Precept*, chapter
3, from Scotus, book 4, distinction 37, question 1, between the *Moral* which
belongs to the Law of Nature strictly taken and the Moral which belongs to the
Law of Nature broadly taken. He says that the former kind consists of the
practical principles which are known from the terms alone to every apprehending
intellect, together with the things evidently and necessarily deduced from
those principles. The latter kind consists of things very consonant with that
Law of Nature and those practical principles, or with the conclusions
necessarily deduced from them; these are called matters of positive right by
others. He says that moral matters of the former kind are so engraved that they
cannot be abolished unless nature itself is altogether abolished. But those of
the latter kind can quite easily fall into oblivion unless they
are cultivated and confirmed through right institution; when institution and
remembrance are added, however, they are generally retained because they are
recognized as agreeing excellently with the very principles impressed upon the
whole of human nature.

Others describe the Moral Law thus: “It is a law by which something is commanded
because it is good, but is not good merely because it is commanded.” They say
this description was given by Augustine in *On Free Choice*, book 1, chapter 3,
and that in this respect it is distinguished from Judicial and Ceremonial laws,
which are also called good in Deuteronomy 6:18, 24, but were not so prior to the
precept. For to slaughter and offer sacrifices in the worship of God, or to
perform any ceremonial duty, would have been simply evil without a preceding
command. But loving and worshiping God, preserving human life, and similar
things are good in themselves; and because they are good they are commanded,
and therefore are moral. Hence, they say, the Apostle calls the Moral Law “holy,
just, and good” in Romans 7:7, 12 and opposes it to his moral depravity and
carnality. Since that carnal nature which was contrary to the law was evil in
itself, the law which is opposed to that carnal nature must likewise be good in
itself. Thus Moral good is spoken of in Micah 6:8, namely, as good in itself.
Ceremonial good also existed, but not in itself; it was such only insofar as it
was instituted for other ends and to promote moral obedience, Jeremiah 6:19–20
and 7:22–23; Psalm 50:13–15; Isaiah 1:14, 16. They further confirm this as
follows: Since God's image is good in itself, and the moral law is—

[1674 logical printed p. 120 | IA leaf 138 | PDF p. 138]

—the exact standard of this image, as is plain from Ephesians 4:24, where God's
image is said to consist in *holiness* and *justice*—the first table is the
standard of the former, and the second of the latter—it follows that, since the
moral law embraces only the things which pertain to this image of God, it must
embrace only things good in themselves.

Although these points please our adversaries, who customarily speak in this
way, the authors of this explanation by no means favor them. For they explain
themselves so as to appear to support the adversaries in no respect, as will be
plain.

First, it must be noted that those who explain the nature of the Moral Law in
this way do not deny that God's good pleasure is the rule of all moral goodness.
Indeed, they affirm it. Thus they distinguish between the *will of decree* and
the *will of command*. They say that by the Former something—whether a creature
or a law—is made good; by the Latter, the practice of such a duty is enjoined.
Thus a law, if it is moral, is first good through God's wise good pleasure and
is then enjoined by the will of command. It therefore possesses a foundational
goodness before it is enjoined and becomes a moral good. They by no means hold
that anything is morally good or evil unless it is first commanded or forbidden
by law. For, they say, what is good or evil in itself must first be commanded or
forbidden before it becomes morally good or evil.

Second, by this Goodness and Evil which exist in actions commanded or forbidden
by the moral law prior to the law's enactment, they understand nothing other
than the agreement or disagreement which that action has with human nature
insofar as it is rational and not corrupted or vitiated. Thus those matters are
Moral which agree and are consonant with rational Nature as such. They say it
befits human nature *to worship God and honor parents*, and indeed, for the sake
of solemn worship, to consecrate or sanctify to God the most suitable proportion
of the time which he grants us to conduct our affairs.

Third, they therefore say that these moral goods, although they agree and
accord with rational nature, do not all agree with it equally and to the same
degree. Some are more immediately good and fitting, while others are so
mediately. They explain and confirm this by the words of Walaeus from Scotus
cited above. Hence they conclude that the precept concerning consecrating the
seventh portion of time to God is moral, although it depends upon the
legislator's will, is not inscribed upon the human heart quite so immediately,
and does not agree with human nature quite so well as love and gratitude toward
God. It certainly cannot be denied that some things participate more in God's
image than others, and consequently that some things are morally worse and less
fitting than others. For who will deny that cursing and hating God are far less
fitting for a Rational Creature than coveting another's goods, although both
are evil in themselves?

Those who follow this opinion, so explained, will therefore be pressed by no
difficulties, as is plain. And from this explanation of the moral law, it
clearly follows that this law is perpetual and universal, and that these are
inseparable attributes of the Moral Law. It must nevertheless be noted that
when they say this law is perpetual and universal, they understand this with
respect to God's ordinary dispensation. For they confess that God, the supreme
legislator, can dispense, and sometimes did dispense in an extraordinary case,
even in these laws. They confirm this by the example of *Abraham* and of marriage
among the children of *Adam*.

Finally, they wish it noted that when they call the Moral Law—

[1674 logical printed p. 121 | IA leaf 139 | PDF p. 139]

—perpetual and universal, they understand primary morals which first and
originally agree with human nature. For they assert that things which are such
only secondarily and accidentally can be changed more easily.

I describe the *Moral Law* thus: *It is a law, or sign of the divine will,
expressed in the Scriptures, which, concerning external actions or internal
motions of the mind that agree with rational nature as it is related both to
God and to one's neighbor—whether they can be referred to the natural law
strictly taken or not—binds everyone at every time from the moment it was first
given, but especially the Church.*

Why I call the *Moral Law* a sign is plain from what was said concerning law in
general in chapter 1.

I say that this sign is *expressed in Scripture* because Scripture contains
every law of God enacted for human beings and is therefore, in its own kind,
naturally fitted to render us perfect, as the Apostle teaches when writing to
Timothy. We must always recur to the Scriptures to become more certain of God's
will and constitution. Even the things inscribed upon human hearts are
expressed and urged more clearly and distinctly here; and other things which,
with natural light obscured by such great darkness, could be perceived only
with great difficulty or not at all are lucidly explained here.

When I say *expressed*, I do not wish this to be understood as if I excluded the
things which can be deduced from Scripture by clear and legitimate consequence,
because I judge that these too are expressed there, although not as clearly or
immediately as others.

I say *concerning external actions and internal motions of the mind* because
this *Moral Law* concerns the whole person. It regulates every motion of the
mind—the first and very first, as well as voluntary motions—the very disposition
of the faculties, and every external human action; and it prescribes what they
ought to be.

I say *agreeing with rational nature, and so forth*, because the formal
rationale of a moral precept is founded here, as we have already explained, yet
without excluding human nature as it is now corrupted.

I add, *whether they can be referred to the natural law strictly taken or not*,
to avoid the dispute about words arising from the various senses of the Natural
law. For certain things pertain to the Moral law yet cannot be said to be
commanded or forbidden by the Natural law strictly taken—that is, if it embraces
only things whose uprightness or agreement with rational nature can readily be
discerned immediately without the aid of any external instruction, teaching, or
training, as the scholastics speak; or principles reducible to the one heading,
*good is to be pursued and evil avoided*; or conclusions immediately deducible
from it. According to Aquinas, the latter two are the two heads of the Decalogue
proposed by Christ. They can nevertheless be reduced to the Natural law taken
more broadly, as is plain from what was said above.

I say *binds everyone at every time* because, as just explained, these things
are inseparably annexed to the Moral Law. In this it is distinguished from all
other Temporary and Occasional commands enacted for one person or a few people
at any particular time, as well as from the Ceremonial and Forensic Law, which
bind neither everyone nor always. But when we say that this law always binds,
we do not set limits upon our supreme Lord, as if he could not free anyone in a
particular case outside—

[1674 logical printed p. 122 | IA leaf 140 | PDF p. 140 | ends before CAP. XI]

—the order from its obligation, whether by a dispensation properly so called,
one improperly so called, or anything similar, as was said above.

I say *from the moment it was first given* because certain Evangelical laws
deserve to be called moral although they were not given to *Adam* before the
fall. Nor do certain adversaries deny this, as will appear later.

Finally, I add *especially the Church*. First, Scripture was given especially
to the Church as the rule of faith and morals, and through it the Church is
particularly bound to obey God, so that its disobedience is aggravated on many
grounds, since it cannot plausibly plead ignorance. Second, I would refer
Evangelical Faith and Repentance to morals as well. Although these are not
required from Nations among whom the Gospel has not yet been proclaimed,
Romans 10:14, they are required from everyone to whom Christ's Gospel has become
known and are most fitting for the corrupted state of human beings and for
rational nature—not because rational nature, now vitiated through sin, inclines
to them, but because they perfect it. Thus Vasquez speaks in *On Supernatural
and Infused Virtues*. And it is certain that the Church was bound to these and
similar things from the first promulgation of the Gospel in paradise, or of the
promise of the Messiah, and perhaps that some descendants of Apostates were as
well, although they were ignorant of this law. For ignorance of even human laws
once clearly given to their Parents does not entirely excuse descendants who
violate the law.
