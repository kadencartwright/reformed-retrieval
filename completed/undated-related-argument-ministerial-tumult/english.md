# On a Related Ministerial Tumult

*A fresh translation of the complete Latin-form witness in CO X/1 220–223.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 220]

If only I never had any other subject for writing to you than to give thanks
to the Lord himself for his gifts to you, and at the same time to exhort you
to continual progress. But Satan's wickedness causes me to feel that this
welcome subject has now been taken from me. For I have learned that he had
somehow breathed upon you, so that, if the Lord had not looked upon you and
brought help in his singular kindness, the whole edifice of the church would
have been threatened with utter ruin.

The evil indeed did not last long—but on whom did its stopping depend? Once a
fire has been kindled, what means is there of extinguishing it? Now, therefore,
since the flame has been put to rest by the Lord through his grace and the
work of his faithful ministers, I have resolved to guard you with an
appropriate warning for the future, lest the same thing happen to you again,
while also setting before your eyes the fault you committed.

I entreat you to take this service in good part. If I seem rather harsh to you,
as the matter will require, do not on that account be any less willing to
admit the rebuke, lest, if you do otherwise, the evil be doubled by you.
Anyone who belongs to the Lord's flock will, I trust, readily recognize who I
am and the affection for your salvation by which I am moved, since I carry
you in my bosom and in my very heart, just like my dearest children.

Nor does this proceed from any ambition. It is enough for me to be regarded
by you as a brother, and I cannot profess myself a servant of Christ without
sincerely acknowledging that I am also yours. It therefore concerns your
welfare that this warning of mine, by which I wished to provide for your
salvation, should not perish or be rendered useless to you through contempt.

First of all, this manner of proceeding is unheard of to me, when you speak
as follows: that it pleases you for that man to preach and to administer the
Supper to you. Certainly a minister ought

### [CO X/1, p. 221]

to be approved by the people before he is put in possession of the ministry.
But if anyone intrudes himself by another route where order has already been
established in a church, he lacks a lawful calling. Nevertheless, I deny that
it follows from this that the people ought to preside over the election of
their pastor.

Scripture testifies that pastors were appointed by the apostles throughout
towns and villages with the people's consent (Acts 14:24). But from this it is
clear that the apostles themselves presided over so important a business by
their wisdom and authority, governing the people so that everything was done
properly and without tumult. The same polity flourished in the Christian
church as long as order and discipline retained a place. Therefore, if you
claim for yourselves the right to choose pastors for yourselves at your own
pleasure, this is nothing other than overturning the order established by God
together with Christian propriety.

There is another evil as well: those rash changes and perpetual innovations
cannot fail to carry with them the surest danger and the worst aftermath. That
fault must be condemned outright. I know that by nature we seek what pleases
us, but it is far more prudent for that native itch to be extinguished once
and for all. This principle has continual application in human life, but
especially in the matter now at issue. By Satan's craft, day by day we are
seized with weariness of one pastor and then another, until the savor and
taste of God's Word vanish in us. And when we are carried along by an
uncertain and immoderate appetite, we no longer admit anything upright and
wholesome.

Now, grant that you did not lack some rationale and appearance, as they call
it. The manner of proceeding nevertheless does not cease to be defective.
But what rationale could there have been? When one of your men had departed
to visit certain churches, another was sent in his place with your judgment
and approval. He could not have been unknown to you, since he had preached
among you long before. Nor was the matter conducted by conjecture, since his
doctrine and life were established. If anyone despises him, he betrays
arrogance joined with pride against God.

What, then, does it mean that this same man—lawfully elected, having
faithfully discharged the office of pastor among you, and possessing the
gifts of God suited to that task—now pleases you less? Whom do you prefer to
him? An unknown man. What ground remains for clearing you now of the most
obvious rashness? You will reply, of course, that this man speaks much more
ornately. So this is how you remember Paul's statement that the kingdom of
God is not in speech but in power (1 Cor. 4:20)?

Therefore, as Paul confirms elsewhere, let that person alone be precious to
us who is a new creation in Christ (2 Cor. 5:17). Let us not cling to the
flesh and that outward appearance which dulls our eyes and prevents us from
judging sincerely, as we ought. We must beware, I say, of masks—that is, of
everything which puts itself on display outwardly, where deception very often
lies hidden. Instead, toward what is principal and true

### [CO X/1, p. 222]

let us direct ourselves, and plant our foot there. “But one must judge
favorably where no evil
appears.” I grant this when the question is whether someone should be
recognized as a brother. But when the question is appointing a pastor, a very
different examination is required.

Meanwhile, by these words of mine I would not wish prejudice to be created
against anyone, since a man is not worse in himself merely because he is
unknown to us. I condemn only this as full of fickleness and rashness: that we
dare employ for that place and that office a man whom we have not adequately
tested, without a lawful testimony about him preceding. Scripture wishes a
pastor to be upright in such a way that it also commands him to be recognized
and regarded as such. Indeed, if anyone thrusts and forces himself forward as
the man introduced by you has done, he has already branded himself with the
mark of ambition, so that no further investigation is needed.

I would certainly rather have my fist cut off than tolerate either being
admitted in that manner or, once admitted, administering the Supper, since
everything you did toward him in this affair was conducted through tumult.
Even a single precedent is of such importance. The example of Moses belongs
entirely to the office of a true pastor, when he wished that there were no one
among the Lord's people who was not a prophet (Num. 11:29). We must also
recognize—and, as far as lies in us, give place to—those who have received
from the Lord the gifts that contribute to the church's edification. I do not
doubt that this applies to our brother, as he openly showed when he admitted
that man even more readily than I myself would have done.

But let the matter be examined in itself. Who suggested to you that it lies
with the people to change a pastor rashly according as this man rather than
that one, or the discourse or ornament in speaking of one man or another,
captivates you more or less? Or who suggested that it lies with the people to
declare that it pleases them for a man to administer the Supper? Why do we not
rather banish canvassing of this sort to the cloisters of monks, since an
entirely different moderation befits God's church? You venture even farther
than is fitting if you render a verdict immediately after one sermon.

I do not think that the reproach for this fault applies to everyone. I can
persuade myself that even a single whisperer could have been the instigator of
this tumult. Nevertheless, since the Lord humbled you so that you were carried
away by that temptation, I would certainly have resisted you more forcefully
than our brother did. Just as I praise his moderation, I also would have
judged that insolent disturbance to be restrained by me with greater
severity.

There are also circumstances that aggravate the evil. You have a pastor being
held in prison. Does stirring up such disturbances while he is absent admit
of any excuse? You create such a tumult among outsiders that, although you
ought to have won them to yourselves by moderation alone, you now make
yourselves liable to their judgment, since you conduct yourselves so
precipitately in the administration of your church.

Nor ought you to be offended by this severity of mine. If I were among you, I
would press this public offense much more forcefully, whose importance

### [CO X/1, p. 223]

and perhaps its cause I weigh more exactly from a distance than you perceive
them amid all that noise and dust of contention. I altogether hope this of
you: that this will be so
received by you that you will also open my mouth to give thanks to God in your
name.

If I have brought you a moderate sadness, I do not regret it, as Paul says
(2 Cor. 7:8–9), provided that it is sadness according to God. That is, may you
so repent of the fault you committed that you act more prudently hereafter.
You know how much I desire to console myself by at the same time providing
you with an occasion for joy.

But when some evil has arisen, true friendship manifests itself when we do
not wink at faults but freely convict them, so that no corrupt or carnal
affection is mixed in. If anyone complains about me here, let him first see
whether he has seriously examined his own guilt, since the censure is so
burdensome to him.

Yet a far better hope concerning you sustains me, as I have had experience of
you thus far. Since you show yourselves obedient to God, I judge that you will
also prove teachable and will receive with an even and peaceful mind the
rebukes directed to you from his Word, so that whatever bitterness lies in
them may contribute greatly to inward health and the salvation of the soul.

Farewell, most beloved brothers. May the Lord cherish you all in the fear of
his name and the love of truth. May he give you continual increase in every
good and in the grace of his Spirit, so that his name may be glorified in you
more and more. Amen.
