QUESTION XVII.

THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT.

Does this command include a prohibition of the right of war and capital
punishment? Are αὐτοχειρία, *self-killing*, and dueling forbidden? We
deny the former and affirm the latter.

I. Because various cases can arise in which homicide may be either lawful or
unlawful and condemnable, the question arises as to what is forbidden by the
sixth commandment, *You shall not kill*, and what is not.

[Marginal heading: I. Judicial homicide is not forbidden.]

II. First, κατ’ ἄρσιν, *by way of denial*, *judicial homicide* is not
forbidden. It is exercised by the public authority of the magistrate against
private persons, who are punished by the sword, the noose, or some other form
of punishment. The magistrate is armed with the sword for this purpose, for
the punishment of evildoers (Rom. 13:4; 1 Pet. 2:14). This does not pertain
only to the heathen magistrates who held office at that time, but to all
magistrates as such, whether believers or not. Indeed, it especially concerns
believing magistrates, who as guardians of both tables ought to provide for
the observance of the law. This would not happen if they were not permitted to
punish the guilty, because when fear of punishment is absent, a window is
opened to every crime. The Anabaptists and Socinians therefore strive in vain
to wrest his sword from the Christian magistrate in this respect.

III. Not only does the magistrate’s office require this, but

[Printed p. 99 | Internet Archive PDF 127 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

public peace and tranquillity demand it. They could never be preserved if the
sword could not be drawn against the guilty so that the state might be purged
of such criminals.

IV. This public right is not opposed by *God’s command*, which concerns
private persons, not magistrates and ministers of God furnished with public
authority. Nor is it opposed by *Christian love*, which can love persons and
yet punish crimes. Indeed, it would conflict with the law of love to leave
criminals unpunished to the ruin of the commonwealth and the injury of the
good. Nor is it opposed by the oracle in Isaiah 11:6 about the wolf dwelling
with the lamb, because that passage does not treat the magistrate’s duty but
the duty of believers: those who were formerly cruel and savage are to grow
gentle after taking Christ’s yoke and live peacefully with one another.

Nor is it opposed by Christ’s words in Matthew 5:39–40 that *evil is not to be
resisted*, and so forth, because they treat only private vengeance, not public
vengeance. The latter is exercised in the name of God, who declares that
vengeance belongs to him. This is the sense of Paul’s words in Romans 12:17:
“Do not return evil for evil.” Nor is it opposed by Matthew 26:52: “He who
takes the sword shall perish by the sword.” For it is one thing to take the
sword without its being given, as all those who seize it from private
vengeance do and are condemned here; it is another thing lawfully to wield a
sword given by God, as magistrates do.

Nor is it opposed by Christ’s action in not condemning the adulteress (John
8:11), because Christ did not act on earth as a magistrate and judge, but as a
minister of the circumcision (Rom. 15:8). Nor is it opposed by the parable in
Matthew 13:29–30 about not uprooting the tares, because by the tares are
understood hypocrites, not open criminals. The passage does not treat the
magistrate’s duty and civil punishment, but the ministry of ecclesiastical
discipline. In exercising it, Christ does not will his servants to be so
severe that they uproot the believers themselves together with the
hypocrites.

[Marginal heading: The right of war is maintained against the Socinians and
Anabaptists.]

V. We make the same determination concerning the *right of war* against the
Socinians and Anabaptists, who think that it has been abolished under the New
Testament. Thus Smalzius says against Frantzius, controversy 6: “We deny that
it is lawful to wage wars and contend that this is unworthy of Christian
piety.” We maintain, on the contrary, that the right of war belongs to the
magistrate and can lawfully be exercised by him when a war is just and
necessary. We detest as sheer robbery unjust and reckless wars undertaken
without a just and necessary cause, from mere ambition or greed, in order to
enlarge the boundaries of an empire.

[Marginal heading: 1. From the Old Testament.]

VI. The reasons are as follows. 1. This was lawful in the Old Testament;
therefore, it ought to be lawful in the New, since the rationale of both is
the same in this respect. Socinus’s objection is not valid: that this was
formerly lawful because God had declared in many ways that he did not
disapprove of it at that time, but that under the New Testament he has in no
way declared, either through Christ or through his apostles, that he approves
of repelling invading enemies when joined with their death. For by the very
fact that Christ did not abolish but confirmed the authority of the
magistrate, he also approved the right to wage war. It belongs to the
magistrate to defend his subjects against unjust violence, and it is certain
that this sometimes cannot be done without war. Add that these matters do not
belong to civil law but to the moral and natural law, which Christ confirmed.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the New Testament.]

VII. Second, the same right is approved more than once in the New Testament.
First, John the Baptist, while preparing the way of the Lord, sanctions
military discipline with laws (Luke 3:14). When he exhorts the soldiers who
ask him what they ought to do to flee the wrath to come and obtain eternal
life, he does not command them to abandon military service. This would
certainly have had to be done if it were intrinsically vicious and unlawful.
Instead, he commands them to remain in it, to be content with their pay, and
to extort nothing from anyone. It does not help to say here that John speaks
only about the proper manner of

[Printed p. 100 | Internet Archive PDF 128 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

waging war and about military duty, but not about the duty of candidates for
eternal life. For if military life were hateful to God in the New Testament,
John would not have prescribed a rule for that life. He would simply have
condemned it and exhorted them to change their way of life. Thus the faith of
the centurion is praised by Christ (Matt. 8:10), the piety of Cornelius by
Luke (Acts 10:1), and Cornelius is honored by an angel’s testimony. Nor does
Paul refuse to protect his life and safety by a military guard (Acts 23).

[Marginal heading: 3. From the magistrate’s office.]

VIII. Third, the magistrate’s office necessarily requires this. The sword is
given to him as the avenger of crimes (Rom. 13:4; 1 Pet. 2:14). But if petty
robberies committed against a few are rightly punished, how much more
severely must the great and public robberies of those who try to devastate the
commonwealth and plunder regions be punished? The magistrate is bound to
undertake the defense of his subjects, care for public peace and safety, and
protect the laws against the disobedient and against open enemies who strive
to ruin them. These things cannot be done without a just war.

[Marginal heading: 4. From Revelation.]

IX. Fourth, Revelation relates and approves various wars of the godly in the
New Testament. The circumstances of the passages sufficiently demonstrate
that these are not to be understood only spiritually, but also of outward war
and a real shedding of blood. It must not be said that only the wars of the
unbelieving Gog and Magog are recorded here and do not concern Christians.
These things mutually follow one another: if the former are unjust offensive
wars, the latter must be just defensive wars.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

X. The statement about New Testament believers in Isaiah 2:4 does not stand
against the right of war: “They shall turn their swords into plowshares and
their spears into sickles”; nor does Zechariah 9:10: “The Messiah shall
overthrow the chariot from Ephraim and the horse from Jerusalem; he shall
destroy the bow of war.” It is one thing to speak about the spiritual and
inward peace of Christ’s kingdom, which is said to be procured through the
Messiah; it is another to speak about civil and outward peace, which has no
place here, since Christ testifies “that he did not come to bring peace but a
sword” (Matt. 10:34).

The meaning of these oracles is only that the spread of Christ’s kingdom is to
be accomplished not by carnal weapons, but solely by the preaching of the
gospel and the power of the Holy Spirit. Yet it cannot be inferred from this
that the magistrate is not permitted to wage war for just and necessary
causes, because the gospel does not abolish civil governments or the
magistrate.

XI. The apostles’ weapons with which they had to fight against Satan, the
world, and the flesh are indeed called spiritual and not carnal (2 Cor.
10:4), because they had been commanded to conquer the world not by outward
force but solely by the proclamation of the Word (Matt. 28:19). The weapons
of believers in spiritual warfare are likewise nothing but prayers and
tears. Yet it does not follow from this that the right of the sword and of
physical weapons does not belong to the magistrate. Just as the calling of
the apostles and believers does not abolish the magistrate’s office, so the
spiritual weapons with which believers fight do not abolish the physical
weapons the magistrate uses.

XII. The private duty of believers is one thing, and the public duty of the
magistrate another. Believers ought to be prepared to shed their own blood
rather than another’s, because the right of the sword and of war has not been
granted to them. The rationale of the magistrate’s duty, however, is
different. Since God has armed him to punish evildoers and to defend the
commonwealth and the church, God has also granted him the power to wage
necessary wars.

XIII. The passages objected from Matthew 5:39 and similar places do not prove
that every war is simply unlawful. 1. Nothing new is commanded there that
cannot be shown to have been commanded in the Old Testament, where war is
nevertheless approved. 2. What is said there is said to private persons
insofar as they indulge their own passions and disturbances in any way, not
to the public

[Printed p. 101 | Internet Archive PDF 129 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

authority appointed by God as an avenger unto wrath against the one who does
evil (Rom. 13). Thus the hatred of hostility and sheer vengeance is forbidden
there, not the care of justice.

XIV. James 4:1 indeed teaches that the origin and cause of wars is evil,
because it is always unjust on one side or the other. It does not follow,
however, that every war is evil on both sides, because the magistrate is
sometimes permitted to repel by force and avenge an injury inflicted upon
him.

[Marginal heading: II. Defensive homicide is not forbidden.]

XV. II. *Defensive homicide* is not forbidden when someone defending his
life against a violent and unjust aggressor kills another while observing the
restraint of blameless defense. For this restraint to be regarded as
observed, the following are required:

1. The attacker must unjustly assail and invade him.
2. The defender must be free of all blame, when every other way of escape,
   morally speaking, whether by fleeing or yielding, has been closed to him.
3. The defense must occur in the very encounter, not after it has ended.
4. Nothing must be done by him from an impulse of anger or a desire and zeal
   for vengeance, but solely with the intention of defense.

XVI. The reason is evident. Although it is not lawful to repay like for like
and avenge oneself, repelling force with force and defending oneself belongs
to the natural and perpetual law, especially when the assault is simply
violent and devoid of all public authority. This extends even to killing the
aggressor, although that death is not intended in itself, but only insofar as
we cannot otherwise protect our life and free ourselves from his unjust
oppression.

The civil laws not only approve this, as is evident from the Code on the
Cornelian Law concerning assassins and on the Aquilian Law—“All laws and all
legal systems permit force to be repelled by force”—but it is also gathered
that God himself not obscurely indicated this in the law. Exodus 22:2
presents a case of private defense from which a judgment can be formed about
the practice of that law: “If a thief breaking into a house is found in the
very act of digging through, or with a housebreaking tool, and dies after
receiving a wound, the one who struck him shall not be guilty of blood. But
if he has done this after sunrise, he has committed homicide and shall
himself die”—that is, if he could recognize that the thief came with the
intention only of stealing, not of killing.

XVII. This defense is wrongly extended to the preservation or recovery of an
often imaginary honor. The devil, who was a murderer from the beginning, has
erected an idol of this honor in the world so that human blood may be
sacrificed to it. This is so both because honor can be recovered but life
never can, and because such a killing would pertain not to lawful defense but
to unlawful vengeance.

It is rightly extended, however, 1. To the *defense of life*, whether one’s
own or one’s neighbors’, especially when they are joined to us by some closer
bond, such as parents, wives, children, friends, and the like. For the one who
does not repel an injury from another when he can is as much at fault as the
one who inflicts it. Yet the attacking person can be of such a kind—for
example, a father or a prince—that it is more fitting for the person attacked
to suffer death itself than to repel the injury by such a defense. 2. It is
rightly extended to the *defense of chastity*, whether our own or another’s.
Examples survive of courageous virgins who killed those attempting to violate
their chastity when they could not escape in any other way. Thus many laws
permit a father or husband to kill with impunity the violator of his daughter
or wife when caught ἐπ’ αὐτοφώρῳ, *in the very act*.

XVIII. Blameless defense is not forbidden in Romans 12:19, but private
vengeance, as the words themselves show: μὴ ἑαυτοὺς ἐκδικοῦντες, *do not
avenge yourselves*. The one who justly defends his life does so not by private
presumption, but by the public authority of the law of nature. The sayings
about loving enemies do not abolish the necessary defense of life, because
love of ourselves is the foundation of love for our neighbor. In Matthew
26:52 the Lord commands Peter to *put his sword back into its sheath, because
he who takes the sword shall perish by the sword*. This does not abolish a
just defense of oneself,

[Printed p. 102 | Internet Archive PDF 130 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

because Peter’s action bore the character not so much of defense—which would
have been useless against so great a multitude—as of vengeance. Further, he
had not awaited the Lord’s command, for the Lord had no need of such a
defender, but had acted too hastily.

[Marginal heading: III. Accidental homicide is not forbidden.]

XIX. Third, *accidental homicide* is not forbidden, whether committed in
one’s own person or in the person of another, provided that it is in no way
intended and that malice and fault are absent, as described in Deuteronomy
19:4–5. Hence refuges and *cities of refuge* were established for such
persons, into which those who had killed could withdraw, lest the *goel*, or
avenger of blood, seized by fury, destroy an innocent person before the case
had been heard. This is wrongly extended in the papacy to all kinds of
scoundrels, willful murderers, abductors of virgins, and the like, for whom
they will the asylum of sacred places to be open.

[Marginal heading: Κατὰ θέσιν, by way of affirmation, all private
homicide is forbidden.]

XX. Κατὰ θέσιν, *by way of affirmation*, *private homicide* is forbidden.
It is carried out by private authority and malicious intent, whether from
hatred and zeal for vengeance or from some other evil passion; whether it is
done by force, cunning, the sword, or poison; and whether directly by oneself
or indirectly through another, in the way David arranged for Uriah to be
killed through others. Hence Abraham would not have sinned against this law
if he had slain his son at God’s command, because he would have done it by the
supreme authority of the one who has the right of life and death over all.

XXI. The rationale for this sanction is threefold: divine, natural, and
civil. The *divine* rationale is that, since man is God’s image, this image
cannot be defaced without a manifest and sacrilegious injury to God, the
archetype, disposer of our life, and its sole Lord. The *natural* rationale is
that, since all things by their nature seek their preservation, those who
take a man’s life are rightly regarded as enemies of human nature. The *civil*
rationale is that civil society, which is injured by other crimes, is utterly
disturbed and overthrown by the crime of homicide.

[Marginal heading: Under this is included αὐτοχειρία, self-killing.]

XXII. Under the homicide condemned by the law we include, 1. αὐτοχειρίαν,
*self-killing*, so highly praised by the Stoics: the act of those who desert
this post without the will of the supreme Commander. For this commandment
forbids αὐτοφονία, *killing oneself*, no less than ἑτεροφονία, *killing
another*—a crime committed against oneself as much as against another.
Although the second table seems to be directed toward one’s neighbor, it must
nevertheless be regarded as pertaining no less to a man with respect to
himself, since everyone is his own neighbor. Indeed, since a man is bound to
love his neighbor as himself and protect his neighbor’s life as his own, the
very prohibition of injuring his neighbor’s life much more strongly forbids
injury to his own life.

XXIII. It is clear from the following that αὐτοχειρίαν, *self-killing*, is a
most grievous sin. 1. The αὐτόχειρ, *self-killer*, sins against *God* by
encroaching upon his *authority*, since he alone is Lord of life; against his
*goodness*, by which he so kindly preserved him alive to this point; and
against his *providence*, whose order he attempts to disturb. He sins against
*himself* by violating the natural inclination by which everyone loves
himself and nourishes and cherishes his own flesh (Eph. 5:29). He sins against
the *commonwealth* by destroying one of its citizens; against the *family* by
violently tearing away one of its members, perhaps its chief member, from its
very vitals and filling all his relatives with disgrace, pain, and grief; and
against the *church and religion* by branding Christian doctrine with a mark
of disgrace, bringing grief to all, a stumbling block to the good, and an
occasion of mockery to enemies.

It is contrary to the *law of nature*, which binds everyone to preservation;
to *trust* and piety toward God, which ought to receive from God every evil
either inflicted upon or threatening it; to *wisdom*, because the deed is
reached not so much by judgment as by madness and an insane impulse. Not even
Seneca could deny this in letter 70: “The wise man ought not to lend his hand
to his own punishment; it is folly

[Printed p. 103 | Internet Archive PDF 131 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to die from fear of death.” It is contrary to *justice*, because a man is no
more the lord of his own life than of another’s. He is a servant who has a
master, a soldier who has a commander; he is stationed at a post and must not
be a λειποτάκτης, *deserter*. He must not withdraw at his own decision,
but be released by another’s. Epictetus noted this very well when he commanded
men ἐκδέχεσθαι τὸν θεὸν, *to wait for God*, until he gives the signal
and releases us from this service.

It is contrary to *fortitude*, because it arises from impatience with an evil
inflicted or from fear of one that may be inflicted, either to remove the
former or forestall the latter. Hence Augustine rightly denies in *The City
of God*, book 1, chapter 21, that it belongs to greatness of soul, and says
that the one who is more able to bear a wretched life than to flee it is
rightly said to have a greater soul. It is contrary to the *consensus of the
wiser* among the heathen, both Greeks and Romans—*Pythagoras, Plato,
Aristotle, Seneca, Cicero*, and others—who confess that this is a crime. To
this pertain the examples of αὐτοχειρίας, *self-killing*, related in
Scripture, which are attributed only to wicked men: Saul (1 Sam. 31:4),
Ahithophel (2 Sam. 17:23), and Judas Iscariot (Matt. 27:5; Acts 1:18).

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXIV. The example of *Samson* in Judges 16 does not favor αὐτοχειρίᾳ,
*self-killing*, even though the collapse of the house he overturned crushed
him no less than the others. This was a singular deed accomplished by an
extraordinary impulse of the Holy Spirit. This is evident both from the
apostle in Hebrews 11:34, who says that Samson accomplished this by faith,
and from the prayers he offered to God to obtain extraordinary strength for
this act and from their being heard (Judg. 16:28). God added strength and
granted the outcome Samson desired, so that he might thus be a distinguished
type of Christ by producing a great slaughter of his enemies through his
death and breaking the tyranny pressing upon the neck of his people. Finally,
his end was not simply private vengeance, but the vindication of God’s glory,
religion, and the people, since he was a public person and an avenger raised
up by God for the people.

XXV. Nor do the examples of Eleazar, the brother of Judas Maccabeus (1 Macc.
6:43–45), who voluntarily rushed beneath the elephant and perished when it
crushed him, and of Razis, who killed himself with his own sword (2 Macc.
14:41–42), favor it, because they are drawn from the Apocryphal books. Nor is
it to be called courage, but the greatest timidity, if someone voluntarily
rushes to his total destruction on account of an uncertain evil.

Nor do those pertain here who exposed themselves to extreme dangers for
their country or friends and wished to purchase the peace and safety of
others by their own death. It is one thing to expose oneself to dangers under
the compulsion of necessity and the impulse of a particular calling from God;
it is another to bring death upon oneself. The person who lays down his life
for someone by not refusing to die for him must not immediately be regarded
as an αὐτόχειρ, *self-killer*. Otherwise, by laying down his life for us,
Christ would have been an αὐτόχειρ, which no one would say.

XXVI. Consider the action of those who destroy themselves together with
their ships by igniting gunpowder. This has sometimes been seen among sailors
who, when reduced to extremity, set fire to their ship so that they would not
be subjected to enemy tyranny, so that the instruments of war they possessed
would not fall to the enemy for their country’s destruction, and so that at
the same stroke they might inflict damage on the enemy by destroying them
together with the ship. Some theologians approve this action or excuse it
from αὐτοχειρίᾳ, *self-killing*, because they did not intend their own
death, but the slaughter of the enemy and the good of their country. Yet we,
with many others, can by no means approve it. By this act they directly and
voluntarily inflict death on themselves as its physical and moral cause, and
so cannot escape the crime of αὐτοφονίαν, *killing themselves*.

Moreover, in doing this they conflict with *trust* in God by limiting his
providence, as though he did not have a thousand ways of escape even when we
think our case nearly hopeless. They conflict with *Christian prudence*,
because they weigh certain and present destruction against an uncertain
possibility and outcome and will the former to outweigh the latter. Although
almost defeated, they themselves could become the victors; the

[Printed p. 104 | Internet Archive PDF 132 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

enemies could spare their lives; some could provide for their lives by
swimming away or by some other means. They conflict with *fortitude*, because
the brave course is not shamefully to desert one’s post from despair of
victory or safety, but to fight to the very end. They conflict with *love* and
gentleness toward helpless persons—the elderly, noncombatants, the sick, and
children—whom the enemies themselves could spare and usually do spare.

Such persons therefore cannot be acquitted of guilt in view of the apostle’s
rule that evils must not be done so that goods may result. No laws, whether
military or civil, can stand against the divine sanction. Although at times
such persons may comparatively be excused from so great a degree of guilt,
they cannot be excused from it entirely, whether they are said to act as
private persons or as public servants. Public authority cannot bind us to the
point of opposing divine authority. Neither the disadvantages imposed upon
the enemy nor the advantages procured for the country can be worth so much as
to acquit the conscience here. Nor can the intention to harm the enemy be
completely separated here from the most certain knowledge that they are
destroying themselves along with the enemy and bringing an undoubted death
upon themselves.

XXVII. It is good to return to God what was given by him, but only where it is
both demanded back from us and returned in the proper manner, not where it is
neither demanded back nor returned in the proper manner. It is granted that
death is sometimes better than life, but a death inflicted by others, not one
seized by us beforehand. Death can lawfully be desired, but not seized. Just
as life is taken up only at God’s decision, so it ought to be laid down only
when he leads the way. To scorn τὸ φοβερώτατον, *the most dreadful thing*,
when inflicted by God is brave, but voluntarily to rush into it is reckless
and insane. Aristotle teaches in *Nicomachean Ethics*, book 3, chapter 8, that
this belongs not to fortitude but cowardice. Although love of country, the
safety of others, and desire for immortality can impel someone bravely to
bear a death inflicted upon him, they ought not to have the same effect in
bringing upon him a death not inflicted.

XXVIII. It is one thing to suffer death, to permit one’s own killing, and
even bravely to offer oneself for it when God so calls; it is another to kill
oneself. The former is lawful in some case—Christ did something of this kind,
as did the martyrs and heroes—but the latter is not.

XXIX. The *handing over of one’s body to be burned alive*, of which Paul
speaks in 1 Corinthians 13:3, is not the reckless and empty act by which
someone voluntarily and without necessity exposes himself to death for
God’s cause. It is the necessary and holy act by which someone obeys God when
he calls him to martyrdom and does not withdraw from that calling by a lie or
denial of the truth from love either of himself or of the world, if he should
happen to be dragged before pagan tribunals by enemies of the gospel.

[Marginal heading: 2. Dueling is unlawful.]

XXX. Second, *dueling* is forbidden. This does not mean every single combat
or fight between two people, such as the necessary defense of life against an
unjust aggressor that we approved above. Nor does it mean one undertaken by
public authority for the common good, such as David’s combat against Goliath.
Rather, it means a fight between private persons undertaken directly and
voluntarily by agreement or compact, with the risk of death or mutilation. We
believe this must be condemned without qualification in both the one who
accepts and the one who issues the challenge, although the challenger’s guilt
is greater, because he directly attacks this command not to kill.

XXXI. No reason can be given that could make dueling honorable and lawful. It
cannot be *the defense of worldly honor and the avoidance of disgrace*,
because such honor is empty and obtains only among empty men. Injury to it
detracts nothing from true fortitude itself, which must not be preferred to
the honor owed to God. Nor can there be any danger of true disgrace when the
one who refuses to accept a duel adds as his reasons the divine prohibition
and the danger to his soul, with which no loss of honor can be compared. Add
that an injured reputation can be provided for by better and gentler means
than the death of the one who injured it. It cannot be the

[Printed p. 105 | Internet Archive PDF 133 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*defense of property*, because neither our life nor another’s ought to be
exchanged for other temporal goods. It cannot be the *manifestation of truth*
or *purgation from a charge*. Since there is no promise from God that he wills
to bear witness to the truth in this way, to consult or appeal to his
extraordinary providence in this manner is nothing other than to tempt God by
a diabolical invention. It cannot be the *execution of vengeance*, because
vengeance is denied to private persons, who would thus be judges in their own
case, and is lawful only for public persons, and that by the process of law.
How greatly the Jesuits *Lessius, Amicus, Escobar*, and others favor this
diabolical practice is evident from the excerpts of their moral theology,
published in 1666, propositions 11, 12, 15, and 16.

[Marginal heading: 3. Anger and hatred.]

XXXII. Third, external homicide and everything pertaining to it are not the
only things forbidden. Since the law is spiritual, anger, hatred, zeal for
vengeance, envy, insult, and whatever conflicts with true and sincere love of
one’s neighbor or with the lawful defense of oneself are also forbidden. All
these are not only condemned in the New Testament (Matt. 5:21), as the
Socinians wrongly maintain, but are already found forbidden and condemned in
the Old (Lev. 19:17–18; Zech. 8:16–17). Although only *effective* and external
homicide is punished capitally by civil law (Lev. 24:17), *affective* and
internal homicide nevertheless comes under the same judgment before God.

XXXIII. Because affirmative duties are contained in negative commandments,
this law also binds us to the defense, assistance, and love of our neighbor,
to gentleness, and to the pursuit of harmony and peace (Rom. 12:18). It is not
enough to injure no one; each must be given his due. It is not enough to kill
no one; we must strive in addition to preserve our neighbor’s life by every
honorable means possible. A lamp is extinguished in two ways: either by
violently blowing out its flame or by failing to supply oil. In the same way,
our neighbor’s life is destroyed either by violent extinction or by unjustly
withholding the alms and assistance by which it would have been preserved.
