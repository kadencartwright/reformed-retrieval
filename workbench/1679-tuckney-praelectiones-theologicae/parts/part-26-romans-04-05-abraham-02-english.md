Before examining the texture of all these things individually, we must
remove an objection which can be raised against Paul's argument. He appears
to argue badly and against dialectical rules, for they reject inference from
a particular to a universal, from a species to a genus, or from an
individual to a species. The negative inference, “Chorebus is not learned;
therefore no human is learned,” is bad, as is the affirmative, “Socrates is
learned; therefore every human is learned.” Does Paul argue any better:
“Abraham was justified by faith and not works of law; therefore every
believer who will be saved is justified in the same way”?

The objection is easily answered from both dialectical and theological
principles.

As to logic, properties belong immediately and primarily to species in
themselves, as the capacity for laughter belongs to humanity. Accidents
belong to species mediately through individuals in whom they primarily
inhere, as learning and erudition belong to Socrates, Plato, and other
learned individuals. Thus predicates of individuals are of two kinds. Some
belong primarily to individuals as such: accidents like learning, virtue,
riches, and similar things. Others primarily inhere in and belong to a genus
or species and are communicated from it to individuals, like sensation,
reason, the capacity for laughter, and similar things.

[1679 printed p. 169 | IA leaf 177]

If we speak of the former kind, individual accidents belonging to particular
individuals as such cannot be extended to all other individuals, the whole
species, or the universal genus. If Socrates as Socrates is learned, it does
not follow that Chorebus, Thersites, people like them, or all people are
learned. The contrary might rather be inferred: one, some, or most people
are unlearned, since Socrates, Plato, and a few others were so well endowed
or educated that they enjoy the distinction of being learned.

But if we speak of attributes in individuals which do not belong to them
immediately but are communicated from their genus or species and either
intimately constitute the specific or generic nature or necessarily follow
or accompany it, we can rightly and validly argue from some individuals,
even a single individual, to the whole species and every other individual
sharing the same specific essence.

We cannot conclude that Chorebus and all people are learned because Socrates
is learned, but we can rightly infer that absolutely every human is rational
and capable of laughter because Socrates is. Socrates is rational not as
Socrates but as a man, and everyone who is human is consequently rational,
because inference from something insofar as it has a nature to everything
having that nature is valid.

The present case is the same. If justification by faith were an individual
attribute of Abraham as Abraham and peculiar to him alone, Paul would reason
very badly from his example to everyone else. But since it is essential and
specific and intimately concerns the nature and essence of justification,
as will soon appear, the argument from Abraham to everyone justified in the
same way is entirely valid, with the dialecticians' hearty approval.

Theological principles make this still clearer, for Paul's argument is
correctly drawn from parity and even from the stronger case.

First, as regards the mode of justification, the way and rationale are
entirely similar and equal for everyone justified, both in the matter and
the person.

1. As to the matter, there is one and the same way of justification under
law and gospel for Abraham and all his children, Jewish or Gentile. Where
the rationale is truly the same for everyone, there can be no difference in
this respect, and we can argue from one to all. In the parable of the
laborers, it is easy to collect what all received from what any one received,
since everyone had the same wage and each received a denarius (Matthew 20).

The rationale here is likewise one for all. Clement of Alexandria says Jews
were justified by law and Gentiles by philosophy. Libertines have similarly
constructed different modes of justification and different ladders to
heaven: one for Gentiles, another for Jews, and another

[1679 printed p. 170 | IA leaf 178]

for Christians. Better instructed by Scripture, however, we have one Lord
and one faith, both the faith believed and the faith by which all of us
believe unto justification. The external sacraments and symbols under the
Old Testament differed from those under the New, but the substance on both
sides was the same: the same covenant of salvation and, substantially, the
same faith in the same Christ, by which they and we attain righteousness and
life.

Peter says in Acts 15:8–9, “Giving them the Holy Spirit as also to us, he
made no distinction between us and them, purifying their hearts by faith.”
Paul likewise says in Romans 3:25, “Whom God set forth as a propitiation by
faith in his blood, to demonstrate his righteousness because of the passing
over of former sins,” that is, “the transgressions under the first covenant”
of Hebrews 9:15. Paul speaks of believers' sins under law and before Christ's
appearance, as Beza, Louis de Dieu, and others rightly judge, which the
contrast with the following verse sufficiently establishes.

By the same propitiation, the same Christ who was the true and sole mercy
seat, and through the same mediating faith, both the sins of the fathers
before Christ and the sins of all of us under the gospel are expiated. I may
say more about this elsewhere. For the present it is enough that Abraham and
all of us share the same path of justification. Thus the rationale is equal
as to the matter.

2. It is also equal as to the person, both the one who justifies and those
who are justified.

As to the justifying person, God, several things readily establish that he
uses the same method to justify Abraham and everyone else.

1. He is the one and same God. Various people can disagree about the same
matter, but the one and same God cannot disagree with himself. I acknowledge
that the one God could make the same thing known in many parts and perform
it in many forms without any loss to his unity. I would therefore have
abstained from this point had Paul himself not employed it and appealed to
God's unity in Romans 3:30: “Since God is one, who will justify the
circumcision from faith and the uncircumcision through faith.” Here Paul
either proves or at least illustrates the one identical rationale of
justifying Jews and Gentiles from the unity of the God who justifies.

Someone may object that the passage distinguishes Jewish and Gentile
justification, the former being “from faith” and the latter “through faith.”

Burgensis tries to distinguish justification “from” and “through” faith.
Long before him Origen thought this change of prepositions was not
superfluous in God's speech and should not be idly passed over, any more than
other passages where *from* and *through* appear distinct in meaning—for
example, Romans 11:36, “From him, through him, and in him are all things,” or
1 Corinthians 11:8, “Woman is from man, and man through woman.” Since *from*
seems to him to denote the primary and principal cause, he says Jews are
justified from faith because they were justified first in order, while
Gentiles are justified through faith because they were drawn to
justification second.

[1679 printed p. 171 | IA leaf 179]

This is characteristically curious and subtle. What he adds is, even by his
own confession, curious and in reality most harmful and Pelagian: those
justified from faith begin from faith and are completed by fulfilling good
works, while those justified through faith begin from good works and receive
the height of perfection through faith.

Calvin also hints at another possible distinction. Jews are justified from
faith because they are born heirs of grace as the right of adoption is
transmitted to them from the fathers; Gentiles are justified through faith
because the covenant comes to them from outside. The English Annotations
offer something similar: the Jews are justified from faith because, though
adorned with other gifts and graces, God regarded only their faith in
receiving them into favor; the Gentiles are justified through faith because,
though ungodly in themselves, through faith they are transferred into a new
spiritual state.

But Augustine's statement in *On the Spirit and the Letter* 29 applies: “From
faith and through faith are said not to signify a difference, but for
variety of expression.” Interpreters generally follow him. Throughout Paul's
discussion, being justified by faith, from faith, and through faith are used
everywhere for the same thing. We read that Jews are justified not only from
faith but through it (Galatians 2:16), and Gentiles not only through faith
but from it (Galatians 3:8, 14).

Distinguishing these phrases here is therefore excessively subtle and
entirely curious, indeed contrary to Paul's purpose, which is to affirm
exactly one rationale of justification for both Jews and Gentiles. Why not
call the distinction harmful, since it corrupts gospel doctrine and the
church's peace? A directly similar distinction between these very
prepositions caused immense disturbances when Romans or Westerners insisted
the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, while Greeks said from
the Father through the Son.

Leaving these subtleties, we may safely and soberly refer the variation of
phrasing with Augustine to variation of speech. Calvin suggests Paul
delighted in verbal variety while expressing the same thing. Peter Martyr
takes it as emphasis: Paul more vehemently and effectively asserts that the
entire justification of both Jews and Gentiles rests upon faith alone.

Similarly, in Romans 11:36, which Origen rashly and dangerously adduces for
his theory, the two phrases “from him” and “through him are all things” both
signify that God is the cause, maker, and author of all. We might finally add
Calvin's view, followed by Diodati, that Paul varied his expression slightly
to rebuke the folly of Jews who invented a distinction between themselves
and Gentiles though they did not differ in justification. If people become
partakers of this grace by faith alone, and faith is one in both,

[1679 printed p. 172 | IA leaf 180]

it is ridiculous to create dissension amid such similarity. Calvin therefore
judges that the words contain irony, as though Paul said: If anyone wants a
difference between Gentile and Jew, let it be that the former obtains
righteousness through faith while the latter obtains it from faith.

You will, I hope, readily pardon this brief incidental excursion intended to
shed some passing light on the passage. I return to the road. Abraham and
everyone else justified have the same rationale as regards the justifying
person: the one God.

2. He is also “in one,” as Job 23:13 says—*be'echad*, according to the
learned interpreter Mercer, “in one matter, purpose, and opinion”—always the
same and consistent with himself. Interpreters teach that in the Romans
passage Paul speaks not so much of unity of essence as unity of purpose.

Humans are frequently changeable and fickle. Ignorance of future and often
adverse events and their own inconstancy makes them revoke what once seemed
most prudent. But with the Father of lights there is no variation or shadow
of turning. Since he is omnipotent and knows all things beforehand, he
always wills the same, being always the same.

This is especially so, 3. if by decree he wills to use the same method in
doing or accomplishing something. That applies here: he decreed to justify
Abraham and all his children, Jewish and Gentile, by the same rationale and
path, namely faith alone. Thus Paul rises to God's foreknowledge in Galatians
3:8: “Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith,”
just as he had previously justified Abraham by the same faith in verse 6 and
therefore all his children in verse 7. In the unity of God's essence, the
constancy of his will, and the immutability of his decree, Abraham and
everyone justified are consequently in precisely the same position, at
least as regards the justifying person.

They are also in the same position as the persons justified: Abraham and all
other believers.

1. Everyone has the same natural depravity and inability to perform good
works in order to be justified by them (Romans 3:23; 8:3–4).

2. Paul presents Abraham not simply as one among the faithful but as the
church's father. He deliberately and significantly calls him “Abraham our
father,” so that he can validly argue from father to children according to
the nature of those correlates. They have the same right to the inheritance
and one identical way of entering it.

From the example of the one Abraham, selected from all fathers, Paul intends
to draw a conclusion necessarily comprehending every believer, because
Abraham was a form and type of the rest. Calvin says excellently: “In many
other matters one example would not suffice for a common rule. But because
Abraham's person was set forth as a mirror or example of the righteousness
which commonly belongs to the whole church, Paul rightly accommodates to the
whole body of the church what was written of that one man.”

[1679 printed p. 173 | IA leaf 181]

Still more significant and pertinent is that Paul deliberately calls him
“our father” and repeats the word five times in this chapter: not father of
Jews only (verse 12), but of all who believe (verse 11), of us all (verse
16), and of many nations (verse 18).

His being our father and the father of every believer contains not only the
correlation from which we derived parity in justification, but also means
that under Christ and in his own manner he was a head of the covenant of
grace. Although made under him, that covenant was equally struck with him
and all his seed: “I will establish my covenant between me and you and your
seed after you throughout their generations, as an everlasting covenant, to
be God to you and your seed after you” (Genesis 17:7).

Luke's genealogy of Christ ascends to Adam, the first human, but Matthew's
does not ascend above Abraham. Above other men Abraham was father of the
faithful, and the covenant of grace was more solemnly and specially renewed
with him and in him with all his seed. It was first revealed to Adam in the
first gospel of Genesis 3:15. Yet Adam was the head of the covenant of works
and through violating it became to us the father of sin and death, in which
respect he is opposed to Christ in the great comparison of Romans 5.

Christ is properly the head of the second covenant and Father of the coming
age (Isaiah 9:6). Yet because the covenant was first established with
Abraham in a more solemn revelation and sacramental sealing, Abraham in his
order and place is called its head and believers' father. Thus Romans
11:16–17 calls him the root and true olive tree into which we Gentiles are
engrafted after Jewish branches are broken off, becoming partakers of the
root and fatness: the same grace Abraham possessed as in the root. He was
therefore like a root and head.

Hebrews 13:5–6 communicates to all believers the same promise made to Joshua
in Joshua 1:5, because Joshua was a type of Christ, in whom all God's
promises are Yes and Amen. In the same manner and order this can be said of
Abraham, with whom God made his covenant not in his name alone but in the
name of all his seed who share the same covenant and grace. Paul therefore
legitimately and validly concludes from Abraham's manner of justification
that all of us are justified in precisely the same way. The words concerned
not him alone but every believer, as Paul explicitly states in Romans
4:23–24: “It was not written for his sake alone that it was imputed to him,
but for ours also, to whom it will be imputed, namely those who believe,” and
so forth.

Since the rationale is equal on both sides, Paul's argument is excellently
drawn from parity.

More than this, 2. it is drawn not only from parity but from the stronger
case, as though Paul said: If Abraham himself was justified by faith alone,
then much more are all of us. This applies both to the matter and the people.

1. As to the matter, Abraham was in reality an exceptionally holy man of
God, furnished with the most splendid virtues and performing outstanding
works. Scripture

[1679 printed p. 174 | IA leaf 182]

therefore describes him as remarkably righteous and proposes him to us as
an example of righteousness. If any mortal were to be justified by works,
he above others would be. This is the force of the question, “What shall we
say our father Abraham found?” If anyone could demand a reward by the merit
of works instead of begging and receiving a free gift, it would especially
be Abraham. If so great and righteous an Abraham could not be justified by
works, much less can the rest of us who are weak and far inferior—indeed, no
one at all.

Chrysostom argues this way on the passage, as does his follower Theophylact,
who says that Paul proves from the greater Abraham that even this man who
accomplished so many and such great things was justified from faith, not
works. If Abraham himself could not be justified by works or any way other
than faith, none of his descendants should expect salvation from another
source or their works—unless some pygmy hopes to accomplish and surpass what
Hercules himself could not. This is therefore an extremely strong argument
from the greater or stronger case, first as to the matter.

2. As to the persons, it is an argument against the Jews whom Paul had among
his chief adversaries in this cause. Abraham was not greater—rather less—in
himself than he was commonly regarded among the Jews. Theophylact calls him
“celebrated among the Jews.” Their veneration of him approached deification,
so that they commonly called the place of the blessed “Abraham's bosom.”
They boasted in him above all others (Matthew 3:9; John 8:39), and when they
wanted an example of justification they relied especially upon his
authority. Once it was established that even he could not be justified by
works, the whole matter was settled. They would not dare answer, much less
claim for themselves or others what their very chief lacked.

Paul rebukes them no less prudently than powerfully, and no argument could
move and restrain them more effectively than Abraham's example. Calvin says
excellently: “He strikes the Jews, for whom no boast was more pleasing than
calling themselves Abraham's children, yet they would never dare attribute
more holiness to themselves than to the holy patriarch. Now that it is
established that he was justified freely, his descendants who arrogate
their own righteousness from law must be overcome by shame and fall
silent.”

Ambrose likewise asks: “How, then, do the Jews think themselves justified by
works of law through Abraham's justification, when they see Abraham
justified not by works of law but by faith?”

These extensive remarks sufficiently answer the objection. Although Paul's
argument moves from a particular example to establish a general doctrine,
it is abundantly clear that a thoroughly valid foundation supports it.
