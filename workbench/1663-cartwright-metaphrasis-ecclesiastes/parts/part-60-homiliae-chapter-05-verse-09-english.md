VERSE 9. Now the profit of the land is for all: the king is served from the
field.

VERSE 10. The person who loves money is not satisfied with money, and the
person who loves any abundance will not be satisfied with its yield. This too
is vanity.

VERSE 11. When this good thing is plentiful, there are many who consume it.
What advantage, then, comes to its owner apart from the sight of his own eyes?

VERSE 12. The sleep of the person who labors is sweet, whether he has eaten
little or much; but the rich man's fullness does not permit him to sleep.

The summary is that the goods which are called goods of Fortune, however much
some of them may excel others, nevertheless all have vanity inherent in them.

The parts are the superiority of agriculture to money, verses 9–10, and the
vanity of agriculture itself, even when it supplies everything abundantly for
food and clothing.

The word *Ærez* is taken here for cultivated land, as the word *sadeh* in the
same verse teaches, and not for either the whole earth or the earth's
inhabitants.

VERSE 9. Now the profit of the land is for all: the king is served from the
field.

In agriculture, as an occupation and condition of life which supplies the
greatest support for preserving this life, a synecdoche appears to include
animal husbandry too, as well as the other kinds of life without which people
cannot maintain this life; for agriculture is the chief and first of them all.
Although animal husbandry brings great assistance to the cultivation and
sustenance of life, about two thousand years had passed before its use for
human food was permitted. Agriculture, by contrast, was commanded by God from
the time of Adam's fall as supremely necessary. Thus it also happened that the
task of cultivating the field was given to the firstborn Cain, while animal
husbandry was allotted and assigned to Abel as less necessary.

He therefore commends this condition of life, which supplies us with food and
clothing, from its accompanying

profit, which it brings with it for the preservation of life, and without
which you could not even live, much less live comfortably. The single fruit of
the earth received in bread is so important for preserving this life that it
is said to strengthen a person's heart, Psalm 104, and is therefore also
called a staff and support, Isaiah 3. He amplifies this from its subjects,
because it benefits everyone, and there is no one whose own strength and
physical powers are sufficient to let him go long without this support. For
amid so great a variety of foods, none can be found which all mortals desire
equally except bread. You may find many who have never tasted meat, others who
abhor fish, and some abstainers who shun wine; almost everyone takes pleasure
in bread. Solomon therefore had good reason to say that cultivation of the
earth is profitable to all.

A still more illustrious proof of this appears in Jacob and his sons. Although
they abounded in the other fruits of the earth, they declared that they were
as good as dead because they lacked grain, unless they went to Egypt for the
sake of grain; yet they undertook that journey only at risk. Genesis 43.

He confirms this by a comparison with the greater case. Not even the king
himself, who abounds in such a supply and variety of everything, can lack this
support of life. So far is he from being able to maintain royal provision and
splendor without it that without it both he and all his people must perish.
Hence Solomon says here that the king is in a certain sense bound to the field
and the soil as if to servitude.

To pass over in silence the examples supplied by secular sources—people
chosen from the plow for supreme command who, after completing the highest
offices, returned to the plow and cultivation of the earth—Holy Scripture
testifies that the revenues of the king of Egypt consisted in livestock.
Genesis 47. In the description of the restoration and renewal of the Church
at Christ's coming, when it assigns each tribe its portion in the land, it
also defines the possession of the kings of Judah, together with those of the
priests and Levites, so that they may not cross beyond it. Although this is
said figuratively, its intention is nevertheless plain: the kings' principal
revenues consisted in the fruits of the land which had fallen to them.

Hence there is the Prophet's statement about the time of the royal mowing,
Amos 7:1. The account of the king's wife in Proverbs 31 also pertains here:
through her own industry she provides not only for all the household goods and
the clothing of the body, but is also said to buy a field which she plants
with vines. Uzziah too, who acquired the name Azariah from God's singular
bounty toward him, is reported by the history to have been most devoted to
agriculture and animal husbandry. 2 Chronicles 26.
