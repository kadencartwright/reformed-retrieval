XXXV. In § 4 of the same chapter, *Paradox*, in order to establish the
obscurity of Discourse, asserts that one and the same Discourse has various
and multiple meanings. He therefore distinguishes between the *meaning simply
so called*, which, as he says, the words carry on their face, and the *true
mean-*

[Page 149 | ONB canvas 203 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000203]

*ing* which the author intends. But *Wolzogen*, in order to undermine
everything which he was afterward to say about this matter, openly admits on
page 106 that *he very readily approves this distinction.* For a Discourse which
has two meanings, one simply so called and the other true, cannot but be
ambiguous to that extent. He indeed says rightly on page 107: *[In the
Interpretation of a Discourse, it is not examined what the words mean in
themselves, but what they ought to mean here. For there is a great difference
between the signification of a word considered apart from every discourse and
only in its first origin, and the signification of the same word when it
appears in the midst of a Discourse, determined by its various construction
and many circumstances.]* From this it is clear that although words or certain
parts of a discourse considered simply and abstractly can have a proper
meaning, when they are employed figuratively or tropically in a discourse,
they have in that discourse no other meaning than that metonymical,
metaphorical, or similar one, and not that meaning simply so called. Therefore
that Discourse has only one meaning, namely, that tropical or figurative one.
*Wolzogen* himself indeed admits this on page 109: *[On the contrary*, he says,
*it must be denied that there is a double meaning in a Discourse of this kind.
For there is no common and general usage where it has been confined according
to the laws of interpretation by the various circumstances of the text and
forced into a particular meaning.]* Since he was going to say these things,
why had he approved the *Exercitator's* distinction? Is this not to betray the
cause far too openly?

XXXVI. In the sixth paragraph of the same chapter, *Paradox* mentions another
distinction, namely, that between Logical and Ethical Truth. He says that the
truth of a Discourse depends upon the former and its true meaning upon the
latter. *Wolzogen* does not reject this distinction either, on page 110. The
one thing he cannot bear is that Ethical truth should be trans-*

[Page 150 | ONB canvas 204 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000204]

*ferred* to the true meanings which the Interpreter ought to expound. And why?
*[For*, he says, *the Interpreter does not labor to know whether some author
believed or did not believe what he wished to expound. Whether he wrote
contrary to his own mind or faithfully expressed the sentiments of his soul
is nothing to our Interpreter, who will never ascertain—and is bound by no care
to ascertain—whether the words and expressions of the speaker or writer
correspond exactly to his thoughts. This concern alone lies upon him: that he
be able to investigate what words and expressions signify from usage.]* By
this reasoning he teaches how one must proceed in Interpreting his own
writings: namely, that one must investigate what his own words and expressions
signify from usage. For through Writings we shall never understand what the
Writer believes or does not believe. There will be no way for us to establish
that he did not write contrary to his own mind all things, or most things, or
at least those few things which agree with the truth, and that he did not write
quite a few other false things according to the judgment of his own mind. Nor
can any basis be imagined upon which we could be certain of his Orthodox faith;
for although he writes most orthodoxly (which I do not yet see that he has
done) and most plainly, doubt about the sentiments of his own mind will always
remain. Indeed, although he confirms everything with the greatest
asseverations, we shall not yet be freed from doubt. For those asseverations or
assertions must be Interpreted in the same manner; that is, in Interpreting
them, one must inquire only what the words and expressions signify from usage,
and must not investigate whether those words and expressions correspond
exactly to his own thoughts. But to return to the matter: Was there nothing
else to be faulted here? Does *Wolzogen* think this distinction relevant, or
that it has anything to do with the Interpretation of Scripture, to which the
*Exercitator* applies it together with the aforesaid distinction in the
following paragraph, namely, paragraph 7? Does he think that there is in the
Sacred Writings an Ethical truth distinct from Logical truth, or that the
latter is distinct from the former? If not, why did he not,

[Page 151 | ONB canvas 205 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000205]

even on this ground, reject it? Why did he not reject it as altogether futile,
wholly irrelevant, indeed utterly false, in this business which was at hand?
Why did he not except the Interpretation of the Sacred Writings? Why did he
say so universally, with no limitation or exception added, that the
Interpreter ought by no means to investigate what the author's mind was, nor
what it was that he wished to express, but only what the words and expressions
signify from usage, unless he had in mind to open a door to his adversary and
abandon the cause?

XXXVII. Applying the aforesaid distinctions, the *Paradoxical Exercitator*
begins thus in paragraph 7: *[Three things, therefore, in a Discourse, as
regards signification, must be carefully distinguished: namely, the meaning
simply so called, the true meaning, and truth. And a distinct consideration of
these meanings and of the perspicuities and obscurities arising from them will
lend no slight light to the aim of our Exercise; whereas neglect of them has
led Interpreters of the Sacred Writings and Theologians into Labyrinths of
errors and Meanders of delusions.]* He illustrates this in the Papists, who
take the meaning simply so called of these words, *This is my body*, which is
clear and at first sight obvious and perspicuous, for the true meaning, with
respect to which, he says, the passage is obscure. And in these propositions,
*God is Lord of heaven and earth, King, reigns, begot, loved, etc., in which*,
he says, *the meaning simply so called of those expressions is easily
gathered, but the true meanings will require deeper investigation.* With
respect to these meanings he wishes these things predicated of God to be not
perspicuous but ambiguous. And at last he concludes thus: *[Our distinction of
the meanings, therefore, and, with respect to them, of the perspicuities and
obscurities, must be carefully maintained by them if they do not wish at once
to fall most shamefully and strike against the rocks of the most absurd
Interpretations.]* What does *Wolzogen* say to these things? Thus he writes

[Page 152 | ONB canvas 206 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000206]

on page 111: *[Hence I conclude that those three things which the author so
carefully distinguishes in paragraph seven—namely, the meaning simply so
called, the true meaning, and truth—can indeed have some use, so that we may
know what must be removed from Interpretation, but nevertheless bear nothing
immediately upon our Interpreter. For the Interpreter seeks none of these
things by virtue of his office: not the meaning simply so called, which,
indeed, even by the judgment of the Exercitator himself, is empty; not the true
meaning which agrees with the author's mind, which we do not know whether the
author could have expressed in sufficiently suitable Discourse, while we can
judge only from the Discourse itself; not truth, for even if there is no truth
in a Discourse, the Discourse can nevertheless be consistent with itself. For
we are not yet speaking here about divine Discourse.]* O how excellently this
champion argues. This champion—or rather betrayer! The adversary speaks
expressly about divine Discourse, or about the Interpretation of Scripture, as
is evident from the passages cited above, although not until chapter 4 did he
descend to the object to be treated more particularly, namely, Sacred
Scripture. And he says that the true meaning, not the meaning simply so called,
must be investigated. But *Wolzogen* expressly denies that even the true
meaning which agrees with the author's mind must be investigated, as if the
discussion here were not about divine Discourse. What else is this than to
flee the dispute, or the pitched battle with his adversary, or to betray the
cause far too openly? Indeed, does he speak less Orthodoxly about the
Interpretation of the Sacred Writings than *Paradox* himself? What did
*Wolzogen* reply to the examples which the *Exercitator* had adduced to
illustrate the matter? Not even a grunt. *Paradox* was saying that the meaning
simply so called is perspicuous and the true meaning obscure. What did
*Wolzogen* say to these things? Did he have nothing to reply? Or did he judge
it to have been said truly and accurately? But a meaning which is no meaning
at all cannot truly or accurately be called perspicuous; nor can that mean-*

[Page 153 | ONB canvas 207 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000207]

*ing which is tropical be called obscure, although to someone blinded by
prejudices it may not be able to appear at first sight.
