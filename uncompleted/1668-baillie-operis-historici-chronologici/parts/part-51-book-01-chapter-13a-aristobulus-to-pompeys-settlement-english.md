### CHAPTER XIII

#### On the events of our seventh epoch, from the kingship of Aristobulus in Judaea to the birth of Christ

Just as the thickest darkness is accustomed to precede the brightest morning,
and as twilight approaches the darkness is generally greater than at midnight,
so the century preceding the most glorious and blessed birth of the Lord
surpassed every preceding century in the gloom both of its iniquities and of
its calamities.

> Marginalia, printed p. 287: *The century before Christ was the most
> wretched*.

Among the Jews, the people of God, affairs then stood as follows. When
Aristobulus, the first king from the Hasmonaeans, died in the very first year
of his reign, his wife Salome, whose surname was Alexandra, a woman of the
greatest shrewdness, released her husband’s three surviving brothers from the
chains into which Aristobulus had cast them. She placed the eldest of them,
Jannaeus, surnamed Alexander, both upon the king’s throne and in his marriage
bed.

Alexander’s first deed was to kill one brother who was plotting against him.
He permitted the other, who had a more peaceable disposition, to pass his
life in tranquillity. He himself, touched throughout his life by exceedingly
little concern for the high priesthood, devoted himself so entirely to his
kingdom that he breathed nothing but Mars and arms. After subduing many
neighboring towns of Syria, he besieged Ptolemais. The besieged summoned
Ptolemy Lathyrus to their aid from Cyprus, where he was living while avoiding
the violence and intrigues of his mother Cleopatra. When Ptolemy set out with
a great army, the besieged feared that Ptolemy would punish them more severely
than Alexander. They repudiated his aid and refused to admit him into the
city. Alexander had lifted the siege from fear of Ptolemy, but, observing the
alienation of the people of Ptolemais from Ptolemy, entered into a treaty of
friendship with him. He agreed upon forty talents so that Ptolemy would join
arms with him against his enemies. Meanwhile Alexander was negotiating
secretly with Ptolemy’s mother and bitterest enemy, urging her to make ready
the forces with which she might drive her son from Syria back into his former
hiding places in Cyprus. When Ptolemy discovered Alexander’s treachery, he
turned his forces against him. He forcibly captured several cities of
Galilee, killed many thousands of Jews, and marched toward Jerusalem.
Alexander met him at the Jordan with eighty thousand men. Both sides fought
vigorously, but at last Alexander fled after thirty thousand of his men—or,
according to others, fifty thousand—had been slain. Whenever Ptolemy found
people in the villages, he ordered them to be cut limb from limb, cooked, and
roasted, so that he might strike the Jews with greater fear for their people,
as though his men inspired the fear “of cannibals” (ἀνθρωποφάγων). While
Ptolemy was devastating Judaea, Cleopatra came against him from Egypt with a
great army. Her son
hastened there to occupy an empty Egypt, but Cleopatra had left everything so
well fortified that Ptolemy was compelled to retreat to his former exile in
Cyprus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 287: *Alexander adds many cities of Syria to the
> kingdom of the Jews*.

Freed from fear of the Egyptians, Alexander turned to the capture of the
neighboring cities of Syria. He very nearly razed Gaza to the ground after
taking it through treachery. While he was joyfully sacrificing in the temple
because of his victories, some Pharisees insulted him. He became so enraged
that he killed six thousand of them. Because they were popular and factious
men, they stirred up many disturbances against him throughout his life, in
which no fewer than fifty thousand Jews died. The rebels would sometimes
summon Arabs, sometimes Syrians, and sometimes others to bring them aid. The
king fought them with varying fortune until, at last victorious, he brought
eight hundred of the chief rebels to Jerusalem and fastened them to crosses.
Their wives and children were first slaughtered before their eyes while he,
in the meantime, feasted splendidly with his friends and concubines.

> Marginalia, printed p. 287: *Alexander, persecutor of the Pharisees*.

At last, as a result of drunkenness, he was seized by a quartan fever. After
an illness of three years, during which he never ceased from war, he died.
Before his death he strongly urged his wife to surrender his body to the
Pharisees to be torn apart at their discretion, after she had pledged that in
future she would administer the kingdom only according to their judgment. Won
over by this device, the Pharisees publicly said every good thing about
Alexander and Alexandra. They gave him a most splendid burial and secured for
her a safe and peaceful kingdom. Alexander had two sons: Hyrcanus, a man of a
peaceable and sluggish disposition, and Aristobulus, who was ambitious and
turbulent. Supported by the favor of the Pharisees, Alexandra retained for
herself alone the reins of government under her husband’s testament, granting
the high priesthood to Hyrcanus and private wealth to Aristobulus. Had the
avarice and pride of the Pharisees permitted it, this prudent woman would have
kept the nation in peace and prosperity. She maintained a large body of
foreign soldiers, who were dispersed among the garrisons and suppressed
seditious movements as soon as they arose.

> Marginalia, printed p. 287: *Alexandra is compelled to court the
> Pharisees*.

At that time civil wars so

troubled her neighbors that the Jews feared nothing from them. Egypt had been
ablaze with civil war for very many years. For as long as she lived, Cleopatra
kept her son Lathyrus, an exile in Cyprus, away from Egypt and maintained her
royal tyranny chiefly with Jewish troops.

The Seleucids so wore one another away in mutual warfare that they aroused
more pity than fear among their neighbors. The brothers Gryphus and Cyzicenus
attacked one another until death. After Gryphus was removed by the treachery
of Heracleon, his son Seleucus killed his uncle Cyzicenus in battle.
Cyzicenus’s son was Antiochus Pius, so named because of his impiety, for he
married Selene. She had formerly married his father Cyzicenus and also
Gryphus, his father’s brother, just as she had first married her own brother
Lathyrus, from whom her mother Cleopatra took her and sent her to Gryphus. By
her Pius fathered Antiochus Asiaticus, who hid in Asia Minor while Tigranes
reigned in Syria. This Pius drove the defeated Seleucus into Cilicia. There,
when Seleucus exacted money too harshly from Mopsuestia to restore his army,
the people rose and burned him alive with his men in the theater.

> Marginalia, printed p. 288: *The Seleucids destroy themselves in civil
> wars*.

Besides Seleucus, Gryphus had four other sons. Their history has been
sufficiently entangled by both ancient and more recent writers, so we shall
attempt to disentangle it briefly. The second of Gryphus’s sons fought Pius
and lost both his army and his life. After him the third, Philip, occupied
part of Syria, while the fourth, Demetrius, was equipped with Lathyrus’s army
and assumed the crown. Both brothers waged war against Pius until Pius fell in
battle while assisting the queen of the Galadeni against the Parthians,
leaving behind Asiaticus, a fainthearted young man.

Demetrius was summoned into Judaea by the Pharisees’ faction and defeated
Alexander in a great battle. Meanwhile, however, his brother Philip invaded
his dominion. To protect his own possessions, Demetrius left Judaea and
besieged Philip in Beroea. Soon both Arabs and Parthians flew to Philip’s aid,
captured Demetrius, and sent him into Parthia. There Mithridates treated him
with sufficient honor, but he soon died of disease. Thereafter Philip alone
of the Seleucids reigned in Syria. The fifth of the brothers, Antiochus
Dionysius, immediately snatched Damascus from him, though not greatly to his
own advantage, for shortly afterward Dionysius was killed while fighting
bravely against the Arabs. Aretas the Arab then added his Damascene dominion
to his own kingdom.

Afflicted by the Seleucids’ constant quarrels with one another, the Syrians
sought a new protector and invited Tigranes of Armenia, who had taken
Mesopotamia and other kingdoms from the Parthians, to rule them. Tigranes
arrived with an army of five hundred thousand. We read nothing more of what
happened to Philip; he appears to have died from disease and fear. With him
the whole stock of Seleucus perished—or at least the kingdom of the Seleucids
came to an end. Pius’s son Asiaticus had hidden in a corner of Cilicia
throughout the eighteen years of Tigranes’s reign. When Lucullus finally broke
the power of Tigranes, Asiaticus aspired to the kingdom of Syria, but Pompey
cut off all his hopes by reducing Syria to a province of the Roman people.

> Marginalia, printed p. 288: *Tigranes occupies Syria*.

Of the lineage and kingdom of Alexander’s commanders, nothing else then
remained except the Lagids, reduced in Egypt to a very few. Ptolemy Physcon
left Egypt to his wife Cleopatra to give to whichever of their sons she
wished. She favored the younger, Alexander, but the people compelled her to
name the elder, Lathyrus, king. Nevertheless she soon expelled him to Cyprus
and reigned alone for very many years, until she finally bestowed the royal
title upon Alexander. Perceiving that even his mother loathed him and plotted
against him, he forestalled her and forced her to drink the poison she had
prepared for him. When the Alexandrian people learned of this illustrious
deed, as Salian calls it—or impious deed, as others call it—they expelled
Alexander from Egypt and recalled Lathyrus from Cyprus to the throne.
Alexander died at Tyre, leaving his treasures and also his son Alexander in
the care of the Romans.

> Marginalia, printed p. 288: *The Ptolemaic Lagids destroy one another*.

Lathyrus did not long survive. At his death, Alexander, son of Alexander,
supported by Sulla and the favor of the Romans, occupied the kingdom of Egypt.
He acted tyrannically, however, and the Alexandrian people killed him nineteen
days after the beginning of his reign. After him Lathyrus’s son—according to
some an illegitimate son—possessed the kingdom under the surname Auletes. In
his son Dionysius and daughter Cleopatra, about whom we shall speak later, the
lineage of the Lagids and of every Greek prince disappeared. The illegitimate
brother of Lathyrus and Alexander held the kingdom of Cyrene. At his death he
left it to the Romans by testament, and they made it a province.

But let us return to Judaea. Free from fear of her neighbors, Alexandra kept
the people in profound peace for nine years by means of mercenary troops and
the favor of the Pharisees. Yet the Pharisees’ exceptionally cruel domination
could not be endured any longer. While the queen allowed them everything,
they put their enemies—whom King Alexander had especially favored—to death
one after another. Since there was no limit and no end to the killings,
Alexander’s surviving friends finally formed a conspiracy and surrounded the
queen in great numbers. They complained about the Pharisees’ cruelty and, so
that they might thereafter live safely from their hands, permission to
withdraw into nearby fortresses they

requested, promising to abstain from every public office. Having obtained
this, Aristobulus slipped away to them and prepared an army as quickly as he
could. Foreseeing the storm, the Pharisees received all the strength and
resources of the kingdom from the now-dying queen, and under the name of
Hyrcanus the high priest they marched against Aristobulus. In the resulting
engagement Aristobulus was victorious, obtained both the kingdom and the high
priesthood, and persuaded his brother Hyrcanus to live privately in peace.

Hyrcanus would gladly have continued in this way until the end of his life,
but Antipater the Idumaean, son of Herod of Ascalon, had been carried away
captive from Ascalon by the Idumaeans when he was a boy. He rose to great
wealth among them and was appointed governor of all Idumaea by King Alexander.
This man of great ambition and boldness feared for himself because of
Aristobulus. He constantly whispered to Hyrcanus that he should guard his
life, against which Aristobulus was undoubtedly plotting. He also kept
repeating in the leading men’s ears how exceedingly unjust it was that
Aristobulus had displaced his elder brother from both the high priesthood and
the kingdom. He did not cease offering such suggestions until he induced
Hyrcanus to flee secretly with him from Jerusalem to Aretas the Arab. Antipater
persuaded Aretas to restore Hyrcanus to the kingdom, and Aretas led fifty
thousand soldiers into Judaea. Aristobulus came against him with as many
troops as he could gather, was defeated, and fled to Jerusalem.

> Marginalia, printed p. 289: *Antipater incites Hyrcanus against
> Aristobulus*.

Meanwhile Pompey was pursuing Tigranes in Armenia. He sent his legate Scaurus
into Syria to receive at Damascus the Syrians who were surrendering themselves
to the protection of the Roman people. Both Hyrcanus and Aristobulus sent
representatives to Scaurus to attract the Romans to their side with gifts.
Scaurus preferred Aristobulus, who promised him forty talents, to Hyrcanus,
and ordered Aretas to return to Arabia so that Aristobulus might reign in
peace. Once restored, Aristobulus defeated Aretas and Antipater in another
battle after Scaurus’s departure. When Pompey himself came to Damascus,
however, Antipater argued Hyrcanus’s cause with such skill and diligence that
Pompey sent the captured Aristobulus to Rome and granted Hyrcanus the high
priesthood with a measure of civil authority, but by no means a royal diadem.
