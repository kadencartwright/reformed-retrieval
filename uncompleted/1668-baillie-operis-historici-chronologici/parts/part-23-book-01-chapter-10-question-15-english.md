### QUESTION XV

#### On the one week and its half.

Our rather lengthy delay over the preceding matters will make us briefer here.
There are two issues in the question: first, which and what numbered week is
that one mentioned by Daniel in chapter 9:27? Second, what is the half of the
week spoken of in the same place?

On the first issue, we follow the common opinion that the general body of
chronologists and interpreters of Scripture, Protestant as well as Roman
Catholic, derives from Theodoret on Daniel 9:27: namely, that this one week is
the seventieth and final week. The prophet’s words in verses 25, 26, and 27
sufficiently demonstrate this. First, when the angel Gabriel divides the
seventy into parts, after seven and sixty-two he names one as the final part.
Then verse 25 affirms that there will be seven weeks and sixty-two weeks up to
the time of the Messiah,

[Printed p. 179 | IA leaf 209 | access PDF 209 | OCR object 208]

so that only one of the seventy remains. Since it comes after all the others,
it must itself be the last. Third, the same is clear from verse 26, where the
Messiah’s death—the principal event of the whole period of seventy—is said to
have occurred after those sixty-two which follow the seven. This event thus
becomes the principal defining mark of our one week, which ought to coincide
with its own mark and not precede it. Finally, verse 27 affirms that in that
one week the gospel was preached, the covenant confirmed, and sacrifices and
the other shadows of the ceremonies abolished when the body itself arrived:
Christ crucified, dead, buried, risen, and ascending into heaven. These events
were accomplished in no other of the seventy except the final one alone.

Moved by these and other arguments, the ancients and more recent writers alike
agreed in establishing that one as the seventieth week, with only one or two
exceptions. This continued until Joseph Scaliger—for whom it is often a very
small matter to rise against all mortals in defense of what he considers
true—carried many of our dearest men in the Reformed Church with him into a
different opinion. This great master of chronology, placing the beginning of
the weeks in the second year of Darius Nothus and their end at Jerusalem’s
destruction, strayed into various ἀτοπήματα, “absurdities.” In the question
before us, as much as in any other, he seems to have gone astray the whole way.
In the earlier edition of his *Emendation of Times*, he correctly divided our
one week into two halves, as the Holy Spirit does. Contrary to every reason,
however, he inserted between those halves a great gap of nearly forty years.
He correctly placed the first half of that week in the three and a half years
in which Christ was baptized, preached the gospel, and was crucified. But he
most ineptly extended the other half to the three and a half years in which
Roman arms thundered around Jerusalem until its final devastation. When many
pronounced this invention of so great a man ridiculous and laughable, in his
later edition he did not indeed choose to defend it, yet he tried to excuse it
from all ridicule and absurdity with the following comparison. Just as, he
says, a person who travels from Paris to Toulouse and conducts his business in
Toulouse over seven months can rightly be said to have spent one month on the
journey, even though one half of that month preceded the six months of business
and the other half followed that half-year, so it can be called one week of
years even though very many years divide it in the middle.

> Marginalia, printed p. 179: *Scaliger gratuitously opposes everyone*;
> *He disagrees with himself*; *He does not defend his great invention at all
> against ridicule*.

In truth, this comparison limps on so many feet that it can scarcely walk
straight on one. It certainly fails at the point on which the argument turns:
Scaliger’s month is one month in every respect, just as Daniel’s week is one.
For among its seven months, the first half of the first and the second half of
the last by no means make one and the same month—not the final month, nor any
one intermediate month.

This opinion of Scaliger’s was certainly so harsh that not only all his
followers elsewhere but even Scaliger himself abandoned it. In his later
edition he makes our one week continuous and, with the general body of learned
men, places Christ’s death in its middle. He errs only in refusing to let the
seventy end with this week and in refusing to make the half-week any portion
of that one week. Instead, he separates the half from that one by as much as
Jerusalem’s destruction was separated from Christ’s death.

> Marginalia, printed p. 179: *At last he makes the one continuous*.

We shall discuss the half shortly. For the present, we accept what his second
revision grants us: namely, that this one week is continuous and is the very
week most excellently distinguished by the incomparable mark of Christ’s
death. Once this is granted, his and his followers’ opinion that the weeks end
at Jerusalem’s destruction is overthrown. Grant, though it is contrary to the
prophet’s order, that the seven weeks—the first part of the seventy—stand
after the sixty-two and the one. Scaliger himself does not dare affirm this,
having finally professed that he did not know the true place and order of
those parts. But grant in any event that those seven stand after the one, just
as it is beyond all doubt that the sixty-two precede this one. It will follow
that after Christ’s death in the middle of the sixty-third week, seven and a
half weeks—that is, fifty-two and a half years—remained until the city’s
destruction. Yet no one has counted more than forty years from Christ’s death
in Tiberius’s eighteenth year to Jerusalem’s destruction in Vespasian’s
second, and the Scaligerians count only thirty-six. Thus their calculation
here deviates from their own reckoning by a full sixteen and a half years.

> Marginalia, printed p. 179: *He wrongly places the one before the seven*.

Perhaps these or other arguments of this kind deterred Junius from following
even these later pronouncements of Scaliger. But the third opinion that Junius
fashioned is no sounder than Scaliger’s. In his annotations on the passage he
maintains that this one is indeed the last of the seventy but that it ends at
Jerusalem’s destruction, thirty-six years before which Christ’s death had
occurred. In this he has the English Annotators as followers.

Much polishing was spent here, and even “the second thoughts of wise men were
worse.” Both Junius and the English Annotators, in their earlier editions,

> themselves, extending that one to the city’s destruction*.

correctly assigned the one week to the Messiah’s death and to the last of the
seventy weeks. But in their later editions, both making a worse judgment, they
end that one at Jerusalem’s destruction. For this purpose, as we noted above,
Junius thought it necessary to change Daniel’s words in the future tense,
*Christ will be exterminated*, into the past construction *the Messiah having
been removed*. The English Annotators expressly approve this violent
corruption of the sacred text, without any pretense of reason. Indeed, they
stray farther from the right path than Junius. He counts thirty-six years from
Christ’s death to the city’s destruction, fewer than anyone has ever assigned
to this interval, but in their annotations on verse 27 they count only
twenty-nine by an entirely new reckoning. They do not explain how, nor can I
conjecture it, unless perhaps one chooses to blame the blunder on the printers’
carelessness.

But leaving this aside, Junius and his English followers plainly offend
against the first part of verse 27, which is before us. In that one week,
whatever it may finally prove to be, there was a notable and extraordinary
preaching of the gospel covenant and a success of the gospel among many—a
great multitude of Jews and gentiles. Yet in Junius’s week before the city’s
destruction we read of nothing singular of this kind beyond what occurred in
earlier times, or indeed of anything that can be compared with those earlier
times.

They appeal to God’s goodness in warning Christians before the siege of the
city to withdraw to Pella, but what does this have to do with the preaching of
the gospel and the covenant confirmed with a multitude of converts from Jews
and gentiles? Nor does what they add about the cessation of sacrifices while
the city was under siege help them. The sacrifices were to be abolished in
the middle of the week, but they maintain that they ceased only two years
before the city’s destruction. That was not in the middle but in the week as
it approached its end. Indeed, sacrifices did not cease even when the city was
under siege, for the priests persevered in offering victims until the final
day of the siege, when the Romans devastated the temple and the city with
sword and flame.

I do not know whether Diodati was moved by these reasons. Along with Junius and
the English he wrongly ends the weeks at the city’s destruction, and with them
equally corrupts the text by wishing Christ to have been exterminated after
the sixty-two and before the one week. Nevertheless, in his annotations on
verse 27 he concedes that the one week must be assigned, if not to any definite
week among the seventy, at least to the one in whose final half Christ preached
the gospel and suffered death on the cross. To err is human; it is less
tolerable when most learned men do not agree with themselves. Diodati, and
especially the latest English Annotators, certainly do not appear to make
consistent assertions here even in their final revision.

> Marginalia, printed p. 180: *Diodati’s error*.

In these difficulties I expected great light from Hugo Grotius’s supreme
intelligence, learning, and diligence. But as soon as I took up his annotations
on Daniel, all that hope was entirely cut off. I found that commentary thinner,
drier, and more unfruitful than any other. Besides the fact that Grotius’s
view of the weeks expressly agrees with the errors of Scaliger and Junius—for
he places their beginning in Darius Nothus’s second year and their end at the
city’s destruction—he also has peculiar delusions about the one now before
us. He indeed makes it the last of the seventy, following the seven and the
sixty-two, and teaches that Christ was killed after the seven and the
sixty-two. Thus far he is correct and agrees with our view. But his annotation
on verse 27, that our one week and its half refer to the city’s destruction,
is inconsistent with what precedes. Certainly no one has enclosed the
Messiah’s death together with the city’s destruction in one week. I am also
displeased that Grotius refuses to understand the confirmation of the covenant
with many as Christ’s gospel covenant, but takes it as the friendship of the
utterly perverse Jews with the utterly wicked Idumaeans, who inopportunely and
disastrously joined their arms against the Romans. This is an exceedingly
crass absurdity.

No more on the first part of the question. On the second—what the half of the
week is—we said something at Question X, to which we now add the following.
First, Scaliger’s imperious insistence that the half must refer not to the one
week just mentioned but to an entirely different week, namely the week that
followed all seventy, must be rejected. This opinion is so paradoxical that I
cannot remember any Protestant interpreter of Scripture who follows it.
Junius, Diodati, and the latest English Annotators, who hold most of Scaliger’s
other paradoxes, certainly abandon their master here. Nevertheless—strange to
say—among the Roman Catholics, Pererius, Torniellus, Salianus, and others, as
Henricus Philippi rightly intimates, plainly fell into this *parorama*,
“oversight,”
of Scaliger’s. They do not agree with him in extending the seventy-first week
to the city’s destruction. Rather, after the seventy weeks have ended at
Christ’s death, they imagine the half of a new week that belongs to none of the
seventy. They certainly fashion a new week for us in whose middle Christ died,
according to Daniel 9:27, but a week whose first half consists of the latter

> error*; *Protestants abandon the Scaligerian view; Jesuits follow it*.

half of the seventieth week and whose latter half consists of the first half
of the seventy-first week, as Scaliger wished.

This seems to be why Petavius, out of respect for his Jesuit brothers, touches
so lightly here on his adversary Scaliger. Although Scaliger’s invention does
not please him, he does not wish to attack it with any arguments and indeed
expressly asserts that it is probable. We, however, argue as follows against
this error of Scaliger and of the Jesuits just named.

First, the demonstrative particle ה prefixed to the word *week* marks the week
just mentioned. That was the seventieth week, not an entirely different one,
namely Scaliger’s seventy-first. Scaliger adduces various instances where the
particle ה does not refer to any antecedent but to something entirely
different.

Reply. We do not examine those instances. We say only that our argument holds
in ordinary and common usage and that no reason can be given for understanding
Daniel to have departed from the particle’s ordinary use.

Second, it is exceedingly harsh to fashion such an ambiguity within the same
verse—indeed, within a small part of one verse—requiring one and the same word
to be understood in different ways at so short an interval.

Third, throughout the angel’s discourse the number of seventy weeks is
precisely defined. Just as we refuted their shortening in Pareus and Rainolds
above, so here we reject their extension three and a half years beyond the
seventy weeks, as contrary to the angel’s express purpose.

Fourth, the named Jesuits make Daniel silent about the seventieth week, which
is mentioned separately above the others as the most wonderful of all.
Scaliger, meanwhile, marks this seventieth with no notable event and closes the
seventy weeks without any mystery. At their end he will have neither the
Messiah dead nor the city destroyed, since he ends the seventy weeks thirty-six
years after Christ’s death and three and a half before the city was destroyed,
that is, a year and a half before the final siege of Jerusalem, when no author
asserts that anything notable occurred.

> Marginalia, printed p. 181: *Arguments against Scaliger and the Jesuits*.

Having rejected the opinion of Scaliger and the Jesuits named above, let us
inquire what should be thought about the half of the one week, namely the last
of the seventy. There is great disagreement here about the half among authors
who agree with one another about the one week. Some interpret the Hebrew חצי
as *half*, others as *middle*. Although *middle* is often confused with
*half*, in this passage the proponents of these different opinions wish them
to be distinguished as follows: a *half* is either equal part of the whole,
while the *middle* lies between those two parts. Those, in turn, who follow the
Latin Vulgate in rendering it *half* go off in three directions. Some favor
the first half of the week, others the latter, and some are indifferent
between the two. Authors of great name, Protestant as well as Roman Catholic,
support these four opinions. As is our custom, we shall briefly and freely
explain what we think of each.

> Marginalia, printed p. 181: *The half of the final week is disputed in
> various ways*.

First, we do not approve Suarez’s indifference, although various members of
his society approve it. Since it pleased the Holy Spirit expressly to
distinguish the parts of that week and to assign the greatest events,
especially the Messiah’s death, to one of them, mortals ought not to move the
boundaries God has set, as though it were equivalent to attribute those great
events to any part of this week. Furthermore, since Christ’s death—the
defining mark of the middle of that week—occurred at a definite moment in
time, it cannot be left indifferently in any part of the entire seven-year
period without violating the truth of the event itself.

> Marginalia, printed p. 181: *Suarez’s indifference rejected*.

Setting aside that ἀδιαφορία, “indifference,” of the Jesuits, those who favor
the latter half begin it with Christ’s baptism in the middle of the fourth year
of the final week, so that they may end the seventy at Christ’s death.

But we make two objections to this opinion. First, if Christ’s death occurred
at the very end of the week, the angel divided the week in half to no purpose
when he assigned to the half an event that occurred at the end. Why is
something that should be ascribed to the end attributed to the middle? Second,
the chronological reckoning does not agree with this calculation, for it
places Christ’s death three and a half years outside its proper location. This
disrupts the chronological scheme, as we shall see later.

> Marginalia, printed p. 181: *Arguments against the latter half*.

As for the third opinion, concerning the first half of the final week, it is
embraced by Calvin, Rollock, Cornelius a Lapide, Petavius, Henricus Philippi,
and others. It places Christ’s baptism at the beginning of this half and his
death at its end. Thus in substance it coincides with the fourth opinion,
which interprets the text as speaking not of the half but of the middle. For
what occurs at the end of the first half actually occurs in the middle of the
whole week. So the Septuagint translators rendered the passage, ἐν τῷ ἡμίσει
τῆς ἑβδομάδος, Junius *in the middle of the week*, the Italian translator
[*nella meta de quella settimana*], “in the middle of that week,” and the
French translator [*au milieu de ceste septimaine*], “in the middle of this
week,” just as

> Marginalia, printed p. 181: *And the first half*.

Vatablus and Montanus have *the middle of the week* in the margin. We approve
this third opinion as to its substance but reject its wording. For it seems an
awkward expression to place something that actually falls in the middle of
the week in the first half, or in any half distinguished from the middle.

The fourth opinion, therefore, which explains Daniel’s words as referring not
to the half but to the middle of the week, seems to us the most straightforward.
Sanctius correctly distinguishes on this passage between the mathematical
middle, which is at an entirely equal distance from the extremes down to a
mathematical point, and the civil middle, in which some breadth is admitted
according to common usage and the ordinary phraseology of Scripture, and
complete equality of distance from the extremes is not required. Yet no great
breadth seems to be required in this passage. Grant that after his baptism
Christ preached the gospel for three years and six months and was crucified at
the end of the sixth month—on these chronological boundaries, more in their
proper place. Christ’s death, if it did not occur at the exact mathematical
point of the middle, nevertheless occurred in so precise a civil middle of
that final week that as little breadth is required here as in any other middle
mentioned in Scripture.

> Marginalia, printed p. 182: *The truth lies in the civil middle*.

The calculations of the most learned chronologists, though differing from one
another in other matters, seem to concur in this reckoning. We shall try to
demonstrate this in the proper place from Junius on Daniel 9:24 and Calvisius
on Artaxerxes’s seventh year, together with the tables of Ubo, Emmius,
Petavius, and Henricus Philippi.
