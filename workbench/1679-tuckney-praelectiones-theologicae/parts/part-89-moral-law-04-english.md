This is the third distinction. The moral law may be considered either
insofar as it declares the law of nature, insofar as it is Mosaic, or,
finally, insofar as it was confirmed and established by Christ in the
gospel.

1. I do not know whether the Antinomians will grant that it binds believers
under the gospel in the first manner, that is, insofar as it declares the
law of nature. I know well enough that everyone else concedes this. I
therefore pass it over.

2. But in the second manner, insofar as it is the Mosaic law or was in the
hand of Moses, as some like to speak, the issue remains unsettled among many.
For the Antinomians deny that it binds believers in this manner:

“What have we to do with Moses? Moses is not in their consciences. Indeed,
those who now have anything to do with Moses rush straight to the devil.”
Such is their candor and such their encomia, so that they sing in harmony
with the Manichaeans who, according to Augustine, claimed to have been
enemies not of the law but of Judaism.

[Marginalia: *Against Faustus*, book 21.]

Yet many others of far greater distinction hold almost the same position:
the moral law binds us as a declaration of the natural law, or principally
as confirmed by Christ in the gospel; but as delivered by Moses, whatever it
was to the Jews, it was established with no new obligation for Christians.
Among the Roman theologians Aquinas, Soto, Medina, Toledo, Suarez, and others
hold this, and among our theologians Zanchi, Pareus, and Rivet, at whose
names I always rise. I am indeed troubled that on this point at least I
embrace the position of Bellarmine, Vázquez, and Lorinus rather than theirs.
For I believe myself so freed through Christ that I nevertheless profess
myself, in a sound sense, bound by the law of Moses. I say, the law of Moses,

[Marginalia: a *Summa Theologiae*, Ia IIae, question 9, article 5; b book 2,
question 5, article 4; c Ia IIae, question 103, article 3; d on Romans 3; e
*On Laws*, book 9; f *On Justification*, book 4, chapter 6; g disputation
108, chapter 3; h on Acts 15.]

but not by the law of Moses insofar as it is the law of Moses, if *insofar
as* is understood reduplicatively. For then every law of Moses—judicial and
even ceremonial—would bind us, as those doctors falsely thought from Acts
15:24, whereas the apostolic synod plainly thought otherwise, indeed the
contrary, in verse 28. Rather, I mean the moral law insofar as it was
delivered by Moses, with *insofar as* understood specifically.

I wish to say only that it binds not *insofar as*, but *although*, it is the
law of Moses.

To make this plainer, we must distinguish with Luther—and, if you please,
even speak with Luther—between Moses most Mosaic and Moses Aaronic.

1. First, if the law in the hand of Moses is taken for the covenant of works,
according to the apostle's words, “Moses describes the righteousness which
is from the law, that the one who does these things shall live by them,”
Romans 10:5, we proceed to bury this most Mosaic Moses, as Luther calls him,
in Moses' own tomb, which for us—at least in this life, except in Christ
alone—is hardly to be found.

[1679 new-series fol. 26 | IA leaf 346]

2. If by the law in Moses' hand we understand the covenant of grace
according to its Mosaic administration, thus far we gladly conduct without
tears the funeral not only of his most Mosaic Moses but also of Moses
Aaronic. For it must freely be admitted that believers under the gospel are
freed from the law of Moses beyond the degree to which the Jews who lived
before Christ were. This is the character and the very mind of the apostle
in 2 Corinthians 3, where he speaks of the mystery of death and of the veil
of Moses being removed under the gospel, and also in that sufficiently long
discourse in the Epistle to the Galatians, where he treats extensively the
childish pedagogy under the law which is wholly abolished in the gospel's
more mature age. In those passages he does not treat only the judicial or
ceremonial law. In the passage to the Corinthians he speaks of the law
“engraved on stones,” and in the Epistle to the Galatians of the law which
was added because of transgressions and which shut everyone under sin. The
first belonged particularly to the Decalogue; the others were common to it
and the other laws.

[Marginalia: 2 Corinthians 3:7; Galatians 3:19–21.]

Believers under the gospel are therefore free not only from the law's
rigor, curse, coercion, and the other things named earlier, from which
believers under the law were also formerly freed, but also from other
circumstances of the moral law that once burdened the Jews:

1. As regards the accompanying ceremonies: the law was then kept in the
ark, prayers were offered with burnt offerings, acts of thanksgiving with
incense, and so forth.

2. As regards the mode of dispensation:

1. In that dispensation the grace of the gospel shone more obscurely, as if
through a narrow opening and from behind a lattice, whereas now it displays
itself with unveiled face, 2 Corinthians 3.

2. There it was accompanied by greater fear and horror, as commonly attends
darkness; here it is accompanied by greater boldness and freeborn
confidence, Romans 8:15; 2 Corinthians 3; Galatians 4:4–6.

3. Finally, that whole pedagogy looked forward to Christ as one yet to come,
whom we now behold before us and venerate since he has come.

From this Mosaic administration of the moral law, therefore, we are now,
thanks be to God, set free. In this sense believers under the gospel have no
business remaining with Moses.

Reasons nevertheless remain why this moral law, although—indeed,
precisely as—it was delivered by Moses, concerns believers under the gospel.

[Marginalia: See Bellarmine, *On Justification*, book 4, chapter 6; Lorinus
on Acts 15; Vázquez, disputation 180, chapter 3.]

Moses was, as is said of Noah, a preacher of righteousness: namely, the Holy
Spirit's secretary in writing the sacred page which also concerns us, and
also a prophet and a man of God. Through him God restored the knowledge of
natural law that had been almost blotted from human minds, and by this
second declaration of his will introduced a new obligation.

[Marginalia: * Deuteronomy 18:15, 18; † Psalm 90:1.]

But if the Antinomians object, as they customarily do, from Deuteronomy
33:4–5 that Moses was only the king of Jeshurun and indeed commanded the law
as an inheritance for Jacob, one which regarded the Israelites alone—as the
preface of the Decalogue and the promise attached to the fifth precept
sufficiently indicate—and bound the Gentiles no more than the King of
Spain's edicts bind us Englishmen; and if they add the apostle's words about
the Gentiles in Romans 2:12, “Whoever sinned without law will also perish
without law,” then the following distinctions answer them.

1. We must distinguish between a promulgated and an unpromulgated law. The
law of Moses did not bind those to whose knowledge

[1679 new-series fol. 27 | IA leaf 347]

it had not come—which was the apostle's meaning—but it held liable those to
whom it became known, and on this account it binds us Christians too.

2. We must distinguish laws proper to the Israelite people, such as the
ceremonial and judicial laws, from others commanding matters common to all,
as the moral law did.

Let it be granted, therefore, that the former laws did not bind the
Gentiles, although even in respect to them there were proselytes from the
Gentiles, and it could be disputed whether a *ger tsedeq* supererogated
beyond the precept. The same account does not hold for the moral law, whose
subject matter concerned Gentiles as much as Jews. If knowledge of it had
come to any of them even from Moses as if from afar, whether they were
proselytes or not, it would have bound them.

3. This will be even more evident from a third distinction which may be
made concerning Moses. He may be considered as he was in himself, an
Israelite and thus appointed as legislator immediately for the Israelites;
or he may be considered as the servant of the Lord of all peoples, through
whom that Lord prepared a second edition, so to speak, of natural law in his
moral law. Thus Moses bound by his edicts not the English as the King of
Spain, but every people situated anywhere in the supreme Emperor's whole
dominion. This is the first reason the law of Moses himself reaches
Christians too.

The second is that in enacting this law Moses was a preacher not only of law
but also of gospel, and to that extent had authority even over believers
under the gospel. Indeed, insofar as the law of Moses either recalled the
covenant of works to memory or displayed that ancient administration of the
covenant of grace, we acknowledge that Moses is opposed to Christ and that
the law delivered through Moses is opposed in Sacred Scripture to the
promise made to Abraham.

[Marginalia: John 1:17; Galatians 3:17.]

But this is not the whole Moses. Elsewhere we find Moses speaking about
Christ, John 5:46; conversing amicably with Christ at the transfiguration,
Matthew 17; and even bearing the office of mediator in the very giving of
the law, and thus being a type of the one mediator Jesus Christ, Galatians
3:19, as if Christ himself had delivered that law *by the hand of Moses*,
that is, through Moses' ministry.

The following supports this:

1. Love, the summary of this Mosaic law, is called the law of Christ,
Galatians 6:2.

2. Even if this law was not the covenant of grace, it was nevertheless
proposed for evangelical ends, namely to lead us to Christ as a pedagogue,
Galatians 3:24.

3. It is likewise proposed in an evangelical manner. For example:

1. It addresses the Israelites as covenant members: “I am Jehovah your
God,” as occurs in the preface to the Decalogue.

2. It grants promises not only to them but also to their descendants to the
thousandth generation, as we read in the second precept.

3. Some of these promises are especially evangelical, such as Deuteronomy
30:6: “Jehovah your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your
offspring, so that you may love Jehovah your God with all your heart and all
your soul, that you may live.”

4. It requires obedience on the ground that he is their God, that is, bound
to them by a gracious covenant. Therefore the clause “I am Jehovah your
God,” just as it is prefixed to the entire Decalogue in Exodus 20, is also

[1679 new-series fol. 28 | IA leaf 348]

appended to nearly every one of its precepts in Leviticus 18 and 19. This is
surely an eminently evangelical motive, one capable of moving believers
under the gospel above all others and making the law itself, commended to us
under this title, most highly esteemed.

This suffices for the second member of the third distinction, namely, the
moral law insofar as it is Mosaic or was delivered by Moses.
