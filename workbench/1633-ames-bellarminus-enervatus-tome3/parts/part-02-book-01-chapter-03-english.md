## Chapter III. On the Effect of the Sacraments

Question 1 is whether the sacraments of the new law are true causes of
justification *ex opere operato*.

### Thesis I

Bellarmine. The question is whether the sacraments are a true and proper
instrumental cause of justification; whether they do this physically or
morally; whether through an inherent quality or through God's motion alone;
whether that outward action alone, which is called a sacrament and is termed
the work performed, actively, proximately or immediately, and instrumentally
effects the grace of justification by the power of the sacramental action
itself, instituted by God for this purpose, and not by the merit of the person
acting or receiving. All Catholics affirm this.

Protestant reply. I. More cautiously than befits such a disputant, Bellarmine
here avoids all the scholastic controversies about the mode—as they call
it—of the causality attributed to the sacraments: whether it is physical or
moral; whether it is in the word, in the element, in the action as exercised,
or in all of them jointly; whether it produces its effect in the final instant
of the sacrament's existence or in the first instant of its nonexistence. Yet
without these matters the cause can scarcely be established, much less
treated accurately. Melchior Cano observes this in *On the Sacraments*, part
4, and rightly warns on that account that this question is worthy of a
conciliar definition, worthy of the authority of all the saints, and finally
worthy of our faith and of testimonies from the sacred writings. II. Yet
Bellarmine avoids them in such a way as to indicate sufficiently that he
favors the cruder scholastics, who maintain that the sacraments are physical
causes of grace. To effect properly, proximately, and immediately—as
Bellarmine attributes to the sacraments—does not belong to a cause acting
morally. He also professes this openly in chapter 15, where at the end of this
controversy he explains his opinion scholastically, something he did not wish
to do at the beginning so that he might play more freely with ambiguous forms
of conclusion. III. He absurdly opposes the merit of the person acting or
receiving to the work performed, as if there were no third possibility,
although there are scholastics who exclude merit yet do not exclude
dispositions from the sacrament's efficacy. Thus Alexander of Hales, part 4,
question 5, member 3, article 4, section 3: “The sacraments dispose a person
and render him more fit to receive grace. Thus they are a cause of grace, not
with respect to its existence but with respect to its inherence, in a more
suitable or conformable manner.” IV. This opinion is falsely ascribed to all—

[1632 witness | printed p. 15]

—Catholics, even if papists are meant. Bonaventure testifies to the contrary
in Book 4, distinction 1, question 4. “There is,” he says, “another opinion of
great masters concerning this, who say that in the sacraments there is no
causality or any power, whether effecting or disposing toward grace, that is
some absolute quality or property. Rather, through a certain assistance of
the divine power, which is the cause of grace, the recipient's faith and
devotion dispose him toward grace. They therefore say that the sacraments are
said to have power, are said to be a cause, and are said to effect something,
according to the common manner of speaking, because of the assistance of the
divine power. If *power* is spoken of properly, according to them, one should
not say that the sacraments possess power; but insofar as the term *power* is
extended to some appointment, when something has an effective appointment to
something else, it is said to have power with respect to it, like a royal
letter sealed with the king's ring.” “Devout faith does not oppose this
position,” Bonaventure adds, “and reason agrees with it.” V. Our opinion is
that the sacraments possess, with respect to grace, all the efficacy that a
practical sign can possess through any relation: that is, they seal to those
who receive them rightly the divine communication of grace, to be received by
faith. Yet they do not effect grace immediately, but by the mediation of God's
Spirit and faith.

Bellarmine's arguments.

2. Bellarmine. The difference between the efficacy of John's baptism and
Christ's baptism is as great as that between water and the Spirit. Matthew 3;
Mark 1; Luke 3; John 1.

Protestant reply. I. What had to be proved was that the outward action of
baptism is the proper and immediate cause of the grace of justification. But
Bellarmine concludes that Christ and his Spirit, and not water, are the cause
of grace. Is this not an excellent proof? II. Christ himself interprets
John's words in Acts 1:5: “John indeed baptized with water, but you shall be
baptized with the Holy Spirit not many days from now.” “These words,” says
Cajetan on Matthew 3, “show that, over against John's baptism, the baptism of
Pentecost is distinguished on the other side, which occurred in the Spirit
and fire in a perceptible manner.” Bellarmine himself says a little later that
“by *fire* in this passage can most properly be understood the outward fire
that descended visibly upon the apostles on the day of Pentecost.” With
respect to this baptism John spoke in the future tense, “He will baptize.” At
the same time, however, he indicates that the inward baptism of grace came
immediately from Christ and his Spirit in John's own baptism.

Bellarmine 2. In Acts 19 Paul commanded those whom he heard had been baptized
with John's baptism to be baptized with Christ's baptism.

Protestant reply. I. It does not appear that those discussed in Acts 19 were
rebaptized, but rather that they were instructed concerning the baptism they
had received. II. If they were rebaptized, it was not because of the
imperfection of John's baptism but be—

[1632 witness | printed p. 16]

—cause of the error of some ignorant person by whom they had been baptized.
The Master of the Sentences himself, Book 4, distinction 2, teaches that those
who did not place their hope in John's baptism and who believed in the Father,
the Son, and the Holy Spirit were not rebaptized. Alexander of Hales teaches
the same in part 4, question 6, member 7. It is certainly clear from the
Gospel history that John baptized at the same time as Christ's disciples.
John 3 and 4. There was no difference of subordination.

3. Bellarmine. At the end of Mark: “He who believes and is baptized shall be
saved.” Here a power of saving is attributed to baptism.

Protestant reply. In this passage it is attributed no more to the outward
action of baptism in itself than to the outward profession of the mouth.
Romans 10:10. It is properly attributed to faith, as is clear from the words
that follow: “But he who does not believe”—it does not say, “he who has not
been baptized”—“shall be condemned.”

4. Bellarmine. John 3: “Unless someone is born anew of water and the Holy
Spirit.” This passage plainly makes water baptism a cause of the new birth.

Protestant reply. I. If these words were pressed rigorously, they would prove
more than Bellarmine undertook to prove: namely, that the very matter of
baptism, water, is in itself the cause of regeneration. II. We do not deny
that water baptism is in some manner an instrumental cause of regeneration,
for it is the seal and instrument of the proper cause and of its effect. Yet
it does not properly, proximately, and immediately effect regeneration. The
word too is a cause of the same effect. 1 Peter 1. But it is not the proper,
proximate, and immediate cause. III. It is sufficiently clear from the context
that the Spirit, not water, is the proper cause of regeneration. First, in
the following verse, when Christ speaks of the same regeneration as an
effect, he does not mention water but only the Spirit: “What is born of the
Spirit is spirit.” So also in verse 8. Second, Christ himself declares in
verse 12 that he had explained the cause and mode of regeneration by an
earthly sign and phrase, adapted more to human comprehension than to the
heavenly reality of the matter: “If I have told you earthly things and you do
not believe, how will you believe if I tell you heavenly things?” By these
words he indicates that what he had said about water should not be understood
crudely and rigidly. Third, Nicodemus is sharply rebuked because, although a
teacher in Israel, he did not know the things that were said. Yet one could
not have expected an ordinary teacher at that time to understand how water
could be the proper and immediate cause of grace, since after so many
centuries our Seraphic Masters do not yet know how to explain it.
Bonaventure says in Book 4, distinction 1, question 4: “It is very difficult
to understand how power is given to a sacrament as an absolute quality. It is
difficult to know when it is given. It is also difficult to know to what it is
given—to the word, that is, or to the element. It is also difficult to know
how long it lasts. It is likewise very dif—

[1632 witness | printed p. 17]

—ficult to understand how it exercises an influence. It is also difficult to
know what its nature is in itself, and many such things.” If it is said that
understanding the mode of the Spirit's operation in our regeneration is
equally difficult, we indeed admit it. Yet it was not difficult for Nicodemus
to understand from the Scriptures that God's Spirit can produce any spiritual
effect whatever. But concerning a proper and immediate action of water in
spiritual matters he could have read absolutely nothing in all Scripture.

5. Bellarmine. Acts 2: “Let each one be baptized for the remission of sins,”
and Acts 22: “Be baptized and wash away your sins.” In these passages the
remission of sins is attributed to baptism.

Protestant reply. The remission of sins consists in the judgment of the
offended God. It cannot be attributed to any outward ceremony except under
the character of a sign or seal that renders that judgment attested.

Bellarmine 2. Who would not marvel at that new figure of speech by which “wash
away your sins” signifies “receive a seal of justification already
received”?

Protestant reply. Bellarmine cannot marvel that justification had previously
been received by these people, for this is his own confession in chapter 13:
“Adults are justified through faith and true contrition before they actually
approach the sacraments.” II. The figure we say is contained in a phrase of
this kind is: “Wash away your sins,” that is, receive washing as a seal of the
remission of sins, whether already received or yet to be received, through
the assurance of conscience. This figure is so far from being new or
remarkable that those great masters whom Bonaventure cites and approves in
Book 4, distinction 1, question 2, approved it long ago. They give this
example: “A king decrees that whoever has such a sign shall have a hundred
pounds. After this decree the sign, because it has an effective appointment,
is said to possess the power to cause someone to have a hundred pounds.”

Bellarmine 3. It is unheard of for the action signified to be put for the
signification of the sign, and for a future action to be put for signifying a
past one.

Protestant reply. Scarcely anything is heard or read more often in Scripture
than for the name of the thing signified to be given to the sign, whether
that thing is past or future. Thus circumcision is called God's covenant in
Genesis 17:10, and Bellarmine himself acknowledges in chapter 9 that it was
“a testimony to Abraham of faith already possessed.”

6. Bellarmine. Acts 8; 2 Timothy 2. The laying on of hands is a true cause of
grace, as is evident from the particle *through*.

Protestant reply. The issue there is not justifying grace but the special
gifts of the Holy Spirit. It is exceedingly absurd to seek their true,
proper, and immediate cause in the outward laying on of hands. *Through* does
not always denote a proper and immediate cause. Miracles were done “through
the hands of the apostles,” Acts 18:3, yet human—

[1632 witness | printed p. 18]

—hands are not the proper and immediate causes of miracles.

7. Bellarmine. In 1 Corinthians 10:17, participation in one bread is set down
as the cause of one body; and in chapter 12, verse 13, Paul proves that we are
one body because through baptism we have received one Spirit.

Protestant reply. Cajetan replies concerning the first passage: “By the unity
of the bread, and so forth, is signified that the sacrament of the Eucharist
makes us all into one, through participation in it not only sacramentally but
also spiritually: the unity of the mystical body.”

8. Bellarmine. In Ephesians 5 and Titus 3 we are most plainly said to be
cleansed and saved by the washing of water and through the washing. It is
therefore an instrumental cause.

Protestant reply. I. We concede the whole because the sacraments are moral
instruments. II. The sacraments are said to effect what God works at the same
time, in accordance with their signification and sealing.

Bellarmine 2. Then we are no more properly said to be cleansed through baptism
than through any other action that happens at the same time.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine here deserves the rod of those great masters whom
Bonaventure mentioned earlier, because he attributes nothing to God's
effective appointment and assistance.

9. Bellarmine. In 1 Peter 3 baptism saves us, as did Noah's water, but that
water effectually saved those who were in the ark.

Protestant reply. I. Comparisons must not be extended beyond their scope.
II. The answer is contained in the passage itself. 1. “Not the putting away
of the filth of the flesh”; that is, as Cajetan interprets it, “the discussion
does not concern the washing that cleanses the body.” 2. “Through the
resurrection of Jesus Christ”; that is, as the same Cajetan says, “through
faith and hope in the resurrection of Jesus Christ.”

10. Bellarmine. Isaiah says in chapter 1, “Wash yourselves; make yourselves
clean.” In Ezekiel 36 clean water is said to be poured upon the faithful. In
Micah 7 sins are said to be cast into the depths of the sea. In Zechariah 13
an open fountain is promised to the house of David.

Protestant reply. The force of this fiction—that the prophets in these
passages foretold outward baptism, something unknown to the apostles—rests
only on allegorical accommodations by one Father or another.

11. Bellarmine. The same is proved from the figures of the sacraments. Genesis
1, 7, 17, and so forth.

Protestant reply. The outward sacraments are figures. God did not institute
figures of figures, nor does Scripture teach them. Much less does Bellarmine,
as he trifles, make the waters of creation before sin was committed a figure
of baptism that washes from sin.

12. Bellarmine. The same is proved from councils and the Fathers.

Protestant reply. Sacramental phrases prove nothing more in the Fathers'
writings than they do in the Scriptures. Other rhetorical phrases can
amplify the same matter, but they prove nothing new.

13. Bellar—

[1632 witness | printed p. 19]

—mine. The sacraments are signs instituted not as theoretical signs merely to
signify, but as practical signs instituted to signify and effect.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself immediately replies: “We sometimes call
all those signs practical that are referred to a work, whether mediately or
immediately.” This is how the term was understood in the dispute concerning
the minister's intention.

Bellarmine. Here, however, we call practical those signs that operate
immediately and by their own proper power.

Protestant reply. Although he assumes this license to change practical signs
into theoretical and theoretical signs into practical at will, it will be
enough for us if the matter itself is proved: namely, that the sacraments are
practical in the sense that they operate grace immediately and by their own
proper power.

14. Bellarmine. If the sacraments operated only by representing something to
the mind, it would be foolish to employ these signs where there is no use of
the mind. But they are employed for those who do not understand, for infants,
deaf people, the insane, and sleeping people are baptized.

Protestant reply. We have no example in Scripture concerning the insane and
sleeping people. Deaf people can use their mind and eyes, although they do not
use their ears. Concerning infants, an answer was given above from
Bonaventure, Book 4, distinction 1, question 2: “Just as another's faith
suffices for an infant, so it suffices that something is signified to the
infant in another.” That is, it is signified to those who understand, and in
them to the infant, the grace that God communicates to the infant. The
inference is therefore false. II. It is also false from Bellarmine's own
concessions. For when he concedes that a practical sign is instituted “to
signify and effect,” it follows that it operates only by signifying, though
not merely by signifying. III. Beyond representation it can operate through
God's effective appointment and assistance, as the masters teach in
Bonaventure at the passage cited above, and yet neither immediately nor by
its own proper power—which is what Bellarmine had to conclude. IV. Moses'
striking of the rock in Exodus 17 was a practical sign of the miracle that
followed, but it did not immediately and by its own power exercise that
practice.

15. Bellarmine. If the sacraments were bare signs instituted to arouse the
mind, they could very easily have been instituted by people and would have
possessed the same power. But this cannot be said; therefore neither can the
former.

Protestant reply. I. Neither do we affirm the former: that the sacraments are
bare signs instituted to arouse the mind. Rather, they are seals of God's
mind and will toward us in the communication of his grace. II. The inference
is not merely false but impious. Bellarmine regards the sacraments of the Old
Testament and John's baptism as bare signs. Yet if he said that those signs
could have come from people and not from heaven, he would be more foolish—

[1632 witness | printed p. 20]

—than the Jewish crowd and more shameless than the Pharisees. Matthew 28:25.
“To determine which sign is to be used for signifying belongs to the one who
signifies. But God is the one who signifies spiritual things to us through
perceptible utterances in the sacraments.” These are the words of Thomas,
part 3, question 60, article 5, reply 1.

16. Bellarmine. The sacraments depend upon God as the principal agent and upon
Christ's passion and death. Therefore they truly operate by effecting. For
otherwise they would not need Christ's merits, nor would Christ have given
them any power.

Protestant reply. Christ merited nothing for the sacraments but for us. Nor
does he properly give his power to them; rather, he communicates it to us
through them. He does this by the merit of his death and the Spirit's
assistance, in whose dispensation God must necessarily be the principal
agent.

17. Bellarmine. If the sacraments operated only by signifying and arousing
faith, they would bring us no certainty of grace. For the sacrament would then
depend upon our faith, not upon Christ's institution.

Protestant reply. Faith is confirmed through them when they operate not merely
by signifying theoretically but also by sealing practically. Consequently,
they do not properly depend upon faith; faith depends upon them, and they upon
God's institution.

18. Bellarmine. The sacraments stand in the opposite relation to faith from
the word, for God's word precedes faith, while the sacraments follow it, at
least in adults. Therefore they do not operate in the same way.

Protestant reply. This is what we teach: the sacraments confirm faith
previously engendered through God's word, at least in adults. Yet they do not
operate in a contrary way, but in a different order.

19. Bellarmine. The sacraments are equally effective if administered in a
private language and if administered in the common language. Therefore they
do not operate in the manner of a sermon but have their own effect beyond the
arousing of faith.

Protestant reply. I. We deny that they are equally effective in every respect.
II. They are effective because they teach not only with words but also with
things. III. In the ordained way they are effective, at least in adults, only
when they are understood.

20. Bellarmine. If the sacraments operated by arousing faith, learned people
who can read the Scriptures would not need them, nor would the unlearned who
hear sermons. Indeed, a picture of baptism painted on a panel would surpass
baptism.

Protestant reply. This last statement about a picture is Naaman's very own
utterance. 2 Kings 5: “Are not Abana and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus,
better than all the waters of Israel?” The rest rests on a twofold error:
I. the sacraments are considered separately from the word and not jointly
with it; II. the sacrament's particular sealing from God's special institution
and blessing is not observed in the sacramental arousing of faith.

21. Bellarmine. If the sacraments operated in the manner of sermons, for exam—

[1632 witness | printed p. 21]

—ple, they would benefit all those present toward faith. But now they benefit
only the recipients. Otherwise it would be enough for one person to be
baptized or communicate on behalf of the whole multitude, and there would be
no need for each person to be baptized and communicate.

Protestant reply. I. If an equally direct mode and the same degree of benefit
are meant, we deny the inference. If the same mode in general, but in a
different degree and redounding indirectly, is meant, we deny the assumption.
II. The very words of the sermon by which Christ told the penitent woman,
“Your sins are forgiven,” benefited, or at least could have benefited, all
who heard them, but not in the same mode and degree as the woman to whom they
were said. Nor would it have been superfluous for any of them later to hear
the same words spoken by Christ to himself.

Our arguments.

22. Protestant argument. The just shall live by faith. Therefore the
sacraments are not causes of grace and life except through the mediation of
faith.

Bellarmine 1. Scripture does not say, “The just shall live by faith alone.”
Therefore let the sacraments retain their place, just as God and Christ do.

Protestant reply. I. It is indeed not said in precisely those words, but it is
nevertheless said clearly enough. II. The sacraments must retain their place,
but if they were immediate instruments of grace, they would occupy another's
place—either faith's or the Holy Spirit's.

Bellarmine 2. Scripture also says that a person lives by the sacraments when
it says that a person is regenerated through baptism.

Protestant reply. But neither does Scripture say this, nor does reason admit
two immediate instrumental causes of righteousness and life.

Bellarmine 3. Those prophetic words, “The just shall live by faith,” do not
literally signify that a just person is made or reckoned just by faith, as the
Lutherans maintain.

Protestant reply. Yet this is what the apostle Paul plainly intends in Romans
1:17.

23. Protestant argument. At the end of Mark the Lord, when treating faith and
baptism, says only of faith, not baptism, that its absence brings
condemnation: “He who does not believe shall be condemned.” Therefore faith,
not baptism, is the proper cause of grace and salvation.

Bellarmine. The Lord does not say, “He who has not been baptized shall be
condemned,” because there was no need to say this, since it was understood of
itself from what had been said: “He who does not believe shall be condemned.”

Protestant reply. But since condemnation is here properly attributed to the
absence of faith, not of baptism, it is sufficiently indicated that grace and
salvation properly depend upon faith, not baptism. If the matter were
otherwise, it would have been necessary for this very reason to say: “He who
has not been baptized shall be condemned.”

Bellarmine. He who does not believe consequently does not wish to be
baptized.

Protestant reply.—

[1632 witness | printed p. 22]

—But a person who believes can be unwilling, or not willing, to be baptized.
Thus Bellarmine in chapter 11: “He who has faith and does not wish to receive
the sacrament is condemned.”

24. Protestant argument. Paul denies that Abraham was justified by
circumcision and says that circumcision was a seal of the righteousness of
faith.

Bellarmine. That is, this privilege was given to Abraham, that he should be
the first to possess the sacrament of circumcision, because his obedience and
faith had merited it.

Protestant reply. That circumcision was a reward or recompense to Abraham for
faith as a merit is Bellarmine's invention alone. The Jews maintained that
circumcision was a merit. Paul proves the contrary, because justification had
preceded it, and because Abraham had nothing about which he could boast before
God. Bellarmine, on the contrary, maintains that Abraham possessed merits
before circumcision and received circumcision as a glorious reward about
which he could boast by his own right.

Bellarmine 2. The reasoning is not the same for circumcision and baptism, for
Paul says that circumcision is nothing. 1 Corinthians 7.

Protestant reply. The same Paul also says that baptism and the Lord's Supper
are nothing without faith and obedience. 1 Corinthians 10. Yet it must be
observed that in that passage the apostle teaches that circumcision is nothing
after Christ's death, not that it previously was nothing to Abraham, unless
perhaps in the sense in which Paul who plants and Apollos who waters are said
to be nothing. 1 Corinthians 3:7.

25. Protestant argument. God alone can forgive sins. Luke 5.

Bellarmine. Christ as man forgave sins, and people do so in Christ's name; but
God alone does so by his own authority.

Protestant reply. Christ forgave as the God-man. People forgive—as the Master
rightly teaches in *Sentences*, Book 4, distinction 18—by showing that people
have been absolved from sin. But the papists maintain that baptism forgives
sins and justifies, not merely by showing and testifying but by properly and
immediately effecting it, which is proper to God and Christ.

26. Protestant argument. Ephesians 5: “Cleansing her by the washing of water
in the word of life,” where the entire power of cleansing is attributed to
the word, whose operation consists in arousing faith.

Bellarmine 1. By the “word of life” is not understood a sermon, whose effect
is faith, but the form of the sacrament, that is, the consecratory word.

Protestant reply. It means the word through which we are regenerated: “But
this is the word which was preached to us,” as Peter expressly testifies in
1 Peter 1:23, 25.

27. Protestant argument. Where there is a promise, faith that receives the
promise is necessarily required. But the word of promise is required in every
sacrament. There—

[1632 witness | printed p. 23]

—fore, and so forth.

Bellarmine 1. The word of promise is indeed required in the institution of
the sacrament, but it is not required in the sacrament's actual use.

Protestant reply. Its use must accord with its institution, and it contains
within itself the essence of the institution. A thing instituted for a
definite end cannot rightly be employed toward that end unless the power of
the institution is employed at the same time. But in the sacraments this power
is God's promise.

Bellarmine 2. Faith can be required as a condition applying the sacrament to
a person, as when a physician offers a medicine and promises health, faith is
required in the sick person by which he receives that promise seriously and
assents to the physician. Yet it is not that faith which heals the sick
person, but the medicine, although without faith the medicine would not have
been received.

Protestant reply. I. Faith applies promises and the things promised, not the
sacraments. Hebrews 11:13. II. In a sick person, faith by which he assents to
the physician is not necessarily required for the medicine's efficacy. A
medicine is effective when administered to the insane against their will.
But faith is necessarily required for the sacrament's efficacy. Thus
Bellarmine in chapter 9: “For one who does not believe, even if he wished to
be baptized, it would benefit him nothing.” III. Scripture everywhere
testifies that faith is what heals and saves sinners.

Bellarmine 3. Both faith and the sacrament can be necessary, so that one
without the other would not suffice.

Protestant reply. But both cannot be necessary as the immediate cause. Either
faith justifies through the sacrament's mediation, as the papists affirm, or
the sacrament through faith's mediation, as we affirm.

28. Protestant argument. Those who say that the sacraments justify properly
and immediately draw people away from God and cause them to rest in the sight
of a corporeal thing, not in God himself.

Bellarmine 1. Then the bronze serpent ought not to have been set forth, nor
ought God himself to have become incarnate.

Protestant reply. It does not follow, because the bronze serpent was not set
forth to justify immediately, and to rest in God incarnate is to rest in God.

Bellarmine 2. When we say that God operates through the sacraments, we draw
people to God. Those whom Christ cured by means of spittle and clay did not
rest in the spittle and clay.

Protestant reply. I. Those who say that God justifies properly and immediately
through the sacraments set before people another proper and immediate object,
besides God and Christ, in which they should rest for justification. II.
Spittle and clay were not the proper and immediate causes of the cures
miraculously effected by Christ.

29. Protestant argument. God's word and the sacraments justify in the same
way, for justification is attributed equally to the word and the sacra—

[1632 witness | printed p. 24]

—ments. Romans 1:16. And just as the hand of our faith that receives the
offered grace must concur with God's hand offering in the word, Hebrews 4:2,
so also in the sacraments.

Bellarmine 1. By the gospel in Romans 1:16 can be understood not preaching but
the gospel mysteries themselves: the incarnation, the passion, and the
sacraments.

Protestant reply. Those two things, which are perpetually joined in Scripture,
are clumsily separated. The same Paul in 1 Corinthians 1:18, 21 expressly
affirms that the word of the cross and the foolishness of preaching save
people.

Bellarmine 2. It is explained there how the gospel saves. For the apostle
adds, “For the righteousness of God is revealed in it from faith to faith.”
But Scripture nowhere explains in this way the mode by which the sacraments
save.

Protestant reply. What Bellarmine intends by his “in this way” he has left
uncertain. But it is plain, and Scripture everywhere declares when it speaks
of the sacraments, that God's righteousness is revealed in them as in signs.
Nor will Bellarmine himself say that every revelation of God's righteousness
is not to be received by faith.

Bellarmine 3. Scripture attributes the power of justifying to both the word
and the sacraments, but it does not say, nor may one infer from this, that they
justify in the same way.

Protestant reply. Since both justify as God's instruments, and both by their
nature signify something to us which must be received by faith for
righteousness and salvation, it follows that both justify through the
mediation of faith.

Bellarmine 4. The word is God's hand that offers. But the sacrament is the
hand that applies and operates. Faith, meanwhile, is our hand, which removes
obstacles and disposes the soul, where such a disposition is necessary.

Protestant reply. I. The word does not merely offer but operates through the
Spirit's assistance, for it is “the ministry of the Spirit.” 2 Corinthians
3:6, 8. The sacrament operates by applying, that is, by offering specially,
as the offering of spiritual food is accurately represented in the Lord's
Supper. Thus far, therefore, there is no difference in the mode. II. Christ
attributes to faith not a preparatory disposition but the very approach to
himself, the eating of his flesh, and the drinking of his blood. John 6:29,
35.

30. Protestant argument. It cannot happen that the sacraments, which are
gross and material things, penetrate to the soul and there, as if by creating,
engender a divine and supernatural power, and that in a moment. Nor can it be
understood what that power of the sacrament would be like, in which part of it
the power would inhere, or when it would operate.

Bellarmine 1. A corporeal and gross thing can act upon the soul, not by its own
nature but by God's power.

Protestant reply. But then that power of God must establish some power in the
corporeal thing which possesses a proportion to the ob—

[1632 witness | printed p. 25]

—ject upon which it acts. For an agent exercising influence upon a passive
recipient must in some manner possess within itself a homogeneous power. Cano
therefore rightly says in *On the Sacraments*, part 4: “A sacrament does not
reach by its instrumental power the supernatural quality which is grace,
because then either a new quality would be produced in the sacrament to reach
this effect or it would not. The first is ridiculous. The second is absurd:
without a new power being added, the sacraments would effect what they could
not effect before.”

Bellarmine 2. The sacraments do not need contact in order to act.

Protestant reply. A corporeal instrument that really, physically, proximately,
and immediately acts upon another thing which it does not touch is a mere
chimera, no example of which can even be invented with any appearance or
likeness of truth. Therefore the argument drawn from the absence of contact
is most firm. The sacraments do not touch the soul. Therefore either they
immediately produce grace where they are not, namely in the soul, or they
effect in the air or an intervening body some quality productive of grace.
Both alternatives are utterly absurd.

Bellarmine 3. It is not improbable that a creature can concur, as a mere
instrument of God, in a true creation.

Protestant reply. Bañez proves in his commentary on Thomas, part 1, question
45, article 5, that a direct contradiction is implied in this fiction,
because it belongs to the intrinsic and essential character of an instrument
that it operate by its own power dispositively toward the principal agent's
action and that it properly conveys and applies the principal agent's power
to the effect. But a creature cannot by any power of its own operate upon
nothing, nor convey and apply God's infinite power to producing a being from
nothing.

Bellarmine 4. Grace is not properly created, but is brought forth from an
obediential potency, that is, from the natural capacity of a person, which is
such that from it can be made whatever God wills.

Protestant reply. If this verbal distinction is admitted, the communication
of grace will nevertheless differ no more from creation than raising up
people from stones differs from the creation of people.

Bellarmine 5. Concerning the time of operation, it could be said that
justification occurs in the last perceptible part of the sacramental action,
or it must be said that justification occurs in the instant in which the
sacramental action ends.

Protestant reply. Therefore either that action operates physically when it no
longer exists at all, or only its final part operates while the rest effect
nothing.

Bellarmine 6. The power through which a sacrament acts is only God's motion or
employment of it, not an inherent quality, whether spiritual or corporeal.

Protestant reply. The sacraments, then, are causes without an inherent power
by which a thing exists. They produce a second act without any first act.
Finally, they operate toward justifica—

[1632 witness | printed p. 26]

—tion in no other way than a stone or a piece of wood could, if it were moved
by God to that end without institution, word, or analogy.

Bellarmine 7. This sacramental power is partly in one part of the sacrament
and partly in another. Accordingly, it perishes piece by piece as the parts of
the sacrament perish. But the final particle operates by the power of all that
preceded it.

Protestant reply. I. Therefore a power that physically produces a thing in an
instant acts successively. II. The whole sacrament never exists at the same
time. III. When all the other parts have ceased, the final particle or
syllable is by itself the sacrament. IV. That final syllable is falsely said
to operate by the power of those that preceded it if they have perished
without their effect. For, as Scotus rightly observes, “The final thing never
causes the effect completively by the power of the preceding things unless
the preceding things leave behind some disposition preliminary to that end,
as drops hollow out a stone by falling repeatedly. But in sacramental words,
the preceding syllables leave behind no disposition before the final one has
been spoken.”

Bellarmine 8. This power is now in the words, now in the element, and now in
both.

Protestant reply. We therefore have a new distribution of the sacraments:
some consist only of the word, some only of the element, and some of both. For
the sacramental power must necessarily be in the whole sacrament. But the
opinion of the older scholastics, who attributed this power neither to the
element nor to the words but to God alone, was much simpler, weightier, and
safer. Thus William of Auvergne in *On the Sacraments*, Book 1, chapter 5:
“But if you ask whether sanctification comes from water, you must know that it
does not, but from God alone, the giver, who when invoked for this purpose is
present, acts, and operates inwardly in the likeness and proportion of what
has to operate outwardly.”
