CHAPTER SEVEN

ON THE STATE OF FALLEN HUMANITY

ON ROMANS 8:7

“Because what the flesh savors is hostility against God; for it is not subject
to God's law, for indeed it cannot be. Verse 8: Therefore, those who are in the
flesh cannot please God.”

Here is a celebrated passage concerning the ἀδυναμία, or inability, of the
natural human being. For it denies concerning

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 325 | PDF p. 376]

the natural human being both the act and the ability, both in the intellect and
in the will, to perform what pleases God.

It is asked whether the Arminians, together with their Socinians, rightly deny
that the knowledge of God is natural to a human being, so that human beings now
after the fall are ἄθεοι, “atheists,” by nature itself, and the fact that human
beings believe God exists must be attributed to grace, not nature. Thus
Episcopius, private disputation *On the Knowledge of God*, corollaries 2–3:
“We deny that the knowledge of God which is possessed from nature is natural.”
Arminius calls the remnants of the image of God which survive in us after the
fall supernatural grace. So also Socinus, *Theological Lectures*, chapter 2,
folio 3.

We decide against them by the following assertions.

Assertion 1. One atheism is purely speculative; another is practical.
Speculative atheism occurs when the mind, considered simply as mind, inclines
to this conclusion: “There is no God.” This is direct atheism. Practical
atheism, however, whether virtual or by consequence, belongs to those who
interpretatively deny that God exists, either by their life or by a doctrinal
consequence.

Assertion 2. Speculative atheism contains under itself two kinds of speculative
atheists. The first are ἄθεοι, purely mental atheists, in settled disposition
and with the mind's full and habitual persuasion. Against the adversaries, no
such speculative atheists exist.

1. “The work of the law is engraved by nature upon human hearts,” Romans
2:14–15. “The heavens declare the glory of God,”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 326 | PDF p. 377]

Psalm 19:1. “That they should seek God, if perhaps by groping they might find
him, although he is not far from each one of us. For in him we live, move, and
have our being,” Acts 7:17, 27–28. “Because what can be known about God is
manifest in them,” Romans 1:19.

2. Natural conscience dictates to all human beings that God exists. And as for
what the Socinians say about peoples in the New Western World, in Brazil, and
in many places of India, who, as Ostorodius says in his *Institutes of the
Christian Religion*, have no suspicion of deity: they nevertheless worship
some divinity and have some religion. This is a sign that, with nature as
their guide, they acknowledge God.

[Marginal note: 4]

3. Human beings would be worse than beasts if they naturally believed nothing
about God; indeed, they would be worse than the devils, who, as Voetius
observes in *Thersites Heautontimorumenos*, section 2, chapter 4, page 34, are
not speculatively ἄθεοι, “atheists.” For “they believe that God exists and
tremble,” James 2:19.

4. Why do those who deny that God exists dread judgment when they are dying,
if they do not know by a natural disposition that God exists? This is evident
in the dying Diagoras, who shuddered at death and judgment. And Suetonius says
of Caligula in chapter 51: “Although he despised the gods so greatly, at the
slightest thunder and lightning he used to close his eyes and cover his head;
but at greater storms he would leap from his bed and hide under it.” They also
report that no one was more timid than Dionysius, that mocker of divine
judgment.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 327 | PDF p. 378]

Assertion 3. Nevertheless, there are ἄθεοι, “atheists,” in a speculative act.
1. Those who by desire and effort would wish that there were no God. 2. Those
who deny him temporarily through doubt, a storm by which even the godly
themselves—I speak from experience—are often shaken. 3. Those who, by malicious
suppression, temporarily bury nature and the sense of deity. For the will has
such command over the intellect that it clouds its act while the disposition
lies hidden in the heart. Yet the disposition by which they are certain that
God exists is always in them.

Assertion 4. Some practical ἄθεοι, “atheists,” are so in doctrine, such as
idolaters, who by consequence deny God and transform him into an idol. Others
say by their ungodly life that there is no God. The world abounds with such
ἀθέοις, “atheists.” And the human being is always an ἄθεος, “atheist,” with the
lips, not with the heart.

It is asked whether the mind is blind and darkened in understanding
supernatural mysteries, so that inward illumination is necessary to understand
them. The Remonstrants teach in their *Apology*, chapter 2, folio 34, that the
light of the Holy Spirit is not required for understanding the senses of
Scripture. Episcopius, disputation 3, thesis 3: “A supernatural light
superadded to the faculty is not required.” *Confession*, chapter 1, section
14: “Unlearned people endowed with ordinary sense and judgment can adequately
attain the meaning of the canonical books.” So teach the Socinians: Ostorodius,
*Institutes*, book 1, chapter 5; Smalcius, in his refutation of the book *On the
Incarnation*, chapter 9, folios 19 and 55, says that “there is nothing in the
whole faith of Christ which reason cannot grasp after the gospel revelation
has come forth.”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 328 | PDF p. 379]

We teach that revelation is threefold. One is on the part of the subject matter
revealed and found in the gospel. Because only God could devise the doctrines
of the gospel, this revelation is from God alone. 2. Another is revelation of
the object, or objective revelation. This is the clear presentation of the
doctrines of the gospel through natural forms, and it is literal revelation,
or external illumination, by which someone knows the grammatical sense of the
gospel. 3. Revelation is on the part of the intellectual faculty, when not only
the object and letter of the gospel with respect to its grammatical sense are
understood—which is accessible to natural and unlearned human beings—but it is
also known by spiritual evidence and by a new power superadded to the
intellect. The present question concerns this revelation. We maintain that the
intellect requires this infused power.

1. Matthew 16:16–17: “Flesh and blood does not reveal to Peter that Mary's son
is the true Messiah, or that Christ, the Son of the living God, but the heavenly
Father.” Matthew 11:25–26: “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that
you have hidden these things from the wise and intelligent and revealed them
to infants. Yes, Father, because so it pleased you.” John 1:5: “And that light
shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it.” 1 John 2:27:
“The anointing of the Spirit teaches you all things.” 1 Corinthians 2:14: “For
the natural human being is not capable of the things which are of God's Spirit;
for they are foolishness to him, and he cannot know them, because they are
spiritually

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 329 | PDF p. 380]

discerned.” Romans 8:7: “Because what the flesh savors is hostility against God;
for it is not subject to God's law, for indeed it cannot be.”

2. The removal of the veil placed over the eyes of the mind, 2 Corinthians
3:16; the shining of light in the heart, 2 Corinthians 4:6; the opening of the
eyes, Psalm 119:18; the giving of eye-salve with which the eyes are anointed so
that we may see, Revelation 3:18; the gift of the Spirit of wisdom and
revelation in the knowledge of Christ, so that with the eyes of our minds
illuminated we may know what the hope of the calling is, Ephesians 1:17–18; and
the gift of strength in the intellect by which, with all the saints, we may be
able to comprehend what the breadth, length, depth, and height are, and what the
love of Christ which surpasses knowledge is, Ephesians 3:18–19—all are special
gifts of the Spirit of sanctification for which we both pray to God and give
thanks in Christ.

3. If the clear presentation of the truth and the bare illumination of the
object were sufficient, without an infused power in the intellect, then human
beings would understand the mysteries of the gospel by the preaching of the
Word alone and by bare persuasion. Thus grace would be nothing other than the
teaching of the law and the gospel, as Pelagius said.

4. If a clear presentation of the truth were sufficient for understanding the
mysteries of God's kingdom, as the Remonstrants say in articles 3 and 4 of the
*Synodical Writings*, page 164, a human being would be no more blind and
powerless to understand them than an entirely natural human being is blind and
powerless to understand Aristotle's acroamatic works and metaphysical
disputations.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 330 | PDF p. 381]

For if a capable intellect is present, a human being needs only a clear
presentation of the truth to acquire knowledge. Therefore, the Spirit's
eye-salve, the anointing which teaches all things, the Spirit of wisdom and
revelation, and the opening of the eyes would be as necessary for understanding
Aristotle's *Metaphysics* as for knowing John's Gospel.

5. This external persuasion through a clear presentation of the truth, apart
from every superadded light and supernatural power in the intellect, is the
work only of a pastor and a person declaiming rhetorically, such as Cicero or
Demosthenes. The operation of the Holy Spirit, by which he opens and illuminates
the heart and teaches a human being inwardly what he hears outwardly, would be
entirely unnecessary here.

6. If the will and affections can embrace everything which the intellect judges
to be good, as the Remonstrants teach, then no grace and no superadded
supernatural power would be necessary for true faith and conversion to Christ.
Thus grace is an empty title and a name without a reality.

7. Every light required in faith would come from the bare letter of the gospel
clearly and distinctly presented.

8. To write commentaries, perceive the senses of Scripture, produce true faith,
and inwardly illuminate the mind, it would not be necessary for us to implore
the Holy

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 331 | PDF p. 382]

Spirit and light infused from heaven. Nor would there be any reason for Peter
rather than Judas to give God thanks for the revealed gospel. Also read and
compare what was disputed above concerning this question and subject in
chapter 1, page 83.

It is asked whether there is in the will a power for supernatural acts, or
whether the will is powerless in supernatural acts. The Arminians deny this
inability. We affirm it.

1. For Scripture speaks thus, Ezekiel 36:26–27: “I will give you a new heart
and put a new spirit in your midst. I will remove the stony heart from your
flesh. I will put my Spirit in you and cause you to walk in my commandments.”
Jeremiah 32:39–40: “I will give them one heart and one way, that they may fear
me. I will put my fear in their heart, that they may not depart from me.”
Ephesians 2:5: “God made us, when dead in the sins in which we walked, alive
together with Christ.” John 6:44: οὐδεὶς δύναται ἐλθεῖν, “No one is able to
come, unless my Father who sent me draws him.” Romans 8:8: “Therefore, those
who are in the flesh cannot please God.” Matthew 7:18: “A bad tree cannot bring
forth good fruits.”

2. The glory of conversion could not be ascribed wholly to God's grace if the
will were not thus powerless.

3. There would be no need at all to pray for the infusion of new strength into
the will if the will were not powerless from itself.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 332 | PDF p. 383]

4. For by nature we are dead in sins, children of wrath, and not even sufficient
to think anything good from ourselves, Ephesians 2:1–3; 2 Corinthians 3:5.

5. For without Christ we can do nothing, John 15:5; that is, unless we are
grafted into him like branches in a tree, drawing life from him.

6. Christ died in vain and purchased the grace of regeneration for us in vain
if the will were from itself the principle of supernatural acts, Ephesians
2:10:13.

It is asked whether the will always and in every condition of this life in
which legislation, exhortation, the promise of rewards, and the threat of
punishments have a place possesses the freedom to refuse and to will the good
shown by the intellect. The Remonstrants affirm it in the *Synodical Writings*,
articles 3–4, page 7. We deny it.

1. For then freedom would not be injured, but only the mind darkened. The will
would not be powerless in spiritual things, nor would a new heart and a new
spirit need to be infused. A clear presentation of the truth would suffice, as
they claim.

2. If the intellect showed that coming to the Son of God is good, as happened
in Balaam and in many unregenerate human beings, then the will could choose to
believe and come to the Son of God without the Father's drawing, contrary to
Christ's statement in John 6:44.

3. The teaching of the law and gospel alone would be

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 333 | PDF p. 384]

sufficient grace for conversion, if the will always possessed the freedom to
will and refuse the good shown by the intellect.

4. Nothing other than the objective presentation of the truth to the intellect
would need to be prayed for unto true conversion, since the intellect can of
itself draw the will to embrace or reject the good shown by the intellect.

5. Whatever the Holy Spirit says in the Scriptures about our inability for
supernatural good will be empty.

6. For the Remonstrants say in the same place that “the will does not have the
freedom and power to will the good unless, in addition to the illumination of
the mind, the imparting of supernatural power is added”—if indeed they are
speaking sincerely.

It is asked whether the Arminians in the same place sincerely say that the will
is corrupt. Answer: No.

For in the *Synodical Writings*, articles 3–4, page 107, they say: “Adam did
not lose the strength to perform the obedience required in the new covenant,
insofar, that is, as it is considered formally—that is, insofar as it is
required in the new covenant.” *Hague Conference*, page 250: “The freedom to
act well or badly remained as a co-created part of nature.” And Grevinchovius,
page 183: “After the fall Adam retained the power to believe, and the rest of
the reprobate retained it in him.” *Remonstrant Apology*, chapter 6, folio 76:
They maintain that indifference toward opposites is essential to the will and
survives in the state of corruption. *Hague

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 334 | PDF p. 385]

Conference*, pages 253–254: “The human will remained so uninjured that it is
not made alive in regeneration, nor is a new will toward the good given.” So
also the impure Socinus, *Theological Lectures*, chapter 5, folios 15–16. Thus,
among the Roman Catholics, Stapleton, volume 2, *On Original Sin*, book 2,
chapter 9, denies that a new power is infused into the will in regeneration;
instead, he says that the preexisting power is only disposed, awakened, and
strengthened.

It is asked whether there is in a person not yet regenerated any prior
disposition on account of which God is moved, by congruous or condign merit, to
confer the grace of conversion.

The state of the question has several forms. For the question may concern first
grace simply so called, which the Jesuits Suarez, Valentia, and Molina call a
remote disposition. Or the question may concern the efficacious grace of
conversion, which is not first grace simply, but only the first grace in the
act of formal regeneration: namely, whether there is in a human being any
condition, disposition, cause, merit, or reason such that, when it is posited,
God infallibly converts the human being.

Or 2. whether there is in a human being any natural preparation which moves God
to give grace, or any supernatural preparation.

Or 3. whether there is on the human being's part a moral condition required of
him by duty, or a disposing cause, merit, or reason, such that, if the human
being fulfills that condition either by the powers of nature or by the aid of
more common grace, God is moved

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 335 | PDF p. 386]

either by common equity and justice, by congruity and divine propriety, by a
special law made in Christ the Mediator and by the force and efficacy of his
merits, or by a generous covenant and promise, infallibly to give either
supernatural aid through whose good use the human being is successively
advanced to the grace of regeneration, or conversion itself to be performed.
The adversaries affirm this. We deny it.

The question, therefore, is not whether God simply requires obedience from
those not yet converted, but whether he requires the kind of obedience which
necessarily has the subsequent grace of conversion, either by title of merit
or by the gratuitous promise of God's mercy.

2. Nor is it asked whether preparations precede in a human being who is to be
converted. We by no means deny that. Rather, it is asked whether those
preparations have in themselves a necessary connection with the grace of
conversion.

Nor 3. is it asked whether the disposition of a new heart is infused into a
human being who uses none of the means of his conversion—that is, one who
neither reads nor hears and is not shaken by the terrors of the law—as into a
log or lifeless stone.

But we teach: 1. God converts human beings with respect to no merit, worth,
uprightness, or infallibilizing cause in the human being, but from his own sheer

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 336 | PDF p. 387]

grace in our Lord Jesus Christ.

2. We teach that the human being is active, not passive, in using the external
means.

3. We teach that free choice is not here the cause which distinguishes and
separates the converted from the unconverted, but God's efficacious grace is.

The first question, therefore, is whether, by any law founded upon Christ's
merits, God confers prevenient grace upon the one who does what is in himself
from the faculty of nature alone. Valentia, Meratius, and others affirm it. We
deny it.

1. Isaiah 65:1: “I was sought by those who did not ask for me, and found by
those who did not seek me.” Ezekiel 16:8: “I said to you in your blood, Live.”
Ezekiel 36:26: “I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in your
midst.” Verse 32: “I will not do this for your sake,” and so forth; “be ashamed
and blush because of your ways.” Ephesians 2:5: “Even when we were dead in
sins, he made us alive together with Christ; by grace you have been saved.”
Colossians 1:12: “Giving thanks to the Father,” and so forth; verse 14, “who
rescued us from the power of darkness.” 2 Timothy 1:9: “Not from our works,”
and so forth. Titus 3:5: “He saved us, not from righteous works which we had
done, but according to his own mercy.”

2. For many who are indisposed and rebellious are called, such as Abraham when
worshiping idols, the thief on the cross, and Saul when ravaging God's church.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 337 | PDF p. 388]

3. No such law was made on account of Christ's merits. For in that case Christ
would have merited the preaching of the gospel, calling, justification, and
sanctification for all endowed with free choice, even Indians and Americans,
and would actually confer all these gifts of grace upon them provided that they
did what was in them from the faculty of nature alone. This is ἄγραφον,
“unwritten.”

4. Thus God would not “have mercy on whom he wills,” but because human beings
will, Romans 9:18.

5. God would not begin the good work in us, Philippians 1:6, but free choice
would begin it in us.

7. Free choice would first and chiefly distinguish the one who begins the work
of conversion from the one who does not begin it, 1 Corinthians 4:7.

It is asked whether God gives the grace of conversion to a human being because
he is better prepared, disposed, and humbled to receive it than another,
although he does not give it on account of any worth or merit in those
preparations, but from equity and congruity, from truthfulness, or from a
generous promise by which he obligated himself to give the grace of conversion
to those who rightly use the gifts of nature or more common grace. The
Arminians and Jesuits affirm it. We teach:

1. Ordinarily, the use of external means, such as reading and hearing the Word,
precedes conversion.

2. And to that extent God does not convert human beings instantaneously

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 338 | PDF p. 389]

as water is changed into wine, nor while they are unaware and thinking of no
such thing, as Balaam's donkey spoke and Caiaphas prophesied, nor unwilling and
resisting, as Simon carried the Savior's cross. Rather, he converts human beings
who are prepared, humbled, cast down, and subdued by consciousness of sin and
the terrors of the law.

3. We acknowledge in these inward preparations a physical and material
conduciveness in the human being through God's grace, by which he is prepared
for the grace of conversion. But against the Jesuits, Arminians, Pelagians, and
Socinians, we deny a moral conduciveness or formal preparation by which God has
obligated himself through a promise or covenant so that, from justice,
truthfulness, or promise, he is bound to give grace because one human being is
so prepared rather than another who is not prepared.

1. The passages of Scripture cited above prove this.

2. In the whole of Scripture there is no promise and no covenant such as this:
“The one who has duly prepared himself by doing this or that will receive
efficacious converting grace as a gratuitous reward for his work.”

3. No one is efficaciously called from works, but freely from grace and sheer
mercy, 2 Timothy 1:12; Titus 3:5; Ephesians 2:3.

4. “It does not belong to the one who wills or the one who runs, but to God who
shows mercy,” Romans 9:16.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 339 | PDF p. 390]

5. Christ establishes God's good pleasure alone as the cause why the gospel is
revealed to infants and not revealed to the prudent, not the wisdom of the
latter or the humility of the former, Matthew 11:25.

6. Flesh and blood is not the cause why Christ is revealed; only Christ's
heavenly Father is, Matthew 16:17.

7. The adversaries deny grace which predetermines to good acts, and they subject
habitual grace to the decision and motion of the created will, so that by
nature the will contributes to a good work before divine grace does. Hence, if
God is moved to give grace because of prior dispositions, conversion will begin
from the effort, contribution, and natural motion of free choice. But, to the
contrary, “God begins the good work in us,” Philippians 1:6. Neither a human
being nor an angel first gives anything to God, Romans 11:33–34.

8. Therefore, if this person is given the grace of conversion because he is
disposed by grace, then he first gave to God, and God is obligated to repay him
from a debt. Thus God does not give grace freely and solely from the intention
of relieving our misery, but for the sake of repaying kindness. On the contrary,
Paul in the same place proves by “Who first gave to him, and it will be repaid
to him?” that no human being or angel gives first to him, because everything a
human being or angel has is from him.

9. James 1:18: “Willingly and freely he regenerated us.” Therefore, he is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 340 | PDF p. 391]

not bound by such a law according to which he gives such grace because of prior
dispositions.

10. The praise and glory of our conversion will not belong wholly to God if
grace is given because free choice—even with the aid of grace which by no means
predetermines the will—prepared the human being for grace, and did so in such a
way that, notwithstanding grace, he could have failed entirely to prepare
himself.

11. Nor will there be reason for us to be grateful to God as the author of
conversion if free choice, determining itself and not determined by God, moves
or fails to move God to give the grace of conversion, unless he wishes to fail
his own justice and truthfulness.

It is asked whether God at least, from congruity and his own propriety, gives
the grace of conversion to all who are prepared and disposed for it. The
adversaries affirm it. We deny it.

1. If by propriety they understand a virtue in God opposed to impropriety, so
that God would act improperly if he denied grace to one well disposed, then God
would be strictly obligated to give grace, which is absurd. But it is
impossible for God to act improperly; and yet he would do nothing contrary to
an obligation or his own propriety if he denied grace to one well disposed. If,
however, by propriety they understand a fitting manner of God's action which
depends solely upon his good pleasure,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 341 | PDF p. 392]

as it is fitting for God to create the world and redeem the human race, and yet
he would have done nothing contrary to his propriety by not creating the world
or not redeeming the human race; and if in this manner God gives grace to one
well disposed from his own propriety, then he gives grace from his good pleasure
alone and from no true debt. This is our thesis.

2. “He will call us, not according to our works, but according to his own
purpose and grace,” 2 Timothy 1:9. Therefore, he gives grace from no debt or
congruous merit.

3. For he said to his people expiring in their own blood, “Live,” Ezekiel 16:8.
Therefore, it is not from congruous merit.

4. Every merit is in some manner a debt. But debt and grace are opposed to one
another, Matthew 20:14–15.

5. There is no infallible connection between prior dispositions and the grace
of conversion. Therefore, it is not given from congruous merit, for acting
against congruous merit conflicts with God's goodness and equity. Proof of the
antecedent: this connection is not founded upon any promise, because no such
promise exists in Scripture, nor upon God's justice or mercy, because God does
not act against justice, against mercy, or in any way against his own nature if
he denies grace to one well disposed.

6. Grace would not be gratuitous in every way

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 342 | PDF p. 393]

if it rested in any way upon our merit.

It is asked whether at least the prayer of a person not yet converted obtains
grace from God—not indeed by title of service and obedience, but nevertheless
by title of entreaty and a humble profession of misery—such as the prayers of
beggars who, while begging, ask for alms without being commanded by a superior.
Thus Martinez de Ripalda, *On the Supernatural Being*, volume 1, book 1,
disputation 19, sections 1–2, and other Jesuits. We deny it.

1. For there is no prayer except that which is commanded of us under the title
of obedience and service. Otherwise, prayer would be ἐθελοθρησκεία,
“self-chosen worship.” For we are expressly commanded to pray, Matthew 7:7;
Psalm 50:15; 1 Thessalonians 5:17; 1 Timothy 2:8.

2. Nor can those not yet converted pray, since they lack the Spirit of
adoption, “who intercedes for us,” Romans 8:18, as Augustine notes in *On the
Gift of Perseverance*, book 2, chapter 23. Nor can anyone pray without faith,
Mark 11:24; James 1:6.

3. For grace would thus be given to those asking and knocking because of the
impetratory force, not because of meritorious force. But the prayer of a person
not yet converted, being without the Holy Spirit and faith, obtains nothing.
And even a prayer which obtains something in some manner merits, according to
the Roman Catholics, because to obtain by praying is to merit by praying.

4. Prayer is nothing other than entreaty and a humble profession of our misery,
and it is wrongly

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 343 | PDF p. 394 | ends before Quæritur, An, quia Deus non dat gratiam homini]

contradistinguished from obedience, because by God's command we are obligated
to entreat and humbly profess our misery, 1 John 1:8–9. Therefore, the prayer
of the unconverted is wrongly compared with the prayers of beggars, who are
nevertheless commanded by the law of nature to beg.
