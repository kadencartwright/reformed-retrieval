VERSE 12. I looked back upon all my works which my hands had made, and upon the
labor I had laboriously performed; but behold, all these things are vanity and
affliction of spirit, so that there is no advantage under the Sun.

Thus far we have learned how he applied his mind to pleasure, how he acquired
all the servants and supports of pleasure, and finally how he received the
pleasure that could be derived from them. Now let us hear the judgment of
Solomon—or rather, of the Holy Spirit. In order that this judgment may be
understood to have been well considered and maturely deliberated, he says that
he surveyed and examined all these things in his mind. Yet the judgment is
this: all things are vanity and, further, affliction of spirit. Finally, none
of all these things has any advantage for establishing that tranquility in
which the human mind may rest. Nor is this surprising, since man's immortal
mind cannot be satisfied with these perishable things, but only with things as
like itself as possible. Nevertheless, nothing in this catalogue of pleasures
is shameful; there is nothing unworthy of a gentlemanly and liberal man. Here
there is nothing about fornication, gluttony, drunkenness, dancing, dice, or
playing cards. Thus it is not difficult, even without a teacher, to judge how
vain and unfruitful these things are and what affliction of mind they will one
day bring to men.

VERSE 13. Therefore I turned myself to look upon wisdom and all madness and
folly: (for what would the man do who should come after that king? That which
has already been done.)

VERSE 14. I saw indeed that there was advantage in wisdom over folly, just as
there is advantage in light over darkness.

VERSE 15. The wise man himself is like one whose eyes are in his head, but the
fool walks in darkness; yet I likewise recognized that the same outcome
happens to all these.

VERSE 16. Therefore I said with my mind, The outcome of the fool will also
happen to me, even to me; why, then, shall I be wise any further? And I said
with my mind, This too is vanity.

VERSE 17. For there will be no remembrance, either of the wise man or of the
fool forever, since the things that now exist are all consigned to oblivion in
the days to come. Yet does the wise man himself die with the fool?

By an occupation the Prophet anticipates and at the same time removes what
might seem able to hinder him from delivering a true and uncorrupted judgment
concerning these shadows and images of happiness when he condemned all these
things as vanity.

The parts are: 1. The removal of the obstacle that might retard Solomon from
pronouncing judgment concerning the preceding shadows of happiness. 2. The
clearer and more explicit method by which Solomon pronounces judgment
concerning them.

The occupation is contained in the latter part of verse 13 and should be
enclosed in parentheses in such a way that the first part of verse 13 is
joined with the beginning of verse 14. For as Solomon ascended the tribunal to
pronounce what is called a definitive judgment concerning these three shadows
of happiness, it could be objected that from his own experience alone—upon
which he was going to lay the foundations of his judgment—he was not a
sufficiently competent judge to pass judgment concerning them. For no general
conclusion can be drawn from a singular and individual case; nor does it
follow that no tranquility of mind can be found in these things during this
life merely because some one man cannot attain it.

From this we learn what is the office and what is the power of a Minister of
the Word: namely, that he should both anticipate in his mind every difficulty
and doubt that can sprout and arise in the minds of his hearers, and then
uproot them entirely with the sickle of the Divine Word as if they were thorns
and briars. He should so level the road of men, rough because of their
immaturity, that every occasion for stumbling and striking the foot is
removed, and they may run with unobstructed foot toward the truth and toward
embracing salvation in the truth. And this is that power in the preaching of
the Word which the Apostle in Titus 2:7–8 calls *incorruptness*
(`ἀδιαφθορίαν`) and *speech beyond condemnation* (`ἀκατάγνωστον λόγον`),
since what is delivered in this way cannot be shaken.

Things would go well with the Church, as far as the Ministry is concerned, if
Ministers were as fully equipped in every respect as the citizens of the
Church appear to be, since a difficulty or doubt is very rarely brought
forward by the Church's citizens—and if the Ministers had resolved every
difficulty and unfolded every covering of the Scriptures. But this is not how
things stand, and it supplies us with no great ground for boasting, since this
is due not so much to the diligence of the Ministers as to the inertia of the
Church's citizens and their thoughtlessness in sacred things.

Solomon's answer is taken from a comparison with a lesser case: no one can
accomplish more, especially no one equal to such and so great a king as no one
ever was before or ever will be afterward—whether equal to him in wisdom for
devising and investigating, or in power and riches for executing, much less
superior to him. This is confirmed by the testimony of God himself; see 1
Kings.

For this very reason Solomon could most properly take cognizance of this case
and decide it by a judicial judgment. And yet this declaration,
which properly belongs to Solomon, is frequent in the mouths of many: they
boast that they will dispute, fight, or contend in a race or wrestling match
with anyone whatsoever and, to express it in their own words, with anyone who
bears a head. All such speech is plainly Thrasonical, that is, supported by no
firm foundation, because those men do not know the eminence of virtues and
gifts in others as Solomon did. To this belongs the proud and swollen boast of
Campion, who, though ignorant of the Greek language, challenged the whole
Senate of learned men to a contest. Thus you may readily recognize God's just
judgment upon idolaters, whom he hands over to a spirit of pride and display,
Romans 1. So far are they from having Solomon as the author and patron of
boasting that, on the contrary, he furnishes an example of modesty. For
although by his own right and the testimony of the Holy Spirit he could truly
have said that everyone would be inferior to him in these pursuits, he says
less: namely, that they will accomplish nothing which he had not done before.
See Genesis 41:16 and Daniel 2:27–28. From this we are taught to abstain from
rash and inconsiderate judgment and not to wish to pronounce judgment upon
matters unknown to us or not thoroughly investigated; see Matthew 7:12 and
James 4:11. So much, then, for the occupation. There follows the clearer and
more explicit method of pronouncing the judgment.

In it two things are considered: first, how these shadows of happiness differ;
second, how they agree.
