# Response to Baudouin's Invectives (1562)

This directory presents the complete Latin prose and a new English translation
of John Calvin's *Responsio ad Balduini convicia* (*Response to Baudouin's
Invectives*).

## Work identity and controversy

The work is Calvin's second response in the controversy. In 1561 Georg
Cassander composed the anonymous *De officio pii ac publicae tranquillitatis
vere amantis viri in hoc religionis dissidio* after Baudouin solicited a
conciliatory formula. Baudouin took the tract to France. Calvin answered it in
the separately published *Responsio ad versipellem quendam mediatorem, qui
pacificandi specie rectum evangelii cursum in Gallia abrumpere molitus est*
(CO IX 525–560). The CO editors explicitly caution that the anonymous
“shape-shifting mediator” was distinct from Baudouin, although Calvin treated
them as one in that first response.

Baudouin then hurried into print a juridical work prepared earlier, *Ad leges
de famosis libellis et de calumniatoribus commentarius*, and added an attack
on Calvin. The present *Responsio ad Balduini convicia* answers that attack.
A letter from Calvin to Theodore Beza dated 24 December 1561 says that the
response was then being printed together with some of Baudouin's letters. In
the same letter Calvin says that weariness had made him regret taking up the
labor and that the reader would recognize how Baudouin's indignity had
exasperated him. The publication bears the date 1562.

The 1562 book was a composite, not merely this response. Its title begins
*Ioannis Calvini responsio ad Balduini convicia* and then lists Baudouin's
letters to Calvin concerning the laws on deserters, a letter by François
Duaren, an admonition by Antonius Contius, an anonymous letter to Baudouin
about consistency in religion and writing, and Jean Crespin's commentary on a
law concerning apostates. It comprised fifteen gatherings, A–P, 117 numbered
pages, and a final blank. Calvin's response, printed in larger italic type,
ends on original p. 40. Fourteen letters from Baudouin and the other documents
follow. The CO editors possessed an exemplar of this original composite and
reprinted only Calvin's opening response in the present location. “Complete”
in this directory therefore means the entire Calvin response, not every
document in the 1562 anthology.

The response was reprinted in Beza's collected *Tractatus* (1576, pp. 507–516;
1597, pp. 424–431; 1611, pp. 365–371; and the Amsterdam collection, pp.
316–321). Early French versions appeared in the collected *Opuscules* of 1566,
pp. 1974–1991, and 1611, pp. 2213–2234. Baudouin issued another response at
Paris in 1562; Beza answered it, with a brief Calvin preface, in a distinct
1563 publication.

## Title, paratext, and editorial apparatus

CO IX 561–562 form a nineteenth-century divisional title page reading
*Ioannis Calvini responsio ad Balduini convicia. 1562* across the space
otherwise occupied by two numbered columns. Its footer, “*Calvini opera. Vol.
IX*,” and gathering signature show that it belongs to the CO volume; it is not
a facsimile of the much longer 1562 composite title page. The next scanned
page, corresponding to columns 563–564, is blank. The continuous authorial
prose begins at CO IX 565.

The body includes square-bracketed locators such as `[pag. 4]` through
`[pag. 40]`, which identify page turns in the 1562 printing. They, the CO
running heads and page numbers, gathering signatures, terminal rule, and the
following CO footnotes are editorial apparatus and are excluded from
`latin.txt`:

- At p. 568 CO prints *Boinus* and records *Boionus* in Beza and Amsterdam.
- At p. 570 CO prints *perfidia* and says Beza and Amsterdam omit it.
- At p. 577 CO prints *Me vero innocentiam* and notes that the early French
  supports “I oppress innocence,” while Beza and Amsterdam read *Meam vero
  innocentiam* (“my innocence”).
- At p. 579 CO identifies *Claudius Cantiuncula* as Claude Chansonnette.
- At p. 580 CO prints *obstrictus* and records *adstrictus* in Beza and
  Amsterdam.

The normalized Latin retains CO's base readings. The English notes flag the
two places where a variant affects interpretation rather than silently
replacing the base text.

## Files

- [`latin.txt`](latin.txt) is a complete diplomatic-normalized transcription
  of Calvin's Latin prose. It preserves CO spelling, capitalization,
  punctuation, and Greek; joins printer line-end divisions; and marks every
  printed CO page containing the prose.
- [`english.md`](english.md) is a fresh English translation made from that
  transcription and checked against the facsimile. Its notes explain material
  textual, lexical, historical, and rhetorical ambiguities.

## Source audit

The Latin was extracted for orientation and then collated manually, line by
line, against 300-DPI renderings of all eight scan pages containing the prose.
Each scan page contains two numbered CO columns. The exact mapping is:

| UNIGE PDF page | CO pages | Content |
|---:|---:|---|
| 360 | 561–562 | CO divisional title spanning both column numbers |
| 361 | 563–564 | Blank opening |
| 362 | 565–566 | Calvin's prose |
| 363 | 567–568 | Calvin's prose |
| 364 | 569–570 | Calvin's prose |
| 365 | 571–572 | Calvin's prose |
| 366 | 573–574 | Calvin's prose |
| 367 | 575–576 | Calvin's prose |
| 368 | 577–578 | Calvin's prose |
| 369 | 579–580 | Calvin's prose and terminal rule |

Four CO turns divide printed words: 566/567 *simu-lans*, 567/568 *doc-trinae*,
573/574 *certa-mine*, and 577/578 *oppri-mere*. The transcription joins each
word and places the new CO marker before the complete joined word. The
576/577 turn falls between the two separate Greek words *Στοργὰς* and *εἶναι*;
the marker therefore remains between them. Other page starts preserve the
first complete printed word at the new CO page.

No complete public-domain English translation was located in the project's
bibliographic survey completed 11 August 2026. This translation was made
afresh from the Latin; no modern English translation supplied its wording.

The English in this directory is an **AI-assisted scholarly draft**. It has
received a sentence-by-sentence source check but not an independent review by
a human Latinist. Until that review occurs, quote the Latin and printed-page
locator for exact-wording claims and identify the English as this project's
draft translation.

Calvin's rhetoric is exceptionally abusive, repeatedly using animal,
excremental, criminal, and psychiatric imagery. The translation preserves
that rhetoric because softening it would misrepresent the source. His claims
about Baudouin, Cassander, Castalio, Servetus, Duaren, inheritance,
child-rearing, apostasy, and attempted return to France remain adversarial
claims in a polemical text; inclusion here is neither endorsement nor
independent historical verification.

Suggested citation:

> John Calvin, *Responsio ad Balduini convicia*, [Geneva: Jean Crespin], 1562,
> in *Calvini Opera* IX (1870), 561–580, normalized Latin and draft English
> translation, *Reformed Retrieval*.
