First, Isaiah 5. “Now therefore,” Jehovah says there, “inhabitants of
Jerusalem—indeed, men of Judah—please judge between me and my vineyard. What
more ought to have been done for my vineyard that I have not done in it? Why,
when I expected it to produce grapes, did it produce wild grapes?”

[Marginalia: Isaiah 5:3, 4. Lecture XXXII.]

Therefore, they say, those Jews had plainly sufficient help for conversion,
and consequently even the wicked and unbelieving can be given help sufficient
for salvation and for ordering a life pleasing to God, although they do not
actually embrace that grace.

[Marginalia: Answer.]

We reply, however, that even if this passage and similar ones may be applied
only to those who are called and live in the visible Church—and consequently
do nothing to establish that universal grace common to absolutely everyone—
nevertheless, in order to understand it rightly, we say that it must be taken
only of the sufficiency of external means: the preaching of the divine word,
benefits, admonitions, chastisements, and other things of this kind by which
the Lord invited them to salvation and repentance. Through these they could
have been converted, had they not been so wicked, rebellious, and stubborn.
It must not be taken of the sufficiency of internal grace by which a bad vine
is changed into a good one. For in that passage the Lord says that he rendered
to this bad vine those things which, had he done them in a good vine, would
undoubtedly have yielded fruits of faith and obedience.

[Marginalia: Vindication.]

But if someone presses the matter and objects, “Then he has no just cause for
remonstrating with the Jews, because he himself by no means supplied them with
internal grace sufficient for them to produce fruits of repentance, although
no one can have that grace unless he gives it.”

[Marginalia: Objection.]

I answer by denying the consequence. Through the sin by which they are made
unable to perform what God requires, they are not themselves released from
the obligation by which they are bound to live to God and render complete
obedience to him. They remain bound to that same duty nevertheless. Nor is
God obliged to bestow grace upon anyone; rather, according to his own most
free good pleasure, he has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills.
Whom he wills, he calls in such a way that they follow; whom he wills, he
calls only in such a way that he removes from them every pretext and excuse
for sin. Thus in the latter the glory of his justice and in the former the
glory of his most free and gratuitous grace shine the more brightly, as we
noted above in the proper place. This same ground of distinction between
those who are called through the ministry of the word is

[Marginalia: Rebuttal.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 176 | De Prædestinatione p. 132]

everywhere impressed upon us by Augustine. Thus in Questions on Exodus 18 he
says, “Pharaoh did not consent flexibly but
resisted inflexibly. It belonged to the divine dispensation,” he says, “that
for such a heart not only a just but an evidently just punishment was being
prepared, by which those who fear God would be corrected.” Elsewhere he says,
“Of two ungodly persons already advanced in age, why is this one called in
such a way that he follows the caller, while the other is either not called or
is not called in such a way that he follows the caller? The judgments are
unsearchable.” Likewise: “Why is the one persuaded in such a way that he is
won over, but the other is not? O depth of the riches!” Likewise: “This grace,
which by divine bounty is secretly bestowed upon human hearts, is rejected by
no hard heart.” Likewise: “If the Father had willed to teach those for whom
the word of the cross is foolishness so that they came to Christ, they would
undoubtedly have come,” and so forth. “But why does he not teach everyone? If
we say that those whom he does not teach are unwilling to learn, we will be
answered: Then what of the words addressed to him, ‘Lord, turning again, you
will make us alive,’” and so forth. Finally, in book 1 of Questions to
Simplicianus, he says: “The effect of mercy cannot be in a person’s power, so
that God has mercy upon him in vain if that person is unwilling. For if he
willed to have mercy upon them, he could call them in the way suited to them,
so that they would both be moved and understand and follow.” And again in the
same place: “God has mercy upon no one in vain. Whomever he has mercy upon, he
calls in the way he knows to be suitable to him, so that he does not reject
the caller.”

[Marginalia: Augustine, Questions on Exodus 18; On the Gift of Perseverance,
chapter 8; On the Spirit and the Letter, chapter 34; on John 6, Tractate 45;
On the Predestination of the Saints, chapter 8; To Simplicianus, book 1,
question 2.]

From these passages it becomes perfectly clear that the fact that this person
follows and obeys while the other does not arises not from the person called,
from either his free choice or even some sufficient grace common to both the
one who follows and the one who refuses, but from the caller and his special
grace. The Lord did not see fit to impart that same grace to the other—by a
judgment sometimes hidden and at other times manifest, but never unjust.

Second, Matthew 23: “Jerusalem, killer of the prophets and stoner of those who
are sent to you, how often I willed to gather your children, as a hen gathers
her chicks under her wings, and you were unwilling?” Behold, they will say,
Christ willed to gather them, and indeed willed it in such a way that he
complained about their unwillingness. But how will he do this legitimately if
he did not help them by his grace so that they could come if they willed,
without which he knew that they were invited in vain?

[Marginalia: Objection 2. Matthew 23:37.]

The answer is easy. First, Christ speaks in that passage not insofar as he is
God, disposing and determining all things with the Father in the eternal
counsel, and drawing, softening, and making alive the hearts of those whom he
wills. For in this respect the statement of Augustine just cited always
remains true: “That grace which by divine bounty is secretly bestowed upon
human hearts is rejected by no hard heart.” Rather, he speaks insofar as he
was the minister of the circumcision—that is, the first and chief herald of
the gospel toward the Jews, by whose preaching he sought their conversion and
salvation. By the will of the sign, as they call it, he sought all whom he
called by the voice of his gospel; but by what they call the will of good
pleasure, only the elect. This latter belongs to Christ not as the Father’s
minister and legate, not as a herald of the gospel sent by the Father to the
Jews in a certain peculiar manner, but as Lord and God coeternal with the
Father. In this respect he knows together with the Father who are his, and
likewise whom he has chosen from among the called and whom he has not.
Consequently, when he speaks as the Father’s legate and herald, as the
minister of the circumcision, he rightly shows that he wills to gather
everyone to salvation: preaching the gospel indiscriminately to all the Jews
and calling everyone to himself outwardly, because this duty was entrusted to
the heralds of the gospel by God and by Christ himself as Lord. But when he
ascends higher to the eternal counsel of God, he speaks quite differently and
shows that many are called but few are elect, that to the latter it has been
given to know the mysteries of his kingdom, but not likewise to the rest.

[Marginalia: Part 1 of the answer. Part 2.]

Next, we showed above in the preceding answer why he can meanwhile justly
complain of the Jews’ stubbornness and reproach their unbelief. They drew that
slavery to sin, blindness, and hardness upon themselves; they became the
authors to themselves of the necessity of persisting in unbelief by turning
away from God their Creator. Although bound by the chains of sin, they
nevertheless rejected the offered grace willingly and were wholly pleased
with themselves in their blindness and obstinacy.

[Marginalia: Proleptically.]

The same must be said about other passages of this kind. For example, when in
Acts 7 they are said to have resisted the Holy Spirit, this means not his
internal operation and saving efficacy—sufficient for believing and obeying,
which no one resists—but the external ministry of the prophets, the apostles,
and Christ himself.

[Marginalia: Extension. Acts 7:51.]

3. They draw what Christ says to the angel of the church of the Laodiceans
toward their own purpose: “Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone
hears my voice and opens the door, I will enter in to him and dine with him,
and he with me.” They say it is clear from this that sufficient grace is
present to everyone at whose door Christ knocks. Otherwise, they say, he
would act absurdly if he knocked, knowing certainly that they could not answer
him or open to him the doors of their hearts. It would be just as if someone
knocked at another’s door knowing that no one was inside to open it, which
would be the action of a foolish and ridiculous person.

[Marginalia: Objection 3. Revelation 3:20.]

But first, this argument has no greater strength for constructing that
imaginary sufficient grace than all the others drawn from commands, threats,
warnings, exhortations, and the whole external calling. We deny that it
therefore follows either that these things are employed in vain for human
conversion or that Christ knocks in vain. For in the elect he knocks
effectually and opens by knocking. When he knocked upon the heart of Lydia,
the disciples going to Emmaus, and Paul himself going to Damascus, he did not
knock in vain; by knocking he at the same time opened within by his own hand,
just as outwardly he struck their ears with the sound of his word. In the
rest, however, he knocks as much as suffices for testimony, judgment, and
rendering them without excuse, but not as much as suffices for remedy and
effectual conversion. Consequently he never knocks in vain, because he always
attains his end and the execution of his counsel in everyone.

[Marginalia: Answer 1.]

Next, that passage does nothing to establish their purpose, because, as is
clear, those words of Christ are directed to people who were already
believers and converted to faith in him. He nevertheless does not cease to
knock kindly at their hearts, now with the gentler hand of the word, now with
the sharper rod of the cross and afflictions, so that their languishing faith
may repeatedly be aroused and his communion with them daily be increased and
confirmed by means of this kind. For opening their hearts and admitting him,
he himself by his Spirit

[Marginalia: Part 2.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 177 | De Prædestinatione p. 133]

supplies them with ready minds and powers. That Christ says these things to
the Church and its members is clear from a comparison with the entirely
similar passage found in Song of Songs 5, to which the Lord seems to allude
here.

[Marginalia: Song of Songs 5:2.]

9. Nor can this opinion be consistent with itself. It asserts that God fails
no one with respect to salvation, but confers upon everyone all the aids of
both nature and grace necessary for obtaining salvation. Yet they are
compelled to confess that not everyone is saved, but some never approach,
while others retreat and fall away. This is truly to make contradictory
statements. For someone to obtain salvation, God must give him not only the
grace to believe if he wills and the ability to persevere if he wills, but
also that he actually believes and actually perseveres. They are compelled to
confess, however, that God does not confer this upon everyone. Therefore they
cannot say without absurdity that God gives everyone the grace necessary for
salvation while very many nevertheless perish by eternal death.

[Marginalia: Error 9.]

Finally, this opinion is really nothing other than a new recasting of errors
first invented by the Pelagians and condemned by the ancient orthodox Fathers
Augustine, Prosper, and others of their age; afterward revived by the papal
monks; and again pierced by the sword of the divine word in the true Church
of God. This will be manifest to anyone who examines their writings, both
from Augustine’s discussions and from those milky-white poems of Prosper,
transparent with the greatest ease.

[Marginalia: Error 10 and last. Confirmation.]

The Pelagians taught that all people were indeed redeemed through Christ but
were not liberated, because, they said, God dispenses his grace to people
according to the capacity of its recipients. They tried to illustrate this
with the following analogy. They supposed that some legate or intercessor had
paid a more than ample price for a captive city, so that he received the
entire captive people from the hand of the one by whom they had been held
under the law of war and thus released every law and necessity of servitude
from the captives. “Meanwhile,” they said, “if perhaps either delight in habit
or the evil flattery of the robber solicits some of the captives, so that each
person, a slave of his own will, refuses the gratuitous benefit and, unsound
in mind, thus prefers his former slavery to liberty, has the ungrateful
person’s contempt diminished the grace of the price? Has it brought any
diminution in the Redeemer’s benevolence because he rejected liberty? Not at
all. Indeed, just as the person who returned can be grateful to the Redeemer,
so the one who remained is guilty of contempt.”

[Marginalia: Augustine, On Grace and Free Choice, book 2, chapter 16.]

Now I ask, what else do the Pelagians of our own day and recent times teach
when they assert that, on God’s and Christ’s part, each and every person has
indeed been redeemed, but that not everyone is saved because they themselves
are unwilling to believe? Therefore, since this is what the Pelagians taught
of old, Augustine refuted them as follows: “They are redeemed but not
liberated; they are washed but not cleansed,” and so forth. “These are the
monstrosities of your opinions”; “these are the paradoxes of the Pelagian
heretics,” and so forth. Afterward, arguing from the price of our redemption,
namely the precious blood of Christ, and why it was poured out, he says: “Let
the Merchant himself answer; let the Redeemer himself say: ‘This is my blood,
which is poured out for many for the remission of sins.’ Go on, go on, and
just as you say that in the Savior’s sacraments people are baptized but not
saved, redeemed but not liberated, so also say that the blood is poured out
for them for the remission of sins, but they are cleansed by the remission of
no sin. Astonishing are the things you say, novel are the things you say,
false are the things you say.”

[Marginalia: Augustine, Against Julian, book 3, chapter 30.]

Prosper, after reporting their opinion about the universal grace of Christ
and the form and limit which those Pelagians assigned to it, said:

“That grace calls and invites everyone, passing no one by; it strives to
bring a common salvation to all and absolve the whole world from sin. But it
is left to each person’s own choice to obey the caller and, with the mind
moved by its own judgment, to stretch toward the offered light, which
withdraws itself from no one but helps those eager for right and illumines
those who will it.”

He then refutes the same opinion as follows: “Tell me how you prove that the
grace of Christ passes over absolutely no person among all who are born,
willing to impart the kingdom and blessed life to every one of them. For even
in our own time it is not certain that Christ’s gospel has now been made known
to everyone in every land—to say nothing of the fact that the beginnings of
the gift could not have run through the whole world at once.”

[Marginalia: Prosper, Poem on the Ungrateful. Refutation 1.]

Again he argues as follows: “For if there is no one anywhere whom he does not
will to be redeemed, whatever the supreme Power wills is undoubtedly
fulfilled. But not everyone is saved, and a great part sits in the darkness
of death and is not made alive. Do the varying movements of souls produce
such things, and does liberty make the cause unequal for all? Then the divine
will is strong or even weak at the choice of man, since the end of the work
lies in him”—that is, since its outcome and result depend upon him. Prosper
leaves this as an absurdity, since on the contrary human salvation depends
entirely upon the omnipotent grace of God: “For him the time to act is always
present; there is no delay from conduct for what he wills to accomplish, nor
does he hang in uncertainty upon any causes.” Prosper again elegantly
maintains that this grace is entirely gratuitous and regards nothing foreseen
in people as its own impelling cause:

“What if you should see this different end by which Christ’s grace
chooses one while another perishes, and bestows salvation,
even in twins? Why do you divide those conceived at one
time and born at one time? You cannot plead
any movements of their choice.”

And afterward:

“But it is neither rightly nor truly said that those
who lack the divine power, and whom grace has neglected,
living in the shadow of death,
are guilty of sin.”

[Marginalia: 2. 3. 4.]

He means contempt for the gospel, or what they call privative unbelief,
although this necessarily follows from the Pelagian hypothesis. Finally,
those ancient heretics leveled against the orthodox the same objection which
their newest successors level against us today, as is clear from Prosper’s
letter to Augustine. This is a sure indication both that the same error is
held by them and that the same truth is defended by us.

[Marginalia: 5.]

Thus, by God’s gift and mercy, we have completed the whole treatment of this
commonplace, both its didactic and its refutative parts, according to the
method we established at the beginning. Relying upon the same help of God, we
will now conclude it with this brief synoptic survey, or recapitulation, of
everything delivered and treated so fully above. First, we prefaced the
treatment itself with a necessary prolegomenon, resolving the question
whether this doctrine of predestination ought to be set forth publicly both
in the assembly of the Church and in Christian schools. There we answered
five arguments of the

[Marginalia: 1. Recapitulation of everything said in the treatment of this
theological commonplace.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 178 | De Prædestinatione p. 134]

opponents and maintained the truth by various arguments, among which we
advanced eight notable uses and advantages of this doctrine.

Next, in the treatment itself, after first setting forth seven precautions
and the method which we judged should be used in it, 1. we approached
predestination in general. We examined the force, use, meaning, extension or
restriction of its name, then its definition, and finally the two common
means subordinate to it, namely, creation and the permission of the human
fall.

[Marginalia: 2.]

2. Having divided it into its species, we approached election first, as its
principal species and the chief subject of our undertaking. In it, I confess,
we treated many things which could otherwise have been treated under
predestination itself in general, as will soon be clear. But we did this
deliberately, in this respect regarding and following a method more conducive
to our easier and clearer knowledge than the order of nature.

[Marginalia: 3.]

First, after discussing and distinguishing the equivocation of the word, we
offered its various definitions, which nevertheless come to the same thing.
From them we brought the nature of election itself to light, especially by
explaining that twofold act of both the divine purpose and decree. We
confirmed from Holy Scripture the twofold distinction—first, between purpose
strictly taken and decree, and second, between the two aspects of that act,
which have a place both in the purpose and in the decree—and compared the two
acts with one another in their fivefold agreement and difference.

[Marginalia: 4.]

2. After briefly touching in general upon both the matter about which election
is concerned—that is, its occupied subject—and its form, which consists in
God’s act of separating and appointing, we proceeded to the mode of that act,
which is situated in Christ, the head and mediator of the elect. Hence we
treated Christ, considered in this way, fully as the bond and foundation of
the elect. We also answered two objections arising from this, showing that he
was not an antecedent cause and that his mediation detracts nothing from his
dignity and excellence above all the elect.

[Marginalia: 5.]

3. In the third place, we passed to the elect united to Christ and, after
merely touching briefly and lightly upon the elect angels, finally came to
elect human beings. In considering their election, we treated the remaining
two causes. First was the efficient cause, both the principal and the
antecedent cause. We handled this first destructively, rejecting the false
causes—namely, the three principal ones that carried any weight—with various
testimonies of Holy Scripture and arguments; then constructively, setting
forth and fully confirming the true cause. Second was the final cause. We
noted both the remote and ultimate end and the proximate and intermediate
ends, appending a noteworthy distinction between an end and a final cause.

[Marginalia: 6.]

4. In the fourth place, we deduced from everything already stated four
conditions, adjuncts, or properties of election. From there, as if advancing
by a step, we passed to its effects, which we said were all the means of our
salvation through which God executes his decree in and toward the elect.
Apart from the appointment of the mediator itself—which is not an effect or
execution, but a certain part of the decree itself belonging to its latter
act—we referred these to four stages: the promise of Christ the God-man as
mediator; the manifestation of the promised mediator; the application of the
one manifested or to be manifested; and the consummation of that application,
that is, glorification. All the particular effects of election can easily be
referred to these stages: the making of the covenant, Christ’s accomplishment
of the work of human redemption, effectual calling, justification, adoption,
sanctification, confirmation in received grace, and so forth. In explaining
the first stage we answered the objection about the apparent contradiction
which seems to exist between the divine decree and God’s command that the
promise be proclaimed indiscriminately to everyone. Under the second, we
showed for whom Christ assumed our flesh and suffered death in it—namely, for
the elect alone, as was demonstrated by various arguments.

[Marginalia: 7.]

In the fifth place, from the effects of election thus explained, we deduced
this notable corollary concerning the signs and indications of our election.
There we gave an extensive explanation of the gifts of the Holy Spirit, both
those which may be common to the reprobate with the elect and those which are
proper and peculiar to the elect. We did so first with greater precision for
the sake of those more advanced and mature in the spiritual and heavenly life
of Christ and in the stature of that new man; then more briefly and simply to
suit the capacity of those whose senses are still less exercised in the
things of that spiritual life, who still need milk and food less solid and
elaborate. Finally, we carefully took precautions and made provision for the
weak and anxious consciences of those for whom no certainty about their
election and adoption arises from the aforesaid testimonies of the effects,
by supplying most wholesome admonitions and counsels.

[Marginalia: 8.]

From there, in the sixth place, we passed to reprobation, the remaining
species of predestination. First we maintained its very name, then its nature
and definition, and in that definition examined the twofold act of
reprobation. From this its antecedent cause became clear. Third, we treated
the final cause, both remote and proximate, and showed by various arguments
that the rejection of the rest serves the salvation of the elect. Fourth, we
set out its effects and consequences and distinguished them from one another.
Fifth, we set forth the conditions both of reprobation taken in the abstract
and of its paronym, that is, of the reprobate themselves. Sixth, we deduced
various corollaries from the preceding doctrine. The first was framed by
setting forth a fivefold agreement and, so to speak, an equal number of
differences between election and reprobation. The second concerned the
threefold use of the doctrine of reprobation. The third concerned its
application, or forming judgments about the persons of the reprobate, where
we presented two rules and answers to three questions.

[Marginalia: 9.]

Thus we concluded the whole didactic or constructive part of this doctrine.
In the second place, we approached its apologetic, vindicatory, or, if you
prefer, defensive or responsive part. We reduced the opponents’ arguments to
four principal classes. First, we set forth and answered those which
concerned the very form and nature of this decree. Here we fully answered five
principal objections, bringing forward a great many distinctions and proving
them from Holy Scripture itself. Second came those which concerned the
antecedent causes, first of election and then of reprobation. This part was
again threefold. Under election we first rejected at least the opponents’
arguments and the testimonies they cited. Under reprobation we answered first
their exceptions and evasions against our arguments and then five of their
objections.

[Marginalia: 10.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 179 | De Prædestinatione p. 135 (through Prælectio XXXII close)]

Third came the arguments which concerned the means of the decree. Only one
occurred concerning the means of election, the one about Christ the mediator,
which was answered above in its proper place. But concerning the means of
reprobation, the principal disputation was instituted about the permission of
sin. There we endeavored to unravel this whole difficulty by explaining God’s
various actions concerning sin and sinners, the various degrees and senses of
his will, and finally the various ways in which sin is considered. Lastly, in
the fourth place came the arguments which concerned the immutable condition
of the decree of election and reprobation and of the elect and reprobate.
There we answered three arguments, most of them fertile and, as it were,
pregnant with many others. The third in particular, which concerned the loss
of justifying faith, embraced seven passages of Scripture, six examples—the
sixth, Solomon’s, having been treated fully by us and his election maintained
by five arguments and two objections answered—and finally eight arguments,
with answers to all of them. Having concluded that part also by testimonies
and the authority of the ancients, we finally not so much refuted—for this
was unnecessary—as simultaneously introduced and collected the errors opposed
to the truth first set forth, explained, and confirmed by us and then
vindicated and defended from the opponents’ objections. Merely by bringing
them forward, their falsity and absurdity and the extent to which each opinion
departed and wandered from the truth were evident from what had already been
said. We laid out seven erroneous and absurd opinions, all belonging to the
Pelagians and Semi-Pelagians—that is, the monks—and the Ubiquitarians. In
these most recent discussions, as you have heard, we convicted the last of
these, devised and recast by the present-day consubstantiators, of ten errors
which we introduced and rejected.

[Marginalia: 11.]

What therefore remains except that, now this sufficiently arduous undertaking
has been brought to its end and this lengthy and prolonged labor which we have
spent upon this very extensive commonplace for your benefit and advancement,
hearers, has at last been happily completed under divine auspices, we should
give thanks to God himself with our whole heart—as great as we can conceive
in mind and express by mouth? Let us ascribe to God himself, as is fitting,
all praise, honor, and glory through Jesus Christ. He saw fit to illuminate,
govern, support, and strengthen us by his Holy Spirit. He kindly supplied and
bestowed upon us both a happy abundance of subject matter, a wealth of words,
an opening of the mouth, an inward movement of the soul, sincere zeal for
propagating his heavenly truth, and some firmness for a memory otherwise,
alas, so unstable and fleeting; and upon you patience in hearing, attention of
ears and mind, and desire to learn and advance in this heavenly doctrine. He
has also supplied both of us, as we hope, with some progress in knowledge and
saving faith, some increase of godliness and holiness, and consequently some
increase in certainty about our election and adoption.

[Marginalia: Final close. By way of doxology.]

Finally, with one accord let us beseech God himself through the same Son, our
Lord Jesus Christ, that, when all our sins and offenses have been forgiven us
in and through him, canceled and blotted out—especially those committed by us
in this work and labor, whether in teaching, hearing, or learning—he would
engrave this saving doctrine, drawn and brought forth from the purest
fountains of his holy word, so deeply upon our hearts and the memories of all
who have received it with their ears that it may never fall or slip from them.
May he so apply this saving truth to us by the sealing of his Spirit that he
always keeps intact in our souls the unshakable certainty of our election,
adoption, and eternal salvation, and full assurance and confidence in his
fatherly love toward us. May he not allow it to be torn from us, weakened or
undermined within us—much less wholly fail—by any force, fraud, or assault of
sin, Satan, and various temptations. On the contrary, may he see fit so to
protect, fortify, strengthen, cultivate, and water in the future this saving
plant of true faith, planted in our hearts by his own hand and nourished and
advanced thus far, that, sending its roots deep and penetrating all the most
rocky places and recesses of this soil of our nature, it may at last grow to
the due measure of its increase and produce the most abundant fruits of
godliness, righteousness, and love. May these be pleasing and acceptable to
you, O kind and merciful Father, in Jesus Christ; useful to your Church; and
serve in every way to build up our brothers, magnify the glory of your name,
advance the kingdom of Jesus Christ, and propagate your heavenly truth. May
they finally supply us ourselves abundantly with the most certain earnest of
salvation, material for true joy, and a ground of solid consolation in both
life and death, in the same Christ, your Son, our Lord and Redeemer, to whom
with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit be all honor, dominion, and majesty
forever. AMEN.
