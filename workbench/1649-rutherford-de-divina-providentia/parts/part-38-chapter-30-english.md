CHAPTER XXX

That the Fathers, Doctors, and Scholastics stand with us concerning God’s
active providence with respect to sin.

I.

Augustine alone—far more practiced than all the rest in this controversy—would
almost suffice for me in place of them all; yet other distinguished men are not
lacking. Augustine speaks thus: *We could recall many other things in which it
plainly appears that by GOD’s hidden judgment perversity of heart comes about,
so that what is truly spoken is not heard and sin consequently occurs.* Again:
*Who would not tremble at those divine judgments by which GOD works whatever he
wills in the hearts of evil people, repaying them according to their deserts,
and so forth?* And elsewhere: *GOD fulfills certain wills of his, which are
surely good, through the evil wills of evil people.* Again: *It must be
understood that all things are either accomplished with the Lord’s help or
permitted when he abandons. Nothing happens unless the Almighty wills it to
happen, either by allowing it to happen or by doing it himself.* And elsewhere:
*Although evil things are not good insofar as they are evil, nevertheless it is
good not only that there should be good things, but also that there should be
evil things.* And in *On Grace and Free Choice*: *Human wills are so within
GOD’s power that he makes them incline wherever and whenever he wills, either
to bestow benefits upon some or to inflict punishments upon others, as he
himself judges—by a most hidden judgment, indeed, but without doubt a most just
one.* In the same place: *For the Al-*

*—mighty also works the motion of their will in human hearts, so that through
them he may do what he himself has willed to do through them, he who knows how
to will nothing unjustly.* In the same place: *And Amaziah did not listen* (2
Chronicles 25) *because it was from GOD that he should be delivered into his
hand, since he had sought the gods of Edom. Behold, GOD, wishing to punish the
sin of idolatry, worked this*—though it was nevertheless a grave sin—*in his
heart.* And in the same place: *Enough, as I think, has been shown to establish
that GOD works in human hearts to incline their wills wherever he wills: either
toward good, according to his mercy, or toward evil, according to their
deserts, by his judgment—sometimes manifest, sometimes hidden, but always
just.* Augustine had the books of the *Retractions* in hand when he wrote the
books *On the Predestination of the Saints*, as he himself testifies. Yet there
he writes: *It is therefore in the power of evil people to sin; but when they
sin, it is not in their power that they commit this or that act with that
malice. It belongs to GOD, who divides and orders the darkness, so that even
what they do against GOD’s will does not fulfill anything except GOD’s will.*
And in the same place: *For GOD works what he wills in human hearts, either by
helping or by judging, so that through them there may also be fulfilled what
his hand and counsel predestined.* In that same place he proves at length that
God inclines, prepares, and turns human wills wherever he wills. Nothing is
clearer in Augustine than that God wills sin insofar as it is punishment. *Is
it not,* he says, *sin and the punishment of sin where it is written, “The Lord
has mixed a spirit of error”? Is it not sin and the punishment of sin where the
prophet says to GOD, “Why have you made us wander, Lord, from your way?” Is it
not sin and the punishment of sin where it is said to GOD, “Behold, you were
angry, and we sinned”?* And in the same place: *What do you mean, Julian, when
you say that those delivered over to their desires, abandoned and driven into
sins through divine patience, are so described—as though the same Apostle had
not joined both these things, patience and power, when he says, “But if, wishing
to show wrath and make his power known,” and so forth?* Augustine adds in the
same place: *Your judgments are a great deep.* And: *O the depth!* I know that
Augustine says elsewhere: *This is our faith: GOD is not the parent of evils,
nor did he make any nature evil; evil things arise from what is voluntary.* He
argues there against Fortunatus the Manichaean, who said that souls are evil by
nature rather than by the will’s choice. Yet Augustine himself appears to
oppose this doctrine when he says: *Nowhere do we read in Scripture, “There is
no will except from GOD,” because it is not true; otherwise GOD would also be
the author of sins—which God forbid.* But Augustine’s answer in the same place
confirms our position.-*

*—Further,* he says, *when an evil will receives the power to fulfill what it
intends, this comes from GOD’s judgment, in whom there is no injustice; for he
also punishes in this way, and does not thereby act unjustly, although he acts
secretly.* The adversaries object that Augustine rejected as blasphemous the
view which ascribes sins to God’s predestination. I answer that he rightly did
so, for they imputed to Augustine the claim that he ascribed sins to a
compelling decree of fate. *It is a detestable and abominable opinion,* he
says, *which believes GOD to be the author of anyone’s evil will, whose
predestination is never outside goodness or justice. Therefore GOD’s
predestination neither excited, persuaded, nor impelled the fall of those who
fall, the wickedness of the unjust, or the desires of sinners.* And in the same
place: *Neither the Father, the Son, nor the Holy Spirit does anything there.*
See what he says elsewhere on this subject. It is therefore abundantly clear
that Augustine’s adversaries attributed to him the ascription of every sin to
a stellar and coercive decree. The Arminians level the same objection against
us today, as do the Remonstrants and Nicolaus Grevinchovius. Blessed Augustine
is compelled to plead that cause in these words: *Nor under the name of grace
do we assert fate,* and so forth. And more plainly afterward: *GOD’s grace
transcends not only all stars and all heavens but also all angels. Moreover,
the advocates of fate ascribe people’s good and evil acts to fate*—just as
Augustine’s adversaries slanderously claimed that he ascribed them to GOD’s
will as imposing fatal necessity—*but in human evils God pursues the repayment
of what is owed to their deserts, whereas he bestows good things through
unmerited grace by his merciful will. In either case he acts not through a
temporal conjunction of stars, but through the eternal and profound counsel of
his severity and goodness.* Bradwardine also defends Augustine very well here.
*In order that the intention of Augustine and the other saints may appear more
clearly,* he says, *attention must be paid to their manner of speaking and
understanding when they deny that GOD wills sin, and the reason for this denial
must be weighed. The reason seems to be this: because the Apostle, Augustine,
and the other saints posited foreknowledge and similar things on GOD’s side,
the Pelagians and other heretics wished to excuse sinners from their sins and
turn the cause and blame back upon GOD, who so predestined and foreknew. The
saints therefore intend to say that predestination is not a compelling cause,
nor is the first cause culpable; rather, those who are sin’s servants sin
freely.* I add that Augustine says that *GOD expressly inclined Shimei’s will—*

*—which was evil through its own fault, toward the sin committed by Shimei, by
a hidden but just judgment.* The Arminians and Jesuits object that, according
to Augustine, people are thus predestined by God to sin. They therefore say
that Augustine’s harsher expressions ought either to have been softened or not
tolerated at all. I answer that no softening is needed for an author who
explains his meaning with perfect clarity: *The Jews committed sin with these
words; he whom nothing escaped foresaw that they would do it, but did not
compel them to do what displeases him.* More plainly: *Whoever says that by
GOD’s predestination people are driven into sins as though compelled by fatal
necessity unto death is not a Catholic.* Augustine therefore rejects only the
fiction of a decree compelling by fate, which we also reject. Let this suffice
for Augustine’s meaning. Prosper: *When we read that some were delivered by
GOD to their own desires, hardened, or abandoned to their great sins, we
confess that they deserved this, because their crimes preceded it, so that
they owed themselves punishments which would even turn their punishment into
guilt.* If, then, God wills the punishment, according to Prosper he wills the
sin which is punishment. Anselm: *For God is said to harden a person when he
does not soften him, and to lead him into temptation when he does not free him;
it is therefore not inappropriate for him to predestine in this way when he
does not correct evil people and their evil works.* Gregory: *Hence it is that
Cain could be admonished by the divine voice and could not be changed, because,
as his guilt’s wickedness required, GOD had already abandoned the heart within
to which he outwardly spoke words as testimony. For just as no one resists the
generosity of the one who calls, so no one opposes the justice of the one who
abandons.* And in the same place: *It necessarily follows that whoever has been
abandoned by GOD is subject to every temptation.* If, then, according to Anselm
and Gregory, God by the just withdrawal of his grace necessitates some people
to sin, it necessarily follows that God willed and decreed that their
stubbornness should occur by his permission. Gregory says openly elsewhere:
*Satan’s temptation is ordered so that those who, when proud, could perish may
be humbled and preserved from destruction. Thus, by the secret order of
dispensation, the iniquity of the devil is permitted to rage*—that is, when he
tempts some to sin for their humiliation—*and from it GOD’s kindness is piously
accomplished.* Irenaeus: *Sin is permitted so that God’s goodness may be shown
and justice accomplished.* God therefore first intends the glory of his
goodness and justice, and to attain what he intends, he permissively-*

*—wills sin to occur.* The same follows from Clement of Alexandria’s words:
*It belongs to divine wisdom, power, and might not only to do the good that
belongs to GOD’s nature, but especially to lead what evil people have devised
to some good and useful end, and usefully to employ things which appear evil.*
Arminius says this testimony does not argue against him, but the following
conclusions follow from Clement: 1. God intends the good end before he permits
sins. 2. Sins are useful to God for a good end, and to that extent are desired
by God. 3. Evil wills are subordinated as instruments to GOD as he employs evils
for a good end; and we seek nothing more. Likewise Fulgentius: *Although GOD is
not the author of evil thoughts, he is nevertheless the ordainer of evil wills,
and he himself determines to work good from each person’s evil work.*
Fulgentius’s controversy was with Julian the Pelagian, who pleaded the same
cause as Arminius, and he did not deny that God draws good from existing sin.
Isidore: *Some are so despised by GOD that they cannot lament their evils even
if they wish.* Jerome: *Shall I say that anything happens without you and that
something can occur against your will? To think this is blasphemous.* Again:
*Whatever good or evil exists in the world happens neither apart from
providence nor by chance, but by GOD’s judgment.* Hugh: *If it is said that GOD
wills evil, this is grievous to hear, and a devout mind does not easily accept
that one who is good wills evil. For it then seems to be said that the good one
loves and approves evil, which is wicked. Therefore the devout mind rejects
this, not because what is said is not said well, but because what is said well
is not understood well.* In the same place: *When he does good and permits evil,
his will appears in the matter, since he wills that which he does and permits
to exist. GOD’s will is his good pleasure, and his will is his operation.*

and Gabriel say that GOD is, in the strict and proper sense of the term, the
cause of sin. Cajetan says that GOD hardened Sihon negatively by withholding
gratuitous gifts, and positively with respect to judgment, inclination, and the
pursuit of sensible good. Nearly all Dominicans—and especially Diego
Álvarez—teach that GOD, by prior causation, truly and efficiently moves free
choice beforehand toward the act of sin, in the manner of a physical cause,
insofar as it is an act and a being. Thus Domingo Báñez. Soto says that the

by which he effects all positive beings.* Thus Capreolus and Ferrariensis; so
also Cano, Vega, Scotus, and nearly all the Thomists. Thomas himself says so
expressly. I add three famous Jesuits who strive to oppose us in this
controversy. Diego Ruiz: *Although sinners are principal causes with respect to
malice, yet with respect to inflicting grievous evils they are GOD’s
instruments.* Therefore GOD pre-conceives and preordains their actions and
prescribes to them their measure and all other circumstances. The same author
says: *Some special energy must be conceded, because of which GOD is said to
harden, beyond the infallibility of the inference from the withdrawal of divine
grace.* And in the same place: *In absolute power it is not repugnant for the
entire collection of motives that infallibly lead to sinning to be caused by
GOD.* Yet among those motives are often carnal reasoning, the ungodly
persuasion of a prostitute, the devil’s temptation, a friend’s depraved
counsels, and the intention of empty vainglory. On the Jesuit’s position,
therefore, it is not repugnant for a heap of sins to be caused by God. No such
insolent and wicked position ever escaped Calvin, Beza, or any of our
theologians. Francisco Suárez speaks thus: *If a prince’s will has decreed to
wage war and plunder whatever it can, it is not evil to incline it toward this
nation rather than that one. In these cases, then, GOD can in accordance with

directly through good angels that this work rather than that one be chosen, or
by granting through good angels a limited and definite faculty with respect to
a particular person,* and so forth. According to Suárez, therefore, God can:
1. suggest to good angels reasons that tempt people and solicit them to sin,
and so command good angels to sin—for to persuade someone to evil is to sin;
2. incline people toward this sin rather than that, and so toward committing a
graver sin rather than a lighter one, since people are indefinitely inclined
to sin. Thus God can both sin and persuade to sin. The Libertines—Quintinus,
Coppinus, Florinus, or Simon Magus—taught nothing more crass. Yet the Jesuits

*—that, although they are evil through their own fault, they are nevertheless
ordered by divine providence toward this evil rather than another, not
positively but permissively.* This is Suárez’s blasphemous refrain. James
Arminius says: *The arguments by which Joseph’s brothers were incited by their
own malice to wish their brother removed, and the opportunities by which they
could remove him, were offered to them by divine dispensation—partly through a
mediate human action and partly through an immediate action of GOD himself.*
Hence, if GOD by an immediate act suggests to people reasons that solicit them
to sin, he does the same thing as though he persuaded them to sin. But if
Arminius means that God concurs only by the actual concurrence of the first
cause toward the entitative act of weighing and considering the moral
enticements to sin, then he recognizes no more special act of active and
efficacious providence here than when rain falls or lilies grow. Arminius
himself would admit that this contradicts his intention and purpose in these
theses, since his purpose here is to recount special acts of special and active
providence—or else he gives the reader pure impostures. He also says that *GOD

himself and his people.* According to Arminius, God here wills that Ahab commit
no crimes by his revealed will, and by that same hidden will he inwardly wills
that Ahab fill up the measure of his crimes. In this way he ascribes hypocrisy,
malicious deceit, and even two contrary wills to God, which is absurd. 2. It
