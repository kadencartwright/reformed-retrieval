3. Let us also see whether this same point can be proved from the passage
upon which Socinus insists, “upon which all sinned.” This brings us to

Socinus's second assertion: these words cannot contain that meaning. The
words “upon which” should not be rendered “in whom”—that is, all sinned
imputatively in Adam—but “because,” “for the reason that,” or “insofar as
all actually sinned in their own persons.” Socinus says: “Although *epi* is
sometimes used for *en*, it is never so used where something is said to
exist or come to be in something else.”

Several things must be said in reply.

1. First I set aside Grotius's interpretation. He explains “in whom all
sinned” as “through whom all underwent punishment,” by a metonymy frequent
among the Hebrews, in whose usage *sin* is put for punishment and *to sin*
for undergoing punishment. By carrying the figure farther through a change
of designation, people are said to “sin” when they suffer some evil even
without fault, as in Genesis 31:37 and Job 5:24, where *to sin* is rendered
“to suffer misfortune.” I answer, first, that this interpretation of “they
sinned” does not fit here. The Apostle's argument requires it to be
explained as sin properly so called, since this clause gives the reason for
the preceding statement: death passed to all because all sinned, since sin
is the cause of death. But to say that all die because all undergo
punishment would be inept and tautological. Second, although Scripture often
puts sin for sin's punishment, I do not think it often—if ever—uses *to sin*
for undergoing punishment or some other evil. Nothing of the sort occurs in
Genesis 31:37, one of the passages Grotius cites. Job 5:24 does say, “You
shall visit your habitation and shall not sin,” which the Septuagint renders
“you shall by no means sin.” A scholion renders it “unto misfortune,” but
others properly translate it “you shall not sin” or “go astray.” This is
farther from our subject. More pertinent is Grotius's rendering of “upon
which” in the same passage as “through whom,” thereby showing that *epi* with
the dative is often used in that way.

2. Let us therefore approach what is more closely connected with the
present business: the explanation of “upon which.” We must inquire whether
it should be rendered “in whom,” “because,” or “insofar as.” It must first
be conceded that it can signify either and can sometimes refer both to a
thing and to a person. Augustine first referred it to a thing, understanding
“in whom” as “in which sin of Adam all sinned.” He later corrected this
interpretation in book 4, chapter 4 of *Against Two Letters of the
Pelagians*, and rightly so. Although it truly enough expresses the reality
of the Apostle's sense and intention—namely, that we all sinned in Adam's
sin—it does not agree with the proper syntax of the words. Since *sin* is
feminine but the relative in “upon which” is masculine or neuter, it cannot
agree with *sin*. It must therefore be rendered either, with some, “because,”
“for the reason that,” “insofar as,” or “to the extent that”; or, with
others, “in whom all sinned.” The former indicates the thing and its cause;
the latter also indicates the cause of the matter, but identifies its
personal subject as well.

The first Latin writer, so far as I understand, who followed the former
interpretation

[1679 printed p. 238 | IA leaf 246]

(for the Syriac translates “because,” and likewise the Arabic) was
Pseudo-Jerome, who, according to Augustine, was Pelagius himself. In his
commentary on the passage he explains “in whom all sinned” thus: “that is,
inasmuch as all sinned after the example of Adam's sin.” Among Greek writers
Theodoret and Photius, whom the Romanist Gagnaeus sufficiently praises, held
the same opinion concerning the exposition of “upon which.” This exposition
also pleased Cajetan and is embraced among our writers by Peter Martyr and
Piscator, who render the words “because,” as Calvin does by “seeing that.”
Louis de Dieu uses “because,” as our English versions use *for that* or
*insomuch*. Socinus eagerly seizes this interpretation in order to maintain
with Pelagius that original sin is derived from Adam not by propagation but
by imitation alone. Erasmus above all treats the matter more fully in his
comments on the passage. Although he repudiates Pelagius there, he
unfortunately still savors too much of him. Erasmus assigns various senses
to the preposition *epi* according to the different cases joined to it, but
says he does not know whether Greek admits the sense in which all are said
to have sinned *in Adam*, as though hidden in him. Since Greek prepositions
have varied usages, he very modestly does not dare assert that *epi* with the
dative is never found where something is declared to exist in another, as a
tree is in its seed. He says only that he had not happened to find such an
example. Socinus likewise says that one can scarcely be found, as does
Piscator himself, although Piscator is utterly removed from Socinianism. For
when the Apostle says in Hebrews 7:10 that Levi was in Abraham's loins, he
does not use *epi* but *en*; and when 1 Corinthians 15:22 says, “As all die
in Adam, so all shall be made alive in Christ,” it does not use *epi* with
Adam and Christ, but *en*.

Nevertheless, many others render “upon which” as “in whom” and refer it to
Adam. Thus Chrysostom asks, “What does ‘upon which all sinned’ mean?” and
answers, “When he fell, even those who had not eaten from the tree became
mortal through him.” Theophylact, a sufficiently competent interpreter of
Chrysostom, explains it more clearly: “What does ‘upon which all sinned’
mean? It means that all sinned in Adam.” He not only refers the relative to
Adam, but, when he wished to express “in Adam” in Greek, used *epi* with the
dative. Erasmus—that devourer of books and man of every kind of
reading—professed, as already quoted, that he had not found such a usage.
This is astonishing unless he meant only that he had not found it in
Scripture, which we will also consider later. Although Origen is varied and
uncertain in interpreting this passage, at the beginning of his exposition
he nevertheless speaks of our existence in Adam's loins, as the Apostle
says Levi was in Abraham's loins. This approaches the interpretation of
“upon which” as “in whom,” namely in Adam. Ambrose expresses it clearly:
“‘In whom’ means in Adam. He said ‘in whom’ although speaking of the woman,
because he referred it not to the sex but to the race.” He then adds, “It is
therefore plain that all sinned in Adam as in one mass.” Above all,
Augustine asserts this sense everywhere and, as Beza puts it, refutes the
other view a thousand times against the Pelagians: “In whom all sinned, when
all were that one man.” He calls the former exposi-

[1679 printed p. 239 | IA leaf 247]

tion new and false in book 6, chapter 12 of *Against Julian*. So many agree
with him that Estius does not hesitate to assert: “It should be known that
no ancient interpreters, Latin or Greek, explain the particle *in whom* by
*insofar as*, except Pelagius among the Latins and Theodoret and Photius
among the Greeks.” He must answer for that himself.

Among our writers, the Genevans and especially Beza follow the Vulgate
translator here and refer the words to Adam. To express this more clearly,
Beza supplied the word *man* and read “in which man all sinned.” Diodati
likewise writes in his Italian version *through that man*, meaning through
that very man in whom all sinned. After “death” he thus repeats the phrase
“through one man” from the beginning of the verse, so that the whole reads:
“Death passed to all men through that one man in whom all sinned.” Piscator
does not readily admit this; Socinus opposes it with all his strength.

For my part, I gladly say with Peter Martyr that I would not contend sharply
whether “upon which” is rendered “in whom,” “because,” or “insofar as.”

Three considerations appear to count against the first rendering, “in
whom.”

1. In general, transpositions, ellipses, and repetitions should not be
invented in the sacred text. I acknowledge that such figures occur often,
especially in Paul's epistles and most of all in Romans, as all interpreters
after Origen admit. Nevertheless, they should not be multiplied without
necessity. No necessity appears here, since without such a transposition or
ellipsis, if “upon which all sinned” is rendered “because” or “insofar as,”
the sense will be not only sound and orthodox but also sufficiently suited
to the Apostle's purpose in this passage.

2. The construction is somewhat harsher, or at least not so easy, if the
relative “which” is referred to so distant an antecedent. It immediately
follows the preceding clause, “and so death passed to all men,” in which
Adam is not mentioned and with which the following words agree very well if
read “because” or “insofar as all sinned.” If nevertheless the relative is
referred to the distant antecedent “one man” at the beginning of the verse,
it might seem “somewhat harsh,” as Pareus puts it more mildly, or “far too
strained,” as Piscator speaks more severely. Yet I have not attained such
confidence—or rather boldness—as a recent, sufficiently learned writer of
our own nation who said, “I venture boldly to assert that no similar
construction and transposition is found in God's word.” At least:

3. I cannot but concede that the examples adduced by many do not fit with
complete precision. In them the preposition *epi* is joined with the dative
to signify that something exists or comes to be in another, as Socinus
puts it—at least as in seed and mass—which Erasmus and Piscator think can
hardly be done. Cornelius a Lapide's examples, “on horseback,” “for gain,”
and “for reward,” have no bearing here. They prove only that *epi* is joined
with the dative, which no one has ever denied. Indeed, Erasmus freely
acknowledges this and gives many examples in which

[1679 printed p. 240 | IA leaf 248]

*epi* with the dative indicates a cause, as “in hope of death”; proximity;
power, as “in the king's hand,” meaning by your command or in the king's
hand; addition, as “besides these”; condition, as “I release you on these
terms”; and position above, as “upon a pillar.” More examples could be added:
it signifies *through* and is put for *dia* in Acts 3:16, “through faith in
his name,” and in 1 Corinthians 8:11, “Through this knowledge of yours the
weak brother will perish.” Here *epi* is not superfluous by a Hebrew idiom,
as someone supposes, but signifies *dia* or *through*. Yet in all these and
similar examples, *epi* does not mean *in* in the sense of something's
existing or coming to be in another—not as in a seed or a mass, as they wish
it to mean here when they say “upon which,” that is, “in Adam,” in whom as
their root all are said to have sinned.

Nor do the examples Beza offers for this purpose appear to fit. For example,
Mark 2:4 says, “They let down the bed upon which the paralytic lay.” Although
Beza and the Vulgate translator render this “in which,” it is rather “upon
which”—not *wherein*, but *whereon the sick of the palsy lay*. This agrees
better with the verb “lay” and with the type of beds called *grabati*, upon
which people used to take their midday rest, according to the more learned
grammarians. Some think the name comes from their not walking: those who sit
and recline on these suspended couches do not touch the ground with their
feet. No more suitable is the other passage they adduce, Hebrews 9:17, “A
testament is valid upon the dead.” Although Erasmus himself, with the
ancient translator, Calvin, Beza, and others, renders this “A testament is
valid in the dead,” *in* does not so properly mean *in* as *among*, so that
“upon the dead” has the same sense as Psalm 88:5, “I am free among the
dead.” Or, if that is less satisfactory, “upon the dead” means “after men
have died,” as our translators render the passage: “A Testament is of force
after men are dead.” Thus “a testament among the dead” means nothing other
than the testament of those who have died, as Socinus rightly judges here.
This at least is certain: the phrase “a testament upon the dead” cannot
signify the manner of existing in another now under discussion. As Socinus
says: “That the testament of one who has died is valid does not demonstrate
that anything exists or comes to be in the dead man. It neither shows that
the testament exists or comes to be in him, nor argues in any way that the
testament is or becomes valid in him.”

These considerations, then, appear to count somewhat against the former
rendering of “upon which” as “in whom,” namely in Adam.

Nevertheless, this rendering and interpretation should not be entirely
rejected. I answer:

1. Equally abrupt and even more abrupt transpositions occur in Scripture,
and relative pronouns are referred to more distant antecedents than this
“upon which” would be referred to “one man” at the beginning of the same
verse. Thus Ephesians 2:1 is connected with verse 5, and Romans 1:1 with
verse 7. Ephe-

[1679 printed p. 241 | IA leaf 249]

sians 3:1 is connected with verse 13 or 14, or even, as some think, with the
first verse of chapter 4, so that the intervening verses and perhaps the
whole of chapter 3 must be enclosed in a parenthesis.

2. Socinus himself admits that *epi* is sometimes, indeed often, used for
*en*. As interpreters have rightly noted, it is sufficiently evident that
Paul in his epistles and Luke in Acts sometimes use the three prepositions
*epi*, *eis*, and *en* interchangeably for the single Latin preposition *in*,
corresponding to a Hebrew preposition which signifies them all.

3. In this very passage and in this very sense, Theophylact rendered “upon
which” by *epi* with Adam in the dative, and Photius's scholia explained it
by “upon Adam” and “through Adam.” Yet they were sufficiently skilled in
Greek. Greek authors also often use the forms “upon which,” “upon whichever,”
and “upon whatsoever” for “because” or “for the reason that.” You will say
that although *epi* is sometimes or often used for *en*, it is never used in
the sense intended here. I therefore answer:

4. We have shown from Acts 3:16 and 1 Corinthians 8:11 that *epi* is used for
*dia*. Grotius adds Luke 5:5, “at your word,” and the passage already
discussed, Hebrews 9:17, “upon the dead.” Grotius himself therefore renders
this “upon which” as “through whom all sinned.” This is sufficient for us if
Adam is understood by “upon which.”

You may object that this is not enough to prove that we sinned in Adam or
that his sin is credited to us. Those who deny this imputation nevertheless
concede that we sinned through Adam: either by imitation alone, as the
Pelagians say; by imitation and occasion, as Theodoret says; or by the
propagation from him of inherent original sin, as the Romanists say.

I answer that this must be treated later. For the present,

I add that *epi* is used for *en* insofar as it signifies *in* when something
is said to exist or come to be in another. Socinus vigorously denies this,
Piscator scarcely believes it, yet Erasmus does not dare deny it, and
rightly so. As we showed above, *epi* with the dative denotes a condition or
even a covenant. Thus Thucydides writes, “the treaty was made on these
terms.” Accordingly, if “upon which” is referred to Adam here, it signifies
not only through him as example, occasion, or cause in any sense, but also
through his covenant, made with us both with him and in him. This has
precisely the same meaning as the other rendering, “in whom”: in him as the
head of the covenant and our representative. Consequently, what he sinned
in that capacity was credited to all of us as sinning in him.

Indeed, *epi* is used for *in* in the strictest sense when it signifies
something's existing or coming to be most properly in another, which Socinus
so sternly demands. What is meant by Demosthenes' phrase “upon you”? What is
more common than to say “what is upon you” or simply “upon you” when
something is signified as lying in our power or placed within us? Compare
the example Erasmus himself gives, “in the king's hand,” which he likewise
renders “in the king's hand.” Although *hand* can be taken tropically and
metonymically for power, the word *in* is used in its strictest sense and
signifies a power existing in and inhering in the king.

But perhaps I have proceeded farther into these grammatical matters than
was proper. To finish the matter once for all and dismiss it,

I will add only this and say again with Peter Martyr: “On this point

[1679 printed p. 242 | IA leaf 250]

I would not contend greatly. Whichever way the words ‘upon which’ are
rendered—‘in whom’ or ‘because’—the phraseology can be ambiguous in either
direction. First, if we render it ‘in whom,’ or even if the Greek had read
‘in whom,’ both the Greek and Latin expressions can readily be used when we
wish to signify nothing other than ‘because,’ ‘inasmuch as,’ or ‘for this
reason.’ Thus Erasmus says that even if it read ‘in whom,’ it would not
greatly obstruct their opinion about imitation, since nothing is more common
than using *in* for *through*. Thus here, whether the text says ‘death passed
to all upon which’ or ‘in which,’ it means ‘through the fact that all
sinned.’ Second, conversely, if ‘upon which’ is rendered ‘because’ or
‘insofar as,’ this would not in sense and interpretation exclude the view
of those who render it ‘in whom’; the sense would come to the same thing.
For if the Apostle had said, ‘death passed to all men insofar as, or because,
all sinned,’ the words ‘in him’ would be understood with the verb ‘sinned’:
namely, in that one man Adam, whose sin did not so belong to him alone that
it did not also belong to all. Cornelius a Lapide says this among the
Romanists; among our writers Peter Martyr, Piscator, and Pareus do so. Even
Erasmus writes: ‘This reading does not simply exclude original sin, for it
can be understood that all sinned in Adam, in whom they also died before
they were born.’”

Although Erasmus later says that this passage is more conveniently
understood of the sins of individuals, and teaches that other passages are
stronger against the Pelagian position—which he openly professes to detest
there—he nevertheless judges both readings able to stand. The Apostle's
statement that “death passed to all men upon which,” that is, “because” or
“insofar as all sinned,” may be understood principally of men's personal
sins, yet it can also be understood of original sin. Indeed, the words taken
absolutely in themselves can comprehend both, for men die on account of
actual and personal sins as well as original sin. But if we weigh the
Apostle's aim, the course of his argument, and the words themselves, we must
understand this not of actual sins in us but of original sin in Adam.
