## Part XX: The Scriptural Proof from the Relation of Contraries

[Part II | PDF page 169 | logical source scan 168 | printed page 167]

Does not the Apostle himself argue from this same proportion of contraries? Speaking of beloved Jacob, to show that foreknowledge of good works—or any foreknowledge—supplied no cause for the decree of love, he says, “before they had done anything good.” Conversely, concerning Esau, whom God hated, that is, destined for his hatred, he likewise says, “before they had done anything evil,” teaching that foreknowledge of evil works did not precede the decree of hatred. Soon afterward, by the same reasoning, he derives both mercy and hardening from one source: God’s will, who has mercy upon WHOM HE WILLS and hardens WHOM HE WILLS, with a will no one can frustrate. Augustine repeatedly uses this argument in *To Simplicianus* 1.2: if hatred depends upon foreseen evil works, it follows that love arises from foreseen good works.

This single argument should satisfy everyone not contentious, even if one concedes that the mass from which vessels are formed for such different ends means the entire corrupted human race, as though God began determining humanity’s end only after foreseeing the fall. Since this corruption is common to everyone and was no more hidden from God in some than others, how could what God equally observed in every person supply the occasion for him to be capitally angry with some but not others? If one answers that this happened because in mercy he willed to except some while just wrath remained upon others, it follows that his excepting some but not others has no cause except that, although all were unworthy of mercy and worthy of wrath, it pleased him to spare some and not others. He would have injured neither group had he destroyed both.

The cause why God hates and destroys those whom he leaves in sin is manifest; the execution of his eternal decree depends upon it. But the Apostle gives three final causes why God determined to choose some for mercy and not have mercy upon the rest: to display his glory by judging justly no less than by showing mercy graciously; to instruct those receiving mercy, showing what they must revere with fear and trembling and how great a benefit binds them; and to adorn the order of the age in that manifold wisdom, as Augustine often explains when illuminating 2 Timothy 2:20. But no impulsive cause why he willed mercy for some and abandonment for others, or these rather than those, should be given or searched beyond his own good pleasure. Nothing is higher than God’s will, by which whatever exists exists well, nor better, since it is itself the norm of every good and right thing.

If sin, whether foreseen or committed, were the one cause both of wrath and destination to wrath, no one would have objected to the Apostle, “Why does he still complain? Who resists his will?” nor would Paul have answered, “O man, who are you to answer back to God?” and “O depth!” Augustine writes of this mystery: “Attend to who you are and what God is. Faithful ignorance is better than rash knowledge.” And: “Why this way for one and not for another? O man, who are you?” Then: “Let us say Alleluia and praise the song; let us not say, ‘Why this and that?’ For all things were created in their time.”

However Dr. Andreae may evade, even if God had created all people for eternal destruction without any fault of theirs—no differently from the many animals he created for slaughter to nourish human beings—God still could not be charged with injustice. This is what I meant, not merely that he could not be so charged if he had mercy upon no sinner. Much less can he be reproved because from eternity he decreed to save some for honor through mercy and condemn others for dishonor by just judgment. Hence Augustine’s statement cited above: “Who created the future reprobate except God? And why, except because he willed? Why did he will? O man, who are you to answer back to God?”

If foreseen corruption—or, as Andreae wrongly prefers throughout this conference, stubbornness against offered gospel grace—supplied an antecedent cause for the subsequent decree of reprobation, the Apostle had an easy, plain answer, vulnerable to no reprobate person. Who doubts that vicious unbelievers are deservedly destined to condemnation? Has Dr. Andreae learned something more deeply than the Apostle in God’s school?
