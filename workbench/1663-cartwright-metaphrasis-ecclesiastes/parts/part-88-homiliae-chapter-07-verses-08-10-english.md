VERSE 8. The end of a thing is better than its beginning; the patient in

VERSE 10. Do not say, “Why were the former days better than these?” For you

Now concluding the preceding teaching about the excellence of death over
birth, Solomon commends moderation of mind.

There are two parts: 1. The conclusion of the preceding teaching. 2. The
commendation of moderation of mind.

VERSE 8. The end of a thing is better than its beginning; the patient in
spirit is better than the proud in spirit.

Having previously commended death over birth, Solomon here recalls the
hypothesis to the thesis: the end of a thing is to be preferred to its
beginning. He does this by taking occasion from the preceding verse 7, where a
wise person is driven to madness by oppression, and one who was formerly
incorrupt afterward perishes through the corruption of gifts. For we see many
who begin a life worthy of praise later turn to contrary pursuits, driven by
prosperous or adverse circumstances. We see many rulers at the beginning of
their administration apply themselves to justice and give people great hope
that they will direct their entire governance of the commonwealth according
to the standard of equity, but later burst forth into the greatest tyranny.
Let King Joash of Judah, 2 Kings 12, be an example.

Among servants we see many who, when they enter service, commend it to their
masters with great diligence and deference. They raise their masters to the
highest expectation of exceedingly attentive and industrious obedience; but
after establishing this opinion of themselves, they later degenerate into
idleness. Hence arose the proverb, “New brooms sweep best.” The same can be
affirmed of every rank of people without exception—husbands, wives, servants,
the common people, teachers, and disciples. It is therefore not inappropriate
to say of them what the master of the feast in John 2 said concerning hosts
who furnish a feast for others. He

affirmed that everyone customarily sets out the most excellent wine at the first course,
and then at the second what is worse and more diluted. At the beginning they
are hot, in the middle lukewarm, and at the end frozen.

Conversely, it sometimes happens that, when beginnings are not very favorable
and no small number of obstacles are presented, it is nevertheless not right
to condemn the whole undertaking as unfortunate or rash, or even to lay it
aside. Indeed, although its beginnings may not be free from blame, it is
contrary to duty immediately to cast aside every good hope concerning a
person. What is said here—that the end of a thing is often better than its
beginning—ought to restrain us.

With respect to others, this should first teach us not to rush our judgment
about them, but to suspend it until, by persisting longer in a pursuit well or
badly begun, a person has given proof of his uprightness or wickedness. We
must not persist shamefully in error through a premature estimate of his
uprightness, while pursuing a reputation for constancy, or be compelled to
retract our own judgment with some embarrassment.

With respect to ourselves, we should strive toward uprightness with every
effort and not abandon the course of integrity we have begun, not even when
its beginnings are entangled in many difficulties. Otherwise we expose
ourselves to greater disgrace than if we had never entered the way of
integrity. Instead we must labor so that our later works may exceed our
earlier ones, as is stated concerning the church at Thyatira in Revelation
2:17.

This rule, however, is bounded by certain limits and barriers of its own and
cannot be understood universally of everything. For the end and departure of
the wicked are more mournful than their birth, whether we consider their
increasing wickedness from day to day or the punishment and torment that
receive them. Nevertheless, so far as rendering a more certain judgment about
each matter is concerned, the end supplies greater help and assistance. Thus
far concerning the first part.

It follows that we speak about the commendation of moderation of mind, which
applies medicine to the evils mentioned in verse 7. First, through patience
under evils and injuries inflicted upon us, that anger is driven away which
impels the wise of this world to madness. Oppressed people collapse beneath
the burden of oppression and turn to a kind of frenzy for no other reason
than that they do not possess a humble and subdued spirit. Hence also these
words: “Am I to bear such things—so unjust, so unworthy?”

This is even clearer from the contrary vice opposed to this moderation of
spirit, namely pride of spirit. A mind swollen with pride and arrogance is
content with no station, however exalted and ample; when it is cast down from
its eminence and prosperous fortune, what tumults will it cause? What storms
will it stir up in itself? What disturbances will it provoke?

The other corruption of morals—the acceptance of gifts to violate right and
judgment—also appears to flow from the same source of impatience and pride. If
a person were content with his own lot and did not, swollen with pride, seek
a richer station than the one granted him by God, he would never permit
himself to be polluted by bribery. Thus through a single moderation of mind he
would apply medicine to the two evils proposed in verse 7, especially because
moderation of mind joined with submission of mind destroys that fierce and
untamed beast, pride of spirit, the parent and seed of both evils.

Solomon describes this moderation of mind by patience, by which it happens
that, though struck by injuries sometimes repeated and multiplied, a person
is nevertheless not provoked to anger and vengeance. It is a virtue proper to
the faithful, by which they set before themselves for imitation their heavenly
Father, who is slow to anger. This virtue

Proverbs 17:27 adorns with the metaphor of coldness. It teaches that a prudent person is one whose
spirit is cool, so that a person moderate in mind resembles a great cauldron
or boiler that is heated only when a large and long-lasting fire is placed
beneath it. He also resembles a fortified citadel, or a city enclosed by a
wall, which is overthrown only by repeated blows from cannon. Proverbs 25, the
last verse.

VERSE 9. Do not be indignant with a troubled spirit, for indignation rests in
the bosom of fools.

Solomon commends this moderation of mind by its opposite, hasty indignation,
from which he dissuades us. He does not condemn indignation altogether, but
only that which is hasty and precipitate and is kindled without any just
cause. This vice is described by adding its contrary. Just as moderation of
mind is described by patience, so here an indignant person is called
“short-souled,” or “brief-souled,” if it is permissible to speak this way, by
Solomon in Proverbs 14:29. Such a person quickly grows hot and can therefore
be compared to a small pot which, when placed over a little fire, grows hot
quickly and suddenly, and to a paper fortification that is demolished and
subdued in a moment. Or rather, to adhere to Solomon's own words, he is
compared to a city whose wall is entirely absent or collapsed. Proverbs 25,
the last verse.

Solomon further dissuades us from this indignation from the subject in whom
it resides, namely the fool. See Proverbs 14:29. It is commonly thought that a
person is foolish and senseless if he does not immediately blaze up at an
injury inflicted upon him and repel force with force or insult with insult.
Indeed, it is counted praiseworthy if someone returns an injury twofold. The
Holy Spirit, however, judges very differently: he accounts as foolish and
senseless the person who is easily indignant and leaps to vengeance.

Then, by means of the adjunct, Solomon identifies this fool as the one in
whose bosom indignation rests. It is possible for a wise and pious person to
be seized at some time by this hasty and troubled indignation, as David was in
1 Samuel 25. Yet because he rejected it in his ordinary course of life and
struggled against it, he should not be reckoned among fools.

VERSE 10. Do not say, “Why were the former days better than these?” For you
would not ask this from wisdom.

Solomon presents a particular kind of indignation, the worst among all the
others: a person tacitly accuses God's ordering of the administration of the
world or the Church, and asks why former times were better than the present.
The pious, however, ought to be content with present circumstances. Hebrews
13:5; Psalm 4:12. For God administers everything for the good and benefit of
his people. Even if they are treated more harshly than they were in former
times, they can nevertheless receive from it fruit not to be regretted,
provided that they make good use of this salutary correction of God and the
harshness in comparison with past times, and, on the authority of the Apostle
in Romans 12, learn “to serve the time.”

Its lawful use is that a person should contend with himself and rehearse the
sins committed against God from which this harshness has sprung, so that, led
by repentance, he may remove adversity from himself. Thus it is within your
power to be happy at any time, provided that you apply yourself to piety. But what Solomon made
famous in a proverb often happens: a sinner brings evil upon himself, then
rages against God and is angry with him.

When Solomon says that this does not proceed from wisdom, he says less than he
wants us to understand. For whoever inquires into these matters too curiously
is found guilty of folly. It is a synecdoche of the genus, “not wisdom” being
put for “folly,” as all understatements are.
