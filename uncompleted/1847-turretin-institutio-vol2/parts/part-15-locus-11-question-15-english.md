QUESTION XV.

FEAST DAYS.

Is it a matter of faith under the New Testament that, besides the Lord’s Day,
there are other feast days properly so called, whose celebration is necessary
in itself and by reason of the mystery, not merely by reason of order or
ecclesiastical polity? We deny this against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. *Feast days* are solemn days recurring in turn annually, weekly, or
monthly, separated from other days for the sake of religion and piety, and as
it were consecrated by a fixed law for the public worship of the Deity. No one
doubts that there were many under the Old Testament, since the distinction of
days formed part of the ceremonial law. But the question between us and the
Roman Catholics is whether they ought to have a place in the Christian
church. Just as the Roman Catholics have retained various rites of the Jews
in other matters, so in this matter also they have interpolated Judaism, or
even paganism itself, in which it is well known that many feast days were
distinguished from non-festival days. They overwhelm Christianity with so
great a mass of feasts that these occupy no small part of the year.

II. The question is not whether the memory of God’s benefits toward us and of
the mysteries accomplished by Christ to obtain salvation for us ought to be
perpetual in our minds and continually recalled by us. For this is

[Printed p. 89 | Internet Archive PDF 117 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

acknowledged by everyone and is done daily through the preaching of the Word
and the administration of the sacraments. The question, rather, is whether,
for the recollection and solemn public commemoration of each of those benefits
and mysteries, certain feast days sacred to God and recurring annually are to
be celebrated every year by Christians. We deny this.

III. The question is not whether annual days may be selected on which
Christ’s nativity, circumcision, passion, or ascension, and similar mysteries
of redemption, are commemorated, or on which the memory of some particular
benefit is customarily celebrated. The orthodox judge this to have been left
to the liberty of the church. Thus, some appoint certain days for such
festivity, not from a necessity of faith but from a counsel of prudence, in
order to stir up piety and devotion more fully. Others, in accordance with
their liberty, retain only the Lord’s Day and celebrate on it at appointed
times the memory of Christ’s mysteries. Yet among them *dissonance in matters
of this kind does not take away concord in faith*, as Augustine once advised
concerning fasting.

The question is whether certain days are holier and more sacred than others
and are a part of divine worship, so that they ought to be celebrated by
reason of the mystery and not merely by reason of ecclesiastical order and
polity, as *Bellarmine* contends in *The Worship of the Saints*, book 3,
chapter 10, and as is the public doctrine and practice of the papacy. We deny
that those days are in themselves holier than others and hold instead that
all days are equal. If any holiness is attributed to them, it belongs not to
the time and day but to the divine worship. Thus, among those who retain their
observance, it rests only on positive law and an ecclesiastical constitution,
not on the necessity of a divine command.

IV. First, feast days properly so called, which are matters of necessary
faith, bind in themselves, and are observed by reason of the mystery, must be
commanded in the Word of God. For the right to prescribe his worship belongs
to God alone, and he omitted from his Word nothing that he judged necessary
for his church. Yet nowhere do we read that such feasts were either instituted
or celebrated by Christ or the apostles. Nor can ἄγραφος, *unwritten*,
tradition be alleged here, since no place can or ought to be given to it in
matters of faith or morals, as was demonstrated in its proper place.

Second, this brings back under the New Testament the distinction of days that
was abrogated under the Old and condemned by Paul (Rom. 14:5–6; Gal. 4:10;
Col. 2:16). Indeed, under other names and a specious pretext, it introduces
into the church even the superstition and idolatry of the ancient pagans.

V. Third, the ancients acknowledge that the feasts were observed neither by
an institution of Christ nor by one of the apostles, but by choice and custom.
There is a notable passage in Socrates, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 5,
chapter 22. Speaking there of the dispute between the Eastern and Western
churches concerning the observance of Easter, which was the first feast day
whose observance began in the Christian church, he says precisely: “Neither
the apostles nor the Gospels themselves imposed a yoke of servitude upon
those who embrace Christ’s doctrine, but left the Easter festival and other
festivals τῇ εὐγνωμοσύνῃ τῶν εὐεργετηθέντων τιμᾶν κατέλιπον, *to the
grateful honoring of those who had received benefits*, to be recalled by the
free and equitable judgment of those who had received benefits on those
days,” and so forth. He later adds: “Neither the Savior nor the apostles
commanded its observance by any law,” and so forth. “For the apostles,” he
continues, “had as their purpose not to make laws about celebrating feast days
but to prescribe to us the rule of piety and right living.” Nicephorus
Callistus repeats this in *Ecclesiastical History*, book 12, chapter 32.

VI. Fourth, these feasts in the papacy teem with pernicious superstitions.
Roman Catholics make feast days holier in themselves than other days, foster
the opinion of the *opus operatum*, *the work performed*, attach merits and
expiations of sins to them, consecrate them to the saints, and thus honor them
with the worship of *latria*. For, according to Durandus, book 4,

[Printed p. 90 | Internet Archive PDF 118 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

chapter 39, even festivals pertain to *latria*. Finally, they establish so
many feast days and distract minds with such difficult trifles and scruples
that great men in the papacy, overcome by weariness of them, have complained
most bitterly.

Hear *Nicholas of Clémanges*, in his book on new festivals, written in 1416.
Among other things, on account of the immense abuse, he proves that some old
feasts ought to be abolished rather than new ones instituted. He complains
that so much concerning the saints is read and spoken in the churches that
the reading of Holy Scripture is now entirely omitted, and so forth. *Pierre
d’Ailly*, in *On the Reform of the Church*, advises the ecclesiastical leaders
of the Council of Constance, among other things, “that provision should be
made lest so many new feasts be solemnized, so that, apart from Sundays and
the greater feasts instituted by the church, work might be permitted after
the office has been heard. This is both because sins are often multiplied
more on feasts in taverns, dances, and other wanton activities taught by
idleness, and because working days scarcely suffice for obtaining the
necessities of life,” and so forth.

*Polydore Vergil*, *On the Inventors of Things*, book 6, chapter 8, writes
that “just as it was once advantageous to institute feasts, so now it seems
better that they be abolished, since a good part of mankind spends this
leisure of feast days not in prayer but, day by day, ever more shamelessly in
increasing every kind of corruption of good morals.” *Erasmus* confirms the
same thing on Matthew 11. Pope *Urban VIII* himself, in his bull concerning
the observance of feasts published in 1642, attempted to restrict the number
of feasts. Among other things, he acknowledges “that, in the course of time,
the enemy had corrupted the feasts originally instituted to glorify the Deity
and turned them into a great offense against him and a grievous loss of
souls,” and so forth.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VII. Although the Lord’s Day is sacred to God, it does not follow that other
feast days can be dedicated to God for the same reason. 1. The Lord’s Day was
instituted and observed by the apostles themselves, while the other feasts
were ordained only by the fathers. 2. The Lord’s Day was commanded as to its
genus, so that one day in the week would be appointed for the public worship
of God, although not as to its species. But these feasts were commanded in
neither way.

VIII. The practice of the Jews, who observed the feast of Purim in Esther and
the feast of Dedication in memory of the temple purified by Judas Maccabeus,
of which John 10:22 speaks, does not immediately prove that this custom ought
to obtain in the Christian church, because of the difference between the
economies of the Old and New Testaments. Or it shows only that a public
commemoration of particular benefits of God can be made on certain annually
recurring days, provided that abuse, an opinion of necessity, mystery, and
worship, superstition, and idolatry are absent.

IX. In Acts 20:16 Paul does not imply that it was necessary for him to
celebrate the feast of Pentecost, but only that he had to hasten to be present
in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost because of the gathering of the Jews,
so that he might have a greater opportunity and opening to preach and to
convert many to Christ.

X. It is one thing to mention Christ’s conception, nativity, death,
resurrection, and ascension on certain days in sermons to the people, and to
take from this an occasion to exhort, console, and instruct the Christian
people for edification, piety, patience, and holiness. It is another thing to
establish by law and necessarily impose upon the Christian people feast days
sacred to God and the saints, which constitute part of religion and divine
worship and are holier than other days. The former can at times be fruitfully
employed in view of the circumstances of time, place, persons, and things,
provided only that abuse, superstition, and idolatry are absent, as was not
infrequently customary in the primitive church. But the latter is not
permitted, both because it belongs to God alone, not men, to prescribe the
things that are part of divine worship, and because religious worship is to
be offered to no creatures but to God alone.

XI. Something can be called *holy* either *absolutely*, by reason of some
inherent holiness, or *relatively*, by reason of its appointment to sacred
use. Such appointment, in turn, is either by divine or human institution.
There are no days to which any quality of inherent holiness can be attached,
for they are incapable of it. Even if it could be attributed to them, this
could not be done by human activity, since men cannot communicate internal
holiness to anything. We do not deny relative holiness to certain days by
reason of their appointment to sacred uses. If this appointment is from God,
as it formerly was on the Sabbath day and in the feasts instituted among the
Jews, there was true relative holiness by reason of the divine will. If it is
from men, holiness can in no way be attributed to days except by reason of
the things done on them and because of the end for which cessation from our
works has been prescribed.

XII. By the feast of which the apostle speaks in 1 Corinthians 5:7–8, he does
not understand a particular day on which the memory of Christ’s passion is
celebrated, but the whole time from our regeneration onward—not merely seven
days, as with the Jewish Passover, but the whole time of our life. This is
evident because the paschal feast is kept when the lamb has been slain and
the unleavened bread is eaten. But under the New Testament, once Christ, the
Lamb of God, has been slain, his power remains forever, and our unleavened
bread of sincerity and truth ought to endure throughout the whole course of
our life.

XIII. If there are Reformed churches that still observe some feast days, such
as those of Christ’s conception, nativity, passion, and ascension, they differ
greatly from the Roman Catholics, because those days are dedicated to God
alone, not to creatures. 2. No holiness is attached to them, nor are they
believed to possess any power and efficacy, as though they were much holier
than the remaining days. 3. They do not bind the faithful to a scrupulous and
excessively strict abstinence from all servile work on them, as though there
were some moral good or part of religion located in that abstinence and, on
the contrary, it were a great crime to do some work on those days. 4. The
church is not driven by any necessity to an unalterable observance of those
days. Rather, just as they were instituted by human authority, so they can be
abolished and changed by that same authority if the utility and necessity of
the church require it. For *everything is dissolved by the same causes
through which it arises*, as the jurists say. In a word, they are regarded as
human institutions, and superstition and the opinion of necessity are absent.

XIV. If certain days among some churches are distinguished by the names of
apostles or martyrs, it must not be thought that they are instituted for the
worship of those persons or terminate in their honor, as the Roman Catholics
do. Thus *Bellarmine*, *The Worship of the Saints*, book 3, chapter 16,
asserts that “the honor of feasts pertains immediately and terminatively to
the saints.” Rather, they are referred to the memory of the saints through
whom Christ built his church, for our benefit, but to the worship and honor of
God alone, who conferred upon the apostles and martyrs whatever praiseworthy
thing they possessed, did, or endured.

Moreover, they neither invoke those saints nor offer incense to them, but give
thanks to God alone, whom they invoke, for the benefits that overflow to us
through the saints’ ministry and example. Hence, we cannot approve the harsh
judgment of those who prosecute as guilty of idolatry the churches in which
those days are still observed with the saints’ names retained, since those
churches agree with us in the doctrine of the worship of the one God and
detest the idolatry of the Roman Catholics.

XV. Although our churches do not condemn that practice simply as evil,
nevertheless the sad outcome makes it clear that the institution of feast
days, received into the church in the papacy from the κακοζηλίᾳ, *bad
imitation*, of the Jews or pagans, gave occasion to the horrible idolatry that
continues and increases in the papacy. Not without serious reasons, therefore,
did our churches prefer to abolish that practice

[Printed p. 92 | Internet Archive PDF 120 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

in their reformation, so that they would contract no contagion and would
carefully guard against the danger from it. For in religion, whenever there
is even the smallest departure from God’s prescription and men wish or think
something to be permitted to them, all things thought safe are to be feared.
Indeed, experience has established from what very small beginnings there
arose in the papacy a remarkable advance into superstition and idolatry
concerning the worship of images, invocation of saints, purgatory, the
sacrifice of the Mass, prayers for the dead, and so forth. Thus, it seems
better to go without some useful but less necessary good than, through its
use, to run into a grave danger of some great evil.
