Lecture XXIX.

Now, in the second place, as for the examples, let us omit those which it is
plain at first sight have nothing to do with the point: namely, the evil angels
before their apostasy and our first parents before the fall. The former were
not human beings and were never elect; the latter, while in the state of
innocence, did not need a Mediator and Redeemer. They therefore could not even
possess the faith of which we speak here—namely, that saving persuasion of the
remission of sins through Christ the Mediator—nor, consequently, the
righteousness which is from faith. They possessed only the righteousness which
belonged to the state of an unimpaired nature and their native condition. The
event itself later proved that this could be lost, and none of us has ever
denied it. Let us therefore pass over these examples, which Bellarmine says he
draws from far back—though not aptly, I say.

[Marginalia: Lecture XXIX. The second part of the proof, through examples.]

1. He puts forward Saul, whom he proves from Scripture to have been good and
righteous at the beginning, since chapter 9 of the first book of Kings—that
is, of Samuel—says that “Saul was choice and good, and there was no man among
the children of Israel better than he; from his shoulders upward he stood
above all the people.” What does Bellarmine infer? “Here,” he says, “a man
excellent in mind and body is plainly described. And since there was no one
among the children of Israel better than he, if this man was not truly
righteous, no one in the whole people of God was then truly righteous. This,
however, is false, since Samuel was still alive, along with many prophets and
sons of the prophets.” Yet who with even a little knowledge of the idiom of the
Hebrew language does not immediately see that the corruption in this Vulgate
rendering arose from the homonymy of two words found there—namely, *tobh* and
*bachur*? The former is a common word familiar to everyone:
it means not only good, but handsome or elegant in appearance. The latter means
not only chosen but also a young man. He is so called among the Hebrews because
he is choice—that is, fit for every task, and particularly for war—rather than
old, as Kimhi says. The Hebrew masters commonly explain this passage in that
way, as the Chaldean paraphrase alone could have taught them: it explains the
word טוב by the Chaldean word שפיר, which means handsome in appearance, as is
clear even from Daniel 4:12, where this word occurs. Thus the true translation
of the passage is: “He had a son, young and handsome, so that no one among the
children of Israel was more elegant or handsome than he, or more excellent in
form.” It is clear that only the form and stature of his body are mentioned
here, even from the explanation which is added: “From his shoulders upward he
was taller than all the people.” Yet those good translators transferred this
from the body to the mind. You can see in passing, therefore, what kind of
theology bad grammar, as they say, produces among them—that is, a corrupt
translation, construction of words, punctuation, or something of this kind.
Yet Bellarmine, who professes himself a master of that language, was not
ashamed to put forward this passage in such a corrupt form. We therefore deny
that Saul was ever endowed with the true faith of which we speak or with
righteousness.

[Marginalia: The first example, Saul. 1 Samuel 9:2. Answer. טוב and בחור. The true translation. Corollary.]

We assert the same concerning Judas the traitor and Simon Magus. That sophist
does not shrink from affirming that they were once righteous and faithful:
because Acts says of the latter that he believed and was baptized, while of
the former it says that he was given to Christ. “Those whom you gave me,”
Christ says in John 17, “I have kept, and none of them perished except that son
of perdition. But if the Father gave him to the Son, he was certainly good,”
says Bellarmine. We readily answer, however, that Simon Magus was not endowed
with true justifying faith, nor Judas likewise, but only with temporary faith.
Beneath it still lay at the bottom of his soul deep hypocrisy and bitterness
of gall, which finally broke out in each of them: it ruined that Magus and
exposed that traitor. Luke in Acts indicates only such a faith in Simon, one
which grew like seed from rocky soil. As for Judas, he was given to Christ
only with respect to outward fellowship and companionship, not with respect
to inward communion in saving faith. Thus he was chosen for that apostolic
office, not for eternal life. We rightly deny that all whom the Father gave to
Christ in that manner and sense were immediately good—that is, truly righteous
and faithful.

[Marginalia: The second and third examples. John 17:12. Answer.]

Therefore, since none of those just cited ever possessed true faith and
righteousness, it follows that none of them ever lost it. Fourth and fifth, as
for David and Peter, Bellarmine says that through their sins they fell from
grace and righteousness, and consequently also from justifying faith, though
it is agreed that they were from the number of the elect. We deny that minor
premise. Although we acknowledge that each fell most grievously, we deny that
they fell entirely from faith and grace. Otherwise they would never have been
renewed and raised up again through true repentance if they had completely
lost that grace and righteousness of Christ which comes from faith—unless we
wish to say that a faithful and elect person can be born again twice or even
more often, or regenerated anew. Consequently, why would it not also be
permissible to repeat the sign of regeneration as often as he is born again?
That would surely be an Anabaptist assertion. We therefore acknowledge that
faith and the Holy Spirit can in some measure be lost through such grievous
falls of the regenerate: namely, with respect to the sense and those more
familiar effects which the elect formerly enjoyed, particularly peace of
conscience and joy in the Holy Spirit. In this way they grieve and quench the
Spirit of God within themselves; and only when restored and raised up again by
repentance—an extraordinary repentance, so to speak—do they recover that vivid
sense of divine favor and the former light of joy, peace, and liberty, as
their own experience testifies. But as for the righteousness with which they
were once endowed through faith, and consequently the gift of faith
itself—that is, that very apprehension, however grievously afflicted and cast
down—they never entirely lose it, not even in

[Marginalia: Conclusion. The fourth and fifth examples. Confirmation of the solution. A distinction to be noted.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 162 | De Prædestinatione p. 118]

the deepest straits of temptation and the gravest struggles against the
terrors of conscience and that dreadful apprehension of divine wrath, arising
from a most acute sense of their sin and the blow of an accusing conscience.
Rather, they struggle so long, supported by the powerful Spirit of God, until
at last they fight through and rise again. Thus, to put it in a word, faith in
them can be grievously wounded, but never entirely killed; it can be pressed,
but not crushed, and be obscured; that spiritual life of Christ and justifying
and sanctifying grace can, so to speak, slumber beneath the ashes of sin and
become insensible, but it can never be extinguished, die, or be wholly uprooted
from their hearts, not even by the force, cunning, fury, and snares of all the
hosts of hell. Have you never seen a tree which appeared to you entirely dead
in winter, but flowered in summer, or indeed at the very beginning of spring?
If all its power of germination—what we call its soul or vegetative life—had
been extinguished, it surely never would have revived. Again, in the case of
most plants, we see whatever rises above the earth die and vanish during a
winter storm, so that those ignorant of the powers and ways of nature would
think them completely destroyed. Yet their roots actually remain vigorous and
succulent beneath the earth, awaiting the spring sun, while only the stem and
branches have been consumed by the winter cold. So too in a dangerous battle,
a soldier can be stunned by a heavier blow to such a degree that, his senses
benumbed for a time, he perceives neither himself nor his enemy, cannot handle
his weapons, and cannot raise sword or shield. Instead, his head bowed, he is
compelled for a little while to lower and relax his hands, until his sense
returns and the warrior comes back to himself. Meanwhile his weapons never
fell away, but always clung tenaciously to his hands, however limp and
insensible for a time. We must judge in this way concerning that saving faith
and that grace and righteousness of Christ which are proper to the elect.
Those once clothed and fortified with that Christian armor never put it off
again, are never stripped of it, and are never wholly disarmed—whether the
faithful sense its presence or, because of a grievous fall, struggle, or
conflict, do not sense it for a time. Thus, to conclude this point, the eclipse
which intervenes because of the sins of the faithful can be neither total nor
final.

[Marginalia: Note well. See Psalm 51:13: “Do not cast me away from your face, and do not take the Spirit of your holiness away from me,” or, “Do not take it back from me.” Similar examples. 1. 2. 3. Application.]

Finally, as for the example of Solomon, I have deliberately reserved him for
the last place because there is some cause for hesitation whether he should be
placed in the order and number of the elect or driven and banished into the
company of the reprobate. Consequently, if he is held to be elect, we must deny
that he ever lost true faith and righteousness; but if he is judged reprobate,
we must assert that he never possessed them. Bellarmine maintains that in his
old age Solomon fell not only from grace and righteousness—as Scripture, he
says, testifies, when Solomon polluted himself with abominable idolatry—but
also that he was plainly reprobate and condemned. He confirms this by the
authority of Cyprian and Augustine. Cyprian writes as follows in his fifth
epistle: “Solomon, Saul, and many others were able to retain the grace given to
them as long as they walked in the ways of the Lord. When the Lord’s discipline
departed from them, grace also departed.” Augustine, in book 22 against Faustus
the Manichaean, writes as follows: “Now what am I to say about Solomon?
Scripture vehemently accuses and condemns him, and makes no mention at all of
his repentance or of God’s indulgence toward him.” Thus he speaks. What, then,
must we say concerning Solomon? In matters of this kind which concern God’s
hidden judgment, it is surely better to remain ignorant without fault than to
make perilous determinations about them. It is better safely to suspend our
judgment until that time when all things are fully revealed than rashly to
wish to break into God’s secrets; and therefore to speak when Scripture alone
speaks and to be silent when it is silent. Yet so that, as far as Scripture
itself allows us to gather, we may vindicate the memory of so great a man from
so bitter a stain, far be it from us ever to join those who number Solomon
among the reprobate and condemned. Various firm reasons draw us toward the
contrary position.

[Marginalia: The sixth example, Solomon. Cyprian. Augustine, vol. 6, book 22 against Faustus, chapter 88, at the beginning of the chapter. Note well.]

First, there is the authority of sacred Scripture itself in 2 Samuel 7, where
a clear promise concerning Solomon is made to David through the ministry of
Nathan the prophet: “When your days are complete and you lie down with your
fathers, I will raise up after you your offspring, who will come from your
body, and I will establish his kingdom. He will build a house for my name, and
I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. I will be a father to him,
and he will be a son to me”—please note and weigh these words—“when he acts
perversely, I will correct him with the rod of men and with human blows, but my
kindness will not be removed from him”—note this clause too—“as I removed it
from Saul, whom I removed from before your face.” From this arise three
arguments demonstrating God’s purpose concerning Solomon’s salvation. 1. God
promises that he will be a father to Solomon and Solomon a son to him, which
would by no means apply to a reprobate. 2. He promises to chastise Solomon’s
iniquity and sins with human blows. He therefore was not going to punish them
with the eternal torment of hell. For what, I ask, is a human rod or what are
human blows? Surely, as I interpret them, those which strike and afflict only
the body, but do not reach the soul and its salvation, which no human being
can torment. Finally, he promises not to remove his kindness from him. He was
therefore not going to reject and exclude Solomon from grace and eternal
salvation because of his sins.

[Marginalia: Reason 1. 2 Samuel 7:12 and following. Application to the subject. Note well.]

But, they say, that entire promise must be understood only of the bodily and
earthly kingdom, which God promises that he will by no means take from Solomon
as he did from Saul; it must not be extended to the eternal salvation of the
soul. Yet it is agreed that those outward and temporal benefits were symbols
of heavenly, spiritual, and eternal things. Thus, just as Saul’s expulsion
from the kingdom was, so to speak, an argument and omen of exclusion from
salvation and grace because of his sins, so conversely the promise that the
kingdom would not be taken from Solomon was a pledge and earnest of the
remission of sins and eternal adoption. Cyprian likewise applies the same
promise, repeated in Psalm 89, to any faithful people who repent and turn
themselves to God, in the second epistle of book 4 of his letters. There he
calls it “at once the severity and clemency of God, who simultaneously
threatens and spares, punishes in order to correct, and, when he has corrected,
preserves.” Therefore, if this applies to Solomon—which Cyprian himself would
not dare deny—it follows from that passage that God corrected Solomon by
chastising him and preserved him when corrected. Next, I gather a very strong
argument for Solomon’s salvation from chapter 12 of the same book, where his
birth is described. It says there that Jehovah loved Solomon and that, by
God’s own command, he was named

[Marginalia: Objection. Answer. Cyprian, Epistle 52. Reason 2.]

in this way, as is clear from chapter 22 of the first book of Chronicles.
Moreover, Nathan the prophet was sent by Jehovah to give him also the other
name Jedidiah, because of Jehovah—that is, to attest the love with which
Jehovah regarded him—for Jedidiah means beloved by God. Therefore, just as the
apostle concludes that Jacob was elect because God had testified concerning
him that he loved him, why should we not gather and conclude the same here
concerning Solomon?

[Marginalia: 2 Samuel 12:24 and 25. 1 Chronicles 22:9.]

Third, the Lord testifies in Luke 13 that all the prophets are in the kingdom
of God together with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Who would dare exclude Solomon
from their number? He was such a rare and excellent instrument and secretary
of the Spirit of God for writing a part of the canonical and divinely inspired
Scriptures, for delivering to God’s Church such wholesome and fruitful
doctrines of faith and precepts of conduct, and indeed for celebrating and
portraying so devoutly and sweetly in that mystical ode the most holy mystery
of our spiritual union or marriage with Christ—a doctrine which is the marrow
of faith itself and of the Christian religion. Peter testifies in the first
chapter of his second epistle that the sacred Scriptures as a whole were
written and signed not only by men of God but by holy men of God. “Prophecy
was not brought forth in former times by human will,” he says, “but holy men of
God spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit.” This descriptive phrase for
them must be carefully noted, since it cannot apply to reprobates. Nor was it
right for that word of salvation and life, conceived and brought forth
immediately by the Holy Spirit’s dictation, to be written and recorded for the
Church by a son of death and hell and a slave of Satan.

[Marginalia: Reason 3. Luke 13:28. 2 Peter 1:21.]

4. It is clear that Solomon, if any other mortal—perhaps excepting
Melchizedek, or indeed, I will boldly say, with no exception even of him—and
not excepting even his father David, was a most illustrious type of Christ.
For throughout his whole life David principally expressed and foreshadowed
Christ’s cross, disgrace, persecutions, afflictions, and troubles inflicted on
him by his enemies, together with the final victory gained from them all. But
Solomon’s kingdom and manner of reigning instead represent the full triumph
and that heavenly and glorious kingdom to which Christ was raised at the right
hand of God the Father after his humiliation. I pass over the manifold wisdom
and knowledge and the other excellent gifts of mind conspicuous in Solomon.

[Marginalia: Reason 4.]

Finally, it is very probable that in the book he entitled Ecclesiastes,
Solomon bore witness to his repentance before the whole Church. Its very
subject—and indeed its threshold and entrance—seems to cry out that it was
written by Solomon after he had already left all those vain and abominable
things, had repented of each of those detestable acts of fornication, and had
awakened, as it were, from that stupor of a drunken conscience, so deadly by
its very nature. Throughout that book he speaks as one with experience, as
one released and freed from the Devil’s snares and led back to the straight
way of salvation and eternal life. He sets down a summary of this way as the
conclusion and crown of that discourse. Therefore, to assert that he was
nevertheless reprobate—though a holy prophet of God, a minister of the Holy
Spirit in recording Scripture, a most illustrious type of Christ in
foreshadowing his kingdom, glory, peace, joy, and wisdom, a man beloved by God
and sealed and marked with that twofold name impressed upon him by God himself
through the prophet as a kind of seal and character, and, finally, one who
turned to God and repented—could appear impious toward God, insulting toward
his word, and injurious to his Church.

[Marginalia: Reason 5. Conclusion of the proof.]

Yet two things can be objected in reply. First, nothing concerning his
repentance is disclosed in sacred Scripture’s account of his life. I
acknowledge that this cast Augustine and the other ancients into such straits
that they had nothing favorable on which to base what they asserted concerning
him. For why would the Holy Spirit have concealed from the Church of God so
great a matter, one which would have been so profitable to it—namely,
Solomon’s conversion and repentance? Next, since the apostle testifies that
all these things happened to them as types and were written for our
admonition, upon whom the ends of the ages have come, what could the fall of
so great a man further indicate and portend for us? What benefit would result
from it for the Church of God if we place him in the number of the elect?
Indeed, in the chapter against Faustus cited above, Augustine admits that he
could devise no allegory by which that sad overthrow might, so to speak, be
given a more favorable color. Meanwhile, he plays with astonishing allegories
about the offenses and sins of other fathers, even David’s own adultery and
murder, and paints them over with astonishing pigments. I am accustomed to
wonder at these, surely, but not to linger over them, because I find in them a
great deal of ingenuity and industry, but little or no weight.

[Marginalia: A twofold objection. 1. 2. 1 Corinthians 10:11. Augustine in the passage cited. Our judgment.]

We answer the former objection as follows. Sacred Scripture deliberately
concealed Solomon’s repentance from us, though not universally—for other
indications are sufficient for us to gather it and come to a knowledge of the
matter—but only in the historical narrative of his life and deeds. This was
to produce detestation for so foul a departure, which we admit was much more
grievous than all others, even Manasseh’s idolatry, and so much more shameful
because Solomon had received more than all others, and because the last things
were so much farther removed from the first. Thus, as the apostle immediately
adds in that same passage in 1 Corinthians, “Let the one who appears to himself
to stand take care that he does not fall.” The wise God’s chastisement is
various and manifold. He severely corrected and tried David for his sins both
in his own person and in his children and household. For Solomon, who fell
still longer and more foully, the peace of outward life and the firm and
secure tranquility of his kingdom continued to the end of his life—although
some rebelled, but still more by plotting and seeking occasions than by
putting their hand to the work. What then? Did such a dreadful scandal remain
unpunished in him? No. Although he repented and obtained mercy, he nevertheless
paid much more grievously than David for the crime he committed: namely, in
his infamy and the infamous memory of his life. Among all posterity he is
marked by this disgrace, which clearly discloses the disease and stain but adds
nothing about the remedy. It leaves his salvation, as it were, in doubt and
obscurity, so that his life might be a terror, example, and lesson to others,
especially kings—not a source of harm: a lesson, I say, toward caution, not a
source of harm encouraging license and security in sin under the hope of
pardon and remission. It would undoubtedly have become the latter if express
mention of his repentance had been made in Scripture.

[Marginalia: The solution of the first objection. 1 Corinthians 10:12.]

As for the latter objection, we say that from this, first, appears the truth
of God’s warning which so earnestly admonished, and indeed threatened, that
marriages should not be contracted with unbelievers, since from them it would
follow that their hearts were

gradually led into idolatry. They would fall almost furtively into those
snares of the Devil, as Scripture testifies happened to Solomon in 1 Kings 11.
From this, second, all who are
adorned with outstanding gifts are outstandingly admonished “not to think more
highly, but to think toward sober judgment.” This especially applies to those
to whom God has granted honor, peace, glory, and riches joined with civil
prudence and knowledge of affairs, so that they may walk cautiously and
carefully and take heed lest they become drunk from that cup of prosperity.
From this, third, the Jews especially ought to have learned neither to seek nor
to expect any outward king as their Messiah, however fortunate and glorious,
since in Solomon God had given them so illustrious a lesson in how fleeting and
fragile the whole outward condition of human affairs is. They ought to have
learned that eternal salvation of the soul, which they were to hope and wait
for from Christ and through Christ alone, has no weight or defense placed in
fleeting splendors of this kind. Finally, if we require a type or allegories,
come then, for Augustine’s sake, let us say that just as the glory and triumph
of the Head himself was typologically foreshadowed in Solomon’s kingdom and
life, as we advised above, so too, in Solomon’s mad and degenerate old age,
there was graphically outlined for us the most foul apostasy of the body—that
is, of the Christian Church—under Antichrist, its departure into every kind of
superstition and to idols taken from the Gentiles and transferred into the
Church. It is no wonder if that Father was unable to perceive this pattern,
which had not yet been fulfilled in his own age.

[Marginalia: 1. 1 Kings 11:1 and 2. 2. 3. 4.]

These points having been established, let us deny to Bellarmine that Solomon
ever entirely fell from the grace of God and faith. Although, to gratify or
comply with his idolatrous wives, he sank to such baseness and faithlessness,
that immortal seed remained in his soul, though miserably buried for a time;
and an ember of his former zeal, indeed some spark just short of being
extinguished, lay hidden beneath those Sodomitic ashes until repentance again
brought it forth into the light and kindled it. We have planted our foot more
firmly here so that, in a doubtful and disputed matter, we might bring and
offer you what light we could draw from sacred Scripture. Thus our judgment
concerning so excellent a king may incline either to the better side, or at
least not to the worse, while the full decision of the whole matter and
infallible certainty are reserved for Christ alone, the righteous, best, and
supreme Judge, on that last day. Let this suffice concerning the examples.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the solution. Analysis.]

There remain some arguments by which our adversary attempts to infer the same
conclusion concerning the loss of justifying faith. The first can be reduced
to this form. Every habit can be lost. But justifying faith is a habit.
Therefore it can be lost. Bellarmine says that even we concede the minor
premise, but proves it as follows: justifying faith is undoubtedly not an act,
since on our view it must adhere firmly and immovably in the soul of a
justified person, which cannot be said of an act. It follows, therefore, that
it is a habit. He confirms the major premise from the nature of all other
habits. They incline toward their act, he says, but do not compel it, and
therefore do not necessarily prevent a contrary act. From this he infers as
follows, to show that the habit of faith can be lost. Whoever performs an act
of unbelief loses the habit of faith. But a believer who has that habit of
faith can commit an act of unbelief. Therefore he can lose faith itself, or
its habit. The minor premise rests on the reason already advanced: namely,
that a habit does not compel its own act, but only inclines toward it, and thus
does not necessarily prevent a contrary act. He proves the major premise,
first, from Scripture, according to which, he says, one who performs an act of
unbelief becomes simply an unbeliever, an enemy of God, and guilty of eternal
death, and therefore loses that habit; and, second, from our doctrine, which
asserts that justifying faith is shaken off and the Holy Spirit poured out
through every sin. Behold his entire argument.

[Marginalia: Transition to the arguments—that is, to the third part of the proof. Reason 1.]

To this we answer, first: as for that universal major premise, I surely do not
know how it, and Bellarmine along with it, will be received even by the
philosophers—that is, the very sons of nature, who sought the salvation and
soundness of their souls in themselves and their own virtue—much less how it
could be admitted by a truly Christian theologian. The Christian theologian
lays the foundation of his salvation far more firmly and stably, indeed infinitely more
stably and immovably, than any stability of human virtue. Even the philosophers
maintained that a habit long confirmed and rooted in the soul passed into a
second nature and therefore could no longer be lost, especially the habit of
perfect virtue. The Peripatetics held that a person is made and called happy
from this virtue exercised and put into act; the Stoics, from the habit itself.
Yet let us deal more liberally with Bellarmine than the philosophers themselves
and concede the major premise concerning habits acquired by our own industry,
custom, and however long and diligent practice, and thus originating from the
powers of our nature alone. But as for those habits divinely granted, which
they call infused and which depend in no way upon our nature but upon God’s
eternal grace and gratuitous benevolence, according to that immutable counsel
of eternal election—what sane person would concede that major premise?
Everyone agrees that justifying faith is of this kind. When God decrees freely
to bestow it on anyone, he also determines so to confirm it in that person
that it will never entirely fall away from him or be wholly extinguished in
him.

[Marginalia: Part 1 of the answer. Distinction.]

It is thus clear that the proof of that major premise which he adds from the
nature and condition of other natural habits is empty and inept. The firm and
unmoved stability of these saving gifts depends not so much upon the nature of
things as upon the immutable counsel and will of the one whose gifts and
calling, the apostle says, are “irrevocable.” Yet to answer more distinctly
the prosyllogism which supports the major premise, we distinguish an act of
unbelief as follows. First, we say it can be taken for a total departure from
faith in Christ and the denial and opposition of acknowledged truth, from
deliberate malice against the light of conscience. This is blasphemy against
the Holy Spirit. In this sense, when the major premise is conceded, we deny
the minor as utterly false. Second, it can be taken for any sin against
conscience committed in life and conduct, for some grievous fall either
against the second table of the law or even against the faith, from weakness
and fear in order to avoid danger, such as the falls of David, Peter, and even
many martyrs. It is evident that Bellarmine takes an act of unbelief in this
sense here, namely, for any sin he calls mortal. We accordingly deny the major
premise as utterly false: namely, that by acts of this kind every habit of
justifying faith is put off and lost. Would he dare say that after committing
adultery and that murder David entirely ceased to believe in God? Or that,
even when Peter shamefully denied Christ with mouth and words, he did not
nevertheless cling to him in his heart and with faith, however

[Marginalia: Part 2. Romans 11:29. A distinction to be noted.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 165 | De Prædestinatione p. 121]

grievously wounded, his heart however injured and his conscience bloodied? Or
that Jonah did not even then apprehend him by faith when he wickedly fled his
God’s command and faithlessly refused his charge? Observe how conspicuous a
testimony and confession of his faith he gives even in the ship before those
blind idolaters. Bellarmine wishes that major premise to rest both upon sacred
Scripture and upon an inference from our doctrine. He is surely exceedingly
shameless, blasphemous toward God’s word, and injurious to his orthodox Church:
he can prove nothing of this kind from sacred Scripture, of which he cites no
passage, and he shamelessly ascribes so false a position to us.

[Marginalia: Part 3.]

Finally, he imagines that we will offer an exception which never entered our
mind: namely, that according to his promise God does not permit people once
truly justified to fall into sin. He then asks us where that promise of God is.
What sort of man shall we say this is? Bellarmine himself, surely—that is, a
bad and inept man who fashions for himself the shadow of an adversary against
whom to fight. But alas, how ineptly! How much more like a dishonest advocate
than one who deals seriously with the matter! We do not offer such an
exception at all. We say instead that one whom God has endowed with true faith
and the righteousness which comes from faith can never fall into a total and
final departure from faith, and indeed can never become wholly ungodly or an
unbeliever. Besides the testimonies already cited above, we could demonstrate
this from various other divine promises, particularly 2 Timothy 2: “The
foundation of God stands firm, sealed with this seal: the Lord knows who are
his.” Again, 1 John 3: “Whoever is born of God does not practice sin, because
his seed remains in him, and he cannot sin because he has been born of God.”
Behold the seed remaining in the regenerate and not disappearing, so that they
can never give and enslave themselves to sin as the unregenerate do. Likewise,
Matthew 16 speaks of the Church built upon the rock, against which the gates
of hell cannot prevail. This promise was undoubtedly made both to the
individual elect and to their entire body, and must be applied only to the
Church of the elect. It is clear and established that through faith we are
built upon that saving rock which Peter confessed. Hence it is clear that the
truly faithful can never depart completely from faith. For then the Devil
would not merely shake but dislodge them and tear them from the rock of
salvation, which would be “to prevail against it,” contrary to this promise of
Christ. Again, Christ promises the
faithful that he will grant whatever we ask according to faith and God’s will.
But Christ himself taught us to ask not to be led into temptation—that is, not
to be deserted by God and delivered entirely to Satan. Therefore he promises
to grant this as well. Again, the apostle’s words in Philippians 1, that “he is
persuaded of this very thing, that he who began a good work in them will
complete it until the day of Jesus Christ,” undoubtedly apply to the elect. It
follows that the work of faith and regeneration once begun in them will never
be abolished and can never again be erased through total or final apostasy.
Finally, not to heap up more testimonies—for we cited the clearest and
strongest ones above for this purpose—see the conspicuous promise made to the
faithful under Christ’s kingdom in Jeremiah 32. After God had said that he
would bring them back into his Church to live securely and that he would be
their God and they would be his people, he adds this as the reason—a reason,
I say, drawn not from causes but from the effects of his grace: “For I will
give them one heart and one way to revere me all their days, for their good
and the good of their children after them. And I will make an everlasting
covenant with them, that I will not depart from them”—or, “turn away from
them”—“to do them good; and I will put reverence for me in their heart, so
that they do not depart from me.” Behold a most conspicuous promise that divine
grace will never be taken from them, and particularly a promise of final
perseverance in faith, sanctification, and reverence for God.

[Marginalia: Refutation of the false exception. 2 Timothy 2:19. 1 John 3:9. Matthew 16:18. John 14:13, 14 and 15:7. Philippians 1:6. Jeremiah 32:39, 40.]

Let us proceed to the second argument, which is surely far more absurd than the
first. It is drawn from the infants of Christians, who, Bellarmine says, are
both truly justified through baptism and receive faith in infancy according
to the position of those whom he calls Lutherans, and indeed even without
baptism according to Calvin and other orthodox theologians, because children
of the faithful are born holy. Yet, he says, who does not see that very many of
them, when they grow up, sin most grievously and indeed become plainly wicked,
and therefore as adults lose that faith, righteousness, and holiness which
they received as infants, either through baptism, as the Lutherans maintain,
or merely by being born of faithful parents, which he falsely attributes and
attaches to Calvin, Bucer, and others—that is, to us orthodox? Anyone can
readily reduce the argument to formal shape. We refrain from doing that, lest
we appear to display logical artifice in an obvious matter with an empty loss
of time.

[Marginalia: Reason 2.]

To this we answer that, as far as the orthodox are concerned—for if those whom
he calls Lutherans have some particular controversy with the papists, there is
no reason for us to make another’s quarrel our own—they say nothing of this
kind. The assertion that all the children of Christians, whether through
baptism or without baptism, are endowed with true faith and righteousness and
are consequently elect to eternal life is therefore sheer slander. The proof
which he cites for this purpose is drawn not so much from us as from the
apostle Paul himself, yet it is inept. This is the assertion that the children
of the faithful are holy, according to the apostle’s argument in Romans 11:
“If the firstfruits are holy, so is the lump; and if the root is holy, so are
the branches.” Likewise, 1 Corinthians 7: “The unbelieving husband is
sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving wife is sanctified in the husband;
otherwise your children would certainly be unclean, but now they are holy.”
They are holy, namely, with respect to participation in the gracious covenant,
or with respect to being comprehended in the covenant, whose seal is baptism.
That is, because of their faithful parents they are said to be born in the
Church and household of God. Consequently, from birth they are immediately
reckoned as his children by virtue of that covenant and marked with its seal;
and we ought truly to regard them as such until they finally demonstrate in
fact that they are otherwise. Nor does that condition common to all children
of the faithful—being born in the Church—conflict with God’s secret election
and justifying faith which depends upon it and is not common to all who are
born and baptized in the Church, any more than it conflicts with the outward
profession of faith in which many boast, though they do not belong to God’s
election, were never endowed with true faith, and indeed will never be so
endowed.

[Marginalia: Answer. Romans 11:16. 1 Corinthians 7:14.]

The third argument is as follows. If faith cannot be lost, whence, he says, are
there so many heretics in the world? For the difference between pagans and
heretics is that pagans never possessed faith, while heretics possessed

and lost it. This is clear from the descriptions of a heretic in Acts 20: “Men
will rise up from among you yourselves, speaking perverse things”; 2 Timothy
2: “Among them are Hymenaeus and Philetus, who have fallen away from the
truth”; and Titus 3: “Avoid a heretic, as one who is perverted and condemned
by his own judgment.” We answer by denying that connection. It certainly does
not follow from this that there will be no heretics in the world. It follows
only that the elect and truly faithful cannot become heretics—that is, they
cannot persist obstinately and with a hardened mind in a damnable and
pernicious opinion concerning the foundation of salvation. The cited
testimonies do nothing to prove the connection. It does not follow that those
who departed from sound faith—that is, the orthodox doctrine of salvation—to
heresy were ever endowed with true and saving faith, but only with bare
knowledge of the truth and general assent, together with outward profession
of it, or at most with a taste and temporary faith. Moreover, the distinction
which he advances between a pagan or unbeliever and a heretic is false, at
least according to the use we give this word. It is not necessary that every
heretic ever actually professed true doctrine, since in this age the great
majority are born of heretical parents, are imbued with the same errors from
their earliest years, and never embraced the truth.

[Marginalia: Answer.]

As for the fourth argument, that exhortations, admonitions, and so forth would
thereby become empty and void, we have refuted it elsewhere. We showed that
the pious zeal, care, concern, prayers, watchfulness, caution, and so forth of
the faithful themselves do not conflict at all with the firmness of election,
saving faith, justification, adoption, and so forth. Consequently, neither do
the means which promote and serve these things—namely, warnings, counsels,
threats themselves, exhortations, and other things of this kind. There is
therefore no reason to linger here now.
