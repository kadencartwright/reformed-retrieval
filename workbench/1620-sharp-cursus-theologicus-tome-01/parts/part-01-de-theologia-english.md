ON THEOLOGY

Since we are about to treat the topics of theology that are called
commonplaces, we shall first discuss the name itself; next the question
whether it exists; third, how many kinds there are; and finally, what theology
itself is.

In Scripture theology is called “the wisdom of God” (1 Cor. 2:6–7; Eph. 3:10),
and also “the mind of the Spirit” (Rom. 8:6); third, “the mystery of the
faith,” from its effect (1 Tim. 3:9), and “the faith” (Gal. 1:23); fourth, “the
word of God” (Heb. 4:12). Now since *logos* is twofold—internal, which is called
reason, and external, which is called speech—theology is called the *logos of
God* in both senses, insofar as it is declarative speech by which God’s will is
expressed. Hence the name “theology” was employed by the doctors of the church
to signify the speech of God, or speech about God and divine things.

The existence of theology is proved, first, by the thing itself, for it has
always existed and still exists in the church; second, by the nature of God,
for since God is supremely good, and every good is communicative of itself, he
cannot but communicate himself to creatures; third, by the external consensus
of all nations, because no nation has ever existed so barbarous that it did
not fashion gods for itself as it pleased, whose oracles it might consult and
whose worship it might celebrate. So far were they from wishing or being able
to lack theology altogether that they instead abused this very name to obtain
divine

[1620 Tome I | col. 2 | BSB frame 00015]

authority for a superstitious religion. Fourth, it is proved by the internal
agreement of every human conscience, which testifies that God exists, and
testifies to divine knowledge or theology; much more is it proved by the
agreement of those who, having understood true theology, worship and venerate
the true God.

One theology is archetypal, another ectypal. Archetypal theology is in God
alone: it is God’s own knowledge of himself, by which he also knows all things
together and individually, most perfectly and most simply, in one undivided
act. Indeed, this theology is God himself, since whatever is in that utterly
simple essence of God is God himself.

Ectypal theology is that which is conformed to that archetype through
revelation and gracious communication. This is either Christ’s insofar as he
is man, which is called the theology of union; or the angels’, by which they
know God and divine things in their own manner—both of which are
unsearchable to us; or the theology communicated to human beings.

The theology communicated to human beings is twofold: natural and
supernatural. Natural theology is twofold: implanted and acquired. Implanted
theology is that whose principles God instilled in the first man before the
fall; Romans 1:19 and 2:15 concern it.

Acquired theology is gained from contemplation of God’s works and from
experience; Romans 1:20 concerns it.

The principles of both these kinds of theology were true, so that a person
could deduce true conclusions from them. Yet at first—

[1620 Tome I | col. 3 | BSB frame 00016]

—even before the fall—neither was perfect in every degree, but only begun.
Consequently, both were afterward to be increased and perfected, partly
through fuller communication and partly through use and experience. But in
humanity now fallen, both have been weakened and corrupted, so that from
these principles a person can neither truly know the true God nor live well
and blessedly. The philosophers therefore attempted in vain to construct
theology from these principles, since, as the apostle testifies in Romans
1:21, they became futile in their thoughts. This whole theology can do nothing
more than render a person “without excuse” (Rom. 1:20).

Finally, this theology, which at first was good in itself, became “demonic”
through human fault (James 3:11).[^1]

Supernatural revealed theology, like every ectypal theology, is considered in
two ways: either as it is in itself or as it is in a subject. In itself it is
perfect, just, and holy, both with respect to the one communicating it—namely,
God, who is perfect, just, and holy—and with respect to the thing communicated,
since a work must in some fashion reflect its maker.

Communicated theology, as it is in a subject—Moses, Peter, and so forth—varies
according to the nature of the subject and undergoes increases and decreases.
It is not the essence of theology itself that is intensified and diminished,
but its existence in a particular subject. In this respect, therefore,
theology can be called greater or lesser. The theology of the angels likewise
undergoes increase (Eph. 3:10; 1 Pet. 1:12), and even Christ’s does; hence he is
said to have increased in wisdom and grace before God and human beings (the
last verse of Luke 2).

Supernatural theology communicated to human beings, considered in itself, is
twofold, corresponding to humanity’s twofold state, namely integrity and
fallenness. In the state of integrity, it was that will of God in threats and
promises, with sacraments continuing, concerning the continuation of the grace
conferred in creation, if a human being in due obedience

[1620 Tome I | col. 4 | BSB frame 00016]

had acknowledged God. But in the state after the fall, supernatural theology
is the wisdom of acknowledging God in Christ. Both are revealed in the
written word, and so theology is defined as follows:

Theology is wisdom concerning divine things, according to the truth of God,
inspired by God and entrusted in Christ to God’s servants through declarative
speech; it is contained in the books of the Old and New Testaments insofar as
it conduces to the glory of God and the salvation of the church.

The proximate genus of theology is wisdom, for it is often designated by this
name in Scripture, as in 1 Corinthians 12:6–7, Ephesians 3:10, and James
3:15.[^2] Theology, however, is not art, because it does not consist in making.
Art also does not concern the actions of the virtues, but theology especially
concerns virtuous actions, namely, the regeneration and sanctification of all
the affections. Nor is it prudence, because theology considered in itself is
not occupied with directing a particular action as it is in a subject; this is
the office of prudence. Nor is it understanding, for insofar as understanding
is taken as an intellectual habit, it is the habit of establishing the
evidence of a self-evident and immediate proposition solely by apprehending
its terms, or solely by sense experience and induction. Theology is not such a
habit. Finally, it is not science, as science is strictly understood, although
there is indeed no condition of science that does not belong to theology.
Nevertheless, it far surpasses and rules over every art and science. Certainty
in theology is not sought by a demonstrative method, as in the other sciences,
but from the utterances of God himself. Nor is the cause of certainty in
theology universal experience, a principle that is first in its own order, or
demonstration, but God’s revelation.

It is therefore wisdom, since all the conditions of wisdom enumerated by the
philos—

[1620 Tome I | col. 5 | BSB frame 00017]

—ophers belong to it. They are these: (1) that it is an intellectual virtue;
(2) that it is distinguished by a definite difference from art, prudence,
science, and understanding; (3) that it knows all things; (4) the noblest
things; (5) the most difficult things; (6) that it reaches the first and
highest causes of the knowledge of things; (7) that it is comprised within the
most certain kind of knowledge; (8) that it exercises rule over every
discipline; (9) that it avoids the Sophists’ rope of contention; (10) that it
is not entangled in the Sophists’ snares; (11) that, setting aside things
subject to destruction, it pursues divine and immortal things; and (12) that
it is dear to God and the servant of true happiness.

The difference is derived from theology’s four causes. The first is the
matter, or the subject with which it is occupied: namely, divine things—God
himself insofar as he has defined himself according to our capacity and
presented himself to be known—and all things insofar as they are from God, are
governed by him, and are ordered to him. Yet God and the things ordered to him
do not constitute this subject simply, but only as they are considered in
Christ. Hence the apostle calls Christ crucified the subject of gospel
preaching and consequently of this wisdom. Theology therefore treats
political matters, household matters, and the like, not simply insofar as they
are such things, but insofar as they regard God in Christ.

The form is that this wisdom is according to divine truth. Divine truth is
considered in two ways: either as the essential truth of God himself, which is
regarded by the theologian not insofar as it is infinite in itself, but as it
is the fountain of true theology; or as it has been communicated by God,
flowing from that fountain like a clear stream. The former is infinite,
indeed, God himself; the latter is finite. The former is exemplary; the latter
is the copy. This communicated truth is spoken of either with respect to its
fountain

[1620 Tome I | col. 6 | BSB frame 00017]

or in itself—whether the whole or its individual parts are considered
absolutely in themselves or comparatively—or with respect to those to whom it
has been communicated. It is twofold, the truth of things and the truth of
words, and this in Scripture. The truth of things is again distinguished
either with respect to existence, because being and truth are reciprocal, or
with respect to the mode of existing. Falsity also is twofold: first, if what
does not exist appears to exist; next, if what exists appears otherwise than
it is. The true form and essence of this revealed truth, however, consists
first in its conformity with that infinite truth and in its being informed by
it.

The principal efficient cause is God the Father, in the Son, through the
Spirit.

The mode of efficiency is also taught, namely inspiration (2 Tim. 3:16; 2 Pet.
1:22; 1 Cor. 2:11–12).[^3] For this theology is not innate in a person, nor is
it a power obtained from nature (1 Cor. 2:14). This inspiration is an action of
God by which he inwardly communicates his will to his servants through the
revelation of the Spirit.

The instrumental cause God uses—not from necessity, but from his mercy, with
regard for our weakness—is human ministry, namely, that of the prophets,
apostles, and evangelists. As God the Father, in Christ, through the Spirit,
inspired this wisdom in his servants, so he entrusted it to them to be
promulgated through declarative speech, and through their ministry committed
it to writing, as it also stands in the books of the Old and New Testaments.

Now the speech of God is threefold. First is *emphytos*, or implanted speech,
and this speech is the Son of God himself. Second is *endiathetos*, speech
introduced into the mind from elsewhere. This speech is in creatures, not in
God, because God can receive nothing from elsewhere. And just as a person
reasons with himself by introduced speech, so God, by ingenerated speech,

[1620 Tome I | col. 7 | BSB frame 00018]

speaks, as it were, with himself. Third is *prophorikos*, or declarative
speech. This prophoric speech of God differs from human declarative speech:
first, in instruments, because the instrument of God’s declarative speech is
spirit, whereas that of human speech is body; second, they differ in source,
because the source of the former is spirit, but of the latter body; third, in
object, because the proper object of the former is spirit, but of the latter
body and its organs; fourth, in the mode of communication, because God’s
spiritual speech is communicated spiritually, but bodily speech bodily. God
declared this speech of his in various ways, namely, through visions, dreams,
a living voice, and inspiration. And thus he left it to his church through his
servants, so that through the pastors and doctors of the church it might be
proclaimed until the consummation of the age.

The end of theology is twofold. The first and highest is the glory of God; the
other, subordinated to it, is the salvation of the church. Although the other
arts and sciences have their own particular ends, they must nevertheless have
these ends proposed to them as ultimate. Yet theology alone regards them
properly and immediately, and the other sciences cannot attain these ends
unless they are directed through theology. Each end of theology alone,
therefore, deserves to be called supreme. From the sublimity and excellence
of theology’s twofold end, it is clear that theology is also the most perfect
and excellent wisdom. This is likewise clear because that wisdom is most
perfect and excellent which proceeds from higher principles, resolves a thing
into its first principles, and does not depend upon other prior principles.
But the principles of all other sciences are not first absolutely, but only in
their own genus—that is, insofar as there are no other prior principles in
that science. Yet there are no principles prior to theology’s principles,
either within it or outside it. Therefore, and so forth. The principles of
theology are two: the first principle of the thing is God

[1620 Tome I | col. 8 | BSB frame 00018]

himself, who is the supremely first principle, before whom nothing is and from
whom all things exist. The second is the principle of cognition, namely, the
word of God, before which nothing was spoken by which this wisdom could have
been known. These principles are distinguished by the order, manner of
operation, and production of their effect. For God addresses us mediately
through the word, and the word mediately leads us to the knowledge of God in
Christ. Since, therefore, the word, or Holy Scripture, is the first principle
of the knowledge more accessible to us, and since God himself cannot rightly
be known by us otherwise than in this way in which he has manifested himself
in Scripture to be known, we shall treat Holy Scripture in the first place.
