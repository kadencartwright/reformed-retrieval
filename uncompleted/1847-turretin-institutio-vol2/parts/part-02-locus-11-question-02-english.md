# LOCUS XI—THE LAW OF GOD

## QUESTION II

### THE NATURE OF THE MORAL LAW

Are the precepts of the Decalogue matters of natural and indispensable right?

We affirm.

I. Four questions can be asked concerning the moral law. 1. What is its
*nature*? 2. What are its *parts*? 3. What is its *use*? 4. What is its
*abrogation*? Under its nature, its immutability and perfection are discussed;
under its parts, the division of the precepts and their genuine meaning; under
its use, its use either absolutely or relatively, with respect to man's
various states in nature, sin, and grace; and under its abrogation, the sense
in which it is said to have been abrogated and the sense in which it is not.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question. The nature of the moral law. Is
it a matter of natural and indispensable right?]

II. The first question about *the nature of the moral law* concerns its
*indispensability*. To understand this, 1. we must first distinguish the terms
that frequently recur in this argument. *Obligation* is the law's right over a
man, by reason of which a man who is subject to the law is bound to obey it.
*Dispensation* occurs when, in a case where the law truly remains in force and
binds, the law's obligation is removed from a particular man while the rest
remain bound.

[Printed p. 9 | Internet Archive PDF 37 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

A *declaration* or *interpretation of the law* occurs when it is declared that
the law does not bind in a certain case. *Annulment* occurs when a law is
abolished before it binds fully; *abrogation*, when one that already bound
fully is abolished and removed altogether; *derogation*, when it is partly
removed and partly remains.

III. 2. A distinction must be made between *simple precepts*—that is, purely
moral precepts, which are matters of natural right—and precepts *mixed* from
the moral and ceremonial, which are partly matters of natural right and partly
of positive right. The fourth precept concerning the Sabbath is moral with
respect to the general requirement of public worship, but ceremonial with
respect to the circumstance of a definite time. The fifth is moral with
respect to the duty prescribed and the promise of long life, but ceremonial
with respect to the appended promise of the land of Canaan.

[Marginal heading: The right of dominion and government.]

IV. 3. It must be observed that God's right, insofar as it regards creatures,
is either a right of *dominion*, which includes the right to possess, dispose
of, and use creatures as a lord or proprietor who can use and enjoy his own
property at his discretion; or a right of *government*, which properly regards
rational creatures. He governs these as Ruler and Lawgiver, to which pertain
legislation, judgment, and execution, so that he has the power to enact law,
judge by it, and execute the sentence pronounced. This right is commonly
distinguished into *natural and positive*. According to the former, God cannot
fail to prescribe to rational creatures duties whose opposites would imply a
contradiction, since they are founded not simply in the divine will but in the
perfection, eminence, holiness, and rectitude of the divine nature. According
to the latter, he freely and from his mere good pleasure prescribed duties
which he either could have refrained from prescribing, or whose opposites,
antecedently in the order of reason to the divine will, he could have willed
and commanded without any prejudice to his perfection and holiness and
without implying any contradiction.

V. Therefore, whatever things are so closely connected with God's nature,
perfection, eminence, and holiness that he cannot command their opposites
without prejudice to his nature, and that their being commanded would imply a
contradiction, belong to this natural right. For it is certain that God cannot
deny himself or do or command anything contrary to his holiness and
perfection. Thus God cannot command hatred of himself, blasphemy, and falsehood
or exempt man from the dependence and obedience he owes him, because this
would be to say that God is not God—that is, the first cause and absolute
Lord. These are the things that Bradwardine, in book 1, chapter 18 of *The
Cause of God*, calls *prior rational things*, things which are naturally such
antecedently to the divine will. But all those things concerning which it
cannot be said that God was bound by the necessity of his nature to one side
of a contradiction, or in which we know that God can make or actually has
made some change in the obligation, are matters of positive right. Such were
the symbolic law given to Adam and the ceremonial laws of the Old Testament,
which depended upon God's free will. The permission of sin is also commonly
referred to this category.

VI. Besides the *uncreated natural divine right*, which is called *primary*,
is founded immediately in God's own nature and holiness, and whose contrary he
could not will or command without denying himself, there is another,
*secondary and created*, which is founded in the nature of things according to
the constitution established by God and in the fitness or mutual
correspondence of things. Although this right is necessary on the supposition
of the order God instituted, according to which he willed the nature of things
to be as it is, it cannot be considered to have the same necessity as the
first, nor do the duties flowing from it have an equal degree of obligation.
The former is absolutely immutable, and there is no

[Printed p. 10 | Internet Archive PDF 38 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

case in which God can relax it, because he would thereby seem to deny the
nature in which it is founded. Thus he could never command or approve hatred
of himself, idolatry, perjury, or falsehood. But although the latter contains
a natural rule of rectitude, because it presupposes a definite state of
things, it could be changed in certain cases when the circumstances of things
and persons changed, though only by the authority of the one who instituted
it. For example, the killing of a man and the taking of another's property,
which are prohibited by the sixth and eighth commandments, can become lawful
when some circumstance changes, as when a divine command or public authority
intervenes. In this respect it can be referred to positive right—not that
which is absolutely and simply such and entirely free, having no foundation
except God's will alone, but positive right in a qualified sense. For although
it is founded in the order of things and created nature, it can admit some
change according to the wisdom of the Lawgiver who instituted that order.

VII. From this distinction of divine right flows the distinction among
precepts that must be maintained here. Those precepts which are so closely
connected with God's nature that, once a rational and governable creature is
posited, it cannot fail to be bound to perform them—such as subjecting and
conforming itself to God and having him alone as its God, and the like—are
without controversy matters of natural right. But those which flow from God's
free good pleasure, and which God was completely free either to impose or not
impose, must be considered matters of positive right. Almost everyone agrees
on this. But they do not agree equally about the particular distinction and
enumeration of the two kinds. Some refer to natural right what others judge to
belong instead to positive right.

[Marginal heading: Indispensability with respect to God and to us.]

VIII. 4. *Immutability and indispensability* can be understood in two ways:
either *absolutely and simply*, with respect both to God and to us, or
*comparatively and in a qualified sense*, with respect to us but not to God.
Some things are absolutely indispensable both to God and to us. Others are
indispensable in a qualified sense with respect to us, because, since we are
not lords or judges but subjects and ὑπόδικοι, *accountable to judgment*, we
cannot add anything to or subtract anything from the law. They are not so,
however, with respect to God, who as supreme Lord and Lawgiver could in certain
cases dispense from a law enacted by himself without sin.

IX. The question therefore comes down to this: Are the precepts—not of the
ceremonial and forensic law, which are known to be matters of mutable positive
right, but of the moral law, considered not according to their appendages but
according to their substance—matters of natural right, whether primary or
secondary, rather than merely positive? Are they therefore necessary not only
hypothetically from the sanction of the divine will, but absolutely and from
the nature of the case, and indispensable not only to men but also to God?

[Marginal heading: Three opinions concerning the nature of the law.]

X. Three opinions on this question are especially well known: two extremes,
which assert either the dispensability or the indispensability of the
commandments, and a third, middle opinion, which holds that they are partly
dispensable and partly indispensable. The *first* maintains that the moral law
is dispensable in all its precepts, since it is founded solely in positive
right, which depends upon God's free will, and that he could therefore change
it at his pleasure. This is the opinion of, 1. various scholastics: Ockham, on
book 2, question 19; Gerson, treatise *On the Spiritual Life*, book 1; Peter of
Ailly, on book 1, question 14; Almain, part 3 of his moral treatise, chapter
15; and others who follow them. They are chiefly driven to it both by their
rashness in removing the second commandment from the Decalogue and by the
power they arrogate to their pontiff to dispense from God's precepts. 2. The
Socinians, who stand for this dispensability chiefly in order to prove that
the Mosaic law is imperfect and required correction. They are joined by those
among our own people who maintain that the moral goodness and evil

[Printed p. 11 | Internet Archive PDF 39 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of things flow from nothing but God's free will, and therefore that things are
good and just only because they were commanded, rather than commanded because
they were just antecedently, so that nothing would prevent him from commanding
their opposite if he wished. The *second*, middle opinion holds that the first
three precepts of the first table are indispensable, the fourth partly so,
and all the remaining precepts of the second table dispensable. Scotus and
Gabriel take this view and therefore exclude those precepts from natural law
strictly so called. They are joined by those among our own people who maintain
that certain moral precepts of the Decalogue which flow absolutely from God's
nature are absolutely indispensable—namely, the first, second, third,
seventh, and ninth—but that others which depend upon God's free will, such as
part of the fourth and the fifth, sixth, eighth, and tenth, although fixed and
indispensable with respect to us, are nevertheless dispensable with respect to
God, who for certain reasons can command the opposite without doing anything
repugnant to his nature. The *third* opinion belongs to those who hold that the
moral law is simply indispensable in all its precepts because it contains the
intrinsic ground of justice and obligation, not insofar as it proceeds from a
law but insofar as it is founded in God's nature and arises from the intrinsic
constitution of the thing and the proportion between the object and the act
as compared with right reason, or rational nature. Thomas and his followers
(I-II, question 100, article 8), William of Auxerre, Richard, Paludanus, and
many others take this view.

XI. This is also the more common opinion of the orthodox, which we follow,
though with this qualification. Just as all the precepts do not rest equally
upon primary natural right—some flowing absolutely from God's nature and
commanding things which God wills most freely yet necessarily, and so
necessarily and immutably that he cannot will the contrary without a
contradiction, while others depend upon the constitution of the nature of
things through the intervention of God's free will—so they must not be
considered to possess the same degree of necessity and immutability. Although
dispensation properly so called has no place in them, a declaration or
interpretation concerning them is nevertheless sometimes given when the
circumstances of things or persons change, as will be seen afterward.

[Marginal heading: The indispensability of the precepts is proved. 1. From
man's necessary dependence upon God.]

XII. The following reasons support our opinion. 1. The rational creature as
such has a necessary and indispensable dependence in the moral order and in
relation to right reason, by which it cannot fail to be obligated to obey God
and be subject to him, because otherwise it would not be a creature, which is
contradictory. Therefore, in God there is naturally and antecedently to his
free will a right to subject the rational creature to himself and obligate it
to obedience, which he cannot deny or decree its opposite without
contradiction. That such a right belongs to him is evident both from God's
independence, primacy, perfection, supreme dominion, and similar attributes
which found such a right, and from a parallel consideration. For every
creature has a natural, necessary, and essential dependence upon God in the
order of being and as a second cause, by which the creature cannot exist and
act without God, nor can God cast off care for it and leave it to itself.
Therefore, the moral dependence of the rational creature upon God as the first
truth and holiness is no less necessary and immutable than the natural
dependence of every creature.

XIII. It is no objection that God was moved to produce the creature by no
necessity but by mere freedom. For although everything outside God is
contingent in this sense—that is, such that he could have refrained from
creating it—nevertheless, on the supposition of its existence, he necessarily
wills and does certain things in or concerning what he wills to exist, so that
he cannot act or will otherwise. To this point

[Printed p. 12 | Internet Archive PDF 40 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

pertain these words of Cajetan on I-II, question 100, article 8: “Although the
divine will is simply free toward what is external, nevertheless, upon the
supposition of one free act it can be necessitated to another. If it wills to
promise absolutely, it is necessitated to fulfill the promise; if it wills to
speak and reveal, it must reveal the truth; if it wills to govern, it can only
govern justly; if it wills to have subjects who use reason, it cannot fail to
be their lawgiver.” Thus, on the supposition that a creature exists and acts,
God must preserve it and concur with it for as long as he wills it to exist.
Likewise, once a rational creature is posited, some law of God which it is
bound to obey must be posited. For it is contradictory that it should exist
and not be subject to God; or be subject to God and not governed by him; or be
governed without a law, and a just law.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the nature of the things commanded.]

XIV. Second, if all the precepts of the law are dispensable and rest only upon
positive right, God was completely free either to impose or not impose them;
indeed, he could also have commanded the opposite without any inconsistency.
For in things which are by nature ἀδιάφορα, *indifferent*, the one who has the
right to command also has the right to prohibit and to command the opposite.
On this hypothesis, therefore, God could not only have imposed no law upon the
rational creature to love and worship him, but could also have imposed a
contrary law that he be hated and blasphemed; that no God be believed in, or
none believed to be just, good, omnipotent, and the like; that he not be
obeyed; and that other gods, even the Devil himself, be worshiped. Thus the
foulest sins—hatred of God, atheism, blasphemy, and the like—would become
praiseworthy, because by the very fact that they fell under a precept they
would be made honorable and would no longer be sins. To state these monstrous
absurdities is to refute them. Who does not see that God cannot do such things
without denying himself and doing what is repugnant to his nature? For since
he is truthful and holy, it is repugnant to his nature to command what is
false and shameful. But if he commanded hatred of himself, atheism, or
idolatry, he would command dreadful falsehoods and the most shameful things
against God's very truth and holiness. It is objected in vain that God's will
is the supreme rule of justice and cannot be regulated from anywhere else,
because it is one thing for it to be such extrinsically, which we grant, and
another intrinsically, which we deny, as was proved in Locus III, Question 18.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the difference between the moral and ceremonial
law.]

XV. Third, if all the precepts of the moral law are dispensable, there will be
no difference between it and the ceremonial law, nor any greater obligation
to obey or greater sin of transgression concerning the one than concerning the
other. Thus one who ate pork or touched a dead body would contract no less
guilt before God than one who blasphemed God or killed a man. But both the
nature of the thing proves this to be utterly absurd and Scripture itself
clearly teaches it, when by instituting a comparison between legal sacrifices
and works of piety and mercy, ceremonial and moral worship, it so often
testifies that the former has no value before God in comparison with the
latter.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the conformity of the moral law with God's eternal
law.]

XVI. Fourth, because the moral law, which is the standard of God's image in
man, must correspond to the eternal and archetypal law which is in God, since
it is its likeness and ἀποτύπωσιν, *impression*, in which he has expressed his
justice and holiness. Hence we cannot conform ourselves to God's example,
whose imitation Scripture so often urges upon us, unless we order our life
according to the precepts of this law. Therefore, when its observance is
inculcated, this command is heard so often: “Be holy, because I am holy.” But
that law is immutable and perpetual. Therefore, the moral law, which is its
copy, must also be immutable.

[Printed p. 13 | Internet Archive PDF 41 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

[Marginal heading: 5. From the identity of the moral law with natural law.]

XVII. Fifth, the moral law is the same in substance as the law of nature,
which is immutable and founded in rational nature. This is so both because the
summary of the law, which consists in love for God and neighbor, has been
impressed upon man by nature, and because all its precepts are drawn from the
light of nature. Nothing is found in them which sound reason does not teach,
nothing which does not pertain to all nations throughout every age, and
nothing which is not necessary for human nature to attain its end. Therefore,
it must be a matter of perpetual right, because rational nature is always the
same and like itself; hence, what rests upon it cannot fail to be so. Nor, if
man's rational nature has been changed through sin concretely and subjectively,
was the law immediately changed abstractly and objectively.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

XVIII. Natural law cannot be said to have been changed through the *division
of fields* and the ownership of possessions and goods introduced by the law of
nations. For no natural law commands that goods be held in common or prohibits
their division. If all things are said to have been common at the beginning,
this must be understood not so much *positively*, as if God had enacted a law
that they be common, as *negatively*, because nothing had been expressly
enacted about it, since it was not yet necessary. But afterward the division
and ownership of goods were justly introduced, with God himself as their
author, to settle disputes, restrain external violence, ensure the certainty
of inheritances, and establish distinctions of condition, without which human
society could not stand.

XIX. Nor was it changed through the *introduction of servitude*, although
under the primeval law everyone had previously been free. They were free only
insofar as they were free from guilt, but when guilt was introduced, servitude
of every kind invaded. He who schemes against another's liberty deservedly
loses his own. And even if the law of nature makes all men equal with respect
to nature, it does not follow that they are equal with respect to qualities
and external condition. Nor was it changed through *prescription and
usucaption*, by which someone acquires ownership of a thing if its owner does
not reclaim it within a definite period. These do not cause another's property
to be retained against the owner's will, which is repugnant to natural equity,
but only cause what was formerly another's to become the property of someone
else once the prior owner treats it as abandoned. For although the owner has
not expressly transferred his right in the thing deposited to the depositary
by a compact or gift, he is nevertheless considered to have transferred it
implicitly, because he neglects and abandons it. Nor was it changed through
the *retention of a sword* which its deranged owner demands back in order to
commit murder. It is retained not to take his property from its lawful owner
for the sake of gain, but to prevent a murder which everyone is bound by
natural right to prevent as far as he can, because one who does not prevent an
evil when he can is as much at fault as the one who does it. Add that it is not
equitable for one who is not master of himself to be master of his property.

XX. God did not dispense from the second precept when he commanded the making
of the cherubim over the mercy seat and the bronze serpent. These figures were
not *personal religious images* representing some supposit, since there was no
similarity of nature between cherubim and angels or between the bronze serpent
and Christ. They were only *symbolic and emblematic*, shadowing forth an office
and qualities, and were not to be regarded as an *object or means of worship*,
the sense in which images are prohibited, but only as a *sacred sign* through
which God willed to shadow forth some mystery to us. Therefore, although they
pertained to religion, they were not on that account objects of religion or
worship. The same must be said of the bronze serpent, which was indeed a
symbol or type of Christ, but not an image.

[Printed p. 14 | Internet Archive PDF 42 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XXI. The fourth precept is moral and perpetual with respect to the substance
of the thing commanded—namely, that some solemn external worship is owed to
God and that a definite time must be assigned to it—but not with respect to
the particular determination of the seventh day, which was a matter of
positive right and therefore could be changed as ceremonial, as will be proved
more fully in its place. Nor, if the Maccabees fought on the Sabbath, was this
done by a dispensation from the law, but by its declaration and true
interpretation. According to this, discretionary works are indeed prohibited,
but not necessary ones, such as legitimate self-defense against enemies. Christ
himself confirms this more than once: “Which of you,” he says, “having one
sheep, if it falls into a pit, will not take hold of it and lift it out?”
(Matt. 12:11). And Mark 2:27–28: “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the
Sabbath.” Hence he healed a man on the Sabbath, and Christ's hungry disciples
plucked heads of grain on the Sabbath.

XXII. When Christ commands us to *hate* our parents (Luke 14:26), he does not
dispense from the fifth precept, but explains its true meaning and indicates
the subordination of the second table to the first, and of man to God. Thus τὸ
μισεῖν, *to hate*, must not be understood absolutely and simply as express and
properly so-called hatred, but *comparatively*, as lesser and diminished love.
It signifies that no natural ties ought to have such power over us that they
draw us away from the duty of piety, and that God must be loved and worshiped
above everyone. Thus, if love of parents is ἀσύστατος, *incompatible*, with
love of God, we must be ready to forsake parents rather than deny God and must
always know that it is better to obey God than men. In this way *to hate* is
often put for *to love less*. Thus in Genesis 29:31, 33 Jacob is said to *hate
Leah*—that is, to have loved her less, for Jacob must not be considered so
cruel that he regarded her with hatred. It is clear from comparison with
Matthew 10:37, where it is explained as loving less, that it must be understood
in this way here: “If anyone loves his parents more than me, he is not worthy
of me.”

XXIII. There was no dispensation from the sixth precept against murder when
Abraham was commanded to sacrifice his son, because the command was only a
test, not absolute. Nor, had he sacrificed him at God's command, would he have
sinned against the law concerning murder, because he would have done it
equipped with public authority—namely, God's command. The law does not condemn
every kind of homicide, because a magistrate is bound to inflict punishments
upon the guilty, and a private person is permitted to kill an unjust aggressor
or robber to preserve his life, provided he observes the restraint of
blameless self-defense. It condemns only homicide committed unjustly and
without authority by a private person. Moses' homicide cannot be considered
contrary to the law and approved by God through dispensation (Exod. 2:12),
because Moses was a public person who already had an internal calling. The
same must be said of Phinehas, who was equipped with public authority and had
an internal calling to the deed, if not an external one. Samson's αὐτοχειρία,
*self-killing*, was not simply contrary to the law, because it was done not
merely by a private impulse, which is condemned, but by the peculiar prompting
and inspiration of him who has absolute right over life and property and so
can reclaim our life or another's whenever he pleases. The following testify
that this inspiration was present here: 1. the prayers he offered to God to
obtain extraordinary strength for this final act, which were heard and
therefore proceeded from faith; 2. the divine strength supplied to him, with
which he overturned so great a structure, whence Augustine rightly observes
that the Spirit who worked miracles through him secretly commanded this; 3.
the testimony of the apostle in Hebrews 11:32, who places Samson among the
examples of faith, which he certainly would not have done had Samson sinned in
this deed and violated the law.

[Printed p. 15 | Internet Archive PDF 43 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XXIV. When Christ commands that the left cheek also be offered to one who
strikes the right and that the cloak also be given to one who takes the tunic
(Matt. 5:39), he does not change the law of nature by which it is implanted in
man to repel an injury and repel force with force. He condemns only τὸ
ἀντιπεπονθὸς, *requital*, or *retaliation*, when vengeance and an equal or
greater injury are added to self-defense. These words must be understood
proverbially and hyperbolically, not κατὰ τὸ ῥητόν, *according to the literal
expression*. Christ himself did not offer the other cheek to one who struck
him (John 18:23), nor did Paul (Acts 23:3). The meaning is that it is better to
be ready to suffer a new injury than to return an equal injury or repay evil
with evil, especially under the pretext of the divine law of retaliation. Thus
the adversative, as often elsewhere, contains a comparison within itself.

XXV. God did not command Hosea to fornicate contrary to the law (Hos. 1:2),
but commanded him to marry a prostitute. Augustine's rule testifies that this
must be understood *parabolically* rather than historically, for he holds that
Scripture must be understood figuratively whenever it commands some vice or
prohibits a virtue. The prophet's purpose also testifies to this: it was to
rebuke the people for spiritual fornication, which he would not have done had
he performed such deeds. Finally, the words themselves show this: “Take for
yourself a wife of prostitution and children of prostitution,” although the
same children could not both be taken together with the mother and begotten
from her. The meaning, therefore, is: Take a prostitute as an illustration,
set it before the people of Israel, and apply this comparison of a
prostituting wife to the Israelites, because they are devoted to spiritual
fornication—namely, idolatry. Even if the names of the wife and her father are
expressed by the prophet, it must not be judged to have been a true history,
because the parable of Lazarus shows that this is not a universal rule. Add
that even if it were granted, though it is not, that these things happened
historically, it would not follow that the prophet was commanded to fornicate,
because it is one thing to fornicate and another to take a prostitute in
marriage. But there is no reason why both the execution and the command should
not be emblematic.

XXVI. Simultaneous *polygamy* conflicts with the *primeval institution* of
marriage, in which God created one man and one woman so that two, not more,
would be one flesh; with its *confirmation* by Christ in Matthew 19:8, where,
in reproving the license of divorce, he says that it was not so from the
beginning; and with the *testimony of the sounder nations*, among whom a man
who had two wives at once was marked with infamy. Whatever Ochino may say in
its defense, therefore, it cannot be doubted that simultaneous polygamy is in
itself unlawful and contrary to natural law. But not everyone agrees on what
must be thought of the ancients who were polygamists. Some hold that a certain
peculiar divine *dispensation* and *ethical permission* intervened, according
to which God relaxed for them the law he had enacted from the beginning so
that the promise concerning the multiplication of Abraham's seed might be
fulfilled more quickly. Otherwise, they argue, it can scarcely be conceived
how so many men of such character would so frequently and persistently have
chosen a manner of life displeasing to God and sought the blessing of offspring
in something which overturned the rules of natural and divine right. They
think this could occur more readily because such polygamy is indeed repugnant
to secondary natural right and the uprightness of nature, which can sometimes
be lawfully changed when the circumstances of things and persons change, but
not to primary right, which is absolutely indispensable. But because such a
dispensation cannot be demonstrated from Scripture and rests only upon
probable reasons, others think it better to recognize here only *toleration*
and a *physical permission* of the fact, not an ethical permission of right.
According to this, God neither simply approved nor absolutely and formally
permitted it as lawful, but only overlooked and tolerated it for his own
reasons as a weakness and blemish. Thus it could be lawful in the forum of
earth, so as to obtain immunity from civil and ecclesiastical penalties among
men, but not in the forum of heaven before God.

[Printed p. 16 | Internet Archive PDF 44 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XXVII. Although Scripture does not rebuke polygamy in the patriarchs, it does
not follow that it was lawful. Various other things are related in Scripture
without rebuke which cannot be excused from sin, such as Lot's incest, Saul's
αὐτοχειρία, *self-killing*, and the like. That it was not approved by God can
be gathered afterward from the most grievous calamities with which he punished
polygamists for it. Consider the domestic cross laid upon Abraham on this
account; consider the quarrels and rivalries of Jacob's wives; it is well known
what David suffered from the crimes of his children which arose from polygamy,
and how dearly it cost Solomon.

XXVIII. Although the patriarchs did not know that they were sinning through
polygamy, this does not prevent them from having sinned through ignorance.
They thought that what was commonly practiced was lawful for everyone, since
the corrupt custom which had gained strength among them obscured or
obliterated the memory of the primeval institution so that they did not
recognize it as sin. Nor does it follow that they died under the guilt of that
sin which they did not know, because they never repented of it. Although they
did not expressly and formally repent of it, they nevertheless implicitly
sought its pardon when they sought pardon for all their sins, even their
hidden ones. Particular repentance for this deed should no more be required
of them than for other deeds concerning which they are not recorded as having
repented. Otherwise, we do not read that Noah repented of his drunkenness,
Lot of his incest, or others of their other sins, yet we do not doubt that
they obtained pardon from God for all their sins through faith in the Messiah.
If every godly person commits many sins through ignorance and error for which
he never offers distinct repentance, and this does not prevent him from
seeking and obtaining their remission by saying with David, “Cleanse me from
hidden faults” (Ps. 19), why should we not say the same of the ancient
polygamists?

XXIX. As for *divorce*, God neither simply commanded nor permitted it. He
willed only to restrain by definite laws the license for divorce that
prevailed among the Jews contrary to the natural law of marriage, in order to
set a limit upon an unlawful thing, not to make it lawful. Hence Christ says
that God *permitted*, or *tolerated*, this “because of the hardness of the
Jews' hearts” (Matt. 19:8).

XXX. If *marriages within prohibited degrees* were contracted at the beginning
between Adam's children, brothers and sisters, because necessity so required,
this occurred not so much by a dispensation properly so called from the law as
by its declaration. Most Jewish teachers resort here to God's *indulgence*, as
if God indulged the first men in this despite its being vicious. But indulgence
inconsistent with natural right and uprightness can have no place, because God
never indulges the doing of evil. Others, such as Augustine, plead *necessity*:
“Since there were no human beings,” he says in book 18, chapter 16 of *The City
of God*, “except those born from those two, men took their sisters as wives.
The more ancient this was under compelling necessity, the more damnable it
became afterward when religion prohibited it.” But neither can this fully
satisfy, because God himself was the cause of that necessity and could easily
have removed this circumstance, had he willed to create several human pairs,
so that men would not be cast into the necessity of violating natural right.
Others solve the matter more suitably by distinguishing *primary and absolute
natural right*, which is founded immediately in God's own nature, from
*secondary natural right*, which is founded in the nature of things and has a
place only in a definite state of things, just as the law prohibiting theft
presupposes the division of property. They hold that such marriages indeed
conflict with this latter natural right in a definite state of things, or in
*nature as constituted* after the multiplication of the human race, and are
unlawful by nature because of reverence for blood. For in a sister and

brother, who are the flesh and image of the parent, the parent himself is to
be revered, and natural modesty prohibits such a union. Thus the pagans
themselves—for example, Plato in book 8 of *The Laws*—call such marriages
μηδαμῶς ὅσια καὶ θεομισῆ, “not at all holy and hated by God,” and Diodorus
Siculus in book 1 says that it is κοινὸν ἔθος ἀνθρώπων, “the common custom of
men that brothers not unite with sisters,” because daily cohabitation and life
together would never be free from suspicion of unlawful love and would provide
occasion for fornication and adultery if such marriages were lawful. But they
do not conflict with primary and absolute natural right. Otherwise, God, who
does not deny himself, could never have dispensed from it, nor could he himself
have instituted or approved such a union even at the beginning of the world.
Therefore, since the constitution of things depends upon God's will, in
certain cases he could change it—if not absolutely, then in a qualified sense
and in a definite state of things—as he knew expedient for the preservation
of human society. And because he willed all men to be born from one blood, it
was then necessary, *while nature was being constituted*, for such a union to
have a place. Moreover, although a union of parents with children can never be
lawful, the same rationale does not apply to the marriage of brothers with
sisters. The reverence of children toward parents is absolute and intrinsic
and can never be removed, but the reverence of sisters toward brothers is only
extrinsic and relative, insofar as they are merely the image of their parents.
Therefore, nothing prevents God from restraining this relation so that it does
not appear in this or that person whom one must marry.

XXXI. The same must be said of the *law of levirate marriage* (Deut. 25:5), by
which the levir, or brother of a deceased man, was bound to marry his brother's
wife to raise up offspring for him. If it is understood of a levir properly so
called, or a natural brother, as the Jews are known to have understood it
(Matt. 22:24–25), then this was a peculiar case in which God willed to provide
for the preservation of the tribes and families of his people. But if it is
extended more broadly to the nearest kinsman outside the prohibited degrees,
as quite a few learned men, especially Calvin, not improbably hold, then the
word יבמה, which signifies any kinsman or relation by marriage, does not
conflict, and every difficulty will be removed. Boaz stood in such a relation
to Ruth the Moabitess (Ruth 3:9).

XXXII. There was no dispensation from the law against theft in the Israelites'
taking the Egyptians' vessels. 1. The Egyptians themselves freely granted
those vessels, for “Jehovah had given this people favor in the eyes of the
Egyptians, so that they gave to them when they asked, and thus they plundered
the Egyptians” (Exod. 12:33, 36). Nor did they demand them back as the
Israelites departed. Indeed, they expelled them together with the vessels,
when they “urgently pressed the people to send them quickly out of their land.”
Thus the Israelites cannot be said absolutely to have taken another's property
against the owners' will. 2. As supreme Lord and indeed just Judge, God justly
transferred the Egyptians' goods to the Israelites as wages for their previous
labors and the harsh servitude by which the Egyptians had oppressed them,
though free by nature. In the same way, a judge permits a creditor to take
pledges in a debtor's house or puts him into possession of the debtor's goods.
What is done by a judge's authority cannot be theft. 3. Not even the Egyptians
themselves can be considered lawful owners in the forum of heaven and before
God, but only usurpers, like all other ungodly men, although in the forum of
earth and among men they are regarded as such. Although the Israelites did not
return what they received as a loan, they cannot therefore be called thieves,
because they could have intended to return it if God had commanded them.

XXXIII. Although it is not lawful to put a sickle into another's harvest,
travelers passing by were nevertheless lawfully granted liberty in former
times either to pluck heads of grain from a field or gather grapes from a
vineyard in order to satisfy hunger (Deut. 23:23–24). This was not by a
dispensation from the law against theft, but by

its true interpretation, because it was a work of necessity permitted by the
supreme Lord for the purpose of binding men together by greater charity. If
these acts have any impropriety in themselves, they cease to be such when a
divine command, prompting, or calling intervenes to impose such a necessity or
grant such a liberty.

XXXIV. Although men can dispense from their own laws, God cannot similarly
dispense from the moral law, because human laws are positive and bind only on
the part of the lawgiver, not from the nature of the case. But the moral law
is a matter of natural right, which binds in itself and from the nature of the
case.
