Question. Is the act formally prohibited, or is the lawlessness or evil of the
act? For it seems that what is commanded is that by performing which we are
formally good, praiseworthy, worthy of reward, and blessed. But by performing
entitative and physical acts we are not good, praiseworthy, or worthy of reward;
we are so by performing acts conformable to GOD’s law. Likewise, simply by
omitting entitative physical acts we are not formally evil,

criminal, blameworthy, and punishable, but because we omit acts commanded by
GOD’s law. Thus Psalm 119:1: *Blessed are those who walk in the law of GOD.*
Those who merely act are not blessed, but those who act in conformity with the
law and rule prescribed by God.

I answer: an act is not commanded formally because it is conformable to GOD’s
law. Rather, because the act itself is commanded, the act becomes conformable
to the law if it is performed. Therefore, the act is not commanded because it
is conformable to the law, or insofar as it is conformable to the law. For in
that case the act would be commanded because the act was commanded: obedience
formally results from the command whenever the act is performed, while
disobedience arises from the interdict and prohibition whenever the act is
omitted or a forbidden act is committed. From these fountains flow moral
goodness and evil, praise and blame, reward or punishment, blessedness or
misery. Conformity with the command or law cannot be prior in nature to the
command, because it is the formal effect of the command, and the command is the
formal ground of the act’s conformity.

Nor is an act commanded insofar as it is entitative and physical, because in
that case all physical and entitative acts would be either commanded or
forbidden. This is plainly false of acts which flow from the vegetative soul,
such as growing, shrinking, maturing, aging, becoming hungry, and becoming
thirsty. Those acts are not capable of being commanded or forbidden and fall
under no law, divine or human. But in acts, or even in inclinations, there are
two things which ground the character of command and prohibition.

First, they are human acts or rational inclinations, not purely natural or
animal ones emanating from the vegetative or sensitive soul. For properly
speaking, a law is not given to a human being insofar as he is a living body,
an animal, or shares a generic nature with a brute. I acknowledge that the acts
of eating, drinking, and sleeping are animal acts. Yet they are not purely
animal; in a human being they become human insofar as they are modified and
limited by law and reason. We are commanded by God and reason to eat, drink,
and sleep in a certain manner—namely, sparingly and soberly—and for a certain
end—namely, GOD’s glory. Since first-prime motions are not purely natural or
animal, and these animal acts are human acts insofar as reason and free choice
modify them, they are consequently capable of law. Second, the other thing
which grounds the character of command and prohibition is GOD’s free

good pleasure. For God forbids Adam to eat from the tree of knowledge because
he wills to do so; he could have commanded eating from that tree if he had
willed. The ground of command and prohibition, or of divine law, is GOD’s good
pleasure, though it has a foundation in rational nature. But since conformity
with the law is subsequent to the command and results from it, it cannot be
prior to the command. Accordingly, a human act is not commanded because it is
conformable to the law; on the contrary, the human act is conformable to the
law because it is commanded. The same must be said of prohibition.

Question. Does God properly dispense with the law when he commands Abraham to
sacrifice his only-begotten son? I answer: by no means. For a dispensation from
the law is when a superior commands a subject to do with impunity the contrary
of what the law commands while the law still remains in force. But when God
commanded Abraham to kill his son, by the very fact that he commanded it he
removed the force and obligation of the sixth commandment of the Decalogue
here and now, so that the commandment of the Decalogue did not bind Abraham in
that act. The Pope, however, dispenses with GOD’s law while it remains in
force; nor does he possess authority by which he could abrogate GOD’s law even
for a single hour. Yet that swine arrogates to himself the power to remove from
GOD’s law both the guilt of fault and the liability to punishment. God himself
cannot do that unless he contradicts his own will.

[Marginalia: God never dispenses with his own law.]

Question. Is it certain that God possesses positive dominion and sovereignty
over nonbeings which never have been created? I answer: it is most certain. If
a potter knows how many clay vessels he could form by his free choice on any
day, or during a period of four or five years if impediments were absent, and
meanwhile resolves within himself to shape none at all but to leave the mass of
clay wholly untouched and unformed, the craftsman has a certain dominion over
those vessels never made and never to be made. In the same way, the supreme
maker of things—with whom are dominion and terror, and who establishes limits
for darkness and nonbeings—knows much more thoroughly the determined and exact
number of nonbeings and of all things never to be created and never to be
future. Yet there lay before him, as it were, a vast clay and mass of pure
nothingness from which all those nonbeings stood ready immediately to leap into
the class and society of creatures at the nod of GOD’s free will. Thus the fact
that they are confined—and which and how many they are—in the eternal prison
and tomb

of nonbeings and in the dark abyss of sheer nothingness—from which all created
things emerged when God most freely commanded—rests solely in GOD’s free good
pleasure. Just as many vessels remain part of the clay, never drawn out of the
clay’s potency, and are nonvessels because the earthly potter so willed and

nothingness. As if enclosed by GOD’s condemnatory sentence in the bosom of pure
nothingness, those nonbeings will remain forever, because the supreme craftsman
and maker of all things was so pleased.

Question. Is GOD’s active hardening formally a positive act? Is it an immanent
or a transient act? I answer: the will or decree to harden in God is an
immanent and eternal act, but GOD’s active hardening, since it is the execution
of that decree, is something temporal and transient. This hardening is denied
to be a negative action or an idle non-softening which leaves a person in his
own free and indifferent power to be hardened or not hardened. Yet the
lawlessness or evil in the creature’s culpable hardening does not require GOD’s
positive concurrence: first, for its existence in the nature of things,
because it is not a positive being; second, for its being unfitting and harmful
to the sinning creature; third, for its adhering to sin as something penal. But
in the following respect GOD’s act is called positive and judicial: first, as
it is distinguished from GOD’s idly permissive action; second, since this
judicial action occurs according to GOD’s active and not merely permissive
decree.

Question. Do the will of sign and the permitting will coincide? For what God
permits to occur does occur, and the permission is a sign that the thing was
willed and decreed by God, at least by a permitting will. I answer according to
the common account: an event, whether morally good or evil, is called the will
of sign because it is a sign that God willed it. The following are established
as signs: *He commands and prohibits, promises, counsels, and fulfills.* But
properly speaking, God does not will the crucifixion of his innocent Son by the
will of sign; he forbids and detests it. In this disputation I take the will of
sign to mean GOD’s approving or commanding will. This approval includes
commands, exhortations, invitations, threats, rebukes, and promises. Although
in a broader sense, whatever God decrees he in some manner approves, and God is
pleased in that thing, though not in the thing as morally
