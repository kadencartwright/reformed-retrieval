# On the Conditions for a General Council

*An undated consilium; a fresh translation from CO X/1 176–178.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 176]

For the disturbances and dissensions by which we see the church divided and
torn to come at last to an end, there is need of a free and general council.

Freedom consists in three heads: the place, the persons, and the method or
manner of proceeding.

As for the place, unless safe access lies open to everyone who ought to be
heard, so that they may debate the disputed matters there, it is well known
that the door has been shut to freedom. A city must therefore be chosen that
lies midway among all the regions from which those who ought to participate
in the council will come. Then it will be necessary for the neighboring
princes through whose dominions they must pass to promise and swear safe
conduct to everyone who will assemble there.

As for the persons, it would be most unjust to permit the bishops alone to
cast decisive votes, since it is more than sufficiently known that their own
case is being tried—a case in which they cannot be suitable judges.

### [CO X/1, p. 177]

Next, even if the authority they claim for themselves is conceded to them,
none of them is free. For they are bound by oath to obedience to the pope and
devoted to the Roman see, which is diametrically opposed to freedom.

A suitable remedy would be for men to be chosen from the party that desires
and demands the church's reformation in both doctrine and morals. Although
they would not have a definitive vote, as it is called, they would
nevertheless be free to interpose against every decree and determination
less than consonant with God's Word; they too would be given a hearing and
their protests admitted. They, for their part, would show that they had just
grounds for contradiction, preventing them from acquiescing in the bishops'
decisions.

It is above all intolerable for the pope to preside over the council as its
head, especially in this new capacity, so that everything depends upon him
and his pleasure. Even if by everyone's permission he obtains first place,
reason nevertheless demands above all that he submit himself to the council
in every respect and swear that he will observe whatever is decreed there.
In this way he must abdicate the dominion he previously seized for himself,
so that he is only one among the number of others.

The bishops in turn would have to be compelled to take their own oath: that
they will be obedient to God's Word and will take no account of the present
status quo; and that if any corruptions are discovered in doctrine,
ceremonies, or morals, they will by no means be their patrons or supporters.

The method of proceeding would be completely illusory if the custom that has
recently prevailed were retained: namely, that all who seek reformation
should set forth their doctrine orally or in writing, and then, in their
absence, the prelates should decree whatever they pleased. It is therefore
altogether necessary for everyone to be present, so that whatever the
bishops wish to deliberate badly or ignorantly may be corrected, and so that
everyone remains free to oppose clear and valid arguments to erroneous
opinions.

It is also worthwhile to establish before everything else what order should
be followed: first, the articles of doctrine on which the parties do not
agree should be examined;

### [CO X/1, p. 178]

from there they should pass to ceremonies; and finally the government of the
church should be discussed.

The heads of doctrine disputed today concern the worship of God: whether it
ought to be tested by the one simple rule of Scripture, or whether it has
been within human power and discretion to make laws; and then, whether human
traditions bind souls under guilt deserving death. Under this head are
included vows, the prohibition of marriage, auricular confession, and
similar things.

Then it must be asked and defined where the confidence of our salvation is
founded: whether upon the merit of works or whether we are saved by God's
free mercy. On this depend the subjects of free will, satisfactions,
purgatory, and similar matters. The invocation of God is joined to assurance
of faith, and it brings with it the question of the intercession of the
saints.

Under the second head, ceremonies, there must be discussion of the heap of
rites they have borrowed from the shadows of the law, and at the same time
of the number of sacraments and the additions made to them.

The third head, the government of the church, contains the definition of the
episcopal office, so that it may be known who rightfully and deservedly claim
this title for themselves. Degrees, orders, primacies, and other similar
things also belong here.

Now it would not be enough to have a council unless it were universal, since
the purpose is to calm and settle all the movements and contentions by which
the church is disturbed. It is true that individual kings and princes, in
order to find a remedy for the disturbances in their dominions, can summon a
national council if their neighbors do not join their consent and efforts.
But if a partial council is summoned which assumes the name “universal,” it
will do nothing other than kindle the fire more and more, increase the
conflicts, and create new disturbances. It is therefore absolutely necessary
that all who desire reformation consent to the council that will be held, so
that in this way all Christendom may grow together. Those who refuse holy
unity and concord should be regarded as schismatics.

## Translator's notes

1. The CO title *Si qua ratio generalis concilii Christianis dari possit* is
   syntactically compressed and may be rendered “Whether any plan for a
   general council can be given to Christians.” The English title states the
   memorandum's practical subject rather than treating that editorial heading
   as an authorial question.
2. “Definitive vote” translates the technical *vox definitiva*: authority to
   join in the council's final judicial decision. Calvin would at minimum
   grant the reforming party a hearing, a right to protest, and power to
   interpose against unscriptural determinations.
3. “In this new capacity” renders *in hac nova qualitate*. The force is the
   enhanced presidency Calvin rejects: not merely precedence of place but
   control on which every outcome depends.
4. “Take no account of the present status quo” renders *nec rationem
   habituros praesentis status*. The bishops must not defend existing
   institutions merely because they exist; doctrine, ceremonies, and morals
   remain answerable to God's Word.
5. “Satisfactions” is retained as a technical plural for works imposed or
   offered to satisfy for sin. Its proximity to merit, free will, and
   purgatory identifies the disputed late-medieval system, not ordinary
   emotional satisfaction.
6. *Umbrae legales* are the ceremonial “shadows of the law,” meaning rites of
   the Mosaic order understood typologically. Calvin accuses the Roman church
   of borrowing a mass of ceremonies from those shadows.
7. A “partial” council is one representing only a party. Calvin distinguishes
   it from a legitimate national council convened to remedy local trouble and
   objects specifically when such a partisan assembly claims universal
   authority.
