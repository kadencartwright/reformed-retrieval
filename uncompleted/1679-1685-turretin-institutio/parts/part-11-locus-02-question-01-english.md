TOPIC TWO—HOLY SCRIPTURE

QUESTION I

ON THE WORD OF GOD

Was Revelation through the Word Necessary?—Affirmative.

I. Just as the Word of God is the sole principle of theology, so its necessity
is rightly asked about before everything else: Was it necessary for God to
reveal himself to us through the Word, or was the Word of God necessary? In
former times there were, and even today there are, various people who think
that human reason affords sufficient aid for living well and happily.
Therefore they consider every revelation proceeding from heaven not only
superfluous but also ridiculous, on the ground that it is entirely probable
that nature has provided for human beings no less than for the other living
creatures. Thus they judge reason, or the light of nature, amply sufficient to
direct life and attain happiness.

II. But the orthodox church has always believed very differently, holding
that the revelation of the Word of God is absolutely and simply necessary for
a human being's salvation. For it is the seed by which we are regenerated, 1
Peter 1:23; the light by which we are directed, Psalm 119:105; the food by
which we are nourished, Hebrews 5:13–14; and the foundation upon which we rest,
Ephesians 2:20.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of the Word is proved from God's goodness.]

III. This is demonstrated by, 1. the supreme goodness of God, which
communicates itself. For since God created the human being for himself—that is,
for a supernatural end and a condition far happier than this earthly one—he
undoubtedly did not wish to fail him in this respect. Instead, through the
Word he disclosed to him happiness itself and the way to attain it, which
reason did not know.

[Marginal heading: From human blindness.]

2. The extreme blindness and corruption of the human being. Although after
sin he still has some remaining light for his guidance in earthly and worldly
matters, in divine and heavenly matters that concern happiness he is so blind
and corrupt that he can neither know any truth nor perform any good unless God
goes before him, 1 Corinthians 2:14; Ephesians 5:8.

[Marginal heading: From right reason.]

3. Right reason, which teaches that God can be known and worshiped unto
salvation only by God's light, just as the sun can be seen by us only by its
own light, Psalm 36:10. Nor would impostors who invented new sacred rites have
had to fabricate conversations with deities or angels—such as Numa Pompilius
with the nymph Aegeria, and Muhammad with Gabriel—unless everyone had been
persuaded that the right manner of worshiping the deity depends upon that
deity's revelation. Hence it has been accepted by the consent of every nation,
even barbarous ones, that for a human being to fare well, besides the reason
which they call the guide of life, a certain heavenly wisdom is also required.
This gave rise to various religions,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 54 | IA leaf 108 | PDF p. 108]

which became prevalent throughout the world. Nor should those be heard here
who maintain that these religions are merely ingenious human inventions for
keeping peoples within the bounds of duty. For although it is certain that
crafty people have invented many things in religion with which they instilled
reverence into the common people so that their dispositions would be more
compliant, they would never have achieved this unless some sense of their own
ignorance and need had already been implanted in the human mind. This made
people more readily allow themselves to be led about by peddlers and
charlatans of this kind.

[Marginal heading: From the twofold human appetite.]

IV. This is confirmed by the twofold appetite naturally implanted in the
human being: one for truth and the other for immortality, the one to know what
is true and the other to enjoy the supreme good, so that the intellect may be
perfected by the contemplation of truth and the will by the enjoyment of the
good, in which the blessed life consists. Since these appetites cannot be in
vain, revelation was necessary to disclose the first truth, the supreme good,
and the way to each, which nature could not do.

[Marginal heading: From God's glory and human salvation.]

Finally, God's glory and human salvation required it, because the school of
nature could neither lead us to the knowledge and lawful worship of the true
God nor disclose the way of salvation by which human beings might struggle
out of the misery of sin to the state of perfect blessedness, which consists
in union with God. A higher school of grace was therefore necessary in which
God would teach us true religion through the Word, instructing us in the
knowledge and worship of himself and raising us in communion with himself to
the enjoyment of eternal salvation—a height to which neither philosophy nor
reason could ever rise.

V. Although God has already revealed himself clearly in the works of creation
and providence, so that “what may be known of God” (τὸ γνωστὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ) is
manifest in them and his invisible things are perceived from the creation of
the world, Romans 1:19–20, nevertheless after sin that revelation through
things could not suffice for salvation. This was so not only with respect to
the subject, because it had no accompanying power of the Spirit by which human
blindness and wickedness might be corrected, but also with respect to the
object, because it contains nothing about the mysteries of salvation and the
mercy of God in Christ, without whom there is no salvation, Acts 4:12. “What
may be known of God” (τὸ γνωστὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ) does indeed occur there, but not
“the object of faith” (τὸ πιστόν); God the creator occurs, but not the
redeemer. Power and divinity—that is, the existence of the deity and his
infinite power—are gathered from the work of creation, but not grace and
saving mercy. It was therefore necessary for the defect of the former
revelation, which had been rendered useless and insufficient through sin, to
be supplied by another and clearer revelation, not only in degree but also in
kind. Thus God would employ not only mute teachers but would also open his
sacred mouth, which would reveal not only the excellence of his powers
but also the mystery of his will for our salvation.

VI. Although natural revelation communicates various things about God and his
properties, his will, and his works, it nevertheless does not teach us the
things which can suffice for the saving knowledge of God without the
supernatural revelation of the Word. It does indeed show that God exists and
what he is like, both in the unity of his essence and in the distinctive
character of his various attributes, but it does not know who he is
individually and with respect to the persons. It reveals imperfectly and
obscurely the will expressed in the law, Romans 2:14–15, but the mystery of the
gospel is wholly hidden from it. It presents the works of creation and
providence, Psalm 19; Acts 14 and 17; Romans 1:19–20. But it does not rise to
the works of redemption and grace, which can become known to us only through
the Word, Romans 10:17 and 16:25–26.
