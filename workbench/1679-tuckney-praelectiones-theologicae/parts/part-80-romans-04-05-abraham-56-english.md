5. The fifth corruption of sound doctrine on justification by Osiander is
that he denies our justification not only to Christ's death and priesthood,
but wholly and universally even to his humanity, while attributing it
entirely to Christ's divine nature alone and to the essential righteousness
of his deity transfused into us.

[1679 printed p. 311 | IA leaf 319]

Yet, as we have already said, Scripture clearly and openly proclaims that in
order for us to become the righteousness of God in Christ—that is, to be
justified by him—he was made sin insofar as, with our sins imputed to him, he
was made a sacrifice as priest. This belongs to his humanity and is foreign
to the divine nature, so that we should recoil from Osiander's impious
position. All the more so because,

6. Finally, in the business of our justification he attempts to abstract
and tear us away not only from Christ's death, priesthood, and humanity, but
from the whole Christ himself, at least as Mediator, when he asserts that
this justification proceeds from God's essential righteousness. But this
righteousness belongs equally and commonly to all the persons of the
Godhead. Therefore, at least in this benefit of justification, nothing
peculiar to Christ will remain, as Calvin very well observed. With respect
to its acquisition, Osiander indeed concedes that by his death Christ
merited that in our justification we should be gifted with this essential
righteousness of God. Thus by his position he will say that Christ
contributes much and contributes it peculiarly. But I answer:

1. He therefore contradicts himself. If, according to his position,
Christ's death makes this contribution, and only this contribution, to our
justification, why does he say that Christ's death contributes to it only
accidentally? Does an accidental influence contribute something so great?
And why does he deny our justification to Christ's priesthood? Does he not
thereby deny it to Christ's death as well? For did he not die insofar as he
was the priest who offered himself as a sacrifice?

Second, I also reply that although by Osiander's position Christ as Mediator
can have a place in the antecedents of our justification, he has nothing
peculiar left in actually and formally producing justification itself.
Osiander rejects from it the imputation of Christ's mediatorial righteousness
as something colder than ice and locates it in the communication and
transfusion of God's own essential righteousness. But as Calvin said
earlier, this essential righteousness of God belongs equally and commonly
to all the persons of the Godhead. Therefore nothing in this respect remains
to be attributed peculiarly to Christ the Mediator. Osiander thus remains a
deist in place of a Christian. But woe to him, and to all of us, if we seek
God outside Christ. We must then expect a sentence not of justification and
absolution but of condemnation, since God outside Christ is “a consuming
fire,” Hebrews 12:29.

And so at last we have seen how this deranged and impious position of
Osiander ruins everything in the subject of justification, sweetest of all
subjects, and indeed in the whole economy of God's grace in the gospel.
Bellarmine therefore spoke most truly: “Osiander's position cannot be
accepted unless one wishes to overturn the whole mystery of human redemption
and reconciliation.”
