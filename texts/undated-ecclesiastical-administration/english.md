# On Ecclesiastical Administration

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
[`latin.txt`](latin.txt). The printed-page markers correspond to the 1871
*Calvini Opera* witness.

**[CO X/1, p. 210]**

When the question is one of increasing the number of ministers, judgment on
that matter belongs both to the consistory and to the whole church. It must be
considered both whether suitable men can readily be found and whether the
church's resources are adequate to support that increase as it ought—or not.

As for the fitness both of pastors and also of elders, or overseers: just as no
one can discharge the office so perfectly that something better might not
still be desired even in the very best man, so too, unless men who have been
lawfully elected and who do what they can are admitted, there will be no end
to disturbances and disputes in the church. To lay down a rule that the
wealthy, and those distinguished by rank and reputation, are to be elected to
ecclesiastical offices plainly conflicts with the order and rule of the
Christian faith. Indeed, this is one thing that especially shows us to be true
disciples of Christ: we are not ashamed to submit to the administration of
those who possess less esteem and distinction in the world.

The consistory's function has its own bounds and limits, within which those
called to exercise it are to confine themselves. Their task, therefore, is to
watch over the Lord's flock so that God may be worshiped purely; likewise to
admonish both privately and publicly, and then also to excommunicate when the
matter requires it. When the consistory keeps itself within these limits and
extends no farther than the Lord permits, no occasion for protest will be
given.

But beyond that ecclesiastical session held in the consistory, we do not see
how establishing some further “ecclesiastical council,” as they call it,
accords with the Lord's own institution. On the contrary, it must there be
recognized and declared to be an entirely new construction, lest a door then
be opened to shifting innovations. This in particular can bring nothing but
certain ruin, when those who

**[CO X/1, p. 211]**

oppose the truth seize from it an opportunity to jeer, as though something
were being done under that name against the civil order.
Nevertheless, we do not disapprove, when a need arises, of choosing prudent
and experienced men to consider what is in the church's interest—provided
that this office is temporary and extraordinary, and in no way ordinary.

## Translation notes

1. *Presbyteri sive inspectores* is rendered “elders, or overseers.” Calvin's
   paired terms identify the office rather than two separate governing
   bodies.
2. *Regula christianismi* literally says “rule of Christianity”; “rule of the
   Christian faith” avoids making the word sound like a modern sociological
   label.
3. *Consessus* is a sitting or session. It refers here to the ecclesiastical
   body as assembled in the consistory.
4. *Status politicus* means the established civil or political order. The
   clause reports the accusation that a new parallel council could provoke;
   it does not identify the consistory itself as a civil institution.
