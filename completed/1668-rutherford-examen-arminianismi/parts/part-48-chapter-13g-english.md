1 TIMOTHY 1:19.

--- Which some, having thrust it away, have made shipwreck concerning the
faith. v. 20. Among them are Hymeneus and Alexander.

It is asked whether Hymeneus, Philetus, Alexander, Demas, David, and Solomon
were truly regenerate and yet afterward apostates. The Remonstrants affirm
it. We deny it.

1. For Hymeneus and Alexander are said to have strayed from the faith as from
a target, that is, from the sound doctrine of faith, since they denied a
principal article of faith, the resurrection of the dead. Here *faith* is
taken for the object of faith, or the faith which is believed, not for the
habit of saving faith or the faith by which one believes unto salvation. A
sound faith concerning the resurrection of the dead does not by itself
strongly prove regeneration.

2. Many people thrust away a good conscience who do not possess it. For they
did not thrust away a good conscience formally, as though they had possessed
it, but interpretively in its cause, namely, sound doctrine, because they were
unwilling to possess it. Paul therefore adds the specific qualification and
explains where their defection lay: *peri tēn pistin enauagēsan*, “they made
shipwreck concerning the faith.”

3. We do not deny that objectively they rejected the same faith that was in
Timothy, namely, the sound doctrine which Timothy

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 577 | PDF p. 626 | split word joined: amplexus]

savingly embraced, but which they held theoretically and in an orthodox
manner until they denied
the resurrection. Yet it was not the same faith in species and nature. Indeed,
the supernaturally infused habit of faith or theology which is in the truly
regenerate differs from the habit of faith or theology acquired by study and
practice, which even an unregenerate person can possess, as wicked prophets
and churchmen can, Matthew 7:22–23.

4. Demas loved the world more than was fitting for a faithful pastor, and did
so during a time of most grievous persecution, and he abandoned another
faithful pastor. It does not therefore follow that he was an apostate.

5. Nor do they rightly conclude from Colossians 4:14 that Demas was written in
the book of life and elect because he greets the saints and was Paul's helper.
That conclusion would require Philippians 4:3 to say that the names of all his
fellow workers without exception were written in the book of life, which it
certainly does not say. Even so, Paul could at that time judge in this way
about each of them according to charity.

6. Weighty theologians think that the Alexander who exposed himself with Paul
to mortal danger for the Gospel in Acts 19 was that coppersmith who eventually
became Paul's most bitter enemy, 2 Timothy 4:14. Nor are all who suffer for the
truth immediately truly regenerate. It is established, for example, that
Socrates drank hemlock because he denied a plurality of gods.

7. We deny that David was an apostate, because he did not walk in the works of
the flesh. Nor does one or another work of the flesh exclude someone from the
kingdom of heaven except demeritoriously. For there is a great difference
between David's being a man deserving death and being demeritoriously excluded
from the kingdom of heaven—which we acknowledge concerning David—and his
being a man under death formally and actually—which we deny concerning him.
Nor is there much force in the adversaries' claim that David was an apostate
because he despised the word of Jehovah and caused the enemies to blaspheme, 2
Samuel 12, and at that point in time had no good works declarative of faith.
For in that case all saints would fall from grace whenever they sin or sleep.
Yet it is certain: 1. That at that time he performed the distinguished parts
of a judge and pronounced against the robber of the ewe lamb a sentence worthy
of a good king; 2. That God had made an eternal covenant with David; 3. That
David prayed that God would not take the Spirit of grace from him—he therefore
possessed the Spirit of adoption; 4. That the Arminians cannot say, consistently
with their principles, that a supernatural habit and power of believing,
praying, and repenting was infused through Nathan the prophet's first sermon,
because they deny every infusion of a supernatural habit. They also deny that
supernatural powers are infused through prophetic sermons, which operate only
persuasively and morally. Indeed, they maintain that every supernatural power
and habit of believing and repenting is acquired only through

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 579 | PDF p. 628 | split word joined: crebris]

repeated acts, frequent practice, and diligence. But it is established from
the history that David earnestly repented and believed at Nathan's first
sermon and composed Psalm 51 as a truly penitential psalm. Therefore, the seed
of God—that is, the habit of saving grace—lay hidden in David beneath that
horrible fall.

Finally, concerning Solomon, we say that he was by no means an apostate. 1.
Scripture asserts that God himself loved him; therefore, he loved him to the
end, John 13:1. Solomon in turn loved God.

2. God promised that he would never forsake Solomon in the way that he had
forsaken Saul the apostate.

3. Although Solomon sinned grievously, it cannot be proved from Holy Scripture
that he placed his trust in idols or attributed divinity to them as the
pagans did. Although, in an example sufficiently lamentable, he perpetrated
outward acts of idolatry, he did not incite the people to idolatry as Aaron
did; he did not compel them as Jeroboam did; he did not worship the idols in
his own person or place them in the Temple.
