Verse 22. Do not despoil the poor man, because he is poor; and do not crush the afflicted man in the very gate.

Verse 23. For the Lord will plead their case, and will seize the soul of those who seize them.

In תדכא (*you crush*) there is a metaphor transferred from solid things reduced to dust, or grain reduced by a pestle, to the oppression of the poor. In שער (*gate*) there is a metonymy of the subject for judgment, which was customarily exercised in the gate.

The purpose is to deter everyone, especially magistrates, from the oppression and crushing of the poor by setting before them God's most just vengeance.

προσωποληψία (*partiality*) might appear to be involved if he had commanded in general that we should despoil no one of whatever

[1632 witness | PDF p. 506 | printed columns 996-997]

rank or condition, rather than restricting this command to the oppression of the poor. But the Spirit most wisely set forth this command, as he does all things, conscious of human perversity, by which people are most ready to rush upon the goods and fortunes of the poor, exhibiting in this matter the disposition of beasts, which leap over wherever the hedge is lowest. Nor is this all; but, what is still worse, they invade the poor man's possession for the very reason which above all ought to have restrained them. For because the poor are weak and stripped of all protection, they should have been relieved by these people—if they had any bowels of mercy—not oppressed. At Prov. 3.27 it is taught that assistance rendered by those who have it in their power to help the poor is just and owed to them; and at Matt. 25.41, 42 the Saviour foretells that all who do not provide food to the hungry, drink to the thirsty, lodging to strangers, and garments to the naked will be consigned to eternal torments. What end or limit, then, will there be to the punishments of those who take from the poor the little they have, and do so—which is like a monstrosity—at that very point in the reasoning by which they are especially invited to help them?

It is also worthy of observation that the person called poor and weak in the former hemistich is called afflicted in the latter. How savage and inhuman it is to add affliction to the afflicted appears abundantly from Ps. 109. Moreover, the person who attacks an unarmed man while himself armed is deservedly condemned by everyone for cowardice. Therefore, when a rich man fortified by friends and money oppresses and despoils a poor man destitute of these supports, he can in no way escape the stain of cowardice. Hence James 5.6 magnifies the oppression committed by the rich, because the just man does not resist those who do him injury. What is more, in this way they take on the express image of those who practise robbery on the king's highway, and indeed of the devil himself, who is accustomed to attack only the unprepared and those who decline battle—James 4.7?

While oppression of the poor by anyone is hateful to God, that which proceeds from a magistrate is much more detestable: because what ought to be the poor man's refuge and asylum is turned into a slaughterhouse and butchery; because the one who is appointed by God as a harbour in which the poor might take refuge when tossed by storms and waves becomes like a rock and reef against which they are dashed; because God's sacred authority, which is appointed for defending them, is used as a pretext for injuries inflicted upon the poor; and finally, because God himself, who is justice, is represented as the author and patron of injustice. Hence it is that when he speaks of the injury done in court, he uses the more severe word *crushing*; and the prophet Isaiah calls this kind of injustice a grinding of faces, Isa. 3.15. Besides the fact that injuries more grievous than those inflicted privately are admitted under the name of law, it wounds the spirit more cruelly when an innocent man sees that, under the pretext of public law, he has not only fallen from his hope of obtaining a good cause in court, but has also been marked with the contrary stain of injustice.

Verse 23. The Prophet refutes the vain opinion of the rich by which, when deceived, they readily rush forth to oppress the poor. For since they take courage to commit this crime from the fact that the poor can neither defend their own side nor have advocates to represent them in legal argument, the Prophet teaches that the God of armies offers himself to them both as patron and as judge, and runs to their defence even when not called. Therefore, this kind of rich man's conflict with a poor man is not unlike the case of someone who attacks another whom he thinks unarmed, only to discover him equipped not merely with hidden armour which he cannot pierce, but also beneath his garment with a dagger by which the attacker himself may be stabbed. The rich, looking only at the things presented to the external senses,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 507 | printed columns 998-999]

rush fearlessly to despoil the poor man; but if with the mind's eye they beheld God helping them, they would be restrained from harming the poor by a stronger rein of fear than the one by which they abstain from the fortunes of the rich. For unless they were blinded in mind, would they provoke God? Are they stronger than he? It is rash and dangerous to lay hands upon Leviathan, Job 40.28, and to contend with a prince. But to reproach the mighty God by oppressing the poor, Prov. 14.31, and to wish to contend with him differs in no respect from the utmost madness.

He adds that God will seize the souls of those who seize the poor. David threatened the man whom he supposed to have taken from a poor man the one sheep he had not only with fourfold restitution, but also with death besides, 2 Sam. 12.5. But if human judgment advanced so far as to determine that life should be taken for so small a thing stolen so wickedly, much more will the judgment of God, whose justice is infinitely more exact, exact the same penalty from the rich who oppress the poor.

By the word *seizing* he appears to indicate an untimely and violent death. For it is no wonder if those bloodsuckers, so swollen with the blood of the poor, do not survive long but burst apart and perish miserably. Let Ahab be an example, 1 Kings 21, who nevertheless had been unwilling to take Naboth's vineyard by force if Naboth had been willing either to sell it for money or to exchange it for a better one.

From this he also intimates that God shuts his eyes for a time while the poor are despoiled, but will not allow their oppressors to go unpunished. Therefore, the rich have no reason to rejoice when they have obtained their prey, since they will be compelled to lose it together with their life—indeed, with their very soul, which is destined to perish eternally; and the poor have this with which to console themselves: that they can appeal to God as their avenger and judge.
