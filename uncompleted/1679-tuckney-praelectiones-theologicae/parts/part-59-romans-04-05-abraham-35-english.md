But this had so far been only a prelude of skirmishing. At last the whole
army charges; Castellio leads all his forces into battle, rises against us
with greater spirit, and asks: “By what power will it come about that the
old man dies at death—by the power of faith or by the power of the body's
death?

“Not by the power of faith. For whether faith is seated in the intellect or
in the will, at death neither intellect nor will has strength. We frequently
experience this in sickness, when the senses both of body and mind are so
weakened that we feel ourselves without strength even for a brief prayer,
to say nothing meanwhile of those whom sudden death extinguishes. At its
instant faith certainly can have no power. Besides, it is too absurd for
anyone even to entertain the thought that the power of faith should
accomplish in a single instant what the same faith could not accomplish
throughout a person's whole life.

“Nor does it occur by the power of death, because that is not for a person
to be healed, but to die. Otherwise robbers would heal a person just as much
by killing him as Christ does by curing him. The souls of the wicked no
less than those of the godly would be cleansed from every sin in death
itself; thus no one would perish, and Christ would contribute nothing to a
person's salvation.

“But if it is said that Christ kills the old man only at death itself, we
will wonder why he does not instead kill him during life, to the greater
glory of his Father and as a more effective example to move others to
imitation.” Castellio then asks whether Christ accomplishes this through
faith or through death, both of which he thinks he has already refuted. More
specifically, “if it is through death, then we attach death, Christ's enemy,
to him as an ally. Though he came to abolish death, he either could not or
would not abolish sin except with death's assistance. But if neither faith
nor death is employed, it follows that salvation should not be ascribed to
faith, though no one affirms more strongly than we do that people are saved
by faith.”

Castellio argues all this at length and, as it seems to him, with force
against our position. I have chosen to transcribe it all together so that
we may have before our eyes, in a single synopsis, every point with which
he attempts to overthrow the truth we are now defending.

If I were to answer all these points generally by saying, “We grasp the
fact but do not know the manner,” it would be the very thing shouted by the
Roman theologians and others whom Castellio favors too much in the
sacramental controversy over the presence of Christ's body in the
Eucharist. Yet it is something that must be said altogether truly of
matters of faith, which rest on divine revelation rather than on scientific
demonstration of their cause or manner. Since faith is the “evidence” of
“things hoped for,” Hebrews 11:1, it contains something that is not indeed
uncertain, but is at least obscure with respect to the cause or manner of
the thing. Castellio therefore will not, I hope, take it amiss if, after we
have established from Scripture and arguments that our position is so, we
cannot elucidate with equal ease why or how it is so. But if he still
demands this too insistently, let him first satisfy us concerning a similar
but more familiar matter. He holds it certain that he was once born and
conceived in his mother's womb; can he explain the manner of it as well?
Solomon, more skilled than Castellio, I think, in both philosophy and
theology, declares it unknown to us. Castellio also knows perfectly well
that he slept during the night and awoke in the morning; but can he tell us
how he was situated at the very instant of falling asleep and waking, or
even say that he distinctly perceived it himself? Thus, if he wishes to be
an impartial judge, he will not charge it as a fault in us, much

[1679 printed p. 282 | IA leaf 290]

less infer that our position is false, if we cannot distinctly grasp or
explain the manner either of our spiritual birth—that is, regeneration,
which our Savior himself says is unknown, John 3:8—or of falling asleep in
death, when this life and sin end together.

Let this serve as the general answer to everything Castellio has heaped up
here. We will now also speak individually about the particular points.
