But let us do what belongs to us and to this passage. The question is
whether, as Osiander maintains, we are justified or righteous by Christ's
essential righteousness as God.

On this matter I admit that several Fathers, especially the Greeks, in their
eloquence and rhetorical hyperboles, and in order to represent more vividly
the image of God impressed upon his children, allowed themselves considerable
freedom of speech, and perhaps somewhat more than was proper. Here we may
refer to Athanasius's saying, “We are deified,” as well as Gregory
Nazianzen's, “becoming sons of God in the flesh,” and several similar sayings
in Basil and others. I hope this alone was what those described by Gerson as
contemplatives meant—as also the theologians called mystics and certain
present-day Platonists among us. They boast that in their divine
contemplations they are abstracted from their obscure personality, their
humanity is annihilated, and they are absorbed into the profound abyss of
deity, into which they are wholly transported. Hersentius expresses it this
way in his commentary on Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite: “When the mind has
reached this degree of love and contemplation, it is carried away by God in
a wonderful and unknown manner, is received by God and in God, becomes so
full of God, and is wholly poured into God, that God's essence is intimately
united to its essence and substance without any created mode.” Yet perhaps
by these admittedly rather bold forms of speech they meant to describe the
most intimate and immediate communion and conjunction with God at the apogee
of their passing contemplations. I admit that the intimate union of God's
essence with ours, allegedly accomplished without any created mode, makes a
far grander sound and suggests an essential or substantial coalescence of us
with God. Our enthusiasts of both the present and previous centuries have
not hesitated openly to affirm this when they say that their saints are
deified by God and, pardon their blasphemous barbarism, “Christified” by
Christ. Thus the blasphemously impious Servetus ridiculously invented a
transfusion of the divine essence into us and obstinately defended it even
to death. Likewise Osiander, whom we are now discussing, affirms that we are
justified—that is, made inherently righteous—by God's essential
righteousness transfused into us.

But to the contrary:

To begin with the least point, this is by no means necessary for our
justification. Justification does require satisfaction of the law.

The law requires us to be righteous, but to this end it no more requires us
to partake of God's essential righteousness than of his essence and nature.
The law as law regards us as God's subjects, obedient and answerable to the
authority of his commands. But Osiander's position and his imagined
justification make us gods ourselves—a claim some enthusiasts have not been
ashamed to assert—and thus people who should not so much offer divine
worship as accept it when rendered to us, which some of them have impiously
done.

Second, it is so far from necessary that, on the contrary, it belongs among
impossibilities and is plainly impossible. Whatever is received is received
according to the mode and measure of the recipient. Since all creatures are
finite in themselves—and even the highest enthusiasts cannot be removed from
their number and rank—they cannot be capable of the infinite divine essence,
and therefore cannot be capable of God's essential righteousness, which in
itself is identical with the divine essence. They cannot even comprehend it
with the understanding, “as far as the superessential perfections,”

[1679 printed p. 308 | IA leaf 316]

as Dionysius speaks, much less contain it within their compass through an
essential indwelling of the divine nature in us and its transfusion into us,
which they blasphemously invent. For if there was no confusion of the natures
and their properties in Christ's hypostatic union—from which we are very far
removed, as the orthodox church has always held and believed—much less can
there be any in this mystical union of our soul with God, although it is the
highest and most intimate that can be conceived.

Consequently, third, just as it is altogether impossible, so it is horribly
blasphemous. God's most righteous sentence will stand forever: “My glory I
will not give to another.” What, then, will be regarded as God's glory if not
his very essential righteousness and thus his essence itself? It is
immeasurably distant from every created thing and is therefore utterly
incommunicable even to the highest creatures. Away, then, with this most
sacrilegious blasphemy of all and a pride more than Luciferian! Lucifer only
wished to be like God; these people wish to be not merely nearly equal but
equal companions of his throne, since they are supposedly partners in his
nature. Since, as we have said, they do not refuse divine worship offered to
them, why do they not show themselves omniscient, omnipresent, and
omnipotent? Let what was once said to Job be said to them: “If you have an
arm like God, thunder with a voice like his; adorn yourself now with
excellence and sublimity; scatter the outbursts of your wrath; behold
everyone who is proud and humble him,” and so forth.

Since this position is so blasphemous, it is unsurprising that it is also
exceedingly absurd and ridiculous. Must it not be placed among absurd
misfortunes that God's essential righteousness should become an accident,
now present to a person and now absent, as Calvin correctly notes somewhere?
Osiander will admit that this righteousness which he now wants to exist in
a justified person was previously absent. Indeed, because he believes that
a justified person can fall from the state of justification, he will not
deny that after the transfusion and mixture he imagines it can again be
expelled or depart. But the substance of the divine essence is not so
accidental, nor is its presence in the person to whom it is essentially
united so fleeting and changeable. The hypostatic union of the two natures
in Christ was certainly inseparable and was not broken even in the three-day
separation of his soul from his body. Therefore this communication of divine
righteousness and essence which they imagine would likewise remain forever
unbroken.
