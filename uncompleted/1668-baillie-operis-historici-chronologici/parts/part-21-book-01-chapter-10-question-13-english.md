### QUESTION XIII

#### Where do Daniel’s seven weeks begin and end, and why are they divided from the remaining weeks?

Answer. The angel divides the seventy weeks into three portions in Daniel
9:25: seven, sixty-two, and one. Quite difficult questions arise about each of
these portions. Concerning the first portion of seven weeks, the questions are
where its boundaries lie and why it is distinguished from the rest. There is
great disagreement about them among ancient and modern authors, Protestants
and Roman Catholics. Most make the seven weeks the first part of the entire
sum. Some, however, prefer to cast this sevenfold period into the final part of
the seventy. The Jesuit Sanctius places Tertullian and Theodoret in this camp
in his commentary on Daniel 9. Suarez, in his commentary on the third part of
Thomas, question 1, joins Chrysostom to them and seems himself to incline in
that direction, although this exceptionally modest man, because of the many
difficulties he perceived on every side, professes that he does not venture to
determine anything.

> Marginalia, printed p. 175: *Where the seven weeks are to be placed*.

> Marginalia, printed p. 175: *Suarez wrongly places them at the end of the
> seventy*.

We find this opinion entirely unacceptable. First, it reverses Daniel’s order
without any necessity, since what the prophet makes the first member of the
seventy these men make the last for no stated reason. Second, by placing the
seven weeks after the sixty-two and the one, they extend the limits of the
seventy beyond Christ’s passion to the destruction of the city. We proved in
the preceding question that this is done wrongly. Third, they extend the weeks
not only as far as the city’s destruction but nine years beyond it. For only
forty years elapsed from Christ’s death to the overthrow of Jerusalem, whereas
the seven weeks of years that they count after Christ’s passion amount to
forty-nine years. Suarez himself admits that seven years, or one week, are
left over. Yet even with this extension they do not wish the seventy to reach
the city’s third destruction under the emperor Hadrian. Let the seven weeks,
then, stand at the head of the seventy, not at its foot, in accordance with the
plain order of Daniel’s words.

The next question is why these seven weeks were separated from the others. On
this point one may see great men departing not only from others but from their
own earlier positions. In his first edition Joseph Scaliger assigned this
reason for the division: at the end of those seven weeks—the forty-ninth year
from the second year of Darius Nothus, in the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes
Mnemon—Nehemiah finished the building of Jerusalem. Even if we were to grant
that forty-nine years, or seven weeks of years, elapsed from Nothus’s second
year to Mnemon’s thirty-second; and even if Jerusalem’s walls were completed
in a few days in Artaxerxes’s twentieth year while Nehemiah continued working
until Artaxerxes’s thirty-second year to adorn the city and the Jewish polity;
we nevertheless cannot grant, for the reasons given above, that these seven
weeks began in Nothus’s second year and closed at the end of Artaxerxes’s
thirty-second.

> Marginalia, printed p. 175: *Why the seven are separated from the rest*.

> Marginalia, printed p. 175: *Scaliger’s inconsistency*.

Junius, Diodati, Polanus, Alsted, Calvisius, Emmius, and other followers of
Scaliger persist in this opinion. Nevertheless, in his later edition Scaliger
himself departed from his position, as Petavius observes well enough, and
openly admits that he does not know where the seven weeks should be placed or
why they were separated from the others. He declares that no greater mystery
lies hidden in this division of the seventy than in Ezekiel 45:12, where the
mina is divided into twenty shekels, twenty-five, and fifteen—a wholly
arbitrary division, and not altogether μυστηριώδη, “mysterious.”

But neither can this opinion of Scaliger be accepted, at least not in its
entirety. For

> Marginalia, printed p. 175: *Another mystery in the sevenfold period*.

[Printed p. 176 | IA leaf 206 | access PDF 206 | OCR object 205]

although a mystery may perhaps not appear in every portion of the seventy, the
angel indicated a great mystery in some of its members. He did so particularly
in the final week, which he expressly separated from the remaining portions
because of the supremely notable event of the Messiah’s death. Whether any
such mystery is to be found in the first portion, the seven weeks, may be
investigated by the learned. The mystery that many have identified in it to
this point will certainly not be readily conceded.

Sanctius cites Jerome for the claim that a Hebrew idiom in numbers always
places the smaller sum before the greater, but this does not apply here. For
although seven is placed before sixty-two, one—the smallest of the sums—here
follows sixty-two. Moreover, where a smaller number is regularly placed before
a greater, this occurs in a definite order and proportion: units before tens,
tens before hundreds, and hundreds before thousands. This pattern applies
neither in this seventy nor in Ezekiel’s division of the shekel. Leaving it
aside, then, let us investigate the other reasons alleged for separating this
member.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Jerome’s false rule about numbers, cited from
> Sanctius*.

Most of those who begin the weeks with Cyrus wish the first seven to close at
the completion of the temple. We readily excuse this gross chronological
error in men of the highest stature—John Calvin, Theodore Beza,
Martin Chemnitz, and Robert Rollock. They reflected on greater and better
matters and were less exact in chronology; moreover, they did not have at hand
many of the excellent aids now available for chronological study.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *The chronological errors of earlier theologians
> should be excused*.

In his commentary on John 2:20 Calvin thinks that the temple’s forty-six years
of construction should be referred to Daniel’s seven weeks, as if the temple
had been built in the latter half of the seventh week, three years before its
end. In his commentary on the same passage, Beza calculates these years by
assigning thirty to Cyrus, eight to Cambyses, two to the Magi, and six to
Darius Hystaspes, but with manifest anachronisms. For the building of the
temple and the release of the Jews from captivity are customarily reckoned not
from Cyrus’s Persian reign, in which thirty years are indeed counted, but from
his rule in Babylon, where he reigned no more than nine. When Beza weighed
this, being a man as admirable for candor as for learning, he immediately
acknowledged his error, professed ἐπέχειν, “that he was suspending judgment,”
and awaited greater light from the labors of chronologists.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Calvin’s error*.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Beza’s*.

The most learned Chemnitz did not display as much modesty here. He persisted
in Beza’s very calculation without any retraction, placing the temple’s
foundations in Cyrus’s first year and assigning Cyrus thirty years, Cambyses
eight, and the Magi two.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Chemnitz’s*.

The deviation of the very saintly Rollock is greater still. He assigns Cyrus
and Cambyses only nine years, the Magi one, and Darius Hystaspes twenty—that
is, the fables of Annius, by which many learned men were deceived for some
time. After
Darius Hystaspes he supposes that a certain Darius Longimanus reigned, though
no such man ever existed, and says that in his sixth year the temple was
completed. But since the ten years of Cyrus, Cambyses, and the Magi, the twenty
of Darius Hystaspes, and the six of Darius Longimanus amount to only
thirty-six, this learned man adds another invention: he distinguishes the
completion of the house from the completion of additions to it. He wishes the
former to have been completed in Darius Longimanus’s sixth year, the
thirty-sixth from Cyrus’s first, and the latter not before the nineteenth and
final year of Darius Longimanus, the forty-ninth from Cyrus’s first. But even
the bleary-eyed have long since seen through these ravings of Annius.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Rollock’s*.

In his harmony on the passage before us, the most learned Jansenius errs as
widely as anyone. To complete the temple’s forty-six years of construction,
he gives Cyrus twenty years and his successor Ahasuerus twenty, and makes the
third king of Persia Darius Longimanus, in whose sixth year he says the temple
was completed. Cartwright is more cautious and passes over this question
entirely untouched.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Jansenius’s*.

We readily excuse these things in learned men who in their time had less
experience in chronology. We do not bear equally well, however, the
carelessness of Howe and Nisbet, who profess exactness in chronology. They
begin Daniel’s weeks with Cyrus’s first year, end the completion of the temple
in the sixth year of Darius Nothus, and close Daniel’s seven weeks at the
temple’s finishing point. From these three serious errors they deduce a fourth
and supremely erroneous θεώρημα, “proposition”: that only forty-nine years
elapsed from Cyrus’s first year to the sixth of Darius Nothus, contrary to the
express testimony of all historians, as they themselves admit.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Howe’s and Nisbet’s*.

Gualterus avoids all these absurdities. He begins the weeks with Artaxerxes’s
seventh year, excludes the temple’s construction entirely from the weeks, and
assigns only twenty-three years to completing the second temple. As for the
Jews’ forty-six years in John 2, together with Calvin and others he not
unreasonably applies them, on Josephus’s authority, to the temple built by
Herod forty-six years earlier. For after the old house of Zerubbabel was for
the most part demolished, Herod erected a new one with immense labor and
expense over eight consecutive years; during the following thirty-eight years
some new ornament was continually added to it.

> Marginalia, printed p. 176: *Gualterus is correct*.

Above we discussed L’Empereur’s new and highly irrational paradox, by which he
counts the seven weeks from Cyrus’s first year to the sixth of Darius Nothus.

[Printed p. 177 | IA leaf 207 | access PDF 207 | OCR object 206]

We no more approve the views of Lyranus the glossator, Paul of Burgos the
supplementer, and the Anonymous replier. They agree closely here in placing
the beginning of the seven weeks in Zedekiah’s fifth year and their end at the
beginning of Cyrus’s reign. These are foul anachronisms, while we consider no
less unsuitable their allegories, by which they make Nebuchadnezzar a type of
Christ and wish these seven weeks to be separated from the rest because of
Nebuchadnezzar’s various relations to Christ.

> Marginalia, printed p. 177: *Lyranus’s deviation*.

What, then? Once we have removed the preceding explanations, can no other
reason be given why these seven weeks were divided from the rest? I say there
would be nothing objectionable if, with Scaliger in his latest φροντίσι,
“thoughts,” we were to say that no reason can be found—or at least that none
is known to us.

Next, we do not deny that the city’s construction may be assigned to these
seven weeks, provided it is not said that the weeks end with that work. Daniel
9:25 indeed attaches the city’s construction to both portions, the seven weeks
and the 72 weeks, so nothing prevents that action from being assigned to the
first member, the seven weeks. I would only deny that the sevenfold period
closes with that action. From Artaxerxes’s seventh year, where we said that all
the weeks, and so also the seven before us, begin, until Artaxerxes’s thirtieth
or thirty-second year, when all Nehemiah’s work in Judea was finished, only
twenty-five years elapsed. These make only three and a half weeks of years,
only half of seven weeks. Once it is granted, therefore, that the seven weeks
do not close with Nehemiah’s work, we very readily concede that the city’s
construction falls within the limits of the sevenfold period. Because this
notable work was the one thing Daniel sought in his prayers, these seven weeks
could have been separated from the others on its account. As for why a smaller
portion of years, one more closely corresponding to the work of construction,
was not adopted, perhaps this was one reason among others—and why should we
not also have liberty to offer our own conjecture? The prophet was dealing in
weeks and sevenfold periods, namely the captivity’s seventy years and seventy
weeks of years, and so wished to place the first portion of that seventy in the
number seven and no other.
