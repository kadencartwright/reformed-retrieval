### Chapter XI

#### First argument

Page 105.

No other cause of God’s eternal election can be established than that which is
expressed by the Spirit of God and in sacred Scripture. But Scripture
establishes no other cause of election than God’s purpose, the counsel of his
will, his good pleasure, and sheer grace (Rom. 8:28; 9:11; 2 Tim. 2:9; Eph.
1:9; 2:5; Matt. 11:26; Luke 12:32; Rom. 9:15; Exod. 33:19; 16:18; Rom.
11:5). Consequently, the teaching that foreseen faith is in any way the cause
of election conflicts with the Word of God. But if it is not the cause of
election, neither is it a condition which is required for election or which
God considers in his counsel of election. For this phrase is not found in
Scripture: that God elected us as believers and on the condition that we
believe in him and persevere in the obedience of faith.

The Remonstrants answered this argument in such a way that they bring forward
scarcely anything that either calls for or deserves a serious refutation. Yet
they must be heard briefly. After rejecting the whole argument because of its
mention of a cause—which, as we saw above, was not posited rashly but is
manifestly contained in their position—they proceed to identify errors, which
they propose in the following order.

1. They think grace and faith are not rightly opposed. This is as though God’s
internal and eternal favor were not something diverse or disparate from the
act or gift of faith, which inheres in mortals and, at least at its beginning,
is also mortal. They cannot fail to see that either these must be called one
and the same thing, or else this argument must be lawful and firm: “This alone
is the cause; therefore that is not.” For the word “alone”

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 70 | printed p. 58]

has the force of excluding and removing everything that is not plainly the
same: by including one thing, it excludes all the rest. It is certainly quite
consistent that something should be given from sheer grace and received
through faith, which is the Apostle’s meaning in Romans 4:1. But it is in no
way consistent that something should be appointed from sheer and sole grace,
yet have another ground and cause why it is appointed.

2. They say that besides God’s good pleasure Scripture establishes another
cause of election on account of which we were elected, namely Christ,
according to Ephesians 1:4: “He elected us in Christ.” But 1. to elect in
Christ and to elect on account of Christ must not be confused. Levi was
tithed in Abraham (Heb. 7:9), but not on account of Abraham as an impelling
cause. In Christ God prepared for us redemption from sins, but he did not
prepare it on account of Christ. For then he would be said to have given
Christ himself on account of Christ. 2. It is plain from the context itself
that with respect to the terminus of election, or the goods appointed for the
elect, Christ has the character of something antecedent and of a cause; but
with respect to the act itself of the divine intention, he instead has the
character of something consequent and of an effect. Thus in verse 3 Paul
affirms that all blessings are communicated to us in Christ. In verse 5,
Adoption is manifestly joined with the words “through Jesus Christ,” not the
act of the one predestining. Nor will the word used there—“through”—permit
this, for it denotes a means dependent upon the appointment of the end and
sufficiently explains that other phrase, “in Christ.” 3. The Remonstrants
themselves appear to explain this part of the statement—“in Christ”—so that it
pertains rather to the condition of the elect than to the cause of electing
(p. 91): “No one is elected in Christ unless he is in Christ, that is, a
believer.” They make electing in Christ and electing believers the same thing.
They therefore do not appear to be sufficiently consistent with themselves
when they say faith is not a cause, yet contend that a cause is denoted by
these words. Certainly the preposition “in” cannot

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 71 | printed p. 59]

denote both the one electing and the one elected at the same time.

4. They say that God established a certain general election by which he would
save believers and condemn unbelievers. Christ is not the cause of this
decree, because, after redemption through Christ had already been entirely
accomplished, “God retained this right unimpaired, at his own discretion, to
apply it or not to apply it and to communicate it to whomever, or to people
of whatever sort, or under whatever condition he wished” (says Nicolaus
Grevinchovius, p. 8). Therefore, when the divine will prescribed the quality
of faith, it was not moved by Christ as a cause. But if Christ is not the
proper cause of this general decree, then neither is he the cause of that
other decree by which God determined to save particular and definite
believers. For this decree is nothing other than a consequence of the other,
or a conclusion deduced from it, in this way: “I will to save all believers;
these are believers; therefore I will to save these people.” Here no cause of
the conclusion is given besides that general decree which is placed in the
major premise and the actual faith which supplies the minor premise.
Therefore, either this particular election has no cause except the cause that
also belongs to the general election—which Nicolaus Grevinchovius
acknowledges (p. 105) as resting immediately upon God’s will and discretion as
its first, sole, and complete cause—or else it must necessarily be said that
faith is its cause. They have nowhere given Christ the place of a cause, but
only the name.

3. “Faith,” they say, “can be a condition although it is not a cause.” They
would indeed say this truly if by the name “condition” they meant only that
without which a thing cannot exist. In this way many natural things have a
conditional necessity for supernatural life—for example, that we exist,
live, are sustained, and partake of reason, and so forth. But there is another
account of a condition that is prescribed, required, and foreknown, such as
they establish faith to be. Nicolaus Grevinchovius has demonstrated this very
well on page 24 in the words already quoted: “I further contend that it is
altogether in keeping with the nature of laws and prescribed conditions

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 72 | printed p. 60]

that the will of a judge should be moved to the reward by the condition
required and fulfilled.”

They admit that this phrase is not found in Scripture: “God elected us as
believers and on the condition that we believe in him.” But, they say, it no
more follows from this that the thing itself is not true than it follows that
the contrary defense is also false, because Scripture does not say that faith
is the fruit of election, that we were elected without account being taken of
faith, or without account being taken of any quality. But 1. there is a
different and unequal account of these phrases and those. It would be most
unfair to demand an express testimony of Scripture which excludes by name
every single thing that must necessarily be removed from the decree of
election, or which describes all the corollaries that may be deduced from it.
For the art and doctrine of any subject ought to be explained neither by
negative precepts nor by superfluous ones. But in an affirmation in which the
very primary condition of so great a matter is handed down as prescribed and
required, who would not expect an explicit law? 2. Scripture says openly,
“when they had done neither good nor evil”—that is, when they were furnished
with no discriminating quality (Rom. 9:12); that we are elected to salvation
through faith exercised toward the truth (2 Thess. 2:13); and that some were
previously designated to know God’s will (Acts 22:14).

### Chapter XII

#### Second argument

Page 111.

The fruit of election cannot be its cause, or a condition which precedes it.
Faith, together with obedience and perseverance, is the fruit of election
(Rom. 8:29; Eph. 1:4–5, compared with John 1:12; Eph. 2:10; Acts 13:38; John
6:37; 8:47; 10:16).

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 73 | printed p. 61]

The minor premise is confidently denied by the Remonstrants, and they will not
grant that it is confirmed by even one of the testimonies brought forward to
support it. They offer various reasons, which must be weighed briefly.

1. They say that in Romans 8:29–30 the Apostle does not treat any absolute
appointment to salvation or calling to faith, but the appointment and calling
of believers to the cross and afflictions. It was sufficiently proved above,
in the first part under argument 4, that this gloss is incongruous; yet one
covering must be stripped from it here. They say, “We assert with Dr. Calvin,”
and so forth, as though they followed Calvin as the author of their assertion,
and not Socinus, when they deny that the Apostle here treats any absolute
election to salvation or calling to faith dependent upon it. The reader must
not think this. For although they do not depart very far from Calvin in their
explanation of the latter part of verse 29, nevertheless in all the other
members of this chain they have him as an opponent no less vigorous here than
in the very book which he wrote on Predestination. On verse 28 he observes
that “our salvation is founded upon election, and its causes are to be sought
nowhere else than in God’s secret good pleasure.” On verse 29 he seems to
refute these people as follows: “The foreknowledge of God which Paul mentions
here is not bare prescience, as certain ignorant people foolishly imagine, but
the adoption by which he always distinguished his sons from the reprobate.”
He proceeds in exactly the same way in the remaining members as well.

2. They complain bitterly about certain words which were inserted
offensively—namely “obedience” and “perseverance”—as though the Remonstrants
taught that God had regard to good works in election. They solemnly profess
that they neither teach nor think this. From this single pretense, therefore,
the reader may learn everything: they swear by heaven that this is not their
meaning and cry out that false things are imputed to them, even though the
truth of this position of theirs has been established by such open evidence
in their formulation of the question, where by sealed documents, as it were,
it is proved that complete obedience according to what is prescribed,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 74 | printed p. 62]

love, worship, honor, fear, humility, the whole contest, consummate obedience,
and good works are placed by them among the prerequisites of election no less
than faith. Nevertheless, lest these things appear to have escaped them
suddenly and unexpectedly, but not otherwise to be contained in their
position or found in their writings, it will be worthwhile to hear what Simon
Episcopius, professor at Leiden, instills into the university youth in his
private theses. He establishes two parts of the new covenant: “1. The
stipulation of our duty, to be ordered according to certain precepts. 2. God’s
most gracious promise and pledge, made to those who fulfill their duty
according to the stipulation of the covenant.” He teaches that this promise,
which they say contains justification and the recompense of the reward—that
is, eternal life—and therefore election itself (“for these decrees of election
and justification are uniform and conformable,” Nicolaus, p. 111), follows in
order upon that stipulation of duty. This is in Disputation 45, theses 1–2.
Now the precepts which he makes antecedent to the spiritual promises are
three, which he calls by the novel expression “precepts of the new covenant”:
Repentance, Faith in Christ, and Obedience strictly taken, or Charity, or
Holiness, “which is nothing other than the sincere and continuous performance,
up to the final limit of life, of the commandments prescribed by the gospel
law. By prescribed commandments he means all those things which are either
pronounced generally by Christ and throughout the Scriptures of the New
Testament”—concerning self-denial, bearing the cross, and so forth—“or are
explained specifically according to the precepts of the divine moral law and
more fully elucidated.” These statements are in Disputations 23, 25, and 27.

From these it is perfectly clear that the Remonstrants’ domestic position,
whatever they sometimes display in public, is precisely the position which
they now so eagerly try to remove from themselves: namely, that obedience, or
the performance of good works, and perseverance in it

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 75 | printed p. 63]

are regarded by God and occupy the place of a condition in the act of
Justification and Election no less than faith itself in Christ. Indeed, to
someone who looks more deeply into the matter itself it will perhaps appear
that through this doctrine much more is granted to those other duties than to
faith. For when they describe election to us as “the will to confer a prize or
reward, with the prescribed and required condition intervening, just as
judges of games award the prize to athletes through the intervening race or
contest,” it is fair that most should be ascribed to the part of the condition
which involves the greatest effort and contest, or in which the more numerous
or more important duties are contained. Yet no one can fail to know that much
more is required and performed in the precepts of universal obedience than in
the prescription of faith. Moreover, since they establish no difference
between the faith and obedience of those elected to salvation and that of
many who will be condemned, except duration alone, or perseverance—and since
the condition of election must nevertheless be placed in something which the
elect person possesses beyond every non-elect person—it appears entirely
fitting that the adequate ground of election should be placed in this
duration or perseverance rather than in faith itself. See Nicolaus
Grevinchovius, pp. 130, 152, 141.

3. The Remonstrants reply with three things concerning those words of the
Apostle in Ephesians 1:4: “He elected us in Christ before the foundation of
the world was laid, that we should be holy and blameless in his sight with
love.” 1. The Apostle speaks about believers, they say, which he indicates
when he says “us,” whom he had previously called blessed and faithful. A
truly acute argument! The Apostle says that we who are now believers were
elected before the foundations of the world were laid; therefore they were
believers before they were elected, or election is therefore not the cause of
faith, but faith is a prerequisite of election. There are also places where
he says to the same and similar people, “He called us, redeemed us,
illuminated us with knowledge of the gospel, and gave us faith.” Will they
conclude from this that faith also precedes

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 76 | printed p. 64]

calling, knowledge, redemption, and even itself, so that they were believers
before they were endowed with faith? 2. They say that faith is not placed here
as the effect of election, but holiness alone, which is a thing distinct from
faith. Although it does not exist without faith, it arises from faith as fruit
from a tree. 1. I embrace in a sound sense what they say about a tree and its
fruit; indeed, I contend that it tells especially against them. For the same
root produces both the tree and its fruit; the nature of each is the same, and
the efficient cause is also the same. Therefore, if election is the radical
cause of holiness, faith also must necessarily arise from the same cause. He
who gives the form also gives the faculties which emanate from or follow that
form; and conversely, he who gives faculties of this kind also gives the form.
Faith therefore has the same cause as those qualities which flow from faith,
and both depend upon election if either one does. It is consequently enough
for the argument if faith and holiness are joined in an individual bond,
although they are not one and the same thing. 2. Nicolaus Grevinchovius admits
on page 119 that faith is comprehended under holiness when the latter word is
used generally for every act separated from common use and consecrated to
divine worship. They therefore do not act rightly when they simply oppose
this here, without a distinction. 3. Every act of the Holy Spirit dwelling in
us is part of our sanctification; for he is called holy because he puts a
sanctifying power into us. But faith is among his principal acts. Therefore,
and so forth. 4. The Remonstrants affirm that by faith in this question they
mean the consummate obedience of faith. I certainly do not know how they can
distinguish this consummate obedience from holiness. 5. This election to
holiness, and so forth, must extend as broadly as the blessing mentioned in
verse 3. The connecting particle *kathōs*—“just as”—plainly denotes this
proportion. Nor would the Apostle’s reasoning be firm unless this Election in
some way comprehended in itself all those blessings

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 77 | printed p. 65]

of which he had previously made mention. Yet it is not for a Christian to
deny that faith has some place among all spiritual blessings. Therefore faith
also is contained in the holiness which flows from election.

4. They say that their position is that “to be holy and blameless before God
is the same as to be glorified in heaven, as also in Ephesians 5:26–27 and
Colossians 1:2, 21–23. These passages,” they say, “cannot be understood except
of the future life. Accordingly, this election pertains to nothing in this
life, but to glory alone.” They say that this can also be confirmed from
Musculus and Piscator. But 1. I would like them to tell me whether this
election of God which is to holiness is to a part of it only, and not rather
to complete holiness. If they should say only a part and degree, then they
very ignorantly deny that the same thing depends upon the same cause. 2. They
also teach with the Scriptures that this election to holiness, life, and
heaven does not raise us from the condition of sin, death, and the world, but
from one degree of holiness, life, and the heavenly nature to another. Anyone
who has even saluted Scripture from its threshold knows how foreign this is
to its constant tenor. But if they grant that we are elected to complete
holiness, then either they will make no one a participant in holiness in this
life, contrary to the express teaching of Scripture in 1 Corinthians 6:11, or
they will be forced to refer this election also to the present life. 2. The
connection which sanctification has in the text with all spiritual blessings
teaches that it is taken here in the most general sense. So too does the fact
that it designates the whole end of election, which Paul himself teaches in
this very chapter must be extended to adoption and other things we customarily
receive here while we live. Since, therefore, in Scripture sanctification
often comprehends not only renewal but also justification, as in John 17:17;
Hebrews 9:14, 20, and 13, it seems especially fitting that absolution also

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 78 | printed p. 66]

should be understood as comprehended in this sanctification. The same must be
thought about the parallel passages in Ephesians 5:26 and Colossians 1:20,
since the whole fruit both of Christ’s love and of his death is placed in that
sanctification. Yet that fruit cannot be confined within the bounds of
heaven. Rather, through imputation he presents to himself as immaculate the
church betrothed to him, though it sojourns on earth and is militant, and also
purges it effectively by degrees. If these things are true—as they are most
true and approved by the best interpreters—then those who restrict so
far-reaching a benefit to heaven alone are completely mistaken, although we
are commanded to await its crowning completion in heaven through faith. 3.
As for their assertion beyond all doubt that this blameless holiness cannot
be understood except of the future condition of believers in heaven, the
contrary will be sufficiently evident if they inspect that excellent passage
in Philippians 2:15. There believers are called to “be blameless and
innocent, children of God without blemish in the midst of a crooked
generation,” and so forth—in the midst of the present generation and world.
Nothing clearer than this could have been brought forward by the light itself,
or anything more suited to this matter. They misuse Piscator’s name when they
say that he interprets Ephesians 5:26–27 in no other way than of the condition
of believers in heaven. For although he teaches that the benefit of
sanctification will not be consummated before the wedding of the Lamb to be
celebrated in heaven, nevertheless in his analysis he says in express words
that verse 26 speaks “of the purgation which belongs to remission,” and that
verse 27, and partly also verse 26, speaks “of that which occurs through the
efficacy of the Spirit in sanctifying or renewing the hearts of believers
more and more every day.” Nor are they fairer to Musculus when they draw him
into the same position. Although on Ephesians 5:26 he teaches that the
perfection of holiness will exist only in heaven, he nevertheless advises
that meanwhile “we are sanctified and cleansed by blood, the Spirit, the Word,
and Baptism, through faith in Christ.” He expounds this more fully on

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 79 | printed p. 67]

Ephesians 1:4, from which he also says his position should be sought, on
Ephesians 5:26 and Colossians 1:2, 6.

5. They answer the fifth verse in precisely the same way in which they twist
the fourth. The Apostle says that we were “predestined to the adoption of
sons,” which cannot be acquired without faith (John 1:12). Hence it is easy to
infer that we are led to faith also by the power of predestination. They
answer: 1. “God considers those who are to be adopted as believers, and as
such predestines them to adoption.” But this conflicts both with the
Scriptures and with right reason. For adoption is the lawful assumption of an
outsider into a family and the right of a son or daughter. Our adoption by God
makes us sons of God; but we were made such through faith. For faith is the
very foundation of this relation, or of adoption in act. The ground of
adoption is therefore included in the production of faith. Now that to which
something is predestined is outside the thing predestined: no one appoints a
thing to what is included in the account of the thing itself. A man is not
predestined to have a soul, nor a rational animal to be a man, for one who
says the one includes the other. Isaac, once he had been begotten by Abraham,
was not predestined to be his son; that very thing was contained in his
begetting. In precisely the same way, someone begotten by God through faith
cannot be predestined to become a son, for when you say he is regenerated
through faith you assume that he is a son. This is a most powerful argument
for demonstrating this whole question with the greatest clarity. For those
who are predestined to some relation and condition cannot fail also to be
appointed to its foundation and ground. When David was once appointed to
become Saul’s son-in-law (1 Sam. 18), he was at the same time appointed to
marry Saul’s daughter. When Saul, or Paul, was predestined to the apostolate
(Rom. 1; Gal. 1), was he not by the same act also appointed to that calling by
which he was actually set apart for that function?

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 80 | printed p. 68]

Thus those who are appointed to adoption are also appointed to association,
or enrollment, among the sons of God through faith. But if even a moment of
distance could be conceived between the production of faith and sonship or
adoption, so that a divine appointment might intervene between them,
believers nevertheless should not be said to be *predestined* to adoption,
because there would be no such antecedence of this act as the meaning of the
word signifies. It would not so much be that believers were adjudged to
adoption as that adoption was adjudged to believers. The Remonstrants object:
“Faith precedes adoption in order, as the testimony of John shows: ‘To those
who believe in Christ’s name he gave the right to become sons of God’” (1:12).
If they mean the kind of order that exists between generation and sonship,
between calling and a function, or between any act and the relation founded
upon it—an order of reason only—then they say nothing to the point. But if,
with certain Papists, they interpret that right of sonship not as a present
dignity accompanying faith itself but as some absent and future power, then
they say nothing relevant to the passage and can readily be refuted from its
very words. For the word used here by the Apostle, *genesthai*, is not in the
future tense but in the past aorist, however incapable Latin may be of
expressing so exact a distinction. Dr. Beza therefore explains it rightly and
truly with the sense “that they were made sons of God,” not “that they should
become” so in the future. Although this interpretation of the word is clear
in itself, it is confirmed by the immediately following words, “those who
were begotten of God.” These sufficiently show that no greater distance is
given between faith and that dignity than between begetting and its
inseparable honor. It is also confirmed most clearly from those homogeneous
expressions of the same Apostle in his first epistle, chapter 3, verses 1–2:
“See what love”—that is, what dignity from love—“the Father has given us, that
we should be called sons of God. Now we are sons of God”—that is, we have
been begotten of God. Here he openly places our dignity

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 81 | printed p. 69]

in the fact that we are called sons of God, and he makes being called and
being the same thing. The following words teach that all these things follow
true faith immediately: “Whoever has this hope.” The Remonstrants are
therefore completely disappointed in their hope when they sought support for
themselves from the very passages most opposed to them. But they are to be
pitied for again taking refuge in that little word “us,” as though they wished
to extort something from unwilling letters and syllables. “He predestined
us,” they say, “therefore believers.” I showed sufficiently above how helpless
and impoverished this argument is.

6. With the first chapter of Ephesians now vindicated to some extent, the
second follows, where these words occur in verse 10: “We are God’s workmanship,
created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared that we should walk
in them.” Here we are taught that all good works, or the whole obedience of
faith, and therefore faith itself, comes from God’s antecedent counsel and
intention for our salvation. The Remonstrants answer: 1. “The discussion here
is not about election, which is from eternity, but about the election which
occurs in regeneration.” Let it be so. Yet if this passage proves a certain
special and prevenient grace toward certain particular people beyond others,
in calling and regenerating them and fitting them to live well and blessedly,
then no one who acknowledges God to be eternal will doubt that this grace was
appointed from eternity through election. They answer: 2. “This preparation
is not of human beings but, as the text says, of good works; it is done by
prescribing, commanding, exhorting, and urging those works.” But 1. this
preparation pertains altogether to people. For in Greek the text does not say
“which he prepared,” but “for which.” The immediate object of this preparation
is therefore the people themselves, who are prepared for those works. The
Seventy interpreters frequently use this kind of expression, as in Proverbs
23:12; 2 Chronicles 4:26, 14; Genesis 24:14; 44:2. If the term “prepared” is
not directly applied to people, there are nevertheless other words no less

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 82 | printed p. 70]

powerful which are referred to people by manifest syntax: “We are his
workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works.” 3. When creating people
for works and preparing works for people are joined together, they
sufficiently indicate that this preparation is attributed to the works in
such a way that it is by no means denied to the people. 4. They think without
any example or even reason that this preparation consists in prescribing and
proposing. Apart from this passage, the verb *proetoimazō* occurs only once
(so far as I know) in the whole New Testament, namely in Romans 9:23, where it
is openly referred to the appointment which precedes calling. For this follows
in the context: “vessels which he prepared for glory, whom he also called.”
This preparation therefore precedes every prescription and proposal. In
addition, the force of the verb itself must not be neglected:
*proētoimase*—“he prepared beforehand.” It indicates the long antecedence of
this divine act before good works and the calling to them. Then the Apostle’s
purpose will not permit us to understand prescription and proposal by
“preparation.” For Paul infers this in order to take the praise of our
righteousness away from works and ascribe it to grace alone. But prescribing
and proposing good works, and so forth, does absolutely nothing either to
humble works or to exalt grace, for under the law good works were prepared by
prescribing, proposing, commanding, exhorting, and urging them. They answer:
3. “If these words were understood of a preparation which occurs by election,
it would nevertheless pertain to believers, because the Apostle says ‘us.’”
Now for the third time they have thrust this feeble argument upon the reader,
whom they seem to have promised themselves would be very foolish. For what
sensible person can they induce to believe that people were believers before
they were created by God, could be called his workmanship, or could in any way
be prepared for any good work? Yet they command us to speak nonsense in this
way, who harass us so shamelessly and press us importunately in this passage,
where our

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 83 | printed p. 71]

workmanship or first creation, which belongs to grace, is urged by the Apostle
to this end: to demonstrate that nothing good could have preceded it.

Concerning that excellent testimony in Acts 13:48—“As many as were appointed
to eternal life believed”—they raise the same cavils as before. “What is
assumed concerning Predestination is not present,” they say, “not verbally,
because they are not said to have been appointed by God or foreordained, but
only appointed; nor in meaning, because a certain internal disposition must
be understood by ‘appointment,’ as is manifestly apparent from the opposite
member of the comparison in verse 46: ‘You judge yourselves unworthy of
eternal life.’” This preposterous distortion of the passage was already
examined under argument 4 of the first part. Let the studious reader look back
there and at the same time compare the passages from Socinus noted there with
these words, which he will see as though taken from Socinus’s own mouth. Now,
as a supplement, I shall weigh briefly those parts of this argumentation to
which they reveal that they attribute the most and in which they place the
greatest confidence.

1. The Remonstrants themselves can neither be ignorant nor even deny that
“known” and “foreknown,” “determined” and “predetermined,” “appointed” and
“predestined” are often found in the same sense. Indeed, later they themselves
infer prescience, without any supporting reason, from the simple word “knows”
in the Apostle’s words at 2 Timothy 2:19. 2. The verb itself has an added
signification more than perfect, which has precisely the same value as if they
had been called “foreordained.” 3. Whatever kind of appointment this was, it
is so clear that it had people as its object and God as its author that those
very people who introduced this scruple—“they are not said to have been
appointed by God”—admit with the same spirit, as it were, that it is something
gratuitous, effected through God’s Word and Spirit. This is the same as if
they said that they were appointed by God. 4. The comparison of verse 48 with
verse 46 is rejected with the same ease with which it is affirmed. For 1. the
proximate subject whose limits are determined by the particle

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 84 | printed p. 72]

*hosoi*, “as many as,” is described by Luke in the words “But when the Gentiles
heard these things.” The comparison therefore seems to be instituted properly
not between the Jews and the Gentiles, but between one part of the Gentiles
and the other. 2. If these descriptions looked mutually to each other—“they
judged themselves unworthy of eternal life,” and “they had been appointed to
eternal life”—nothing nevertheless prevents human desert on the one side and
divine mercy on the other from being compared with each other. Thus Moses
once said in Deuteronomy 9:5: “Not because of your righteousness or because of
your upright heart are you entering,” and so forth, “but because of the
wickedness of those nations Jehovah your God expels them from before you, and
that he may perform the word which he promised.” 3. The very word Paul uses
there, “you judge,” very readily permits that opposition with the decree or
divine appointment, but it is not sufficiently suited to an internal and
inherent quality.

7. Three testimonies taken from John remain. Chapter 6, verse 37: “Whatever
the Father gives me will come to me”; chapter 10, verse 20: “But you do not
believe, for you are not of my sheep”; and John 8:47: “You do not hear the
words of God because you are not of God.” To the first they reply by referring
the reader to what they said above. But nothing is found there which deserves
to be read twice, for once read, it betrays its weakness by its own evidence.
They determine nothing about the sense of the passage, but only skeptically
suppose, “If this, if that.” They do not dare establish what Christ means
there, because they know no ground of interpretation and cannot even devise
an honorable one. It is certain from Christ’s own interpretation in verse 35
that “coming to Christ” means nothing other than believing in him: “He who
comes to me shall by no means hunger, and he who believes in me shall never
thirst.” It is also certain that this donation of the Father is some act of
God antecedent to this faith, from which faith will nevertheless necessarily
follow at last. The words themselves proclaim it: “Whatever the Father has
given me will come to me.”

Hence it is manifest that this passage, which they deny, pertains to the very
heart of the matter. The second passage is John 7:47: “You do not hear because
you are not of God.” They oppose two things. 1. “If to be of God is the same
as to be elected by God, then to be of the Devil in verse 44 would be the same
as to be elected by the Devil, for the phrase is the same.” The first author
of this argument was Faustus Socinus, as can be seen in his disputation *On
Christ the Savior*, part 4, chapter 12. But the Remonstrants could have
observed from other passages of Scripture that it is invalid. For the phrase
is also the same in John 6: to eat bodily bread, to eat manna, and to eat
Christ. Yet only the last of these, “to eat,” is the same as to Believe, not
the others. Thus to be born of God signifies to be regenerated through the
inspiration of new life; but to be born of the Devil or to be born of the
world does not signify this. The same ground of distinction exists between
being of God and being of the Devil. The former appears to contain four
things: a certain origin from God, a divine generative power, divine
possession, and also imitation of God. To these four things correspond to
some extent in the latter: seduction by the Devil from the beginning,
effective hardening, possession, and imitation. This proportion contains a
sufficiently just cause for using the same phrase. But it is most unfair and
unworthy of Christians to propose that the ground of origin is altogether the
same on each side, so that those who are of God are not so through election
unless these others also exist in the same way from the Devil. 2. “But Beza,”
they say, “explains this passage differently, asserting that its sense is:
‘You are not of God because you do not hear God’s Word.’” Yet this is not to
explain the passage differently with respect to the position itself, but to
change the sequence of the words and turn the order or form of the argument
into another kind. Others affirm that Christ argued from cause to effect;
Beza prefers from effect to cause. With respect to the things themselves—the
effect, namely, and the cause—

Concerning the third passage, John 10:26, “You do not believe because you are
not of my sheep,” the Remonstrants reply with three things. First, “To be a
sheep of Christ is not altogether the same as to be elected to eternal life,
because in Matthew 10:6 and 15:24 Christ calls the house of Israel lost sheep,
among whom there were many who were not elect.” But by precisely the same
reasoning they could prove that to be elect is not altogether the same as to
be elect, to be holy as to be holy, to be justified as to be justified, or
sons of God as God’s sons. For whole assemblies are customarily adorned in
Scripture with all these titles, even though many in them were not such.
Indeed, all these things are often attributed to this same people Israel—not
to say more often than the name “sheep.” Second, they say that from our
interpretation “unbelief is the fruit of Reprobation.” It follows that faith
is the fruit of election, and that obstinate unbelief is a consequence and
sign of reprobation, or of election denied, but not its proper fruit or
effect. Third, they affirm that the genuine sense is that “they did not
believe because they did not acknowledge Christ as Shepherd and did not wish
to hear his voice,” and so forth. But they ought first to show this sense
before commending it as genuine. To “believe” in this passage is exactly the
same as to “hear” in the former one. This interpretation therefore amounts to
Christ saying, as it were, “You do not wish to hear because you do not wish to
hear.” Thus far concerning the second argument.
