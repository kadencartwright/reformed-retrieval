18. Objection 15. I now proceed to the remaining objections. Vasquez and
Molina have a few things which are not in Suárez and which we have not
examined. Thus Vasquez: “Although the decree changes and necessitates the will
extrinsically, not intrinsically and physically, freedom is nevertheless
removed, because on the supposition of the decree it cannot fail to act. The
act, therefore, is not possible; for, as the Philosopher has it, that is
possible upon the positing of which nothing absurd follows.” Reply. The Jesuit
does not correctly explain that axiom. For that is possible upon the actual
positing of which, dependently upon and subordinately to the motion of the
first cause, nothing absurd follows. 2. An absurdity does follow if the act is
removed from a freedom determined to acting by God's decree, because God's
decree places freedom in that vital condition in which it will freely do what
has been determined by the decree. Therefore, if it did not do this, freedom
would be impaired, which is absurd.

19. Objection 16. For the same reason for which the Stoics' fate is rejected,
the absolute decree also ought to be rejected. But antiquity rejected that
fate because by it people can do no more good or more evil, nor omit less,
than they do and omit. Yet this also follows from the absolute decree.
Therefore, etc. So Molina, Lessius, and the Remonstrants. Reply. 1. I deny the
major. For the Stoics' fate is rejected because by it rational creatures
cannot do more or less good or evil, on the ground that a constellation or
created thing determines freedom and acts upon it—something no creature can

folio 14.]

[Marginalia: Lessius, *On the Efficacy of Grace*, chapter 5; Remonstrants, in
the synodical writings, article 4, pp. 249–250.]

[1651 logical printed p. 157 | IA leaf 175 | PDF p. 175]

accomplish while freedom remains unimpaired. For Jehovah alone bends the will
wherever he wills. Proverbs 21. We indeed grant that freedom would be removed
if a created cause bent the will in this way. But God can determine the will
without overturning it. 2. Fate is rejected because the Stoics bound God
himself, the ruler of free acts, to fate. Hence Homer's Jupiter mourns the
slaying of his son Sarpedon, who died by fate, and Neptune mourns because he
cannot prevent Ulysses' return to his homeland contrary to the laws of fate.
As for Grevinchovius's trifling claim that advocates of fate, such as
Cleanthes, entreated Jove with prayers just as we entreat Jehovah, it is
nothing against us. For they declared that they prayed by fate and that Jove
heard by fate. Thus Plutarch, in his work on fate, acknowledges a divine
ordering, but a fated one; and under that name the Stoics are deservedly
beaten by the fathers, Augustine and others. 3. It is perfectly certain that
the Jesuits and Arminians bind God to fate. “The order of prior and posterior
among the decrees of the divine will,” says Vasquez, “arises not from God's
good pleasure alone”—behold the most fatal fate—“but from the varying order of
the things which are predestined by God.” In the same place: just as “God does
not concur in heating before the fire cooperates, so he does not predefine
that he will concur before he sees the fire's determination.” But, to point
this out in passing, Vasquez himself here tears down his own concomitant
decree, since he wishes the determination of the second cause to be known by
God earlier, and consequently to exist earlier, than God's predefinition of
anything. On this account God cannot determine the order,

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, pp. 64, 65.]

[Marginalia: Vasquez, vol. 1, question 99, chapter 8, folio 51; in the same
place, folio 5.]

[1651 logical printed p. 158 | IA leaf 176 | PDF p. 176]

position, arrangement, intensity, remission, or manner of operation in second
causes, nor the being of contingent effects, otherwise than as these are
predetermined by the second causes themselves antecedently to God's will. He
can no more do so than he can bring it about that what already is should not
be—which is simply impossible and entangles God in fate.

20. Objection 17. If all things are fixed by this decree, then it is necessary
that the same people should attain the same glory in Christ through the
remission of the same sins; it would be necessary that they should be born to
the same people—sacrilegious persons, adulterers, and so forth—be fed on the
same food, milk, bread, or herbs, and perform the same meritorious works, and
so forth. Thus Lessius. Reply. This childish argument proves that all things
occur by hypothetical necessity, which we grant. If God had willed to appoint
the contrary of all these things, why could he not have appointed it?

21. Objection 18. If free acts are necessary in relation to God's decree,
then in relation to the decree they are not contingent, because in that case
they would be necessary and not necessary in relation to the same thing and
in the same respect. Thus contingent acts also are not contingent to God,
which is absurd. For God decreed both beings and the modes of beings. This is
gathered from Fonseca. And “a person does not rightly sin or obey,” say the
Remonstrants, “by external or internal operation, or by cessation from
operation, if it is necessary for him to sin or obey.”

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Commentary on the Metaphysics*, book 6, chapter 2,
question 4, section 6.]

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, chapter 6, folio 78.]

[1651 logical printed p. 159 | IA leaf 177 | PDF p. 177]

Reply. 1. Here the adversaries deceive. For necessity is not properly from
the decree, but from the positing of the decree; nor is it properly an
internal necessity, of the kind that belongs to things, but something common
to the necessary and the contingent. For by the efficacy of the divine will
the futurition of all beings, both necessary and contingent, is established.
Therefore, just as natural effects cannot fail to exist by internal necessity
in relation to natural causes, and yet can exist or not exist by an external
necessity in relation to the divine decree—in relation to which all things
otherwise necessary in the highest degree, such as the rising of the sun, an
eclipse, and the motion of the heavenly bodies, can exist or not exist—so by
the same right contingent effects, possessing internal contingency in
relation to second causes, can exist or not exist, while by external
necessity in relation to God's will they cannot fail to exist. Hence the
statement that contingent things are contingent to God is understood in two
ways. 1. As though, notwithstanding the positing of the decree, they could
absolutely exist or not exist. This must not be said, for when God decrees
they cannot fail to exist. 2. They are contingent in relation to the decree
in this sense: the decree establishes in them a contingent, and thus a free,
futurition in relation to the natural mode of acting in the second cause. And
what prevents God from considering contingent effects both in relation to his
decree and in relation to the second causes? For the statement that all
things happen necessarily in relation to God's

[1651 logical printed p. 160 | IA leaf 178 | PDF p. 178]

decree is also understood in three ways. 1. In such a way that necessity is
combined with the things willed by God. We do not teach this, for God decreed
that contingent effects should occur contingently, not necessarily; thus he
decreed both beings and the modes of beings. 2. In such a way that the word
“necessity” is combined with God's will. Nor in this way did God necessarily,
but most freely, decree that all things should occur, whether contingently or
necessarily. Or 3. in such a way that necessity is referred to the futurition
of contingent beings. In this sense contingent things are necessary to God in
relation to his decree, and it by no means follows: Therefore they are not
contingent to God. 3. Contrary to what the Remonstrants think, it matters a
great deal whether the necessity is external or internal. For because the
necessity is external and is from almighty God, whose prerogative it is by his
infinite wisdom to determine freedom while its natural indifference remains
unimpaired, that necessity does not remove native freedom.

22. Objection 19. Under divine determination the will acts either necessarily
or not at all. Reply. It acts both necessarily and freely; but that necessity
harmonizes with freedom, as has been said.

23. Objection 20. If all things are decreed absolutely, then when someone asks
why you perform ten works today, neither more nor fewer, the answer must be:
because God decreed it. The decree would be the cause of all things, and an
astonishing providence would be required for so many acts. Reply. If he denies
that God's decree and will are the cause of all things, he contradicts
Scripture:

[Marginalia: Fonseca, in the same place.]

[Marginalia: Lessius, *On Predestination*, section 2, chapter 7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 161 | IA leaf 179 | PDF p. 179]

Romans 11:36; Revelation 4:11; Matthew 10:29. But because it is the first and
universal cause of all things, in which the mind does not rest, would all
questions be answered badly by means of it? 2. We adore that astonishing
providence whose ways are unsearchable. Romans 12. Let the Jesuit laugh if he
wishes.

24. Objection 21. If God absolutely determined all things precisely to one
side, then God's freedom to arrange things otherwise than they are arranged
perishes. Thus Vorstius. “God establishes no statute,” says Jackson, “which
opposes the power of his absolute and eternal dominion.” And “an excessively
strict obligation to positive laws, or immutable decrees,” he says, “deprives
both legislators and teachers of their natural freedom and often of the
opportunity to act.” Reply. 1. In a crude and wholly anthropomorphic fashion
(a madness toward which the wild and uncultivated brain of this innovator
always inclines), he measures God's freedom as though, by determining his own
will, God poured out and exhausted his freedom. But tell me, innovator: after
the decree to create the world, redeem the human race, and raise the Son of
God from the dead—decrees which have now been fulfilled—has God lost anything
of his absolute freedom? Then God is less free now than before the world was
made. What? Speak, I beg you: the more free acts a person exercises, is he
less free? You will not say so, unless by writing such heterodox things you
have declared yourself to have lost the freedom to write sounder things,
which I certainly judge to be perfectly true. 2. Through absolute decrees

[Marginalia: Vorstius, *On God*, on disputation 6, p. 368.]

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Divine Essence*, chapter 10, section 1; in the
same work, p. 123, at section 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 162 | IA leaf 180 | PDF p. 180]

God loses no freedom, because no new judgment or new knowledge arises in God
by which he begins today to know certain things which he did not know from
eternity. If that were the case, it would have been necessary for him either
to change those things unadvisedly or never to have made them. 3. He thinks it
would be far better for God if his decrees were alterable, so that he might
thereby retain the freedom to act well. According to Jackson, therefore, God
loses the opportunity to act well through immutable decrees; and so it would
be better to imagine God mutable, that he might be free. Can absolute freedom
and immutability not coexist in God? 4. Because God is infinitely wise and
infinitely just, therefore his decrees are mutable. For Jackson says that if
wisdom and justice in the pope were equal to his dignity and power, it would
be far safer for him to rescind decrees and deeds with an inspiration or
expiration of breath. Therefore God's decrees ought to be mutable because
God's justice and wisdom are infinite; and so justice and freedom in God
fight face-to-face on opposing sides. 5. As the most learned William Twisse
has excellently observed, Jackson confounds the liberty of nature, equally
common to everyone, with the liberty of condition or authority, which is
greater in a prince than in a subject. Hence, after enacting an irrevocable
law, a prince possesses the same liberty of nature as before he enacted the
law; yet he does not possess liberty of condition such that he can change his
decrees without the mark of inconstancy. After his decrees God is perfectly
free by liberty of nature, just as

[Marginalia: Twisse, *Against Jackson*, chapter 10, section 2, p. 390.]

[1651 logical printed p. 163 | IA leaf 181 | PDF p. 181]

he was before the decree was established. But after issuing a decree he could
not be God if he rescinded his decrees; and yet he most freely executes his
decrees. For he sees, and can see, no reason why he should rescind them. 6. By
the same reasoning God would lose the opportunity to act well through his
promises just as through his decrees. Therefore God can also violate his
promises. 7. It is well known that Vorstius blasphemously ascribed corporeity,
repentance, and many human passions to God. So here he wishes God to be able
to change his decrees lest freedom of decreeing perish.

25. Objection 22. Jackson objects thus: “The claim that the course of each
person's life and the final sentences appointed for each must be immutable
because they are predetermined by the immutable, omnipotent decree has no
greater appearance of truth than if we said that God must be black because he
makes ravens black, and so forth.” Reply. No one but Jackson argues in this
way: Because God made ravens—therefore say that your Arminian God is a raven,
for you have established him as mutable. 2. No one asserts that God's decrees
make the decreed things immutable, for in that way he would make creatures
not creatures. 3. That the decree is immutable and yet the things decreed are
mutable in relation to the decree is a contradiction. For if God decrees that
Peter should believe, by modal necessity it cannot come about that he does
not believe. For in that manner Jackson establishes God's decrees as
immutable in which the decrees of the Persians were immutable, p. 123. But if
things decreed by the Persians, insofar as

[Marginalia: Jackson, chapter 9, section 2, p. 119, in the same place.]

[1651 logical printed p. 164 | IA leaf 182 | PDF p. 182]

they were decreed, could be revoked and changed, was not the decree
revocable? Let him define an immutable decree for us. Will it leave the
decreed things able either to be or not to be? Plainly this is a disjunctive
decree; and even if every decree in God is removed, Judas must either believe
or not believe, either be condemned or not condemned. There is no need—indeed,
it is ridiculous—for God to decree this. 4. Sentences concerning people's
death or life are acts of God. But if those sentences are mutable, then God's
acts and volitions are mutable. 5. The claim that people's death and life are
thus in equilibrium for God smacks of a God who is ignorant, mutable,
suspended, wavering, and by no means infinite, who awaits created volitions
in order either to determine something or to know anything with certainty.

26. Objection 23. “My Father works today,” John 5. Therefore, says Jackson,
God decrees today; he works all things according to the counsel of his will.
Therefore, etc. Reply. He confounds the decree with the execution of the
decree. 2. It follows, then, that God does something in time which he did not
decree from eternity that he would do, and that new volitions begin in God,
some today and others tomorrow. All these things are absurd. Today he will
decree creation!

27. Objection 24. It follows that acts of justice are omitted because God
absolutely and simply decreed that they should not occur; thus God will be the
preventer of justice and good acts. Reply. 1. The most illustrious Dr. Twisse:
the will to omit a good thing will have been decreed by God, and no other
action can be imagined. That is, it pleases Arminius that God decreed that
acts of justice

[Marginalia: Twisse, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius's Anti-Perkins*,
book 2, charge 3, section 1, p. 20.]

[1651 logical printed p. 165 | IA leaf 183 | PDF p. 183]

should be omitted by people, provided only that they willed. God therefore
decreed that will to omit, either by an absolute or a relative will. If by an
absolute will, the point at issue is granted. If by a relative will, then he
decreed that creatures should will to omit acts of justice, provided they
willed. 2. Does God not claim from this life into blessedness many believers
who, if they survived long enough, would perform many acts of justice? And
then God prevents acts of justice. The Tyrians would have repented if God had
employed signs; foreign nations would have heard Ezekiel if God had sent him
to prophesy. But he did not will to grant signs to the Tyrians or prophets to
the nations. Likewise Isaiah 63:17; 1 Samuel 2:25. 3. Properly speaking, the
preventer of justice is one who by violence twists and calls a creature away
elsewhere when it is inclined toward acts of justice. But those who freely
omit acts of justice, and by that very act freely prevent those same acts
which God absolutely decreed should be omitted, prevent themselves from
acting justly. Therefore, they freely omit the acts, and also do so by the
necessity of the divine decree.
