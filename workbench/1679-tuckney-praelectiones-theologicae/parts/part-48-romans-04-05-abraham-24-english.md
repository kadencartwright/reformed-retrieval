Only one matter remains to be examined more carefully. Bellarmine places it
at the beginning of his proofs and uses it to establish the perfection of
faith in this life, namely:

[1679 printed p. 262 | IA leaf 270]

“If faith cannot be perfect in this life, it will never be capable of
perfection at all, for in the other life it will not be perfected but
abolished. Yet who would believe that so eminent a virtue will never be
perfect?” Sebastian Castellio sings the same refrain in his treatise *On
the Obedience to Be Rendered to God*, saying, “The old man must be put to
death through faith, but faith does not appear destined to have a place in
the future life. Therefore this must be done in the present life.” For this
reason, in his little book *On Justification*, he argues with enough leaps
against those who teach that sin is finally and completely extinguished in
the saints at death. Or, as he rather mockingly charges against them, they
have devised some sort of purgatory—not after death, where their predecessors
placed it, but in death itself—not indeed under that name, but certainly in
reality. Dr. Gell has matching arguments and taunts. Since these matters
deserve some further discussion and seem capable of contributing something
to a fuller elucidation of the cause now in our hands, let us, if you
please, consider each of them briefly in passing.

1. If faith is not perfected in this life, can it never be perfected,
because it will be abolished in the future life, as Bellarmine says?

2. Are the imperfections of faith and the remnants of every sin not
extinguished at death, as Castellio denies?

As to the first question:

First, we can say very rightly with our Most Reverend Davenant, “What if it
were never perfected?” Even if the imperfection of faith, at least with
respect to some of its acts, does not belong to its essence, as Durandus
denies, it nevertheless accompanies it, as Durandus himself grants. What
harm, I ask, would follow? On their view, at least, faith is only a virtue
for the way. It serves to apprehend Christ our Bridegroom and Redeemer and
to prepare us here by grace for glory. If it suffices for this office,
although in itself it is not free from every defect, why should we require
its complete perfection either in this life, where with God's help and
acceptance it performs its office notwithstanding its weakness, or in the
future life, where, as they hold, it will be abolished? As for apprehending
Christ unto justification, faith does not justify insofar as it is a
perfect virtue in itself. It justifies as it receives Christ, being needy
in itself, and rests upon him, being weak in itself. Its intrinsic
perfection in every degree is therefore not required. A very small and weak
hand suffices to receive an offered gift. In this respect the faith of the
weakest believers is “equally precious” with that of the strongest, as the
Apostle testifies. God's grace shines more brightly in this, for he willed
that the riches of his grace alone should be supremely celebrated in this
work. As for the other part of faith's office—our preparation by grace for
glory through purifying the heart, conquering the world, and extinguishing
Satan's flaming darts—faith can accomplish this by Christ's power and
efficacy while itself remaining imperfect in some measure. Moses of old,
because of the guilt of his offenses, could neither enter the Promised Land
himself nor bring the people into it. Yet he led them through the trackless
wilderness and brought them to the very borders of Canaan to be led in by
Joshua. The same can be said of the faith of wayfarers. It can accompany
and guide them on the way and thus execute its office, although it has
never here been perfect in every respect

[1679 printed p. 263 | IA leaf 271]

and will be entirely abolished in the future life, as Bellarmine and his
party hold. Yet it would never be abolished if it could be a perfect
righteousness, just as the legal ceremonies would never have vanished if
they could have perfected those who approached, Hebrews 8:13 and 10:1–2.

Second, however, we say that faith is perfected in the heavenly homeland,
even though it should be entirely abolished there. It is perfected in the
same way that Christ fulfilled the ceremonial law when he abolished it:
not by a bare and simple abolition, but by substituting and presenting the
body or signified reality for its shadow. Or it is perfected as infancy is
abolished by maturity or adulthood—not by simply destroying it, as occurs
through an untimely death, but by perfecting it as it is advanced to a more
perfect state. Consequently this abolition is perfection, since it is not
so much a change of the thing as of its mode, from a less perfect mode to a
more perfect one. For faith, with respect to its general and root essence,
is knowledge, persuasion, or adherence. In this respect it remains in the
heavenly homeland, although it is entirely changed with respect to certain
particular and accidental conditions proper to this life and its wayfarers,
conditions which denote and contain some imperfection. Yet this change
should be judged a perfection more than an abolition or destruction.

Meanwhile, I should mention something that one of our theologians has
rightly observed: this perfection or imperfection has no bearing at all on
the cause we are now treating. When we remove inherent righteousness from
being the cause or matter of our justification because of its imperfection,
we do not mean the innocent imperfection of nature that belongs to faith in
this lower state of wayfarers when compared with the enjoyment of those who
possess the goal. We mean the culpable and sinful imperfection arising from
the defect of what could and ought to be present in this state of wayfarers.
We affirm and prove that such a defect always inheres here in all our
inherent righteousness and in faith itself, even when mature. We therefore
do not derive our justification from it as a meritorious, material, or
formal cause.
