# On Lending at Interest

*An undated French letter on “usury” in the historical sense of interest-taking; a fresh translation from CO X/1 245–249.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 245]

John Calvin to one of his friends.

I have not yet experienced it myself, but I have learned from the examples of others how perilous it is to answer the question on which you ask my advice. For if we prohibit all lending at interest absolutely, we bind consciences with a bond stricter than God himself does. If we permit the very least, many people immediately take an unbridled license under this cover, and they cannot bear to have any limit set to it by a qualification.

If I were writing to you alone, I would not fear such a thing, for your prudence and the moderation of your disposition are well known to me. But because you ask advice for someone else, I fear that, seizing upon one word, he may permit himself somewhat more than I desire. Nevertheless, because I do not doubt that you will carefully consider what is expedient and how far, according to the person's character and the present circumstances, I will tell you what I think.

First, there is no testimony in Scripture by which every taking of interest is absolutely condemned. For Christ's saying, commonly regarded as perfectly clear—namely, “lend” (Luke 6:35)—has been falsely twisted to this meaning. For just as elsewhere, in rebuking

### [CO X/1, p. 246]

lavish guests and the ambitious banquets of the rich, he commands them instead to call the blind, the lame, and other poor people from the streets who cannot return the favor, so also here, wishing to correct the world's vicious custom in lending money, he commands us to lend chiefly to those from whom there is no hope of recovery.

We, however, customarily look first to where money may be placed securely. Instead, we ought to help the poor, with whom the money is at risk. Christ's words thus amount to saying that he commands assistance to the poor rather than the rich. We therefore do not yet see that every taking of interest is forbidden.

The law of Moses (Deuteronomy 23:19) is civil law, and it binds us no further than equity and the principle of humanity carry. Certainly it would be highly desirable for interest-taking to be driven from the whole world and even for its name to be unknown. But because that is impossible, one must yield to the common good.

We have passages in the Prophets and the Psalms in which the Holy Spirit is angry against interest-taking: here is one characteristic of a wicked city, that interest is found in its public squares (Psalm 55:12). But because the Hebrew word *tost* generally signifies fraud, it can be explained otherwise. Yet let us suppose that the prophet is speaking there specifically about interest. It is no wonder if, among the principal evils, he says that interest-taking runs its course. The reason is that cruelty and many wicked deceptions are very often joined with the unlawful permission to take interest. What am I saying? Interest-taking almost always has these two inseparable companions: tyrannical cruelty and the art of deception. Hence elsewhere the Holy Spirit places among the praises of a holy, God-fearing man that he has abstained

### [CO X/1, p. 247]

from interest-taking, so that it is a very rare example to see a good man who is at the same time a lender at interest.

The prophet Ezekiel (22:12) goes still further. Among the horrible offenses against which God's provoked vengeance had been kindled against the Jews, he uses these two Hebrew words, *Nesec* and *Tarbit*. The first means interest, which was so named in Hebrew because it gnaws. The second word signifies an increment, addition, or increase, and not without reason, for each person seeking his own private profit took—or rather seized—a gain from another person's loss.

There is no doubt that the prophets spoke more severely about interest because it was expressly forbidden to the Jews. When they set themselves against God's express command, therefore, they deserved to be rebuked more harshly.

Here an objection is made that interest-taking will likewise be unlawful for us today for the same reason it was forbidden to the Jews, because there is a fraternal union among us. To that I answer that there is some difference in the civil union. The situation of the place in which God had settled the Jews and many other circumstances allowed them to trade conveniently among themselves without interest. Our union is not similar. I therefore still do not recognize that interest is simply forbidden to us, except insofar as it is contrary to equity or charity.

The argument of Saint Ambrose, which Chrysostom also advances, is too frivolous in my judgment: namely, that money does not beget money. What of the sea? What of the land? I receive rent from letting a house. Is that because money grows there? Yet returns proceed from fields, from which money is made. The use of houses can likewise be purchased with money. And what then? Is money not more fruitful in commerce than some possessions one could name? It will be lawful to lease a plot by imposing rent, yet unlawful to take some fruit from money? What then? When one buys a field, does money not beget money? How do merchants increase their property? They use industry, you will say.

Certainly I admit what children can see: if you shut money in a chest, it will be sterile. Yet no one borrows from us on the condition that he suppress the idle money and not put it to profitable use. The fruit, therefore, comes not from the money but from its return. We must consequently conclude that such subtleties move people at first sight, but if examined more closely they vanish of themselves, for they have nothing solid within them. I now conclude that interest must be judged not according to

### [CO X/1, p. 248]

some certain and particular pronouncement of God, but only according to the rule of equity.

The matter will be clearer through an example. There will be a certain rich man with possessions and income but no ready money. There will be another man moderately rich in property—at least somewhat less wealthy—but with more cash at hand. If some opportunity presents itself, the latter would willingly purchase an estate with his money. Meanwhile the first man will earnestly ask him for a loan. It is within the latter's power, under the title of a purchase, to impose a rent upon the first man's property until the money is repaid. In this way the arrangement would be better for him; nevertheless, he will be content with interest.

Why will the former bargain be just and honorable, but this one false and wicked? For he acts more kindly toward his brother by agreeing to interest than if he compelled him to mortgage the property. What is this but playing with God like a child, judging what is done by names rather than by truth, as though it were in our power, by changing the name, to make vices out of virtues or virtues out of vices?

I have not undertaken to debate the matter here. It is enough to point it out so that you may weigh it more diligently within yourself. Nevertheless, I would have you always remember that it is the things themselves, not words or manners of speaking, which are brought to judgment here.

I now come to the qualifications. We must carefully consider, as I said at the beginning, what caution is needed, because almost everyone seeks a single little word by which to indulge himself beyond measure. It is therefore necessary to use this preface: when I permit some taking of interest, I do not thereby make every kind lawful. Next, I do not approve if anyone proposes to make a trade of profiting from interest. Furthermore, I concede nothing without adding certain qualifications.

The first is that interest must not be taken from the poor and that no one wholly straitened by need or afflicted by disaster may be constrained. The second is that the lender must not be so intent on gain that he fails in necessary duties, nor, in wishing to place his money securely, may he disdain his poor brothers. The third is that nothing may enter into the arrangement that does not accord with natural equity and that, when the matter is examined according to Christ's rule—namely, what you

### [CO X/1, p. 249]

would have people do to you, and so forth—it must be found to agree in every respect. The fourth qualification is that the borrower make as much or more profit from the borrowed money. Fifth, we must not judge what is lawful for us by common and accepted custom, nor measure what is right and equitable by the world's iniquity, but take our rule from God's Word.

Sixth, we must not consider only the private advantage of the person with whom we deal, but also what is expedient for the public. For it is perfectly evident that the interest a merchant pays is a public charge. We must therefore take good care that the bargain is beneficial to the community rather than harmful. Seventh, one must not exceed the measure permitted by the public laws of the region or place. Yet even that is not always sufficient, for the laws often permit what they could not correct or repress by prohibition. Equity must therefore take precedence and cut away whatever is excessive.

Far be it from me, however, to press my opinion upon you, since I desire nothing more than that everyone should be so humane that there would be no need to say anything about this matter. I have summarized these things briefly more from a desire to oblige you than from confidence that I can satisfy you. In keeping with your goodwill toward me, however, you will kindly accept this service of mine, such as it is.

Farewell, most excellent and honored friend. May God preserve you with your family. Amen.

## Translator's notes

1. Throughout this work *usure* denotes the charging or taking of interest broadly. CO itself warns that the word does not have only the sense “usury” bears in modern French; translating every occurrence as modern English “usury” would prejudge the argument Calvin is making.
2. The scriptural wording is translated from the French witness. Luke 6:35 is represented by the single imperative *prestez* (“lend”); the fuller wording appears only in CO's editorial Latin footnote and is not inserted into the historical text.
3. CO prints the form *tost* for the Hebrew term in the Psalm and marks it *sic*, adding Hebrew *tok* in a footnote. The source form is retained in `french.txt`; the note here flags it rather than silently substituting a modern transliteration.
4. The Hebrew forms *Nesec* and *Tarbit* are likewise retained as printed. Calvin explains the first through a verb meaning to bite or gnaw and the second as an addition or increase.
5. *Pension* can mean rent, return, or periodic payment. In the argument against “money does not beget money,” the French shifts abruptly from singular *pension* to plural *elles* in *elles procedent des champs dou largent se faict*. “Returns” supplies that plural referent, while *dou* is read as *d'où*: the money is made from the fields, not the fields acquired with money. In the later example, the lender could structure the transaction as a purchase secured upon the borrower's estate and receive a rent; Calvin argues that changing the legal name does not determine its morality.
6. The third qualification is syntactically compressed and uses an older negative construction. Its place in the seven-part list requires the arrangement to agree throughout with natural equity and Christ's rule, not merely to evade one prohibited form.
7. This is a historical theological judgment, not present-day financial, tax, or legal advice. Its categories and examples presuppose sixteenth-century markets and law.
