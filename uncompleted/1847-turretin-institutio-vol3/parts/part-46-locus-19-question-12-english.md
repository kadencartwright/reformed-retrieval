QUESTION XII.

ON THE NECESSITY OF BAPTISM.

Was baptism merely a temporary rite, distinguishing believers from
unbelievers, which ought to have continued only for a time? We deny against the
Socinians.

I. A twofold question is raised concerning the necessity of baptism. The first
is that of the Socinians, who err by deficiency and refuse to acknowledge not
only

[1847 vol. III printed p. 328 | IA leaf 362 | PDF p. 362]

a necessity of means but even a necessity of command. The other is that of the
papists, who err by excess and assert its absolute necessity. We treat the
former now and the latter afterward.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

II. Just as the Socinians deny that the sacraments are signs and seals of the
covenant and regard them only as tokens of profession, so they deny that
baptism was commanded by Christ for perpetual use in the church. They regard
it only as a temporary rite distinguishing believers from unbelievers, which
continued, or ought to have continued, only for a time. They say it was not
commanded by Christ but practiced by the apostles without a command, as
Socinus’s *Disputation on Baptism* and the Racovian Catechism state. We, on the
contrary, hold that baptism is a sacrament divinely instituted and administered
by the apostles at Christ’s command for perpetual use in the church to seal
the remission of sins and regeneration.

[Marginalia: It is proved that baptism is of perpetual use. 1. From Matthew
28:19.]

III. The reasons are as follows. 1. An express command of Christ concerning it
exists, for he explicitly instituted it and commanded that it be observed
(Matt. 27:19 [sic]): “Go, teach all nations, baptizing them, etc.” It cannot be
replied that “this command concerned only the church while it was being
constituted, not after it had been constituted.” That this is false is evident:
(1) from the promise that Christ adds in the same place concerning his presence
until the consummation of the age with those who were to teach and baptize; (2)
because the office of teaching all nations is not confined to the persons of
the apostles but extends to all pastors who were to perform the ministry of the
gospel until the end; and (3) because the necessity of baptism is not temporary
but perpetual, in both the constituted church and the church being
constituted—namely, entrance into the church and the sealing of the covenant,
which pertain to all believers of every age (Acts 2:38; Rom. 6:4; Titus 3:5;
Col. 2:12). Therefore, since this command is universal with respect to persons,
because all nations are to be taught and baptized; places, because both things
were to be done throughout the whole world; and times, because they were to be
continued until the consummation of the age, it would wrongly be restricted to
only those believers, whether Jews or gentiles, from whom the apostles chiefly
gathered the primitive church.

[Marginalia: 2. From the end of the command.]

IV. 2. It was ordained for the use of the church in every age: (1) as an
ordinary means of gathering and preserving the church; (2) it was commanded to
the church gathered not from one nation but from all nations; (3) the unity of
the church is placed no less in the unity of baptism than in the unity of faith
(Eph. 4:4); and (4) baptism succeeded circumcision. Therefore, just as
circumcision was always to have a place among the people until Christ’s first
advent, so baptism must continue until his second.

[Marginalia: 3. From the parity of the Supper and baptism.]

V. 3. The same account must be given of each sacrament. But the Supper must
continue until Christ’s advent (1 Cor. 11:6 [sic]). Therefore baptism must also
continue. Nor can any ground for a distinction be given, because in each there
is an equal testimony and sealing of mystical grace (1 Cor. 12:13).

[Marginalia: 4. From the end and effects of baptism.]

VI. 4. According to the apostles’ teaching, the end and effects of baptism
pertain not only to converts from unbelief but also to those born of believers
who have always professed the same faith of Christ. These effects include
communion with Christ in baptism (Rom. 6:3–4; Gal. 3:27), remission of sins
(Acts 22:16), regeneration by the Spirit (Titus 3:5), and eternal life (Mark
16:16).

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

VII. Paul does not deny absolutely that he was sent to baptize, but to preach
the gospel (1 Cor. 1:17), as is evident from verses 14 and 16. He denies it
comparatively, that is, he was sent first and chiefly to

[1847 vol. III printed p. 329 | IA leaf 363 | PDF p. 363]

preach the gospel, because this was necessary above other things for founding
the church.

VIII. Although Christ’s discipline is spiritual and directed to the inner
person (Rom. 12:1), it does not follow that it admits no external rites. (1) It
is *logikos* (λογικός), or rational, worship—not to the exclusion of every
external rite, but in a qualified sense, as opposed to Levitical worship
consisting of brute and *alogoi* (ἀλόγοις), or irrational, sacrifices. (2)
Christ’s discipline is directed to perfecting the inner person, but in such a
way that it also employs external means to advance this work. (3) It is
spiritual according to its internal power and efficacy, but according to its
external dispensation it is accommodated to human capacity (John 3:12; Rom.
6:19), nor does it reject words or similar visible human symbols and aids.

IX. Figures and types foreshadowing Christ to come were abrogated by Christ,
but rites and ceremonies that signify Christ as having died and seal his
benefits were not.

X. Although the effect of salvation is denied to the water of baptism (1 Pet.
3:21), it does not follow that baptism is not necessary by necessity of
command, because not everything commanded by Christ immediately effects
salvation. Many things are commanded that contribute only to well-being, not
simply to being. Moreover, the effect of salvation is not denied to water
simply, but in a qualified sense: water does not produce it principally but
instrumentally, not in opposition to the thing signified but in conjunction
with it, and not really but morally.

XI. The institution of baptism and the prohibition of things strangled and of
blood cannot be treated alike, because an express command of Christ is given
concerning the former but not the latter. Moreover, the reason for the
prohibition was temporary, namely the scandal it would cause weak Jews, while
the reason for baptism is perpetual. It serves not only to distinguish a
believer from an unbeliever but also to be the washing of regeneration for
believers in every age. Therefore, the apostles interpreted the decree about
things strangled and blood in their epistles and, when Christian liberty had
been fully confirmed, abolished it as useless (1 Cor. 10; Col. 2). This cannot
be said of baptism.

XII. It cannot be said that in Matthew 28:19 Christ instituted the baptism of
doctrine, not of water, because he expressly distinguishes doctrine from
baptism—“teach and baptize”—to show that he does not comprehend the former
under the latter. Add that he repeats, declares, and confirms the divinely
given institution of John’s baptism, which until then had been practiced only
among the Jews. But John’s baptism was a baptism of water (John 1:26).
Therefore Christ also commanded the baptism of water here.

XIII. It is one thing to baptize verbally in the name of the Trinity and
another to do so in reality. When the apostles baptized in the name of Christ
as the meritorious and proximate cause of salvation, in reality they baptized
in the name of the Trinity, because by synecdoche the Trinity is contained
under the name of Christ alone: the Father is the one who anoints, the Holy
Spirit is the anointing itself (Acts 10:38), and the Son is the anointed one.

XIV. There were two kinds of Christ’s disciples. One consisted of those who
passed from John’s school to Christ and had already been baptized by John
(John 1 and 3). The other consisted of those whom Christ himself gathered into
the company of his followers. John testifies at least generally that these
were baptized by Christ’s apostles (John 4:1–2).

XV. Although by Christ’s institution baptism belongs to those who are to be
taught, it does not follow that it was to be administered only to unbelievers.
Christ joins teaching with baptism in view of the condition of that time, when
the gathering of the church occurred mostly from unbelieving Jews and
gentiles. Moreover, not only are unbelievers taught so that they may become
believers, but believers are also taught, both

[1847 vol. III printed p. 330 | IA leaf 364 | PDF p. 364]

adults and younger persons, so that they may advance and persevere in faith
and increasingly show themselves by their conduct to be Christ’s disciples.

XVI. Although the greater part of those recorded as baptized by the apostles
were unbelievers, because the Christian church was still being constituted and
had not yet been constituted, it does not follow either that there are no
examples of believers being baptized, since all the pious covenant Jews who
were baptized were to be regarded not as unbelievers but as believers, or that
baptism cannot continually be administered to the children of Christians who
are in the covenant.
