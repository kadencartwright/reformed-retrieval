VERSE 10. Whatever he may be, his name has already been declared, and it is
known that he is human and cannot contend with one stronger than himself.

Solomon gives another reply by conceding the objection, namely, that there is
some good in the multiplied possession of riches, especially if use is joined
to possession. Yet from their effects he confirms the weakness of riches:
neither possession nor use has such force and power that they can deliver the
possessor and owner from the human condition—that is, from fragility and
transience. Therefore, he says, whatever a person may be, though riches,
resources, honor, and so forth flow around him from every side, his very name
makes it known that he is human and that nothing human is alien to him.

If riches could free us from human frailty and raise us to the firmness and
stability of angels—if they could make us secure from the calamities with
which human nature is surrounded—there would be some reason to pursue them
greedily and thirstily. There have been insane people, prodigies rather than
human beings, who were abundantly equipped with power, honors, and resources
and burst forth into such madness that they allowed themselves to be
persuaded that they were gods and should be worshiped as gods. Yet they found
this mental frenzy not only vain but fatal to themselves. Among these are the
Roman beasts and false prophets.

In every age, including our own, there are many who swell with pride, riches,
and honor. Although they do not seek the title of god and wish to appear to
abhor it, they nevertheless think everything permitted to them at their
pleasure, persuade themselves that no laws, divine or human, bind them, and
wish to be answerable to no one. What else do they do but reject the title
and name of god because it would make everyone hate and abominate them, while
proudly arrogating the rights and privileges owed to God alone? Therefore,
amid the accumulation of all the greatest external goods, let them remember
that they are human—that is, wretched and liable to many miseries.

And let us take care to surpass the human lot by the treasures of virtue and
piety and to obtain a condition better than that of Adam while he was still
in Paradise—indeed, more than this, a condition happier and in some measure
more excellent than the lot of the elect angels, because in our human nature
we are conformed to Christ the Lord more than the angels are.

Solomon confirms this fragile and transient condition of humanity from an
effect of God, namely, the decree by which he consigned and adjudged to vanity
both us and

all these things of ours under the Sun. He magnifies the firmness of this
decree or counsel by a comparison with lesser things: no one can break or
burst through God's counsel by contending against it, even if fortified with
boundless resources, power, and honors. Therefore, when people labor to
increase their property without limit so as to free themselves from this
life's dangers, calamities, and sorrows, they beat the air and, as the saying
goes, wash a brick. God has confined them under this misery and vanity after
first subjecting them to the tyranny of sin.
