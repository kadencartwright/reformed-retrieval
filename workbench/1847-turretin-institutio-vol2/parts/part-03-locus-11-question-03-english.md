## QUESTION III

### THE PERFECTION OF THE MORAL LAW

Is the moral law so perfect a rule of life and conduct that nothing can be
added to it, or ought to be corrected in it, for the true worship of God? Or
did Christ not only complete it as imperfect but also correct it as contrary
to his teaching?

The former is affirmed; the latter denied, against the Socinians,
Anabaptists, Remonstrants, and Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Socinians.]

I. This question is disputed between us and various parties. First are the
*Socinians*, who contend that Christ's precepts were one thing and Moses'
another, and that Christ supplemented and enlarged the moral law with various
additions because it was imperfect, and corrected it in certain respects
because it was less than right. Hence they maintain that Christ made various
corrections to the law and various additions to each precept of the Decalogue
by which they are perfected. With respect to the first, these are
*a definite manner of praying prescribed in the Lord's Prayer, and the
religious worship of Christ as Mediator*; with respect to the second, *the
avoidance of idols or images*; the third, *the prohibition of an oath even in
true matters*; the fourth, *the abrogation of the Sabbath without the
substitution of any other day*; the fifth, and the second table in general,
*love of one's enemy*; the sixth, *the prohibition of anger and vengeance,
whether private or public*; the seventh, *the prohibition of a lustful look,
of divorce except for adultery, of impurity, and of obscenity*; the eighth,
*the prohibition of avarice and the distribution of superfluous possessions
to the poor*; the ninth, *the prohibition of vain and frivolous speech and of
unjust judgment*, and so forth; the tenth, *the prohibition of desire, not
only so that we do not take pleasure in it, but so that, as far as our mind is
concerned, we do not even conceive it*. They also contend that Christ gave new
precepts besides the Decalogue, of which these three are chief: 1. *self-
denial*; 2. *bearing the cross for Christ*; 3. *the imitation of Christ*. On
these see the Racovian Catechism on Christ's prophetic office, chapters 1 and
2; Ostorodt, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, chapter 22; and Volkelius,
book 4 of *The True Religion*, chapter 21. Their purpose is: 1. to establish
the impious doctrine that Christ is Savior not by a λύτρον, *ransom*, but by
teaching; 2. to establish the justification of men by works—not indeed by the
merit of works, in which they differ from the Roman Catholics, but by the
acceptilation of those works; 3. to establish that the reward of the soul's
immortality did not exist under the Old Testament but was bestowed through
Christ.

[Marginal heading: The Anabaptists and Remonstrants.]

2. The *Anabaptists* and *Remonstrants* follow them: the former in the
Frankenthal Colloquy, article 1, sections 10 and 11, and the Emden Colloquy,
articles 11, 12, and 13; the latter in their *Apology*, chapter 12, where they
approve the Socinian thesis concerning the law's imperfection and, in the
hypothesis, posit and defend various additions, and in chapter 13, where they
treat at length the three precepts added by Christ, especially the first.

II. The *Manichaeans* had already anticipated them. Augustine testifies in
book 19 of *Against Faustus the Manichaean* that the same question was debated

[Printed p. 19 | Internet Archive PDF 47 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

between them and the Catholics. They taught that Christ partly fulfilled the
moral law by adding the precepts found in Matthew 5 and partly destroyed it,
which Augustine refutes throughout that book. The *Muslims* seem to have drawn
from their cisterns what is found in the Quran, surah 12: Moses brought a less
perfect law, Christ a more perfect one, but Muhammad the most perfect one.

[Marginal heading: The Roman Catholics.]

The *Roman Catholics* also err here in more than one way by asserting the
law's imperfection. Thus *Bellarmine*, book 4 of *On Justification*, chapter 3,
teaches that Christ made the law perfect from imperfect, or at least more
perfect from perfect, and that the very difference between the law and the
gospel is the difference between an initial and a perfect doctrine.
*Maldonatus* on Matthew 5 obstinately contends that Christ corrected the old
law by adjoining those things that were lacking for evangelical perfection.
Against all these, however, the orthodox judge the moral law so perfect that
it required neither supplementation nor correction, but only genuine
interpretation. They hold that whatever is alleged to have been added either
is false and gratuitously invented, or was already contained in the law
explicitly or implicitly.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

III. The question is not whether the law needed illumination, confirmation,
and vindication against the corruptions of the scribes and Pharisees and the
perverse judgments of men. We readily grant this and will demonstrate it in
what follows. Rather, the question is whether it needed supplementation or
correction, as if it were imperfect or less than right. Our adversaries
affirm; we deny. The question is of great importance because our adversaries
seek nothing other than to transform the gospel into a new law and so
establish the righteousness of works in place of the righteousness of faith.

[Marginal heading: The perfection of the law is proved.]

IV. The reasons of the orthodox are: 1. *The law is perfect* (Ps. 19:7), not
only in a qualified sense and with respect to that age under the Old
Testament, but also absolutely with respect to its nature. It is so perfect
that it not only contains everything to be done and lacks nothing, as
*Genebrard* and *Lorinus* testify on this passage, but also nothing is to be
added to it or taken from it (Deut. 4:2; 12:32). 2. It is perfect
*extensively* with respect to its *parts*, because in love for God and one's
neighbor it adequately embraces every duty owed to them, as Christ teaches in
Matthew 22:37. It is perfect *intensively* with respect to its *degrees*,
because it demands not just any love but the most perfect love, than which no
greater can be given. It is perfect *finally* with respect to its perfect use
and effect, because it can give life and happiness to those who observe it:
“The one who does these things will live by them” (Lev. 18:5). This could not
be said if the law needed either supplementation or correction.

V. Second, “Christ did not come to destroy it, but to fulfill it” (Matt.
5:17). Here τὸ πληρῶσαι, *to fulfill*, does not mean to complete something
imperfect or correct something contrary, which would instead be ἀναλῦσαι, *to
destroy*, but, in a Hebrew form of speech, to do what has been commanded.
Thus Christ fulfilled the law not by addition or correction, but by
observance and execution. Christ fulfilled the law in three ways: as
*doctrine*, by faithfully preaching, solidly confirming, and vigorously
vindicating it; as a *rule*, by full and constant observance; and as a *type*,
by complete consummation, presenting in himself the truth of the types and
oracles and the body of the shadows.

VI. Third, Christ delivered no precepts of the law in the New Testament,
either by himself or through the apostles, other than those Moses had given
(Matt. 22:37; Rom. 13:9), nor does he present any other explanation of them
than the prophets had once presented. Hence John calls the precept of love
both *old and new* (1 John 2:7–8): old with respect to its first promulgation
in the Old Testament, and new with respect to its renewal and illumination in
the New.

[Printed p. 20 | Internet Archive PDF 48 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

VII. Fourth, the law cannot be supplemented or corrected without being
charged with imperfection and defect, and thus without the defect itself being
referred back to God, the author of the law. Even to think this is impious.

VIII. Fifth, *self-denial, bearing the cross, and the imitation of Christ* were
already commanded under the Old Testament. For when we are commanded to love
God above all things, are we not thereby commanded to deny ourselves for God
and patiently bear the cross when he imposes it upon us? And since the
imitation of God is so often prescribed, is not the imitation of Christ, who
is true God, thereby also commanded? Add that the imitation of Christ is
nothing other than imitation with respect to the moral virtues, whose rule is
to be sought nowhere but in the law. This very point is confirmed by various
examples from the Old Testament. With respect to *self-denial*, it is seen in
Abraham leaving his country and offering his son, the Levites slaying their
brothers (Exod. 32), Job blessing God in adversity (chapter 1), and Moses,
Daniel, and his companions spurning every pleasure (Heb. 11:25–26; Dan. 1:8
and following). With respect to *bearing the cross*, it is seen in Moses (Heb.
11:25–26), the prophets under Ahab (1 Kings 18), Zechariah (2 Chron. 24),
Daniel's companions (chapter 3), and the Jewish church under Antiochus (Ps.
44; Heb. 11). With respect to *the imitation of Christ*, it is seen in the
faithful conforming themselves to the pattern of God.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

IX. It is one thing *to correct the law itself*; it is another *to overthrow
the false interpretation* of the scribes and Pharisees. It is one thing to
introduce into the law *a new meaning* that it did not have; it is another
merely to shed *new light* by unfolding what lay hidden in the law and was not
heeded by its teachers, thus declaring it by *explanation* and restoring it by
*vindication*. In Matthew 5 Christ did the latter, not the former. He does not
act as a new Lawgiver, but only as Interpreter and Vindicator of the law given
through Moses. He opposes his sayings not to the sayings or writings of Moses,
but to those of the scribes and Pharisees, just as they boasted that they had
received them from ancient teachers. This is clear: 1. From Christ's
*proposition* in verse 20, where he speaks of the righteousness of the
Pharisees: “Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and
Pharisees.” Therefore, he intends to proceed against Pharisaic righteousness,
not against the righteousness commanded by the law of Moses. 2. From his
*manner of speaking*. He does not say, “It is written in the law of Moses,” or
“The law” or “Moses said to you,” but “*You have heard*”—that is, from the
scribes or Pharisees—“*that it was said to the ancients*,” or “*by the
ancients*.” Thus he shows that he refutes only the traditions of the elders
(Matt. 15:2–3), not the law of Moses itself. 3. From the *example adduced* in
verse 43 concerning love of one's neighbor and hatred of one's enemy. Moses
did not say this, but even commands the contrary (Lev. 19:18; Exod. 23:4–5),
as does Solomon following him (Prov. 25:21–22). Rather, it came from the
teachers of the Jews and the principal Pharisees. 4. From *the things
themselves* either
commanded or forbidden by Christ, which will afterward be proved to be
contained explicitly or implicitly in the law itself, and from the other
things he rejects, which are not found in the law, as will be clear in what
follows.

X. The words ἐρρέθη τοῖς ἀρχαίοις may be taken *subjectively*, as equivalent
to ὑπὸ τῶν ἀρχαίων, “it was said by the ancients,” as our *Beza* holds and
*Piscator* after him; or they may be taken *objectively*, as others following
the *Syriac* prefer and as seems more suitable, so that they imply πρὸς τοὺς
ἀρχαίους, “it was said to the ancients.” Whichever way the words are taken, it
does not follow that Moses said these things to the ancient Israelites of his
time, because this word is used indiscriminately of any ancestors. The Lord's
meaning is simply that the Jewish teachers who corrupted the law were
accustomed to preface the precepts they had depraved with this specious
introduction: “It was said to the ancients.” That is, the precepts they were
accustomed to inculcate were not new but old and had long before been handed
down to the ancestors of the Jews. Therefore, the antithesis Christ
establishes is not between himself and Moses or the law delivered by Moses,
but between himself and the glosses of the scribes and Pharisees

[Printed p. 21 | Internet Archive PDF 49 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

upon the law of Moses, by which they excessively restricted and wrongly
interpreted the law. He teaches that they, not he, violate the law's letter
and meaning, although his enemies charged him with doing so.

XI. Although Christ quotes Moses' words in verses 21, 27, 31, 33, and 38, he
does not cite them in the Mosaic sense, but in the Pharisaic sense. The words
indeed were common, but the meaning was different. The Pharisees interpreted
Moses' words very badly by restricting to outward acts words that also
extended to inward motions, as Christ teaches by disclosing the true and
genuine meaning of the law. Thus Christ corrects various things expressed in
the law, but not precisely insofar as they are expressed there; rather,
insofar as they were customarily presented by the scribes and Pharisees, not
according to Moses' meaning but according to their own παρεισάκτῳ, *imported*,
meaning—not an ἐμφύτῳ, *innate*, and γνησίῳ, *genuine*, meaning, but a
ὑποβολιμαίῳ, *spurious*, one.

XII. Christ does not correct the law concerning the third precept on oaths in
verses 33 and 34, but merely expounds and vindicates it from the corruptions
of the Pharisees. They restricted the general prohibition against *taking
God's name in vain* to the *perjury* of someone who had sworn by God's name,
and loosened the bridle for frivolous and rash oaths in daily speech and for
oaths by creatures, as if one did not sin against the law or the Lawgiver by
swearing rashly and frivolously, or even falsely, provided it was not by God's
name but by the name of some creature. On their view, the only sin was to
commit perjury and fail to fulfill or render what was sworn. One who swore by
God's name or by offerings sacred to God was indeed bound, but not one who
swore by the temple, the altar, or the like (Matt. 23:16–18); oaths of the
latter sort could be made and broken with impunity. Christ refutes these
utterly false glosses, showing that neither vain and rash oaths nor those
framed by any creatures whatever are lawful. In doing so, he adds or corrects
nothing in the law. For the law prohibits not only perjury but also
*frivolous and rash oaths* when it forbids taking God's name לשוא, *in vain*.
The Socinians wrongly confuse this—with Grotius supporting them—with לשקר,
*falsely* or *for a lie*, which is treated in Leviticus 19:12, as if only
perjury were condemned. But the former extends much more broadly and is
rightly applied to the vanity and frivolity of rash oaths. Hence the
Septuagint renders it ἐπὶ ματαίῳ, *for a vain thing*, Aquila εἰς κενήν, *for
an empty thing*, and the Targum למגנא, which is the same as the Hebrew חנם,
*gratuitously and without cause*. Therefore, although God's name is taken in
vain by committing perjury, it can often be taken in vain without perjury,
namely, in rash and frivolous oaths.

XIII. *Oaths by creatures* were also sufficiently prohibited by the law in
the Old Testament. They were prohibited *implicitly* by referring an oath to
God alone as an act of λατρείας, *worship*, owed to God alone: “Fear Jehovah
your God, worship him, and swear by his name” (Deut. 6:13; 10:20; see also
Isa. 65:16). They were prohibited *explicitly* by condemning oaths framed by
those that are not gods: “They swear by those that are not gods” (Jer. 5:7).
This is also proved by the *nature* of an oath, which formally is an invocation
of God as witness, whether explicitly or implicitly, and by the conditions
required in its object—omniscience, omnipresence, and omnipotence—which belong
to no creature. Nor do any examples of such oaths in the Old Testament stand
in the way, because they are examples of *fact*, not *right*; of *practice*,
not *law*. Nowhere was a law enacted for framing an oath by creatures.

XIV. Christ adds ὅλως, “*one must not swear at all*,” and James confirms it in
chapter 5:12. This cannot be understood absolutely and universally of every
oath, as though one simply must not swear. That would conflict both with the
*nature of the thing*, which is good and necessary in itself and, as such,
commanded for the confirmation of truth and the end of every dispute (Heb.
6:16),

[Printed p. 22 | Internet Archive PDF 50 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and with the *practice of Christ himself* and the apostles, who often swore.
Rather, it must be understood *in a qualified sense* of the rash and frivolous
oaths, or the oaths framed by creatures, that were being treated in this
passage. The explanation that follows clearly proves this, for he says that
“one must not swear at all, neither by heaven nor by earth,” and so forth.
That is, none of the perverse formulas for such an oath that were in use among
the Jews is to be employed. James condemns them in a general summary in
chapter 5:12 when he adds “nor any other oath.” Otherwise, if he had wished to
proscribe absolutely every oath, he should have added “nor by God.” Therefore,
the universal expression is wrongly separated from the *subjoined kinds of
oath*, or referred to the *genus and substance* of an oath instead of its
*form* as expressed by Matthew. Nor do Christ's added words, “Let your speech
be yes, yes; no, no,” prove the contrary. Christ is not speaking absolutely
and universally about every kind of speech, an extraordinary practice, or
cases of necessity in which conscience must be cleared, one's neighbor
helped, and God's glory advanced. He is speaking about ordinary habit and
practice in lesser cases and about familiar conversation, in which a simple
affirmation or denial, τὸ ναὶ ναί, οὒ οὔ, *yes, yes; no, no*, ought to
suffice. Three things are commended here: 1. the *simplicity* of speech, as
opposed to a habit of swearing; 2. its *truth*, as opposed to lying and
equivocations; 3. its *constancy* and reliability, as opposed to frivolity and
inconstancy. As for τὸ περισσὸν, *what is more*, which is said to be ἐκ τοῦ
πονηροῦ, *from evil*, it cannot extend to necessary and just additions in
solemn oaths concerning God's glory, our neighbor's welfare, or our own,
which God permits and commands. It must refer only to superfluous and
unnecessary additions that conflict with the simplicity and truthfulness of
words and occur in frivolous and rash oaths.

XV. The sixth precept concerning murder in Matthew 5:21 is not corrected but
vindicated. The Pharisees interpreted it only of outward murder, for which
they established capital punishment, but Christ extends it to the inward
motions of anger and to insults in speech. For these he establishes eternal
punishments that nevertheless differ in degree, alluding to the various
degrees of capital punishment customary among the Jews, concerning which see
Locus IX, Question 4, Thesis 17. Thus Christ teaches that in the Pharisaic
school the precept against killing is wrongly restricted to actual slaughter
because this alone was punished capitally among men. In God's forum, however,
where an account must be rendered not only of deeds but also of words and
thoughts, the precept also extends to things otherwise exempt from the
punishments customarily inflicted by men.

XVI. Although Christ quotes the words of the law concerning *retaliation* in
Matthew 5:38, which are found in Exodus 21:24—“an eye for an eye, a tooth for
a tooth”—and opposes to them his teaching *not to resist evil*, he does not
thereby correct them as though he wished to abolish all vengeance absolutely.
For as God, he himself ordained the magistrate and armed him with the sword
for the terror and punishment of the wicked (Rom. 13). And when he himself
was struck on one cheek, he did not offer the other, but resisted the evil by
defending his innocence and rebuking his adversaries' injustice (John 18:23),
which Paul also imitates in Acts 23:3. Instead, Christ merely refutes the
Pharisees' gloss, by which they transferred to private vengeance what the law
of Moses had sanctioned concerning the magistrate's public vengeance. Christ
wishes his people to be so far removed from private vengeance that they ought
to prefer having injuries doubled against them to avenging them themselves.
Moses had already declared this: “You shall not avenge,” he says, “nor keep
anger against your own people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself”
(Lev. 19:18), evidently because “vengeance and recompense belong to the Lord,”
as he says elsewhere (Deut. 32:35). The law of retaliation can still have a
place in its own sense, not precisely and formally, but by reason of moral
valuation.

[Printed p. 23 | Internet Archive PDF 51 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XVII. Christ's words in Matthew 5:39–40 about *turning one's cheek to the one
who strikes it and leaving one's cloak to the one who asks for it* are to be
understood not so much *positively*, as though one must actually turn the
cheek and give up the cloak—something neither Christ himself nor Paul did when
struck—as *negatively*. That is, they forbid resisting and avenging oneself by
a private, disorderly, and unnecessary resistance. They are to be understood
not *literally* and κατὰ τὸ ῥητόν, *according to the words*, but *figuratively*
and *hyperbolically*, κατὰ τὸ νοητόν, *according to the intended meaning*, of
bearing another injury rather than avenging the first. Thus one must attend
here not to the words themselves but to their meaning and purpose, and not to
the specific form of the precept but to its general subject, περὶ τῆς
ὑπομονῆς and ἀνεξικακίας, *patience and forbearance*. Therefore, *culpable
resistance* arising from vengeance is forbidden here, but not *lawful defense*
and blameless self-protection.

XVIII. Christ did not correct the seventh precept concerning adultery when he
extended it to desire and looking at a woman (Matt. 5:28), but disclosed the
true meaning of the law. Because the law is spiritual, it regards not only
outward motions but inward ones as well, as the last precept concerning desire
proves. This is contrary to the Pharisees, who restricted this law to the
outward act of fornication alone.

XIX. Christ's precept *not to dismiss one's wife except for the cause of
adultery* agrees with the law of Moses in Deuteronomy 24:1, where עֶרְוַת
דָּבָר, ἄσχημον πρᾶγμα, *a shameful thing*, can most suitably be referred to
that cause, and with the original institution Christ recounts in Matthew
19:4–5. Nor are the words about a certificate of divorce that Christ quotes
reported in the sense they had in the law, but in the sense in which the
Pharisees interpreted them. 1. The Pharisees spoke categorically and
imperatively: Δότω αὐτῇ ἀποστάσιον, “Let him give her a certificate of
divorce.” But Moses' words are hypothetical in Deuteronomy 24:1: “If a man
dismisses his wife, and so forth, and writes her a certificate of separation.”
2. Moses has a restriction in the permission of divorce, *because of
shamefulness*, עֶרְוַת דָּבָר, but the Pharisees have no limitation: “Whoever
dismisses his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce.” It was as if
dismissing one's wife for any cause whatsoever did not at all conflict with
the law, provided the formula for dismissal was observed and the husband by a
formal certificate ordered the woman to keep her possessions and conduct her
affairs for herself. 3. The Pharisees also wrongly drew Moses' words into an
approval of divorce, although it was only a *toleration and permission*. This
did not institute divorce in itself, but only incidentally placed a limit upon
it because of the hardness of the Jews' hearts (Matt. 19:7–8). Hence in place
of τοῦ ἐνετείλατο, *commanded*, which the Pharisees had used, Christ adds the
verb ἐπέτρεψεν, *permitted*. It was not lawful by a *moral dispensation*, but
only by a *forensic and civil* one; not by a *relaxation of right*, but only by
a *relaxation of punishment*; nor was it immune from guilt in the internal
forum of conscience, but only immune from punishment in the civil and
external forum.

XX. The precept concerning love of one's neighbor cannot be said to have been
corrected by Christ with respect to its *object*, as though the object of love
were narrower under the Old Testament—namely, a *neighbor* who shared the same
covenant and religion—but broader in the New Testament because it extends to
all men universally, even enemies. The command of love is the same in both,
with the same object, form, end, and love, both *extensively* with respect to
its *object* and *intensively* with respect to its *manner* or *degree*. The
*neighbor* whose love the law prescribes means not only a Jew and fellow
countryman, but any foreigner and even an enemy. In this sense the *Egyptians*
are called *neighbors* in Exodus 11:2 and the *Philistines* in Judges 14:20.
Thus in Leviticus 19:18 even an enemy is reckoned under the name of neighbor
when it is commanded that they “not avenge themselves or keep anger against
him.” In Proverbs 25:21–22 Solomon commands one “to feed a hungry enemy and
give drink to a thirsty one,” as in Elisha's example (2 Kings 6:22). Indeed,
in Exodus 23:4–5 God commands us *to help an enemy's beast and lead it back
onto the road*. Finally, the neighbor means the one against whom we must not
bear false witness

[Printed p. 24 | Internet Archive PDF 52 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

or covet his wife and goods, which is said not merely of some people but of
all. Nor does it stand in the way that in Matthew 5:43–44 the words “You shall
love your neighbor and hate your enemy” are quoted as though drawn from the
law, because Christ there criticizes an addition of the Pharisaic gloss, not
an imperfection of the law, which neither has this now nor ever had it. The law
of retaliation given under the Old Testament does not restrict the precept of
love, because it treats the magistrate's right, not the duties of love; the
punishments a judge inflicts upon the guilty, not the vengeance a private
person seeks.

XXI. If the Israelites are forbidden in Exodus 34:12 and Deuteronomy 7:2 to
enter marriage alliances, make a covenant, or even show mercy to the seven
peoples of Canaan: 1. It does not follow that hatred of them is commanded,
because there
is a middle course between friendship, which brings an association of minds
and duties, and hatred. Nor is mercy toward them absolutely prohibited, but
only *in a qualified sense* by reason of the vengeance God had entrusted
against those who were to be killed at God's express command, and this so that
they would not become a snare to God's people. 2. The command does not concern
hatred but destruction, because God had devoted them to destruction as his
enemies on account of their horrible crimes. 3. This did not have to be done
absolutely, but only when they had refused peace (Deut. 20:10). 4. This also
holds in the New Testament, not only when we are forbidden to pray for those
who sin unto death (1 John 5:16) and to greet one who brings another doctrine
(2 John), but also by the very fact that wars are lawful.

XXII. 2. The precept of love was not corrected by Christ with respect to its
*manner and intensity*, whether we regard God or our neighbor. With respect to
God, there can be no greater love than that by which we are commanded to love
God as the highest good, and thus not only because of the benefits he bestows
upon us but also because of his excellence, than which no greater can be
given. With respect to our neighbor, his love can receive no higher degree
because we are commanded to love him as ourselves. Nor does the gospel's
command that we even lay down our lives for the brothers (1 John 3:16) prove
that we ought to love our neighbor more than ourselves, so that the manner of
Christian love is greater than the love commanded by the law. 1. The command
to lay down one's life for a brother was not unknown under the Old Testament,
as is clear from the example of Moses wishing to die for the people, David
wishing to die for Absalom (2 Sam. 18), and the three mighty men prepared to
die for David (chapter 23). It was not unknown even to the pagans, to which
belong the examples of those who devoted themselves to death for their
country, and of friends who did not refuse to die for friends, such as Damon
and Pythias and Pylades and Orestes, as Paul himself acknowledges in Romans
5:6–7. 2. The point of comparison in “as ourselves” is not to be understood
κανονικῶς, *as a rule*, but παραδειγματικῶς, *as an example*. It does not
provide the *measure and rule* according to which we must love our neighbor,
but only an *example*, and indeed one than which there commonly is none more
intense, because by nature we are inclined to love ourselves (Eph. 5:29).
Thus one who lays down his life for a brother does not love his brother more
than himself, but as himself, because he does what he would wish done for
himself in a similar case. 3. One who lays down his life for a brother indeed
loves his own life less than another's welfare. But he does so both because
another's welfare is far more precious than temporal life, and especially
because God's glory and indeed our own salvation—which we must love more than
our own life—are involved in it. Hence this life is freely to be poured out so
that eternal life may be obtained.

XXIII. Christ does not call the precept of love *new* in John 13:34 and *the
law of Christ* in Galatians 6:2 *absolutely and simply* with respect to the
*substance of the command*, which was the same in both testaments. He calls it
new *in a qualified sense* and *comparatively* with respect to its *manner*,
because Christ *renewed* it and restored it to its former splendor after men's
evil conduct had rendered it almost obsolete, and because he illustrated and
sanctioned it with *new reasons and*

[Printed p. 25 | Internet Archive PDF 53 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*motives*, such as both Christ's example and his greatest benefits. For if he
loved us so greatly, we in turn ought—as love is repaid with love—to love both
him and our brothers for his sake as a return of love (1 John 4:11). It is
also new with respect to *duration*, because it is perpetual and eternal.

XXIV. Nothing can be said to have been added by Christ to the essentials of
the precepts of the moral law. First, there is no *new manner of praying*
defined by the Lord's Prayer. The precept to worship and adore God necessarily
contains prayer, and although Christ instituted the Lord's Prayer with respect
to its form, with respect to its *matter and the things requested* it has
nothing that is not expressed in the prayers of the saints in the Old
Testament. Nor is there *new worship of Christ* and of the Father in him. The
worship of Christ was also commanded often in the Old Testament (Ps. 2:12;
45:11; 97:7; Exod. 23) and confirmed by various examples: Abraham (Gen. 18:23
and following), Jacob (Gen. 48:16), and Daniel (Dan. 9:17 and following).
Indeed, the fathers offered no prayer except at least implicitly in Christ's
name, insofar as it rested upon the divine promises whose foundation was the
Messiah (2 Cor. 1:20).

XXV. Second, nothing was added by the prohibition not only of the *worship* of
*images and idols*, but also of their *manufacture* and of *frequenting* places
sacred to idols. This very thing had already been commanded under the Old
Testament when the idols of the nations were ordered to be burned, their
altars destroyed, and their groves cut down (Exod. 23:24; Deut. 12:2–3).
Indeed, it was forbidden to use the names of idols in speech (Exod. 23:13; Ps.
16:4) or to cultivate association with idolaters that was harmful to religion
(Deut. 7:2–3).

XXVI. Third, nothing was added by calling Christ as a witness, since he is the
searcher of hearts (Rev. 2:23). For just as Christ was worshiped in the Old
Testament as coeternal and coessential with the Father, so people swore by his
name (Isa. 45:23; Rom. 14:11). Nor was anything added by an absolute
prohibition of oaths. For, as was said before, Christ's teaching in Matthew 5
must not be understood absolutely and simply, but in a limited way according
to the subject matter: those rash and perverse formulas of swearing by
creatures that were used in daily speech. Fourth, nothing was added by *the
abrogation of the Sabbath*, because, as will be proved afterward, it was
abrogated only with respect to what was ceremonial, not what was moral.

XXVII. Fifth, nothing was added by the honor of magistrates, masters, and
husbands, who ought to be honored like fathers. For it is
certain that even in the Old Testament the name of fathers also includes
magistrates, lords or masters, and husbands (Gen. 41:43; 45:8; Job 29:16; 2
Kings 5:13), who ought to use their power moderately and paternally, not
despotically (Deut. 5:20–21; 17:15; 24:1; Mal. 2:14–15). Sixth, nothing was
added by the *prohibition of anger and injuries* toward a brother and of *all
vengeance whatsoever*. With respect to the former, by prohibiting murder the
law prohibits whatever leads to it, both outward injuries and abusive words
and the inward motions of anger and hatred. With respect to the latter, Christ
forbids only private vengeance in accordance with the law, not public
vengeance, which cannot be separated from the magistrate's office (Rom.
13:1–2).

XXVIII. Seventh, nothing was added by prohibiting a lustful look at a woman,
divorce except for the cause of adultery, and every kind of fornication and
filthy speech. All these are already contained under the law, which embraces
an entire genus under one species by forbidding every impurity that displays
itself either in deed or in words. The tenth precept concerning desire
confirms this same point even more strongly. Divorce, however, as was said
above, was not permitted or commanded by the moral law, but only tolerated by
the forensic law because of the people's σκληροκαρδίαν, *hardness of heart*.

XXIX. Eighth, nothing was added by the prohibition of avarice and luxury in
food and clothing, because both were already forbidden in the law: the former
in Exodus 20:17; 22:25; Psalm 119:36; and Isaiah 3:17, and the latter in
Deuteronomy 21:20; Proverbs 23:20; and Isaiah 3:16–17; 5:11–12. Ninth, nothing
was added by the prohibition of every lie, detraction, and malicious
accusation, because
this is also contained in the law and forbidden elsewhere (Ps. 15:3; 101:5).
Tenth, nothing was added by the command not to set one's mind upon a coveted
thing, because Paul teaches in Romans 7:7–8 that all desire is condemned in
general, and therefore all its species and degrees. In a word, all the
additions proposed by our adversaries either are contained under the precepts
themselves—as species under a genus, parts under a whole, effects under
causes, and consequences under antecedents, and conversely—or are
heterogeneous to the precepts, foreign to their purpose and subject, or even
contrary to them, as is clear from what has been said.

XXX. What is in no way contained under love for God and one's neighbor,
neither explicitly nor implicitly, neither with respect to the letter nor the
meaning, is rightly said to have been added to the law. But *self-denial,
bearing the cross, and the imitation of Christ* are contained in the law, if
not expressly, at least implicitly and with respect to its meaning, because we
cannot love God with the whole heart without being prepared to deny ourselves,
bear the cross for him, and imitate Christ, who is true God.

XXXI. Although the law expressly treats only duties toward God and one's
neighbor, duties toward ourselves are necessarily contained under them. We
cannot love God with the whole heart without holiness of every kind, nor will
we rightly love our neighbor as ourselves unless we regulate our self-love by
God's law.

XXXII. Christ is compared with Moses in Hebrews 2:1–3, not in legislation as
though he were a new Lawgiver more perfect than Moses. In this respect they
are instead contrasted when the law is said to have been given through Moses,
but grace and truth through Christ (John 1:17). Rather, they are compared
partly in the kind of doctrine, which there is legal and here evangelical,
and partly in dignity and glory, in which Christ far surpasses Moses.

XXXIII. Although the law does not command repentance *directly and formally*,
because it gives the sinner no hope of pardon and repentance thus properly
belongs to the gospel, nevertheless it refers to repentance *materially and
indirectly*, because in it is prescribed the rule for living well and pleasing
God, which is the aim of repentance.
