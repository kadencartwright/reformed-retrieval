CHAPTER XXXIV.

From the Premises He Infers, First, That It Belongs to Magistrates to Restore
Discipline.

If, then, reformed discipline is so splendid, so pious, and so necessary, who
does not see that it is the magistrates’ duty to awaken from their torpor and
think seriously about restoring it? Martin Bucer so admonished Edward VI about
this duty that Scultingius said, “He stirred up the Anglo-Calvinists, who are
better than the Puritans.” But if Episcopal government had to be reformed by
the magistrate under Edward VI, how much more now? Least of all should Hosius
be heard when he says that the reformation of the Church belongs not at all to
the magistrate, but to the Bishops. For by deplorable expe—

—rience we have already learned sufficiently that the Bishops themselves are
those who most need to be reformed. With this in view, amid the darkness of
Papism, those who were more clear-sighted always implored the magistrates’
aid. Thus Theodoric of Niem summoned the Emperor to reform the ecclesiastical
estate; Franciscus Zabarella described it as the Emperor’s duty to convene a
council against the Pope lest the Church be endangered; and, finally, John
Pico exhorted Emperor Maximilian to take upon himself the care of reforming
the Church in accordance with the office laid upon him.

These last examples also rebuke another error which, though the Pontifical
error was exploded long ago, has grown up among the Evangelicals themselves,
whether through supine negligence or contempt for divine things, and is no
less destructive to God’s Church than that former error. For although princes
now know well that the right of reforming belongs to them, they nevertheless
think they have discharged their duty properly if they delegate the entire
care of reforming the Church to Bishops whom they suppose pious and have
themselves chosen. King Joash did not act so. He not only rebuked the delaying
priests, saying, “Why do you not restore the house of God?” 2 Kings 12:7, but
also took upon himself the care of restoring it.

Thus Bishop Leo wrote to Emperor Leo: “You must understand without delay,
Emperor, that royal power has been conferred upon you not only for the
government of the world but especially for the defense of the Church.” From
this it appears that the care of the Church should no more be delegated to
others than the care of the commonwealth. Isidore similarly says: “The princes
of the age shall know that they owe God an account for the Church which they
undertake to protect from God. For whether the peace and discipline of the
Church is increased or dissolved, he who entrusted his Church to their power
will require an account from them.” Augustine likewise says: “They marvel
that Christian powers are stirred against the detestable destroyers of the
Church. But if they were not stirred, how would they render God an account of
their rule? Let your charity attend to what I say, because this belongs to the
kings of the age—Christian kings, namely—that in their times they should
always desire their mother the Church, from whom they were spiritually born,
to be at peace.”

These testimonies show that a prince must render an account to God for
ecclesiastical affairs just as for civil affairs. Their reformation and
correction must therefore be cared for by the princes themselves, not by the
Bishops. But, you will say, nothing from this supports the reformation which
we demand. Lous exhorts princes “to keep it out”; John Whitgift, “to suppress
and extirpate it.” Yet they rely upon no reasons except those already
answered. “The Sanhedrin is worse than the Papacy,” says Lous; and Whitgift
condenses everything Zwingli said against the Anabaptists in order to stir the
magistrates’ minds against that discipline, which differs from Papal tyranny
and Anabaptist confusion as much as Christ differs from Antichrist and, to
speak with Tertullian, beehives from wasps’ nests.

I fear that princes who listen to these men will fall, to the ruin of the
whole Church, into the error of Theodosius, who in other respects was a most
pious Emperor. The Bishops admonished him to restrain the tyranny of Flavian.
For it was unjust that the man who had driven civil tyrants from the necks of

Is it fitting that matters should proceed in this way in our time? Let the
Princes hear Christ, the King of kings. He did not identify himself as such in
Revelation 1:5 for nothing, but so that he might make known to Kings that not
they but he is the supreme King in the Church, while kings are only his
servants and ought to obey his word. He entrusted to their care and fidelity
that polity of the Church which must be fostered, not excluded; defended, not
extirpated. For the English Church is truly the antitype of Laodicea. Indeed,

half closed, Revelation 3:20, these words of David now have their place in

weighty words: “I counsel, moreover, that you bring discipline fully into your
Churches as soon as it can be done. For if it is not received at the beginning,
when men burn with desire for the Gospel, it will not easily be admitted when
some coldness afterwards creeps in, as happens. That labor without it is
fruitless may be shown to you by the example of very many Churches. From the
foundations of their institution they were unwilling to submit to this most
wholesome yoke, and afterwards could never, as regards morals and life, be
brought into order by any just rule. Hence it comes about—and I say this with
great sorrow—that nearly everything is insecure and threatens ruin on every
side. It is therefore a serious loss and the sure destruction of Churches if

CHAPTER XXXV.

The Second Corollary: Bishops Must Not Contend by Possession Alone, Lest They
Prove Themselves Obstinate, Impatient, Proud, Destructive, and Persecutors.

Second, he addresses the Bishops themselves and their supporters, who are
especially hostile and bitterly opposed to the reformation of discipline. He
first commands them to cease contending by possession, for that has always
been both the custom and the disease of evil men. Thus Livy disapproved what
the elder Dionysius said about a tyrant: that he was accustomed to leave his
kingdom not while riding a horse, but only when dragged away by his feet.
Robert Capito, Bishop of Lincoln, was displeased that the Church of his time
could not be freed from the Egyptian bondage of the Pope and prelates except
at the point of the sword and with bloodshed.

Today, too, a certain Florentine nobleman is displeased by what he relates in
his preface to Guicciardini’s *Paralipomena* concerning Paul III: “More
plainly and frankly than cautiously, when conversation among his intimates
once turned to that power of his, Paul III was accustomed to laugh at those
men as scholastics ignorant of affairs, who so anxiously derived it from
Christ. He said that possession of it alone was the best and strongest title.”
Leo X held the same opinion as Paul III, but was less plain about it: he
preferred to contend by possession alone rather than openly profess possession
alone. Thus our Whitaker says: “One year before Luther, Leo X provided at
session 11 of the Lateran Council that preachers must not preach about the
coming of Antichrist. But if the Lord should reveal certain things to some,
which he promises through Amos that he will do, they must not publish them
before the Apostolic See has examined them or, if that cannot conveniently be
done, the Bishop with others; whoever acts contrary to this must be
excommunicated.”

What does this have to do with the English Bishops, you will ask? Very much.
Some are plainer and more frank with Paul III, for they do not derive the
Episcopate from Christ but contend by human right and prescription alone.
Others, though they derive the Episcopate from Apostolic ordination,
nevertheless establish it as mutable and so are accustomed to press possession
above all. This may be seen in Downam, who from this argues that our boldness
is intolerable: we wish Bishops to be changed in that kingdom where Bishops

writing, anything revealed by the Lord and his Word to anyone through anyone.
I do not say that their government can thereby savor of a suspicion of

Bishop and approved by him, it is judged worthy of censure to reproach

or to refute a novel falsehood preached by someone else.

What of the fact that they proudly refused a disputation offered by the
ministers on the fairest conditions? What of the fact that when men are
summoned to their courts, they are accustomed to say that they were called not
to the schools to dispute, but to the tribunal to be judged? What of the fact
that they remove from places, strip of goods, punish with prisons, and strike
with excommunications all who do not submit to their will?

Is this not to contend by possession rather than by reason? But I see that
what was included in the canon law from Augustine is all too true: “How can
humility exist where falsehood reigns, especially when circumstances are so
disposed that a proud title covered with fraud can be defended only with a
proud brow and even a proud arm?” Indeed, since, as Primasius has it, “no one
sins more dangerously than the man who defends sins even by a word,” and since
these men endeavor most dangerously to defend their sin not only by a word
but also with an uplifted hand, let them consider themselves admonished by
Jerome’s words: “Take up Christian severity for correcting this work, and sing
what is called a *palinode*.”

That this might be done more fittingly, I would have them consider within
their own minds how unworthy a thing it is that those who wish to be regarded
as pillars of the Church should deserve to hear with Cicero’s Anaxagoras the
saying: “I do not know how it is that most men prefer to remain in error rather
than abandon an opinion which they have once embraced.” Aristotle once said:
“For the sake of preserving truth, it is right to overturn even our own
doctrines, especially since we are Philosophers. For though both are dear, it
is nevertheless a noble thing to value truth more highly.” Let Tertullian’s
saying come to mind here: “If a pagan man was so considerate, how much more
ought we who are called priests to be?” He was moved because he was a
philosopher. Will we not be moved because we are Christians, indeed priests of
Christians? “Away with obstinacy; there is no shame in passing to better
things,” says Ambrose. Augustine rightly goes further: “It belongs to great
wisdom for a man to retract what he formerly spoke wrongly.”

The English Bishops must therefore change themselves after Peter’s example,

Third, the English Bishops’ contention recalled above breathes no slight pride,
not only against their fellow presbyters but also against Christ himself.
Caligula is said to have removed the head of Jupiter and put his own in its
place. In the same way, these men have removed the discipline instituted by
Christ, substituted their own, and abolished the presbyters’ authority as
heads. Christ established the presbyters as heads of the Church, so that the
Bishops alone might be regarded as the Church’s supreme and principal heads.

Those familiar words are well known: “What are you doing in my house, you who
are not mine? Who has placed this idol in my house?” Does it seem a very light
thing to you to usurp a place and rank in Christ’s Church against Christ’s
will? Chrysostom rightly says: “He who does not use sacred Scripture but
ascends by another way is not a shepherd but a thief.” Their subjugating the
presbyters to themselves therefore falls back upon Christ himself—unless,
perhaps, we suppose Jerome to have erred. For he says: “Let the man who does
not wish presbyters to do what is commanded by God say who is greater than
Christ. At your command, O most merciless priests, a presbyter has only the
name of presbyter; he does not retain the fullness and perfection which
belongs to his consecration. As we read, presbyters themselves are called
Bishops, according to the Scripture, ‘who placed you as Bishops to govern the
Church of God.’ But proud priests hate this name in the presbyters, for they
do not wish to be what Christ was, who washed his disciples’ feet.”

Gregory the Great has similar words from the *antigraphon* when he disputes
against the unlawful Episcopate of Constantinople. “What answer,” you ask,
“will you give at the examination of the last judgment to Christ, the head of
the universal Church, when you strive to subject all his members to yourself
by the title ‘universal’?” John sought nothing other, as Fyldus rightly says,
than that ministers and Bishops should believe that they all depended upon him
and that their ordinations flowed from him. While the English Bishops seek the
same thing among the English ministers, do they not subject Christ’s members
to themselves contrary to Christ’s will? In Gregory’s judgment, therefore,
they will have no answer to give Christ on the day of judgment.

If Jerome had seen these things, would he not blaze with anger? He complains

one thing alone: they prohibited a presbyter from preaching in a Bishop’s
presence. Let his own words be heard so that it may be understood how
tyrannical Episcopal government in England is. “What, I ask, O priests, is
this honor of yours, that you arrogate dominion to yourselves, since through
your power the diligence owed to God is taken from the pastors, a kind of

In Jerome’s time presbyters were pastors of the Churches; now in England the
Bishop is pastor of the whole Diocese, while the presbyters are only his
delegates and assistants. In Jerome’s age it was monstrous for Bishops alone
to rule in the Church; now in England the Bishops alone rule. This is so not
only because a presbyter may not preach without their permission—which,
nevertheless, is for them alone to rule according to Jerome—but because, once
the presbyter has been excluded, they govern alone, which had not yet happened
in Jerome’s time.

Jerome judged that calamity grew among the flocks and the Lord’s patrimony
suffered loss through the Bishops because diligence in preaching had been
taken from the presbyter in a Bishop’s presence. Are the Bishops not much more
harmful in England today? After more than two hundred Churches were recently
despoiled of their pastors, the Bishops substituted men who for the most part
were either unlearned, frivolous, of less approved life, nonresident,
negligent, or certainly asleep. This, then, is the first address of reformed
discipline to the English Bishops: they must cease contending for their
possession in the manner recalled above.
