V. 13. Miseries to his father is a foolish son; and the contentions of a wife are a continual dripping.

In בן (*son*) there is a synecdoche. Likewise in אביו (*to his father*) and אשת (*of a wife*.

The purpose is that the Holy Spirit should stimulate us to the search for a pious wife and to the religious education of sons.

More often in the Proverbs the wickedness of foolish sons has been proclaimed as exceedingly troublesome to parents. For since, even amid the highest obedience of children, parents are tormented by grave care arising from education, with how great a torment must those be vexed who have wicked children, especially when this comes about through the parents’ fault? But now a comparison seems to be instituted between the trouble which disobedient sons bring and that which a contentious wife presents. More evil, however, seems to be in a contentious wife than in foolish sons.

And indeed the Prophet frankly acknowledges that a foolish son is a thing full of troubles, whence he uses the plural number, which often amounts to the superlative degree. But if a comparison is instituted to determine which of two evils is worse, a contentious woman is to be preferred among evils to a foolish son. The reason is that she is like a continual dripping, which gradually and step by step eats away her husband, as a drop eats away a stone. And just as houses rot and are corrupted by continual dripping, neither interrupted at all nor intermittent, so, etc.

The reason, moreover, why he likens her to continual dripping is designated more openly elsewhere, as we shall see, but is indicated obscurely here: namely, because he cannot be separated and parted from her. A foolish son he can indeed drive from his house, and indeed he ought to expel him according to the law, Deut. 21.18. But a contentious wife who is not an adulteress he cannot send away from himself and his bed, much less from the house. Thus it could be doubted whether a greater evil threatens the husband from an adulteress than from a contentious woman who cannot be recalled to good conduct; just as it can be doubted whether it is more desirable to be struck once by a heavy blow or pierced more sharply than to be battered daily, and indeed hourly, by dull pains and lighter blows.

But if a morose wife by herself brings so great an evil upon her husband, it is grievous to have both children and wife disobedient together. Therefore effort must be given, etc., as above in the purpose of the Proverb.

All these things can be applied to a difficult and morose husband.

V. 14. A house and substance are an inheritance from fathers; but from Jehovah is an understanding woman.

In אבות (*fathers*) there is a synecdoche of the part: fathers for ancestors, both male and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 409 | printed columns 792-793]

female. In הון (*substance*) there is likewise a synecdoche for all revenues; so in אשה (*woman,*) for one joined in marriage.

The purpose is to commend the singular benefit of a wife who fears God, and to correct the error of men who judge too meanly.

In the preceding Proverb the Prophet adorned, from the opposite of a foolish and unwise wife, the benefit of an excellent and prudent wife. But in this place he magnifies that benefit by comparison with a lesser good, which nevertheless is valued more among mortals than a pious and prudent wife.

Here a somewhat difficult scruple confronts us: how Solomon in this Proverb sets God in opposition to ancestors, referring the benefit of house and substance to ancestors, but the gift of a prudent and pious wife to God, by this very appearance taking away and detracting from God what supremely belongs to him. For every perfect gift and every good giving descends from above, from the Father of lights, Jas. 1.17.

Since, therefore, house, income, and revenues are among good things and flow from God’s beneficence, it is necessary. To this are referred the frequent statements in the Scriptures, especially in Moses and Joshua, concerning the inheritance of the land of Canaan attributed by God to Israel in general, but to the individual tribes in particular. And even individually it is said to David that God had given him both wives and the house of his lord Saul, 2 Sam. 12. I answer that for two reasons the gift of a house, etc., is assigned in this place to men, not to God. The former is to teach that so great an interval exists between a house, etc., and a prudent wife that a house, etc., in comparison with a pious wife is a human good, while a pious wife in comparison with them is a certain divine good. Not that a house, etc., simply and purely is not also God’s gift. Secondly, because there is a certain greater and more clear-sighted providence of God in the obtaining of pious wives than in the manner of obtaining an inheritance.

For an inheritance generally descends to children either by the law of the fatherland, by custom, or by the singular love of parents, so that long before an heir is written into a testament or legal instrument, who he is may be known. But in the obtaining of a wife it very often happens that those are joined in marriage who, on account of the interval between their places and the dissimilarity of their condition, seemed least of all to be joined in the judgment either of parents or spouses. Thus the gift of God proceeds more immediately from him than an inheritance does.

Here, therefore, let us learn our error, whereby we indeed set a great price upon the possession of house and patrimony, while we reckon the merits of a prudent and pious wife common and contemptible. For how many will be found who in marriage do not have the greatest regard for dowry, lineage, or beauty rather than for piety and prudence? If she is splendidly endowed, born in an illustrious station, if she is beautiful, immediately all vie in seeking her, little concerned about piety. By contrast, if she is pious and prudent, and bare of dowry and patrimony, she lies despised and scorned. Yet here it must be noted that a pious and prudent woman is to be preferred by far both to patrimony and revenues, and indeed to resources and riches joined and associated with patrimony.

At Eccles. 7.13 a certain prudence and skill in the administration of affairs is preferred far above money; and Solomon teaches that it is better to lie hidden in the shade of wisdom than in that of money, since knowledge often preserves a man alive when money does not do so but casts him into peril and danger of life. Therefore a woman prudent in civil matters and skilled in the administration of a household is to be preferred to a rich woman; much more is that wisdom to be preferred which is heavenly and joined with the fear of God, especially if they coincide in one woman.

Hence it follows that by a certain special consideration one’s own prudent wife is to be sought from God by prayers,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 410 | printed columns 794-795]

and all the things said concerning a wife are likewise to be transferred to a God-fearing and skilful husband, since the reasoning is equal for both.

Here someone may ask whether an evil and foolish wife is likewise from the Lord. I answer that she indeed is from God, but is sent upon the ungodly under the designation of punishment and upon the pious for the sake of trial; and therefore this evil must be borne calmly and patiently.

Vers. 15. Sluggishness causes one to fall into a deep sleep; and the deceitful soul will hunger.

In נפש (*soul*) there is a synecdoche of the part.

The purpose is that both idleness and a fraudulent occupation and manner of living drive a man to extreme poverty and want.

Common reason teaches that honest and moderate labour sharpens and stirs the spirits, but that through leisure and idleness the spirits are lulled and dulled. For heaviness of the head and an abundance of humours in the brain arise from leisure. Hence it is that Solomon at Prov. 6 reproaches the idle and sluggish man for his somnolence, vers. 9.10, and from his immoderate sleep makes sport of him. Hence it is that there have not been lacking among the wise of this world those who affirmed that the life of the idle and leisured man is a certain death, and that the idle man in a certain manner buries himself alive.

Then from the latter part of this Proverb the meaning of *hunger* is defined, which must be carried back to this point so that it may teach what is the fruit both of sluggishness and immoderate sleep. For since leisure is the parent of poverty and hunger is the offspring of poverty, it comes about that, since immoderate sleep summons poverty, it must also summon hunger. Hence at Prov. 6.11 poverty is taught to creep suddenly upon the idle man like a traveller, and to assail him with a certain inescapable force, as an armed soldier is accustomed to invade one who is naked and unarmed. Hence also it is that at 20.13 he says that he who loves sleep becomes poor.

From this it seems to follow that the man who lies awake for every art and opportunity of becoming rich will live in an abundance of all things, and that the hand which is restless will be enriched. By no means, says Solomon, if he acts deceitfully, if he does not conduct himself honestly and incorruptly in increasing the household estate. For the road to poverty through very great and wicked labours is just as short as through leisure, if the occupation is unlawful. If thieves, robbers, and pirates, who in a brief moment load themselves with great booty and spoils, considered these things within themselves; if those who violate the Lord’s day in order to acquire some little profit did so; if those who in commerce, purchase, or sale overreach their neighbours, even their brethren, did so, they would never weary themselves in grasping advantages of this kind.

Therefore the only method and short path of becoming rich is for someone to occupy himself both diligently and incorruptly in an honest and lawful craft.

Vers. 16. He who keeps the commandment keeps his own soul; but he who despises his ways will die.

In נפשו (*his soul*) there is a synecdoche of the part. In דרכיו (*his ways*) there is a synecdoche of the kind for God’s command.

The purpose is that a man either saves or destroys himself according as he diligently observes God’s commands in the direction of his own life or dissolutely neglects them.

The first form of speaking is to be observed, in which *the commandment* in general is put for God’s commandment. For that form declares that God’s command alone is worthy of obtaining the name of commandment. For just as at vers. 14 inheritance and substance,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 411 | printed columns 796-797]

in comparison with a pious wife, do not merit being called God’s gift, so in this place the commands of men, in comparison with God’s command, are unworthy of being held among commands. Hence it is that sacred scripture alone obtains the name *Scripture*, and in common speech by *the word* we understand *the word of God*. Hence in Hosea the King’s commands are contemptuously called צו (with 2 letters). This indeed is established from the fact that the commands of Princes are to be kept only insofar as they properly agree with God’s command. As far as they agree with God’s command, they are fortified by God’s own authority; but if, etc.

Concerning this command of God, it is commanded that we observe it; and by that very fact we are bidden both properly to know it and, once known, to retain it in memory. For no one will observe a commandment which he either does not know or does not retain in memory. Then, because he adds that the person who keeps the commandment saves himself and his life, he teaches with what care and with what enkindled and aroused zeal he should do this. Elsewhere the diligent guarding of a thing is likened to guarding the pupil of the eye, which men also guard studiously and solicitously. But the guarding of our life, or soul, is commended to us much more, especially since, when this life is lost through contempt of the commandment, the loss of the future life also occurs at the same time.

There are certain commands of men for whose observance a great reward is proposed, but for whose violation either no punishment or a light one is appointed. But in obeying God’s precepts, just as the greatest reward is proposed, so for their violation the gravest punishment is declared, namely, eternal death.

Then it must be noted that this commandment is to be applied as a rule to the transaction of all actions and affairs, so that there is nothing so either great or small that it is not examined by the commandment as by a canon.

Finally, it must be noted that the person who does not observe the commandment holds it in contempt, even if he properly understands it and properly remembers it, since contempt for the commandment is here opposed to observance of the commandment. Hence neglect of the command must necessarily be contained under contempt. See Heb. 2.3.

Vers. 17. He lends to Jehovah who graciously bestows upon the poor; and He will repay that man’s benefit to him.

In מלוה (*lending*) there is a metaphor transferred from creditors to God on account of the benefit which the person who assists the poor receives from the Lord, just as a creditor receives it from a grateful and mindful debtor.

The purpose is to rebuke men’s dryness toward the poor, which arises from this: they think everything by which they show favour to a poor man perishes.

What constricts and hardens our inward parts toward the poor, turns our eyes away from looking upon them, and closes our hand against assisting them is that they possess nothing from which they can favour us in return. For in showing favour we always direct an eye toward remuneration, just as a hireling does toward wages; if none shines upon us, the fountain of our kindness is immediately stopped up. And indeed this kind of men is hard and iron-like. There are others of somewhat milder disposition who would gladly either give or lend, provided that they were completely assured of the return of what they give or lend. The poor are no more aided by these than by the former men, who pursue gain. But Christ numbers this kind of men in the class of the ungodly and sinners, Luke 6.34.

In order to pluck this deeply implanted error utterly from our minds, God sets various likenesses before our eyes,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 412 | printed columns 798-799]

as though they were images, by which he may persuade our minds that the benefit conferred upon the needy does not perish: that they entrust seed to the earth, and indeed to exceedingly fertile and well-watered earth, 2 Cor. 9.6.9; Eccles. 11.1; likewise that they heap up treasures placed most safely in an impregnable place, Matt. 6.20; moreover, that they procure friends for themselves who are ready to receive them into eternal dwellings, Luke 16.9. But I hardly know whether there is any more illustrious, effective, or forceful argument for accomplishing this than the one applied in this place, where the person who favours a poor man with some thing is said to lend to Jehovah.

Hence it is that Christ commands us to give to everyone who asks, Luke 6.30, and to lend, and to receive nothing back, not even the principal; and at Luke 14 he teaches the host by whom he had been invited that, when arranging a banquet, he should invite as guests not rich neighbours, as is the received custom. Here the reason he sets forth is memorable: lest, he says, they invite you as a guest in turn and return like for like. He attaches as the reason for not inviting them the very thing by which alone men are impelled to invite them as guests. By contrast, he commands the poor to be invited, substituting as the reason for inviting them the very thing by which alone men are deterred from inviting them: namely, that they have nothing from which they might make compensation.

What then? Will that which I either give or lend perish, you ask? By no means, I say. For if you are so hard that you are willing to give nothing without hope of profit, you are fortified by a promise that you will receive manifold fruit, namely, a hundred for one, Matt. 19.29, which is interest hitherto unheard of.

It commonly happens, however, that poor people are found who, restrained by shame from begging, nevertheless desire to receive some money as a loan. Do you refuse to lend this unless security is given? Behold, you have as surety God himself, the eternal One, who in this Proverb offers himself as sponsor for the poor man. What? Does he not seem to you certain and sufficient, to whom alone belongs whatever gold or silver exists anywhere, whatever land or revenues are found anywhere? Is he insufficiently trustworthy, who has never broken faith; who even freely gave what he never promised or undertook to give who is beneficent even toward his enemies? You have Scripture as his written bond, and the sacraments as seals of the promise.

To this is added that, besides the fact that you make a bargain in this way, it is for your singular honour to have God in some manner indebted to you. For who does not reckon it an honour to himself if he has lent something to a most wealthy and powerful King which the latter cannot conveniently do without? What of the fact that God reckons what is conferred upon the poor not only as lent to himself, but as though given? Matt. 25.4 [printed thus].

But it is asked, when will God compensate this benefit? Often in this life, Ps. 112.9, but always in the resurrection of the just, Luke 14, namely, when it will be most in your interest. There are many who redeem estates and revenues at great cost which they themselves will not enjoy for many years, indeed which will not devolve upon their own relatives, children, or grandchildren unless they themselves have died. How much more fitting is it that we rest in expectation of the divine *conpensation* [the Latin prints *conpensationis*], which is most certain?
