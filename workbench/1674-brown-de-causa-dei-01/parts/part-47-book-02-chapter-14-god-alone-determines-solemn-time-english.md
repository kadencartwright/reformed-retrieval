CHAPTER XIV

God Alone Has the Power to Determine This Solemn, Necessary, and Sufficient
Time

That this power belongs to God alone—the power to determine *principal,
solemn, necessary, and ordinarily sufficient time*—is abundantly clear from
what was said in the three preceding chapters. For if this power to determine
this time for themselves belongs neither to individuals, nor to the magistrate,
whether pagan or Christian, nor to the rulers of the church on behalf of
others, then this power must belong to God alone. We proved before that this
time must necessarily be determined, and no one distinct from those already
named can be imagined by whom it could be determined. Nevertheless, we will
confirm this with other arguments by way of superabundance.

*First*, the power to determine *this principal and solemn time* belongs only
to him who can *by right* make necessary to religion a time which was not
necessary before. But God alone can do this by right. Therefore, and so forth.
There is no need for us to prove the *major* premise, since it is sufficiently
clear from what was said before that this principal and solemn time is necessary
to religion. And if it is not necessary antecedently to the divine
determination, it must become necessary through that determination. That it is
not necessary before the divine determination is plainly evident from this:
this necessity depends upon God alone. For he alone is the author both of
religion and of time, as well as of this necessary connection between such a
time and religion. The *minor* premise needs no proof, since to him alone
belongs by right the power to make this *relation* of time to worship and to
change it at pleasure. He once made time necessary. He alone has dominion over
consciences and can bind them to observe such a time.

*Second*, this power belongs only to him who can *by right* make sufficient to
religion a time which was not sufficient before. But God alone can do this by
right. Therefore, and so forth. This *major* premise is no less certain than
the preceding one, for this solemn time whose determination we are now
discussing is ordinarily sufficient for religion. But no time can be called
ordinarily sufficient for religion in itself, or antecedently to every divine
determination. The *minor* premise can be proved from the following: 1. God
alone has the power to declare what time is sufficient for his glory. 2. He
alone can constitute that time. 3. It deserves to be called sufficient only
with respect to his acceptance. 4. He alone can institute the time necessary
for promoting the eternal good of our soul. 6. He alone can determine this
sufficient time infallibly and without error. 6. He alone can satisfy tender
consciences concerning this sufficiency of the determined time.

*Third*, this power belongs only to him who can institute time for performing
both domestic and solitary exercises. But God alone can institute this time.
Therefore, and so forth. The truth of the *major* premise is clear from the
fact that this time, as was demonstrated above, must have such a length of
duration that on it may be performed not

[1674 logical printed p. 329 | IA leaf 347 | PDF p. 347]

only the exercises of public worship but also those of domestic and solitary
worship. The *minor* premise is certain, because: 1. God alone can give
positive precepts even to households and private people, and also to private
persons with respect to solitary duties. 2. He alone can search the inward
secrets of the heart and summon the inmost recesses of the soul to examination,
since he is the searcher of the heart; and he alone is Lord of the conscience,
can give commands to consciences immediately, and can bind them to obedience.

*Fourth*, this power belongs only to him who can authoritatively bind everyone
to dedicate the determined time to divine worship, whether public or private,
and to spend it piously. But God alone can do this. Therefore, and so forth.
The truth of the *major* premise is clear from the fact that this time necessary
to religion is determined for this purpose: that those to whom it is prescribed
may be bound to spend it piously and in divine exercises. Otherwise the entire
determination would be made in vain. The *minor* premise is confirmed by the
fact that he alone can bind absolutely everyone—whether servants or masters,
parents or children—to observe this time holily and strictly.

*Fifth*, this power—namely, the power to determine *principal and solemn
time*—belongs only to him who can render this determination perpetual. But God
alone can do this. Therefore, and so forth. The truth of the *major* premise is
clear from the fact that principal and solemn time is both necessary and
sufficient, and hence is such that it is neither superabundant nor deficient:
it does not require anything to be added, since it is sufficient, nor anything
to be taken away, since it is necessary. It is thus immutably such and
perpetually binding. The *minor* premise needs no proof, since everyone—at least
all the orthodox—concedes that God's precepts alone are perpetual and
immutable, and that nothing decreed by human beings can or ought to bind
perpetually and immutably.

*Sixth*, the power to determine this *solemn, necessary, and sufficient time*
belongs only to him who is wise enough to make the determination, both with
respect to the *duration* of the time and with respect to its *frequency*. But
this can be said of God alone. Therefore, and so forth. The *major* premise is
certain, because no one should be said to possess the power to accomplish that
which he has not been furnished with wisdom to do. The *minor* premise is
abundantly evident from what was said before, for we proved above that no human
being, or class of human beings, has been furnished with the wisdom required to
make this determination of *principal, solemn, necessary, and sufficient time*.

*Seventh*, the power to determine this *solemn, necessary, and sufficient
time* belongs only to him who possesses the power and authority to bind all
people of every age to observe holily the determination that has been made. But
God alone possesses this power and authority. Therefore, and so forth. The
*major* premise is clear from the fact that, since this solemn time is
*necessary* as well as *sufficient*, it must necessarily be observed by
everyone in every age and must be the same both in *duration* and in
*frequency*, and, as far as possible, also with respect to the *season* or
*when*. The *minor* premise is certain, since no human being has the power to
give precepts to all the other inhabitants of the entire world, much less to
command everyone through every age, as is evident. No sane person will deny
that this power belongs to God.

*Eighth*, the power to constitute and determine *this principal, solemn,
necessary, and sufficient time* belongs only to him to whom all

[1674 logical printed p. 330 | IA leaf 348 | PDF p. 348]

time originally belongs. But all time originally belongs to God alone, so that
none of it can be called ours or applied to our earthly affairs except by his
indulgence. Therefore, and so forth. The *minor* premise is certain, because he
alone is the creator of time, its absolute lord, and its sole dispenser; and he
can give or take it away at pleasure, as every Christian will acknowledge. The
truth of the *major* premise is clear from the fact that he alone, to whom all
time belongs, can apply time to this business and time to that business as he
pleases. Thus he can grant human beings this quantity of time, and no greater,
for conducting earthly affairs and reserve the remainder for divine worship.
Therefore, he alone who can grant time to human beings can determine time for
divine worship or religion. It was demonstrated above that a human being is not
the absolute lord of his time, but possesses by God's mere indulgence whatever
time he has to expend upon earthly things.

*Ninth*, the power to determine *this principal, solemn, necessary, and
sufficient time* belongs only to him who can, by his blessing, render the time
thus determined and applied to divine things sufficient for promoting our
eternal salvation. But it belongs to God alone to render time sufficient, by
his blessing, for promoting our eternal salvation. Therefore, and so forth.
The *major* premise is certain, because otherwise that time cannot be called
sufficient for promoting our eternal good. Hence God is said to have blessed
the seventh day both in Genesis 2 and in Exodus 20. And this express
determination carries with it a special blessing both upon the exercises of
piety performed at that time and also upon the worldly works in which we are
occupied during the remaining time. The *minor* premise needs no proof, since
no one can deny that God is the sole author of every blessing, or that he alone
can bless whatever person or thing he wills.

*Tenth*, the power to determine *principal and solemn time* belongs only to him
who possesses the power to determine the very season. For, as was demonstrated
above, the time's τὸ *how long* and *how often*, or its *duration* and
*frequency*, are far more necessary to religion than its τὸ *when*, or
*opportunity*. And he with whom alone rests the determining of what is less can
also alone determine what is greater. But the very *season* of time—its
beginning or the particular day—rests with God alone to determine. Therefore,
and so forth.

That the power to determine the particular day belongs to God alone is clear
from the following. 1. Otherwise no divine blessing could be expected, for a
human being has no power to attach the divine blessing to his institutions.
Nor is God accustomed to bless human institutions, at least those continued
for some time, but only his own institutions. Every blessing of God flows from
God's free good pleasure. We therefore cannot be certain of it unless he in
some manner reveals this good pleasure of his to us. But nowhere has he
promised that he will bless all human institutions. 2. Because of his dominion
over all time, he alone can determine whichever day he wills. For it belongs
to him to designate not only the *proportion* of time but also its *order*.
God unquestionably has greater dominion over us and over all time than any
master has over his servants or over their time. If a master grants his servant
some time each day to arrange his own affairs properly, the servant is not free
to

[1674 logical printed p. 331 | IA leaf 349 | PDF p. 349]

spend whichever hour or whatever part of the day he wishes upon his own
affairs; rather, even in this he must await his master's express determination,
unless the master himself has expressly declared otherwise. But our Lord has
not declared that he has granted human beings this faculty to institute
whichever day they wish in the cycle of the week. The power to determine the
day itself is therefore one of our Lord's prerogatives. And, as a testimony of
our subjection, we are bound to await his determination even in this matter.
3. Otherwise one and the same day would not be observed by all the inhabitants
of the earth, even as far as this is possible. For it is impossible, without
some divine revelation, for everyone to learn which day, or which numbered day,
had been determined. And it is no less impossible for everyone to observe one
and the same day, as is more than sufficiently clear from the discrepant
determination of the day of Easter. That one and the same day ought to be
observed by everyone, as far as possible, is clear from what was said before;
and this is confirmed by the practice of all who profess the same religion and
who both formerly and today always observe the same day. For all Christians
observe the *first* day of the week, and Muslims the *sixth* day.

*Eleventh*, if this power—namely, the power to determine *principal, solemn,
sufficient, and necessary time*—did not belong to God alone, no one would be
faulted for devising some time from his own imagination and assigning it to
religion or the worship of God. But this is false. Therefore the former is
false as well. The connection of the *major* premise is certain, for if the
power to determine this time were granted to human beings, they could *by
right* determine whatever time they wished. The *minor* premise is clear even
from the fact that Jeroboam sinned grievously in 1 Kings 12:32 by establishing
a feast in the eighth month, on the fifteenth day of that month, like the feast
celebrated in Judah, as is clear from verse 33, where it is said that he had
devised it from his own heart. Since he sinned grievously by this institution,
it is evident that he violated God's law. But what law, I ask, was it? No
particular law forbidding such a thing exists. He therefore violated some
moral law of God prohibiting anyone from determining *solemn, sufficient, and
necessary time*, whatever that time might finally be, whether the fourth or the
second. Hence it is clear that God granted this privilege to no human
being but reserved it unimpaired to himself.

*Twelfth*, all the orthodox fault the papists for instituting feast days. And
they condemn their feasts not only because they idolatrously dedicate some to
*angels and saints*, or because the multitude of them is burdensome to the
church, but also because by instituting such feasts they are guilty of
superstition, making a time necessary to religion which God had not made
necessary. Therefore, the power to determine *time necessary and sufficient
for religion* belongs to God alone, not to human beings. The *antecedent* is
abundantly evident from the writings of the orthodox. The *consequence* is
certain, for it could not be charged against them as a crime that they
instituted such times if this power did not belong to God alone; much less
could the crime of superstition be laid against them for that reason, because
where there is no law, there is no transgression of the law.

But perhaps our adversaries will object that the papists who institute such
feast days ought to be blamed because all days are now equal under the gospel.
*I answer*: 1. Few, if any, of the orthodox employ this argument. 2. The
adversaries themselves acknowledge that some *sufficient time* must necessarily
be determined for religion, and they maintain that

[1674 logical printed p. 332 | IA leaf 350 | PDF p. 350]

this is *moral and natural*. 3. We proved and demonstrated above
the necessity of determining *principal, solemn, necessary, and sufficient
time*. 4. It is false, as will appear in its proper place, that all time is now
equal. 5. It would then follow that Jeroboam transgressed only some ceremonial
law and not also a moral one. For if he also transgressed a moral law, that law
still remains in force, because the moral law is immutable, as the adversaries
themselves say. If he transgressed only a ceremonial law, it will not then be a
moral duty of a human being to observe, as necessary to religion, only those
times which God himself instituted. I am now arguing *ad hominem*. Moreover,
the text conveys something very different when it aggravates his guilt on this
ground: that he had devised that day in his own mind, or from his own heart,
that impious king.

*Thirteenth*, if this *solemn and necessary time* were not determined by God,
Christians could in no way be certain and persuaded of the sufficiency and
necessity of the determined time. For human authority cannot satisfy
consciences in a matter of such importance; the conscience can rest upon
divine authority alone and acquiesce in it alone.

*Fourteenth*, the adversaries themselves are compelled to acknowledge this
when they say that a human being cannot determine what time ought necessarily
to be assigned as due to divine worship. For if a human being cannot determine
this time, and if it must necessarily be determined, as was proved above, it
follows of itself that it must be determined by God.

*Fifteenth*. This must be affirmed even with the pagans themselves as
witnesses: namely, that the power to determine *principal and solemn time,
necessary and ordinarily sufficient for divine worship*, belongs to God alone.
For just as their priests pretended that other religious institutions had been
determined by the gods through oracles, visions, or dreams, so also they
ascribed the determination of *the time of solemn worship* to the gods. For
Plato speaks as follows in *Laws*, book 2: *The gods, taking pity on the human
race, laborious by nature, themselves appointed respites from toil.* They
therefore understood well enough that this twofold principle is naturally
implanted in human minds: *first*, that God ought to be worshiped only with
worship prescribed by himself; *then*, that the time which he himself
determines for this business ought to be given to the worship of God as
necessary and solemn. Our adversaries are pressed all the more by this
argument, because they commonly conclude from pagan practice what is morally
natural and what is not.

1. *It is objected* that it appears no more necessary for *this solemn,
necessary, and sufficient time* to be determined by God than for sufficient
time to be determined and expended upon *public* worship. *I answer*: 1. Once
the *solemn time* has been determined, no further determination need be made of
the portion of that solemn time to be applied to public worship, except insofar
as the common necessity that all the members assemble to worship God solemnly
requires it. A determination by the rulers of the church can satisfy this
necessity, since they can sufficiently understand how to make this
determination for edification; and variation can be admitted without any loss
to religion. But this cannot be said of solemn time, namely, that some notable
change in it, whether with respect to *duration* or to *frequency*, would not
be detrimental to the exercises of religion. Yet when an entire day is
dedicated to sacred exercises, both private and public, from the absence of a

[1674 logical printed p. 333 | IA leaf 351 | PDF p. 351]

determination of the duration of public worship nothing is taken away from
religion. For if in one place the time determined for public worship is shorter
or less frequent than elsewhere, where public worship can more conveniently be
instituted both for a longer continuous period and more frequently on the
appointed day, religion is secure, because time not spent in public worship
can be spent in private and domestic exercises of piety. *Then*, under the Old
Testament, where *solemn time* had been determined, the portion of solemn time
spent in the exercise of public worship had not been determined, but could
vary according to the different constitution of circumstances. *Finally*,
from the fact that God did not determine the time to be applied precisely to
public worship, it can be concluded all the more firmly that he determined
*solemn and necessary time* in which the duties not only of public worship but
also of private and solitary worship can and ought to be performed. For without
this determination religion itself and our communion with God would be in the
greatest danger, as was explained above. In this way what public worship cannot
complete and accomplish is supplied, whether it is administered for a longer
or a shorter time.

2. *It is objected*. The determination of this *solemn time* appears no more
necessary than the determination, for every day, of time sufficient for
domestic and solitary worship. *I answer* as above. 1. Indeed, the determination
of *solemn time* is more necessary precisely because no time has been determined
to be spent each day in those private exercises. By this means what was lacking
in those private exercises is supplied, and that carnal impression which
worldly cares may have made—and which brief, modest, and small daily periods
and exercises could not erase—is more thoroughly effaced. 2. It appears that
God was unwilling to determine this time partly so that he might make trial of
people's zeal, wisdom, Christian prudence, and so forth, and of how much
resourcefulness they possessed for redeeming the time; and partly because, as
was said, it was not so necessary. 3. The determination of *solemn time*, but
not of this private time, exposes the depraved iniquity of profane people. They
not only neglect the worship for which no time has been precisely determined,
but also despise the very day which God has precisely determined. And those who
do not observe the day already determined would not observe the time to be
spent daily in private worship even if it had been determined. For those who
regard this one determination as an unbearable burden would surely regard
several determinations as a far greater burden, rightly to be rejected. 4. God
saw fit to determine the weekly Sabbath for the Jews, though he did not
determine for them the time to perform domestic and solitary worship each day.
It therefore seemed to him more necessary to determine the former than the
latter time. 5. Similarly, God once saw fit to determine in Leviticus 23 that a
tenth portion of goods was to be given to himself, and later to the Levites and
priests; but he did not see fit also to determine how great a portion of goods
was to be given to the poor and to other pious uses. 6. Therefore this
consequence is invalid: because he did not determine everything in that class,
or this or that particular thing, therefore he determined nothing in that
class; or therefore he did not determine that which is the most necessary,
principal, and greatest thing in that class.
