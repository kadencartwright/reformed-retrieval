## ON THE CHURCH MILITANT

The first part of the Catholic Church, the triumphant, has been discussed.
The second, which is called militant, follows.

The Church Militant is the assembly of the faithful which is governed on
earth under a definite head and which, armed with that spiritual armor, fights
under his banner against Satan, the world, the flesh, and all afflictions. It
is considered in two ways:

1. With respect to the head to which it is subject.
2. With respect to the body of which it consists.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 44 | printed col. 88 | bsb10175568]

## ON THE HEAD OF THE CHURCH

The HEAD of the Church—which is so called metaphorically—is its supreme
Prince, Monarch, Bridegroom, Spouse, and so forth. It is considered in two
ways:

1. Generally.
2. Singularly.

Generally, the head is God, one in essence, three in persons, from whom, in
whom, through whom, and for whose sake all things exist.

Singularly, the formal and analogical head of the Church Militant is the same
as that of the Catholic Church, namely, Christ alone, the God-man, as in 1
Corinthians 12:27; Ephesians 1:22; 4:15; 5:23; Colossians 1:18; and 2:10. He
governs the Church, his body, because it is his kingdom, house, bride, and so
forth.

The spiritual government which Christ exercises over the Church is twofold:

1. Supreme and principal.
2. Subordinate and ministerial.

The supreme government is proper to Christ. By it he reigns as head in his
body and governs each and every one of his members with the scepter of the
Word and the Spirit; and he administers all the affairs of his kingdom, both
internal and external, most effectually both toward his own and toward his
enemies, Ephesians 1:23; Philippians 2:13. This kingdom of Christ is twofold:

1. Natural.
2. Of dispensation.

The natural is that eternal kingdom in which he reigns with equal power and
glory with the Father and the Holy Spirit, in the unity of essence and of
their common operations, in and over all things, infinitely, immutably, from
eternity and to eternity. That kingdom is not to be treated here.

The government κατ’ οἰκονομίαν—according to the economy—or

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 45 | printed col. 89 | bsb10175568]

of dispensation is that gracious kingdom which he takes upon himself for the
salvation of his body. This government is inferior to the natural government
and will have an end, because he will hand this kingdom over to the Father, 1
Corinthians 15:25, 28. With respect to this government, the government of the
Church is monarchical.

Ministerial government is twofold: 1. invisible, namely, through the Holy
Spirit and the ministry of angels, as in John 16:7; Hebrews 1:14; 2. visible
and external, namely, the government of the Church through the Word and
discipline, which is delegated to certain human beings.

The principal efficient cause of the external ministerial government of the
Church Militant is God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit; its proximate cause,
however, is Christ.

The impelling cause was not necessity on the part of the one instituting it,
but human weakness:

1. Because we cannot bear the majesty of God speaking in our presence.
2. Because Christ himself no longer dwells among us in visible presence.

The administering causes are bishops and presbyters lawfully called to this
office. There is a twofold question about this cause.

### QUESTION 1: Whether the External Government of the Church Belongs Chiefly to Secular Princes

Brentius and others affirm that it does. We deny it, relying on these
arguments.

Argument 1. The government of the Church is said in Scripture to rest with
bishops and presbyters, Acts 20:28; 1 Corinthians 12:11; Ephesians 2:11;
Hebrews 13:17; and elsewhere. But nowhere is it said to rest with secular
princes. Therefore.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 45 | printed col. 90 | bsb10175568]

Argument 2. If the government of the Church rests with secular princes, it
follows that in the primitive Church, and afterward down to the time of
Constantine, there was no government in the Church, because there were no
secular princes. But the latter is absurd. Therefore.

Argument 3. Secular princes as such are not members of the Church. They govern
human beings insofar as they are human beings, and chiefly with respect to
their bodies; but the government of the Church concerns human beings only
insofar as they are Christians, and chiefly with respect to the soul. Princes,
however, only insofar as they are Christians, have no dominion over other
Christians. Therefore.

Argument 4. Secular princes are bound, taught, absolved, blessed, and so forth
by bishops. But the lesser is blessed by the greater, Hebrews 7:7. Thus
Ambrose excommunicated Theodosius, and so forth. Therefore.

Argument 5. If this supreme government rested with secular princes, then just
as they can discharge the functions of lower judges in civil adjudication, so
they could discharge those of lower ecclesiastical officers. Thus they could
preach in the place of bishops, administer the sacraments, and so forth. But
the latter is absurd. Therefore the former is also absurd.

Argument 6. In some kingdoms women can be secular princes no less than men;
but women are not permitted even to speak in the Church, 1 Corinthians 14:34.
Therefore much less are they permitted to govern the Church.

Argument 7. Ecclesiastical jurisdiction has always been distinct from civil
jurisdiction in matter, form, and end, and thus also in its administrators,
as in 2 Chronicles 19 Jehoshaphat distinguished these jurisdictions.
Therefore.

Argument 8. Ambrose, speaking about Valentinian in letter 33: *Do not burden
yourself, Emperor, by thinking that you have any imperial right in those
things which are divine. The right over public fortifications has been*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 46 | printed col. 91 | bsb10175568]

*entrusted to you, not the right over sacred things*, and so forth. Athanasius,
in his *Letter to Those Living the Solitary Life*, reproves Constantius for
involving himself in ecclesiastical matters, and adds that Bishop Hosius said
to Constantius: *Do not give commands to us in this kind of matter, but rather
learn these things from us. For God has entrusted the empire to you, but to us
he has entrusted those things which belong to the Church.*

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. They are servants of the Church, 2 Corinthians 4:5, and therefore
much more of kings, to whom they ought to be subject, 1 Peter 2:13.

Response. There is an equivocation in the word “servant,” because it is taken
not in its proper sense but for laboring for another’s benefit. Thus the
magistrate is called the servant of the commonwealth, and the teacher the
servant of the pupil, and so forth. Furthermore, bishops serve kings only in
civil matters, not in ecclesiastical matters, because they are within the
Church, not above the Church; indeed, insofar as they are kings, they are not
even in the Church.

Objection 2. Moses, Joshua, Eli, Samuel, David, Solomon, and others were
leaders and kings, and they often involved themselves in the affairs of
religion. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied.

1. Reasoning from extraordinary deeds is not valid for abolishing or
establishing ordinary government. For if they involved themselves in the
affairs of religion, they did so either when no ministry had been established,
as Moses did, who abstained from the priestly office after Aaron’s
consecration; or when the civil polity was confused, as Eli and Samuel did; or
when the priests were corrupt, as Josiah and others did. Therefore.

Objection 3. Kings are guardians and avengers of the Law of God, Romans 13:4.
Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because they are guardians and avengers
civilly, not

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 46 | printed col. 92 | bsb10175568]

ecclesiastically. For they restrain heresies, blasphemies, sacrileges, and so
forth only civilly, by edicts and punishments, but they do not declare what
the orthodox faith is, as the examples of Constantine and others teach.

### QUESTION 2: Whether the Government of the Church Belongs Chiefly to Bishops and Presbyters

Bellarmine denies it in book 1, chapter 8, *On the Roman Pontiff*.

We affirm it, relying on these arguments.

Argument 1. Scripture nowhere asserts that it rests with one person alone,
but in several places asserts that it rests with bishops and presbyters,
Matthew 18:17, 20; 1 Corinthians 5:4; 12:11; Acts 20:28; Ephesians 4:11;
Hebrews 13:17; 1 Peter 5:1–2. Therefore.

Argument 2. When the first controversy arose in the primitive Church, the
bishops and presbyters judged it, Acts 15:6 and following; no one man did so,
indeed, not even Peter alone, although he took part in the council. He neither
spoke first in the council nor delivered the decision in the name of the
synod; James did so, verse 13, contrary to what the Papists maintain.
Therefore.

Argument 3. Cyprian, book 3, letter 19 to Cletus: *Since this matter*, he says,
*concerns the counsel and judgment of us all, it is for us to judge. I do not
dare to claim the entire matter for myself alone*, and so forth. Ambrose on 1
Timothy 1:5: *The synagogue, and afterward the Church, had elders, without
whose counsel nothing was done.* Jerome, letter 1 to Tur.: *Before*, he says,
*factions in religion arose at the instigation of the devil, the churches
were governed by the common counsel of the presbyters*; and afterward: *Later
throughout the whole world*, he says, *it was decreed that one elected from
the presbyters should be placed over the others.* Therefore in the primitive
and pure Church the supreme ministerial government rested with

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 47 | printed col. 93 | bsb10175568]

bishops and presbyters. For the remaining matters pertaining to this, see the
treatments of councils and the ministry.

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. Nowhere in Scripture is supreme power said to have been conferred
upon a council of priests. Therefore. The antecedent is proved: all power
granted by Christ to the apostles and disciples was granted to all and each,
because a council was not needed to exercise it. For individual bishops could
then—and now can—teach, baptize, loose, bind, ordain ministers, and so forth.
Therefore.

Response. 1. The antecedent is denied, because in the passages cited above,
the supreme ministerial power is said to have been given to a council of
bishops and presbyters.

2. The proof of the antecedent tells against Bellarmine, because if all power
granted by Christ was granted to individuals, then no power was granted to
Peter above the others, and thus he was not constituted the head of the rest.

Finally, it is false when he says that individual bishops can separately
ordain ministers, bind, and loose—that is, excommunicate and receive the
excommunicated back into the Church—because that power was granted to the
senate of bishops and presbyters, as will be said in its place.

Objection 2. If the supreme government of the Church rested with the leading
men, it would follow that the Church is almost always without rulers, and that
the ecclesiastical commonwealth is consequently most wretched. But the latter
is absurd. Therefore.

The proposition is proved. Since the leading men are equals among themselves,
they cannot administer the common good as is fitting unless they are either
assembled or elect one person whom all obey. But neither can be said: not the
first, because

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 47 | printed col. 94 | bsb10175568]

they very rarely assemble for a general council—for in the first three
hundred years there was no general assembly, and afterward scarcely once in
every hundred years; nor the second, because the leading men never appointed
anyone whom the universal Church obeyed, at least for a time. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied. Furthermore, its proof is inept, because
although the leading men are not always in a general council, they can still
administer the common good according to general statutes and through lesser
assemblies, as we see in the time of the apostles and today.

2. This does not follow: there was no council in the first three hundred years
because of the most severe persecutions; therefore there could or should have
been none by which the Church was governed.

3. It is false when he says that there was no general council at that time,
because in Acts 15 there was a general council of the apostles; even in times
of persecution bishops held various assemblies. Finally, it is not true when
Bellarmine says that the leading men never elected one person whom they would
obey, except in the most corrupt times.

Objection 3. If the supreme power were in the assembly of leading men, then
the more numerous the council convened, the more authority it would have. But
the latter is absurd, because the Council of Rimini, comprising six hundred
bishops, was regarded as having no authority because it was rejected by the
pope, while the First Council of Constantinople, comprising one hundred and
fifty bishops, was regarded as having supreme authority because it was
approved by the pope. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because the authority of a council
depends not on the number of bishops, but on lawful convocation and an
orthodox decision of questions according to the Word of God.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 48 | printed col. 95 | bsb10175568]

Furthermore, it is false that the Council of Rimini was rejected because it
was rejected by the pope; it is rejected because it erred.

Objection 4. Aristocracy is more harmful to the Church than democracy, which
is absolutely the worst form of government. Therefore. The antecedent is
proved: heresy is the Church’s supreme evil, but heresies are stirred up by
the leading men, for all heresiarchs have been either bishops or presbyters.
Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because that which Christ instituted must
be believed to be most salutary for the Church.

2. This does not follow: the leading men stirred up heresies; therefore
aristocracy is harmful to the Church. For the abuse of a thing does not take
away its right use.

Furthermore, the leading men also refuted all the heresies. The following is
similar: more kings have been tyrants than those who came from the leading men
and the people; therefore government by kings is not to be tolerated in the
commonwealth. Finally, Bellarmine contradicts himself. For in his treatment of
Scripture, book 2, chapter 15, he bars the laity from reading Scripture because
heresies arise from it, but here he attributes all heresies to the leading
men. Therefore, if his argument were sound, the leading men would have to be
barred from reading Scripture.

So much for the first external cause of the ministerial government of the
Church Militant, namely, the efficient cause. The second now follows, namely,
the end. It is twofold: the primary end is the glory of God and the salvation
of the Church; the secondary is fourfold.

First, God uses the ministry of human beings to demonstrate his grace toward
us, since he takes from human beings, not from angels, those in whom he has
placed the message of reconciliation, 2 Corinthians 5:19.

2. So that he may

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 48 | printed col. 96 | bsb10175568]

exercise us in humility when he wills the Word to be preached and the Church
to be governed by human beings who are for the most part inferior in birth,
rank, and other respects,
Matthew 10:42; 1 Corinthians 1:27.

3. So that by this bond we may be bound more closely in mutual love,
Ephesians 4:4.

4. So that, knowing no one is sufficient for himself but all need another’s
work, because to each one a measure of grace has been given, Ephesians 4:7, we
may give each person his due and despise no one.
