[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 398 | PDF p. 449 | begins at Hisce etiam aliæ quæstiunculæ tres affines sunt]

Three other little questions allied to these also arise.

It is asked, first, whether, because God excludes no one from heaven except the
one who opposes Christ with a new and vincible rebellion, the Remonstrants
therefore rightly conclude in the same place that the gospel can be preached
at each and every moment in every place. We deny it.

1. Neither physically nor morally do human beings have such power that they
could preach the gospel in the most remote regions of the world, where Christ
has never even been heard of.

2. This vain consequence supposes that the gospel is preached or not preached
on account of human merits or demerits.

3. Some nations make themselves unworthy of the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 399 | PDF p. 450]

gospel, as the adversaries admit, and are therefore deprived of the preaching
of the gospel by the order of justice. Can the gospel be preached there at each
and every moment when God's justice opposes it?

4. If, because no one is excluded from heaven except the one who opposes Christ
with a new and vincible rebellion, the gospel can therefore be preached in
every place at every hour, then all nations before hearing the gospel—when they
are in a state in which they cannot oppose Christ with a new contumacy—either
are worthy of the gospel, which is absurd, or are unworthy of it. Thus those
who are neither excluded from heaven, nor have merited eternal punishments, nor
appear to be stained by any sin, as the adversaries will have it, are unworthy
of the gospel, which is unjust.

5. If the gospel can be preached in every place and at every moment, then the
gospel could have been preached in Bithynia at the very moment when the Holy
Spirit forbade the Apostles to preach there. This is absurd.

It is asked, second, whether, because God excludes no one except a rebel against
Christ from gospel grace and heaven, each and every person is therefore bound
by precisely the same right and in the same manner to believe in Jesus Christ
announced in the gospel. The Remonstrants say so in the *Synodical Writings*,
article 2, page 285.

We say that all are bound to believe in Christ by an equal right—that is, by
an equal gospel command—and with an equal conscience moved by the authority

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 400 | PDF p. 451]

of God who commands, for all are equally bound to obey God. But we deny that
all, elect and reprobate alike, are bound to believe by the same formal right,
or by the same right of title and ownership in Christ the Savior, because by
this latter right the elect alone are bound to believe.

1. According to the intention of God the Father and of the dying Christ,
Christ is not the Savior of the reprobate, but only of the elect. For the
intention of dying for someone and election, or the intention of saving, have
the same extent in God. But the Arminians and Jesuits do not dare say that
Christ is the Savior of the reprobate according to God's peremptory intention.
By the absolute decree to deny them congruent calling, the Jesuits deny that
Christ is their Savior.

2. Remission of sins, the Righteousness of Christ, regeneration, and faith
have not been procured for the reprobate by Christ's merit. But all these have
been acquired for the elect by the merit of Christ's death. Therefore, there
is not an equal right binding the elect and the reprobate to believe.

It is asked, third, whether, because God excludes from heaven and punishes with
eternal punishment only those who despise the Redeemer and spurn divine grace,
each and every unbeliever—because they are unwilling to believe in this their
Redeemer, though they can by grace—is therefore liable to eternal punishment
and by their own demerit deprived of the fruit of

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 401 | PDF p. 452]

procured reconciliation. The Remonstrants say so in express words in the same
place. We deny it.

1. The Indians and Americans, and those who have heard nothing of Christ and
his gospel, will perish without the Law, Romans 2:12, and much more without the
gospel. They are not bound to believe, according to Romans 10:14: “How will
they believe in him of whom they have not heard?” And according to John 15:22:
“If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have sin”—that is, they
would not have been guilty of the sin of unbelief.

2. It is unjust that human beings should not be condemned because of sin truly
inhering in them both by nature and by a depraved disposition, yet should be
condemned solely because they are unwilling to believe in Christ, of whom they
have heard nothing, since faith is simply impossible through the absence and
lack of a revealed object. Therefore, our theologians rightly affirm that no
one is condemned because of negative unbelief, but only because of positive
unbelief.

ROMANS 5:10.

For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of
his Son, much more, having been reconciled, we shall be saved through his life.

From this passage it is clear that all those for whom Christ died come under
the name of foes and enemies, and consequently are unbelievers and rebels.
Hence:

It is asked whether the Remonstrants rightly affirm in the *Synodical
Writings*, article 2, page 286, that Christ did not die for

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 402 | PDF p. 453]

unbelievers and rebels as such, but that by Christ's death grace was procured
for sinners insofar as they are considered in the common state of fall and
sin, and in other sins, with impenitence alone excepted. It was procured under
this condition: that the fruit of this grace should come only to those who
would believe in the Redeemer, but should by no means pertain to those who
would be unwilling to believe in the Redeemer. We deny it.

1. According to the Arminians, Christ died for each and every human being. But
the finally impenitent are human beings.

2. If no grace has been procured for overcoming unbelief and rebellion against
Christ, then this rebellion, according to the Arminians, is not sin. For it is
not in a human being's power to avoid a sin for whose avoidance no grace is
available to them, not even in the fullness of grace in Christ, and whose
remission is physically impossible. But in Christ no grace has been procured
by which human beings can avoid final unbelief, because Christ did not pour
out his blood to expiate that sin. Therefore, they are not justly condemned
because of that sin.

3. If the grace of reconciliation has not been procured for rebels as such,
then all who truly believe abstain from final rebellion—which alone is the
cause of condemnation—by the powers of pure nature, without any aid of grace.
They therefore believe to the end—which is to abstain from final rebellion

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 403 | PDF p. 454]

and unbelief—and save themselves by the powers of pure nature, because, as
they teach, no grace, even in Christ, is available to human beings for avoiding
final unbelief.

4. They wrongly treat unbelievers and rebels as the same. For by “unbelievers”
they understand those who are finally contumacious against Christ. But many
are rebels against Christ for a time, such as Saul and the thief who repented
on the cross, and they are not finally impenitent. Thus Christ must have died
for some rebels as such and for some temporary unbelievers as such.

5. Without any Scripture or reason they teach in this manner that on the tree
of the cross Christ satisfied divine justice for some sins of those who
perish, but not for all their sins.

6. The reason brought by the Hague Conferencers for this distinction on page
171 is futile—namely, that Christ's death precedes faith and unbelief in order,
and that it is therefore absurd that Christ died for believers or unbelievers.
For Christ's death does not precede in order our hostilities toward God,
unrighteousness, and ungodliness, since Scripture says that Christ died for
the unrighteous, for the ungodly, and for us while we were still his enemies.
As though our natural ungodliness did not chiefly include unbelief and hatred
toward Christ, with the result that we come to him only when drawn by the
Father, John 6:44, and as though no one were by

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 404 | PDF p. 455]

nature ungodly and a hater of Christ! But if Original Sin is not sin, as the
Arminians maintain, Christ expiated no sins on the tree of the cross for those
who obey after hearing the gospel. For Original Sin is not sin, and sins that
precede the gospel call do not exclude and shut human beings out of heaven
unless a new rebellion against Christ is added; and those who obey the gospel
do not oppose it with a new rebellion. Nor did he expiate any sins for those
reprobates who, after hearing the gospel, oppose it with a new rebellion and
finally resist it, because original sin in them is not sin, and rebellion
cannot be sin because no grace has been procured in Christ for avoiding
rebellion. For, as they teach, Christ neither procured grace nor died for such
people.

7. It follows from this that human beings before mature age, the time when they
begin to be capable of rebellion against Christ, are not sinners or children
of wrath, but are innocent human beings in a state of pure nature.

8. Christ blotted out all the sins of the elect, 1 John 1:7. Therefore, he also
blotted out their unbelief and their guilt from always having spurned and
rejected the gospel call, until by the grace proper to the elect God overcame
their hardness. Therefore, Christ died for unbelievers as such, and, because
they were unbelievers, he died in order to blot out their unbelief

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 405 | PDF p. 456]

with his blood. Further, if we consider God's intention and counsel, Christ
died only for believers as such—that is, for his sheep, friends, brothers,
children of God, and Church—with the counsel that by his merits he should
acquire remission of sins and salvation, faith and regeneration, for those who
would believe and for no others.

2 CORINTHIANS 5:19.

God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself.

From this context it follows that in God's mind all the sins of the elect were
removed from eternity. Hence it is asked whether, through the grace of
reconciliation procured by Christ's death, the affection of Mercy by which God
wills all to be saved—whose outgoing had been hindered by justice injured
through sin—was changed into a full purpose of the will to give eternal life
to believers. The Remonstrants say so in the same place, page 284. We deny it.

1. In God there is no half-mercy or half-will to show mercy that can be
increased and changed into a full will. For God's will is not capable of
increase or alteration.

2. Since Christ's merit and his death are something temporal, they can no more
be the cause of the divine will than there can be a cause of the divine essence
itself.

3. The change of divine wrath and hatred into love and benevolence is not a
change in God

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 406 | PDF p. 457]

*ad intra*, but only a change *ad extra* in the effects of God as angry with
and hating sinners.

1 PETER 2:24.

Who bore our sins in his body upon the tree.

For Christ to bear our sins on the tree is for Christ to bear the punishments
due to our sins that we ourselves would have suffered eternally in our own
persons. Hence that celebrated question:

Did Christ, on the tree of the cross, in his death and passion, sustain the
place, stead, and person of those for whom he died, so that he bore the
punishments due to them and God reckoned them as though dead because Christ
died for them and in their stead? The Remonstrants will have it that Christ
died for our good to this extent: that his death would turn to our good unless
it were owing to ourselves that it failed to do so. Nevertheless, they deny
that Christ died for us—that is, in our stead and place. The Arminians indeed
teach in words that Christ died for us. The *Hague Conference*, page 185:
“Christ died for our sins,
bearing in our stead that death which was owed to our sins.” But Arminius
states it otherwise in *Against Perkins*, page 673: “I would say that Christ
sustained our person, not with respect to the act—that is, the offering—but
with respect to the passion. For he was made a curse for us and was made an
offering for sin. Hence it appears, since all human beings universally are
sinners and subject to the curse, and Christ assumed the human nature common
to all, that it is probable

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 407 | PDF p. 458]

that he sustained the person of all human beings.”

But this distinction is senseless. For the act of offering his body on the
tree of the cross and the very suffering of death are really one and the same
movement. Therefore, when he says that Christ offered his body to God the
Father not in the place and stead of all mortals, yet suffered death in the
place and stead of all mortals, he actually says that in dying on the tree of
the cross Christ sustained the person of all mortals and that in dying he did
not sustain the stead and person of all mortals.

But Christ can be said to have sustained our person in various ways. 1. It
could be as though the elect had substituted Christ in their own place and
stead, just as a City sends a Commissioner to an Assembly who bears the stead
and person of the whole City in the Assembly. We acknowledge that in this
sense Christ did not sustain our person at all. For the Lord—not human
beings—“laid our iniquities upon him,” Isaiah 53:6.

2. He can be said to have sustained our person as though he satisfied justice
and, by suffering for us, merited that right and title to eternal life which
we ourselves would have merited on the supposition that we had suffered for
our sins what he suffered. The adversaries and all opponents of imputed
righteousness fasten this meaning upon us, and infer that we would be

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 408 | PDF p. 459]

as righteous as Christ himself. But we teach that, because of the dignity of
the person suffering, Christ merited far more by his death than we could have
merited even if one supposed, impossibly, that in so brief a time we had
suffered everything he suffered. Hence Bellarmine, *On Justification*, book 2,
chapter 2, argument 5: “If Christ's righteousness were truly imputed to us, so
that through it we were held and reckoned righteous just as if it were
properly our own intrinsic and formal righteousness, ---- we ought to be
reckoned as righteous as Christ himself, and ought to be called redeemers and
Saviors of the world.” But the Jesuit himself answers for us in the same
place, argument 4: “For if they wished only”—he speaks of our theologians—“that
Christ's merits be imputed to us because they have been given to us and we can
offer them to God the Father for our sins, since Christ took upon himself the
burden of making satisfaction for us and reconciling us to God the Father,
their position would be correct.” And certainly this is for Christ to have died
in our stead and place and is just as if we ourselves had died.

3. Christ can be said to have borne our person as though by dying he had
performed for us and in our stead everything required for our actual
salvation, as though the condition of faith were not necessarily required of
us for actual justification and glorification. Arminius also slanderously
fastened this meaning upon us, though we do not teach it.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 409 | PDF p. 460]

4. Nevertheless, Christ died in our place and stead in this sense: that he
suffered the punishments eternally due to us for sins, and, by satisfying
God's justice for us, he thus far pleaded our cause in dying, so that God
reckons it the same as if we had died and requires nothing else to satisfy his
justice and actually redeem us from the guilt of sin. But for the application
of that Redemption he requires faith made effectual through love.

This is proved. 1. To die for someone is to die in place of the person who was
to die so that the person who was to die might live, just as a surety or
sponsor pays the price for a bankrupt. Legally, in the creditor's sight, this
is the same as if the bankrupt himself paid the price. In this sense David
wished to die for Absalom and Paul to become anathema for the Jews—namely, in
the place of Absalom and the Jews, so that they might live. Therefore, Christ
did not die merely for the good of the Elect in such a way that they could
nevertheless die eternally, as the Arminians teach.

2. Scripture says the same: “Christ died for us,” Romans 5:8; “for sinners,
the just for the unjust,” 1 Peter 3:18; “for his friends,” John 15:13; “for his
sheep,” John 10:11; “for the Church,” Ephesians 5:25; and “for many,” Matthew
20:28. That is, in the stead and place of sinners, the unjust, the sheep, and
so forth.

3. If Christ suffered in his body what we ought to have suffered, then he

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 410 | PDF p. 461]

died in our place. But the former is true. Galatians 3:13: “He was made a curse
for us.” But it is
expressly said that we were subject to the curse of the Law, according to this:
“Cursed is the one who has not continued in all things written in the book of
the Law, to do them.” If “he was bruised because of our iniquities,” if “the
chastisement of our peace was laid upon him,” and “by his stripes we, we were
healed,” as the Prophet says, Isaiah 53:5; if “he bore our sins in his body on
the tree,” as in 1 Peter 2:23; if he, though innocent, “who knew no sin, was
made sin for us,” 2 Corinthians 4:21; and if “because of the transgression of
my people the stroke was upon him,” Isaiah 53:8, then he rightly died in our
place. The reason is that either he suffered as a mere example of obedience,
which the ungodly Socinus affirms contrary to the Scriptures; or, by the order
of justice, he was struck as a surety in our place, which is the position
maintained; or, finally, he poured out his blood with an indeterminate end, so
that the good overflowing from his death turned to our good just as much as the
obedience of the fallible first Adam turned to our good. This is absurd. For
in that case the second covenant is not more excellent than the first, and the
second Adam is no stronger to save than the first.

4. He who died in such a way that he gave himself as a λύτρον, “ransom-price,”
and ἀντίλυτρον, “substitute-ransom”—a redemption-price of the kind given for
captives, where the λύτρον stands in the captive's place, and a price of the
kind given for purchased things, where the price stands in the purchased
thing's place—died in our place and stead. But Christ died for us in this way,
Matthew

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 411 | PDF p. 462 | ends before Huic etiam affinis est altera Quæstio]

20:28; 1 Timothy 2:6; 1 Peter 3:18–19; 1 Corinthians 6:20. And that statement
of Arminius in *Against Perkins*, page 673, is futile: “It does not belong to
the essence of a Mediator that he should sustain anyone's person. ---- For he
sustains the person of neither party.” It is indeed true that sustaining the
person of disputing and dissenting parties does not belong to the essence of a
Mediator in general. But our argument is not from a Mediator in general, but
from such a Mediator in particular who is a surety and who promised and
rendered to injured justice the satisfaction of passive obedience. Hence it
does altogether belong to the essence of such a Mediator—one who poured out
his blood and life for the other party—to sustain the person and stead of that
party.
