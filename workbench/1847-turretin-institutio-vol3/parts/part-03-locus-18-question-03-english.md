QUESTION THREE

ON THE MEMBERS OF THE CHURCH

Besides the elect who have been called, are reprobates and unbelievers, whether
secret or open, true members of Christ’s church? We deny against the papists.

I. The first question that arises concerning the church considered with
respect to its inward state concerns its members, or the parts that belong to
it. This question lies between us and the papists, who err both by excess and
by defect: either by extending the church’s boundaries too far with respect to
certain people, or by contracting them too narrowly with respect to others.
They err by excess when they wish not only the elect and faithful but also
hypocrites and reprobates to belong to the church properly so called. They err
by defect when they wish catechumens, the excommunicated, and schismatics to be
barred from it. Hence a twofold controversy arises: first, whether the church
consists only of the faithful and elect or also of reprobates; and second,
whether catechumens, the excommunicated, and schismatics belong to the church.

[Marginalia: 1. The question about the church’s parts: Do reprobates also
belong to it?]

II. The first inquiry is whether, besides the elect who have been called,
reprobates and unbelievers, whether secret or open, are to be regarded as true
members of the church. Bellarmine maintains this along with others in *On the
Church Militant*, book 3, chapters 2, 7, 9, and following. There he teaches
that the definition of the church, after pagans, heretics, the excommunicated,
and schismatics have been excepted, “includes all others, even if they are
reprobate, wicked, and impious.” As he immediately adds, “For someone to be
called absolutely a part of the true church of which the Scriptures speak, we
do not think any inward virtue is required, but only the outward communion,
especially that of faith and the sacraments, which is perceived by the senses
themselves.”

His aim is that no church be acknowledged except the one whose head is the
Roman bishop. They see well enough that if they restrict it to the elect and
faithful, it is possible for the pope not to belong to it. They therefore
wished to extend it even to reprobates, provided they are contained under one
visible head. Hence at the Council of Constance this proposition of John Hus
was condemned as heretical: “The holy universal church is the universality of
the predestined.” The controversy is of such importance that its decision
governs nearly all the other questions about the church disputed between us
and the papists. We must therefore treat it all the more carefully.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

III. For the state of the question, observe first that the church can be
considered with respect either to its outward or to its inward state. The
outward state corresponds to the outward call through the word. On this
depends the church’s outward form, which consists in profession of faith, or
the outward and perceptible communion of the same sacred rites. The inward
state, however, corresponds to the inward and effectual call

[1847 vol. III printed p. 12 | IA leaf 46 | PDF p. 46]

through the word and Spirit. This call constitutes the church’s essential and
inward form in the communion of faith—that is, in the mystical union of the
faithful with Christ through faith and with one another through love. The
question is not whether reprobates belong to the outward state, which we do not
deny, but whether they belong to the inward state, which we deny.

IV. Observe second that the question does not concern those called members of
the church equivocally and *according to appearance*, or according to their
outward form, but those who are members properly and univocally.

3. The question is not whether the elect and faithful are in the church, which
our adversaries admit, but whether the church is constituted only from the
faithful and elect, which we maintain. It is one thing for the faithful to be
in the church; it is another for the church to be composed of them alone and
for faith and piety to belong to the church’s essence. It is one thing for the
elect to be the purer and more essential part of the church, which the papists
do not deny; it is another for them to be the church’s whole essence, so that
no other true members are to be sought outside them, which we maintain.

4. The question does not concern sinners of every kind, even grievous ones,
as the elect can also be. It concerns reprobates as such, who neither possess
true faith and repentance nor ever will.

5. The question does not concern the elect considered only in their eternal
appointment, in which sense it is certain that they cannot be actual members
of the church. It concerns them in the execution of that appointment, which
takes place through calling, by which the elect become members actually and in
fact.

V. Hence it is evident that Cardinal Perron wrongly states the question as
whether the church should be defined by predestination, which is hidden. We
wish effectual calling to be joined with predestination. Stapleton likewise
errs when he maintains that the question is whether the church consists of the
elect who are not called, as if these things were opposed when they are
subordinate to one another, especially when the inward call is in view.
Bellarmine deals a little more candidly when he says that the inquiry is
“whether only the predestined can belong to the church, or reprobates also.”
Yet he errs in speaking only of predestination, since it should not be
separated from calling.

VI. The question therefore comes down to this: Besides the elect who have been
effectually called, that is, true believers, do reprobates and unbelievers also
belong to the church and ought they to be regarded as its true members? Or
does the church’s essential form consist in the mere outward profession of
faith, or is it located in the inward reality of faith? Can the impious and
impenitent, provided that they outwardly attach themselves to the society that
professes Christ’s doctrine, be said to constitute the church, even though
otherwise destitute of faith and holiness? Or are only the pious and faithful,
who not only profess the faith but truly believe and have been regenerated,
given this name and admitted to this privilege?

The papists would affirm the former; we maintain the latter, contending: 1.
that the name *church* is predicated principally of it by reason of its inward
form; 2. that the true church’s members properly consist of the elect, and
although many others are mingled with them in outward society, those others
must never be confused with them; 3. that the promises belong to the elect
alone; and 4. that those who are Christians only with respect to outward form
and profession are, properly speaking, not members, but are such only
equivocally in the judgment of human beings, not in the judgment of God.

[Marginalia: It is proved that the church consists only of the elect who have
been called.]

[Marginalia: 1. Because the church is the body of Christ.]

VII. There are various reasons why we maintain this. 1. It is proved from the
titles given to the church in relation to Christ. These titles are such that
they cannot belong to the profane and hypocrites or be fulfilled in mere
outward profession. Thus the church is called the body of Christ, and all the
members of the church are called members of Christ (Ephesians 5:23;
Colossians 1:18; 1 Corinthians 12:27). But the body of Christ is nothing other
than that which will actually be saved and of which he is called the Savior
(Ephesians 5:23), the body that is joined together through its joints and
grows with growth from God (Ephesians 4:16). Nor are there any members except
those animated by the life of the Head, in whom Christ dwells through faith
(Ephesians 3:17), and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 13 | IA leaf 47 | PDF p. 47]

who are led by his Spirit, since “if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ,
he is not his” (Romans 8:9). No one can doubt that this cannot belong to
reprobates and unbelievers but is proper to the elect and faithful alone.

To this pertains Paul’s comparison of the church to the human body in 1
Corinthians 12. All its members are united to one another by the same bond of
the Spirit. This could not be said if some members were destitute of Christ’s
Spirit and were not so much members of Christ as members of the devil.
Finally, “Christ cannot have condemned members,” as Augustine says in *Against
Cresconius*, book 2, chapter 21, and “That which will not remain with him
forever is not truly the body of Christ,” as he likewise says in *On Christian
Doctrine*, book 2, chapter 32.

VIII. It is futile for anyone to reply with Stapleton that “the church extends
more widely than Christ’s body and also contains those who are joined by
profession alone, who are members by subjection although not by participation.”
The church in outward communion does indeed also include those who are joined
by profession alone. The same cannot be said of the inward state of which we
speak, according to which the church and Christ’s body are coextensive, as is
evident from Paul (Ephesians 5:23–24). There is no true church that is not
Christ’s body, apart from the church of which he is the Head.

Nor would one reply any better with Bellarmine that “some members of Christ’s
body are living and belong to the soul of the church, while others are dead
and withered and belong only to the body; reprobates are such members” (chapter
9). This assumes without proof that a dead member can be a member of a living
body, which is impossible, since there is no true member that does not
participate in the body’s life. Thus Bellarmine himself testifies that various
papists—Torquemada, Alexander of Hales, Hugh, Thomas, Peter de Soto, and
Cano—teach that they are members only equivocally. Bellarmine himself does not
differ when he wishes them to belong only to the body of the church, that is,
to outward communion, which no one denies, but not to its soul. Who would say
that someone who does not belong to the church’s soul truly belongs to the
church?

Nor does it help when he maintains that members can be considered in two ways,
“either as certain things or as instruments; an evil bishop is not a member in
the former sense but is in the latter, insofar as God uses him as an instrument
for his work.” It is one thing to be a member and another to be an instrument.
A member need not be an instrument, nor must an instrument always be a member.
A craftsman uses an axe, a scribe a stylus and pen, a painter a brush, and an
artisan his tools; when they are set aside, he is not thereby diminished or
mutilated in any part. And if a member sometimes has the character of an
instrument, it cannot act except as a living member animated by the same
spirit and life. It must be a member before it can be operative, and if it
ceases to be a living member, by that very fact it is no longer an instrument.
Thus fingers, the tongue, and eyes are not organs of action unless joined to
and animated by the body.

[Marginalia: 2. Because the church is the bride of Christ.]

IX. Second, the church is called the bride of Christ (Ephesians 5:23), whom he
has betrothed to himself in justice, in truth, and forever (Hosea 2:19). She is
so closely united with him that they are not two but one (Ephesians 5:31). He
loves and cherishes her as his own flesh. She must be presented to Christ as a
pure and chaste virgin (2 Corinthians 11:2) and is adorned for her Bridegroom
(Revelation 19:7; 21:2). Her marriage, begun here by betrothal in grace, must
be consummated by the wedding of the Lamb celebrated in glory (Revelation
19:9). No one would say that these attributes belong to reprobates and worldly
people, whom Christ has never united to himself. Since they cling to the world
and pollute themselves with spiritual adultery, they cannot be said to have
any communion with Christ either in grace or in glory.

[Marginalia: 3. Because the church is the sheepfold.]

X. Third, the church is the sheepfold (John 10), which is composed only of
sheep who hear Christ’s voice, acknowledge and follow him, and to whom he gives
eternal life so that they will never

[1847 vol. III printed p. 14 | IA leaf 48 | PDF p. 48]

perish. But reprobates and unbelievers are not Christ’s sheep (John 10:26):
“You are not of my sheep.” They do not acknowledge, hear, and follow him (John
8:47): “You do not hear.” Nor are they acknowledged, led, and blessed by
Christ (Matthew 7:23): “I never knew you”; and, “Depart from me, you cursed”
(Matthew 25:41).

Nor should it be objected with Perron in *Reply*, book 1, chapter 6, that
“hearing the Shepherd’s voice is the duty of sheep, but is not the essential
form of the church or of sheep.” The duty in question belongs to all Christ’s
sheep as an essential property. If anyone does not perform it, he must not be
regarded as a true sheep of Christ. Nor is the property of hearing Christ
rightly separated here from its form, since it flows necessarily from that
form—indeed, the form of mystical sheep properly consists in it.

Nor should it be added with Bellarmine that “goats are also in the sheepfold,
because they are separated at the end.” It is one thing to be *in the
sheepfold*, that is, in the place where they gather and remain with respect to
outward profession. In this way we do not deny that goats are in the sheepfold
and are therefore sometimes called sheep equivocally, because they are covered
in sheepskins and mingled with sheep. It is another thing to be *of the
sheepfold*, that is, of the society of the sheep themselves. In this sense we
deny that they can be called sheep properly so called, because they neither
hear Christ’s voice nor follow him.

[Marginalia: 4. Because the church is the house of God.]

XI. Fourth, the church is called the building and house of God (Matthew 16:18;
1 Peter 2:5; Ephesians 2:20), and it is a house that cannot consist of
reprobates and unbelievers, but only of the elect and faithful.

1. This is evident from its Author, because God is its architect (Matthew
16:18), and he does not build the impious. 2. It is evident from its material,
because its stones are living, not dead (1 Peter 2:5). 3. It is evident from
its foundation, because they are built upon the rock, that is, Christ the
cornerstone, not upon sand as the impious are (Matthew 7:24; 16:18). 4. It is
evident from its structure, because the whole building “is fitly joined
together” (Ephesians 2:21); that is, it coheres suitably and in proper
proportion through the bond of love, so that nothing is lacking, harmful, or
superfluous. But there can be no communion of the impious with the good, of
injustice with righteousness, or of light with darkness. 5. It is evident
from victory over enemies and unshaken firmness, because the gates of hell
cannot prevail against it (Matthew 16:18), nor can it collapse and be
destroyed by any assault (Matthew 7:24). This cannot be said of reprobates. 6.
It is evident from its end and use, which belong only to the pious, because it
must be “the house of the living God, the pillar and support of the truth” (1
Timothy 3:15), “a dwelling place in the Spirit and a holy temple to the Lord,”
in which he may dwell forever (Ephesians 2:22; 1 Corinthians 3:17; 2
Corinthians 6:16–17; Revelation 21:2–4).

XII. Perron objects in vain in the place cited that “the victory that the
gates of hell obtain over private persons through moral vices does not harm
the body of the church, because vices harm only those who commit or approve
them, not the communion of the church.” Since individual persons must
constitute that house as its parts (Hebrews 3:6), however, it follows that if
they fall, either wholly or in part, the house itself also collapses. The
apostle also teaches that God’s temple is destroyed through the sins of
individual persons: “If anyone destroys the temple of God, God will destroy
him” (1 Corinthians 3:17). Therefore, either those who collapse must be said
to have been placed outside the foundation, not built by the Father or joined
to the other members by the Spirit’s cement, or else—what is utterly
absurd—Christ’s provision is not effectual. For if a part of the building
falls despite its author, foundation, and structure, one may argue from the
lesser to the greater.

Nor can it be said that the promise concerns the whole church and not
individuals, so that it will always remain although some parts fall away. This
cannot be attributed to the whole except with respect to the individuals who
constitute that whole. Thus Paul does not say only that a building is fitly
joined and grows into a holy temple to the Lord, but “the whole building,”
that is, not some part but the entire building; and in Ephesians 4:16 he

[1847 vol. III printed p. 15 | IA leaf 49 | PDF p. 49]

says that “the whole body” is joined together by its joints, and so forth.
Furthermore, not only does Augustine support us here, as will be proved below,
but various papists do also. Ferus explains Matthew 16 of the church according
to the Spirit, which embraces only the elect. Jansen understands it as the
kingdom of Christ against which the kingdom of the devil will be hostile.
Cajetan understands the church that consists of the congregation of the
faithful. Maldonatus wishes the sense to be that the church founded by Christ
upon the rock will completely overcome the devil’s power, so that he can
resist it by no device and no strength.

[Marginalia: 5. Because the church of the called must correspond to
predestination.]

XIII. Fifth, the church must be such as it was conceived from eternity in
God’s decree. Calling, by which the church is gathered into the being of the
church, is nothing other than the execution and fruit of eternal election. The
true essence of anything cannot be known better than from the first idea that
its author proposed to himself, especially when in its execution he has not
departed from it in the least but has most perfectly executed what he had
conceived of it in his mind. No one can doubt that God has done this.

Now the church was appointed not merely to outward profession of faith and
holiness, but to true faith and holiness. “He chose us,” Paul says, “in Christ
that we should be holy” (Ephesians 1:4), and “he predestined us for adoption in
Christ, that we should be to the praise of his grace,” and so forth. He
“predestined us to be conformed to the image of his Son” (Romans 8:29), and
“God chose us from the beginning for salvation through sanctification of the
Spirit and faith in the truth” (2 Thessalonians 2:13).

Thus the church is described by those who are called “according to purpose”
(Romans 8:28), by “the elect according to the foreknowledge of God unto
sanctification” (1 Peter 1:2), by “the elect” (Luke 18:7), by “a chosen nation
and a people of possession” (1 Peter 2:9), and by “the assembly of the
firstborn whose names are written in heaven” (Hebrews 12:23). These
descriptions can be predicated only of the faithful, not of the impious and
reprobate.

XIV. Perron assumes without warrant that Hebrews 12 treats the church
triumphant “to which” we are said “to have come,” just as Philippians 3 says
that “our citizenship is in heaven,” and that by the *firstborn* it means the
patriarchs and fathers of the Old Testament. It does not say that this is the
assembly of those who are in heaven, but of those whose names are written in
heaven—that is, who have been elected from eternity, according to the style of
Scripture (Luke 10:20; Philippians 4:3; Revelation 21:27).

Second, *firstborn* is a general term that belongs equally to all the faithful.
Their regeneration makes them the firstfruits of the creatures and the
firstborn among human beings, and gives them the reality of the typical and
legal birthright: they are specially consecrated to God to be kings and
priests, enjoy a double portion, and be delivered from the destroyer’s sword
by the blood of the Lamb.

Third, Paul says that they have *come*, which denotes actual and real
communion, not merely one in hope. In the same way they are said to have come
to God the Judge and to Jesus the Mediator by true and real communion.

Fourth, it is so far from being necessary to understand the church triumphant
here to the exclusion of the militant that the Hebrews could not have had
communion with the church triumphant except through the militant, since the
latter is a part of the same whole. Nor can anyone be a member of the church
militant who will not in due time have a part with the church triumphant.
Bellarmine does not deny this in *On the Church Militant*, book 3, chapter 5,
when he says, “The church triumphant is united with the militant—indeed, it is
rather one with it—and therefore no one can wish to be separated from one
without being separated from the other.”

They escape no better when they reply, “If the church militant is understood,
it is called the assembly of the firstborn whose names are written in heaven,
not because only the elect are in it, but because the elect are only in it,
although others are also found in it.” John forecloses this escape when he says
in Revelation 21 that nothing unclean

[1847 vol. III printed p. 16 | IA leaf 50 | PDF p. 50]

and abominable will enter it, but only those written in the Lamb’s book of
life. For if only the elect belong to the church triumphant, only they are also
members of the militant, which differs from the triumphant only in state. The
following argument will confirm this more clearly.

[Marginalia: 6. Because on the day of judgment God will acknowledge only the
faithful elect as the church.]

XV. Sixth, the church is such as God will declare it to be on the last day,
because the church he will bless in glory is no other than that which he
justifies and regenerates here in grace. At that time Christ will not create a
new church, new believers, new children of God, and new members of the church.
He will only declare who were such in this life in order to reward them.
Indeed, he testifies that those whom he will not acknowledge as his own never
were such and were never regarded as such by him: “I never knew you” (Matthew
7:23). This is so even though they may have predicted future things,
prophesied, and performed various miracles in Christ’s name while living in
outward and visible communion.

But only the faithful and pious, who bear the name of sheep and for whom the
kingdom of heaven has been prepared by God’s eternal election, are to be
acknowledged by Christ and admitted into the church triumphant, according to
Christ’s own words: “Come, you blessed of my Father” (Matthew 25:34), while
hypocrites, the impious, and the impenitent are excluded.

[Marginalia: 7. Because the church is the communion of saints.]

XVI. Seventh, the church must be such as we believe and confess it to be in
the creed. But we believe a holy church, which itself is also the communion of
saints. It is evident that these two statements pertain to the same thing and
that the first explains the second, so that the church’s nature and quality
may be expressed more clearly. The saints are gathered into Christ’s society
on no other condition than that they mutually share with one another whatever
benefits God bestows upon them. Since holiness is therefore the church’s
proper and genuine characteristic, as Scripture teaches throughout (Isaiah
35:8; 52:11; Joel 3:17; 2 Corinthians 6:16–17; Ephesians 5:26; Song of Songs
4:7), everyone sees that none but true believers and the elect, to whom such
holiness belongs, can be its members.

XVII. It must not be said here, 1. that the church is called holy from
profession, baptism, and outward union with the Head. The question concerns a
true attribute, not an apparent and shadowy one; a real and inward holiness by
which the church is holy to God, not an outward and empty holiness that appears
such to human eyes. Profession and baptism certainly denote an outward and
sacramental holiness, but it is plainly useless—indeed, harmful—without the
inward and spiritual holiness that God chiefly regards and requires. Just as
the true Jew is not one outwardly, nor is circumcision that which is in the
flesh, but a Jew is one inwardly and circumcision is in Spirit and truth
(Romans 2:28), so also a Christian is called holy not merely from outward
profession but from inward reality.

The added statement that the church is denominated holy from Christ its Head
is true, but by this very fact our position concerning the church’s inward
holiness is confirmed. Holiness is derived from the Head into the entire body,
which participates in the Head’s life and influence. But there is no union of
the impious with Christ the Head. If they were united to Christ, they would no
longer be impious but pious—unless we wished to say that the holy Head has a
profane and impious body, which is impossible.

2. It is no better to say that it is called holy on account of one part,
because a thing is named from its better part. If it is called holy on account
of one part alone, it will have to be called impure on account of another,
since a designation is drawn not only from the better but also from the
greater part. Second, I acknowledge that the visible assembly is denominated
from its better part, but I say that the true church is that assembly of the
better part, or the better part of the assembly. For the church as such
differs from the world, and the church’s holiness consists in its being
separated from the world. Thus when the church is called holy, this is only
with respect to the assembly of true believers, not the others who, although
they are in the church, are nevertheless not of the church.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 17 | IA leaf 51 | PDF p. 51]

[Marginalia: 8. Because reprobates belong to the devil’s kingdom.]

XVIII. Eighth, those who are reprobates belong to the devil’s kingdom and
body. Therefore they cannot belong to the catholic church, nor is there any
communion between these two: “What communion has Christ with Belial, light with
darkness, injustice with righteousness, or the temple of God with idols?” (2
Corinthians 6:14–15). And, “You do not believe because you are not of my sheep”
(John 10:26). Elsewhere he calls them children of the devil: “You are of your
father the devil” (John 8:44). Who would say that such people are true members
of the church?

Nor should it be replied with Stapleton that it is not absurd for them “to
belong to God and to be estranged from God,” that is, “to belong to Christ’s
body and the devil’s body in different ways.” These things are inconsistent.
Those who belong to the devil may be called and appear to belong to God, but
they cannot actually belong to God.

Above all others, however, John’s words in 1 John 2:19 are clear. Speaking of
apostates and deceivers, he says, “They went out from us, but they were not of
us; for if they had been of us, they would surely have remained with us.” From
this I reason as follows. 1. Whoever goes out from the church is not of the
church. But reprobates go out from it. Therefore they are not of it. Again,
whoever is of the church always remains in the church. But reprobates do not
remain; they go out from it. Therefore they are not of it.

The basis of the apostle’s reasoning is the perseverance of true believers.
Those who lack it testify by that very fact that they never were what they
professed themselves to be. They were indeed among true believers and in the
visible assembly of the church, but they were not among the true believers and
the church’s true members.

Augustine explains this admirably in *On Rebuke and Grace*, chapter 9, where
he distinguishes the children of God. Some, he says, are not yet regarded by
us as children but are so regarded by God himself. Conversely, some are called
children by us who are not children to God himself. “There are indeed some,”
he says, “who on account of grace received even for a time are called children
of God by us, but are not such to God. Of them John says, ‘They went out from
us, but they were not of us,’ and so forth. He does not say, ‘They went out
from us, but because they did not remain with us, they are now not of us.’ He
says, ‘They went out from us, but they were not of us’; that is, even when
they appeared to be among us, they were not of us.” A little later he says
that “those who do not remain were not children of God even at the time when
they bore the name and profession of the children of God.”

XIX. Bellarmine replies in vain in *On the Church Militant*, book 3, chapter
10, that such people are said not to have been of us because they were not
Catholics in mind and will, but were heretics and antichrists; nevertheless,
they are said to have gone out from us because although they were not of us in
mind and will, they were of us in outward profession. In the same chapter he
adds concerning the cited passage that Augustine does not mean that they were
not truly in the church, but that they were not in it in the manner they
appeared to be; for if they were in no way within but only appeared to be,
then by the same reasoning they did not truly go out.

This is brought forward in vain. It is one thing to be in the church’s outward
communion, which we readily grant; in relation to it apostates are truly said
to go out when they fall away. It is another thing to be in and of its inward
communion, in which sense John denies that such people were of us. Bellarmine
could not deny this elsewhere, in *On Grace*, book 2, chapter 12, when he
refers this passage to predestination. “It is true,” he says, “that they are
not of us even when they appear to be of us, both because they are not among
the elect and because they have already been cut off from us in heart.”

Our adversaries also distinguish in vain here between being a child of God and
a member of the church according to the truth of essence or form and according
to the truth of the end or permanence: “Such people were indeed members of the
church in the former way, but not in the latter” (Bellarmine, book 3, chapter
7). In these matters, however, the end is so connected with essence and form
that it cannot be separated from them. Thus whoever is a child of God in the
truth of essence must also be such in the truth of the end. One cannot truly
be a child in the truth of essence without being an heir (Romans 8:17),

[1847 vol. III printed p. 18 | IA leaf 52 | PDF p. 52]

and therefore without attaining the end, namely, the inheritance, in due time.
No one can be a true member of the church except through love and the Spirit,
as Bellarmine admits in the same place from 1 John 4 and 1 Corinthians 12. But
love does not fail, nor can the Spirit once given be taken away or
extinguished, since he is an incorruptible seed and an irrevocable gift.

[Marginalia: 9. Because Christ restricts the church to the faithful.]

XX. Ninth, the church’s nature and unity must be such as Christ describes it
in John 17. When he prays not only for the apostles but also for the entire
church of believers who were to believe through their word, he asks that they
may be one with one another as he and the Father are one: “That they may be
one, as you, Father, are in me and I in you, that they also may be one in us”
(v. 21). The church’s unity must therefore follow the pattern of the most
perfect unity of the Father and Son and bear some image of it, although it
falls far below it. Who would believe that Christ has in view the outward unity
of mere profession, which is common to good and evil people, rather than the
inward and mystical unity of true faith and love?

[Marginalia: 10. Because the promises belong only to the faithful.]

XXI. Tenth, the only true church is that to which the saving promises of grace
properly belong, because they are fruits of the covenant of grace that God has
made with it. No one would say that they belong to the impious and reprobate,
who have no part in the heavenly inheritance. They belong only to the elect
and faithful, for whom the kingdom was appointed from eternity (Matthew 25),
and who are the people of God’s possession (Titus 2:14), the beloved and
vessels of grace (Romans 9:23, 25–26), the seed (Isaiah 53:10), and the
children of promise (Romans 9:8).

[Marginalia: 11. The fathers acknowledge this.]

XXII. Finally, the fathers everywhere support us here; we note only a few
passages. Clement of Alexandria says near the end of *Stromata*, book 7: “From
what has been said, therefore, I think it has become evident that there is one
true church, the truly ancient one, into which the righteous according to
God’s purpose are enrolled.”

Cyprian says in epistle 55: “‘Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of
eternal life, and we have believed and known that you are the Son of the living
God.’ He thus signifies and shows that those who have departed from Christ
perish by their own fault, but that the church which believes in Christ and,
once it has known something, holds it fast, never departs from him at all;
those are the church who remain in the house of God. But those whom we see not
made firm with the stability of wheat but blown about like chaff by the spirit
of the scattering enemy are not the planting planted by God the Father,” and
so forth. And in epistle 76: “This is my judgment: whoever has obtained divine
grace in the church by the law and right of faith is to be regarded as a
lawful Christian.” See *On the Unity of the Church*, chapters 4 and 5.

Ambrose says near the end of his exposition of Psalm 35, “As the saints are
members of Christ, so the impious are members of the devil.” Gregory the Great
says in book 28 on Job, chapter 9, “Within these measurements, therefore, are
all the elect; outside them are all the reprobate, even if they appear to be
within the boundary of faith. Thus it is written in Revelation 11: ‘Cast out
the court which is outside the temple, and do not measure it,’” and so forth.
Jerome says on Psalm 101 and Ephesians 5, “One who is a sinner and stained with
some defilement,” and so forth, “is not called part of Christ’s church.”

XXIII. But before all others Augustine established this truth in innumerable
passages, from which we select some of the clearer ones. In *On Christian
Doctrine*, book 2, chapter 32: “That which will not be with him forever is not
truly the Lord’s body.” And in the same place: “Hypocrites must be said not to
be with him, not only forever but even now, although they appear to be in his
church.” In *On the Unity of the Church*, chapter 4: “Whoever disagrees with
the Holy Scriptures about the Head himself is not in the church, even if he is
found in all the places in which the church is designated.” And in chapter 18:
“Since both good and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 19 | IA leaf 53 | PDF p. 53]

evil people give and receive the sacrament of baptism, and only the good who
have been spiritually regenerated are built into the body and members of
Christ, this church is certainly among the good, as it is said, ‘As a lily
among thorns, so is my beloved among the daughters.’ It is among those who
build upon the rock, that is, who hear the word and do it. It is therefore not
among those who build upon sand,” and so forth.

And in *On Baptism*, book 1, chapter 17: “What is chaff is always chaff,
separated from the unity of that church which is without wrinkle and spot,
even when it is mingled with the congregation of saints in carnal hardness.”
In book 5, chapter 27: “What is said in the Song of Songs, where the church is
described in this way—‘A garden enclosed, my sister, my bride, a fountain
sealed, a well of living water, a paradise with the fruit of apples’—I do not
dare understand except of the holy and righteous,” and so forth. In book 6,
chapter 3: “It is most plainly evident from this that the evil do not belong to
God’s church, although they appear to be within.”

And in book 7, chapter 51: “I think I do not speak rashly when I say that some
are in the house of God in such a way that they themselves are also the same
house of God which is said to be built upon the rock, which is called the one
dove, the beautiful bride without spot,” and so forth. “But it is evident that
others are said to be in the house in such a way that they do not belong to
the structure of the house or to the society of fruitful and peaceable
righteousness,” and so forth.

In *Against Cresconius*, book 2, chapter 21: “Those who have been condemned by
Christ on account of an evil and polluted conscience are no longer in the
body of Christ, which is the church, because Christ cannot have condemned
members.” And in tractate 3 on 1 John 2: “All heretics,” he says, “and all
schismatics went out from us; that is, they go out from the church. But they
would not go out if they were of us; therefore, before they went out they were
not of us.” Afterward he adds that “they are not among the members, because
members are never opposed to one another, but they are like evil fluids in the
body. These fluids went out from me, but they were not of me. What does this
mean, ‘They were not of me’? It means that they were not cut off from my
flesh,” and so forth.

It must not be said that Augustine ever retracted this position. In
*Retractions*, book 2, chapter 18, for example, he testifies that what he said
in *On Baptism* about the church without wrinkle and spot must not be
understood as though it were already such, but because it is being prepared to
be such when it appears glorious. He said this against the Pelagians, who
asserted the sinlessness of a faithful person. His purpose was to teach that
various sins that must be corrected still remain in the church militant, on
account of which, he adds, they ought to say daily, “Forgive us our debts.” His
purpose was not to maintain that the true church is composed of both good and
evil people.

Although in *Against the Donatists after the Conference*, chapter 20, he shows
that in the outward body of the church evil people are always mingled with the
good for as long as it lives on earth, he does not therefore maintain that
evil people properly belong to the true church, which is constituted from the
good. Indeed, he expressly teaches that the good are separated from them: “Now
by living well they are spiritually separated from such people, but afterward
they will also be separated bodily.” And in the same place: “Therefore, they
were in one body, both mingled and separated—mingled by bodily contact, but
separated by a withdrawal of the will.” And in tractate 3 on John’s epistle:
“They are in Christ’s body in the way evil fluids are. When these are vomited
out, the body is relieved; so also when evil people go out, the church is
relieved and says, ‘These fluids went out from me, but they were not of me,’”
and so forth. Thus Bellarmine in book 3, chapter 9, wishes them to be in the
church as fluids, teeth, hairs, and other things that are not members.

[Marginalia: Sources of the answers.]

XXIV. It is one thing to be in the church in visible communion and to use the
same profession of faith and the same sacraments. It is another to be of the
church’s inward and invisible communion and to be bound together by the same
bond of faith and the Spirit. I grant that reprobates and the impious are in
the church in the former sense, but deny it in the latter. Christ denies it:
“You are of your father the devil” (John 8:44), and “You are not of my sheep”
(John 10:26). John denies it: “They were not of us,” he says (1 John 2:19).
And just as “not all

[1847 vol. III printed p. 20 | IA leaf 54 | PDF p. 54]

who are from Israel are Israel” (Romans 9:6)—that is, those descended from
Israel according to the flesh are nevertheless not the true Israel according
to the Spirit, to whom the promises were made—so not all who are in the church
are nevertheless of the church.

XXV. When the church is compared to a threshing floor in which chaff is mixed
with wheat, a net in which there are good and bad fish, and a wedding feast at
which there is a guest without a wedding garment, it is considered with
respect to its outward and visible state, not its inward and invisible state.
These parables prove that the church militant on earth is mixed with and
confused among a great foreign multitude. They do not prove that the catholic
church consists of and is composed of those people. Augustine distinguishes
well here between being *mixed* and being *a part*, between adhering or growing
alongside something and being a member. Earth is joined to gold, chaff to
wheat, and bad fish to good, but the earth is not therefore gold, the chaff is
not wheat, and the bad fish are not good. “Thus whether they appear to live
within or are openly outside, what is flesh is flesh and what is chaff is
chaff,” Augustine says in *On Baptism*, book 1, chapter 17.

XXVI. The great house in which there are gold and silver vessels for honor and
earthen and wooden ones for dishonor (2 Timothy 2:20) is either the world, in
which there are good as well as evil people, as Chrysostom and Theophylact
explain it; or, better, the visible church. In it are the good, illustrious in
faith and holiness, who are grace’s golden vessels; and the evil, or hypocrites
and the impious, who for a time display some piety and faith but afterward
fall back miserably and show themselves to be vessels prepared for dishonor.
They are therefore in the house but not of the house, within it in body but
not in soul.

XXVII. It is one thing for only the elect and predestined to be members of the
church, which we maintain; it is another for election alone to suffice to
constitute someone a member of the church, which we deny. Together with
election, effectual calling is required, which is the effect of election.
Therefore most of our adversaries’ arguments are empty because they assume
that, on our view, election alone makes someone a member of the church.

Thus they say that on our hypothesis, 1. “Turks, Muslims, and heretics can be
members of the church, provided they are predestined.” But they cannot be
called actual members of the church until they are effectually called,
although they are members by appointment, just as Christ called Gentiles who
had not yet been called his sheep (John 10:16).

2. They say that “everything will be uncertain, and neither the sheep will
know their shepherd, nor the shepherd his sheep, nor brothers one another,
since predestination is hidden in God, not in man.” Although no one can know
this *a priori*, which belongs to God alone, who knows those who are his (2
Timothy 2:19), it can nevertheless be known *a posteriori* by the judgment of
love from the fruits of faith and love (John 13:35; 1 John 3:9–10). And though
we cannot form a certain judgment concerning this or that individual, whether
he is a true member of the church, we can nevertheless know with certainty
what the true church of Christ is and where it is.

XXVIII. All who have been baptized inwardly as well as outwardly are true
members of the church, because in baptism they put on Christ (Galatians 3:27)
and are baptized into one body (1 Corinthians 12:13). But the case of
reprobates and the impious is not the same. They are indeed baptized outwardly
but not inwardly: with water for washing away the filth of the flesh (1 Peter
3:21), but not with the Spirit for the cleansing of sin; with the visible
symbol, but not with the invisible thing signified.

XXIX. The church is rightly compared to an organic body that has diverse
members and functions with respect to the diversity of gifts and ministries,
but not with respect to a diversity of states, as though some were elect and
others reprobate. In this sense there is identity of essence, because all must
be living members of Christ’s body. They therefore differ materially in
nation, sex, age, condition, and disposition, but agree formally in the same
faith and Spirit. And if some people discharge offices in the church but are
nevertheless not true believers, they belong only

[1847 vol. III printed p. 21 | IA leaf 55 | PDF p. 55]

to the outward body, not the inward and mystical one. They are instruments for
building the house, but are not the house itself or a part of it. Thus the
builders of Noah’s ark were not saved in it, and the servants of King Hiram
who labored in building the temple were pagans.

XXX. Although we regard only true believers as members of the church, we do
not therefore favor the error of the Novatians, Cathars, and Donatists, or of
the Anabaptists of our own day. The papists slanderously attribute to us the
view of those who wish the church to consist of perfectly holy people. Besides
the fact that hypocrites are mingled with true believers in outward communion,
the elect, who alone formally belong to Christ’s mystical body, are always
subject to various blemishes and sins for as long as they live on earth (1
John 1:8). Thus the moon never shines so brightly that it does not still
retain various spots.
