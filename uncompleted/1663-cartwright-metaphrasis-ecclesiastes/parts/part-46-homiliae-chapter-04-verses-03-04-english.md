VERSE 3. But again I saw this to be vanity beneath the sun.

VERSE 4. There is one man who lacks a second, who has neither son nor brother;
yet there is no end to all his labor, and his eye is not satisfied with
riches, so that he says, “For whom do I labor, depriving my soul even of good?”
This too is vanity and an evil occupation.

The vanity of this inactivity and sloth, clear in itself, is condemned on
greater authority by the Holy Spirit through Solomon, since it is entangled
in many troubles even in this life, as has been shown.

He opposes to the inactive and idle man one who sets no limit for himself in
labor. Because this man sees that the idle man is hated by everyone, he trusts
that he will be approved for allowing himself no respite from labor. Yet he is
no less at fault than the idle man discussed before. Thus fools fall into
Charybdis while avoiding Scylla. The idle man considers the trouble which
wearies those who have performed excellent deeds when they are scorched by
the kindled flame of envy, and so corrupts himself in idleness. In the same
way, the miser looks upon the disgrace and poverty which overwhelm the idle
man and commits himself to labors. See Genesis 28:8; Numbers 14:4, 40–44; John
13:8–9. Thus, turning aside either to the right or the left,

we never keep to the straight path as long as we follow our corrupt and
depraved reason as guide.

The cause assigned for this is an insatiable desire to possess things. It is
intensified by its object: the miser cannot be filled even by the things he
sees with his eyes, although he were to possess them all. Thus he imitates the
grave, which is never satisfied, and the fire, which never says, “There is
enough wood.” He joins house to house and field to field as though he alone
were to dwell on earth. Yet the desire for possession is not quenched by
possessing, but is further kindled and inflamed: love of money grows as much
as money itself does. Nor is he unlike the elephant in Job 40:14, which trusts
that it can drain the whole river Jordan.

He next convicts this avarice by its effect: the miser cheats his own natural
appetite and allows himself scarcely anything in food or in the care of his
body to pass this life comfortably and strengthen himself against its
troubles and labors. Yet God supplies us all these things abundantly so that
we may use them. Therefore the apostle exhorts the rich to be ready to give
and prepared to share with others. 1 Timothy 6:17–18. It is not to be expected
that a man who has so constrained himself that he denies himself the supports
of this life will exercise beneficence toward others. For in proportion as
many things are stored up, many must also be brought out. God has therefore
given us two hands: with one we gather the necessities of life, and with the
other we bring what has been gathered into common use for life. It is judged
cruel to muzzle an ox that labors and treads out the grain; how much more
savage and cruel is it to waste yourself with hunger and lack of food after
labors have exhausted you? Men commonly compare the miser to a pig because he
is of no use except at his death. According to Solomon's description, however,
the miser is worse than a pig. A pig fattens itself by feeding and afterward
becomes useful to its master and his entire household. The miser begrudges
himself his own benefit and necessary food.

He magnifies this vanity by contrasting circumstances. The miser is so greedy
and tenacious even though he has no second person—neither son nor brother—to
name as heir or to succeed him in his heaped-up wealth. We learned above that
the ardent zeal for accumulating riches which makes men fathers of children
ought to be cooled, since we often do not know whether a child will become
foolish or wise. How much more ought the fact that we have neither son nor
brother to succeed to what we acquired by labor break our desire to possess?
Hence Abraham appeared to make little of God's bounty, though God had promised
it to him lavishly, because he had no son to appoint as heir unless perhaps he
adopted his servant Eliezer as heir.

It follows from this that men who have no children ought to relax their zeal
for acquiring wealth. Parents, to be sure, ought as a duty to store something
up for their children—2 Corinthians 12:14—and, in proportion to the number of
their children, to exert their zeal and strength in these affairs. Yet even
if they abound exceedingly, they ought to establish a limit in these things
which they must not pass. The limit is that before all else they seek the
things which belong to the kingdom of heaven, in the glory of God and in
their own salvation and that of others.

Even if they are parents of numerous offspring, have amassed great wealth,
and are most blessed in the things of this life, David teaches from his own
example that no one knows who will gather what he has amassed. Psalm 39:6–7.
The experience of our times confirms this. Men have combined their counsels
and sought out ingenious devices from every quarter by which they might
transmit their inheritance to all their posterity without interruption. Yet
no one has yet been found who

ever accomplished what he attempted. Therefore, since no one should exceed
the limit in accumulating riches even in the most secure and desirable
condition, much less should he exceed it when so deprived of sons and
brothers. The prophet confirms the matter further in the same place, Psalm
39:6, when he declares that man's days are like a handbreadth because of their
brevity.

He relates another cause of this extreme tenacity: the miser never reasons
with himself and his own mind or asks to what end he sets no limit in amassing
wealth; he never calls himself to account. It is therefore worthwhile to
remember David's admonition in Psalm 4:5. If the miser were to discuss the
matter with his own mind in this way—“What fruit returns to me from so much
labor and anxious care? I am not refreshed by the labor of my hands, nor is
there anyone joined to me by blood who will possess by hereditary right after
my death the things I have amassed”—he would plainly desist from such great
madness. The proverb “a dog in the manger” therefore truly applies to him.

God reproaches in many other cases this brutish stupidity by which men refuse
to reason with themselves and their own minds. Someone might ask here why
Solomon brought this kind of man forward to prove the vanity of life, since
scarcely ten men could be found in an entire province who have neither son nor
brother to succeed them at death. I answer that Solomon selected this man to
magnify the shamefulness of the matter, while comprehending under him other
misers who would be affected no differently even though they do not lack
heirs. The difference is that the others have something with which to cloak
their avarice, while this man is deprived of every pretext.

Having indicated the usefulness of human society in general, he pursues it in
its parts.

The parts are the usefulness of human society
in general and in its parts.
