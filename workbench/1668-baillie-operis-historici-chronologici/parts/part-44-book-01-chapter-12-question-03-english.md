### QUESTION III

#### A Time, Times, and Half a Time

We read this expression, *a time, times*, and the division or *half of a time*,
in Daniel 7:25, likewise 12:7, and Revelation 12:14. What span of time is
expressed by this phrase in these parallel passages is exceedingly difficult
to explain. Although I would not wish to appropriate Pareus’s hyperbole here,
“as many opinions as heads,” it is nevertheless certain that resolving this
doubt—like many others in sacred chronology—requires

> Marginalia, printed p. 242: *The question’s difficulty*.

[Printed p. 243 | IA leaf 273 | access PDF 273 | OCR object 272]

the singular counsel of Palmoni, Jesus Christ, the marvelous enumerator. The
prophet Daniel himself
could not resolve the similar but much easier question about times in 9:14
until the angel Palmoni explained it to him.

This name is compound, either from the almost proverbial words *Peloni Almoni*,
denoting a marvelous man or such a man whose name is kept silent and not
expressed, from פלא, *was marvelous or hidden*, and אלם, *became mute, was
silent*. These fit Christ most aptly, whose name is marvelous, hidden, and
secret. Or, as Calvin and others hold, it is from פלא and מנה, *he counted*,
so that Palmoni signifies a marvelous enumerator, or the enumerator of secret
times which God hides from human eyes, and thus the resolver of every
chronological doubt. This encomium is suited to none so well as to Christ, the
infinite wisdom of the Father. Trusting in his aid, we approach this very
difficult question.

> Marginalia, printed p. 243: *Who Peloni Almoni is*.

We shall not quarrel over the translation of the words. For although there is
some difference between the Chaldean of chapter 7 and the Hebrew of chapter
12—since in the former passage all the vernacular translations, with Arias
Montanus, rightly render פלג עדן as *a part of a time*, not, with the Latin
Vulgate following the Seventy’s ἥμισυ καιροῦ, as *half a time*—the principal
hinge of the controversy does not turn on the difference between the words
*part* and *half*. In chapter 12, moreover, we read חצי, *half*; and the
Apostle in Revelation, apparently looking back to both passages in Daniel,
writes *half*. When he descends more particularly either to years or to days,
he counts precisely as many as constitute half the years. Accordingly, just
as I would not press too hard, with the very learned Junius and his many
followers, the addition of ten days to three years, neither would I press,
with others, the complete identity of these passages, as though what Daniel
had called a *part* in general in chapter 7, he himself in chapter 12 and the
Apostle in Revelation had determined to be exactly a half.

> Marginalia, printed p. 243: *The difference between a part and a half is
> slight*.

There are greater controversies about the matter itself. First we shall refute
the opinions that displease us, and then confirm the one we think most likely
to be true.

First we reject the invention of the more recent rabbis, as Jacchiades explains
it on Daniel 7, 8, and 12 in L’Empereur. He interprets *a time and times* as the
appointed duration of times, or a season of two times. He holds that those two
times are twice a thousand years, because, according to Moses in Psalm 90, a
day for God extends to a thousand years, and because Adam died on the same day
that he ate from the forbidden tree, since he did not complete his thousandth
year. As for the part of a time, Jacchiades interprets it as part of a
millennium, namely three hundred years. Thus he wishes the appointed duration
of two times and a part of a time to mean 2,300 years, which he says Daniel
8:14 expresses explicitly. When those have elapsed, the Messiah is to come
and the Jews are to rise, so that with the Messiah they may spend in earthly
pleasures those three hundred years Daniel mentions at the end of chapter 12.
Finally, when the world has been abolished and bodies dissolved into nothing,
they are to live with the Messiah in their pure souls in heavenly and
everlasting glory. They count 3,391 years from the beginning of the world to
the times of Cyrus and Daniel and add 2,300 to them, so that the Messiah is to
come at approximately the year of the world 5700 and the world is to be
destroyed at the end of the six-thousandth year, as though at the end of the
sixth day of the divine week, so that on the Sabbath and seventh day, at the
beginning of the seven-thousandth year, the eternal happiness of the new world
may begin.

> Marginalia, printed p. 243: *The rabbis’ opinion attacked*.

We reject this opinion of the more recent rabbis—although in far too many
respects it shares the delusion of the more recent chiliasts in England—on
account of the many falsehoods it contains. There is first the greatest
absurdity, that it postpones the Messiah’s advent for a full 1,700 years after
the time most explicitly marked by Daniel in chapter 9, shortly before
Jerusalem was destroyed by the Romans. There is also the second absurdity,
that it places the Jews’ bodily resurrection three hundred years before the
end of the world and the universal judgment, and the third, that it destroys
every person’s body forever and grants eternal life only to souls without
bodies, contrary not only to many Scriptures but also to many of the rabbis
themselves.

Leaving these aside, we note other peculiar absurdities in this opinion. We do
not, with L’Empereur, censure him for interpreting the fourth beast as Rome
rather than Antiochus, the ten kings as those who take possession of parts of
the Roman Empire simultaneously rather than successively, and the little horn
as one or a pair of men differing in religion, the Turk and the Roman pontiff.
In these oversights L’Empereur follows the hypotheses of Junius that we refuted
above. But the following are the points we censure in Jacchiades together with
L’Empereur.

First. To make the text serve his stage, he corrupts the sacred text and
deletes the conjunction *and*. All the manuscripts read *a time and times*;
after striking out *and*, he reads *a time of times* or *the appointed
duration of times*. By thus joining the two words in a governing construction,
he makes the first word, *time*, signify not even one

[Printed p. 244 | IA leaf 274 | access PDF 274 | OCR object 273]

year. Yet if *times* were to be interpreted as millennia, *a time*
would necessarily have to be interpreted as an entire millennium, so that,
contrary to all the Jews’ reckonings, the Messiah’s advent would necessarily
be postponed until the seven-thousandth year. Moreover, although the
conjunction is elided in Daniel 12:7, the distinctive accent *Paschta*, the
first of the royal accents and equivalent to a comma, is expressly written. It
is equivalent to the conjunction and denotes the half of two times, whatever
interval those two times may indicate.

Second. Whether, with many, you extend *a time and times* to one year and two
years, that is, a three-year period, or with others to one hundred and two
hundred, that is, three hundred years, no sober person has ever extended these
times to millennia. Granted, a thousand years or a hundred thousand years,
compared with God’s eternity, are altogether nothing and like yesterday when it
has passed away. Yet sacred Scripture nowhere mentions any day consisting of
a thousand years. The examples either in Genesis or in the Psalm written by
Moses do nothing to support this dream of an unsound brain. Indeed, if the
passages in Daniel were to be interpreted as days each answering to a thousand
years, those three and a half years would have to be multiplied into as many
thousand years as there are days in those three and a half years, namely 3,870,
so that the Messiah would not be expected (as I calculate on the spur of the
moment) before the year 7,260. Yet no one has ever affirmed this.

Third. In interpreting the part of a time as three hundred years, he derives
this number from Daniel 8:14. But that passage is not parallel to those now
before us. The latter, according to every interpreter, concerns the third
beast and Antiochus Epiphanes; the former, according to us and most
interpreters, concerns the fourth and its little horn. The latter concerns
2,300 natural days, or six years and some months, nearly twice the period of
three and a half years. It is evident that those days must be understood as
natural days, both because the event in the history of Antiochus answers to
them clearly enough and because in verse 26 the Prophet expressly describes
these days by morning and evening, something attributed in all Scripture to no
days except natural ones.

As for Jacchiades’s objection from the same verse, that the vision in chapter 8
cannot be understood of Antiochus because it is said to concern many days:
Answer. From Daniel to Antiochus there were very many days, indeed several
centuries of years—as many as the whole Persian monarchy and nearly the whole
Greek monarchy lasted.

Finally, we observe fourth that Rabbi Jacchiades was more cautious here than
some of the English chiliasts. They placed the Jews’ conversion, the overthrow
of the Roman Empire and even of the Turks, and the advent either of Christ
himself or of his glorious kingdom on earth at a time so near that they have
already been convicted of rashness or can be convicted by people still alive.
Jacchiades, however, postpones the Messiah’s first advent so long that he was
certain no one living with him, nor anyone else now on earth, could convict him
of vanity. Indeed, if another millennium were added to his very long
reckoning, he could easily excuse himself by the inadvertent omission of only
the little word *and*, or even of a semicolon that could then easily be added
to supply the new thousand.

The second opinion is that of the Roman Catholics, who in both the passages in
Daniel and those in Revelation interpret the interval before us as the
persecution of Antichrist, not Antiochus, lasting precisely three and a half
years. Indeed, chiefly upon this foundation they demonstrate—to use the phrase
of Bellarmine, Sanders, Lessius, and other Jesuits—that the Roman pontiff
cannot be Antichrist and that Antichrist has not yet come into the world. Many
of our men have written many things in this dispute against the Jesuits. For
our present purpose we note only these few points.

> Marginalia, printed p. 244: *The Roman Catholics’ opinion is false*.

First. Those who maintain that Antiochus Epiphanes is designated by Daniel in
the passages cited—as Scaliger, Junius, Rollock, and many others of our men
contend by various arguments—overthrow the first foundation of the Roman
Catholic opinion. For even if it is granted that the fury of Antiochus’s
tyranny continued for three and a half years, what does that have to do with
Antichrist?

Next. Even if John in his prophetic history of Antichrist in Revelation
alludes to the times of Antiochus, must the times of the type therefore be so
accommodated to the times of the antitype that the latter cannot extend beyond
the former’s precise limits? Such a strict identity between the durations of
types and antitypes is not ordinarily required.

Third. What if we were to grant the Roman Catholics—a point on which the
orthodox are not particularly difficult—that in the long series of Roman
antichrists someone should arise at the end of the age who, though surrounded
by exceedingly wicked companions, is himself yet worse, preeminent in
wickedness, and resembles Antiochus’s days most closely? They would gain
nothing from this concession.

We observe, second, that Calvin, Bullinger, and the others whom we followed in
the preceding question, when they deny that

[Printed p. 245 | IA leaf 275 | access PDF 275 | OCR object 274]

Antiochus is designated by the little horn (at least in Daniel 7) and apply the
whole fourth beast to the Roman kingdom, nevertheless concede nothing to the
Roman Catholics in the controversy about Antichrist. For it makes no
difference to this matter whether Antiochus, Antichrist, or anyone else is
indicated by the Prophet in that passage. The whole present question concerns
the duration of that enemy of God, whoever he may finally be. Even if it were
proved that Daniel everywhere speaks only of Antiochus and attributes *a time,
times, and half a time* to him alone, the difficulty would remain untouched in
the passages of Revelation. For just as Junius and the crowd of his followers
interpret *a time, times, and half a time*, and the days and months that make
up three and a half years, in Revelation as prophetic days, months, and years,
where each day is extended into a year, what would prevent us and others from
using the same interpretation in Daniel? By it, in assigning three and a half
years to the Roman pontiff’s supreme tyranny, we would no more be compelled to
confine it within the bounds of astronomical years than those who extend an
equal number of their prophetic years to 1,260 astronomical years.

We observe, third, that those who from the cited passages of either Daniel or
John give Antichrist precisely three and a half years seem to do so without
the support of any suitable reason. Their trumpeting the authority of the
Fathers at the top of their lungs will move no sensible man. For what does the
human authority of the Fathers without Scripture contribute to divine faith?
How very little weight the Fathers have when they try to expound the obscurities
of prophecies not yet fulfilled according to the hypotheses of traditions
that the Roman Catholics themselves admit with us are false. Nearly all of
them acknowledge that the Fathers, misled by the pope’s fables, absorbed many
dreams of the chiliasts and left behind much that is uncertain, frivolous,
and false about the kingdoms of Christ and Antichrist—things rejected today
by the Jesuits no less than by us. They ought not, therefore, to press us in
this argument with the Fathers’ authority.

How great the rashness is of those who, through profound indolence, embrace
all the Fathers’ dicta without any examination is palpable in the present
matter. In book 20 of *The City of God*, chapter 23, Augustine proves that by
*times* Daniel understands two years and no more, because the Hebrew text (he
means the Chaldean dialect of Hebrew) reads the word in the dual, not the
plural: for the Hebrews, he says, *are said*, like the Greeks, to have a dual
number. The most reverend Father candidly says *are said*, for he himself was
entirely ignorant of what the Hebrews did or did not have. But this, like
other matters in Hebrew, he believed with implicit faith from Jerome, who was
most skilled in those letters. The Jesuit Coquaeus, in his notes on this
passage, made the annotations of Ludovicus Vives worse, as he often does
elsewhere, by omitting many things. He borrows everything he has from the
Jesuit Vives without acknowledgment, but each commentator passes over the
gross error I address. Vives does note part of Augustine’s error, indicating
that the Greek interpreter has the plural καιρῶν, not the dual καιροῖν, but
does not explain what the Chaldean has here. In the matter itself I am not so
surprised at Augustine’s simplicity in receiving from Jerome what he was unable
to examine himself as I am at Jerome’s—I will not say intention to falsify, but
certainly his far too careless precipitancy in dictating. There is not one
iota in Daniel about the dual number. For both the Chaldean text at 7:25,
*iddanin*, עדנין, and the Hebrew at chapter 12, *mognadim*, מועדים, plainly
have the plural, not the dual. If anyone wished to invent a Hebrew manuscript
of Jerome’s different from our printed copies, how would this novel invention
be proved? Especially since the Greek interpreter, indeed all the Latin ones,
and Jerome’s own translation expressly give the plural here.

> Marginalia, printed p. 245: *Augustine misled by Jerome*.

We observe, fourth, that three and a half years are ill fitted to Antichrist’s
kingdom if you consult the rest of Scripture, especially the passages that
make the day of Christ’s advent hidden from all angels, indeed even from Christ
himself as man, much less other mortals, and say that Christ will come to
judgment unexpectedly like a thief at night. The opinion we are refuting,
however, makes the day of Christ’s advent utterly determinate and perfectly
clear to the whole world. As soon as Antichrist appears, count from there
three and a half years, concerning which Daniel 12:7 speaks, then thirty
additional days expressed in verse 11, and finally the forty-five days named
in verse 12. According to Bellarmine and most Roman Catholics, you will then
have the very day of Christ’s advent with absolute certainty, clarity, and
precision. Yet this contradiction of the plain Scriptures is so gross that
both Ribera on Revelation 20 and Alcazar on chapter 11 reject it. Both teach
with us that the day of the Lord is altogether unknown to mortals, and thus
that Antichrist’s kingdom ought not to be determined by such a short and
distinct period of three and a half years.

Finally, we observe fifth that this Roman Catholic opinion does not agree very
well with their other positions even in this argument. For they customarily
ascribe to Antichrist deeds that would be impossible for any man, however much
aided by an evil spirit, to perform in the threefold period

[Printed p. 246 | IA leaf 276 | access PDF 276 | OCR object 275]

we are discussing. Alexander the Macedonian, who far surpassed all mortals in
the magnitude,
number, and speed of his victories, spent twelve years subduing some part of
Asia and a small part of Africa. Will that man of sin subject the whole globe
in three and a half years? They hold that in the same period Antichrist will
draw the Jewish people—by far the most numerous of all the nations living on
earth today, dispersed far and wide through Europe, Africa, Asia, and
America—into his most foul heresy and idolatry, even before beginning his war
against the nations. Will that Jewish world, notorious above all mortals for
obstinacy and hardness of heart in believing anything, and particularly
averse to idolatry since the burning of the first temple, be persuaded in so
brief a moment of time universally to embrace all Antichrist’s treachery and
most horrible idolatry? Christ and the Apostles, with the miraculous presence
of the Holy Spirit, spent many years bringing part of the world to the true
faith. Will the diabolical illusions of Antichrist so greatly surpass divine
power? Solomon, the richest and wisest of kings, with several hundred thousand
laborers could erect the temple and royal palace only in twenty-one years.
Will this villain, in so small a portion of his three and a half years, finish
the temple, royal palaces, and all Jerusalem rebuilt most magnificently from
the foundations? Are all the Christian churches, the whole clergy, and the
Roman pontiff himself so poorly founded in the faith that within a few months
they could all either willingly be persuaded, or by persecution compelled, to
open apostasy from Christ to Antichrist? That the limits of so brief a period
should suffice any one mortal, supported by however great a created power, to
accomplish so many and such great things is so unlike the truth that the
Jesuit Alcazar, in his laborious commentary on Revelation 11:2, openly abandons
his companions’ opinion. He takes the three and a half years attributed by the
Fathers to Antichrist as prophetic and parabolic, that is, as years that must
be extended far beyond astronomical limits.

The third opinion is that of Hugo Grotius and his parasitical imitator Dr.
Hammond. Anyone who consults their commentaries on the passages before us will
notice that here, as so often elsewhere, they pursue peculiar and absurd
novelties. They hold that Daniel everywhere indicates the times of Antiochus
and makes no mention whatever of Antichrist; in this they follow Junius closely
enough. But in Revelation they are astonishingly changeable and brazen.
Against all Protestants, they assign Antichrist only three and a half years
and hold that our interval in chapters 11, 12, and 13 alike is precisely
astronomical, without any extension of the natural days, so that, gratifying
the Roman Catholics by this reasoning, they may remove every mark of
Antichrist far from their pontiff. Indeed, when some of our men—especially the
very learned Maresius, with moderation enough and excessive reverence for
Grotius’s name—opposed these novelties, Grotius not only raged against Maresius
but poured out a great flood of slanders and insults against all the Reformed
churches. He did not set a limit to his fury until he had fully disclosed his
offense at the grossest heresies, idolatries, and tyrannies that the Council of
Trent itself offers the world, and had heaped whole wagonloads of abuse upon
the entire Protestant nation, because he did not find them sufficiently ready,
at his warning, to acknowledge their schism and submit their necks again to
the old pontiff’s yoke, too rashly cast off long ago. This is the catastrophe
of the tragedy of the Dutch Arminians. By it they showed their English Laudian
followers—especially Dr. Hammond, the present leader of that party—a very
level road toward Rome, except that both delight all too often in wandering
aside onto the byway of Socinianism. In any event, I attempt to show how
foolishly Grotius and Hammond each corrupt the passages before us, with any
bearer of the Christian name as judge (I do not reject the Roman Catholics
themselves here).

> Marginalia, printed p. 246: *The absurdity of Grotius’s and Hammond’s
> opinion*.

They interpret the forty-two months and 1,260 days of chapter 11, verses 2, 3,
and following, as the three and a half astronomical years during which the
city of Jerusalem, rebuilt by Aelius Hadrian under his name Aelia, was trodden
down by the gentiles of the Roman colony, who worshiped idols in the temple of
Jupiter Olympius built on Zion. They say it was likewise trodden down by
profane Jews, against whom two Christian churches, one from the Jews and the
other from the gentiles, then gathered in Jerusalem, and their two bishops,
preached during the stated period. This continued until Barcozab, leader of
the seditious Jews, seized Aelia and crushed those two churches because they
adhered to the Roman side against him. After lying as though dead beneath
Barcozab’s feet for a short time, they were liberated by God’s miraculous power
when the Roman legions drove Barcozab from Jerusalem; they flourished again,
continuing in Jerusalem to this day and destined to continue there forever.

> Marginalia, printed p. 246: *The falsity of their commentaries on Revelation
> 11*.

We attack this most wretched corruption of the apostolic text, contrary to the
opinion of all

[Printed p. 247 | IA leaf 277 | access PDF 277 | OCR object 276]

Christians ancient and modern, from the text itself as follows. First. What
the angel measured in verses 1 and 2 was something existing in the nature of
things, which God pursued with singular love and honor. But in Hadrian’s days
the temple at Jerusalem and the altar of burnt offering did not exist in the
nature of things, nor has anyone worshiped God in that place from those times
to the present day. How little regard God had after Christ’s death for those
places once most holy, and how great his aversion from them, is evident not
only from Titus’s dreadful desolation of those places but also from God’s
repeated vengeance in Julian’s days and from the most foul abomination now
visible there: a Turkish mosque that for several centuries has stood in the
very courts of the Holy of Holies.

Second. What was given to the gentiles to trample was the temple’s outer court,
that part of Mount Moriah beyond the bronze altar which was enclosed by its own
wall for the people’s sacred assemblies. But that whole court and all Mount
Moriah as the temple’s ancient site, so far as the history is clear even
according to the opponents, were excluded from Hadrian’s new city Aelia.

Third. The city trampled by the gentiles was holy. But Hadrian’s Aelia was not
such a city, since it was filled with profane gentiles who rejected even the
name Jerusalem. It was not built on the old city’s ruins but so recoiled from
all the former holiness of the neighboring place that it had an image of a
swine depicted on its very gate as a mark of filth and pollution.

Fourth. The city mentioned by the Apostle was so monstrous in lust and the
persecution of the saints that it was distinguished by the titles Sodom and
Egypt. Yet no one has recorded anything of the sort about Aelia in Hadrian’s
days.

Fifth. The Holy City was trampled by the gentiles for only three and a half
years. But the temple of Jupiter Olympius stood on Zion and pagan gentiles
ruled Aelia for more than two hundred years, until Constantine’s reign.

Sixth. The distinction between two churches at Jerusalem, one of Jews and the
other of gentiles, having two bishops not subordinate to one another, is an
extraordinary dream of Grotius and Hammond contrary to the express Scriptures,
which often affirm only one church at Jerusalem, composed of any number of
congregations. Hammond will also earn little gratitude from a bishop of his
own party by doubling the bishops at Jerusalem, for two bishops in one city
are to them two monsters, though according to them the ancient church never
saw such things. A multiplicity of languages certainly did not multiply
churches, unless we wish to establish as many churches at Jerusalem as we read
of foreign languages there in Acts 2:9. Most Jews at that time were
Hellenists, who spoke and wrote Greek. Even after Jews and gentiles, as they
maintain, were joined in one church under one bishop, there was as great a
diversity of languages in that church as before, when they invent distinct
churches on account of that diversity. Moreover, the long catalog in Eusebius
and others of bishops who governed the Jerusalem church down to Hadrian’s days
consists solely of those who spoke the Jews’ language and were descended from
the circumcised. This division of Jewish churches from gentile churches, not
only at Jerusalem but also at Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, and everywhere at
that time, is plainly a ridiculous and exceedingly offensive invention.

Seventh. It is false that the prophets’ preaching at Jerusalem lasted no more
than three and a half years. After Titus destroyed the city, the Christians
of Pella returned to the few houses that remained, and the church continued
there until Aelia was built and thereafter down to Constantine. Nor can those
three and a half years be found in Hadrian’s days: Barcocheva’s war began in
Hadrian’s eleventh year and did not end before his twentieth.

Eighth. If we believe Baronius, from whom Grotius and Hammond borrow every one
of their witnesses without exception (though they completely conceal whose
heifer they plowed with), the entire foundation of their structure collapses.
Hadrian did not found Aelia until the Jewish war had ended and Barcocheva was
killed, so Barcocheva never saw Aelia. But even if Aelia is conceded to have
been built before the war began, as Dio Cassius intimates against Epiphanius
and Cassiodorus, Grotius and Hammond cite not one iota from any author that
even faintly implies Barcocheva ever entered Aelia, much less ruled there.
They do bring one or two witnesses from Baronius to prove that Barcocheva
killed many Christians, but no ancient author is put forward as affirming that
he killed any of them at Jerusalem.

Ninth. When they restrict the forty-two months and 1,260 days to a defined
period of three and a half years, why do they extend to an indefinite time the
three and a half days during which, as they maintain, the Christians’ corpses
lay unburied in Aelia’s streets—namely, the entire time when

[Printed p. 248 | IA leaf 278 | access PDF 278 | OCR object 277]

Barcocheva ruled that city? On this reasoning those three and a half days will
be longer than three and a half years. Even if only three and a half years
elapsed from Aelia’s construction in Hadrian’s eleventh year to Barcocheva’s
rule and his persecution of the Christians at Jerusalem in Hadrian’s
fourteenth, six complete years elapsed from there to the war’s end, when
Barcocheva’s power was overthrown in Hadrian’s twentieth. Indeed, no fewer
than four years elapsed before Hadrian’s eighteenth, when Julius Severus,
appointed to succeed Rufus in the proconsulship of Asia, began to check
Barcocheva’s power.

Tenth. The two witnesses performed most splendid miracles in their three and
a half years, like those of Moses in Egypt and Elijah in Israel. But nothing
of this sort is read of the Christians of Aelia in Hadrian’s days, even in
Prochorus and Dorotheus, the spurious authors of many invented miracles in
that age.

Finally, after the witnesses’ resurrection, Christian affairs flourished in
the Holy City and those of the pagan idolaters were abased beyond measure. Yet
after Barcocheva’s death and the Jews’ defeat, pagan idolatry declined very
little at Jerusalem and no extraordinary gain came to the Christian church.
The Romans continued to rule the city and the idol of Jupiter Olympius to be
solemnly worshiped on Zion. Indeed, at Christ’s very tomb and place of his
passion, and at the place of his birth in Bethlehem, statues of three
abominable demons—Jupiter, Venus, and Adonis—were placed and honored daily
with public sacrifices until Constantine’s days. No more about chapter 11.

Grotius and Hammond interpret the woman’s flight into the wilderness in
chapter 12 for 1,260 days in verse 6 and for *a time, times, and half a time*
in verse 14 as two flights of the church from Rome. The first was in Claudius’s
second year, when Simon Magus came to Rome to forestall the Christians’ first
rise there. His heresy then grew so much that a statue was publicly erected to
him at Rome as to a holy God. The other was in Nero’s twelfth year, when Simon
Magus, having returned to Rome, flew in the air and was cast down by Peter’s
prayers. He first broke his legs in the fall and then, confounded by shame,
threw himself headfirst from the top of an inn and breathed his last. Nero,
blaming Peter for the death of his friend Simon, expelled the Christians from
the city, and their exile lasted three and a half years.

> Marginalia, printed p. 248: *They make equally absurd comments on chapter
> 12*.

Let us see from the sacred text itself how much absurdity these thoroughly
perverse novelties contain.

First. In Claudius’s second year the Roman church, or the catholic church at
Rome, was by no means the woman clothed with the sun and crowned with twelve
stars. The Jewish church was then such a church, but least of all the Roman,
which at that time had scarcely begun to cry in its cradle. No apostle had yet
come there, neither Peter nor Paul. Perhaps some at Rome had then been
converted to the faith through information from Priscilla, Aquila, and others,
but no instituted church, formed congregation, or established ministry yet
appeared at Rome.

Second. How empty is their claim that Simon Magus, signified by the dragon’s
tail, seduced a great part of the Roman clergy by his tricks, thus drawing a
third of heaven’s stars to earth. There was then no clergy at Rome to seduce;
nor indeed was the Roman clergy ever seduced by Simon himself or by his
disciples Carpocrates and Menander into the Gnostics’ heresies and horrible
crimes.

Third. They hold that the male child born of the woman, who was to rule the
whole world with an iron rod, was the Romans then converted to the faith. But
those converted to Christ at Rome in Claudius’s second year were so few and of
such slight authority that ruling the world and possessing any rod, much less
an iron one, were utterly remote from their thought and fortune. What our good
men insinuate here, moreover, is the Roman pontiff’s dominion over the whole
world, as though established by divine right through this text—so gross an
absurdity that most Roman Catholics reject it.

Fourth. That any Christians fled Rome into the wilderness because of Simon’s
teachings and honors, much less that for this reason they continued in exile
for three and a half years, is a gratuitous dream without any witness. The
Jews’ expulsion from Rome did not occur before Claudius’s ninth year, as
Baronius demonstrates, and how long it lasted is entirely uncertain. Simon,
moreover, favored the Jews with his followers to the Christians’ detriment.

Fifth. Without any shame they dare affirm, against the whole ancient and
modern Christian people, that Michael’s battles with the dragon refer to
Peter’s contest with Simon Magus at Rome. Besides the fact that the whole
story of Peter’s contest with Simon appears to many learned men to be a mere
fable, this commentary openly conflicts with Revelation’s own text. At the
beginning of chapter 4 Christ teaches that the things expressed in all the
following visions were not past or present but would happen in the future.
Yet in Nero’s twelfth year, when Simon Magus is said to have been cast down by

[Printed p. 249 | IA leaf 279 | access PDF 279 | OCR object 278]

Peter’s prayers, none of these visions had been revealed to John. Indeed, John
was not banished to Patmos before Domitian’s tenth year, long after both Peter
and Simon Magus had died, if any faith is to be given either ancient or recent
witnesses to Christian chronology.

Sixth. A three-and-a-half-year exile of the Christians after Simon’s death in
Nero’s twelfth year conflicts with sacred history. In the year after Nero’s
twelfth came the civil wars of Otho, Galba, and Vitellius, when Grotius himself
affirms peace was restored to the Christians. The true persecution of
Christians, which occurred in Nero’s tenth year when he wished to transfer to
them the blame for the city he had burned, did not last more than one year. In
his eleventh Paul freely preached the gospel at Rome, and in his twelfth he
was crowned with martyrdom.

Seventh. He holds that the angels cast out of heaven with the dragon were the
heretical disciples of Simon, who were cast out of the church after their
master’s death. But the church was more troubled by the Gnostics after Simon’s
death, if we believe Hammond himself, than before it, when—Samaria excepted—
the rest of the church remained nearly untouched by the heresy of Simon and
the Gnostics.

Finally, eighth, after the dragon was cast down from heaven there was great
peace and triumph for the church and a vast increase of Christ’s kingdom. Yet
all the contrary appeared, even on the opponents’ testimony, when Simon was
overcome by Peter’s prayers. According to them, in Nero’s twelfth year and in
vengeance for Simon, Nero punished the princes of the Apostles, Peter and
Paul, with death, and scattered the church with a great persecution. Although
that affliction appears to have cooled when Nero was killed the following
year, it boiled up again a few years later under Domitian.

As for chapter 13, they interpret the forty-two months as the three and a half
years of Domitian’s persecution. They understand the former beast, one of
whose heads was mortally wounded and later healed, as Roman idolatry badly
weakened by the burning of the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus but restored when
Domitian rebuilt that temple on the Capitol. They hold that the latter beast
was Apollonius of Tyana, who by his magic confirmed the Romans in idolatry and
made an image that spoke: he brought Achilles’ shade to the world above and
made it converse with mortals. Answer. These ravings would need no refutation
if the authors’ great learning and the allure of novelty did not bait the
itching minds of some. We therefore argue against this commentary too.

> Marginalia, printed p. 249: *Likewise on chapter 13*.

First. If the beast’s duration denotes the time of Domitian’s persecution,
then Domitian was that beast. But they do not want Domitian or any other
emperors to have been beasts. They insist on Roman idolatry in the abstract,
without any individual Roman having been the beast in the concrete.

Second. Their second beast is a person, namely Apollonius of Tyana. The first,
therefore, will also be a person, since throughout this chapter the Apostle
describes it in the guise of a person.

Third. Domitian’s persecution cannot be restricted to three and a half years.
Suppose it ended, as they hold, at his death in his sixteenth year; it certainly
did not begin in his thirteenth, but four years earlier, in his ninth, when he
put the Apostle John at Rome into a vessel filled with boiling oil, then
banished him unharmed to the island of Patmos. In his twelfth year he killed
Antipas at Pergamum, Ignatius at Antioch, members of the Roman clergy, and
other notable martyrs.

Fourth. This beast and the time of its persecution are wrongly distinguished
from the former beasts, the idolatrous and papal Roman Empire, since the
Apostle mentions the same parts of those beasts and the same duration of three
and a half years.

Fifth. This beast is marked above all others for cruelty and blasphemy. But
although Domitian was very cruel and blasphemous, he was no more cruel or
blasphemous than various Roman emperors before and after him.

Sixth. Their wish to signify the Capitol’s burning in Domitian’s time by the
beast’s mortal wound is a ridiculous invention. Roman idolatry suffered no
harm whatever from the burning of that one building. The Capitol often burned
both before and after, and temples of idols were burned in various accidents.
Recently, under Vitellius, the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus had been devastated
by fire and rebuilt more magnificently by Vespasian, yet Jupiter’s worship
neither gained nor lost anything thereby.

Seventh. That second beast was not Apollonius of Tyana, for his false miracles
followed John’s Revelation on the island of Patmos, while most of Apollonius’s
deeds preceded it. Moreover, the latter beast’s work and labor were to serve
the former beast. But Apollonius of Tyana remained until death a professed
enemy of the Roman emperor Domitian. He never cared for Roman idolatry but
pursued the worship of the sun and stars after the manner of the Persian
Magi. Achilles’ shade,

[Printed p. 250 | IA leaf 280 | access PDF 280 | OCR object 279]

which Apollonius is imagined to have made speak, is so irrelevant to the
present matter that Hammond himself rejects this delirium of his master
Grotius.

We shall pursue the third opinion no further and shall treat the remaining
ones more briefly.

The fourth is that of John Foxe, the English martyrologist, who near the end of
book 1 of his *Martyrology*, as Abbot cites him, solemnly and seriously affirms
that by divine inspiration he was taught to interpret the apocalyptic months
as sabbatical. For him a sabbatical month is seven years, and thus forty-two
periods of seven years make 294 years. He places their beginning at the death
of John the Baptist and their end at the beginning of Constantine’s reign. In
this way he interprets all our numbers as the Jews’ and Roman pagans’
persecutions of the Christian church. Pareus and Abbot relate this opinion
with great reverence, though neither dares embrace it; we too reject it.

> Marginalia, printed p. 250: *Foxe’s opinion to be rejected*.

First. It scarcely becomes serious men to put forward divine inspirations for
their doctrines, since arguments of this kind would immediately open a broad
door to enthusiasts. Foreseeing this, the very serious man entirely suppresses
in his commentary on Revelation the inspiration he recounts in his
*Martyrology*.

Second. He brings no example from Scripture or any approved author in which a
month stands for seven years or a week of years, nor can any suitable reason
be given why a month should be taken as seven years. Commentaries offering
neither examples nor reasons must not be approved.

Third. As we proved from the beginning of chapter 4, the visions before us
concern future things, not past. But Foxe’s commentary extends them to many
years preceding Revelation and John’s arrival on Patmos.

Fourth. Foxe rightly interprets the times expressed in chapters 11, 12, and 13
of Revelation as one and the same period. But just as he wrongly begins that
period at John the Baptist’s death, he wrongly ends it at the beginning of
Constantine’s reign. He would thereby exclude from all those passages the
common opinion of everyone concerning every persecution of Antichrist.

Fifth. What most displeases us in this opinion is that it both doubles and
rends the interval before us. He ascribes the first 294 years to the pagans’
persecutions and the later ones to the Turks’ persecutions, which he begins in
1300 and ends in 1596. This doubling of periods is unusual and cannot be
admitted, much less can the rending of the periods with such great gaps between
them be digested, so that *a time, times, and half a time* would signify two
periods of 296 years with more than a thousand years inserted between them.

Finally, sixth. Time, which reveals everything, long ago disclosed this
invention’s emptiness with perfect clarity. Foxe’s reckoning ends the Turkish
Empire in 1594. Nevertheless it still stands unmoved sixty-two years beyond
that limit.

Yet I approve the modesty of this opinion. Its author prescribes nothing
magisterially to anyone but submits his views to the learned for examination.
Indeed, he offers his entire commentary on Revelation under the very humble
title εἰκασμῶν, or conjectures.

The fifth opinion makes all our numbers prophetic and precisely determines
them as 1,260 years, resolving *a time, times, and half a time* into three and
a half years, forty-two months, or 1,260 days, each of which they extend in
the manner of the Prophets to an astronomical year. They end this period in
the year of Christ 1650, to which they refer the fall of the Roman Empire, the
city of Rome, Antichrist, and Turkish tyranny. At that time, they teach, the
Jews’ conversion and the fifth monarchy of the saints, especially of believing
Jews, are to be expected.

> Marginalia, printed p. 250: *Brightman’s and Mede’s opinion refuted*.

Thomas Brightman, a learned and pious man, laid the foundations of this
interpretation. Joseph Mede, Thomas Parker, and Ephraim Huit, men likewise both
learned and pious, tread his path in most respects. Yet we cannot approve this
opinion.

First, it is entirely new, and its authors profess to depart from the mind of
all theologians before them. Though their words are often modest enough, the
result is that they prefer their opinions to everyone else’s with sufficient
confidence and arrogance, and under far too magnificent titles, *The
Apocalyptic Key* and *The Apocalypse of the Apocalypse*.

Second. Time itself has now refuted their rashness. The year 1650 came, and
five more years have passed beyond their most precisely determined reckoning;
yet Rome, the Roman pontiff, the Roman emperor, and the Turkish Empire still
stand today, the Jews’ obstinacy endures,

[Printed p. 251 | IA leaf 281 | access PDF 281 | OCR object 280]

and everything continues in the same condition in which it was seen in the
previous century. Brightman does sometimes extend his calculation to 1686,
but elsewhere he lays the foundations on which his followers erected the
present limit of 1650. When twelve years ago I first heard that date publicly
inculcated in London in sermons before Parliament itself, I greatly wondered
at the boldness of men otherwise modest.

Third. These interpretations have caused very great harm to the British
churches today. What else so excited and prepared the minds of so many for so
many monstrous errors and crimes as the star of these paradoxes about the rise,
in the sight and lifetime of us now living, of a new heaven and new earth and
the remolding of everything old into an entirely new form? Twelve years ago I
was deeply grieved when I found very many of the most eminent theologians of
every party in England—Episcopacy-seekers no less than Presbyterians, but
especially Independents and Anabaptists—imbued with the fantasies of
Brightman, Mede, and similar men. Later I greatly rejoiced when I saw most good
men’s minds cleansed of this leaven. Both the timely labors of various men
against the chiliasts and, above all, God’s unexpected governance of human
affairs have long poured cold water on that fire, utterly frustrating the
lofty and wide-ranging but carnal and empty hopes of even pious men. May the
Lord grant that the plague born from this beginning in the flighty and unsound
minds of many may die of itself as quickly as possible.

Fourth. Although these men reject others’ opinions because of a discrepancy of
a few months or even days, in their own they pass very lightly over an
anachronism of years, indeed of 140 years, in one healing of the beast’s wound,
as may be seen in Brightman on Revelation 13:5. But I do not intend to descend
here into a detailed censure of this opinion, lest the present question grow
too much beyond the scale of the others.

The sixth opinion is that of Junius and his followers Polanus, Rollock,
Diodati, Napier, the English Annotators, and others. They hold that all our
numbers are definite, but astronomical in Daniel and prophetic in Revelation.
In Daniel they include not three and a half years but three years and a
fraction of a year, which they restrict from Maccabees to ten days. Perhaps
more about this calculation elsewhere. Here I note only that the middle
English Annotators, on Daniel 7:25, prefer half a year, or six months, to the
fraction of ten days; but the last Annotators here abandon the middle ones and,
without naming them, sharply sting their interpretation of half a year. They
not only condemn it as false but rather pointedly cast it back upon the
Tridentine papists. In Daniel the first English Annotators do not follow
Junius, but throughout Revelation they have nothing except, as they
acknowledge, a copy of Junius’s notes. On Revelation 11:2–3 and 13:5 Junius
turns the months and days into 1,260 years, beginning them at Christ’s passion
and ending them in the pontificate of Boniface III in the year of Christ 1294.
But at chapter 12, verse 14, he interprets *a time, times, and half a time* as
three and a half astronomical years from the beginning of the Jewish war to
Jerusalem’s ruin, when the Christians who fled the city lived at Pella; yet in
verse 6 of the same chapter he interprets the same flight of the woman
prophetically as 1,260 years. In Revelation Diodati, like Calvin and Beza
before him, is entirely silent, having nothing to offer on these mysteries.

> Marginalia, printed p. 251: *The opinion of Junius and his followers to be
> rejected*.

In this opinion of Junius we find wanting, first, that he restricts Daniel
7:25 to Antiochus Epiphanes’s three and a half years, which we attacked above
with many arguments and authorities.

Second. Without any reason or authority he interprets *a time, times, and half
a time* in Revelation 12:14 as the astronomical years during which the
Christians remained at Pella, contrary to all the passages in chapters 11 and
13 and in chapter 12 itself that he makes parallel.

Third. He begins his prophetic reckoning at Christ’s passion, although chapter
4:1 expressly says these visions concern future things.

Fourth. He ends his reckoning of antichristian persecutions in the year of
Christ 1294, though their heat and fury against the witnesses of truth in
chapter 11, verse 7, continued for many years thereafter.

Our Napier, joining Brightman to Junius and adding something of his own, begins
the 1,260 years with Constantine’s reign, when the emperor granted the city of
Rome to Pope Sylvester, and thus ends them in 1560, when Scotland, England,
and other nations cast off the papal yoke.

> Marginalia, printed p. 251: *Napier’s also*.

[Printed p. 252 | IA leaf 282 | access PDF 282 | OCR object 281]

This calculation cannot be approved. No tyranny of the Roman pontiff appeared
in Constantine’s days, nor until three hundred years after Constantine did
Emperor Phocas grant Pope Boniface the title of universal bishop. Next, if we
count 1,260 years from Sylvester, we arrive not in 1560 but in 1573, when no
defection from the Roman pontiff occurred anywhere. Finally, the Reformed
churches’ secession from the Roman pontiff preceded 1560 by many years; indeed,
after that year the papal kingdom began to increase more than decline.

The final opinion, in which we rest, is that of Bullinger, Bibliander, Abbot,
Pareus, and others. They readily grant that the year-numbers in Daniel 7 and
Revelation 11, 12, and 13 are prophetic and not altogether astronomical, but
rather indefinitely prophetic. They grant that they are most precisely
defined for God, but that their limits are so hidden from men that they
pronounce them simply indeterminate.

> Marginalia, printed p. 252: *Bullinger’s opinion is true*.

The English Annotators object from Bellarmine that in Scripture whole numbers
may stand for indefinite ones, but not fractional numbers. Pareus long ago
refuted this, giving many examples of indefinite fractional numbers: Matthew
18:12, *You shall forgive your brother seventy times seven*; nothing is meant
here except “very often.” Revelation 14:1, *One hundred forty-four thousand
were sealed*; here fractional numbers do not stand for any certain and defined
number. And in verse 20, *one thousand six hundred stadia*, and often
elsewhere, defined fractional numbers are put for indefinite ones.

For final proof of this opinion there is no need to bring anything beyond the
arguments by which we refuted the preceding opinions, for when they fall ours
will stand. Certainly determination of the numbers before us has turned out so
badly for every author down to the present day that it is safest to leave their
determination to God alone, or at least to extend their end beyond our days,
lest by setting too near a limit we, like many others, be convicted of vanity
and rashness while still alive and watching. The very learned Pareus wisely
guarded himself here. Having fixed the numbers’ indeterminacy as his settled
opinion, he nevertheless does not seem averse, if it were necessary to
determine them, to letting these 1,260 prophetic years begin in the
pontificate of Boniface III, in the year of Christ 603, and end in 1866. Some
of our great-great-grandchildren may perhaps see what will happen in the
Christian world more than two hundred years hence. No one now living will
affirm anything certain about so remote a time. As for its first limit, the
year of Christ 603, Antichrist’s tyranny had indeed gained much strength by
that time, but Pareus does not explain why this epoch’s beginning should be
referred to that particular moment.

Calvin and our Forbes may be numbered in this last opinion. Although Calvin
was of such humble modesty that he always kept his hand from Revelation, in
his commentaries on Daniel 7 and 12 he holds that *a time, times, and half a
time* signifies nothing other than, indefinitely, *a long time, a longer time,
and a shortened time*. In this he appropriately agrees with our view, as also
in refusing to refer the fourth beast and little horn of chapter 7 to
Antiochus Epiphanes rather than the Roman Empire. Yet the most reverend man has
few followers here, if one excepts the first English Annotators, in holding
that the little horn is the first emperors before Vespasian, and in abstracting
so completely from every determination of the times that he charges anyone
who wishes to descend in *a time, times, and half a time* beyond some
indefinitely *long, longer, and brief* time—or beyond ten years, a hundred, and
another period of unexpected brevity—with the Pythagoreans’ ravings about
numbers.

As for Forbes, he rightly rejects every determination of years in our passages
of Revelation, whether they are astronomical or prophetic, but wrongly rejects
every indetermination as well, and for reasons that seem too slight to deserve
time in refuting. What he produces, dissenting from all men, as he arrogantly
says, appears to us plainly trifling: namely, that the passages in Revelation,
though to be understood indefinitely, are nevertheless expressed in definite
numbers because the events of the Old Testament to which those histories in
Revelation refer were accomplished in the definite time expressed in
Revelation. In his novel interpretation he boasts far too much of this as
though it were a great mystery. By this futile, slight, and fruitless subtlety
he certainly pours far more darkness than light upon Revelation’s text. He
also removes every chronological marker from its histories and, if anyone
does, involves everything in great confusion. The pursuit of novelty and
singularity has rarely turned out well for anyone.
