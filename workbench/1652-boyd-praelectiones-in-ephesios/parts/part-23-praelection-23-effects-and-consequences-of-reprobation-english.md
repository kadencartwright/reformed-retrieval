[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 130 | De Prædestinatione p. 88 (from Lecture XXIII opening)]

It follows now that we should touch upon the effects or consequences of
reprobation. Yet these must be distinguished from each other in such a way
that we do not also concede that everything which we confess necessarily
follows from this decree in and toward the reprobate is an effect of
reprobation. Consequences extend more widely than effects. We confess that
every kind of sin, perseverance in sin, and finally the punishment of sin and
eternal damnation necessarily and infallibly follow from this decree; but we
deny and utterly deny that these are its effects, or that God’s decree is
their cause. We nevertheless confess and concede that both the former and the
latter—both effects, I say, and consequences—are included in that latter act
of reprobation and in their own way comprehended within its compass. For
those whom God passed over by not electing, he also decreed to raise up in
order to display his power and justice in them; to permit them to fall; to
forsake them in their sins; and justly to punish or condemn them for their
sins. These things concern the execution of reprobation. Hence Fulgentius
says, “In such people God begins his judgment by desertion and completes it by
torment.”

[Marginalia: The distinction between effects and consequences. Explanation
of effects and consequences. Fulgentius, book 1, *To Monimus*. Desertion 1.]

For a more precise account of the doctrine, divine desertion can now be set
down as twofold. The first is desertion for testing, in which God omits to
confirm the creature by not granting him the grace necessary for resisting
temptations and persevering in good. He does this not by taking away any good
or gift already conferred, but by not granting a subsequent grace to confirm
the former grace. Without this confirming grace, although that first grace
may be called sufficient for a person to stand and persevere, it will by no
means be effectual for persevering in good and resisting temptation. In this
way God deserted man before man himself sinned. He had indeed given him the
ability to do so if he willed, but had not given him the will to do what he
was able to do; for if he had received this, he certainly would have
persevered, as Augustine neatly teaches: “For he could indeed have persevered
if he had willed; that he did not will it came from free choice, which at that
time was so free that it could will both well and badly.”

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On Rebuke and Grace*, chapter 11.]

But it pleased God to desert man at the beginning in this way—that is, not to
sustain, support, and strengthen him so as to prevent his fall—in order that
man and his descendants might understand that he could indeed fall from
himself and through himself, but could not persist in good, much less rise
again from his fall. Consequently, they were to depend wholly upon God’s
mercy, humbly acknowledging that they owed their fall and ruin to themselves,
but their salvation and raising from death to life to the grace of God alone.
The same Augustine teaches us this elegantly in sermon 11 *On the Words of
the Apostle*: “The one who made him”—that is, man while he was still
upright—“was governing him. He willed to desert the one by whom he had been
made; God permitted it, as though saying: Let him desert me and discover
himself, and let him prove by his misery that he can do nothing without me.
In this way, therefore, God willed to show man what free choice can accomplish
without God. O evil free choice without God!” and so forth.

[Marginalia: Augustine, tome 10, sermon 11, *On the Words of the Apostle*.]

Here it should be noted in passing that Augustine obscurely suggests to us
that between Adam’s temptation and sin there undoubtedly intervened an
impulse and warning of conscience. At its direction he immediately understood
that he had been given leave and left to himself by God as God deserted and
tested him, and that, when left to himself, he could not persist in good or
resist the temptation. Nevertheless, he freely willed his own fall—that is,
as Augustine says here, he willed to desert God, enticed and ensnared by a
false and deceitful hope of a greater good. Thus every pretext for his sin is
taken away.

The latter desertion has the character of punishment: it is the loss and
deprivation of the gifts and grace with which the soul had at first been
adorned. It is also abandonment in the state of sin, when the saving grace of
effectual calling, true faith, and repentance is not conferred. This includes
the concealment of the mysteries of salvation from the reprobate in Matthew
11, blinding, hardening, and handing them over to Satan’s power so that by the
efficacy of error he may seduce wretched people and drive them more and more
into sin. How God is said to harden people, and whether that saying of
Augustine in book 1 of his *Questions to Simplician* should be accepted in
every respect—where he infers that “God’s hardening is his unwillingness to
show mercy, so that he does not inflict something by which a person becomes
worse, but merely does not bestow that by which he becomes better”—perhaps
there will be an occasion to consider elsewhere. For the present it will
suffice to warn that this latter desertion follows sin as its punishment and
is inflicted upon a person because of sin, and that the maxim or rule, “Those
deserted by God first deserted God,” must be understood of this desertion,
not of the former one.

[Marginalia: Desertion 2. Matthew 11. Augustine, tome 4, book 1, *Questions to
Simplician*, folio 146.]

Therefore, to conclude this article, we say that the effect of reprobation on
God’s part is this latter desertion, by which the reprobate are deprived of
all saving grace, God’s fatherly favor, faith, righteousness, and holiness. On
the part of reprobate people, however, we say that its infallible consequence
is perseverance in sin, the blinding of the mind, the hardening of the heart,
a reprobate understanding, final impenitence, either insensibility or despair,
and finally punishment and eternal damnation. We confess that all these are
ordained by God’s decree as things serving its execution, but that they cannot
rightly be called effects of that decree. God does not produce them in the
reprobate, but permits them to happen, directs what has been done to his own
end, and requites it with just condemnation. The sins of the reprobate do not
flow from God’s predestination as their true and proper cause, but from their
own corrupt nature, because they are bad trees and can bear no other kind of
fruit. Justin Martyr rightly says, “God is not the cause that the unrighteous,
whether angels or people, are lost; rather, each is through his own fault such
as he will appear to be.”

[Marginalia: Conclusion. Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho the Jew*.]

Now, as regards the attributes and conditions of this decree: first, we
profess it to be free and absolute in such a way that in the former act—which
properly deserves the name of reprobation—it places no consideration in the
creature beyond God’s own good pleasure and presupposes no condition. In the
latter act, however, it includes sin as the cause of punishment and eternal
damnation. Second, we profess it to be as firm and immutable as election
itself, and consequently that the number of the reprobate can no more be
changed, increased, or diminished

[Marginalia: Concerning its conditions.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 133 | De Prædestinatione p. 89]

than the number of the elect, since the firmness and immutability of both rest
on the same causes, principles, and foundations: namely, God’s immutable
nature and will, for he neither makes his counsel void nor retracts it; and
his own infallible and most certain foreknowledge, by which he knows from
eternity what he has willed and decreed should happen concerning all his
creatures, and therefore what their state will be for eternity. This state
cannot be other than the one which God already sees as present. Hence they are
said to be fitted and appointed for destruction, and their condemnation is
said to have been neither idle nor asleep for a long time already. Also worthy
of remembrance is the passage in John 12 which gives this reason for the
Jews’ obstinate unbelief in the face of so many signs and miracles of Christ:
“Therefore they could not believe, because Isaiah said again, ‘He has blinded
their eyes and hardened their hearts, lest they should see with their eyes,
understand with their heart, turn themselves, and I should heal them’”—as
though he said that God had determined otherwise concerning them in his
counsel, which he had disclosed to us long before through the prophet Isaiah.

[Marginalia: John 12:39 and 40.]

Therefore, God’s counsel concerning the reprobate remains firm and immutable.
The prophet who rebuked Amaziah, king of Judah, in God’s name and by his
authority rested in this counsel in 2 Chronicles 25. When that king proudly
resisted, despised God’s warnings and threats, and even threatened the prophet
who warned him with death, the prophet stopped, saying, “I recognize that God
has entered into counsel to destroy you, since you do this and do not listen
to my counsel.” Likewise, in 1 Samuel 2 it is said that the sons of Eli the
priest, those ungodly, impure scoundrels, did not listen to their father’s
voice because Jehovah willed to strike them with death.

[Marginalia: 2 Chronicles 25:16. 1 Samuel 2:25.]

Finally, regarding the object of this decree, or the subject upon which it is
occupied—that is, the reprobate themselves, in whom and concerning whom it is
engaged—their attributes and conditions are sufficiently clear from the
things already said, and especially from the comparison instituted with the
contrary, namely, the elect. First, if the elect are few when compared with
the reprobate, why should it not follow that the reprobate are very many? As
that Pseudo-Ezra, whoever he may be, says in chapter 4, section 8, verse 2 of
his Commentaries, the earth abundantly supplies earthy matter from which
earthenware may be made, but only the smallest quantity of dust from which
gold may be made. So it is with those vessels of honor and mercy when compared
with vessels of wrath and disgrace. Likewise in Isaiah 53 he laments that
more remain unbelieving than believe the gospel: “Who has believed our
preaching,” he says, “and to whom has the arm of Jehovah been revealed?” And
by the parable of the sower and the seed in Luke 8, the Lord teaches that far
more people hear God’s word without profit than receive it with fruit. For
when he divides his hearers into four classes, it is evident that only a
fourth part receive that heavenly seed in a good and honest heart so that they
bear fruit with patience. And in Luke 18 the Lord warns that he will scarcely
find faith on the earth when he returns.

[Marginalia: Concerning the number of the reprobate. Apocryphal Ezra 4:8,
verse 2. Isaiah 53:1. Luke 8:15. Luke 18:8.]

Human reason must not rise up again here, take God to court concerning the
number of the reprobate, demand a reason, and indeed tacitly complain that he
willed there to be more reprobate than elect. For godly minds marvel at and
revere the divine goodness far more because God deigned to exempt even one
person from death and receive him into life and grace than they marvel at his
justice because he reserved the rest for the punishments which all had
deserved. They rest in his will alone as the cause not only supreme and first,
but also most wise and just.

[Marginalia: An anticipated causal explanation. Cause 1.]

Second, just as from reprobation considered simply in itself, so also from
this immense multitude of the reprobate in comparison with the elect, the
elect recognize God’s grace toward themselves as all the more illustrious,
with the greatest joy and thanksgiving. Just as God’s special favor toward
the people of Israel stood out much more because he had once chosen this
people alone for his own lot and possession while leaving and rejecting all
the rest, so surely it is in the matter of election. From this, therefore,
they both know and perceive all the more how dear they have been to God. From
the sense and recognition of such singular and intimate benevolence, they are
kindled all the more ardently to love him and be zealous for his glory when
they consider that they are not merely among the number of the elect, but
among the number of so few, while such an immense multitude is excluded from
that grace.

[Marginalia: Cause 2. Note the analogy.]

To be the subject of some king, or to be enrolled among the number of his
citizens, is nothing or little, because this is shared with many thousands,
and they obtain nothing rare or dear by that benefit. But those whom he
receives among the number of his intimate friends and companions, with whom
he deigns to share both table and mind—a privilege which ordinarily falls to
very few—recognize and perceive themselves to be wonderfully bound above all
others to his singular love and benevolence. Therefore, not only from
reprobation considered plainly and simply, but also from the great number of
the reprobate, the glorious riches of God’s grace toward the elect shine more
brightly, while their salvation is advanced more and more through the love of
God kindled in their hearts, their gratitude toward God, and their zeal to
confirm and seal their own election. In one word, God willed the elect, when
compared with the rest, to be gold among the other metals and metallic
substances, gems among stones, and pearls among the other pebbles of the sea,
so that the value and importance of eternal salvation might become known to
everyone.

[Marginalia: Another analogy. Conclusion. A third analogy.]

Next, if we call to mind the attributes which we ascribed to the elect above,
we shall find that their contraries belong to the reprobate. They are said to
be hated and detested by God; they also give themselves to doing evil in his
sight, as Elijah testifies concerning Ahab in 1 Kings 20. They are workers of
iniquity in Matthew 7; rebels and obstinate people in Isaiah 65; made by God
for the day of evil in Proverbs 16; and appointed to stumble at the word of
the gospel and strike against Christ as a stone of stumbling and a rock of
offense in 1 Peter 2. They are vessels of wrath made for dishonor, long ago
appointed to this condemnation. The mysteries of the kingdom of heaven are
concealed from them, and it is not given to them to know those mysteries in
Matthew 13. They are hardened by God in Romans 9, struck with a spirit of
sleep and stupor in Isaiah 29, not of God in John 6, not written in the book
of life in Revelation 17, not among Christ’s sheep in John 10, not converted
to God in Mark 4, and unable to receive the Spirit of truth in John 14. Christ
does not acknowledge them as his own in Matthew 7, nor does he entreat the
Father for them in John 17. Finally, they are accursed forever, and God’s
wrath remains upon them, in Matthew 25 and John 3. Some of these things are
peculiar to those who, though called through the gospel, did not truly believe
and become converted; others are common to the called and uncalled alike. We
must observe this distinction: the condition and condemnation of the former
will be far worse and more severe than that of the latter. It remains now for
us, in the last place, to derive certain corollaries from the preceding
discussion. The first will contain a comparison between election and
reprobation,

[Marginalia: Concerning their remaining conditions.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 134 | De Prædestinatione p. 90]

undertaken briefly so that, when they are placed side by side, they may shine
more clearly in relation to one another, and so that what they have in common
and what is proper and peculiar to each may appear. First, then, they agree
with one another in the efficient cause: both the principal cause, God, and
the impelling cause, his sheer and free good pleasure. Second, they agree in
the common final cause, namely, the manifestation of God’s glory. Third, they
agree in the common subject or matter, namely, a human being considered not
only absolutely but also as fallen into sin and corrupted. For just as
election is the decree to snatch those given to Christ out of the common
destruction through mercy, so the decree of reprobation is to leave those not
given to Christ in that same destruction through justice. Therefore, just as
election did not find worthy people but made them so, reprobation made no one
worthy of eternal death—that is, it did not cast anyone headlong into
sin—but by a just judgment left those who had cast themselves headlong into
it by their own fault in that same condition. Fourth, they agree in certain
common means, namely, creation and the permission of the human fall, which
serve both parts of predestination. Finally, they agree in eternity and
immutability: each was made from eternity, and each is firm and invariable,
so that God’s counsel cannot be made void, the reprobate can never become
elect, and the elect can never become reprobate. In these ways they agree with
each other.

[Marginalia: The first part. Agreement in the efficient cause. In the common
end. In the common subject. In two means. And in two corresponding
conditions.]

They differ from each other, however, first in their proper forms, which are
opposite and contradictory. The former is the decree to show mercy, to
deliver from destruction, and to receive into salvation; the latter is the
decree to leave in death, to forsake in sin, and justly to punish because of
it. Second, they differ in their effects and consequences, and this in two
ways. First, the effects or consequences of each are opposite and contrary:
effectual calling, faith, justification, adoption, sanctification, and
glorification—or salvation and eternal life—are properly called effects of
election. By contrast, God’s hatred, desertion in sins, and deprivation of all
saving grace are effects of reprobation. Second, the proper means of election
are its effects and depend upon it of itself as their proper cause, as is
clear from the effects just listed. But in reprobation certain means are
given which are not properly its effects, but only its consequences: namely,
the sin and wicked life of the reprobate.

[Marginalia: The second part. Difference in form. In effects in two ways. Note
this distinction.]

Again, the relation of the means of election to their proximate end—the
salvation of the elect—is not the same as the relation of the contrary means
of reprobation to their end—the condemnation of the reprobate. The good works
of the elect are not the meritorious cause of their salvation, but only the
way to it. Its entire meritorious cause lies outside them: namely, Christ’s
death alone and the merit of his obedience. But the evil works of the
reprobate are the meritorious cause of their death and condemnation. God
willed, as was noted above, that every cause of life and salvation should lie
outside us, in Christ the Mediator alone, to his praise and glory; but that
every cause of death and condemnation should lie within the condemned
themselves, to their confusion, so that justice might shine in the latter as
mercy does in the former.

From this follows another difference in the proper means of each decree. In
election we are said to be elected and predestined both to the end, namely
salvation, and also to the means, namely good works and holiness. But it is
not so in reprobation. Although the reprobate may be said to have been
predestined to death and to the eternal punishment to be paid for their sins,
they cannot and ought not properly to be said to have been predestined to the
sins themselves. Sin, precisely as sin, cannot be the end of that best and
most holy divine counsel. “Nor can God have predestined man to that which he
himself had decreed to prohibit by his command, erase by mercy, and punish by
justice; nor can he be the author of that of which he is both the just avenger
and the merciful disposer,” as Fulgentius says in *To Monimus*.

[Marginalia: Another difference in the means. Fulgentius, book 1, *To
Monimus*.]

But someone may say that the Apostle Peter, in chapter 2 of his first epistle,
speaking of the unbelieving Jews, asserts that they were appointed for this
very purpose: that they should stumble and be rebellious. Yet to stumble at
the word of the gospel and be rebellious against Christ is a most grievous
sin. Therefore, the reprobate were appointed—that is, predestined—to sin. We
may answer this and other objections of this kind in two ways. First, by
conceding that they were predestined to sin, not as the evil of fault, but as
the punishment of sin inflicted upon them by God’s just judgment. God punishes
preceding sins most severely when he gives a sinner over to sins until he
completes the measure of his iniquities. Yet the just punishment of sin is
something good and possesses the character of good in the eyes of a just
judge.

[Marginalia: Objection. Response 1.]

Second, the reprobate were predestined to sin not as an effect, but as a
necessary and infallible consequence of God’s decree, by which he determined
justly to leave the reprobate to themselves and to Satan in the state in which
he knew they could do nothing other than sin. For God is said to ordain and
appoint people to that which, though he could graciously prevent it, he justly
permits and wisely directs to his glory and their destruction, even though he
in no way produces it in them.

[Marginalia: Response 2.]

For example, just as he hardens and blinds the reprobate, so he is also said
to have ordained them to blindness and hardening. Yet he blinds and hardens
them not by infusing malice into them or implanting blindness, but by leaving
them to themselves and to their own blindness and malice and by handing them
over to Satan—in one word, by deserting them and depriving them of his saving
and renewing grace. Therefore, in the same sense and manner he ordained or
appointed them to this very desertion. From this it is also clear that there
is a difference between these forms of speech when they are used of the elect
and when they are used of the reprobate. In one manner and sense the elect
are said to have been appointed to faith and good works: namely, so that God
might effect and produce these things in them by the efficacy of his Spirit,
according to the purpose of his counsel. In another sense the ungodly are
appointed to rebellion and to stumbling against Christ as preached in the
gospel: namely, not so that God might make them such, but so that, by deserting
them, he might leave them such as they are in themselves and from their own
nature—unbelieving and rebellious—even though he could, if he willed, make
them no less faithful and obedient than the rest. He is said to have appointed
them to this in order to teach us that their being and remaining such does not
happen rashly or without cause, but from the ordinance of his counsel, in
which it was determined concerning them long ago that through their own fault
they should be and remain such, and so should be vessels of wrath and
dishonor.

[Marginalia: Explanation. A distinction to be observed.]

Far be it from us to say, with certain sophists, that the vessels of wrath are
made by themselves or the devil and fitted for destruction. To be made
vessels of wrath and fitted for destruction does not mean to be made a sinner
or to contract the desert of punishment—that is, guilt. We freely confess
that they are sinners and contract guilt from themselves, with the devil as
their instigator and persuader. Rather, it means to ordain them justly to the
punishment due to sin. Yet it is God’s office to ordain to punishment.
Therefore, it is God who made them vessels of wrath and dishonor fitted for

[Marginalia: Anticipation.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 135 | De Prædestinatione p. 91]

destruction. Augustine teaches this rightly and subtly in book 1 of his
*Questions to Simplician*: “Behold, he hates Esau in that he himself made him
a vessel for dishonor.” And soon afterward: “As for his making vessels for
dishonor from the number of the ungodly whom he does not justify, he does not
hate in them what he makes. Insofar as they are ungodly, they are detestable;
but insofar as they are made vessels, they are made for some use, so that
through their ordered punishments those vessels which are made for honor may
advance. God therefore does not hate them insofar as they are people, nor
insofar as they are vessels—that is, neither what he does in them by creating
nor what he does in them by ordering, for he hates nothing of what he has
made. But he makes them vessels of destruction for the correction and use of
others. He hates in them the ungodliness which he himself did not make. Just
as a judge hates the deed in a man, but does not hate the fact that he is
given to the mines—the thief does the former, the judge the latter—so God,
when he makes vessels of destruction from the mass of the ungodly, does not
hate what he does: namely, the work of his ordering.” From this it is clear
both what it means to make vessels for dishonor or destruction—namely, to
ordain them to just punishment for the advancement and correction of
others—and by whom those vessels are made, that is, ordained: by God himself
as the just and eternal Judge.

[Marginalia: Augustine, tome 4, book 1, *Questions to Simplician*, question 2.
Analogy.]

Now to return to our purpose: election and reprobation differ from each other
not only in their forms, effects, means, and consequences, but also in their
proper subjects, because election concerns people who are to be saved, while
reprobation concerns those who are to be condemned. Finally, they differ in
their proper ends, because election is appointed to illuminate the glory of
grace and mercy, but reprobation the glory of justice. Let this be the first
corollary.

[Marginalia: Return to the subject. Also in their subjects. And in their
ends.]

The second corollary will concern the threefold use of this doctrine. First,
it shuts the mouths of blasphemous people who conclude, from the fact that the
decree of reprobation can neither be made void nor changed, that attention to
faith and repentance is given in vain. Those whom God has rejected from grace
will necessarily perish; therefore, they resolve to live as they please and
according to the inclination of their soul, lest they run in vain and labor
to no purpose. Yet they do not understand that in this way they speak
contradictory things and entangle themselves in a contradiction. Those whom
God passed over in reprobation he also decreed, as is clear from the preceding
discussion, to forsake in their sins, blindness of mind, and hardness of
heart, so that they neither can nor will turn to him, believe the gospel, and
repent. By contrast, those to whom he has granted this holy zeal and godly
purpose and desire for faith, conversion, and repentance he has by no means
decreed to exclude from grace—that is, reprobated from eternity. Rather, he
wills that all who hear his voice resounding today should not stiffen and
harden their souls, but seek him while he is near and while he may yet be
found. They must not allow the acceptable time, the time of grace and
gracious calling, to slip away from them without use or fruit while they are
entangled in the snares and baited by the enticements of the world, the flesh,
and Satan—that is, of sin—and kept from God’s kingdom.

[Marginalia: Corollary 2. Use 1.]

Second, this same rule leads us as if by the hand to the miserable and
terrifying condition of very many people upon whom the splendor of the gospel
has shone for so long, and by whom Christ has been heard in his word for so
long, so clearly, and so continuously, yet with so little progress, faith,
conversion, and newness of life. When we see the majority of people, after so
many years of gospel light, equally—I might say—blind and deaf, equally
insensible and devoted to the flesh and the world, and finally just as
estranged from that spiritual life and the kingdom of Christ as at the first
moment of their calling, this surely sets before our minds God’s terrifying
judgment against this most ungrateful dregs of an aging world. We do not wish
to pass judgment upon anyone, for that belongs to Christ alone as the just
Judge. We only warn what the Apostle says through Christ’s Spirit in Hebrews
6: that land which has drunk in the rain that often falls upon it and yet
produces nothing but thorns and thistles is reprobate or rejected and near to
a curse, and its end tends toward burning. A smith’s anvil is hardened by the
frequency and force of blows and made more fit to resist than to yield or be
softened. The same happens to a reprobate heart struck so often by the blows
of the divine word that it daily contracts from them greater insensibility
and security. This evil is of such a nature that the more it gains strength,
the less it is perceived; and therefore, the less it presses and urges an
insensible and secure person, the more serious and worse it is, and the more
it must be feared and lamented.

[Marginalia: Use 2. Hebrews 6:7 and 8. Analogy.]

Indeed, when we see a guilty criminal bound with chains and fetters and led
by officers from prison through the street to the judge’s tribunal, there to
hear from his mouth the sentence of the death he has deserved, we customarily
go to meet him, pity his lot, and even comfort him with friendly words. We
exhort him to patience, faith, repentance, and calling upon divine mercy, and
from Christian charity warn him to flee to Christ’s grace and grasp his cross
by faith, together with whatever else God’s Spirit deigns to suggest to us.
How much more miserable, then, is the condition of the sinner who is born a
slave of Satan and sin and consequently subject to divine wrath? As soon as he
comes forth into this light, he begins to be led and to hasten toward the
eternal Judge’s tribunal through this brief life, which is like the road by
which one arrives there by advancing continuously and without delay. Just as
one carried by ship advances continually on his journey whether he walks
about on the ship in the meantime or rests, so a wretched person—whether he
eats, laughs, sleeps, or does and contrives something else—always approaches
that throne, there to receive the sentence of eternal death, which will never
be deferred or removed. Although he meanwhile idles securely, his
condemnation, as Peter says, is not idle, nor does his destruction sleep, even
while he snores into both ears.

[Marginalia: Another analogy. Application. Analogy. 2 Peter 2:3.]

Hence, in the third place, as concerns ourselves, when we are taught that the
reprobate are deserted by God and left to themselves so that they never
arrive at true faith and repentance or recognize the way of peace and
holiness, we are stirred—unless we prefer to perish with them—to rouse
ourselves from this dangerous lethargy of our nature. If we are wise, we never
cease to leave that broad, downward road of eternal destruction, and indeed
to snatch ourselves away from it in a kind of flight and rush until we feel
that we are now seriously and truly treading that narrow and upward path of
eternal life. We recognize how dreadful it is to stand, even for the smallest
span of time, upon the road to hell with the reprobate. All who live in
ignorance and sin press along that road and are not renewed in mind and life
to new obedience by sincerely denying themselves and crucifying the world and
the flesh with its desires, even if they had professed the gospel for a
hundred years. And this is the second corollary.

[Marginalia: Use 3.]

The third corollary will concern the application of this doctrine to the
persons of human beings and the judgment which we can or ought to pass
concerning the reprobation of some of them.

[Marginalia: Corollary 3, concerning judgment.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 136 | De Prædestinatione p. 92]

On this matter it must be maintained that this belongs properly and chiefly
to God himself. He alone both knows and has predetermined the state of every
creature; he alone also has a thorough knowledge of human hearts and minds,
how much grace he has given or denied to each person, what their sins are,
what they will do in the future, and what they will be. This can belong to no
human being or angel. Therefore, as far as people are concerned, they may pass
sentence neither upon their own reprobation nor that of others, unless God
deigns to reveal his will to them in a special manner and equips them with a
peculiar gift of discernment. Thus we sometimes read that the prophets and
apostles were divinely equipped with this Spirit of discernment so that they
recognized certain people as rejected and reprobated by God’s counsel.

[Marginalia: Exception.]

From this came David’s imprecations against his enemies, which occur in many
of the Psalms, especially Psalms 35, 69, and 109. In these he curses not only
their sins—which is also lawful for us—but their persons as devoted to God’s
wrath and destruction, which is not lawful for us. For he acted and spoke as
one instructed by God’s Holy Spirit through a peculiar gift of prophecy, and
foretold the destruction due to their crimes. In the same way, the Apostle
Paul prays against Alexander the coppersmith: “May the Lord,” he says, “repay
him according to his deeds.” These men conceived such prayers with a pure
mind, from a right zeal for divine glory, and by a singular gift of
discernment. The prayers were therefore holy and lawful in them, though not
to be imitated by us.

[Marginalia: Confirmation. 2 Timothy 4:14. Note carefully.]

It is indeed true that by this same gift of discerning the final obstinacy of
some reprobate person, God sometimes equips his Church when assembled as
one—that is, the congregation and synod of the Church and the body considered
as a whole—even though the individual members ought not to arrogate this same
judgment to themselves but await the consent of the body of the Church. John
suggests this in 1 John 5, saying that there is a sin unto death concerning
which he did not wish God to be entreated. He seems to take for granted there
that the Church in that age could recognize this sin. And when Paul
pronounces the curse in 1 Corinthians 16, “If anyone does not love the Lord
Jesus Christ, let him be anathema. Maranatha,” he seems to suggest that the
Church at that time, on some definite occasion, could pass a sentence of
eternal execration upon someone, although I do not think this was done
ordinarily or customarily. Meanwhile, it is established that the ancient and
purer Church with unanimous consent directed prayers to God against Julian
the Apostate, and the outcome itself demonstrated that they were neither vain
nor ineffectual.

[Marginalia: 1 John 5:16. 1 Corinthians 16:22.]

As far as we and any private persons—that is, those furnished only with an
ordinary calling and ordinary gifts—are concerned, we must hold these two
rules in order to direct our judgments by the unfailing standard of truth,
charity, and sobriety. First, every member of the Church ought certainly to
believe his own election and be fully persuaded concerning it. This is shown
as follows. Believing in Christ is God’s own command, binding the conscience,
as John teaches plainly in chapter 3 of his first epistle: “This, therefore,
is God’s commandment: that we should believe in the name of his Son Jesus
Christ and love one another, as he commanded us.” Yet to believe in Christ is
not only to place confidence for your salvation in him in such a way that you
determine with certainty that through Christ you have been redeemed, in him
justified, reconciled to God, and finally destined to be glorified in heaven.
It is also to believe that in Christ you were elected from eternity to all
these things, for this is the foundation of all the rest. Otherwise, you do
not place confidence for your whole salvation in him, but only for a part or
in part. Moreover, every believer ought to be certain and fully persuaded of
his adoption through Christ Jesus. Yet adoption is joined to election by such
a close and inseparable bond that the former necessarily depends upon the
latter. For, says the Apostle, all who have been adopted in Christ were
predestined from eternity to this very adoption. This rule concerns
ourselves.

[Marginalia: Amplification from unlike things. Rule 1. 1 John 3, penultimate
verse.]

The other rule concerns our neighbors who live in the outward communion of
the Church: namely, that we must exercise the judgment of charity toward them
and regard them as elect until God himself reveals otherwise. This is why the
preachers of the gospel set God’s word before everyone without distinction,
acting from this judgment of charity, although from that same word they
otherwise know that in God’s threshing floor chaff is mixed with grain; that
in his field darnel grows together with wheat; that in his fold goats still
live together with sheep; and, finally, that in his net not only edible and
wholesome fish, but also harmful fish fit to be cast away are enclosed.

[Marginalia: Rule 2.]

If someone asks what should be thought of those who still live outside the
Church, and indeed are its enemies and attack and persecute it, the answer is
that, as regards their final state, our judgment concerning them must be
suspended and held back by a certain holy suspension of judgment. We must
plead with God in prayer for their conversion rather than their destruction.
We will indeed acknowledge that they are on the road of death and
destruction, but at the same time we will confess that God is able to draw
them from it onto the path of life and salvation. Since his will is not known
to us, let us pronounce nothing rashly about their end.

[Marginalia: Question 1.]

It may also be asked what should be said concerning those whom we see end
their lives in an abominable and horrible death, raving, raging, and
blaspheming. We answer that it very often happens through the force and
violence of diseases that a sick person is deprived of the use of mind and
reason. Consequently, we must consider not so much the act suffered by one
who is no longer master of his mind—an act which should be imputed not so much
to him as to the disease—as the life he lived before it. If that life was
godly and Christian, then surely we who profess faith working through love
must judge charitably in his favor. If it was not, the whole matter must be
committed and left to divine judgment, and no sentence passed upon another’s
servant who stands or falls before his own Lord. Meanwhile, we must revere the
judgments of him who, even in this life, not rarely inflicts a clearly visible
mark of his wrath upon the ungodly in order to correct and instruct us by
their example, so that we ourselves may learn “to serve with reverence and
rejoice with trembling,” as the psalmist admonishes in Psalm 2.

[Marginalia: Question 2. Psalm 2:11.]

Nevertheless, it will help to remember these three axioms in a case of this
kind. First, we must by no means judge anyone’s eternal state from these
outward things. As Solomon teaches us in Ecclesiastes 9, with respect to these
external and bodily matters, all the same things happen to the just and the
unjust, to the godly and the ungodly alike. Second, a good and holy life
cannot end in an evil death, however bitter and to be prayed against it may
appear. One who has lived well cannot die badly, whatever circumstance God
uses to release and summon him from here, because “every death of his saints
is precious in the eyes of Jehovah.” Christ himself, by his own death upon
that cross—a kind of death under a curse—made death

[Marginalia: Ecclesiastes 9. Psalm 116:15.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 137 | De Prædestinatione p. 93 (through Lecture XXIII close)]

blessed and sanctified it for believers. Finally, it rarely happens that
those who have lived ungodly lives die godly deaths; that those who have spent
their life in the flesh end it in the Spirit; or that those who sold
themselves to the devil while living in sin are brought to God as they die.
Who could persuade someone of this and promise it with certainty? Far from us
be so dangerous an error, recognized at last only when no remedy remains.

Lastly, to add an appendix to this question, someone moved by a certain human
affection toward his ancestors, whose case he sincerely pities, may ask what
should be determined and judged concerning those who lived and passed from
this life while the papacy flourished and the darkness of the papal kingdom
prevailed. To this we answer, first, that the inquisitiveness of the human
mind must be restrained here—indeed, that bold intrusion into the secrets of
the divine counsel which God himself has expressly warned us not to touch,
much less investigate curiously, in Deuteronomy 29. Every thought of ours
that rises up too fiercely against God’s providence toward ourselves or our
own must be pressed down, crushed, and choked by holy sobriety, reverence for
the divine majesty, love and zeal for him, and indeed terror, as if by a mass
of infinite weight. We must lead our whole reason captive to Christ’s
obedience with that spiritual joy.

[Marginalia: Question 3. Response 1. Deuteronomy 29:29.]

Second, we say that we are also able to hope and think all the best things
concerning them. Although the papacy itself—that is, the papal tyranny and
corruption—is not God’s Church, but rather the Church’s ulcer and plague,
attacking and assailing its very soul and throat and, like a most malignant
quinsy, occupying and besieging the very throat of the Christian Church,
nevertheless the Church which that man of sin occupied through violence,
wickedness, and every kind of fraud was the temple of God—that is, Christ’s
body, sick and languishing from that cancer. Therefore, in every past age,
even in the midst of Antichrist’s tyranny, Christ Jesus undoubtedly had many
true and living members. He had preserved for himself a remnant undefiled by
idols, as in the time of Elijah. How much more was this so in that apostasy
under Antichrist, since the Christian Church was not enclosed within the
borders of one nation, but spread throughout the whole world? Furthermore,
when ordinary means fail, God customarily employs extraordinary means by
which he provides for the salvation of his own. His secret and fatherly care
and providence undoubtedly did this under the darkness of Antichrist,
especially since John clearly testifies to us in Revelation 12 that, when the
woman fled from the dragon for a time and escaped into the wilderness, there
remained even then a remnant of her seed with whom the dragon waged war:
those, he says, “who keep God’s commandments and have the testimony of Jesus
Christ.” There is therefore no reason on this ground for us to bar or exclude
our ancestors, or those who lived in earlier ages, from the threshold of
salvation.
