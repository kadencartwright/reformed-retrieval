## Book IV. On the Sacrament of the Eucharist

### Chapter I

The first question concerns the presence of the Lord's body and blood in this
sacrament: whether, after consecration, they are contained really and
substantially under the species of bread and wine, in the same space in which
the bread and wine were previously contained. The papists affirm this; we
deny it.

#### Bellarmine's arguments

##### Thesis I

Bellarmine. The first argument is drawn from the figures of the Old Testament.
Figures must necessarily be inferior to the things figured. But the figures
of the sacrament of the Eucharist—the Passover lamb, the blood of the
covenant, and manna—either are more excellent than, or are not inferior to,
simple natural bread signifying Christ's body. Therefore the Eucharist is not
simple natural bread signifying Christ's body, but Christ's body itself.

Protestant reply. 1. This whole argument is drawn from a nonexistent thing.
For the sacraments of the New Testament had no figures by which they were
prefigured as sacraments. They are indeed, in relation to the old sacraments,
ἀντίτυπα, or corresponding types, but not ἀρχέτυπα, or exemplars expressed in
them. 2. By this argument the real and substantial presence of Christ is no
more proved in the Eucharist than in Baptism. For the figures of Baptism which
Bellarmine recounts in *On Baptism*, chapter 13, ought to have been inferior
to the things figured; yet they were more excellent than, or not inferior to,
simple natural water signifying the forgiveness of sins. 3. By the same
reasoning one can also prove the real and substantial presence of a wooden
cross in every sign of the cross traced on the forehead. For the figures of
this sign which Bellarmine enumerates in Tome 2, *On Images*, Book 2, chapter
29, ought to have been more excellent than the things figured; yet they were
more excellent than, or not inferior to, the simple figure of a cross traced
transiently in the air with a finger. 4. Bellarmine acts most ineptly in
constructing an argument about the nature of the sacraments—as he himself
admits in chapter 4—“from a comparison of the things with one another, their
signification being omitted,” since the definition of a sacrament handed down
by the Council of Trent and most highly approved by Bellarmine, *On the
Sacraments*, Book 1, chapter 11, locates its nature in signifying and ef—

[1632 witness | printed p. 68]

—fecting. 5. In signifying and effecting, the sacraments of the Old Testament
were inferior to those of the New, not in the thing and substance either
signifying or signified, as was proved above. 6. Some outward element of an
old sacrament could in itself be more excellent than the elements of the new
ones, because at that time Christ had to be represented most vividly in blood
and sacrifices. But after Christ's death there is no place for blood and
slaughter in sacred worship. 7. Yet Bellarmine makes not only the Passover
lamb but also the blood of the covenant mentioned in Hebrews 9:20 a figure of
the Eucharist, and not of Christ's passion. This is manifest blasphemy, to be
refuted not with arguments but rather with stones.

2. Bellarmine. The same thing is proved from the Lord's promise concerning the
Eucharist, found in John 6.

Protestant reply. 1. We answer with Gabriel in *On the Canon of the Mass*,
lecture 84: “Throughout that whole chapter the Lord speaks about spiritual
eating and drinking, not carnal or sacramental.” And with Cajetan: “If it is
considered perceptively, it appears that the formal discourse is not about the
sacrament.” 2. Nevertheless, because the same doctrine is properly sealed in
the Eucharist and the same act is exercised in a distinctive way in the due
reception of the Eucharist, most of the things said there can rightly be
applied to the sacrament, without thereby lending any support to the papists'
dream about a carnal presence. To make this clearer, we must briefly see what
is said on each side.

#### Bellarmine's arguments concerning John 6

3. Bellarmine. The Lord speaks of the future: “The bread that I will give.”
But eating by faith is a thing belonging to every age.

Protestant reply. 1. He also speaks in the future about the Spirit, who
belonged to every age, John 4:14: “the water that I will give him.” 2. Cajetan
answers on the passage: “He explains most clearly that this bread will be his
passion and death, by saying that the bread which I will give is my flesh—not
mystical, not spiritual, but the flesh which I will give. And by saying *I
will give*, he signifies that by a voluntary gift he will suffer and die.”

Bellarmine. “I will give for the life of the world” cannot be explained except
of a thing truly and properly future; therefore neither can the first phrase,
“the bread that I will give.”

Protestant reply. We concede the whole.

Bellarmine. The Lord speaks about the Father in the present—“The Father
gives”—but about himself in the future—“the bread that I will give.”

Protestant reply. The Lord also speaks about himself in the present in verse
33: “who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.”

4. Bellarmine.

[1632 witness | printed p. 69]

So great is the similarity of these words to the words of the Supper that it
seems plainly to cry out that what was promised here is delivered there.

Protestant reply. 1. There is customarily the greatest similarity between the
words concerning grace sealed in a sacrament and those concerning the
sacrament. 2. We concede that in the sacrament, duly received, what was
promised is in its own manner delivered—but not in the sacrament alone.

5. Bellarmine. From the Jews' dispute and the disciples' stumbling it is
gathered that something new and wonderful was being proposed by Christ. But
Christ did not correct their understanding or explain his own.

Protestant reply. 1. This argument, at least in part, is drawn from the
testimony of carnal, weak, and unlearned people. 2. What was proposed really
was new to them, and it is still wonderful to everyone. 3. Christ corrected
their understanding and explained his own, verses 35, 40, 62, and 63.

6. Bellarmine. The very frequent distinction between flesh and blood, and
between food and drink, openly indicates that Christ is speaking about the
communication of himself under the species of bread and wine. Otherwise, what
purpose would this distinction serve, especially when repeated so often?

Protestant reply. 1. Cajetan answers: “These points are easily rejected by
observing that a little earlier in this same chapter Jesus said, ‘The one who
comes to me will not hunger; the one who believes in me will never thirst.’
For it is clearly evident in these words that the Lord's distinction between
hungering and thirsting does not refer to the sacrament of the Eucharist. This
is equivalent to distinguishing between eating and drinking, since hunger
pertains to eating and thirst to drinking. Therefore no effective argument
can be drawn from the distinction into eating and drinking to infer that the
discourse concerns the sacrament of the Eucharist. Similarly, the distinction
between flesh and blood does not help, but rather leads to the contrary. For
the flesh is not described as distinct from the blood in just any manner, but
by separation as food from drink; yet it is agreed that real separation in the
sacrament exists only representatively.” 2. With the same Cajetan we say:
“Christ used this distinction between food and drink to show that his death is
the soul's perfect nourishment, for perfect nourishment consists of food and
drink.” The rationale of that promise in Luke 22:30 is similar: “that you may
eat and drink at my table in my kingdom.” 3. If in this distinction the
drinking of the blood is commanded separately by Christ, then the one who has
taken it away from the people is Antichrist.

7. Bellarmine. The expression “my flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly
drink” signifies different manners of receiving Christ's flesh and blood.

Protestant reply. Cajetan answers: “This is said to signify that his flesh and
his natural blood—that is, his death—possess the character of the soul's food
and drink, not falsely, not by opinion, but according to truth”; that is, the
character of perfect nourishment.

8. Bellar—

[1632 witness | printed p. 70]

—mine. The comparison with manna refers not to Christ's body as received by
faith alone, but as received in the sacrament.

Protestant reply. The quality of the comparison does not belong to
sacramental eating in itself, but to spiritual eating alone. Those who ate
manna died; those who eat this bread do not die.

9. Bellarmine. The multiplication of loaves, to which this disputation of
Christ was attached, was a notable figure of the sacrament.

Protestant reply. It was the occasion on account of which the Jews sought
bodily food from Christ, and Christ took that occasion to instruct them about
spiritual food. But that it was a figure of the sacrament cannot be concluded
in any way—or by any figure.

10. Bellarmine. That oath, “Amen, amen,” repeated many times, shows that the
statement must be understood properly, not figuratively.

Protestant reply. 1. “Amen, amen” is not an oath. 2. This formula of
affirmation is used in figurative statements, as in John 10: “Amen, amen, I
say to you, the one who does not enter the sheepfold through the door,” and so
forth.

Bellarmine. It is not permissible to confirm by an oath anything except the
plainest statements, which cannot be twisted into another sense.

Protestant reply. 1. They ought to be such to the one swearing, and this one
was such to Christ. 2. A very plain and certain statement can be explained
figuratively. 3. Bellarmine admits in *On Baptism*, chapter 22, “that there are
no passages of Scripture in which one person cannot disagree with another if
he wishes to act obstinately.” There is therefore nothing in all of Scripture
that Bellarmine will confirm with an oath, since it can be twisted into
another sense.

Bellarmine. John 3 deals with Baptism; therefore John 6 deals with the
Eucharist.

Protestant reply. 1. The antecedent cannot be proved, and even if it were
proved, the consequence has no force. 2. If a comparison of parallels is to
be admitted in these passages, then according to the papists' position the
Eucharist will also be necessary for the salvation of infants. This was the
error of Cyprian, Pope Innocent, Augustine, and the whole Western Church for
several centuries, arising from the same interpretation of this passage. 3.
If the comparison of similar things is drawn in detail, then just as the water
in John 3 is distinguished from the Spirit, so the bread here must also be
distinguished from the body. And just as the Spirit there is not really and
essentially present under the species of water, neither is the body here under
the species of bread. 4. We nevertheless acknowledge that these passages
agree in this: just as John 3 delivers the general doctrine of regeneration,
which is sealed in Baptism, so John 6 delivers the general doctrine of eating
Christ, which is mystically sealed in the Eucharist.

12. Bellarmine.

[1632 witness | printed p. 71]

The Fathers interpreted these passages as referring to the sacrament.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine himself admits that many Fathers erred by
deducing from these passages the necessity of the Eucharist. 2. Many Fathers,
Jansenius says, merely applied these words to the sacrament.

Bellarmine. In commentaries and disputations the literal sense ought to be
expressed, not mystical senses and accommodation.

Protestant reply. Nothing forbids setting out in commentaries and disputations
a legitimate application of the true sense. Moreover, anyone with even a
passing acquaintance with the Fathers knows that they indulged far too much
in their mystical senses, allegories, and much more forced applications.
Bellarmine himself admits in chapter 7 that various Fathers interpreted these
words mystically.

Bellarmine. Why will it not be permissible to say the same about John 3?

Protestant reply. It is permissible to say it about various Fathers.

Bellarmine. Not a single Father taught that this passage speaks in no way
about sacramental eating.

Protestant reply. Nor do we teach this, because spiritual eating is the
reality and soul of what is called sacramental eating.

13. Bellarmine. If Christ is not speaking about the Eucharist in John 6, then
John wrote absolutely nothing about this sacrament, which is very absurd.

Protestant reply. 1. It is no more absurd that John, writing last, omitted
those things which pertain to the outward sacrament than that he omitted many
things pertaining to Christ's nativity, since they had already been
sufficiently explained by others. 2. We concede that it was all the less
necessary for John later to mention the Supper because in this passage he had
so clearly taught that spiritual eating which alone is saving in the Supper.

14. Bellarmine. It would follow that Christ never explained the fruit and
excellence of this sacrament, and that no divine command to receive this
sacrament exists.

Protestant reply. The fruit is explained in this passage, but that same fruit,
together with the command to do this, is set forth more distinctly and
particularly in the institution itself. The consequence therefore has no
force.

#### Our arguments concerning John 6

15. Protestant argument. This passage deals with the eating that gives life;
but this is proper to spiritual eating. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Bellarmine. Sacramental eating gives life in itself. That it does not give
life without faith arises from the unfitness of the person approaching.

Protestant reply. 1. Cajetan observes well: “It does not say, ‘the one who
eats wor—’”

[1632 witness | printed p. 72]

—thily, but ‘the one who eats,’ so that we may understand that it speaks of
that eating which does not require the qualification *worthily*, which is not
common to eating worthily or unworthily.” 2. This passage deals with the
eating which exists through faith and thus can never be without faith, verses
29, 35, 40, and 47.

16. Protestant argument. This passage deals with the eating without which
life is not possessed; but life can be possessed without the sacrament.

Bellarmine. This passage pertains only to adults, who are bound to receive
this sacrament at some time either in reality, if they can, or at least in
desire.

Protestant reply. 1. Although some act pertaining to eating may be proper to
adults, its substance is nevertheless absolutely necessary for the salvation
of every infant: namely, that Christ be in them and they in Christ. 2. What
Bellarmine here calls *desire*, and the scholastics customarily call the
*vow* of the sacrament, has absolutely no force except insofar as it includes
spiritual eating within itself. Hence Cajetan also says on John 6 that
spiritual reception consists in the vow. 3. The spiritual eating of Christ
can exist without any knowledge of the sacrament of the Eucharist, as it
formerly did in the Fathers and as it can now happen in someone who believes
in Christ but has not yet been sufficiently instructed about the Eucharist.
There is no desire or vow for an unknown thing. 4. If Christ's words are to be
understood as referring to adults and a vow, then that statement was certainly
not so plain that it could not have been twisted into another sense.
Therefore, according to the rule previously handed down by Bellarmine, it
ought not to have been confirmed by “Amen, amen.”

17. Protestant argument. This passage deals only with the food received by
faith, verses 29, 33, 40, and 47.

Bellarmine. Three kinds of bread are mentioned: 1. Material bread. 2. Christ
incarnate, who is to be apprehended by faith. 3. The Eucharist, in these words:
“the bread that I will give you.”

Protestant reply. 1. The Lord does not present material bread as something to
be sought; it is therefore introduced in vain to establish a threefold
division. 2. Christ incarnate and the same Christ in the Eucharist are not two
kinds of bread, unless there are two Christs. 3. The claim that a transition
is made from Christ to the Eucharist in those words of verse 51, “but the
bread that I will give,” is an exceedingly ridiculous invention. For: 1.
Those words, together with the words following, are part of the answer to the
Jews' objection concerning Christ's words in verse 41. But if another bread is
understood in the answer besides the one at issue, then the answer would not
address the question itself. 2. The conclusion of the discourse in verse 58,
“This is that bread which came down from heaven,” and so forth, openly cries
out that everything said in the an—

[1632 witness | printed p. 73]

—swer concerns the same bread about which the question was asked in verse 41:
“I am that bread which came down from heaven.” For the question, the
conclusion, and the premises ought to concern one and the same subject. 3. In
verse 51 itself—“I am that living bread which came down from heaven,” and so
forth; “but the bread that I will give”—it is manifestly evident that the
bread to be given by Christ is the same as the bread that came down in verse
41, which Bellarmine admits was Christ, not the Eucharist. Here certainly, if
ever, those words which Bellarmine misuses in *On the Effect of the
Sacraments*, Book 2, chapter 3, find their place: “This equivocation would be
vicious. It will be permissible to pervert all the Scriptures if it is
permissible to twist such plain words so many times.”

18. Protestant argument. If the Eucharist is to be understood literally in
this chapter, then the sacrilege of those who have taken away the other part
of the sacrament—that is, the blood—from the people can never be defended,
since verse 53 denies that life can be possessed without drinking it.

Bellarmine 1. The force of the command does not consist in the manner of
receiving, but in the thing which is to be received.

Protestant reply. This evasion is manifest, for in 1 Corinthians 11 the
apostle expressly locates the force of the command in the manner of receiving,
and Bellarmine taught a little earlier that the sacrament's efficacy depends
upon the manner of receiving. Furthermore, if no outward manner is commanded,
then eating is no more commanded than drinking, and it will consequently be
enough to receive the Eucharist by sight, smell, or some other similar manner.

Bellarmine 2. The words of the command indeed express the manner of food and
drink, but the lawgiver's intention was to bind people only to receive the
things themselves.

Protestant reply. 1. A law has no intention apart from its words. 2. According
to Bellarmine's position, the lawgiver has here confirmed, both by an oath and
by the gravest threat, words that express something different from what he
intended. Yet earlier, in chapter 5, Bellarmine contended by this argument that
this very statement was most plain and could not be twisted into another
sense.

Bellarmine 3. The fruit of this reception arises from the things themselves
which are received, not from the manner of receiving.

Protestant reply. But the things themselves cannot be received in the manner
and degree required for our benefit unless they are received in the manner
Christ prescribed.

Bellarmine 4. Explaining what he had said, Christ states: “The one who eats me
will live because of me.” Therefore to eat the flesh and drink the blood is
the same as to eat the whole Christ himself.

Protestant reply. All these things can be truly affirmed. Meanwhile, however,
in the sacrament Christ did not set forth his whole self to be eaten at once,
so that we would thereby gather that we receive his flesh and his blo—

[1632 witness | printed p. 74]

—od. Rather, he set forth his flesh and blood distinctly, so that we might
thereby be certain that we have received the whole Christ, and received him as
dead for us.
