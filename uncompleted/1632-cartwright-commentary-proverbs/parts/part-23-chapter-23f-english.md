Verse 12. [Bring] your heart to instruction, and your ears to words of knowledge.

The purpose is to exhort the godly to devote themselves wholly to the knowledge of God.

The same thing is commanded in Proverbs 2:2, but in the reverse order. There he first requires the attention of the ears, then the application of the mind. By this we are taught that, in

[1632 witness | PDF p. 517 | printed columns 1018-1019]

receiving heavenly things, the ear supplies mutual help and support to the mind, and the mind to the ear. For an awakened mind makes the ear more attentive to hearing, and a circumcised ear makes the mind more eager; just as, conversely, a dull ear makes the mind more sluggish, and a coarse and gross mind makes the ear more deaf.

But he seems to ascribe free choice to man, as though it were in man's own power to apply his mind to instruction if he wished. I answer that this way of prescribing duties reminds us of what we owe, not of what we can perform. Next, it must not be denied that the regenerate possess free choice. That those whom Solomon addresses in this book are of this kind is evident from his frequent designation of them as sons. For those whom the Son has freed are truly free, John 8:36.

But if someone objects that hearing the word also lies within the free choice of the wicked, and therefore is not peculiar to the sons of God, the answer is that bare or outward hearing of the word is among those things which the wicked can either do at will or leave undone. But it is not in their power to apply the inward ear, which is what is required here. For the circumcision of the ear, like that of the heart, proceeds only from God's regenerating grace and his freely bestowed kindness.

But the godly have already applied their minds to instruction. Why, then, are they further wearied with exhortations of this kind? I answer that the mind, which by its own nature is very far removed from God and the true knowledge of him, is brought nearer to him only gradually and step by step, as the word “bringing” plainly indicates. Nor, as long as we live here, can it finish this journey of its own, although it advances daily. Therefore, just as the minds of the godly were first turned toward seeking God by holy exhortations, so they must be kept and established by the same means, lest they turn back; and they must be urged onward to make progress every day.

By joining instruction with knowledge, he shows that it is not enough for us to understand true doctrine unless our will and affections are also brought into obedience to it. That this is signified by the word מוסר is clear both from other passages and from Psalm 2, verse 9.

### Translator's notes

The verse lemma prints *Adhuc*, “still” or “hitherto,” where an imperative is required. The translation's bracketed “[Bring]” makes explicit the probable correction to *Adduc*, supported by Cartwright's own later reference to *adducendi*, “bringing.” The printed reference to Psalm 2:9 is retained; the verse number should be checked rather than silently changed. Hebrew מוסר denotes instruction or discipline.
