We proved the minor premise from Romans 5:12, where we read, “In whom all
sinned.” Socinus in particular created so much difficulty for us in its
explanation and defense that it required the preceding longer digression.
We therefore now have the minor premise of our third argument sufficiently
proved. Its major premise remains to be dispatched briefly. It was this: we
participate in Christ's righteousness for justification in the same manner
as we participate in Adam's sin for condemnation; therefore Christ's
righteousness is credited in the same manner as Adam's sin was credited.
The whole comparison of Christ with Adam, repeated so often from verse 12 to
the end of the chapter, proves this plainly enough. It emphasizes six, even
eight times, that we have righteousness from Christ in the same manner as we
have sin and death from Adam.

But here one Davus throws everything into confusion. Socinus opposes us and
most shamefully perverts the comparison of Adam and Christ, claiming that
Paul established it thus: “Just as through one man, Adam, sin entered the
world and death through sin, and so death passed to all men because or
insofar as all sinned, so through one man, Christ, obedience entered the
world and life through obedience, and so life passed to all men, in whom,
or because, or insofar as they obeyed.” With one impious stroke he thereby
erases every imputation both of Adam's sin and Christ's righteousness. His
heretical position is this: we are constituted sinners not by the imputation
but by the imitation of Adam's sin, and consequently we are liable to death
through sin, that is, only through sins actually committed by us. Likewise,
we are righteous not through the imputation of Christ's righteousness.
Instead, obedience entered the world through Christ in that he gave us
lessons in obedience by both word and example; taught by him and imitating
him, we render actual obedience to God, and so finally participate in life
insofar as we actually obey. This is indeed a primary article of the
Socinian creed and Socinus's explanation—or rather the foulest possible
corruption—of Paul's comparison. To put it briefly: as sin and death with
it come to us through our actual sins in imitation of Adam, so righteousness
and life from it come to us through our actual obedience in imitation of
Christ. This is pure and deadly Socinian poison.

But no trace or footprint of such a heretical connection or demonstration
appears anywhere in the Apostle's text. For:

In the protasis the Apostle does not say

that we were made sinners through Adam's sin merely as an occasion and
example;

nor that we are constituted guilty of death through our own personal and
actual sin by imitating Adam, as Socinus asserts with Pelagius.

He says precisely the contrary. For:

In almost every verse he presses that one fall of that one man as the cause
and fountain of all our sins.

Moreover, in verse 14 he affirms that those who “had not sinned after the
likeness of Adam's transgression” were thereby made liable to death—that is,
those who did not sin by an imitation of his transgression, an actual
transgression

[1679 printed p. 246 | IA leaf 254]

in their own persons, since their age made them unable to do so (for he is
speaking of infants).

Similarly, nowhere in the apodosis does he say that Christ's obedience is
proposed merely as an occasion or example from which we, by actually
imitating him and through our own obedience, might become righteous and
thus meritorious participants in eternal life, just as through the demerit
of our own sin we were made guilty of death. Quite the contrary: he
continually presents and presses the righteous act “of the one,” that is,
Christ, the obedience of that one—not ours—God's grace, the gift, the gift
in grace, the righteous act, and the gracious gift. It is something we
received, not performed ourselves, received freely, not merited, so that by
it we might be constituted righteous and participants in eternal life.

Let this most heretical Socinian position and this foul corruption of the
most delightful comparison of Christ and Adam therefore be banished from us
to the farthest distance forever.

Piscator explains Paul's instituted comparison far more correctly and
suitably to the Apostle's mind: “Just as through Adam sin entered all men
and death through sin, because all sinned in Adam, so through Christ
righteousness entered all believers and life through righteousness, because
all believers satisfied for sins in Christ.” Thus sin and death come through
the imputation of Adam's actual sin and the consequent propagation of
original sin. Likewise righteousness and life come through Christ's
mediatorial righteousness credited to believers and the grace consequently
infused: from the former as their meritorious and efficient cause, and from
the latter as the effect of that imputed righteousness and the way and means
to eternal life.

One may object that this comparison states the fact but not the manner. It
says that we have righteousness and life from Christ as we have sin and
death from Adam, yet not that the manner is the same, as though having sin
from Adam by imputation necessarily means having righteousness and life by
a corresponding imputation. To establish a comparison it is enough that
the things agree in their essential point even while the manner and other
circumstances remain widely different. Two considerations may support this
objection:

1. In this comparison the Apostle asserts similarity in the main point in
verses 12, 18, and 19, but proves dissimilarity in some respects in the
intervening verses 15, 16, and 17: “But the gift is not like the trespass,”
and so forth.

2. If we have righteousness through Christ in precisely the same manner in
every respect, and thus by imputation, as we have sin from Adam, then just
as it is truly said that we ourselves sinned in or through Adam, so it can
equally truly be said that we ourselves satisfied God's righteousness in
Christ. Some of our theologians recoil from that manner of speaking,
although Piscator used it, as we have just heard. It would likewise make us
our own redeemers, the charge Bellarmine brings against our position that we
are justified through the imputation of Christ's righteousness in book 2,
chapter 7, *On Justification*.

I answer in general by conceding that a true similarity does not require
the compared things to agree in absolutely every respect. They would then
not be similar things but precisely the same thing. Nevertheless, they must
agree in the respect in which they are compared.

The point in which Adam and Christ are compared here is not only that each
is the fountain and source of his people—Adam of sin and death, Christ of

[1679 printed p. 247 | IA leaf 255]

righteousness and life—but that the way and manner of derivation is similar
in each case. It occurs partly by physical generation but also, and in the
first place, by moral reckoning or imputation. Thus sin is derived from Adam
to his descendants by inherence through natural propagation, yet first in
order is attributed through the imputation of Adam's sin. Likewise,
righteousness is derived from Christ to his faithful people by the
generation of inherent grace in sanctification, yet his own righteousness
is attributed to us through imputation in justification. The following two
considerations prove that this is what the comparison of Adam and Christ
intends.

First, the whole comparison in chapter 5 is a further confirmation of the
matters disputed in the preceding four chapters. Those chapters dealt
entirely with imputed righteousness—chapter 4 mentioned imputation eleven
times—not inherent righteousness. Paul begins to treat the latter in
chapter 6 as an effect or companion of imputed righteousness, though he had
already removed it from being justification's formal cause. In the first
three chapters he had proved that no righteousness sufficient for
justification could be found anywhere among either Gentiles or Jews.

Second, this comparison demonstrates that death passed to infants, who “did
not sin after the likeness of Adam's transgression,” verse 14—that is,
actually. Yet they would not have died unless they had first sinned.
Therefore they sinned, though not personally in themselves and consequently
in another. Another's sin could belong to them in no way except by
imputation; therefore another's righteousness, namely Christ's, could come
to them only by a corresponding imputation. Thus they are justified through
his righteousness credited to them. Let this suffice as a general answer to
the objection. In particular, as to:

The first point, that the Apostle himself says there is dissimilarity in
this comparison of similar things in the words, “But the gift is not like
the trespass,” and so forth, I confess it is true and even necessary. Had
they agreed in every respect, they would have been not similar but the same.
They therefore differ in various respects, which Chrysostom enumerates
individually. The comparison sets grace against sin; God's grace against a
man's sin; the grace not only of one man, Christ, but also of God the Father
against the sin of one man; the forgiveness of many sins against one man's
single sin; death on the one side and life on the other. It is much more
fitting in itself for one to be saved because of another than punished, and
Christ's grace is far more efficacious to save than man's sin to condemn.
Louis de Dieu adds that “the abundance” of grace is opposed to the death
contracted from Adam: that death could be overcome through grace and turned
into life for believers, but this grace can never be overcome and turned
back into death. Although these and six hundred other things in this
comparison and between the things compared are dissimilar or even wholly
contrary, nothing prevents them from being entirely alike in the respects
in which they are compared as similar. The Apostle's principal points were
these two: each Adam was the fountain for his people, the former of sin and
the latter of righteousness; and each derived it to his people in the same
manner, namely through imputation, as we showed above.
