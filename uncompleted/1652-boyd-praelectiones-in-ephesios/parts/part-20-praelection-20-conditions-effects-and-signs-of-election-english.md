[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 113 | De Prædestinatione p. 71 (from Lecture XX opening)]

Before we come to the effects, it follows now that we should consider some of
election’s conditions or properties. These seem to touch the nature of a thing
more closely than its effects do and to be more intrinsic to it. Since these
properties are already sufficiently known for the most part from what has
been said, there is no need to linger over demonstrating them. First, it is
clear that election was made from eternity, since it was made before the
foundations of the world were laid, that is, as the same Apostle says
elsewhere, before all secular ages, 2 Timothy 1. It therefore occurred not only
before we believed but even before we were born, just as Romans 9 says of
Jacob: “When the children were not yet born and had done neither good nor
evil,” and so forth. Hence Augustine says splendidly in book 5, chapter 5 of
*On Predestination and Grace*: “Before he made us, he foreknew us, and in that
very foreknowledge he chose us when he had not yet made us.” And shortly
afterward: “We were made within the world; we were chosen before the world.”

[Marginalia: Lecture XX. Transition. Condition 1. 2 Timothy 1. Romans 9:11.
Augustine, *On Predestination and Grace*, chapter 5.]

Second, election is also entirely free and gratuitous. In making this decree
God was not bound by any necessity or obligated to any creature, so that he
would be required to impart the same grace to everyone. Rather, by his
entirely free will he passed over those whom he willed and took up those whom
he willed, as the Apostle says in Romans 9: “God has mercy on whom he wills,
but hardens whom he wills”; and afterward: “Does not the potter have power
over the clay to make from the same lump one vessel for honor and another for
dishonor?” We taught abundantly in the proper place above that it is
gratuitous and proceeds from God’s mere grace, good pleasure, and mercy.

[Marginalia: Condition 2. Romans 9:18 and 21.]

3. Election is immutable, firm, certain, and stable, so that it can never be
changed or made void. This is not only because it is the first principle of
our salvation, as will soon be stated, but also because it is the decree of
the one who is entirely immutable by his nature, as he testifies about himself
in Malachi 3: “I am Jehovah and do not change.” On this depends and in this is
founded that firm and unshaken stability of the divine counsel to which he
himself testifies in Isaiah 46: “My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish
all my will,” or all my good pleasure. Hence the Apostle in 2 Timothy 2 calls
the decree of election “God’s firm foundation,” for he says, “God’s foundation
stands firm, having this seal: The Lord knows those who are his.” He does this
not only because election is the source and principle of all the means of
salvation, but also to indicate its firmness and immutability, since this is a
necessary condition of every foundation: it must be firm and immutable so that
the other things can rest upon it.

[Marginalia: Condition 3. Malachi 3:6. Isaiah 46:10. 2 Timothy 2:19.]

It follows that election’s firmness does not depend upon us, our will, or our
perseverance, but rather that these depend upon it as their cause. The
stability of election, or of the decree itself, depends upon the most constant
and immutable will of God and upon the eternal mercy of the one who has mercy
on us. He will never repent of it, since his gifts and calling toward the elect
are without repentance. Consequently, those who make election uncertain and
repeatedly changeable and, so to speak, capable of turning in either direction
—as everyone necessarily does who seeks any cause of it in human beings
themselves or wishes it to depend upon them in any way—not only tear up and
undermine the foundations of our salvation, as far as they are able, but are
also proved to wrong and blaspheme God himself, since they imagine him mutable
and unstable in his counsels.

[Marginalia: Corollary. Romans 11:29.]

Last, election is known and knowable to us, so to speak, only *a posteriori*,
that is, from its effects, and not immediately in itself. For this reason the
Apostle says in the statement from 2 Timothy cited above that it has “a seal:
The Lord knows those who are his.”

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 114 | De Prædestinatione p. 72]

He compares it to something closed to us by
a seal, not only with respect to others, because we do not know for certain
and cannot know without special revelation whether this or that person, for
example, is elect or not—which belongs to God alone—but also because God’s
eternal counsel, regarded simply and immediately in itself, is to us an
impenetrable abyss and a height inscrutable to every creature. “For who has
known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?” the Apostle
exclaims, as you know. It is no more possible for someone immediately and, so
to speak, *a priori* to inspect this secret counsel of God than to leap from
here into glorious heaven itself in a single bound, since he cannot discover
even what lies hidden at the center of the earth, though it is much nearer to
him.

[Marginalia: Condition 4. Romans 11:33.]

It follows, therefore, that we must judge election *a posteriori*, that is,
from its effects. This is the only remaining way to form a judgment about our
election, and it is most firm, most safe, and fully sufficient for us. For then
divine goodness manifests to us his counsel concerning our salvation founded
upon our eternal election: when he actually confers upon us the means of
salvation decreed in that same counsel; when he causes each one of us to
experience in his own heart the effects of this election—indeed, to feel them
deeply and be inwardly affected by them. Then he deigns, as it were, to open
and unfold for us this Book, otherwise sealed with seven seals and
inaccessible to our minds, and to break its seals for us.

[Marginalia: Corollary. Revelation 5:9.]

Therefore, so that we may attain this knowledge which is so sweet and saving,
and acquire this most desirable understanding—in comparison with which all
other knowledge is mere ignorance, indeed nothing but anguish and torment of
mind—we must next examine the effects of election. We can certainly set them
out briefly, although the subject is itself very long and extensive. For since,
as has been said, eternal election is the first principle of our salvation,
all the means of our salvation flow from it as from their cause and are
therefore its effects: all the stages by which God leads his elect to the end,
that is, to eternal salvation, and all the saving gifts and works in us are so
many effects of our election. Indeed, the catholic and invisible Church
itself, which is nothing other than “the assembly of those predestined to
eternal life,” as one of Christ’s faithful witnesses once rightly said, will
itself, together with each of its members, be an effect of election. In another
respect it is the subject occupied, that is, the object in which this decree
of election is occupied, yet this does not prevent it from also being an
effect of election. A key, for example, is both an effect of the smith’s art
and an object in the making of which, or by instruction in the manufacture of
which, that art is occupied; so too is a ship with respect to the shipwright’s
art.

[Marginalia: Transition. Summary proposition concerning the effects of
election. John Hus. Analogy.]

Some establish the following stages in the execution of the decree of
election: the designation of the Mediator, the promise of the one designated,
the exhibition of the one promised, the application of the one exhibited or
yet to be exhibited, and the completion of the application. This accords with
Bernard’s saying: “The kingdom of God is granted, promised, shown, and
received: it is granted in predestination, promised in calling, shown in
justification, and received in glorification.” They speak correctly and
orthodoxly. By this division, or by this distinction and arrangement of the
stages, they consider not so much the effects of election itself as the parts
of the counsel and the means comprehended within its scope, especially in its
second act. We taught in the proper place above that this act concerns the
execution of the decree and is occupied in arranging and ordering the means
that serve and lead to the end. Thus Christ was designated Mediator and head
of the elect; his mission or exhibition in our nature was decreed; likewise,
the promise that he would be exhibited, the application or reception by the
elect of the one exhibited or yet to be exhibited, and finally the
consummation of that reception begun here, or the full glorification of the
elect. Since all these means of salvation were decreed, ordered, and
predetermined in the counsel of election itself, their actual execution in
time is rightly established and called the effects of election, as election
is the source and foundation of all God’s saving benefits in Christ.

[Marginalia: Thus Perkins. Bernard, *On the Words of the Book of Wisdom*.
Corollary applying those five stages to the present subject.]

It follows from the five stages introduced above that only the latter four
properly belong to the effects of election. The first, the designation of the
Mediator, is a part of the decree made from eternity rather than an effect of
that decree, as is clear from the preceding discussion. Christ’s mediation,
that is, Christ himself as the God-man Mediator, his manifestation in the
flesh, and the whole performance and execution of that office of Mediator and
Redeemer are rightly called an effect of election. Yet his designation and
appointment made from eternity are not themselves properly called an effect,
since they are the first and chief part of the counsel itself as occupied in
ordering and decreeing the means of salvation, and the foundation of the
remaining means.

Therefore, among these proper effects of election, which are so many means and
stages upon that ladder of salvation, the second stage will rightly take first
place: the promise of the Mediator. By this promise Christ, destined from
eternity for the salvation and spiritual life of elect humanity, is revealed
and offered to them in God’s word, together with the grace to be obtained
through him. In this promise, or revelation of Christ made in God’s word under
both Testaments, two things must be considered, as we may note in passing: the
promise itself and God’s command. The promise itself is universal with respect
to each and every believer, as is clear from various testimonies. John 3: “God
so loved the world,” and so forth; “the one who believes in me has eternal
life.” Matthew 11: “Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will
refresh you.” Mark 16: “The one who believes and is baptized will be saved; the
one who does not believe will be condemned.” Acts 10: “Everyone who believes
in him receives remission of sins through his name.” And chapter 13: “Everyone
who believes is justified in him.” Romans 1: “The gospel is the power of God
for salvation to everyone who believes.” And chapter 10: “Christ is the end of
the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.” And Galatians 3:
“Scripture confined all things under sin, so that the promise from faith in
Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe.”

[Marginalia: Effect 1. The promise. John 3:16 and 6:47. Matthew 11:28. Mark
16:16. Acts 10:43 and 13:39. Romans 1:16 and 10:4. Galatians 3:22.]

The command joined with the promise is the exhortation to believe, by which
God exhorts everyone and commands all those to whom the promise is revealed
and proclaimed to receive and embrace it. This command is more general than
the promise. While the promise belongs only to believers and is made to them,
the command belongs both to believers and unbelievers; God exhorts both to
believe. He does so first because the elect are mingled and gathered in the
same assemblies with the ungodly, and the heralds of the divine word do not
know how to distinguish the former from the latter.

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 115 | De Prædestinatione p. 73]

It is therefore their duty
and belongs to their office to call everyone gathered without distinction
from darkness to light, that is, to exhort them to faith and repentance.
Second, by their ministry God wills to render the reprobate themselves, and
those whom he knows will never repent, without excuse: to convict them of
unbelief and stubbornness, and, once convicted, to condemn them on the last
day before that dreadful tribunal.

[Marginalia: Note the distinction between the promise and the command to
believe.]

If someone objects that on this reasoning God’s command will conflict with his
decree, since he commands people to do what he decreed from eternity would
never be done, and that God is therefore either contrary to himself in this
respect or plays with people—either of which is absurd and blasphemous even to
think—then we would be saying either that he commands what is contrary to his
secret will or that he pretends to will what he actually does not will: the
faith and conversion, and therefore the salvation, of everyone whom he exhorts
to faith and repentance through the ministry of his gospel. This is indeed
the chief objection of bold and stubborn flesh against this doctrine. We have
therefore judged that its discussion should be delayed no longer. We answer
first by denying that connection, or, if you prefer, that either absurdity
follows from the doctrine just delivered. It does not follow from God’s
demanding faith from everyone without distinction to whom the gospel is
preached, and his exhorting all without discrimination to faith and repentance
by the voice of his heralds, that he either commands what is contrary to his
will or, determining one thing while presenting another, plays with human
beings. Away with the blasphemous expression! These things are not truly
contrary or in conflict with each other. Rather, what he executes and works
toward and in human beings through the ministry of his word agrees and
harmonizes perfectly with his decree. Indeed, they flow and depend upon that
decree as their cause.

[Marginalia: Objection. Answer.]

To understand this more correctly, let us lay down these two things as a
foundation. First, in this life God does not reveal all his will to us in the
commands and promises of the gospel. He reveals it in his word only to the
extent he knows to be expedient and fitting both for the salvation of the
elect and the government of this world. Thus he leaves room for the final
manifestation of all things which he has decreed to reserve for the last day.
That certainly would not happen if in the present age we could already
distinguish the reprobate from the elect plainly and with certainty. He has
therefore adapted and accommodated his command and the will revealed in his
word to this counsel. He has charged his servants faithfully to discharge
their office by calling everyone, preaching Christ to everyone, offering grace
and salvation in him to everyone, and earnestly and insistently exhorting
everyone to faith and repentance, as though they were going to convert and
draw everyone to salvation through their ministry. Meanwhile, he does not
will them to be curiously anxious or anxiously curious about the rest, that
is, the outcome itself, but to leave it to God’s secret providence and counsel.
In this way what he decreed to happen will happen: only the elect will be
drawn, effectually called, believe, and repent, while the rest will be
hardened.

[Marginalia: The prior supposition. Inference.]

Do these things, then, conflict with one another: the universal call through
the gospel and God’s counsel to save only the elect while leaving the rest to
themselves and hardening them? These two are so far from opposing each other
that, on the contrary, this is the most fitting way to execute the divine
counsel toward both the elect and the reprobate: to call everyone so that only
the elect come and the rest are rendered inexcusable because they were called
but refused to come. This would certainly not apply equally if God directed
his voice only to his elect while the others were unaware, did not hear it,
and thought it had nothing to do with them. They could then offer some pretext
and excuse that they had not been invited or called with the rest and would
otherwise have come too. But since everyone has been called without
distinction, while they have stopped and remained in their natural condition
and refused to come when invited to Christ, their mouth is now stopped before
the judge and every excuse and pretext is removed. This is the very execution
of the divine decree and will toward them. “If I had not come and spoken to
them,” the Lord says in John 15, “they would not have sin, but now they have no
pretext for their sin.”

[Marginalia: Note carefully. John 15:22.]

I will illustrate this further by an analogy. A king determines in his royal
munificence to honor and benefit certain ones among his subjects whom, by his
right, he has embraced above the rest with gratuitous benevolence and love. He
nevertheless intends to stop the mouths of the others, who are utterly
unworthy of his favor, and deprive them of every pretext for complaint or
excuse. He therefore proclaims to all his subjects through a herald that they
are to celebrate a most famous public assembly and solemn festival. Those
invited whom he wished to be present and judged worthy above the rest of his
kindness and generosity obey and approach. The rest refuse to come, make
excuses, neglect and despise the king’s edict, and occupy themselves with
other frivolous matters. Thus, when the king requires an account of their duty
and summons and presents them before his judge, they remain without excuse.
But if he had sent some of his attendants to warn only his friends secretly
from the rest to be present and obey the king’s will, the others, however
stubborn and rebellious, would certainly have had some pretext both for
excusing themselves and for accusing the king of partiality: namely, that they
had not been warned and invited with the others. This has a place concerning
and among those who are invited to Christ by the voice of the gospel and
reject his grace. But there is another principle concerning those who have
never heard anything of him throughout their entire life, and another
objection is commonly made here against divine providence. God willing, we
will touch on it elsewhere. For now it is enough, by presenting the true cause
of the call and of this general command, to have vindicated our God’s ways in
executing both judgment and mercy toward human beings from each of those
accusations—or rather blasphemies—of contradiction and of pretense and
falsehood.

[Marginalia: Analogy.]

If someone still presses and urges that God at least seems to act unjustly—far
be the blasphemous expression!—toward those whom he condemns for their unbelief
and sentences to eternal punishment, since he knows that no one will obey his
call unless he inwardly draws that person by his Spirit and converts him to
himself; indeed, not even the elect would believe and follow Christ’s call
unless they were inwardly taught by the heavenly Father and

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 116 | De Prædestinatione p. 74]

brought to faith
by the effective drawing and touch of his Spirit: why, then, does he require
from the rest what he does not give and what they cannot have unless he gives
it, namely a good mind that submits inwardly to the divine voice?

[Marginalia: Further objection.]

To this we answer: 1. Although they cannot obey God and follow him when he
calls, they nevertheless ought to and remain obligated to do so. But he is by
no means obligated to pay their debt for them or to effect within them by the
grace of his Spirit what they are bound to perform of themselves. He is not
bound to any creature or obligated by any reason or right. Why, then, should
he not require from everyone that duty owed which he deigns to perform in only
some?

[Marginalia: Answer 1.]

Second, just as no one comes to Christ except spontaneously and by a free
will, so no one rejects Christ except of his own accord and by his own free
will. Yet there is this distinction—lest anyone take these words wrongly and
contrary to our meaning. Those who come to Christ spontaneously and
willingly have this spontaneity, so to speak, and free will from God’s Spirit
himself, because he gave them the will to follow, assent, and embrace Christ.
Hence he is said to draw not people who are unwilling and resistant, but by
making them willing and spontaneous. Those who spontaneously and by their own
free will reject Christ do so from the corruption of their nature and from a
will enslaved to sin. Yet that will is by no means coerced and does not act
unwillingly in what it does, but acts from counsel and choice. In this respect
the will, as will, has from its very nature and from its essential and
inseparable property that it is free in willing what it wills and refusing
what it does not will. This is so whether it serves in slavery to sin, as
before regeneration, or can serve only righteousness and holiness and will
nothing but good, as after glorification. The necessity of serving either sin
or righteousness does not remove the will’s natural liberty in willing or
refusing, by which the will is a will. Original sin likewise did not remove
the natural faculty of understanding, by which the intellect is intellect and
the rational soul is rational; it removed in part the very light of saving
knowledge and in part the holiness of understanding. Once that holiness was
lost, our understanding was blind in divine and spiritual matters until it
was renewed. So too original sin removed from humanity in part the very act of
willing well and holily as soon as the person began to will perversely and
devilishly against the covenant and command of his holy God, and in part the
holiness of willing. Once this was lost, he could not will anything rightly
and holily that pertains to his salvation and that spiritual life, but freely
and spontaneously rushed to the contrary side.

[Marginalia: Answer 2. Note this distinction carefully.]

Therefore, if by freedom of the will you understand that original holiness
which was its conformity to the rectitude of the divine will, by which a
person willed what is pleasing to God, just, right, and consonant with his
law—while nevertheless willing in such a way that he could also will the
contrary—then in this sense we acknowledge that freedom was lost through sin.
But if you take freedom of the will properly, for that natural faculty by
which the will freely wills whatever it wills, whether good or evil, because
this is its essential property: that it acts by choice and of its own accord,
unlike a fire which burns and heats by a determined nature so that it cannot
suspend its action or act otherwise than it does—then in this sense we must
acknowledge that freedom is an inseparable property of the will. Considering
this, Augustine everywhere attributes free choice to a person even after the
fall, although he by no means denied that it was enslaved to sin. Yet by the
very fact that it is choice or will, it must certainly be free by its nature.

[Marginalia: Augustine’s testimony.]

It follows that those who spontaneously and willingly reject Christ’s grace,
however corrupt their will, are deservedly punished and condemned, because
they are conscious in themselves that they did what they did of their own
accord and could have embraced Christ and the gospel if they had willed. No
one except the truly regenerate seriously acknowledges and feels that truly
embracing Christ by faith comes solely from grace and the secret operation of
the Holy Spirit. People who are still natural and governed by the soul—and
the Roman Catholics disclose themselves to be such by this very mark—think
that they have by nature those powers and that freedom of will or choice by
which they can either embrace or reject the gospel. So it is with everyone at
the first moment of conversion. Those not yet instructed in the knowledge of
sound faith and doctrine think by their natural movement and will either that
they reject the grace offered to them when they refuse it or admit it when
they believe. But afterward, taught by God’s Spirit, they acknowledge that
only because they were effectually drawn by him were they able to come and did
actually come. As the rest rejected Christ of their own accord, so it appears
to them and they are conscious in themselves that they did this of their own
accord in such a way that they could have done the contrary, that is, embraced
Christ, if they had willed. They are therefore inexcusable before God. They
have no reason to attempt to rise beyond their capacity and powers to that
secret counsel of God to confer grace and powers only upon those whom he
willed, since they did not perform what they knew, or at least thought, that
they could perform. This is the first supposition.

[Marginalia: Note carefully concerning people governed by the soul.
Conclusion.]

The second supposition is that various senses and various degrees of willing,
so to speak, and thus various relations, can be observed in God’s will. For
example, God is said to will some things considered simply and in themselves:
those which he approves in themselves as right, good, and holy, such as faith
in Jesus Christ or obedience to his commands. Other things are considered as
related to this or that person, that is, with respect to and consideration of
this or that person: those same things which he decreed by his mere grace to
effect and produce in them. Thus, if you regard conversion, faith, and
obedience simply, God simply wills them, that is, approves them as good and
holy. But if you refer those same things to this or that person, say, this
elect person or that reprobate, and ask whether God wills that person’s faith
and conversion, we answer that he does not will these things in the same way
in both. In the latter, the reprobate, he wills them only by commanding,
warning, and exhorting—if he has deigned to warn and exhort that person
through his heralds—for the end set out above: to reveal the glory of his
justice all the more illustriously in him once he has been stripped of every
excuse and pretext. But in the former, namely the elect, he also wills by
effecting, because he decreed by grace to effect these things in him so that
he might gloriously display mercy in him, since he has mercy on whom he wills
and hardens whom he wills.

[Marginalia: The second supposition. Note this distinction.]

According to these diverse manners, degrees, and relations of willing, God can
be said both in some sense to will and not to will something: to will by
approving

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 117 | De Prædestinatione p. 75]

the thing itself regarded simply and absolutely in itself, and not
to will it with respect to a particular person in whom he never decreed to
effect that thing. The things themselves, therefore—to conclude this passage
at last—which are the objects of the divine will or refusal, that is, which
God is said to will or not to will, can be entirely contrary or contradictory
to one another: to believe and not to believe, to repent and not to repent,
and so forth. Thus, if God willed and did not will these things in the same
manner, sense, and relation in the same person or toward the same person, his
will would certainly contradict and oppose itself. Yet he never both wills
and does not will these things in the same person in the same manner. Toward a
reprobate person, for example, he wills only by commanding justly, never by
decreeing mercifully. Let this suffice concerning the first stage that has a
place among the effects of election, namely the promise of the Mediator.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the distinction. Transition.]

As for the second, it is the exhibition in the fullness of time of the
Mediator who was promised. By it the Son of God, born a human being, paid the
ransom for human sins. This ransom was of infinite worth and price, of
infinite power and efficacy, both with respect to merit and to operation. We
must nevertheless rightly distinguish its actual efficacy from its capacity
or sufficiency, so to speak, according to that common and received distinction
among theologians. We acknowledge that the ransom paid by the Son of God made
human was sufficient to expiate the sins of each and every human being, even
if there were many myriads of worlds. Yet with respect to the efficacy it
actually exerts, and therefore not only with respect to the outcome and effect
itself but also to God’s eternal counsel, we say that Christ paid it only for
the elect and predestined. This could be proved by various reasons if this
passage required it at present.

[Marginalia: Effect 2. A distinction to be noted.]

First, he prayed only for them in that truly expiatory and oblationary prayer
of John 17. That prayer was, as it were, the canon of that sacrifice—to use
the Roman Catholics’ name for that blasphemous formula of their mass. He
therefore offered himself in sacrifice to the Father only for them, because
these offices and functions are conjoined. Second, he took and bore only their
place on the cross. For when Christ died, rose, ascended, and so forth, all
those for whom he died on the cross also died, rose, ascended, and sat in the
heavenly places in and together with him. But this applies only to the elect,
since only they were actually and truly absolved from their sins and
reconciled to God when Christ rose. Therefore, he died only for them. Finally,
since that expiatory victim sanctifies only them, it was offered only for
them, as the Apostle teaches in Hebrews 9. Christ actually sanctifies and
actually saves those whose Mediator he actually and truly is. Therefore, he
was exhibited only for the elect, came actually into the world for them, and
died on the cross for them.

[Marginalia: Reason 1. Reason 2. Reason 3. Hebrews 9:13 and 14.]

Augustine also teaches us this in various places in his *Tractates on John*.
“He who did not spare his own Son,” he says, “but delivered him up for us all,
how did he not also give us all good things with him? But for which of us? For
those foreknown, predestined, justified, and glorified.” So too Cyril says in
his work on John: “But the Lord Jesus, separating his own from those who are
not his, says, ‘I pray only for those who keep my word and have accepted my
yoke.’ For he commits no injustice when he attributes the benefit of mediation
only to those whose Mediator and High Priest he is.” Most of both the ancient
and more recent writers, and of the Scholastics too, speak in the same way. But
there is no need to dwell on it here now.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *Tractate 45 on John*. Cyril, book 11 on John, chapter
14.]

Let us come to the third effect of election: the application of the Mediator,
whether already exhibited in the flesh or still to be exhibited, as in the
ancient Church. By this application Christ is given to us by God the Father
through the Holy Spirit in the word and Sacraments, while we receive him by
true and living faith, and so he truly becomes for us wisdom, justification,
sanctification, and redemption from God the Father. Thus this stage
comprehends the benefits actually conferred upon us in time or begun in this
life, such as effectual calling, adoption, justification, and sanctification.
Finally, the last effect, which is the completion of the application of Christ
begun here, is glorification itself, by which God through Christ will be all
things in the elect themselves. From this it is clear, as we said above, that
the decree of election is the foundation of our entire salvation and the
source and principle of all good and saving gifts, as was evident from the
stages of our salvation just introduced and enumerated.

[Marginalia: Effect 3. Effect 4. Accumulation.]

From it comes true faith, for “as many as were ordained to eternal life
believed,” Acts 13. From it comes effectual calling, for “they were called
according to the purpose, and those whom he predestined he also called,”
Romans 8. From it comes the righteousness of faith, for “election obtained it,
but the rest were hardened,” Romans 11. From it comes adoption, for they were
“predestined for adoption,” Ephesians 1. From it comes sanctification, for “he
chose us that we might be holy and blameless,” in the same place. From it come
good works, for “he prepared them that we should walk in them,” chapter 2 of
the same epistle. From it comes the preservation of the elect from idolatry
when it rages everywhere and, like a flood, inundates an entire people or
region. “I have reserved for myself seven thousand,” says the Lord, “who have
not bowed the knee to Baal.” Pseudo-Christs and pseudo-prophets will not be
able to lead the elect astray, though they will be able to lead the rest
astray, Matthew 24. Except for the elect, whose “names were inscribed in the
book of life of the Lamb slain from the foundations of the world,” the rest
worshiped that Beast, Revelation 13. From it comes final perseverance and
preservation from total and final defection, for “all that the Father gives
me,” says the Lord in John 6, “will come to me, and the one who comes to me I
will not cast out.” Likewise, “This is my Father’s will, that I should lose
nothing of all that he has given me.” Thus “God’s foundation stands firm,
having this seal: The Lord knows those who are his,” 2 Timothy 2. Finally, from
it comes glorification itself and eternal life. For “those whom he elected and
predestined he also glorified,” Romans 8. Likewise, “I give life to my sheep,
and they will never perish, nor will anyone snatch them from my hand.” And
Psalm 65: “Blessed is the one whom you chose and brought near to dwell in your
courts, for we”—as though he said, those of us who belong to the number of
your elect—“will be satisfied with the good things of your house and the holy
things of your temple,” that is, with grace, peace, righteousness, and joy in
the Holy Spirit in your Church in this life, and with eternal glory in the
life to come. So in the parable of those invited to the wedding, this reason
is given why some were admitted with the King to the wedding while others,
although for a time hidden in the external assembly

and company of the faithful without the wedding garment, were cast into outer darkness and
condemned: “Many are called, but few are elect.” This reveals and indicates
election itself as the source and foundation of the actual discrimination and
distinction that will finally be made at the end of the ages between those
admitted to eternal life and those sent into eternal fire. Let this suffice
concerning its effects.

Before we proceed to the opposite kind of decree, it remains to deduce several
appendices and corollaries from the doctrine already delivered. The first and
chief will rightly concern the marks, signs, and testimonies of our election
and the manner and way in which a person can form a judgment about his own
election. It will also concern judging charitably about others whom he knows by
thinking soberly and moderately of them. Since we have already warned that
election must be judged not *a priori* but *a posteriori*, that is, from the
effects just explained to us, the manner and method by which we can discover
whether our names are inscribed in heaven in the book of life—that is,
whether we are among the number of the elect or not—now first becomes fully
known to us.

[Marginalia: Transition. Corollary.]

To acquire this knowledge with greater certainty, this rule must first be laid
down as a foundation: those whom God predestined for the end, that is, glory
and eternal life, he also predestined for the means of attaining it. For
example, he also effectually calls them and grants them true faith, whence
faith is called “the faith of God’s elect” in Titus 1. He also justifies,
adopts, regenerates, and sanctifies them, whence Peter calls them “elect
according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, unto sanctification of the
Spirit, through the obedience of Christ.” Finally, he predestined them to new
obedience, so that they would walk in the good works prepared for them and for
which they were created in Christ Jesus. From these means we may ascend to the
source and cause, that is, to the secret counsel of eternal election itself,
as though by Jacob’s ladder stretching from earth to heaven, and thus
conclude: whoever has been effectually called, granted faith, justified,
sanctified, endowed with true and living repentance and a serious and constant
conversion to God, is also necessarily elect and predestined from eternity to
attain eternal life in Christ Jesus.

[Marginalia: Supposition. Titus 1:1. 1 Peter 1:2.]

As for the minor premise or assumption, which is the application to each
individual particularly and by name of these signs and effects of election,
it proceeds in this way: But I too have been called inwardly by Christ’s own
Spirit and granted living and true faith, repentance, and so forth. This, I
say, depends upon the testimony of God’s Spirit bearing witness in our hearts
and upon the testimony of our own spirit. As the Apostle teaches us in Romans
8, “God’s Spirit together with our spirit bears witness that we are children
of God.” It is therefore worth explaining each of these testimonies more
particularly and accurately, since the certainty of our election and adoption
depends upon them.

[Marginalia: Romans 8:16. On the testimony of the Holy Spirit.]

As for the testimony of the Holy Spirit bearing witness to our adoption in
our hearts, it must be maintained that God’s Spirit now ordinarily works in
believers’ hearts neither by some extraordinary revelation nor by enthusiasm
or fanatical rapture apart from the word and gospel. That is an invention of
fanatical people and impostors such as the frenzied Anabaptists and others of
that kind. Rather, he works by applying the promises of the gospel to the
hearts of believers in the very teaching, reading, or preaching of the word,
and by that practical syllogism which we proposed above. For while ministers
of Christ, chosen and appointed to this office in the Church, propose, expound,
and apply those general propositions concerning all believers—for example,
“Whoever believes in Christ and repents is elect and has eternal life”—God’s
Spirit meanwhile illuminates the minds of the elect, opens, softens, and
affects their hearts, and gives them both the ability and the will to believe.
Thus, with a holy boldness and freedom of mind and with the ardor and desire
of faith, they apply these promises to themselves and form the minor premise:
“But I believe in Christ. I deny myself in order to follow him, live for him,
and consecrate myself and my life to him. In him I place and repose all my
confidence of salvation and all my joy and comfort.” This assumption, truly
made and proceeding from the inmost heart, cannot come from flesh and blood,
but from Christ’s own Spirit. Hence follows the joyful conclusion, full of
consolation: Therefore, I certainly determine that I too am a child of God and
chosen for life.

[Marginalia: Note the assumption.]

But we must advance still further here so that we may clearly distinguish this
gracious and blessed persuasion and internal testimony of the Holy Spirit from
Satan’s illusions and impostures and from the carnal and secure presumption of
many people. By this presumption people flatter themselves in their sins,
fondle themselves, and try to persuade themselves that things which actually
come from the flesh and their own corrupt nature come from God’s Spirit.
Everywhere you may see and hear many asserting that they are certain of their
salvation and persuaded of their election and are unwilling ever to doubt it,
while they still live an entirely carnal life full of security and ignorance.
What, then? Certainly the entire persuasion of such people is only carnal
presumption. For the true testimony of the Holy Spirit is easily known in
anyone from its fruits. In Romans 8 Paul joined two fruits that are excellent
and especially conspicuous when he says that the Spirit of adoption causes us
to cry, “Abba, Father.”

[Marginalia: Further treatment. Romans 8:15.]

First, he indicates that the true testimony of the Holy Spirit concerning our
adoption, and therefore our election, is joined with serious and ardent
prayers, indeed with inexpressible groans and sighs. By them the faithful heart,
seriously burdened and afflicted by the weight of its sins, as it were fills
heaven and earth by crying and sighing to God to be relieved of this burden,
to feel Christ’s peace and joy and the love of God the Father poured out in
its heart, and moreover to receive the glorious redemption of the body through
Christ’s coming. In a carnal and secure person you will see nothing of this
kind concerning heavenly things. Whatever he babbles about the certainty of
his salvation, he is destitute of “the Spirit of prayers and supplications”
whom God pours out upon believers.

[Marginalia: Zechariah 12:10.]

Second, this testimony of God’s Spirit is also joined with a certain marvelous
affection of truly faithful love, reverence, and obedience in an elect heart
toward God as its Father. By this affection it loves and cherishes him above
all things and is touched and affected by the greatest reverence for the
divine Majesty. Wherever it may be, it walks before him in simplicity, fearing
his displeasure above everything and regarding it as more bitter than hell
itself. Above all it strives to commend and approve itself before him in
integrity, humility, and modesty. You will by no means see this affection in a
carnal and secure person. When an opportunity to sin is offered, he is
immediately carried away to fulfill his desires and is restrained from
committing sin not by love and reverence for God,

but perhaps for other reasons.

the others also commonly grow green together and put forth the eyes of their
buds and the beginnings of flowers and leaves, so too many other gifts and
special graces are joined with the true testimony of the Holy Spirit.
