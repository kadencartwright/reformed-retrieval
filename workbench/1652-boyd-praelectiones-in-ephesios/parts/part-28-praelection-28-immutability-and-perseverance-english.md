Lecture XXVIII.

There now remains the last class of objections, concerning the immutable
certainty of election and reprobation. By these, of course, the heterodox
attempt to undermine it, as though nothing certain and settled on this matter
had been established from eternity, or as though that counsel and decree of
almighty God could be rendered void—the contrary of which we demonstrated
above by the firmest reasons and clearest testimonies.

[Marginalia: Lecture XXVIII. Transition to the fourth class of objections.]

What, then, can or commonly is objected against this by our adversaries? First,
of course, some passages and testimonies of sacred Scripture, either twisted
by them in bad faith to a foreign sense or even understood ineptly and
childishly. For example, Isaiah 14:1 says, “Jehovah will have mercy on Jacob
and will choose the Israelites again, that he may restore them to their land.”
Similarly, Zechariah 2 says, “Jehovah will possess Judah as his portion in the
holy land and will choose Jerusalem again.” The former election, they say, was
rendered void, and therefore divine election is subject to change. Yet who
does not see that, in the manner and style of the prophets, these things are
ascribed to God anthropopathically, according to the effects and evidences of
wrath or love which in turn he displayed toward his people as occasion and
circumstances arose, while that same secret counsel and decree always remained
constant in the meantime? For when the Lord exercises and chastises even his
elect with various afflictions and miseries—exposing them for a time to the
mockery and cruelty of enemies and unbelievers, delivering them to their
dominion, or withdrawing during temptation their sense of his gracious
presence—then he appears to have rejected them. They themselves consequently
complain most grievously that they have been deserted by God, as is abundantly
evident from the book of Psalms alone. Conversely, when the most merciful Lord
shows that he is again appeased and favorable toward his own, who had
previously been afflicted, cast down, and severely tried—vindicating them from
their miseries and restoring them to their former state—he is again said to
have chosen them; that is, to have given abundant and conspicuous testimony of
his election and actually proved the constancy of his love by effects of this
kind. The same must be said of any passages of the same kind frequently
occurring in sacred Scripture.

[Marginalia: Isaiah 14:1. Zechariah 2:16. Answer.]

Second, they argue as follows from other passages of Scripture. If the elect
can be erased from the book of life, then election is changeable, so that the
elect can become reprobate; and, by the same reasoning, reprobation is in turn
changeable, so that the reprobate can also become elect. But the former is
true. Therefore the latter is as well. The assumption is proved, first, from
Exodus 32. Moses prays to Jehovah in this way: “Otherwise, erase me now from
your book which you have written”—that is, rather than not pardon this sin of
your people. Likewise, they drag into this sense the answer which God gives
when he says that he will erase from his book the person who has sinned
against him. From this they gather at least that God actually erases some from
the book of life, namely those who have sinned against him, and therefore that
these people change from elect persons written in the book of life into
reprobates and are struck from that register. Again, in Psalm 69 the Psalmist,
calling down destruction upon his enemies, says: “Let them be erased from the
book of the living and not be enrolled with the righteous.” Again, Revelation
3 says: “He who overcomes will be clothed in white garments, and I will never
erase his name from the book of life, but I will acknowledge his name in the
sight of my Father and in the sight of his angels.” Therefore those who do not
overcome, but allow themselves to be overcome by the Devil and sins, must be
said to be erased by God from his book. Again, in Romans 9 Paul wished to be
anathema from Christ for his brothers, his kindred according to the flesh.
Therefore this could have happened to him, and for the same reason it could
happen equally to others. Again, in 1 Corinthians 9 he says, “I beat my body
and reduce it to servitude, lest somehow, after I have preached to others, I
myself should become reprobate.” Therefore the elect can become reprobate.
Again, in 2 Peter 1 he says, “Be diligent to make your calling and election
firm.” Therefore election is not immutable; otherwise it could not be made
firm by any diligence of ours unless it were in itself liable to change.
Finally, why is there need of more? Judas Iscariot himself became a reprobate
at the end, although it is evident that he was formerly elect even from that
saying of the Savior in John 6, “Did I not choose you twelve?” From all these
passages they conclude that the elect can be erased from the book of life,
separated from Christ, and made reprobate. But, in a word,

the inference in all these testimonies just introduced is rightly to be
denied, as will be sufficiently evident from this brief examination of each
one.

First, as regards the passage from Exodus: (1) it does not follow that
whatever Moses asked of God could immediately have been done. For he desired,
and even expected, to be brought into the promised land, yet he did not obtain
it because God had decreed otherwise. Therefore, even if he had simply wished
to be struck from the register of the elect, it still would not follow from
this that it could have been done. Nothing is affirmed in that passage;
rather, only the most holy man’s wish and desire is expressed. Further, that
very wish or petition of his must by no means be understood without a
condition: first, the condition which Christ most expressly stated in his own
agony, namely, if it were possible. He says, “Father, if it is possible, let
this cup pass from me.” Then there is the condition expressed in the context
itself: “If not”—that is, if you are unwilling to pardon this sin of the
people, rather than let your sentence stand to destroy this entire people
utterly and devote them in your wrath to slaughter and final destruction. God,
however, heard Moses’ petition according to his own counsel, for he chose to
pardon his people rather than erase Moses from the book of life. Nor did he
utterly cut off the Israelites, as the event showed and as the words that
follow there direct us to understand, but he employed a certain moderation in
his judgments and punishments when visiting their sins. Yet it is evident that
there is no consequence from a hypothetical and conditional proposition to a
categorical and absolute one. We therefore certainly do not agree with
Cyprian, that most candid and eloquent—indeed, most blessed—martyr of Christ,
who in his fifth sermon, *On the Lapsed*, denies that Moses was heard by God in
this petition: “That friend of God,” he says, “who spoke face to face with God,
could not obtain what he asked, nor did he appease by his entreaty the offense
of an indignant God.” Others likewise deny from him that Moses’ wish was
approved by God, although Moses himself expressly testifies in Deuteronomy 9
that Jehovah listened to him on this occasion as well, since he postponed and
moderated his wrath and did not utterly erase the people by destruction, but
brought at least their children into the land of Canaan.

[Marginalia: Answer. 1. Concerning Exodus 32. The true exposition. Cyprian, *On the Lapsed*, fol. 222 of the Froben edition, and Polanus from him. Deuteronomy 9:19.]

As for what God adds in his answer—“I would erase the one who has sinned
against me”—(1) it must be taken not simply but also hypothetically, as if he
said, “If I were to erase anyone, I would erase the guilty rather than the
innocent.” Further, this must be understood not of just any sin but only of
one committed from settled malice and hatred of the truth, after which no
repentance can follow. Finally, this statement, like the statements that
follow in which some are said to be erased or destined to be erased from the
book of life, must be taken only metaphorically and anthropopathically, like
the former statements. They neither can nor should be understood of the
elect, but only of stubborn enemies of God and the Church, whether open
enemies, such as persecutors, or enemies concealed and hidden for a time in
the very bosom of the Church, such as hypocrites and the rest of the tares of
this kind, who were in fact never written in God’s book. Yet these things are
said of them in a merely human fashion. As long as they remain in the visible
Church, profess Christian truth with others, and embrace it in appearance,
they seem both to themselves and to others to be written in the register of
the elect. They are then erased and struck from it when it is declared that
they were in fact never written there.

[Marginalia: Note carefully.]

Augustine elegantly explains in this way the cited passage from Psalm 69 in
his treatment of that Psalm in volume 8 of his works. Let us hear him as our
answer: “Brothers, we should not understand this as though God writes someone
in the book of life and then erases him. If a man said, ‘What I have written,
I have written,’ concerning the title on which was written ‘King of the Jews,’
does God write someone and then erase him?” Note the elegant argument from the
lesser, or less probable, case. A little later he says: “This was said
according to their hope, because they thought themselves truly written there.
What, then, does ‘Let them be erased from the book of life’ mean? Let it be
evident even to them that they are not there.” He proves and confirms this
exposition from the words which immediately follow, when the prophet adds,
“And let them not be written with the righteous.” By these words he indicates
that those who in this sense are said to be erased from the book of life were
in fact never written with the righteous. He says: “‘They shall be erased from
the book of the living,’ that is, they will recognize that they are not there.
For the verse that follows explains what was said: ‘And let them not be
written with the righteous.’ Therefore, I said, ‘Let them be erased’ according
to their hope; but according to your equity, what do I say? ‘Let them not be
written.’” Thus he argues subtly and shrewdly in his usual manner and genius.
It is as though he said: what, in respect to their own vain hope and the
deceptive opinion of others, is to be erased from the book of life is, in
respect to your hidden judgment, not to be written in it and to be found never
to have been written there.

[Marginalia: 2. Concerning Psalm 69. Augustine on Psalm 69.]

Third, as to the passage from Revelation, “He who overcomes will not be
erased,” the inference must be denied: therefore the one who does not overcome
will be erased, as though he had previously been written there—except in the
sense already explained, namely, that in the end it will become evident that
he was never written there.

[Marginalia: 3. Concerning Revelation 3.]

Fourth, as regards the apostle’s desire, it does not follow there from wishing
to being able. Nor in that passage does Paul assert that he could actually
have become anathema or been utterly separated from Christ and fellowship
with him, especially since at the end of the preceding chapter he had borne
such magnificent testimony to the contrary: “For I am persuaded,” he says,
“that neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities nor powers,
neither things present nor things to come, neither height nor depth nor any
other created thing can separate us from the love of God which is in Christ
Jesus our Lord.” How much less can this most holy wish, breathing zeal and
most ardent love, do so? For if all adverse and harmful things cannot separate
us, how much less can those things which both attest and advance this
inseparable fellowship with Christ? Such in particular is this burning zeal,
by which the apostle professes from a most sincere heart that he wishes he
might redeem God’s glory in the salvation of the Jews, even at the cost and
expense of his own eternal salvation. Further, this wish must also be
understood under the same condition which we mentioned above: namely, if it
were possible. It is as though he said: “If a choice were given to me and this
could happen in any way, or if it could stand with God’s secret counsel—if it
did not conflict with his immutable decree and will—then as far as I am
concerned I am ready myself to be deprived of the eternal happiness acquired
for me in Christ and most certainly reserved through Christ, on the condition
that Christ himself be glorified in the salvation of my brothers and that his
kingdom be advanced and enlarged by this addition.”

[Marginalia: 4. Concerning Romans 9. Romans 8:37 and 38.]

Likewise, it does not follow from 1 Corinthians 9 that he could have become
reprobate. (1) Most people understand by “reprobate” or *adokimos* in this
passage not someone rejected from the eternal grace of election and opposed
to one chosen for life, but rather

[Marginalia: 5. Concerning 1 Corinthians 9.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 158 | De Prædestinatione p. 114]

one rejected in the business under discussion—that is, a boxer or runner who
is unfit, or
even a teacher who is unworthy to be heard while teaching others. It is as
though the apostle said, “May I not be found actually and in fact to be such a
person if my life and conduct disagree with my doctrine, if in my own life I
do not follow the same rule which I prescribe in teaching others.” Similarly,
in Hebrews 6 bad and unfruitful soil is called reprobate or rejected, that is,
worthy to be abandoned and unworthy to be sown or cultivated. In Jeremiah 6,
silver that is not of the right standard or vein, which cannot be purified or
refined by any skill or furnace, is called reprobate, that is, worthy to be
cast away. But because this can be said only of those who have also been
rejected from eternal life and grace—that they are reprobate silver or soil,
as we saw above in the proper place—and because Paul himself could not be a
good and faithful Christian yet meanwhile an apostle worthy of rejection, a
teacher unworthy to be heard because of an unsound life dissenting from sound
doctrine, we could therefore answer as follows even if we were to understand
that word here and elsewhere of eternal reprobation: to become reprobate in
this passage is to be revealed and declared to be the kind of person one has
been from eternity. Thus, “Lest I become” means, lest I finally show in reality
that I am, and always have been, such a person. Yet I would prefer to interpret
this word of an effect and consequence rather than of an antecedent cause or
principle, that is, eternal reprobation. Even so, the inference is to be
denied. “For what kind of consequence is this,” says the most learned Beza on
this passage, “Paul labored lest he should become reprobate, that is, rejected
and worthy of condemnation; therefore he could have become reprobate? On the
contrary, it is a most certain indication and argument that those who
constantly and sincerely labor with Paul to be faithful, and who diligently
apply themselves to their salvation according to the prescription of the
divine word, can neither become reprobates nor will ever be such. For the godly
solicitude of believers is so far from conflicting with the certainty of
election that it instead powerfully confirms it in their hearts, even by this
very zeal for godly living. Just as the Lord wills the elect constantly to
persevere to the end as they press toward the goal in the right course, so by
this perpetual godly care and solicitude he also wills that same perseverance
to be nourished, cherished, increased, and daily intensified in them all the
way to the goal.”

[Marginalia: Hebrews 6:8. Jeremiah 6:3. 2. Beza’s Annotations.]

From this it is also clear why the inference from that passage of Peter must
be denied. The explanation lies in the distinction that this confirmation of
election, so to speak, can be taken in two ways. (1) It can be taken on the
side of the thing itself, or on the side of God, who is the author of
election. In this way it cannot be confirmed by any work or diligence of ours,
since the foundation of God already stands firm and immovable. Next, it can be
taken on the side of believing people, in respect of the certainty which they
can and ought to have concerning their own election and calling. In this
sense, according to Peter’s counsel or command, we must apply ourselves to
this above all throughout our life: that we make our election firm for
ourselves, having, increasing, and daily sealing its undoubted certainty in
our hearts until the end. But to infer from this the changeability of election
itself—that is, of the divine decree itself—would be the act of neither a
sound mind nor judgment. It would be just as if someone reasoned this way: a
person can doubt the truth of a necessary thing, and therefore must endeavor
to conceive in his mind the firmest certainty about its truth, just as the
thing itself—for example, that every triangle has three angles—is of necessary
and eternal truth. Therefore, that thing can become false or is not of
perpetual and necessary truth. Who would tolerate someone raving in that way?

[Marginalia: 6. On 2 Peter. The distinction concerning confirmation. An argument from an equal case.]

Finally, as for what is asserted about Judas the traitor from John 6, where he
is called elect with the others, this is a petty trap whose answer is evident
to anyone from the ambiguity of the word “elect.” It signifies not one
received and predestined from eternity to life and grace, but only one called
with the others to the apostolic office. And this was the second argument.

[Marginalia: 7. The example of Judas.]

The third argument can be formulated as follows. Those who can lose the faith
and righteousness once received from God can also fall away from the grace of
election, or become reprobates from having been elect. But the elect can
suffer or incur the former. Therefore they can incur the latter as well, and
consequently eternal election will not be firm and immutable. The major
premise rests upon the inseparable bond of election, justifying faith, and
justification itself. These necessarily depend upon election as their own
cause and principle and are indeed included in election itself, which is
God’s decree to confer upon the elect not only glory but grace beforehand:
namely, the grace of faith, justification, and adoption, conferred in time
according to that counsel of election. Thus, while election itself stands
immutable, the grace of faith and righteousness once conferred appears unable
to be lost. The minor premise is now proved by various testimonies and
examples from sacred Scripture, as well as by intrinsic arguments—that is,
arguments inherent in and drawn from the innermost heart of the very subject
under discussion—which seem to demonstrate the same thing. Once all these
have been discussed in order, we will put an end to this answering or
vindicating treatise.

[Marginalia: Objection 3. Proof of the minor. Part 1. Testimonies.]

The first testimony is from Ezekiel 18: “When the righteous man turns away
from his righteousness, and while practicing iniquity dies in those things,
he dies because of the iniquity which he practices.” Bellarmine argues as
follows: “What could be clearer?” He says this in book 3, chapter 14 of *On
Justification*, where he introduces all these testimonies, examples, and
arguments to prove that the faith and righteousness once conferred upon the
elect can be lost. “For how, I ask, does a righteous person turn away from his
righteousness if he is justified by faith alone and faith once conceived
cannot be extinguished?” Thus far he. From this his argument can be formulated
as follows. If the faith of the elect, or justifying faith, could not be lost,
then the righteous person could not turn away from his righteousness. But he
can, according to the prophet. Therefore the former can happen as well. We
answer, first, that there is an ambiguity in the middle term “righteous.” In
the conditional proposition it signifies one who is truly righteous, and
righteousness is taken for the gratuitous forgiveness of sins and the
imputation of Christ’s obedience and righteousness. In the assumption,
however, by the name “righteous” one can understand any hypocrite or temporary
believer, who offers himself under some outwardly honorable appearance of life
and can for a time appear righteous both in his own and others’ opinion.
Righteousness is taken for outward holiness of life and works, or for that
Pharisaic observance of the law, in the sense in which Paul before his
conversion and many of the Pharisees were and were called righteous. The
prophet’s statement must therefore be understood in this way, not of someone
truly righteous, that is, one justified and sanctified in and through Christ.
For he is like a tree planted by streams of water whose leaves never fall. The
one who is truly righteous, says the royal prophet in Psalm 112, “will not be
moved forever; he is in perpetual remembrance; his righteousness endures
forever.” Instead, the Lord speaks through his prophet about those righteous
only in appearance and according to their own or others’ opinion: “The
righteous acts which he did will not be remembered,” but

[Marginalia: 1. Ezekiel 18:26. Bellarmine, vol. 4, book 3 of *On Justification*, chapter 14. Answer. 1. Psalm 1:3. Psalm 112:6, 9. Ezekiel 18:24.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 159 | De Prædestinatione p. 115]

he will die for his transgressions because he turned aside to sin. Christ
himself likewise says in Matthew 9, “I did not come to call the righteous but
sinners to repentance.” Further, even if we grant that the prophet speaks of
those truly justified and regenerate, nevertheless nothing can be gathered
from his words as our opponents recite them and as we also translate them:
“When the righteous person turns away from his righteousness,” or “when he
shall have turned away.” It does not follow that this can actually happen, for
a condition posits nothing simply in existence, as they say.

[Marginalia: Matthew 9:13. 2.]

Second, from Luke 8: those on the rock receive the word with joy and, although
they have no roots, believe for a time and withdraw in a time of temptation.
The adversary says: “It is not faith that they lack, but perseverance in
faith.” From this an objection can be formed as follows. Those who receive the
seed of God’s word with joy and believe, but do not persevere in faith, can
lose faith. But the elect can do the former, according to the Lord in Luke.
Therefore they can lose faith. We answer briefly that the minor premise is
false and is not proved by the testimony introduced, which speaks only of
temporary faith, what the apostle in Hebrews 6 calls a certain taste of
heavenly things. This is not the faith of the elect, about whom the controversy
has been instituted. It is therefore evident that the ambiguity of faith,
believing, and receiving the seed of God’s word with joy must be carefully
distinguished here.

[Marginalia: 2. Luke 8:13. Answer.]

Third, John 15 says: “Every branch in me which does not bear fruit he removes,”
namely, my heavenly Father, says the Lord. From this Bellarmine argues: “The
branches which are afterward removed and cast into the fire were at some time
on the vine and indeed alive, because,” says that sophist, “branches are not grafted
in from elsewhere, but are born on the vine. Nor are dead but living branches
grafted in.” See how he distorts that most delightful comparison of the Lord,
as though the comparison corresponded in this respect: branches are born from
the vine itself and have no origin other than from it. Yet we are not born in
Christ as children of adoption, as if we were such by nature. We are children
of wrath in Adam and from Adam, and therefore are not native branches but
shoots grafted in and adopted through faith. But to the subject: the objection
is constructed as follows. If some of the elect are removed from Christ, that
true vine, then they can lose justifying faith and righteousness itself. But
the former is true, as is gathered from this passage. Therefore the latter is
as well. We answer that the assumption is false and is not proved by the
passage introduced, because those fruitless branches which must therefore be
cut off and cast into the fire are not elect but reprobate. Indeed, they never
were truly alive, but were endowed only with the outward appearance of life
through outward fellowship and profession, or with its superficial beginning,
so to speak, through a taste of God’s word. What the adversary advances to
prove the contrary from the example of branches which are born alive from the
vine has no force. As we have just warned, this is assuredly nothing but a
distortion of the Lord’s comparison. We are not born branches in the vine but
are grafted into it by faith, and this in two ways. Some are truly grafted in,
as all the elect who are endowed with true faith are actually given life by
that grafting and bear fruit in it. They never wither and never fall or are cut
from it. Others are grafted only apparently and in appearance, as hypocrites
and temporary believers are. Still others are fruitless branches which were
never given life in the vine and never truly coalesced with it through true
faith and the fellowship of the Spirit. To state the matter in a word, nearly
all the other testimonies continually suffer from the same defect of failing
to prove the point at issue. We are speaking about the elect, but they allege
against us things which have no bearing upon the elect, as will become clear
from a particular review of them all. For example:

[Marginalia: 3. John 15:2. The objection itself. Solution. Distinction concerning the branches. Summary of the review.]

Fourth, Matthew 24 says: “Because iniquity will abound, the love of many will
grow cold; but he who perseveres to the end will be saved.” The adversary
objects in this way, saying that we understand from this passage that not all
righteous people persevere and therefore not all are saved. For that cooling
of love, so to speak, plainly signifies its extinction; otherwise it would not
be opposed to perseverance to the end. From this the argument itself can be
constructed as follows. If some of the elect do not persevere to the end, then
some of them can lose faith and righteousness. But Christ asserts the former
there. Therefore the latter follows. We answer by denying the assumption as
manifestly false. Nor does the Savior’s statement prove it, for later in the
same chapter he himself asserts elsewhere that it is impossible for the elect
to be led astray by false prophets. Consequently, love is never entirely
extinguished in them, nor are they among the number of those who do not
persevere to the end. Those people are only hypocrites and temporary
believers, and it is utterly absurd to think that they were ever endowed with
true faith and righteousness.

[Marginalia: 4. Matthew 24:12 and 13. Answer. Matthew 24:24.]

Fifth, Galatians 5 says: “You have been emptied of Christ”—or, “You have been
made empty, separated from Christ”—“whoever are justified by the law, and you
have fallen from grace.” How, they ask, could they be emptied of Christ and
fall from grace if they had never truly been in Christ and grace? From this
the argument is constructed as follows. Those who can be emptied of Christ
and fall from grace can also lose faith. But some of the elect can do this.
Therefore they can also lose faith. We answer, first, that the phrases the
apostle uses in that passage must be correctly understood: “to be nullified
from Christ” and “to fall from grace.” The former is explained in various
ways. The old Vulgate has “you have been emptied of Christ.” Jerome expressly
rebukes this and renders it “you have ceased from the work of Christ.” Erasmus,
whom Piscator follows, has “Christ has become idle for you,” saying that the
Greek phrase contains a hypallage in place of what should have been said:
“Christ has been nullified for you,” which might have seemed rather harsh to
say. Finally, Beza, approaching closest to the Vulgate, has, “You have been
made empty, separated from Christ,” except that he inserts the word
“separated” to soften the construction. I add that it also serves to remove
the ambiguity. For if it were said, “You have been made empty or emptied by
Christ,” the preposition could be taken not effectively or antithetically but
causally, that is, not so that they are understood as amputated and cut off
from Christ, but as though Christ had emptied them or made them empty. That is
absurd and contrary to the apostle’s meaning. Taking the Greek words in this
sense, therefore, we say that both phrases denote the same thing for the
apostle in that passage, and that the latter, as the clearer, is subordinated
exegetically to the former. To fall from Christ’s grace, or to be made empty
of it as one separated from Christ, means nothing else in that passage than
to defect from the purity of gospel doctrine once admitted and approved at
least in outward profession, and to lapse into any error or heresy opposed to
the foundation, and consequently to be excluded from fellowship with Christ,
without

[Marginalia: 5. Galatians 5:4. Answer. The various translations of this phrase. The true sense.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 160 | De Prædestinatione p. 116]

having any right in him or his benefits, or any association with him. In the
meantime, however, those people appeared to be in Christ and to stand in his
grace as long as they professed gospel truth with faith and some kind of
assent. The apostle says in this passage that this happened to those Galatians
who had turned back from the gospel to legal ceremonies. It could not happen
to the elect, who embrace Christ with true faith and are truly justified in
him. We therefore answer the objection itself by distinguishing the antecedent
of the conditional proposition. Those who can be emptied of true and inward
fellowship with Christ and fall from justifying and saving grace can lose
faith. Thus the connection is true. But those who can fall away only from
outward profession and the grace of temporary faith, and in this way be
emptied of Christ, can lose justifying faith. Thus the connection is false,
because they never possessed true faith. As for the minor premise, it is
denied in both senses of all the elect. We admit that this can happen in the
latter sense to some who are called, as it did to those Galatians of whom Paul
speaks there, but we deny that they were from the number of the elect. As for
his words, “You who are justified in the law,” his meaning is readily
apparent, namely, “You who seek righteousness from observing the law,” as he
says in Romans 9. You thus appear to yourselves to be justified, although in
fact you are nothing less. For while you hope to appear before God filled with
the merits of your works and that legal righteousness, you will actually be
found “empty skins full of conceit”—that is, empty skins with respect to
righteousness but full of empty opinion. Perhaps alluding to this, the apostle
says here that they had been alienated from Christ, made empty, or emptied,
for outside and apart from him there is not even any part—much less the
fullness—of righteousness, life, or saving grace at all.

[Marginalia: The solution itself. Romans 9:31.]

Finally, the same applies to the assertions in 1 Timothy 1: “Some have made
shipwreck of faith”; in chapter 4: “The Spirit expressly says that in later
times some will depart from the faith”; and in chapter 6: “The root of all
evils is the love of money, after which some, while they coveted it, wandered
from the faith.” Again, Hebrews 6 says: “It is impossible for those once
enlightened, who have tasted that heavenly gift, become partakers of the Holy

they fall away, to be renewed again to repentance.” Again, 2 Peter 2 says: “It
would have been better for them not to have known the way of righteousness
than, after knowing it, to turn back from the holy commandment delivered to
them. For that true proverb has happened to them: the dog returned to its own
vomit and the washed sow to wallowing in the mire.” Especially pertinent is
what he had said earlier in verse 18 of the same chapter: those vain and impure
people, whose pestilent disposition and pernicious efforts he describes in
that passage, entice through the lascivious desires of the flesh those who had
truly escaped the ones living in error. The adversary presses these
testimonies as follows: “What can be said here? Did those who departed from
faith not possess true faith? And how did those who had never been on the ship
make shipwreck? Were those who had been enlightened, had tasted the heavenly
gift, and had become partakers of the Holy Spirit not truly justified? Yet
they fell away. Again, could a true falling away have been explained more
clearly?” He means the passage from Peter. “For the one who returns to his
vomit had certainly vomited before and had cleansed his stomach by vomiting;
and the washed sow would not be said to return to wallowing in the mire unless
it had previously come out of it and had truly been washed.” From these
passages the argument itself can easily be constructed as follows. Those who
make shipwreck of faith; who, after being enlightened and so forth, fall away;
who, after being cleansed and washed, return to their vomit and wallowing; and
finally who, after once truly avoiding the company of the wicked, are enticed
again by the lascivious desires of the flesh—these lose faith and
righteousness. But this can happen to very many of the elect. Therefore they
can lose faith and righteousness.

[Marginalia: 6. 1 Timothy 1:19, 4:1, and 6:10. Hebrews 6:4, 5. 2 Peter 2 and verse 18. Bellarmine’s words. The objection itself.]

We must answer, however, by steadfastly denying both the major and minor
premises. Not all those described in the major premise ever possessed true
faith; they were never truly justified and must not be reckoned among the
elect, but only among hypocrites and temporary believers, according to 1 John
2: “They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us,
they surely would have remained with us.” Nor do the cited passages prove the
contrary, for they must be understood either of outward profession alone and
assent given to gospel doctrine, and of outward fellowship with the visible
Church; or of that superficial and temporary taste of heavenly blessings
which takes no root in their souls, does not penetrate the very bottom and
innermost recess of the heart, so to speak, and which they never truly digest
within themselves, but merely touch and taste with a shallow and fleeting
emotion, whether because they crave novelty, are astonished by the greatness
of those things, or are dazzled by their splendor; or because they hope to
possess along with Christ what the flesh desires and delights in, including
the world’s goods and pleasures, without any circumcision of the heart,
sincere renewal of the mind, and consequently transformation of their whole
self into another, new and spiritual person. Those temporary believers, when
they receive the seed of the gospel with a kind of sprinkling or breath of
joy, when for a time they rejoice in the rays of its sun—as the Pharisees did
in that lamp which went before Christ, namely, John’s preaching—by no means
set these things before themselves or keep them before their eyes. Thus a
little later they spit out and loathe what they had tasted; or, when the heat
of persecution or desire arises, they depart from the truth and cannot bring
themselves to deny themselves, crucify the flesh and the world, and take up
and endure a continual cross. They cannot make that happiest loss of all the
merchandise of flesh and world in order to gain Christ, that union of infinite
dignity and price, as all their good. Or, finally, these passages must be
understood merely of historical knowledge, of a general sense without special
application and confidence, and of other degrees and rudiments of faith of
this kind which can be common to the reprobate along with the elect—not of the
faith of the elect, which is solid and sincere and is perpetually joined to a
zeal for true repentance.

[Marginalia: Answer. 1 John 2:19. 1, 2. Note the character of hypocrites and temporary believers. John 5:35.]

As for Peter’s statement that “they truly escaped those who live in error,” we
must maintain, first, that the word “truly” is not read consistently in all
copies. In some the word “scarcely,” or in others “a little,” is found in its
place. The old interpreter appears to have read it in this way, for he renders
it “a little”; it ought to be translated “nearly” or “almost.”

[Marginalia: Anticipation.]

other than that they actually and in fact had truly separated themselves from
the wicked, both with respect to outward conduct and with respect to outward
moral discipline,
