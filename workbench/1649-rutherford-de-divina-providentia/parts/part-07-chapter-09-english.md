# CHAPTER IX

## Is the permission of sin absolute and dependent upon God's free good
pleasure, or is it conditional and resting upon the determination of the
created will which causes that permission? Gabriel Pennottus, Ruiz, Smising,
Jacob Arminius, and others are called into the debate.

This fourth question concerning permission is particularly necessary in this
treatise. For Diego Ruiz, the other Jesuits, and the Arminians maintain that one
who is permitted to sin always has God's concurrence ready at hand for not
sinning. Consequently, it will not be from God or his pure good pleasure

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *Treatise on the Will*, disputation 26, figure 7.]

[Chapter IX; printed page 87; PDF page 139]

that God's permission exists in reality, at least in second act; instead, it
will be due to the free will of human beings that Adam and his posterity are
ever actually permitted by God to sin. But the question's title can concern
either man's first sin or the comparative hardening and resistance which one
person opposes to God when he calls rather than another who does not resist,
but opens his heart to God when he knocks and answers when he calls. We teach
that the existence of such permission comes from God's pure and simple choice,
not from the cooperation of the created will. Our opponents, however, maintain
that whether any creature is permitted by God to sin rests upon the free
influence of the created will. Or the question can concern judicial permission,
which people commonly summon upon themselves through crimes previously
committed. Many merit God's permissive desertion, and we teach that such
permission flows not from God's pure good pleasure but from human demerit and
from divine justice punishing sin with sin. The question does not concern a
purely penal permission in this sense, for we confess that God's justice is
preeminent here.

The next question is this: Are people permitted to sin because they will the
permission by a volition numerically distinct from that by which they will the
permitted sin? For by the same volition by which they will sin, they are said
virtually and interpretatively to will permission itself. On our opponents'
account, God permits the will to fall because the will itself concurs with and
influences the fall prior by nature to God's permission being given. Thus God
permits the will to fall because it wills to fall. The will does not, however,
will to fall away and fall because—as we teach—God first permits it to fall and
denies it his strengthening aids, which, if given, would keep it from falling.
Our thesis is therefore as follows.

1. “God, as absolute, free, and independent, is Lord of his permission. God's
permission therefore exists in second act because God so willed, not because
the created will so willed.”

2. “It belongs to God's independent dominion that this determinate number of
divine permissions exists—neither more nor fewer—and likewise this number of
free acts of human beings and angels, with no more.”

3. “It belongs to the dominion of divine providence that room should be made
through so many determinate permissions and free acts. I suppose 25,962 for the
glory”

[Chapter IX; printed page 88; BSB canvas 140 — leaf absent from the controlling IA witness]

“of punitive justice as 1,659,100, and for sparing mercy in Christ as
4,768,579,200,000.”

### The Jesuit-Arminian and Socinian-Pelagian Antithesis

1. “The created will is the maker and supreme mistress of divine permissions
and free acts. God's permission therefore exists in second act because the
created will so wills. So many free acts, and no more, exist not because God
wills but because the creature wills against God's will.”

2. “That the determinate number of free acts and divine permissions exists—no
more and no fewer—belongs to the dominion of the created will, not to God.”

3. “It belongs to the dominion of the human and angelic will that room should
be made through 25,962 determinate permissions, for the measure of the glory of
justice as 1,659,100, and for mercy in Christ as 4,768,579,200,000; and through
so many free acts of the creature, neither more nor fewer.”

2. These three points are arguments against a conditional permission of God
and the same number of signal injuries to God's providence. I add a fourth
argument to them. If the power of free choice is such that the existence or
nonexistence of permission depends upon it, then God will be the author of all
the devils' temptations toward parricides, sexual crimes, poisonings,
blasphemies, and lies. For 1. God permits the devils to tempt toward every
wicked deed.

2. He denies the devils grace sufficient for not tempting. Grant that the
devils' will influences the temptation before God permits their will to exert
that influence. Nevertheless, God abandons their will to its own indifference
and denies the grace required to avoid the act of tempting. Thus the devils are
necessitated both to create the permission because of the lack of grace and to
the temptation itself by that same lack. This follows according to our
opponents' doctrine.

[Marginal summary: A conditional permission of God which depends upon the
creature's will is contrary to God's providence and grace in many respects.]

5. If it belongs to the created will that permission be posited or not posited
in reality, then not sinning rather than sinning will also belong to the created
will alone. I therefore pray in vain that God not permit me to sin, because
whether God permits or does not permit me to sin belongs not to God or his free
determination but to my free will. “For no one asks God,” Augustine says, “for
what he has in his own power.” Therefore I give thanks to God in vain that I am
not led into

[Chapter IX; printed page 89; PDF page 140; BSB canvas 141]

temptation. For if being permitted rather than not being permitted to sin lies
within the will's indifferent choice, then not being led into temptation rather
than being led into temptation will also lie within my choice. Holy fear that I
not be permitted to sin, confidence in God by which I trust that I will not be
permitted or deserted by God, and humble subjection toward the God who is able
to permit would consequently collapse altogether. All these virtues would
ultimately terminate upon free choice, not upon God as permitting or not
permitting.

6. If the existence of permission in second act belonged not to God but to
created liberty, then God could neither infallibly procure obedience nor prevent
sin. The reason for the consequence is this: if God is necessitated by the
created will to posit permission in second act, he cannot refrain from positing
it unless he violates liberty, and therefore cannot prevent sin. He is also
necessitated to posit non-permission because created liberty so wills. Obedience
would thus have to be posited even if it were granted that God willed it not to
be posited. It is evident that this follows, because God permits people to sin
because they will, not the reverse; for, according to our opponents, they do not
sin because God permits them to sin. The created will will therefore
necessitate God to posit permission, and consequently God will be necessitated
to posit non-permission if the created will so wills.

Leonard Lessius enumerates two causes of permission. 1. “Lest a good work appear
compelled.” 2. “So that the creature may be solicitous not to fail God's grace.
If,” he says, “God did not permit evils, that solicitude would cease and we
would entrust everything to God.”

[Marginal reference: Lessius, *On the Divine Perfections*, book 11, chapter 6.]

I answer: 1. On Lessius's position, permission is God's mere nonaction upon the
will, which was refuted above. Yet permission in second act adds nothing to
permission in first act except the permitted sin, which is only a consequence
of permission and is not God's work. And if a free act were compelled unless
God permitted people to act freely, the good angels and those confirmed in
grace would be permitted by God to sin. That is absurd, for their actions are
not compelled. Indeed, Christ would be permitted to sin, which is absurd.

[Marginal summary: Lessius's causes of permission are refuted.]

2. If human beings are permitted to act freely lest the free agent fail to
cooperate with God, then in the good angels and those confirmed in grace

[Chapter IX; printed page 90; PDF page 141]

there would be no pious solicitude not to fail God's cooperating grace. That is
absurd to say.

3. It follows that solicitude not to fail God's grace is prior by nature to the
permission of evil. For if we are anxious and piously solicitous not to fail
God's cooperating grace—that is, lest we will to dissent from cooperating grace
—then God does not permit us to sin. But if we are not solicitous, God permits
us to sin. Yet the very absence of pious solicitude is sin and culpable sloth.
Therefore that solicitude is preceded by another solicitude not to fail the
grace of God which cooperates with that solicitude in us; and concerning that
prior solicitude the same question will return again, to infinity.

4. This opinion also makes the will the maker of permissions, even of the final
permissions of apostates, and refers the determination of the number of the
elect and reprobate, of those who perish and those who are saved, to the created
will rather than, with blessed Paul, to the depth of the supreme Potter's
wisdom and liberty.

[Marginal summary: This conditional permission removes pious solicitude and
refers the certain number of the elect and reprobate to the created will rather
than God's will.]

4. Pennottus explains the dependence of permission upon free choice as follows:
“It must be said,” he writes, “that between the good and evil use of sufficient
help there is a middle, namely, the non-use of sufficient help, which is neither
formally a sin nor a morally good act, but a middle by negation between the two.
A soldier who uses his weapons well is furnished with greater help. If he does
not use them to gain victory but is overthrown by the enemy, the non-use of the
weapons is prior to his being struck and overthrown by the enemy. Thus the
non-use of sufficient help is prior to the permission of sin, and permission is
prior to the fall itself. — Nor is it objectionable for one thing to be prior
to another in a different genus of causes, as is evident in a window's opening
through the entrance of wind. The non-use of grace as a deficient cause is
posterior to permission, but as a material and demeritorious cause it is prior
to permission.”

[Marginal reference: Pennottus, *Defense of Human Liberty*, book 7, chapter 7,
folio 8.]

I answer: 1. It is absurd to invent “a middle between the good and evil use of
sufficient help—namely, non-use—which is not sin.” For not using grace is a
free moral act performed by one who is subject to law, and it is opposed to the
use of grace, or a good act. It will therefore be either a good or an evil act.
It is not good, because it is opposed to a good act; but, as Aristotle teaches,
good cannot be opposed to good. It is therefore evil, because at the proper
time either form or privation must necessarily be present in a capable subject.

2. Not to use grace is not to use a talent divinely granted, and that is
culpable sloth. Matthew 25:17, 9.

[Marginal summary: Aristotle. Not using grace is not a cause of divine
permission.]

[Chapter IX; printed page 91; PDF page 142]

It is an act opposed to the stirring up of grace to which Paul exhorts Timothy.
It is therefore sin.

4. If not using sufficient grace is not sin, then not believing the faith which
God commands will not be sin. For to use sufficient grace is to believe, and
not believing adds nothing to the non-use of sufficient grace.

5. A soldier who freely fails to use his weapons when he could use them is the
culpable cause of his ruin. Therefore one who does not use grace will be the
culpable cause of his fall.

6. If not using grace is not sin, God can justly give efficacious grace to one
who does not use sufficient grace. But it is contradictory for efficacious
grace to be given to one who does not use sufficient grace, because efficacious
grace is itself the use and actual efficacy of sufficient grace. Thus the
non-use of grace would be the use of grace, which is incoherent. But if God
denied efficacious grace to one who does not use sufficient grace, then,
according to Pennottus, that non-use of grace is not sin or even a good act but
a middle act. It follows that God denies efficacious grace through no fault of
the person, and by the same reasoning gives efficacious grace through no human
diligence. This plainly overturns the tenets of the Arminians and Jesuits.

2. “Because sin immediately follows from the non-use of grace”—which the Jesuit
does not deny—and “the non-use of grace itself,” as the Jesuit says, “as a
deficient cause is posterior to permission,” God in permitting the non-use of
grace will be the deficient cause of the non-use and thus of the sin which
follows from that non-use. This is Jesuit incoherence.

3. If the non-use of grace in the genus of material cause is the meritorious
cause of permission, that non-use is sin, the contrary of what the Jesuit
asserts. For what merits penal desertion is sin, and for the Jesuits permission
is penal desertion.

4. Sin will likewise be prior to every desertion. That is absurd, for then the
first sin was committed before the sinner was permitted to sin. Nor does the
analogy of opening a window prove anything, as will be taught more suitably
elsewhere.

5. “There is,” Pennottus says, “a twofold cause of permission: 1. the natural
demand of liberty which operates defectibly; 2. the non-use of sufficient
help.” Corvinus and the Remonstrants, together with their Jacob Arminius, assign
the former cause. “But it must be noted,” Corvinus says, “that when the liberty
of choice is called the cause of permission, along with permission one must
consider the rationale of exacting obedience; that is, God's entire institution
by which he both strictly proposed the law and also made man such that he could
obey the law thus”

[Marginal references: Pennottus, no. 16; Corvinus against Molinaeus, chapter 6,
question 6; the Remonstrants' *Confession*, chapter 6, thesis 9.]

[Chapter IX; printed page 92; PDF page 143]

“proposed, or could disobey it. Hence it follows that if an entirely efficacious
impediment, or one so sufficient that it would certainly prevent sin, were
given, it would plainly conflict with the institution itself.”

I answer: 1. When it is asked why God permits sin, either the final cause is
sought, and it is answered that God permits sin so that the glory of his name
may be displayed; or the impulsive reason is sought why God deals in this way
with a free will rather than with other agents—for example, why he permits this
person rather than that one finally to sin. Our opponents answer that the
proximate cause of permission is the good or evil non-use of liberty. Others
more loosely assign the nature of liberty as the cause. But suppose the nature
of the free will were the cause why God permits sin, because if he infallibly
bent the will toward one of the opposites he would violate it unless he
permitted and released it to its own indifference toward acting or not acting.
It would then follow that God equally permits Christ's will and the will of all
the saints to blaspheme and sin against the Holy Spirit, for he violates neither
Christ's will nor the saints' wills. Indeed, he would permit the devils and
wicked people to act well and holily, for he does not violate their wills. But
the actual fall into atrocious sins adds nothing to the nature of permission,
because it is a consequence and something extrinsic to permission.

Nor does Corvinus's statement help: “The cause of permission is not the bare
nature of liberty, but together with it God's entire institution, by which he
strictly proposed the law and made man such that he could obey and disobey.” The
strictness of the proposed law and liberty to obey and disobey add nothing to
the nature of the liberty of a man subject to law from his creation. By the
very fact that Adam possessed the power to be unwilling or willing, he
possessed the power to will well and to will badly, supposing that he was
subject to law. For, given that Adam was created subject to law, it was
impossible for him in moral acts to be unwilling or willing without thereby
being unwilling or willing well and holily, or badly and impiously.

[Marginal summary: The cause of permission assigned by Pennottus and the
Arminians, especially Arnoldus Corvinus, is examined.]

2. Given that the will has such a nature, on this knowledge it would be
impossible for God to convert more people than he converts or to leave
unconverted more than he leaves unconverted. The determinate number of the
elect and reprobate would thus belong to human beings, not to God. For, they
say, God would overturn the nature of liberty unless he permitted it its own
absolute indifference, as the learned Jes-*

[Chapter IX; printed page 93; PDF page 144]

“uit Theodorus Smising, a professor at Louvain, asserts. We say,” he writes,
“that God is not bound by the obligation of his essential rectitude to avoid a
sin of created rectitude. First, he cannot do so morally—that is, not without
the very great inconvenience of disturbing the entire order of nature,
necessitating free causes to good, and failing to offer them, as he does to
natural causes, a concurrence congruent with their nature. Second, he cannot do
so physically either, because, given the law ordained by himself through which
he wills to concur with all second causes regularly and for the most part
according to their demand, it is physically impossible, by reason of his
immutability and omnipotence, for him not to put such a law into execution.”
Here, reader, behold the candor—a miracle of the Jesuit creature!

[Marginal reference: Smising, theological disputation 1, volume 1, tractate 3,
disputation 3, *On God's Will*, question 3, h. 17.]

It is evident from this that, on our opponents' position, for God to convert and
thus save more than are converted and saved conflicts with God's immutability
and omnipotence. By the same reasoning, because the permission of sin is within
the free power of those who are permitted to sin, God cannot convert people
later or sooner, or in a different place or time, than the place and time in
which they are in fact converted. Nor can he give a more intense or greater
faith—or a weaker and more infirm one—than he actually and in fact gives. What,
then, is the point of the prayer, “Lord, increase our faith”? For if the
permission of sin is within the power of those who are permitted, then the
permission of sin, and therefore of unbelief, to degree four rather than degree
six or seven will also be within their power. Sin to degree seven requires
greater permission than sin to degree four, and thus a greater release and
relaxation of the will. By the same reasoning, God cannot leave unconverted and
damn more than he in fact leaves unconverted and damns.

[Marginal summary: Smising candidly confesses that God cannot prevent sin; sin
therefore drains away God's dominion over the conversion and nonconversion of
more than are converted or unconverted, and drains providence from God's will.]

But Scripture speaks otherwise. Romans 10:23: “God can graft them in.” Romans
14:4: “God is able to establish him.” Jude 14: “He is able to keep us immune
from falling.” Ephesians 3:20: “He can do all things beyond what we are able to
ask.” Therefore, given the nature of liberty which he granted us, God can
convert more, convert them sooner, and from his gracious liberality give a
greater faith than he in fact gives to people. Yet it would be altogether
absurd if anyone answered Paul that God cannot do this unless he overturns
created liberty. It is contradictory that God could procure the free obedience
of a free creature by overturning and destroying liberty. It would be as though
Paul said that God can cause contradictions to be true simultaneously and is
omnipotent—a thing incoherent with omnipotence and infinite wisdom and sim-*

[Chapter IX; printed page 94; PDF page 145]

*ply impossible for him to do. But if the permission of sin depends upon
created liberty, so that it either exists or does not exist, these consequences
necessarily follow.

3. Either an impediment effective against sin according to God's foreknowledge
can be placed, or an ineffective one according to foreknowledge, or an
impediment according to no foreknowledge. If the first, then according to
Corvinus God placed against sin an impediment which conflicted with the legal
institution. This is absurd. If the second, then obedience was necessitated by
an effective impediment, which is a grave absurdity to the Arminians; for thus
obedience will be nonobedience. This follows from Corvinus's principles, for he
maintains that a motion of the will made by God according to a foreknowledge
which will render the free act infallible overturns liberty; Corvinus argues in
this way as well. And thus, according to the Arminians, God's foreknowledge
destroys human liberty. This was Cicero's impious dogma. If the third, God moves
the will in a wholly conjectural manner, not knowing which arguments will
infallibly move the will and which will not. This is utterly unworthy of divine
providence.

[Marginal summary: Corvinus's Arminian prevention of sin according to no
foreknowledge is refuted.]

[Marginal reference: Corvinus, *Against Moulin*, chapter 6, question 6, p. 167.]

4. Under the old covenant—and under the new also—it is essential to the will
to be able to be unwilling or willing when all the things requisite for action
are in place. But if an effective impediment to sin conflicted with the first
covenant, it would then conflict equally with the second; otherwise something
essential to the free will would have had to be removed through the fall.

5. Since Adam stood for a time and performed at least one act of obedience, it
follows that, in that one act of obedience at least, God proposed the law to
Adam according to a foreknowledge by which sin was to be effectively
prevented; and thus, at least in the first act of obedience, the will was
determined and necessitated. Or it follows that God procured Adam's first act
of free obedience according to no foreknowledge, and therefore according to no
providence.

Corvinus replies to this: *Although this single act could be prevented while
liberty remained intact, Adam could not in general be prevented from sinning
by an impediment effective according to God's foreknowledge, except by an
injury to the liberty which is directed toward evil.*

[Marginal reference: Corvinus, passage cited.]

I answer: 1. If God can prevent one sinful act while liberty toward evil
remains intact, and do so effectively, he can prevent two, he can prevent
three, and he can also effectively prevent a thousand acts. It is a foul and
watered-down fiction that God's foreknowledge is awake in one act but asleep
and buried in others, as though the Keeper of Isra-*

[Chapter IX; printed page 95; PDF page 146]

*el sometimes slumbered.

2. Indeed, if Arminian liberty stands, God cannot effectively prevent even one
sinful act. For if the will is indifferent, it can resist every motion of God
of whatever kind; but according to our opponents it is absolutely indifferent,
and is so essentially, with equal regard to being unwilling and willing.

3. If God cannot effectively prevent sinful acts in general “except by an
injury to liberty toward evil,” neither can he effectively prevent every sin
except by an injury to liberty with respect to exercise, by which it can act or
not act. For liberty toward evil in a person subject to law adds nothing to
liberty with respect to exercise. By the very fact that a person subject to law
can morally will or be unwilling, he can morally will or be unwilling well or
badly; for no moral acts in exercise and use which proceed from deliberative
reason are indifferent.

4. God's foreknowledge, which is later by nature than the acts foreknown,
cannot overturn liberty toward evil. It neither determines the will nor moves
it to will or be unwilling any more than I can be called the cause of the
motion of wheels on a carriage because I see the little wheels running and
moving and behold them with my eyes. But the Arminians' intent here can be
nothing other than sacrilegiously to make God's foreknowledge destroy the
liberty and contingency of second causes, lest people become servants of
providence—which is Cicero's impious dogma. The reader will observe in their
writings that the Arminians speak confusedly and hesitantly about God's
foreknowledge of contingent acts.

[Marginal summary: The Arminians necessarily have to deny God's
foreknowledge.]

5. Even if liberty toward evil were removed, liberty with respect to its
essence would remain, as it does in Christ, in the good angels, and in the
regenerate who are perfectly confirmed in grace. They are forbidden to
blaspheme the Holy Spirit under threat of punishment. Consequently, the true
nature and essence of a liberty subject to law, and of free obedience liable
to punishment and reward, would remain; this preserves God's legislative
wisdom.

6. But I return to the Jesuit. If God confers upon Adam, when he uses
sufficient grace well, an aid effective according to foreknowledge, then if
Adam has effectively used sufficient grace well, God must reward that good use
with effective aid. By the same reasoning, God must reward that effective
grace—for effective grace is essentially the good use of sufficient grace—with
another effective grace. Thus, once Adam had used sufficient grace well, it
would be im-*

[Marginal summary: Pennottus, who asserts that God confers upon Adam when he
uses sufficient grace another grace effective according to foreknowledge, is
refuted.]

[Chapter IX; printed page 96; PDF page 147]

*possible for him to sin. But they will say that God gives effective aid not
absolutely but conditionally, if a person wished to use it. Yet this is surely
exceedingly absurd, for God would thus say, “I give Adam effective aid provided
it is effective, and I give ineffective aid if it is ineffective.” But if the
Jesuits say that God gives to the person who uses sufficient aid well an aid
which is ineffective according to foreknowledge, then these absurdities
follow:

1. God's good pleasure is the cause of permission rather than the non-use of
sufficient grace. To the Jesuits this is a grave absurdity.

2. God rewards the good use of grace with ineffective grace. To them this is
unjust.

3. The person who uses sufficient grace badly obtains from God just as much of
the reward of effective grace as the person who uses it well, because each is
rewarded only with an aid of grace which is ineffective according to God's
foreknowledge.

7. Pennottus adds: “God permits a fall in two ways. 1. By denying every aid
which, out of infinitely many possible aids, he knows will be effective. There
is no cause for that permission on the reprobate's part; it belongs to God's
inscrutable and secret will. 2. He permits by denying every aid which would in
fact be effective unless malice placed an obstacle in its way. In this manner
the cause of permission is in the reprobate. — The sufficient demeritorious
cause why God can permit sin is original sin in everyone; but the cause why God
in fact permits sin and in fact denies effective aid is original sin and the
actual abuse of grace.”

[Marginal reference: Pennottus, *Bulwark of Human Liberty*, book 7, chapter 10,
no. 5.]

I answer: 1. This reply of the Jesuit does not remove my argument made above. I
ask why a person is permitted to sin who has performed six or seven immediately
preceding acts that were exceedingly good and praiseworthy, since the actual
good use of grace is now necessarily present in such a person. Either God will
give an aid which he knows will be effective, and if God does this by promise
and the order of justice, it will be impossible for someone who has once been
obedient in act ever to sin—which conflicts with Scripture, reason, the
Fathers, and all experience—or God will give an aid which he knows will be
ineffective. The absurdities I advanced above then follow, and it will follow
that the person is in fact permitted to sin without any demerit of his own.
Indeed, God and God's grace will fail the person who does everything that lies
within him, which is a grave absurdity to our opponents.

[Marginal summary: The cause of permission in fact is not the actual abuse of
grace, as Pennottus maintains.]

2. Sin exists antecedently to every decree of God, and the sinner freely places
himself in that order of things in which he will finally resist God when God
calls-*

[Chapter IX; printed page 97; PDF page 148]

*him. There is therefore no secret will of God here which is absolute, if the
Jesuit grants a conditional will resting on a person's demerit. But that will
is not secret and inscrutable. The antecedent is proved. A person's placing
himself in such an order—for example, Judas the traitor's hearing the sermon in
such a place and time, such an intent and application of his mind to weigh the
reasons and the senses and weight of things, such dissent, and the acts of
impenitence and unbelief—consists of truly free acts. They therefore possess
futurition antecedently both to an act of middle knowledge and to an act of the
divine will. Thus it is plain that God can never see an aid which he “knows will
be effective.” For God cannot foreknow a nonexistent thing, or foresee as
future those acts of obedience by Judas the traitor which will never be future
from eternity to eternity; and Judas's will could freely render every possible
aid ineffective.

Therefore, when the Jesuit says that “God denies an aid which would in fact be
effective unless malice placed an obstacle,” it is the same as if he said,
“God denies effective aid unless he were to deny effective aid.” For if a
person places an obstacle, the aid was necessarily and essentially ineffective.
The Jesuit therefore plays games when he says that God gives effective aid if
he gives effective aid, and gives ineffective aid if what was given was
ineffective.

[Marginal summary: The Jesuits defend the absurdity concerning the sufficient
and effective aid of grace which they charge against us.]

3. To deny effective aid unless a person were to place an obstacle is for God
to deny sufficient grace. For an aid which would in fact produce assent to the
good unless a person placed an obstacle is sufficient grace. But on this
account a person does not sin by placing an obstacle in the way of grace, for
according to the Jesuits and Arminians no one sins where sufficient grace is
lacking. Yet if God denies effective aid when a person has placed an obstacle,
then according to this doctrine he denies the sufficient aid which would
suffice to remove the obstacle.

4. The placement of an obstacle belongs to the essence of ineffective grace.
Thus Adam, in the first fall, placed an obstacle in God's way and therefore
sinned before he was permitted to sin, because he placed the obstacle before
God's effective aid of grace was given, through which he would by no means have
been permitted to place an obstacle. But no one sins unless he has been
permitted to sin; this position therefore overturns God's providence from its
foundations.

5. If a person “has not placed an obstacle in the way of God's grace”—that is,
if he has given assent to sufficient grace (for assenting and cooperating with
sufficient grace is not placing an obstacle in grace's way)—God cannot on fair
and just grounds, indeed cannot without a miracle or contradiction, deny such
a person-*

[Chapter IX; printed page 98; PDF page 149]

*who thus cooperates effective aid. For such cooperation and the non-placement
of an obstacle essentially include the effective aid of grace. Assent to or
cooperation with grace, and the denial of grace's effective aid, are truly
contradictory: the Former is essentially assenting to grace, and the Latter is
essentially not assenting to grace. But it is absurd for God to give effective
aid under a condition which involves a contradiction.

6. “The sufficient demeritorious cause,” the Jesuit says, “why God can permit
sin is original sin in everyone; but the cause why God in fact permits sin and
in fact denies effective aid is original sin and the actual abuse of grace.” I
answer: original sin is not the demeritorious and adequate cause why God can
simply permit sin in everyone. God permitted the good angels and Adam before
the fall, who were stained by no blemish of original sin, to sin. Nor can
original sin be called the demeritorious cause why God permits the sin of final
impenitence against the gospel in Judas the traitor rather than in Peter the
apostle. Although original sin is a cause why God could justly permit anyone to
fall, it cannot be the comparative cause why this person rather than that one
is permitted finally to resist God when he calls. God permits some to sin
finally in impenitence up to the last moment of life, but preserves others unto
the glorious day of Christ's coming.

I now ask what cause there is why God in fact permits some finally to fall and,
impenitent in sin, to close their day, while he does not permit others but
preserves them blameless unto Christ's coming. According to the Jesuits,
original sin is not the cause of this actual distinction which occurs in fact,
because all are equally guilty of original sin. Nor can the abuse of grace be
called its cause. For if final permission is causally founded upon the non-use
of sufficient grace, preservation unto Christ's glorious coming will likewise
be causally founded upon actual cooperation performed with sufficient grace,
unless our opponents show a difference from the Jesuit's principles. Yet in
this matter they flee from God's supreme liberty to his justice.

this compact: “If the reprobate have not been stained with original sin and
have not actually abused sufficient grace, then I will in fact-*
