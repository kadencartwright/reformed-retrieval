Verse 15. My son, if your mind is wise, my mind will rejoice; I too, with my whole being, will rejoice.

Verse 16. My kidneys will exult when the lips speak the most upright things.

In בני, “son,” there is a synecdoche standing for children of either sex. In כליותי, “my kidneys,” there is a metaphor transferred from the kidneys, which are covered by many wrappings and hidden far from the eyes, to the mind's [most hidden] thoughts, to distinguish this joy from sardonic and feigned laughter. Or it is a synecdoche of the part, so that the meaning is that no part of the body is without its own benefit flowing from this joy. So also Psalm 16, verse 9.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 519 | printed columns 1022-1023]

The purpose is to stir the godly to the pursuit and practice of true wisdom.

First he establishes the mind as the subject in which true wisdom resides. Here, although he includes all the faculties of the mind, he particularly means the will and affections, without which understanding and remembering not only contribute nothing to attaining happiness but become causes of more severe judgment. Hence, in Proverbs 2:1 he commands us to store wisdom away, and in verse 4 to search for it as for treasure; to keep it as the pupil of the eye, Proverbs 7:2; to love it as a sister, as a kinswoman, as rings put upon the fingers. And in Proverbs 4:6 he says, “Love wisdom, and it will preserve you; and it will honor you if you embrace it closely.” Accordingly, we ought to give all our effort and zeal so that wisdom may flow into the innermost recesses of our mind. To acquire it we must, if necessary, sell everything we hold most dear, along with that merchant in jewels in Matthew 13:46. Much more must we cast aside the vicious inclinations to drunkenness, avarice, fornication, and crimes of that kind, which openly conflict with it.

The fruit of this wisdom in the heart is set forth in verse 16, namely, right and upright speech. [For only good things are brought forth from the heart's good treasure], Matthew 12:35. And in 2 Kings 9:11: as the man is, so is his speech. He mentions this especially for the following reason: someone who does not sin in speech is perfect, James 3:2.

But this must not be taken so narrowly that it is not extended to all the fruits of godliness hidden in the mind. For we must hold to that saying in Galatians 5:25: “If we live in the Spirit, let us also walk in the Spirit.” For just as those who live this life express it in the vital acts of rising, walking, eating, drinking, and other actions of that kind, so those endowed with true godliness prove themselves such by words and deeds.

But a still further fruit of wisdom must be noted, by which we should be stirred, as by a spur, to pursue and love it. For by storing wisdom within and bringing it forth in words and deeds, we will do something most pleasing to our parents. We will fill their old age, made burdensome and bitter by sickness and the other discomforts that age brings with it, with the sweetest pleasure. We will thus compensate them for the anxious cares and concerns formerly undertaken for us, so that nothing in body or mind will be so hidden as to have no share in this joy. Conversely, by embracing folly we will bring their gray heads down to the grave in sorrow.

Finally, from this passage it is gathered that parents ought to rejoice deeply when they see unmistakable fruits of inward godliness in their children. Nor, if godliness is absent, should they think that they have ever obtained any sufficiently just cause for joy. Accordingly, they ought to be concerned not so much that their children be rich and honored as that they be cultivated in heavenly wisdom and true godliness.

### Translator's notes

The anatomical “kidneys” is retained because Cartwright explicitly explains the bodily metaphor. The source prints *oculentissimas*, a form suggesting “most conspicuous,” where the comparison with organs hidden from sight calls for “most hidden.” The bracketed translation records a probable correction in sense, perhaps to *occultissimas*, without silently changing the Latin. Verse 15's repeated *quantus, quantus* is rendered “with my whole being.” The square brackets around the sentence beginning “For only good things” belong to the source. Nasal abbreviations, including *existūt*, *nō*, *ambulādi*, and *eā*, remain in the Latin.
