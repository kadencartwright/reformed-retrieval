[Marginal summary: Twofold providence of God.]

6. I would also distinguish here two forms of GOD'S providence: natural
providence, which belongs to God as Creator regarding the ends of creation;
and supernatural providence, which belongs to God insofar as he regards the
absolutely ultimate end and directs all things to it. With respect to the
former providence, Augustine and Prosper rightly said that *no one was created
by GOD in order to perish*, because the end of creation and of the Creator is
that he should impart being (τὸ esse) to creatures, which is a good of the
creature. But with respect to supernatural providence and GOD'S supremely
preeminent end, God gives natural being and permits sin by an inscrutable
dispensation, so that the glory of justice may be manifested in the
destruction of a person procured by his own most just demerit.

Bellarmine therefore speaks weakly and foolishly when he says that *vessels
made for dishonor have it from GOD that they are vessels, but have it from the
condition of the matter, not from GOD, that they are for dishonor*. For
creatures do not-*

[Chapter XVIII; printed page 229; IA PDF page 289; BSB canvas 281]

*order themselves toward destruction except meritoriously—that is, by
sinning. But the question is not whether creatures, by sinning, dispositively
order themselves toward damnation by committing the things that incur actual
damnation. The question concerns GOD'S predestinative ordering. Dr. Pareus
therefore says soundly: *He takes “to be dishonorable” and “to be for dishonor”
as the same thing. That they are dishonorable they have from the condition of
the matter—namely, in the damnable mass. Just as a decayed statue has it from
the craftsman that it is a statue but from the condition of the material that
it is decayed, so the fact that they are constructed “unto dishonor” (εἰς
ἀτιμίαν), for dishonor, they have not from the material (except meritoriously)
but from GOD, who orders the reprobate vessels toward dishonor. For the
particle εἰς denotes an end, and an end is intended not by the matter but by
the efficient cause.*

[Marginal reference: Dr. Pareus, on Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*, book 2,
chapter 12, lecture 52, no. 4.]

Fourth, Becanus says that in the words “For this I raised you up,” and so forth,
God speaks of rejection from grace, not rejection from glory. For, he says,
*if you ask comparatively why, of two sinners—whether tainted by original sin
alone or also by actual sin—GOD shows greater mercy (that of efficacious grace)
to one than to the other, there is no cause other than GOD'S will.* But I
answer: Becanus also says in the same place, *GOD is cruel if he condemns a
harmless and innocent person to everlasting torments.* I take up the premise:
yet, according to Becanus, God by his will alone deprives comparatively
innocent people of that grace without which he foreknows that they will plunge
into eternal torments. For God's will alone, and not sin, is the cause why
efficacious grace is shown to one and in no way shown to another.

[Marginal reference: Becanus, in his little work on Calvin's predestination,
question 1.]

The other question now to be examined is: *Does GOD predestine human beings to
sin?*

Assertion 1. From eternity GOD preordained human beings to the material acts
of sin. 1. GOD absolutely decreed all contingent acts; otherwise, all positive
entities would not be subordinated to GOD'S active providence. 2. No free acts
can happen casually or fortuitously—that is, beyond GOD'S intention. But if
they were decreed not absolutely but only conditionally—that is, at a stage of
reason antecedent to GOD'S decree—and thus happened before and apart from
God's decreeing intention, these material acts of sin would happen altogether
fortuitously and by chance. 3. In the prayer “Your will be done,” we ask GOD to
prevent such free acts efficaciously, both in ourselves and in others; but the
acts that God does not efficaciously prevent, he efficaciously procures to
happen and absolutely decrees.

Assertion 2. This assertion—“sin, insofar as it is sin, can be ordered toward
the glory of GOD”—might have the following meaning, as if the particle
“insofar as” were taken specifically, as in this statement: “a man, insofar as
he is hairy, is red.” But this meaning is invalid, because the predicate “can
be ordered” would have to be restricted to the subject sin alone, just as only
the hairy man is red. Yet sin alone is not capable of being ordered toward the
display of GOD'S glory, for obedience and all creatures are capable of being
ordered toward the display of GOD'S glory.

But if the discussion concerns GOD'S glory manifested through the punishment
or pardon of sin, then “insofar as” is taken reduplicatively in this assertion:
“sin, insofar as it is sin, can be ordered toward GOD'S glory.” Thus God orders
sin, insofar as it is something punishable and pardonable, toward the
declaration of the glory of justice and mercy. Theodorus Beza and Robert
Rollock therefore rightly reject our formula of speech, “GOD wills sin insofar
as it is sin,” because GOD in no way wills anything contrary to his holy will
as such. Nevertheless, GOD permissively wills sin insofar as it supplies the
basis of a suitability for displaying GOD'S glory—namely, insofar as sin is
punishable and pardonable.

Nor should it be said that God does not will sin *qua* sin and under the formal
aspect of sin, but only according to its material being and insofar as it is a
bare entitative act. For the entitative act of sin is not formally a display
of the glory of justice and mercy, because God neither punishes nor, properly
speaking, pardons the entitative act of sin. Rather, the wickedness itself
adhering to the act is what formally displays the divine glory.

Hence the question is asked whether sin is properly a means of GOD'S glory,
and whether it is a means in itself or accidentally. I answer that it is a
means, and a means in itself, not an accidental means, as Arminius maintains.
For the worse sin is in the category of dishonorable evil, the better and
fitter a means it is in the category of a useful good conducive to displaying
the glory of punitive justice and pardoning mercy. An experiential declaration
of the glory of justice and mercy is impossible except through sin, because
nothing is properly punishable or properly pardonable except sin. 2. Every
accidental means must be reduced to some means in itself, but sin is a means,
and indeed the only means, of declaring this kind of glory. Nothing else is a
means in itself, because the glory of GOD'S vindicatory justice and pardoning
mercy is not displayed by either-*

*punishing or pardoning the creature's obedience. For the just Judge of the
world neither punishes, nor can punish or pardon, anything except sin.

“But sin,” they say, “obscures GOD'S glory, insofar as the creature does not
submit its will to GOD'S command and the Creator's will, but transgresses it by
sinning. Therefore, if obedience is a means in itself of GOD'S glory,
disobedience will be an accidental means.”

I answer: A fallacy based on the ambiguity of the word *glory* lurks here. That
which obscures GOD'S legislative glory, or the glory owed to GOD as lawgiver,
is certainly not a means of legislative glory either in itself or
accidentally. In this manner obedience alone is a means of GOD'S glory. Thus
the premise that sin obscures GOD'S legislative glory is true. And the
statement that whatever obscures the glory of punitive justice and pardoning
mercy is not a means of GOD'S glory is also true. But now the corresponding
premise is manifestly false, because sin does not obscure the glory of
punitive justice and pardoning mercy. Rather, sin wonderfully commends and
displays that glory—and sin alone does this; nothing other than sin does so.

Assertion 3. The permission of sin is in itself a means of GOD'S glory and is
an effect of predestination willed in itself and directly. Thomas, the
Salmantican College, Fasolus, and others concede this; and in support of this
position the Salmanticans cite Anselm, Ambrose, Bede, Cajetan, Bañez, Ripa, and
Cumelem. I am not detained by their statement that *bare permission,
distinguished from sin, is a means willed in itself by GOD, but not permission
insofar as permitted sin follows from it, because GOD cannot will permitted
sin even though he wills the permission of sin. For permission is a good
thing, but permitted sin is an evil thing.*

I meet this argument as follows. Bare permission of sin apart from every
existence of sin is not a means displaying the glory of punitive justice or
pardoning mercy. For the permission of sin is neither something punishable nor
something pardonable, because it is not sin; and God punishes or pardons
nothing except sin. 2. The permission of sin is a way and step toward sin, and
thus a remote means that, according to the adversaries, is joined by no
necessary connection with the glory of justice and mercy to be declared. It
is therefore a remote means from which the glory of justice and mercy is by no
means necessarily declared. For they teach that when the permission of sin is
posited, sin does not necessarily follow. Hence the permission-*

permitted sin is posited, the glory of divine justice and mercy cannot fail to
follow; for “that on account of which a thing is such is itself more fully

Meisner objects: *This is not universally true: one who wills the end wills
the means to the end. For a magistrate wills the promulgation of good laws but
not evil conduct. A gardener wills the end served by a dried-up fruitful
tree—namely, the heating of his lodging—but he did not will the tree's withering
itself. Next,* he says, *we distinguish between a means in itself, or by
nature (φύσει), and an accidental means, or a means by imposition (θέσει). Sin
is an accidental means of GOD'S glory and is such by imposition (θέσει) alone,
not by nature (φύσει) and in itself. One who wills the end also wills all the
means in themselves that lead to the end; one who wills the end does not will
all accidental means.*

[Marginal reference: Meisner, *Anthropology*, decade 4, disputation 4, question

I answer: 1. Meisner remembers poorly when he makes the promulgation of good
laws the end and evil conduct the means to the end. For the promulgation of
good laws is a means, and the expulsion of evil conduct is the end; and a just
magistrate wills both in themselves. Nor does the gardener intend to heat his
lodging by the withering of a tree. Indeed, he neither wills nor properly
permits a fruitful tree to wither, for that withering occurs when the good
gardener is simply unwilling and opposed to it. Sin, however, does not happen
when God is simply unwilling and opposed to it, nor does it happen without his
permitting it. 2. We proved above that sin is by nature and in itself a means
of declaring the glory of justice and mercy, not an accidental means.

Peter de Arubal objects: *A remote means cannot be loved from the intention of
the ultimate end except from delight in the nearer means that depends on the
remote end. Thus a man cannot seek a horse except from delight in going to
court. Therefore GOD cannot love the glory of punitive justice, at least
absolutely and in itself, unless he loves sin, which is the nearer means to
that end. But this is absurd. Therefore GOD does not absolutely intend such
glory.*

[Marginal reference: Arubal the Jesuit, vol. 1, question 23, article 4,
disputation 82, no. 2.]

I answer: One who wills the end wills both the proximate and remote means, but
he need not will the end and the means with the same love and the same delight.
Here it is enough that God efficiently wills the declaration of the glory of
his vindicatory justice with an effective and practical delight, since he is
the efficient cause in punishing and pardoning. But he need not-*

[Chapter XVIII; printed page 233; IA PDF page 293; BSB canvas 285]

*will all the means to that end with the same effective delight. It is enough
that he wills sins with a permissive delight, which is perfectly consistent
with the moral detestation of sin. Thus a person who wills health—the end—with
an effective delight and for its own sake need not will the opening of a vein
by the same effective will. It is enough that he wills the opening of the vein
for the sake of something else and by permitting the surgeon to open it.
Indeed, although he wills health for its own sake, he can nevertheless will
the opening of the vein in such a way that he detests it as inimical to the
particular nature. Similarly, someone can will in itself and efficiently to go
to court, yet can will to hire a horse with a will that is, as it were, mixed—
partly voluntary and partly involuntary—because perhaps the horse is hired at
far too great a price.

Assertion 4. The permission of sin and sin itself are means displaying GOD'S
justice, not by a speculative display but by one that is practical and
experiential. For GOD does not intend to declare just any glory whatsoever:
the glory of justice, say, if a person should choose finally to persevere in
sin; or that of mercy if he should choose to repent; or simply legislative
glory if Adam had chosen never to sin. Nor, as Corvinus and Vorstius maintain,
does God intend the declaration of the glory of justice and mercy *through the
legislation itself, through the threat of punishments, and through revelation,
even if sin had never existed.*

I prove it as follows. God decreed from eternity to declare in rational
creatures the same glory that he actually declares in time (Ephesians 3:9; 2
Timothy 1:9; 1 Peter 1:19–20). For the same CHRIST who was sacrificed and
preached in time is the one who was to be sacrificed and preached in GOD'S
eternal decree. Consequently, the same glory was manifested in CHRIST
crucified that was to be manifested in GOD'S eternal decree “before the ages
began” and “before the foundations of the world were laid.” But that was not a
disjunctive glory of CHRIST or glory of GOD the Lawgiver. Scripture contains
nothing about such a wavering and double-minded glory.

preordained by GOD, is not a glory to be declared speculatively and by bare
revelation. It is a practical glory disclosed by experiential knowledge in
the utterly just punishment of Pharaoh, in bearing the vessels of wrath with
great patience, and in saving the vessels of mercy. Thus Paul says after
Moses: “For this very purpose I raised you up, that I might declare in you”—by
an experience painful to you—“my power, and that my name might be proclaimed

*earth.” He does not say, “that my power might be capable of being declared”
and “that my name might be capable of being proclaimed,” but “that I might
actually declare my power” and “that my name might actually be proclaimed
throughout the whole earth.” Verse 22: “What if God, willing to display his
wrath and make his power known, endured with great patience vessels of wrath
constructed for destruction?” Verse 23: “And that he might make known the
richness of his glory in the vessels of mercy that he prepared for glory.”

It follows from this that GOD decreed and preordained from eternity to
manifest the very same glory of power and patience that he declares actually,
in the event, and by experiential manifestation in the destruction of the
reprobate—and to do so in this manner, not to manifest some other glory. He
also destined those numerically identical persons to this event. Likewise,
GOD decreed from eternity to manifest and declare the very same rich glory,
not another, that God declares by a gracious and merciful experience toward
the vessels of mercy—the same glory, I say, for which he “prepared them for
glory.”

3. The question is not whether God needs sin in order to glorify himself, as
the adversaries suppose. 2. Nor is the question whether God could have
declared his glory by means other than the efficacious permission of sin, the
permitted sin itself, and finally the punishment and pardon of permitted sin.
Who would constrain the Spirit of JEHOVAH here? But once it is posited that he
had approved that these events should actually be established, and that God
had decreed to manifest this determinate glory of vindicatory justice and
pardoning mercy, we insignificant mortals do not see how, under this
dispensation of GOD, God could have declared such glory otherwise to human
beings and angels by experiential knowledge than through these very means:
permitted sins, justly punished and mercifully pardoned in CHRIST the Mediator.

Otherwise, if the God of infinite wisdom had instituted another dispensation,
God could have displayed punitive justice and pardoning mercy by bare
revelation, the threat of punishment, and promise. But that declaration would
have been speculative, not experiential and practical. And because there is
no declaration of the glory of GOD'S justice and mercy other than this
practical and experiential one, no other was decreed from eternity by God and
preordained to be declared. Those who dream of another seem to have been God's
counselors and to think about God beyond what is written. For every decree of
GOD that we are bound to believe is either contained in GOD'S express word or
declared to us by the event. But the decree-*

*I mean the adversaries'—the Jesuits' and Arminians'—disjunctive decree. It is
neither contained in GOD'S word nor declared to us by the event. For they
imagine God to be uncertain, doubtful, and wavering, as though he had
determined within himself from eternity as follows:

*I intend to declare among human beings and angels either my legislative
glory, which is owed to me as lawgiver, if all angels and all human beings
persist in perfect obedience to my law; or the glory of justice, if some of
the angels fall away; or the glory of punitive justice, if all human beings
finally resist the gospel command to believe in CHRIST and repent; or the
glory of pardoning mercy, if all believe in CHRIST; or partly the glory of
justice and partly that of mercy, if some human beings believe and some do
not.*

We desire that such a decree concerning the manifestation of GOD'S glory be
demonstrated from Scripture or reason. Surely a commander in war who resolves
to take this town, but, if he fails to attain that end, to subdue another town;
then, if he does not obtain his wish, to take this third one by storm; and, if
his hope again fails to answer his wish, to bring a fourth little town under
his power; but finally, if the whole business remains undone, his hope is
frustrated, and his plan fails in every respect, he is compelled to return
home and spend his life in peace with his arms laid aside—such a commander was
not omniscient, but was ignorant of the event, doubtful, and wavering when he
formed his plan.

So too a shipmaster may resolve to bring his ship into this harbor, but, if he
is overcome by the power of the winds, to sail into another harbor; and if the
storm does not allow him to gain that harbor either, to bring the passengers
ashore on this coast by oar and skiff. Yet if the force of the winds compels
him to yield in all these matters, and at last the ship is driven into the
deep sea, dashed against rocks, and broken apart, he did not precisely
foreknow or understand the fortunes, events, and dangers of the sea, but was
doubtful and altogether wavering in forming his plans.

May we imagine anything like this about our JEHOVAH, the infinite, omniscient,
omnipotent Creator? Far be it, far be it! Hence we must say that God decreed
and preordained from eternity to declare and manifest, by this experiential
manifestation, this determinate glory of punitive justice and pardoning mercy,
not another glory; in these and these determinate persons, not others; and
through these means, not others—namely, through the efficacious permission of
sin, through these sins that occur, through this punishment, and through this
pardon of sins. Hence God did not decree to declare the glory of justice and-*

dispensation of GOD that he wisely instituted.

Assertion 5. GOD did not predestine human beings to sin with effective or
approving delight, as the adversaries prove. But God did preordain with a
permissive delight that human beings should sin, so that he would thus display
the glory of vindicatory justice and mercy. The latter part is proved. “Some
stumble at the word, unto which they also were appointed” (εἰς ὃ καὶ
ἐτέθησαν). Likewise: “Certain persons arose who long ago were designated for
this condemnation”—“forewritten for this judgment” (προγεγραμμένοι εἰς τοῦτο
τὸ κρίμα). Likewise: “The sons of Eli did not listen to their father's voice,
because” (כִּי)—Corvinus wrongly translates it “therefore”—“JEHOVAH willed to
kill them”; that is, “the sentence to kill them stood,” says Junius.

For if JEHOVAH decreed that Eli's sons should be killed by the Philistines
(this active killing, on the part of those doing the killing, was indeed an
unjust action and the most atrocious crime of killing JEHOVAH'S priests), then
God willed and decreed that the Philistines should kill them. Those who answer
that GOD intended and decreed the active killing of Eli's sons do not evade
the point by saying that his intention and preordination was not primary,
absolute, and antecedent to the criminal life of Eli's sons, but was secondary,
conditional, and consequent upon their abominable fornication. For whatever
GOD'S intention may be, from this passage I prove that God willed and
preordained the sinful and wicked killing of people who were already
scandalously wicked, which is our thesis. Nor will the adversaries say that
GOD intends sin by either an absolute and antecedent will, a conditional and
consequent will, or indeed any will by which GOD wills sin.

preordained them with permissive delight to sin.
