Finally, Romans 7:24 may be brought forward to establish our position:
“Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from the body of this death?”
Or, as Erasmus and others render it, “from this body liable to death.” As
long as the soul dwells in this body, sin too will dwell within it (“sin
dwelling in me,” as he had said in verse 17). It will at last be cast out
only when the soul flies from this bodily prison at death.

[1679 printed p. 277 | IA leaf 285]

There are indeed some, especially among both ancient writers and our more
recent theologians, who interpret this “body of death” as the “body of sin,”
as the Apostle calls it in Romans 6:6. He groans while oppressed beneath it
and eagerly desires by Christ's grace to be delivered from it—not only by
death, but in whatever way deliverance may at last come. Thus Origen says,
“It is rightly called the body of death, because sin, which is the cause of
death, dwells in it.” Ambrose says that it is “all sins: for the many make
one body, and each is like a member.” Augustine speaks to the same effect:
“To be delivered from the body of death is, when every sickness of fleshly
desire has been healed, to receive the body again not for punishment but
for glory.”

Calvin likewise explains it as the mass and heap of sin from which the whole
person is composed, as if it were a kind of body or a thing subsisting in
itself; Piscator gives this as a paraphrase of the phrase. Erasmus says,
“He calls it the body of death because the body is liable to sin through
its affections.” This also explains why, in the words “from the body of this
death,” Louis de Dieu does not refer the pronoun “this” to its more distant
antecedent, “body,” as Erasmus, Calvin, Beza in his final translation, and
others do when they render it “from this body of death.” Instead, with Beza
in his earlier editions and with the Genevan translators, he refers it to
the nearer word “death” and renders it “from the body of this death.” He
believes that this has the greatest emphasis and indicates that the death
Paul felt from the remnants of sin in his body was most grievous and
wretched. Thus “body of death” is said metonymically for a vicious body—that
is, one deserving death, just as “son of death” means a person guilty of
death—or for a death-dealing body, in the same sense in which Scripture
speaks of the “savor of death” and the “tree of life,” as Grotius explains
it. Contrary to Cornelius a Lapide, this exposition does not seem improper,
remote, or foreign to me. It agrees very well with the course of the
Apostle's preceding discussion in this passage: after speaking of sin
dwelling in him and of his struggle against it, he earnestly desires at
last to be delivered from it.

Yet this sense does not exclude the other, more customary exposition of the
passage. It renders this “body of death” not “death-dealing,” as though it
meant sin (which causes and inflicts death), but “mortal,” or liable to
death. Thus Theophylact; Erasmus, Estius, and others, both ours and Roman,
likewise render it “liable to death.” They understand the sense to be that
the Apostle, thoroughly weary of indwelling sin and his perpetual struggle
against it here, longs for death as the sole remedy for his evil. Thus
Calvin. As Theophylact rightly observes, from the first transgression our
body was made both sinful and mortal. All believers groan beneath both
burdens as long as they sojourn here. Yet by the supreme omnipotence of God
and grace of Christ, after the mother gave birth to the daughter, the
daughter was made to devour the mother: just as sin first introduced death,
so death at last drives sin away forever. Thus the Apostle desires to be
delivered from the body of death—that is, through death, from the mortal
body—so that at the same time he might also be delivered from the body of
death, that is, from death-dealing sin. The two expositions thus coalesce
into one complete exposition, and interpreters combine them both in their
commentaries.

[1679 printed p. 278 | IA leaf 286]

Thus Estius comments on “from that body of death,” that is, from this mortal
body: “During this mortality, the law and servitude of sin remain in the
body. But he desires to be delivered and rescued from this mortal body in
such a way that he may someday receive the same body immortal and free from
all corruption and servitude to sin.”

Beza likewise says this on the words “from the body of death”: “By ‘body’ he
designates this fleshly mass of the body, so as at the same time to signify
that in ourselves we are nothing other than a mass of sin and death, from
which he desires to be delivered.” And on the words “Wretched man that I
am,” he says something that also serves our present purpose: “It is indeed
wretched still to be in part subject to sin, which by its nature renders us
liable to death; but we must cry to the Lord, who will someday make us
victorious through death itself.” On the words “Who will deliver me?” he
adds by way of paraphrase, “The struggle, of course, continues until it is
ended by death. For in death that at last comes to pass which we read in
Job 3:17: ‘There the wicked cease from troubling, and there those whose
strength is exhausted rest.’” Although this was said of the external
afflictions of this life then coming to an end, it can equally be applied
to deliverance from the troubles of sin, which comes to all the godly only
then.

To this I will add only Pareus's comment on this passage of Romans now under
discussion: “It teaches that these conflicts are not ended as long as we
carry about a mortal body, but are finally broken off by death, when we at
last put off the body of sin.”

From these testimonies of Holy Scripture that we have adduced, we have, I
think, sufficiently established the first point. This will also suffice to
repel the excessive boldness and confidence of those who insult us—whom
they wish to treat as adversaries—with pompous words, as though the victory
were already won: “What passage of Scripture can they bring forward to
prove these things? We are certain that no such passage appears in the word
of God.” Yet here are several to which no answer has yet been given.
