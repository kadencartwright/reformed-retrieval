The Aetolians’ principal concern and hope was Antiochus. He foresaw that, after
Philip had been defeated and led in triumph by the Romans, his own turn would
come next; and yet the imprudent man hurled himself headlong into the mass he
saw before him. To secure himself against the Romans—now masters of all Greece
even without an army—he wished to fortify the cities of Asia Minor near the
Hellespont. Attalus, king of Pergamum, and the Rhodians turned as many of these
cities as they could away from Antiochus, especially Lampsacus and Smyrna.
These cities sent envoys to the Roman Senate to implore aid against Antiochus’s
tyranny. Meanwhile Antiochus had crossed into Thrace and brought many cities
under his power, especially Lysimachia, which he had strongly fortified at
great expense. The Romans sent envoys to Antiochus and demanded that he keep
out of Europe and permit the Greek cities in Asia to be free. While Antiochus
was deliberating about these harsh demands of the Romans, Hannibal came to him
as a fugitive from Carthage.

After the Second Punic War had ended, Hannibal, as first man of its senate,
governed the Carthaginian commonwealth in peace. By his prudence he corrected
many abuses in its magistracies and treasury. Yet his great services to his
country stirred up the envy of many against him, and they disparaged the man
at Rome through private messages, alleging that he was planning another war.
Publius Scipio defended Hannibal in the Senate against his slanderers, but
could not thereby prevent envoys from being sent to Carthage to investigate
Hannibal’s actions. The shrewd man foresaw the danger and hurriedly snatched
himself away in flight to Antiochus in Asia. At first he was received by him
with the highest honor; and when the Aetolians had persuaded Antiochus to wage
war against the Romans, Hannibal exerted all his strength

to have Italy made the seat of the war, declaring that the Romans could be
subdued by no arms but those of the Italians. He asked to be given only ten
thousand infantry and one thousand cavalry, with a few ships, and pledged
that he would raise a war in the heart of Italy no less terrible than the
former one. But Antiochus disregarded all Hannibal’s counsel, whether from
envy, lest the entire glory of the war accrue to Hannibal rather than himself,
or through the deception of the Roman envoys at his court, who held frequent
and very familiar conversations with Hannibal for the very purpose of making
him suspect in Antiochus’s eyes. He was drawn into Greece by the Aetolians,
where few soldiers and few ships accompanied him. Yet he promised with grand
words that, like a second Xerxes, he would fill Greece with his soldiers and
cover the sea with his fleets. The Aetolians too threatened that they would
soon arrive on the banks of the Tiber to besiege Rome. But those bombastic
words turned neither the Achaeans nor Philip away from the Romans.

> Marginalia, printed p. 222: *Antiochus imprudently disregards Hannibal*.

When the consul Acilius Glabrio came into Greece with an army, Antiochus and
the Aetolians began to tremble, the Greeks to join the Romans, and Antiochus
to fortify a camp in the narrows of Thermopylae. The consul attacked these
positions in that most strongly fortified place in vain, until the proconsul
Marcus Porcius Cato, with the utmost toil and exertion, led several companies
by unknown paths to Antiochus’s rear. When Antiochus noticed this, he threw
himself headlong into flight and did not stop until he reached Ephesus. There
he assembled a great army of seventy thousand infantry and twelve thousand
cavalry, with many Indian elephants and scythed chariots. Nevertheless, the
fainthearted man earnestly begged the Romans for peace.

Asia was assigned as a province to the consul Lucius Scipio, not by lot but
by the Senate, after Publius Scipio Africanus offered to accompany his brother
to Asia as his lieutenant. From Antiochus’s envoys for peace Scipio demanded
fifteen thousand talents and Asia on this side of the Taurus. When Antiochus
found these terms intolerable, the armies met near Magnesia. Hannibal was
absent in Syria, procuring a fleet from the Tyrians and other Phoenicians;
Africanus too was absent, hindered by illness. Antiochus had twice as many men
as the consul Lucius, but when they came to blows, the Asian soldiers could
not withstand the Roman force and immediately turned their backs. Antiochus
lost about fifty thousand infantry and four thousand cavalry, while the
Romans lost fewer than four hundred. Defeated, Antiochus submitted himself to
Africanus’s judgment, and Africanus added nothing to the former conditions.
Scipio Asiaticus’s triumph was more splendid than that of anyone before him,
and more treasure from his victory was carried into the treasury than Rome
had seen up to that day. The Romans demanded that Antiochus hand Hannibal over
to them, but the prudent man foresaw his danger and fled, first to Crete and
then to Prusias the Bithynian. When the Romans pursued him even there, weary
of flight, he drank poison.

The fortune of the Scipios at Rome in that same year was not much better.
Cato’s extreme envy had stirred the tribunes of the plebs against Africanus,
that excellent and wholly innocent first man of the Senate, alleging that,
corrupted by gifts, he had given Antiochus excessively generous peace terms.
He came on the appointed day to answer the charge of extortion, but his entire
defense was that on that very day of the year he had subdued Carthage. On this
account he put on his triumphal crown and urged and persuaded everyone to
follow him to the Capitol to offer sacrifices to Jupiter for that signal
service to the Roman people. By this most honorable device he eluded his
enemies’ loathsome slander. But when his adversaries persisted in troubling
him, he went into voluntary exile at Liternum, where he remained hidden until
his death, which soon followed in his sixtieth year. When the same charge of
extortion or embezzlement was brought by the same tribunes against his brother
Asiaticus, the man who had served the commonwealth so well was condemned by
the most ungrateful people to a fine far exceeding all his property. When
everything was examined, the meagerness of Asiaticus’s fortune abundantly
refuted his adversaries’ slander about excessive and ill-gotten wealth. Yet
the people were never willing to reverse their most unjust judgment.

> Marginalia, printed p. 222: *The Roman people are most ungrateful to both
> Scipios*.

Around the same time Philopoemen, the greatest of the Greeks, died an
unworthy death at the age of seventy. The city of Messene had taken up arms
and seceded from the Achaean confederacy. Philopoemen hastened to subdue them
with far too small a force, but, overwhelmed by the number of the Messenians,
the Achaeans retreated. Though weakened by age and sickness, Philopoemen
withdrew in the rearmost line so that he might protect his fleeing men and
ward off the pursuing enemy with his own hands. There his horse stumbled and
threw him to the ground; abandoned by his men, he fell into the enemy’s hands.
Led into Messene in triumph, he was displayed in the theater before the whole
people amid great wonder. That same night, however, the magistrate thrust him
in chains into a deep underground prison, where the public executioner forced
him to drink hemlock. A few days later the Messenians, defeated by Lycortas,
praetor of the Achaeans and father of the historian Polybius, surrendered.
Most of those who had been complicit in Philopoemen’s death killed themselves.
The survivors, in chains, accompanied his funeral from Messene to Megalopolis,
and at

Antiochus left Asia on this side of the Taurus to the Romans and sent twenty
hostages to Rome as security for the peace, among whom was his second son,
Antiochus Epiphanes; his elder son Seleucus succeeded to the kingdom. To pay
the fine promised to the Romans, their father approached the temple of
Jupiter Belus in Elymais near the Persian Gulf to plunder it. A riotous mob
cut him down together with his entire army in the thirty-sixth year of his
reign. Justin would have him killed at the temple of Jupiter Dindymaeus, but
it was Cybele, not Jupiter, who had a temple on Mount Dindymus in Phrygia.
Moreover, the Dindymaean temple never enjoyed any reputation for riches, nor
was Antiochus lord of Phrygia at that time. The temple of Jupiter of Dodona,
the reading found in some copies of Justin, did abound in treasures, but it
had been plundered by the Aetolians in Epirus long before.
