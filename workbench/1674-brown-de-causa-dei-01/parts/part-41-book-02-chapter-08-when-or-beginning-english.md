CHAPTER VIII

Whether and How the Determination of Time with Respect to *When*, or Its
Beginning, Is Useful to Religion

We said in the preceding chapter how useful the determination of time is to
religion and divine worship, both with respect to *how long* at once and with
respect to *how often*, separately and jointly. It remains for us now to
inquire whether it is also necessary that the third aspect of time—namely,
*when*, or the season—be determined in the same way. We will first see whether
this usefulness obtains in any other important affairs, and then proceed to
explain the religious *when*.

1. In other affairs it is useful and necessary to determine the order or time
of beginning, or *when*, only insofar as some accompanying accident requires
it. For, as we said above, this aspect of time is useful or disadvantageous for
advancing an undertaking only accidentally. Therefore, the determination of
this aspect is required only when such accidents make this opportunity, and
not that one, suitable and opportune for undertaking or advancing the work.
For example, when at this hour rather than another we are more ready and
better composed to perform such a work; when fewer impediments occur; when
more assistance is at hand from elsewhere; and when, once this determination
has been made, the determination already made of both continuation and
frequency can be observed more exactly.

2. When the observance of the determination of solemn and principal time—that
is, of continuation and frequency—requires that the season, or time with
respect to *when*, also be determined, then this determination too is useful.
For example, if one day each week must be given to study, it may seem useful
for that day to be named, lest study perhaps be wholly neglected. Yet,
speaking precisely about this aspect considered in itself, it does not appear
very necessary that the student himself or someone else predetermine that day,
so that it must necessarily be the first, second, or last day of the week. For
no day is in itself more suitable for study than another; all are equal. And
once the determination of one day in the weekly cycle is made,

[1674 logical printed p. 269 | IA leaf 287 | PDF p. 287]

it follows of itself that at least the last day of the week must be given to
study if none of the
preceding days has been applied to this undertaking.

3. Hence it is clear that this aspect of time differs greatly from the two
preceding ones. For no notable change can be made in the determination of
principal time, whether with respect to *how long* or *how often*, separately
or jointly, without notable harm to the undertaking to be performed during
that time. But a change can be made in determining this aspect of *when*, or
the time at which the work must be begun in order—for example, from the first
day of the week to the last—without any notable detriment. For that frequent
continuation, namely, a whole day every week, will contribute equally to the
advancement of study whether that day is the last or the first. Therefore,
without any inconvenience this determination can be changed because of some
incidental convenience or inconvenience, while the prior necessary
determination, which concerns the duration and frequency of time, always
remains fixed and immutable.

4. Although, as was said, it is not necessary that the determination of this
aspect be immutable, when the undertaking to be performed at that season
concerns several people in the same vicinity who are bound to come together
and assist one another, it is useful and necessary to determine the *when*, or
the time at or in which they must prepare for and come together for the work,
and to establish the same beginning of time for all. For here some accident
occurs which necessarily requires this, namely, the common benefit and
interest of all. Unless the time for beginning and undertaking that work were
determined, those fellow workers who must come together would be an impediment
rather than an aid to one another: some would arrive as others left, and these
would return in turn as the former departed. Thus they might perhaps never
undertake the common work shoulder to shoulder.

Now, to apply these things to divine worship or religion, we say the following.

1. The determination of time for religion with respect to the *when*, or the
order or beginning of time, is not useful in itself, but can be useful
accidentally insofar as determining it can contribute to the better observance
of time determined with respect to *how long* and *how often*. For if one day
in each week is thus determined, it is useful to indicate which day it is—the
first, second, or seventh—lest other affairs disturb and impede the holy
observance of the day when, if a particular day had been predetermined, they
could easily have been removed or arranged so as to present no impediment.

2. This determination can also be useful insofar as other accidents may press
for it: for example, if the assistance of others can be expected at that time,
or if sacred duties can be performed with greater freedom and a better
disposition of mind. Otherwise it makes no difference which day is destined
for sacred duties. The first day contributes no more in itself to advancing
God's glory and our salvation than the second, third, sixth, or seventh, nor
does the seventh more than the first, and so forth.

3. Yet there can be another accident which commends the usefulness of this
determination in sacred things beyond anything found in worldly affairs,
namely, the commemoration of some particular work of God performed at such a
time or on such a day. Certain days instituted under the Old Testament were
of this kind. The determination of such a day can be useful for stirring up
our gratitude and arousing our affections for such a benefit received, and so
forth.

[1674 logical printed p. 270 | IA leaf 288 | PDF p. 288]

Nevertheless, it always remains certain that the determination of this aspect
of time has in itself no importance for advancing religion in general, and is
therefore not substantially necessary.

4. Hence it follows that this determinate time, or *when*, can be changed—for
example, from the last day to the first of the week, or conversely from the
first to the last—without any detriment other than what may perhaps arise from
some accident. For even when this change is made, the same proportion of time,
recurring equally often, will always be spent upon sacred duties. Therefore,
there is no substantial advantage or disadvantage, suitability or
unsuitability, in either changing or constantly prescribing this aspect of
time—namely, the season or order—considered in itself. Hence it follows that the
same day prescribed under the Old Testament could also be perpetually
prescribed under the New Testament without the least detriment to religion if
nothing accidental pressed for a change. And if some accidental consideration
commends a change, that day can be changed, but by one endowed with authority.
It also follows that, for the exercise and advancement of religion in general,
it makes no difference whether the first or another day is now established in
place of the last, unless some accidental consideration declares that this day
ought to be preferred to all others.

5. Since determining both the duration and frequency of time is equally useful
to absolutely everyone, once that determination is given it will be useful, at
least accidentally, to make some determination of this aspect or season;
indeed, to determine the same *when* for everyone, so that they may not be an
impediment to one another, but an aid, in every kind, part, and exercise of
divine worship.

6. It must nevertheless be noted that, just as every accident is by nature
posterior to the substance of which it is an accident, even though it may be
simultaneous in time—as when it is an inseparable accident—so this accidental
determination of the season or order of time is by nature posterior to the
substantial determination of duration and frequency, although simultaneous
with it in time. Therefore, although the seventh day in the order of the week
was determined from the beginning, together with one day in the weekly cycle,
the determination of one day in that cycle was nevertheless prior by nature,
since it was substantially useful, while the other determination—namely, of a
particular day—was useful only accidentally.
