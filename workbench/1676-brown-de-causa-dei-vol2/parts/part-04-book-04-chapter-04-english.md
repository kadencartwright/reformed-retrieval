# CHAPTER IV

## Whether and to what extent the law of the Decalogue should be said to have been abrogated

The principal question raised between us and the *Antinomians* concerns the
abrogation of the Decalogue, or moral law. We explained fully above in Book I,
chapter X, what the moral law is. It was also stated in chapter I of the same
book what the abrogation of a law is. From what was said there it is also
apparent how the *abrogation* of a law differs from the *interpretation* of a
law and from other modifications of laws. Here I will only note that when a
law is abrogated, all its force is removed: either with respect to the
obligation it introduces to perform one thing or omit another, so that it no
longer establishes a duty; or with respect to the rewards or punishments
annexed to it, so that no reward is now to be expected by those who perform
what was formerly commanded and omit what was prohibited, nor can punishment
be inflicted upon those who omit what was formerly commanded and perform what
was prohibited.

Therefore, when it is asked whether the moral law has been abrogated, the
question properly is whether the moral law is a sign of the divine will
concerning our duty to perform this and omit that—that is, whether we are
bound to do what the moral law commands and to omit what it prohibits, or
not.

This question can concern either the regenerate alone or both the regenerate
and others. Thus the question can be either this: Has this law been abrogated
for everyone under the New Testament? Or this: Has it been abrogated only for
the regenerate?

This abrogation can take place in various ways: by the mere cessation of an
obligation, when, namely, a law was enacted only for some definite period and
was to have no force beyond that period; by the express abolition and recall
of a law enacted for an indefinite period; or, finally, by the enactment of a
contrary and incompatible statute.

This moral law can be considered in itself, insofar as it is a sign of the
divine will concerning the duty it establishes; that is, insofar as it
reveals to us God's will concerning our duty, thereby directs us in
performing it, teaches us what we must do and what we must omit, and obliges
and holds us bound to perform what is commanded and omit what is prohibited.
Or it can be considered insofar as the character of a covenant is annexed to
it—that is, insofar as a reward is promised to the obedient and this
condition is accepted.

Furthermore, the following things concerning the moral law must be
considered: 1. *Condemnation*, by which it condemns all sinners and
transgressors of the law and, because of the annexed punishment, sentences
them to eternal death, or constitutes them guilty of death. 2. *Strictness*,
in requiring exact, perfect, and rigorous observance. 3. *Compulsion and
servile fear*, by which it urges, forces, and constrains people to render
obedience to the law. 4. *Justification*, through which eternal life is
awarded to those who fulfill the stipulated condition of the covenant; that
is, those who observe the law perfectly have a right to eternal life or to
the promised reward.

The moral law can be considered either as it was first given in the state of
innocence,

[Printed p. 33 | IA PDF 41 | IA leaf 41 | IA decausadei02brow]

or as it was promulgated from Mount Sinai and proposed in a special manner to
that Jewish people.

With these points premised, let us see in what sense the moral law can be said
to have been abrogated now under the Gospel.

First, theologians say that this law has been abrogated insofar as it contains
the covenant of works. Indeed, ever since Adam fell and violated this covenant
which God had made with him as the common parent, head, and representative of
all, it has been impossible for anyone to obtain eternal life through this
covenant: both because no one has it in his power to satisfy divine justice
for sin committed, and because, on account of the viciousness and depravity of
nature—which is part of the death or punishment inflicted—no one can keep this
law perfectly. Therefore, from that time God never proposed that this law be
observed for the purpose that anyone might eventually attain salvation by its
perfect observance. After the fall no law was ever given which could give
life; otherwise, as the apostle argues in Galatians 3:21, righteousness would
truly have been by the law. *“But Scripture enclosed all things under sin, so
that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who
believe,”* Galatians 3:22. *“The wrath of God is revealed from heaven against
all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men,”* says the apostle in Romans 1:18.
After saying much to confirm this, in chapter 3, verse 9, he says that he had
charged both Jews and Greeks with all being under sin, which he also confirms
from Scripture in the following verses. Thus in verse 20 he concludes that
*“no flesh will be justified by the works of the law in the sight of God.”*
*“All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, being justified freely
by his grace through the redemption accomplished in Jesus Christ,”* Romans
3:23–24. For this was the powerlessness of the law when it was deprived of
strength by the flesh, Romans 8:3. Therefore this law has been abrogated to
the extent that the character of a covenant was annexed to it, or insofar as
it contained a condition by whose fulfillment a right to eternal life could
be acquired. For once this covenant of friendship was broken it became
irreparable. See further Galatians 3:10–11.

Yet although this covenant of works has been abolished to such an extent that
no one should now expect salvation through the works of the law, divine
justice must nevertheless be satisfied for the violation of this covenant.
Hence Christ, the surety of believers, whom God appointed *“as a propitiation
through faith in his blood,”* Romans 3:25, *“freed his own from the law of sin
and death,”* Romans 8:3. For *“by sending him in a form like flesh subject to
sin, God condemned sin in the flesh on account of sin, so that the right of
the law might be fulfilled in his own,”* Romans 8:3–4. *“For he who knew no
sin was made sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in
him,”* 2 Corinthians 5:21. *“Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by
becoming a curse for us; for it is written”* (Deuteronomy 21:23), *“Cursed is
everyone who hangs upon a tree,”* Galatians 3:13. Therefore this law has been
fully satisfied by our surety. He paid the penalties for our transgression
and perfectly observed the law. Thus, *“as through the disobedience of one man
many were constituted sinners, so through the obedience of one many will be
constituted righteous,”* Romans 5:19. Yet it does not follow from this that we
are justified before God by the works of the law, because this righteousness
or obedience is not our personal righteousness, which the law required, but
is imputed to us. It does not inhere in us but is in our surety, and through
faith—the condition of the covenant of grace, in which a new way is proposed
to bring fallen sinners subject to divine wrath to salvation—it is awarded to
us. Thus believers *“put on Christ,”* Romans 13:14,

[Printed p. 34 | IA PDF 42 | IA leaf 42 | IA decausadei02brow]

and are found in him, not having their own righteousness which is from the
law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the righteousness from God
through faith, Philippians 3:9.

Therefore, although this covenant of works has been antiquated and abolished
to this extent—that no one can now obtain salvation from that covenant insofar
as it requires of us perfect and complete obedience in every respect, to be
rendered by our own powers and in our own person—we nevertheless cannot,
properly speaking, say that this covenant has been simply and totally
abrogated, but rather that it has been mitigated or relaxed. [*“It is not
indeed totally abrogated,”* says Apollonius in *On the Law of God*, p. 276.
*“The righteousness of the law in our justification is abrogated and
abolished. For our surety and mediator was bound in our place to fulfill all
the righteousness of the law, fully bear the curse of the law, and satisfy
its most perfect obedience. This righteousness of the surety is imputed to us for
our justification before God,”* Romans 5:18–19; 2 Corinthians 5:21. *“In this
respect, however, the mitigation and remission of the law with regard to our
justification consists in this: the strict righteousness of the law is not
required to be rendered by us in our own person and by our own powers; only
faith is required, which is to be infused into us by the gift of God, by which
we apprehend Christ and his righteousness and receive grace and the gift of
righteousness,”* Romans 5:17 and 3:28; Galatians 3:16.] Elsewhere he states
the following, namely on p. 267: [*“The moral law, properly, exactly, and
rigorously speaking, has not been abrogated, abolished, and simply removed;
rather, it has been mitigated, relaxed, or derogated. For although believers
under the covenant of grace do not acquire justification before God from the
perfect and personal observance of the moral law, nor does the condemnation
and rigor of the law rest upon them—and thus the law has been mitigated and
relaxed with regard to our persons, since by our own powers we do not acquire
righteousness and life through the law itself—nevertheless, because through
our surety Christ we have perfectly satisfied the obedience of the law, it is
apparent that the law has not been abrogated and totally or simply removed.
But if God had abolished the law in such a way that neither man himself nor
his surety had to satisfy the curse and perfect obedience of the law, then
the law certainly would have been properly and simply abrogated. But since we
were bound, either in our own person or in our surety, to fulfill the law
perfectly and to bear its curse, and God never revoked that obligation; and
since, after perfect satisfaction was rendered to the law through our surety,
God still holds us continually under the obligation of the law to live in
conformity with it, it cannot properly and generally be said that the moral
law has been abrogated and simply removed. It should therefore be said more
properly that a certain change has been made in us in relation to the moral
law than that the law has been changed or abrogated in itself. For the
obligation, rigor, and condemnation of the law remained immovable and
perpetual, and had to be satisfied. But when we are transferred into the
state of grace, we are not bound to fulfill the law perfectly by our own work
for righteousness and life; the mediator and surety fulfilled it in our
place. Nor, when we sin, are we subject to the curse of the law, which the
same mediator bore in our place. The law therefore remains and continues in
the same force; but we have been changed and withdrawn from the rigor of the
law through our surety, because in our place he took upon himself the
obligation of the law and satisfied it most fully.”*]

Nevertheless, all unbelievers and all who are not regenerate and have not yet
fled to Christ with true and saving faith remain under this covenant—not,
indeed, so that they may obtain eternal life through obedience to it, but
because they still remain under its curse, most certainly awaiting the
execution of their sentence and the infliction of the punishment imposed in
that covenant, namely eternal death, unless they betake themselves to Christ,

[Printed p. 35 | IA PDF 43 | IA leaf 43 | IA decausadei02brow]

the sole anchor of salvation and safest refuge offered in the Gospel, and
embrace him wholly as he is offered. For *“he who does not believe is
condemned already,”* John 3:18. Although such people may outwardly and with
respect to profession perhaps be under the covenant of grace and members of
the visible Church, inwardly and in reality they are slaves of the devil,
John 8:44; 1 John 3:8, 10, 12; a brood of vipers, Matthew 3:7 and 12:34; a
dwelling place of Satan, Ephesians 2:2; *“children of wrath,”* verse 3;
without Christ, having no hope and without God in the world, verse 12; under
the dominion of darkness, Colossians 1:13; and under the curse, Galatians
3:10.

From this deliverance of believers from this covenant—which is common to
believers under both the Old and New Testaments—it does not follow, properly
speaking, that the moral law has been abrogated. They are not freed from the
law insofar as it is a law, but only from the law insofar as it contained the
covenant of works. The covenant of works was something additional, annexed to
the law from God's superabundant grace and favor. Therefore, when this is
removed, the law remains whole and unimpaired insofar as it is a sign of the
divine will concerning the duty we owe.

Second, under the Mosaic administration the same moral law was repeated and
solemnly promulgated from Mount Sinai, and with this law the covenant of works
itself was also renewed. For promises and threats were annexed to the
commands, the same as had existed of old in the covenant of works, such as
these: *“Whoever does these things shall live by them,”* Leviticus 18:5;
Galatians 3:12; Romans 10:5; Ezekiel 20:11, 13, 21; and *“Cursed is everyone
who does not continue in all things written in the book of the law, to do
them,”* Deuteronomy 27:26; Galatians 3:10. Yet it must be noted—as we will
also demonstrate more fully in its proper place later—that although the
covenant of works was then renewed and plainly displayed, it was nevertheless
adapted to that state of grace. Therefore this covenant was not proposed
absolutely so that they might eventually obtain life according to it. Rather,
God proposed it for an entirely different end: namely, that in this way they
might be pressed more and compelled more forcefully to flee to the promised
Messiah; or that, enclosed on every side, oppressed by insuperable
difficulties, and reduced to bondage, they might seek more diligently, long
more ardently for, and embrace more willingly the grace offered and promised
in the Messiah the mediator. God indeed proposed this covenant to them in
this form for this end, but not so that they should seek salvation according
to this covenant or expect eternal life to be granted finally through the
perfect obedience to be rendered to the law. For, according to Paul in
Galatians 3:17, the law could not make useless the promise made or renewed to
Abraham four hundred and thirty years earlier. *“But the law was added
because of transgressions, until that seed should come to whom the promise was
made,”* Galatians 3:19. Therefore, although that covenant which God made
absolutely with the Israelites was the covenant of grace, in which salvation
was to be expected and sought solely through the promised Messiah, it was
nevertheless dispensed in a legal manner, so to speak. Thus, by reason of the
proposed covenant of works—although not in its tenor and total rigor—they were
held bound to obedience with a certain rigor, bondage, and fear. This was not
so that they might obtain righteousness and eternal life through perfect
observance of the law, but so that through the pedagogy of this law, enclosed
under sin, kept under the law's protection, and confined for the faith which
was to be revealed, they might look to Christ and be justified by faith, as
the apostle testifies, Galatians 3:22–24. Hence it is that

[Printed p. 36 | IA PDF 44 | IA leaf 44 | IA decausadei02brow]

from the rigor of the precepts and the annexed threat there arose such great
fear and bondage in their obedience and approach to God that they were in an
infantile state—which differs nothing from the state of servants, Galatians
4:1—and reduced to bondage, verse 4. Hence also they did not have so great a
measure and abundance of the Spirit of adoption and of confidence in
approaching God as believers under the New Testament commonly have,
Galatians 4:5–7; Hebrews 4:14, 16 and 10:19–22. Hence too the covenant of
grace did not appear explained so clearly and distinctly, but was presented
more obscurely and shadowed forth by many coverings, types, and figures. For
this reason that ministry is said to have been *“a ministry of the letter, of
death, and of condemnation,”* 2 Corinthians 3:6–7, 9; the law is said not to
have been given so as to be able to give life; the law is said to work wrath;
and similar things. All these and similar statements, as Apollonius rightly
notes above on p. 186, are for the most part said synecdochically of the
Mosaic covenant—of that part of this covenant which contains the promulgation
of the natural law and all moral commands—and do not pertain to the whole
administration of the Mosaic covenant. They regard that promulgation of the
law in itself, separated from the promises and from the administration of
grace, and consequently opposed to Christ, to whom the Mosaic covenant leads
people. They treat the law according to its matter and precepts, not according
to the end intended by God in the law.

We concede that the law considered in this way has been abolished under the
New Testament. For now the Gospel is revealed more plainly, and all those
coverings in which it was formerly veiled are now removed. *“For the preceding
command is made void because of its weakness and uselessness, for the law
brought nothing to completion; but a better hope was introduced, through
which we draw near to God,”* Hebrews 7:18–19. *“But now our High Priest has
obtained a ministry as much more excellent as the covenant of which he is
mediator is more excellent, since it was established on more excellent
promises. For if that first covenant had been such that nothing could have
been lacking in it, no place would have been sought for a second. For, finding
fault with them, he says, ‘Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, when I
will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah,
not according to the covenant which I made with their fathers on the day when
I took hold of their hand to bring them out of Egypt, for they did not
continue in my covenant,’”* Hebrews 8:6–9. We have now been freed from that
pedagogue who exercised a certain authority with rigorous strictness and
severity. *“But after faith has come, we are no longer under a pedagogue, for
you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus,”* Galatians 3:25–26.
We are no longer under tutors and governors or reduced to bondage under the
elements of the world in an infantile state, because the fullness of time has
now come, and *“God sent forth his Son, born of a woman, born subject to the
law, to redeem those who were subject to the law, that we might receive
adoption. Therefore we are no longer servants but sons,”* Galatians 4:1–7. We
now have a great High Priest who has passed through the heavens and was
tempted in all things in the same way, yet without sin. Therefore we ought to
approach the throne of grace with freedom of speech, Hebrews 4:14–16. We now
have freedom to enter the sanctuary through the blood of Jesus, and we now
can and ought to draw near with a true and fully assured faith, Hebrews
10:19, 22. What is now useless is removed, 2 Corinthians 3:11. [*“Therefore,
insofar as the law,”* says Apollonius above on pp. 295–296, *“serves this legal
dispensation of the covenant of grace under the Old Testament, it has been
abrogated in the New Testament. Hence the time of the New Testament is called
by the apostle ‘a time of reformation,’”* Hebrews 9:10, *“and the Testament
itself is called new and a better covenant, which was established on better
promises.*

[Printed p. 37 | IA PDF 45 | IA leaf 45 | IA decausadei02brow]

*For although we do not have the promise of a better eternal life than the
ancient fathers had before Christ under the Mosaic administration of the Old
Testament, we now have a more illustrious promise of the same eternal life
than they formerly had. Christ is exhibited to us not as one who would come
at some future time, as formerly, and would render satisfaction for sins and
claim for himself and the Church a right to eternal life and glory, but as one
now manifested in the flesh who has completed the fullest satisfaction and
the true expiation of sins. And through Christ the remission of sins and
eternal life are now given to us, not as formerly, in advance on account of a
bond undertaken by our surety Christ but not yet discharged, while the
handwritings of sins remained in the instituted worship. Rather, they are
given by a title to the right sought, with complete liberty and immunity
from the old yoke of the law. Thus from now on no offering for sins, either
real or typical, is needed; and even in outward worship and religion that
daily repeated remembrance of sins which accompanied and was bound up with
the dispensation of the Mosaic covenant has ceased. While that dispensation
continued, the promise of the new covenant, ‘I will remember their iniquity
no more,’ had not yet attained its complete fullness. Hence the promises in
the old Mosaic covenant were sealed only typically by the death and blood of
animals; but now they have been most fully confirmed by the death and blood of
the testator himself. Therefore, insofar as this old covenant legally
dispensed was annexed to the law given by Moses, and the law accordingly
rendered its ministry to this covenant as such, when this covenant was
antiquated, the law was also to that extent antiquated and transferred.”*]
Some things here must be taken with a grain of salt.

Yet it does not follow from this that the law considered in this way has been
so totally abolished under the Gospel that it is no longer a means in the
hand of the Holy Spirit for promoting true conversion, nor that ministers of
the Gospel should no longer preach and press it for this end: to awaken secure
sinners sleeping in the lap of ease and shake them with terrors, so that,
convicted of their sins and perceiving their miserable state, they may flee to
Christ. When the Spirit comes as an advocate, his first work is to convict the
world of sin, John 16:7–8. Through the law comes the knowledge of sin, Romans
3:20. Christ himself followed this method in that excellent sermon which he
preached on the mountain in Matthew, chapters 5, 6, and 7, and which he
directed toward the conversion of his hearers. For he interpreted the moral
law and diligently pressed and inculcated the obedience to be rendered to it.
Christ said that he did not come to call the righteous but sinners, Matthew
9:13, and that those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who
are ill, verse 12. No one, however, is righteous or well by nature, since all
are guilty of sin and therefore children of wrath. Rather, he means those who
know that they are sinners and who are distressed because of their fear of
divine wrath. Since we are all by nature sufficiently secure, there is need
of a lively and plain preaching of the law, so that, when wounded, we may
betake ourselves to the physician and, conscious of our misery, flee to
Christ the deliverer, who calls all the weary and burdened to himself, Matthew
11:28. Did Peter not follow this method in that first sermon of his by which
he made his hearers pierced in heart? Acts 2:36–37. Those words of Paul in
Galatians 2:19 also confirm this use of the law: *“For through the law I died
to the law, that I might live to God.”* To the same purpose belong the praises
of the divine word, of which the law is a part: *“The law of the Lord is
perfect, converting the soul,”* Psalm 19:7. See also Psalm 119 throughout,
especially verses 50, 93, and 130. *“All Scripture is divinely inspired*

[Printed p. 38 | IA PDF 46 | IA leaf 46 | IA decausadei02brow]

*and useful for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, and for discipline in
righteousness, so that the man of God may be perfect, perfectly equipped for
every good work,”* 2 Timothy 3:16–17. The Scriptures themselves can make a
person wise for salvation, verse 15. *“The word of God is living and
effective, sharper than any two-edged sword,”* Hebrews 4:12. Hence the word of
God in general is also called the seed of our regeneration, James 1:18; 1
Peter 1:23. Moreover, this law, with the Holy Spirit cooperating and
assisting, is useful and effective for producing growth in grace and holiness
in those who are converted, as the whole of Psalm 119 demonstrates. For there
David often prays to be instructed more and more in that law, and professes
that he meditates upon it, delights in it, and loves it above all things. It
is also useful for effecting the first beginning of grace, or regeneration.
[*“For the Holy Spirit,”* says Apollonius above on pp. 208–209, *“can through
the preaching of the law set forth the lovable purity, beauty, usefulness,
and excellence of the virtues, so as to bend and turn human minds to love,
desire, and practice them. For what is regeneration but the inscription of
the moral law upon human hearts by the operation of the Spirit? The same power
of the word and operation of the Spirit are required for growth in grace and
virtues as for their first beginning.”*] He further adds, to prove the very
point we maintain: [*“When a minister of the word of God has expounded in the
ecclesiastical ministry the law of God and the virtues commanded in it, he
can in faith pray God that, by his Holy Spirit, he would be present with the
preached word and by his blessing make it powerful and effective for the
conversion of people not yet regenerate. Universal practice demonstrates
that this certainly cannot be denied. Consequently, through the operation of
the Holy Spirit, that word of the law is an effective instrument and means by
which the conversion and repentance of people are procured.”*]

As for the objection taken from 2 Corinthians 3:7–9, where the apostle,
comparing the law with the Gospel, calls the law *“a ministry of death and the
letter which kills,”* but the Gospel *“a ministry of the Spirit, of
righteousness, and of life,”* Apollonius answers it above on p. 210 in this
way: [*“The apostle here speaks of the law insofar as its ministry is
considered apart from the operation and gifts of the Holy Spirit, but of the
ministry of the Gospel as joined with the operation and gifts of the Holy
Spirit. Beza rightly observes that if the ministry of the Gospel were
considered and taken apart from the Spirit of God and his saving operation,
even that ministry would be a ministry of death and the letter which kills.
For it is just as impossible for us by our own powers and natural ability,
without the operation of the Spirit, to believe in Christ as it is to observe
and fulfill the law with perfect obedience. But when we assert that the
ministry of the law is an instrument of our regeneration, we establish this
with respect to the operation of the Holy Spirit joined to the law. The
Spirit uses the law as an instrument to produce conversion in human minds. 2.
The apostle does not state these things about the law absolutely and simply,
but* συγκριτικῶς, *comparatively in relation to the Gospel. For the chief and
principal subject matter which the law treats is the prescription of duties
and commands to be performed by us; it treats the promises of saving grace
and the mercy of God the Savior more obscurely and less principally. The
chief and principal subject matter of the Gospel, however, is Christ, the
fountain of life and righteousness, the author and giver of the gifts of the
Holy Spirit, and the promise of grace; it treats the commands*

[Printed p. 39 | IA PDF 47 | IA leaf 47 | IA decausadei02brow]

*of the law less principally. Accordingly, when the law is considered in
comparison with the Gospel, it is a ministry of death and a dead letter—not
absolutely, as though life, the Holy Spirit, and the righteousness of Christ
were in no way present with the ministry of the law, but because they are not
present in the same measure and manner. Nor do they flow out and become
communicated to people from the law's principal matter and subject; rather,
they flow out and become communicated to people from the matter of the
Gospel, the promises of grace, and Christ the Savior, principally, evidently,
and abundantly revealed and offered in the Gospel.”*] We may add the following
to these words. Since the apostle here compares the law with the Gospel, it is
clear that when he calls it a ministry of death and a dead letter, he means
the law separated from the Gospel—that is, not administered evangelically or
preached for the purpose of preparing the way to receive the Gospel. We
willingly concede this, and so we desire the law to be preached, but
evangelically: that is, mixed with the Gospel and only to convict sinners of
sin, so that they may long more ardently for Christ and embrace the offered
mediator more eagerly.

Nor are we moved by that objection taken from Galatians 3:2, where the apostle
addresses the Galatians in this way: *“This only I wish to learn from you: Did
you receive the Spirit by the works of the law, or did you receive him by the
hearing of faith?”* 1. Some commentators understand by the Spirit here the
extraordinary gifts of the Spirit; see Serarius in Marloratus on this passage.
Or, as Beza says on the passage, they understand those spiritual graces by
which the preaching of the Gospel among the Galatians was sealed, as in Mark
16:17; Acts 15:8; 1 Corinthians 1:5, 7 and chapter 12, which in verse 5 he
calls “powers.” [*“Nor,”* he continues, *“does the apostle argue
carelessly in this way, since in the beginning God used gifts of this kind as
irrefutable witnesses to the omnipotence of the one God, so as to render all
unbelievers ἀναπολογήτους, ‘without excuse,’”* Mark 16:20.] 2. But even if the
ordinary gifts of the Spirit are also included here, this passage proves
nothing against us, for we are not speaking of the law in the sense in which
the apostle takes it here. The apostle speaks of the law as it was pressed by
the false apostles against whom he was then contending. They pressed the works
of the law either as sufficient in themselves for salvation or as joined with
Christ. If the law is pressed in that way, we deny that it can in any manner
advance salvation and conversion, because it does not then serve the Gospel
but rather utterly destroys it. 3. We speak of the law administered
evangelically and directed to an evangelical end, not of the law set against
the Gospel and applied to overthrow it. We say that the law administered in
such a way that people see from it how great their misery and need are, and
how necessary it is for them, utterly rejecting and renouncing themselves,
their power, and their strength, to flee to Christ as the sole remedy of
salvation, is a means of the Spirit for promoting conversion. But we deny
this of the law if it is preached so that we acquire a right to eternal life
and justification before God, either wholly or partly, by observing it.

Nor does the objection taken from Galatians 3:24–25 prove otherwise: *“The
law was a pedagogue pointing to Christ, but after faith came we are no longer
under a pedagogue.”* For this use of the law for which we contend is not
properly pedagogical in the way it was under the Old Testament. That is, it

From what has now been said it is clear that although the law has been
abolished even with respect to the use which obtained under the Old
Testament, we nevertheless cannot properly say that the law itself has been
abolished, since that use was something incidental to the law and still
obtains to some extent, as we have now proved. Indeed, even if this entire
use, which we maintain still continues, had been utterly abolished, the law,
insofar as it is a law, could remain in force and bind to obedience.

Third, theologians commonly say that the law has been abrogated with respect
to *justification*. It is indeed true that since Adam fell—who, by reason of
the covenant of works made with him, could expect justification and life
through perfect observance of the law—no one has ever been able, or is now
able, to acquire a right to life or to be justified in this way. The apostle
proves this fully in the Epistle to the *Romans* and in that to the
*Galatians*; indeed, the entire Scripture of the Old and New Testaments cries
it aloud, testifying that another way of justification, diametrically opposed
to this one, has been exhibited to all Adam's descendants. Hence it follows
that even insofar as it contained the covenant of works, the law was not
promulgated to the ancient Church for the purpose that they should be
constituted righteous before God and acquire a right to eternal life by
rendering to it complete and entirely perfect obedience in their own persons
and by their own powers, as we have already explained and proved. Therefore
this privilege is not peculiar to believers under the New Testament but is
common to them and to believers under the old covenant.

Nevertheless, we cannot say that the law, even insofar as the covenant of
works was annexed to it, was simply and totally abrogated. Christ, the surety
and mediator of all believers, was bound in their place to fulfill all the
righteousness of the law and actually did so, bearing the curse of the law
which hung over them all and rendering the perfect obedience which it likewise
required. Rather, as was said, the law was mitigated or relaxed, since what
it could have required from us it actually required from our surety, as the
most wise mercy and clemency of God ordained. Those who believe in him and by
grace fulfill the condition of the new covenant are no less freed from that
eternal curse than if they themselves had satisfied divine justice, and they
obtain no less certain a right to eternal life than if they themselves had
perfectly observed the law by all their powers.

Much less does it follow from this that the law, properly speaking, has been
abolished, since this covenantal character is separable from the law itself.
The law could have been a law binding man to obedience even if the character
of a covenant had never been annexed to it, or even if Adam or anyone else
could never have been justified and acquired a right to life through perfect
obedience. Therefore, when this character has been utterly abolished with
respect to believers, it does not follow that the law itself has also been
abolished for them insofar as it is a law, so that by force of the law they
are no longer bound to do these things and omit their contraries.

Much less does it follow that the law has been abrogated for unbelievers or
those who have not yet embraced Christ offered in the Gospel, since they are
and remain under the law, and no other way of salvation lies open to them so
long as they cling to the state of nature, although that way also is
impossible. Their condition is all the more to be lamented because

Seventh, they say that the law has been abrogated for believers *insofar as it

Gospel in the same way as the law; taking occasion from it, corruption rages
and those wicked people become worse.

touch the nature of the law itself, so that one may by no means conclude from
them that the law itself has been abolished.

The orthodox do not say that God's moral law has been abolished insofar as it
is a rule of life and conduct for both the godly and the ungodly. We too have
confirmed this in part thus far and will prove it more fully in the following
chapter. From the fact that it is the perpetual standard of holiness

1. It restrains people so that they do not break out into manifest crimes,
James 2:2; Psalm 119:101, 104, 128.

2. By revealing the divine will and teaching a person his duty, it both
directs and binds him to walk accordingly, Romans 7:12, 22, 25; Psalm
119:4–6; 1 Corinthians 7:19; Galatians 5:14, 16, 18–23.

3. It lays open and reveals the wicked defilements of nature, heart, and life,

heart, and also reveals those very first motions which the Roman Catholics
still refuse to see and acknowledge as sins.

4. Those who measure and examine themselves by this law are convicted of sin,

5. They perceive more clearly how entirely necessary Christ is to them and
how perfect his obedience is, Galatians 3:24; Romans 7:24–25 and 8:3–4. For
in this way they see how destitute of righteousness they are and how vain it
is to seek righteousness in their own works, and at last they are compelled
to cry, *“O wretched man that I am!”*

6. It teaches how holy God's nature and will are, Leviticus 11:44–45 and
20:7–8; Romans 7:12.

7. It convicts them of how powerless they are to observe the law, Romans 3:20
and 7:7.

With respect to the regenerate, it has these further functions:

1. By prohibiting sins it restrains their corruptions, James 2:2; Psalm
119:101, 104, 128.

2. By gravely threatening sins, it indicates both what their sins have
deserved and what afflictions they can expect because of them in this life,
even though they have been freed from the curse which the law threatens, Ezra
9:13–14; Psalm 89:30–34.

3. Its added promises demonstrate how acceptable and approved by God their
obedience is, and what blessings they can expect on that account, Leviticus
26:1–14 compared with 2 Corinthians 6:16; Ephesians 6:2–3; Psalm 37:11
compared with Matthew 5:5; Psalm 19:11, although these blessings are not owed
by the law according to the covenant of works, Galatians 2:16; Luke 17:10.

4. It awakens and moves them to do good, as experience proves.

5. It causes them to value Christ and the benefits of his death more highly.
Hence after that struggle Paul exclaimed in Romans 7, *“I thank God through
Jesus Christ our Lord.”* Thus they are moved to gratitude, Luke 1:68–69,
74–75; Colossians 1:12–14; Titus 2:11–14; Romans 12:2.

of grace under the Gospel. Rather, they harmonize perfectly with it,
Galatians 3:21, since the will of a regenerate person has now been so imbued
and subdued by the Spirit of Christ that he can eagerly, sweetly, and
spontaneously perform the same things which the law requires, at least to some

4. They are deprived of every excuse, Romans 1:20 compared with Romans 2:15.
