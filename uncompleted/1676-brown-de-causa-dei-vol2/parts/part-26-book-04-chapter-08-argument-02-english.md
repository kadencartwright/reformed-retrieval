# CHAPTER VIII

## The law of the Decalogue is proved moral by other arguments

In the preceding chapter we set forth and defended our first argument for
proving that the law of the Decalogue is moral. Others now follow.

*Second*, that law is moral which Christ did not abolish, but confirmed and
ratified so that it would bind the Church of God until the end of the world.
The law of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so forth. The major
premise needs no confirmation, since it rests upon Christ's authority, which
no Christian would dare call into doubt. The minor premise is clear from
Matthew 5:17–20. To make this testimony plain, however, it will be useful to
set out briefly the following few points.

1. Christ uses various phrases here which denote the same thing and explain
one another. What in verse 17 he calls *the Law and the Prophets*, in verse 18
he calls only *the law*, and in verse 19 *these commandments*. What he calls
*one jot or one stroke* in verse 18, in verse 19 he calls *one of the least of
these commandments*. What in verse 17 he calls *abolishing the law*, in verse
19 he calls *breaking the law and teaching people to do so*. What he further
calls *fulfilling the law* and *all things being accomplished* in verse 18, he
calls *doing and teaching* in verse 19. Finally, what he calls *being called
least in the kingdom of heaven* in verse 19, in verse 20 he calls *by no means
entering the kingdom of heaven*.

2. The law of which he speaks here was very well known, so that none of his
hearers could have been in doubt about which law was under discussion. For he
begins his discourse in this way: *Do not suppose that I came to abolish the
law*—that is, the well-known law, the written law.

3. He is not speaking, however, of the entire law of Moses, as it is called.
For it is certain that he came to abolish by far the greater part of this law,
namely, the whole ceremonial law. Indeed, in this same sermon he removes the
law concerning divorce given by Moses and recalls everyone to the original
institution of marriage.

4. He therefore speaks of the law of the Decalogue, which was most properly
called *the law*, and of all those precepts which were substantial explanations
or applications added by the Prophets in their sermons, exhortations, rebukes,
and so forth. For, as Bucer says in Marlorat on the passage, he joins the
Prophets to the law in order to declare more openly what he means by the law:
namely, the teaching and instruction for life which the Prophets also handed
down.

[Printed p. 98 | IA PDF 106 | IA leaf 106 | IA decausadei02brow]

5. Some supposed, others suspected, and others falsely said that he had come
to abolish this law. He, however, took it badly that they entertained even the
slightest such suspicion about him. Therefore, to free everyone from this
fabricated, false, and wicked suspicion, he says, *Do not suppose that I came
to abolish the law*—that is, to remove its obligation. Consequently, he did
not intend to abolish either the moral law itself, whose epitome is the
Decalogue, or the teaching of the Prophets who explained and applied this law
and pressed it with threats or promises.

6. Indeed, on the contrary, he openly declares and categorically asserts that
he came not to abolish but to fulfill it (verse 17), and thus to demonstrate
by his practice the perpetual obligation of that law and by his teaching to
confirm the perpetuity of those laws. For *fulfill* is here opposed to
*abolish*. A law can be abolished, as the context teaches, both by practice
and by teaching. Therefore, when he says that he came not to abolish the law,
whether by practice or by teaching, but on the contrary to fulfill it, he
testifies that he came to fulfill it both by practice and by teaching. See
Calvin on the passage.

7. Indeed, prefacing his statement with his customary affirmation—which is
most forceful—he directly and plainly affirms that *until heaven and earth
have passed away*, neither one jot nor one stroke of the whole law will pass
away until all things have been accomplished. What, I ask, could be said more
plainly to declare and confirm the obligation of this law? Does he not teach
here that in the whole fabric of the world nothing is as stable as the truth
of the law is firm? Compare Jeremiah 33:20. See the commentators in Marlorat
on the passage.

8. By affirming this, he proves that he came not to abolish but to fulfill the
law, as the causal particle γὰρ, *for*, demonstrates. He thereby gives the
reason why he does not wish to dissolve that law's obligation, either by
practice or by teaching: namely, because it has been so established that this
law must bind perpetually, and not one jot or one stroke of it is to pass away
until all things have been accomplished.

9. He not only states this assertion, but also applies this general teaching
specifically for the use of his hearers, denouncing the gravest wrath upon
those who violate and abolish it, whether by practice or by teaching. For he
threatens in this way: *Whoever therefore breaks one of the least of these
commandments and so teaches people will be called least in the kingdom of
heaven.* That is, whoever teaches the abrogation of this law, whether by
practice or by doctrine, will be excluded from the kingdom of heaven. By this
he sufficiently indicates what a grave sin it is to violate this law and to
teach its abrogation or setting aside. He further demonstrates how free he
himself is from this accusation, how greatly those who suspect such a thing of
him are mistaken, and how his soul recoils from saying even once that this law
has been abrogated or that its obligation has been dissolved. Here, according
to Calvin, he speaks of the Ten Words, by whose rule all God's children ought
to shape their lives.

10. Conversely, he promises those who do and teach what even the least
commandment of this law enjoins that they *will be called great in the kingdom
of heaven* (verse 19). By this he plainly declares how highly he regards those
who testify by practice and teaching that this law binds perpetually.

11. The connection of verse 19 with the preceding verse—ὃς ἐὰν οὖν,
*whoever therefore*—shows that the preceding verse must be understood not of
the outcome of an event, but of the establishment of a right;

[Printed p. 99 | IA PDF 107 | IA leaf 107 | IA decausadei02brow]

not of God's decretive will, but of his commanding will. For from it he proves
that breaking or transgressing this law makes one liable to eternal death.
Therefore, when he said above in verse 18 that not one jot of the law would
pass away until all things had been accomplished, he was not speaking merely
of how the event would turn out, as though his meaning had been that no
promise, threat, or decree would fail to occur. Rather, he was speaking of the
act of legislation which establishes the duty of an office. For from it he
infers that the person who breaks or transgresses this law or these
commandments, or teaches that they may lawfully be violated, is liable to
death.

12. Finally, he teaches that his disciples must surpass the Pharisees, both
in exact observance of this law and in its legitimate explanation and proper
interpretation. Although the Pharisees seemed to observe the law exactly as
far as its external letter was concerned, they were far too lax with respect
to the inward disposition of the soul and the interpretation of the law
according to its proper breadth, as is clear from the interpretation Christ
adds. Therefore Christ says in verse 20: *For I tell you that unless your
righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will by no means
enter the kingdom of heaven.* Thus he also confirms what was said above:
namely, that all who break one of those least commandments and so teach people
are liable to eternal death.

From these points the truth of the minor premise becomes plain. Nevertheless,
the opponents object as follows.

*Objection 1.* Christ speaks here of the whole law contained in the
Pentateuch, as well as of the Prophets and the predictions of the prophets.
For he speaks of the Law and the Prophets, and these comprehend more than the
Decalogue, as is clear from Matthew 11:13; Luke 24:44; 16:16; and John 1:17.

*Answer.* We readily grant that the phrase *the Law and the Prophets* is
sometimes taken in a sense different from the one in which we contend it is
taken here. But this does no prejudice to us or to our position, since
everyone knows that Scripture contains various statements and various words
which are taken in different senses in different passages, and that we must
interpret them according to the purpose of the passage and its other
circumstances. This is so well known that we would waste our time by
presenting examples. Therefore the proof attached here has no force at all.

2. That Christ is not speaking here of the predictions of the Prophets is
clear from the following. (1) By *the Law or the Prophets* in verse 19 he means
nothing other than *the law itself* in verse 18, *the commandments themselves*
in verse 19, and that with which human righteousness is concerned and
Pharisaic righteousness was concerned in verse 20. (2) Throughout this entire
discourse Christ says absolutely nothing about predictions, but only about
commandments. He offers no example of a prediction, but brings forward many
examples of laws. (3) Who at that time, I ask, entertained the suspicion that
Christ wished to destroy the predictions of the Prophets? Or how could such a
suspicion have arisen? (4) How could anyone *break* those predictions and
teach others to do so, as Christ says in verse 19? (5) How can predictions be
called commandments? (6) How can anyone *do* or *teach* predictions? Let us
see what the opponents add to confirm this claim.

*Instance 1.* But Christ came to fulfill the predictions of the Prophets, and
he says this openly here in verse 17.

*Answer.* We indeed grant that Christ came to fulfill the predictions,

[Printed p. 100 | IA PDF 108 | IA leaf 108 | IA decausadei02brow]

and that this is abundantly proved in other passages of Scripture. But for the
reasons already given, it does not follow from this that predictions are meant
here. Moreover, *fulfill* here is the same as *not break*, as *do* and *teach*,
and as *exceed in righteousness* and *surpass the Pharisees themselves*. But
these expressions cannot suitably be applied to predictions at all.

*Instance 2.* Verse 18 proves this clearly, for by a jot and a stroke he means
the least prediction, which will not pass away but will certainly be
fulfilled.

*Answer.* *One jot or one stroke of the law* in verse 18 is nothing other than
*one of the least commandments* in verse 19.

*Instance 3.* The following sense is more suitable in verse 18: heaven and
earth will pass away before the predictions fail to take effect. It is more
suitable than the other sense: heaven and earth will pass away before the
moral law fails to be observed.

*Answer.* The context will supply us with the true interpretation of these
words. Above in verse 17 Christ said that he came not to abolish the law but to
fulfill it, both by practice and by teaching. He confirms this with that
weighty affirmation: *For*—he says—*truly I tell you*, and so forth. That is,
heaven and earth will pass away before I break or abolish it by either my
practice or my teaching. The reason which follows in verse 19 confirms and
explains this: *Whoever therefore*—he says—*breaks one of the least of these
commandments and so teaches*, and so forth.

*Instance 4.* In Luke 16:16–17 we have the same words as those found here in
verse 18, and there Christ speaks of prophecies, as is clear from the parallel
passage in Matthew 11:13.

*Answer.* We grant that the words of Luke 16:17 do not differ from the words
Matthew uses in chapter 5:18. But Luke 16:17 and Matthew 11:13 do not appear to
be parallel, for they speak neither about the same subject nor about things
done at the same time. Luke 7:24 and Matthew 11:13, however, are parallel, for
both speak of John the Baptist, of whom no mention is made in Luke 17:17.
Next, even if Luke 16:16 were speaking of the prophecies of the prophets—and
yet the text only reads, *The Law and the Prophets until John*, namely,
*existed*, not *prophesied*—it still does not follow that the words of Matthew
5:18 must be interpreted in the same way as the words of Luke 16:17. For the
same words, used in distinct subject matters, can be explained in different
ways and denote different things.

*Objection 2.* Not only the moral laws are meant here, but also the ceremonial
and judicial laws and everything contained in the book of Moses, for Christ
also fulfilled them in their own way.

*Answer.* It cannot be denied that Christ came to fulfill the ceremonial law,
but there is no basis at all for saying that he speaks of this law here.
Indeed, the contrary is clear from what was said before. Christ says in verse
17 that he came not to abolish the law but to fulfill it, where he means the
same law, not first one law and then another, for the nature of the contrast
proves that Christ fulfilled the very same law which he did not abolish. This
was not the ceremonial law, however, because he entirely abolished that law
and came to abolish it. 2. Further, we know that Paul and the other disciples
of Christ taught the abrogation of the ceremonial law and abolished this law
(Galatians 2:18). What then? Shall we say for this reason that they were least
in the kingdom of heaven? 3. Did Christ not know that this ceremonial law was
to be abrogated by his death and that the judicial law was to perish with that
commonwealth? Who then will affirm that Christ intended to denounce so great a
punishment upon those who broke one of the least commandments of that law and

[Printed p. 101 | IA PDF 109 | IA leaf 109 | IA decausadei02brow]

taught others to do so, and conversely to exhort people to observe it both in
teaching and in practice by adding so great a promise, while adding no time
limit? 4. Since, therefore, it is certain that the moral law is meant in verse
19, what reason will prove that the same law is not meant in verse 18? Is not
the connection of verse 19 with verse 18 manifest? 5. Indeed, does verse 19
not contain a particular application, by way of inference, of the preceding
teaching? 6. In Christ's explanation and defense of the law of which he
speaks, it is evident that he speaks of the moral law. He presents various
examples of this law, and no examples of either the ceremonial or judicial
law, except when he shows that they have been abrogated and abolished, as in
verse 31, or when he uses them to illustrate and explain moral laws, as in
verses 23–25. It is therefore clear that he speaks only of the moral law, which
alone he explains, confirms, and defends from the glosses of the Pharisees. 7.
Indeed, Primerose, part 2, chapter 9, section 2, p. 174, admits that *the Law
and the Prophets* means only moral precepts wherever a moral matter is under
discussion, as in Matthew 7:12: *Therefore, whatever you wish people to do to
you, do the same to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets.* Matthew 22:40
may be added to this. But it is plain that a moral matter is under discussion
here. This is sufficient on this argument.
