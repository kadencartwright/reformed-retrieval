The Bohemian Confession, article 8; *Corpus Confessionum*, part 2, page 187:
“They further teach that the manifestly ungodly, impenitent, and obstinate—that
is, those who do not listen to the church’s admonitions—are to be restrained by
her censure and penalty, which by received usage is called *anathema*, or
excommunication. This is to be done without respect of persons to all whose
ungodliness is known, who are addicted to grosser sins, and who, though often
admonished, still persist in their sins. These conspicuous and manifest people
who refuse to repent, whatever their rank or condition, ecclesiastical or
secular, are to be excluded from the fellowship of the church—not by human
force, but by the word and interdict of the Lord, who commands it to be done in
this way. Matthew 18: ‘If your brother sins against you,’ and so forth. Paul
likewise says, ‘We command you, brothers, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ,
to withdraw yourselves from every brother,’ and so forth; and elsewhere,
‘Drive out that evil person from among yourselves.’” This ecclesiastical
censure, namely excommunication, is therefore given by divine right, whatever
Velthuysen may say.

for this ministry are to be called from the godly and faithful people. They
must, moreover, possess an honorable manner of life, and first be examined;
only then, with prayer and the laying on of hands, are they to be confirmed in
this office by the eld-

ecclesiastical discipline is therefore granted.

Article 14: “Here they teach that it is to be believed that the keys were
delivered by Christ to the church. Speaking to Peter on behalf of all, Christ
says concerning them, ‘I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven.’
These keys are an administration of Christ delivered to the church and her
ministers. It has continued in her to the present day and will not come to an
end before the consummation of the world. According to Christ’s mandate and
office, as Holy Scripture relates, the task and authority of this

### Assertion I

Christ, insofar as he is mediator, is king and possesses a kingdom in which he
reigns and which he governs. I believe that no Christian will deny this
assertion. Nevertheless, we will confirm it by the testimony of Scripture,
both the Old and the New Testaments.

I. In Psalm 2:6 the Father says, “I, anointing my king, appointed him over
Zion, the mountain of my holiness.” These words are to be understood of Christ
insofar as he is mediator, for it is insofar as he is mediator that he was
placed upon that throne by the Father. Hence Christ the mediator acknowledges
that the Father is greater than he in John 14:28. He is also called the
Father’s servant in Isaiah 42:1. Insofar as he is mediator, he is called the
Father’s king: “anointing my king.” Therefore, this is the case.

Moreover, in the same respect he is to be called the king of Zion as that in
which the Father says to him in verse 8, “Ask of me, and I will give the
nations for your possession and the ends of the earth for the right of your
possession.” But these words are to be understood of Christ as mediator. All
the circumstances of the text therefore confirm that this is to be understood
of Christ as mediator, namely that he is called king and appointed over Zion.

II. In Psalm 45 Christ is repeatedly called king, also as mediator, as in
verses 2, 12, and 16. That the psalmist speaks of Christ as mediator is
abundantly confirmed by the context. As mediator he had grace poured upon his
lips in verse 3 and was blessed forever in the same verse. As mediator he was
anointed and has companions: he was anoin-

[Volume II; printed p. 48; ONB canvas 56]

ted “with the oil of gladness above his companions” in verse 8 and has a bride
at his right hand in verse 10. The whole text confirms this so clearly that
anyone who does not see it must be blind.

III. Jeremiah 23:5, 6: “Behold, the days are coming, says Jehovah, in which I
will raise up for David a righteous branch who will reign as king, prosper,
and execute justice and righteousness in the earth. In his days Judah will be
saved and Israel will dwell securely, and this is his name by which he will be
called: O Jehovah our righteousness.” That these words are to be understood of
Christ insofar as he is mediator is certain, and will be beyond controversy
among all who weigh the text. For he is called king here in the same respect
in which he is called David’s branch, or one descended from David; see Isaiah
11:1; Revelation 5:5; 22:16. He was not called this insofar as he is God, for
in that respect he was David’s Lord; Psalm 110:1; Matthew 22:44. Next, he is
called a king to be raised up in the future and is called a branch. Yet he was
a branch insofar as he was God’s servant; Zechariah 3:8. He was a king promised
to Judah and Israel. Finally, he is called king here insofar as he is our
righteousness. He is therefore so called insofar as he is mediator; see Daniel
9:24; Isaiah 53:11; 1 Corinthians 1:30, 31.

IV. Zechariah 14:9: “Jehovah will be king over the earth.” These words contain
a prophecy concerning Christ and his spiritual kingdom. See also Hosea 2:5.

This is likewise clear from the New Testament. First, in John 1:49 Nathanael
says, “You are the Son of God; you are the king of Israel.” This testimony is
approved by Christ as a most certain indication of faith.

II. In John 18:37 Christ was asked whether he was a king. He answered, “You say
that I am a king. For this I was born, and for this I came into the world, that
I might bear testimony to the truth.” It is therefore most true that Christ is
king, since he confessed it before Pontius Pilate when the question was a
matter requiring confession. Our Lord Jesus was punished with death because
he was called a king, and thus he confirmed this truth by his death.

[Volume II; printed p. 49; ONB canvas 57]

“This truth”—says our Gillespie in the epistle to the reader prefixed to the
vernacular book entitled *Aaron’s Rod Blossoming*—“namely, that Christ Jesus is
king, possesses a kingdom distinct from others, and has in his church a
government distinct from civil government, possesses above all other truths
this prerogative and distinguishing mark: Christ suffered death for it and
confirmed it by his blood. For from the history of the passion it is to be
observed that this was the sole article of accusation which he confessed and
acknowledged; Luke 23:3; John 18:33, 36, 37. This was the charge which the Jews
especially aggravated, prosecuted, and pressed; Luke 23:2; John 19:12, 15. It
was the charge that especially moved Pilate to pronounce the sentence of
condemnation; John 19:12, 13. Finally, it was recalled in that inscription
fastened to the cross; John 19:19. Although with respect to God and the
satisfaction of divine justice for our sins his death was a *lytron*, the
price of redemption, nevertheless with respect to the men who persecuted,
accused, and condemned him, it was a *martyrion*, a testimony for sealing the
truth.” Thus far Gillespie.

III. Acts 2:36: “Therefore, let all the house of Israel know with certainty
that God made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ.” “He calls
him Lord,” Gualter says on the passage, “because he is our redeemer. For who
does not know that redeemers possess a right of dominion over those whom they
have redeemed with their money or at the peril of their own lives? For this
reason he says through the prophet, ‘I am the Lord; that is my name, and I will
not give my glory to another’; Isaiah 42. We have indeed spoken a little
earlier about the office of king, to which the name of redeemer or lord also
belongs. Certainly, Christ enacts laws after the manner of a king and governs
the citizens of his kingdom according to them.” Calvin comments upon the
passage as follows: “He joins the title Lord with the name Christ because it
was commonly familiar among the Jews that the redeemer was to be anointed on
this condition: that he would be the head of the church and

[Volume II; printed p. 50; ONB canvas 58]

be given the power of governing her and supreme control over all her affairs.”
Christ, insofar as he is our mediator, therefore possesses supreme and royal
power and authority. It is in this respect that he himself says that “all power
in heaven and on earth has been given to him”; Matthew 28:18. Thus he is said
to sit at the Father’s right hand in Ephesians 1:20 and to possess a name above
every name in Philippians 2:9.

Since Christ is king, he must possess a kingdom, for these are correlatives. It
is also evident that this kingdom, as well as its throne, is frequently
mentioned. See Luke 1:32, 33; Matthew 13:24, 47; 20:1; 22:2; 16:19; Isaiah
9:7; Daniel 7:14; Psalm 45:6; Hebrews 1:8; Psalm 89:29, 36; Acts 2:30. There is
no need, however, to dwell upon a matter so clear.

### Assertion II

The kingdom proper to Christ as mediator is distinct from the ecumenical or
universal kingdom which he rules and governs insofar as he is God and equal to
the Father in power and glory.

Although this is sufficiently evident from what has been said, we will explain
it briefly.

1. This mediatorial kingdom is an acquired kingdom, for the Father places
Christ in this kingdom, and does so in time; Psalm 2:6. He freely became and
was made mediator; likewise, he freely received this kingdom, and it was given
to him. The kingdom he possesses in common with the Father and the Holy Spirit
is not of this kind.

2. This kingdom is promised for the good of the church in Jeremiah 33:5, 6.
The dominion he possesses in common with the Father is not said to be promised.

3. For administering this kingdom, he possesses gifts and qualifications
promised and granted by the Father. For this purpose he is said to have been
anointed and to possess grace poured upon his lips; Psalm 45. Thus Isaiah 11:2:
“The Spi-

[Volume II; printed p. 51; ONB canvas 59]

rit of Jehovah will rest upon him, the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the
Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of Jehovah.”

4. He was made king of this kingdom; Acts 2:36. Insofar as he is God and
coequal with the Father, however, although begotten from eternity, he is not
said to have been made. The kingdom of which he was made king therefore
differs from that which he possesses as the second person in the Holy Trinity.

5. He also possesses this mediatorial kingdom insofar as he is God’s servant.
Hence it follows that this kingdom is delegated. In Holy Scripture Christ is
frequently called God’s servant; Isaiah 42:1; 49:6; 52:19; Matthew 12:18. The
kingdom he possesses as the second person of the Trinity, however, is not
delegated but essential, flowing necessarily from the divine nature
communicated to him by eternal generation. Nor is he as such called God’s
servant.

6. Insofar as he is king of this kingdom, he is not equal to the Father, but
lesser and inferior, as he himself acknowledges in John 14:28. With respect to
the other kingdom, however, the Father and he are one.

7. This mediatorial kingdom is administered in a very different manner from
God’s kingdom: namely by evangelical institutions and ordinances, and by
weapons that are not carnal but spiritual; 2 Corinthians 10:4.

8. This mediatorial kingdom is proper to Christ alone and is peculiarly
attributed to him, and he governs and rules it in a peculiar manner. That other
kingdom, however, is not peculiar to him but is common to him with the Father
and the Holy Spirit.

9. This mediatorial kingdom is not to be administered forever. According to
Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:24, 25, Christ, once death itself has at last been
overcome, will deliver this mediatorial or economic scepter to his Father. The
other kingdom, however, will never be laid aside.

Finally, this mediatorial kingdom is limited and re-

[Volume II; printed p. 52; ONB canvas 60]

stricted. Hence Christ is said to rule over his house in Hebrews 3:6; the
house of Jacob in Luke 1:32, 33; to sit on David’s throne in Luke 1:32 and
Isaiah 9:7; to rule over those who possess Jerusalem in Isaiah 22:22; to sit on
Mount Zion in Psalm 2:6; and to govern the people of Israel in Matthew 2:6.
Thus he is called the head of the church in Colossians 1:18 and Ephesians
1:22, 23; 4:15, 16; the king of Israel in John 1:46; and the savior of Israel
and Judah in Jeremiah 33:6. See also Isaiah 9:6; Zechariah 9:9; Matthew 2:2. It
is therefore distinct from the kingdom which embraces all things, even the
devils themselves.

The celebrated Apollonius expresses this difference as follows in *The
Supreme Right concerning Sacred Things*, part 1, pages 33, 34: “1. Christ’s
mediatorial kingdom, considered separately according to the economy, is not
absolutely highest or supreme, but is subordinated to and dependent upon God.
In it the king and mediator is lesser and inferior to God, unequal to him and
his servant; 1 Corinthians 11:3; Isaiah 42:1; John 14:28; 1 Corinthians 3, the
last verse. These things cannot be said of the natural, universal, divine
kingdom. 2. The economic kingdom is a temporal and contingent adjunct of the
Son of God arising from God’s free decree, just as is the mediation upon which
it rests. But God’s natural dominion is a divine attribute necessarily
following from the supreme majesty and excellence of the divine nature. Hence,
as for the force, power, and majesty of this kingdom, it was proper to God from
eternity; as for its act and exercise, from the creation of the world. But
before the fall and the promulgation of the office of mediator, the Son of God
did not actually possess the economic kingdom and dominion. 3. The mediator’s
right of dominion rests upon the merit of the satisfaction which the mediator
discharged in the church’s place. But God possesses the right of natural and
universal dominion from his divine nature, and it rests upon the preeminence or
excellence of deity. Hence, the act and effect of mediatorial dominion is the
acquisition, government, and defense of the church; Psalm 110. But the act and
effect of the universal kingdom is providence, government, and the ordering of
all the affairs of the whole world. 4. God’s universal kingdom does not remove
sin or heal man’s rebellion

[Volume II; printed p. 53; ONB canvas 61]

against the law and commandments of God laid upon him, but disposes, orders,
and governs it by providence. The mediator’s economic
kingdom, however, is saving and effectual for the salvation of those in and
for whom it is exercised, and the mediator sustains it; Jeremiah 23:5, 6. For
the mediatorial kingdom is considered either insofar as it is economic and
domestic, in which respect theologians analogically call Christ a natural
head—that is, he bears the likeness of a natural head—and the church a natural
body, because he infuses the church with the Spirit’s vital power and she is
spiritually nourished and governed by Christ as her natural head; or it is
considered insofar as it is a kingdom and political dominion. In this respect
Christ is also analogically a political head, or bears the likeness of a
political head, insofar as he outwardly rules the church and through the
church’s government—that is, ecclesiastical polity and discipline—effectually
leads the church to salvation.” This distinction is also approved by
Maccovius, *Commonplaces*, pages 628 and 633; Trelcatius Junior, *Institutes of
Theology*, book 2, “On the Office of Christ,” page 96 in my copy; Essenius,
*System of Theology*, title 12, thesis 105—see also the title “On God,” thesis
166—and Hoornbeeck, *Practical Theology*, book 5, chapter 10, page 532.

The Erastians, who maintain that all ecclesiastical power flows from the
supreme magistrate and is subordinated to him, and that the magistrate as such
is Christ’s vicar in the church, receive this distinction between the kingdoms
with jeers. They understand well enough that if this distinction stands, the
case they defend with such impotence is finished. Yet although they reject
this distinction, all who contend for Christ’s deity against the Socinians
know how useful and necessary it is.

But let us see which passages of Scripture they object against us. There are
not a few: Hebrews 1:2; John 3:35; Psalm 2:8; 1 Corinthians 11:3; 15:28;
Hebrews 2:8; Psalm 8:6, 7; Philippians 2:8–10; Matthew 28:18; Ephesians 1:22;
Colossians 1:15,*

[Volume II; printed p. 54; ONB canvas 62]

*16, 17; 2:10; 1 Timothy 6:15. It will be easy to answer them all once the
following distinction between Christ’s dominion or power and his kingdom has
been set forth and explained. His power and dominion extend to many things
that do not properly belong to his kingdom, and he possesses dominion over
many who are not properly to be called subjects. Three things, therefore,
must be considered here. First, Christ’s subjects, or the members of the
kingdom properly so called, to whom Christ is properly king and head. Second,
Christ’s enemies, or the church’s foes, whom he will break with a rod of iron
in Psalm 2:9, possess as his footstool in Psalm 110:1, and finally overcome;
Revelation 17:14; 1 Corinthians 15:25, 26. Third, all other things, whether
animate or inanimate, on earth, in the air, or under the earth, that are or
will in any way be necessary for advancing the good of the church and
displaying Christ’s glory. Now, although Christ possesses dominion over all
these things, even the devils, and has them subjected to himself according to
the last verse of 1 Peter 3, he is nevertheless not properly to be called their
king, nor do all these things properly belong to this economic kingdom. With
this consideration set forth, it will be easy to answer the passages objected.

For Christ is called the heir of all things in Hebrews 1:2, namely in order to
advance the church’s good. He possesses and holds within his power everything
that can in any way pertain to advancing and preserving the happiness of the
faithful, both in the present age and in the age to come. Alternatively, this
passage may be understood of Christ insofar as he is God, an interpretation I
see does not displease Aretius in his comments upon it. Thus all things were
given to Christ in John 3:35 so that the salvation of his members might be
certain and indubitable, just as for this end he possessed the Spirit, given to
him by the Father; Isaiah 61:1–3. The psalmist in Psalm 2:8 indeed speaks about
this kingdom, or the church, which in its own time is to extend to the very
ends of the earth and the most remote islands. Hence it is wrongly*

[Volume II; printed p. 55; ONB canvas 63]

*concluded that nations not yet converted to Christ are to be regarded as
Christ’s subjects. This promise clearly implies that the nations had not yet
been given to Christ as his possession or inheritance, but were to be given
afterward, and therefore were not yet members of this kingdom of Christ.

Christ is called the head of every man in 1 Corinthians 11:3—that is, every
believer who is a member of the church. For, as the celebrated Dickson says on
the passage, “The apostle speaks about the order divinely established in the
church’s mystical body.” See also Pareus and Aretius on the passage. Nor ought
it to seem absurd to anyone that the universal term is taken restrictively
here, since this occurs frequently in Holy Scripture, as is familiar from our
disputations with the Arminians concerning Christ’s redemption. See Genesis
16:12; Daniel 3:10; 1 Corinthians 7:2; Colossians 1:28; 1 Corinthians 12:7;
Luke 16:16; Jeremiah 30:6; Hebrews 2:9.

All things are said to be subjected beneath Christ’s feet in 1 Corinthians
15:27; Hebrews 2:8; Psalm 8:6, 7. This does not mean that Christ exercises his
royal office over absolutely every person as a subject. Otherwise he would
likewise have as subjects properly so called inanimate brutes, flocks and
herds, wild beasts, the birds of heaven, and the fish of the sea. Rather, it
means that he possesses all things in his power for the good of the church.
See Calvin on 1 Corinthians 15:27, 28. It also means that with respect to glory
and honor he excels all things and will finally have all his adversaries
subjected to himself. For Calvin says on Hebrews 2:8: “After he has declared
that Christ rules all creatures without exception, he adds an objection to
the contrary: ‘But not yet do all things obey Christ’s rule.’ To answer this,
he teaches that what immediately follows concerning glory and honor is already
seen complete in Christ, as if he had said, ‘Even if that universal subjection
is not yet evident to us, let us be content that, having undergone death, he
was raised to the highest degree of honor. For what is still lacking will
finally be completed in its own time.’” The apostle speaks of this same power,
glory, and dignity*

[Volume II; printed p. 56; ONB canvas 64]

*in Philippians 2:8–10, not of royal authority; otherwise he would be called
the king of the angels beneath the earth. Calvin rightly says on the passage:
“Name is here taken for dignity, which usage is familiar enough in all
languages. It therefore signifies that the highest power was given to Christ
and that he was placed in the supreme degree of honor, so that no equal dignity
is found either in heaven or on earth.” See also Aretius on the passage.

As for Matthew 28:18, it is clear that supreme ecclesiastical power is meant
there, by which he can institute in the church whatever he wills and send whom
he wills to whatever people he wills: “Go therefore,” he says, “and teach all
nations.” Therefore the power and authority required for gathering and
governing the church is meant here, as well as that with which he is equipped
to remove every impediment and defeat every enemy. See the commentators
already mentioned, to whom add Gualter on the passage.

Nor does Ephesians 1:22 favor our adversaries. The words may be taken according
to the Syriac version: “He who was appointed over all things is the head of the
church.” Or, as some prefer, “over all things” can be taken adverbially, so
that the sense is that he is appointed the church’s head in a singular and
eminent manner. See the distinguished Bodius on the passage. Or it must be said
that he was exalted over all things and possesses dominion over everything in
order to procure and advance the church’s good. In this respect, verses 20 and
21 also say that he was placed at God’s right hand, far above every
principality, power, might, dominion, and every name that is named, not only in
this age but also in the age to come. Verse 22 says that he possesses all
things subjected beneath his feet. Yet all these powers and dominions do not
properly belong to his mediatorial kingdom. With respect to them Christ is not
to be called king and head. See Aretius on the passage. Moreover, “over all
things” can designate the members of the church, whether they are officers or
others, as Aretius ob-

[Volume II; printed p. 57; ONB canvas 65 (upper portion)]

serves: “The phrase added here, ‘over all things,’ must also be considered
carefully, for it signifies that no part within the church is without Christ’s
operation, so that both ministers and hearers are governed by him.”

Finally, however these words are taken, it is sufficiently clear that Christ
rules the church in a different way from all other things. He is called the
head of the church, and the church is called Christ’s body and fullness. He
does not rule all other things as their head. For who will say that he is the
head of brute beasts and heathens no less than of Christians? Therefore, only
those whose head he is properly belong to his mediatorial kingdom.

As for Colossians 1, everyone who does not favor Socinus knows that in verses
15–17 the apostle speaks of Christ insofar as he is coequal with the Father,
and in verse 18 of his special dignity insofar as he is the head of the church.
See Calvin and Beza on the passage. In this same passage, therefore, the
apostle distinguishes Christ’s mediatorial kingdom from his universal kingdom.
Some think that in chapter 2:10 the apostle speaks of Christ insofar as he is
God, because verse 9 says that “the fullness of deity dwells in Christ”—that
is, the whole of God, as Calvin speaks on the passage. But even if Christ is
taken here as the God-man considered economically, this passage will do
nothing for our adversaries, because these principalities and powers are good
angels, who constitute part of the church taken absolutely, as Trelcatius
testifies above on page 214. Consult Aretius on the passage. Finally, as for 1
Timothy 6:15, the whole context confirms that the apostle speaks of Christ as
God. See also Calvin and Aretius on the passage. Enough has now been said about
the second assertion.
