# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 20.* Galatians 4:18: *But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not
under law.* From this they conclude that believers are now freed even from
the law's direction.

*Answer.* What should be replied here is sufficiently clear from what was
said in response to Objections 17 and 18. Indeed, the circumstances and
purpose of the passage abundantly prove that the words *you are not under
law* must be taken in a far different sense from that in which the
Antinomians take them.

1. The apostle exhorts them to godliness and dissuades them from abusing
Christian liberty, for he did not want them to use it as an occasion given to
the flesh, according to verse 13. The apostle would therefore have
contradicted himself and rendered the exhortation he gave entirely
ineffectual if he had said they were not under the direction and obligation
of the moral law. For they could have replied, “Since we have been freed from
every obligation of the law, why may we not indulge our inclination? How can
we abuse this liberty of ours by providing an occasion for the flesh, when
there are now no works of the flesh forbidden to us by God's law?”

2. If the Antinomians' sense must be admitted, how could the apostle say in
verse 14 that *the whole law is fulfilled in one saying*? What do those who
are not under law have to do with this law? Why did he exhort them to mutual
love from that law under which they are not? What kind of motive, I ask,
would it be to tell those who are not under law that by performing such a
duty they would fulfill the whole law? Could this argument have been
sufficiently suitable if they had been entirely freed from obligation to
that law? To whom would the emptiness of such an argument not have been
obvious?

3. *To be led by the Spirit* is opposed to *not to be under law* here in verse
18. And in verses 16 and 17 *the Spirit* is opposed to *the desire of the
flesh*. It is therefore clear that *to be under law* and *to be under the
desire of the flesh*, or the dominion of sin, are the same, for the logic of
the context requires this. In verse 17 he had said that the flesh and the
Spirit are opposed to one another. From this he infers, to strengthen them
against the assaults of sinful desire, that if they were led by the Spirit
they would not be under law—that is, that the flesh would not then exercise
dominion. For this is similar to the argument he has in verse 16: *But I say,
walk by the Spirit, and you will not fulfill what the flesh desires.* Piscator
wants the words ὃ μὴ τελέσητε to be read in this way, *you do not
fulfill*, or *you will not fulfill*. For, he says, *do not fulfill* in Greek is
μὴ τελεῖτε. Bullinger and Münster also translate it in this way, as is
confirmed by what follows in verse 17, where the consequence of this
connection is confirmed from the nature of opposites.

4. It cannot be denied that believers under the Old Testament were led by
the Spirit. But if they too were led by the Spirit, it follows that they too
were freed from obligation to the law, if that is the meaning of the words
*you are not under law*. Yet this conflicts with our opponents' hypothesis.
These words must therefore have another meaning.

5. The works of the

Spirit are the same things commanded by the moral law:
namely, love, joy, peace, the restraint of anger, and so forth. And the works
of the flesh enumerated in verses 19, 20, and 21 are the same things forbidden
by the moral law, as is evident. Therefore to be led by the Spirit and to be
under the law's direction are not opposites, but subordinate things which do
not conflict. For if the Spirit works in us the same things which the law
commands, he does not conflict with the law or dissolve its obligation, but
rather confirms it. Therefore, since *to be under law* and *to be led by the
Spirit* are opposed here, the clause *to be under law* denotes something far
different from being under its direction and obligation. Rather, *to be under
law* will denote the same as to be under the command and dominion of the
flesh, which desires against the Spirit and produces fruits opposed and
contrary to the fruits of the Spirit.

*Objection 21.* Galatians 5:23: *Against such there is no law.*

*Answer.* It is one thing *to have a law opposed to us*, and another *to be
under the direction and obligation of the law*. This phrase seems to denote
something far different. For the law, which commands good and righteous
things, cannot be said to oppose the regenerate, whose new nature agrees
perfectly with the law. The law is opposed only to the corruption which
yearns after evil and unrighteous things. It therefore follows that the law
does not oppose regenerate people who are such; it does not follow that its
direction and obligation do not apply. The regenerate may therefore properly
be said to be under the law's obligation and direction even though the law is
not opposed to them.

2. The Greek expression τῶν τοιούτων can be taken either as masculine
or as neuter with the same sense, and however it is taken, it does not favor
our opponents. For if it is taken as masculine, the commentators commonly
give us this sense: namely, that those who strive to produce these and
similar fruits of the Spirit are freed from the law's curse and condemnation.
For after enumerating certain works of the flesh, he had added this clause in
verse 21: *I forewarn you that those who do such things will not be heirs of
the kingdom of God.* Thus, after enumerating certain fruits of the Spirit, he
adds the clause *against such there is no law*—namely, no law which excludes
them from the kingdom of heaven. If it is taken as neuter, they explain it in
this way: namely, that these are the things which the law does not forbid but,
on the contrary, expressly commands. As Dr. Beza writes, the apostle adds this
to show that these weightier things are to be done rather than those external
prohibitions concerning foods and days which the false apostles nevertheless
urged as the sum and principal head of the divine law.

3. For my part, I do not see why *law* here cannot be taken in the same sense
as in verse 18 and Romans 6:14: namely, as denoting the reigning corruption of
the flesh. The meaning would be that this corruption or desire of the flesh
does not prevail against those who are so led by the Spirit that they produce
such fruits, nor does it exercise dominion over them. What follows confirms
this, for it contains the reason for this connection: namely, that those who
belong to Christ and are led by Christ's Spirit have crucified the flesh with
its affections and desires. The argument is as follows: Those who have
crucified the flesh with its affections and desires are not under the
dominion of the flesh's desire; that is, that nature does not have dominion
over them, and such a law does not prevail against them. But those who produce
the works of the Holy Spirit, the fruits of the Spirit, are such people.
Therefore, and so forth. Hence it is sufficiently evident that this passage
provides nothing for the Antinomians.
