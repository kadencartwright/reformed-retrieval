Second Question

Are the remnants of inherent sin entirely removed for the first time at
death?

Theologians hold three different opinions on this question.

The first is held by those who think the complete abolition of inherent sin
occurs not in death itself but some time afterward.

The second is held by those who think it is completed in certain people
before death, while they live here.

The third is held by those who maintain that it occurs neither before nor
after death, but at the very instant of death, together with the soul's
separation from the body. Sin is then eternally divorced at once from the
soul itself and the whole person, provided that person is a true believer.

Some pious and learned men among us hold the first view. They think this
complete eradication of sin occurs not in death itself—indeed, not before
the dead body is reduced to ashes. Their principal reason is that Lazarus
and certain others, though truly dead and subsequently restored to life,
must be thought not to have cast off sin completely. After the second life
granted them they remained subject to death and therefore also to sin.

We will not linger long in refuting this opinion. Yet permit us to touch
very briefly upon both the opinion itself and its proof.

[1679 printed p. 270 | IA leaf 278]

First, as to the opinion itself, I say two things: first, that it is strange,
and second, that it is false.

1. It is strange, both because it is, as I think, novel, and because one may
wonder why they place this abolition of sin in the very reduction of the
body to ashes—not before or after it. What peculiar property does this
reduction to ashes possess by which it abolishes sin? Is it because the
image of the old man, namely sin, remains unabolished as long as the form of
the dead body remains intact, and both are abolished together when the body
becomes ashes? Nonsense! The learned men who defend this opinion would not
say this. Although Lazarus's resurrection, of which they speak, and that of
certain others preceded this reduction to ashes, the resurrection of those
who returned to life at Christ's death or resurrection, Matthew 27:52–53,
perhaps followed long after it. This is certainly true if we accept the
view of those who identify them as Adam, Eve, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and
David, or of those who think they were people who had seen Christ in the
flesh, such as Zechariah, Simeon, and John the Baptist, as reported by Lucas
Brugensis. These had been reduced to ashes long before Christ's death. Yet
they all returned to life and lived among the living for some time. The
author of the *Questions to the Orthodox*, question 85, holds that they were
raised immortal and taken into heaven with Christ at his ascension;
Maldonatus agrees, as does Calvin. Nevertheless, among the Greek Fathers,
Theophylact and Euthymius believe that they afterward died, as do Augustine
among the Latins, epistle 99 and book 3 of *The Miracles of Scripture*,
chapter 13, and Aquinas among the Roman theologians, part 3, question 53,
article 3. This also agrees with their opinion that the righteous of the
Old Law were to be consummated at the final resurrection together with the
Apostles and the righteous of the New Law. Although Calvin favors the
former opinion more, he nevertheless admits that there is nothing absurd
in saying that, after discharging this office, they rested in their tombs
again.

If this is true, the argument would strike against its own proponents.
Those people returned to life long after the reduction to ashes in which
this view says the remnants of sin were destroyed, and they died again.
They were consequently not entirely free from sin, since they were not free
from its effect, namely death. Hence we may say that this opinion is not
only strange, but, second,

2. false. If sin inheres in a dead person not yet reduced to ashes, it must
inhere either in the body separated from the soul or in the soul already
separated from the body. But it inheres in neither.

It does not inhere in the body separated from the soul, for since the body
lacks all reason, sense, and life, it cannot possibly be a subject of sin.

Nor does it inhere in the soul separated from the body—we are speaking of
the souls of the righteous. Unless we dream with the Roman theologians of
some purgatory or intermediate state after this life, the judgment of our
theologians, and indeed of Holy Scripture itself, necessarily requires us
to grant that the souls of the righteous are admitted into heaven
immediately at death, certainly long before the body's reduction to ash-

[1679 printed p. 271 | IA leaf 279]

es. Nothing defiled by sin enters there. Therefore, before the body is
reduced to ashes, these souls are whole and entirely free from sin. Let
this suffice concerning the opinion itself.

As for the proof from the example of Lazarus raised from death, its claim
is that although he was dead, he was raised before being reduced to ashes
and had not cast off sin entirely, since he remained subject to death. This
could not have happened, it argues, if the image of God had been perfectly
consummated at his first death, for together with sin it would also have
abolished death, sin's wages, simultaneously and forever.

I answer:

1. It is wrong to take an extraordinary example and use it to form or
fashion a rule to be applied commonly to everyone. Lazarus's death was such
an extraordinary example, and we know little about the state of his soul
during those four days of death. It would be rash to affirm anything
certain about it, or to draw whatever it was into the common state of all
the other dead.

2. The argument does not follow. If the image of God in Lazarus was in some
respect not perfected at his death with regard to death, since he afterward
died a second time, it does not follow that it was not perfected in another
respect, namely with regard to sin, so that he could have been entirely
freed from sin and its indwelling even though he afterward died. I do not
say that Lazarus, once raised from death, was free from every sin throughout
the whole of his subsequent life. Holy Scripture says nothing about this,
and we must affirm nothing rashly here. I wish only to say that this
inference is weak: if the image of God in the dead is not perfected with
respect to the dominion of death, neither is it perfected with respect to
the inherence of sin. Whatever Lazarus's case may have been, it is certain
that all the righteous are freed from every remnant of sin immediately at
death—or at least, according to the patrons of this opinion, as soon as
they are reduced to ashes. Yet they are not then freed from the dominion of
death; this will occur only on the last day. Therefore, although “death
first entered the world through sin,” Romans 5:12, it does not depart at the
same time as sin. When the cause is removed, the effect is indeed removed,
but in its appointed time and order, as the all-wise God has ordained, so
that “death will be the last enemy destroyed,” 1 Corinthians 15:26.
