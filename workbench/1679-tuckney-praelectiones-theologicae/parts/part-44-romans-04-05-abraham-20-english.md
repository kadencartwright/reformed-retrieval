We are still considering whether Christ's righteousness is credited to us
for justification. Since our whole hope and salvation rests upon it as a
foundation, we have dwelt on it at length. The entire host of Socinians,
Remonstrants, and Romanists rises against it from various principles.
Bellarmine especially attacks it in book 2, chapters 7 through 15, *On
Justification*, presenting ten arguments in chapter 7.

1. The first is that it has no scriptural testimony. We dispatched it
especially from three passages of Scripture which plainly speak of Christ's
righteousness credited to us, as we explained more fully.

2, 3. His second and third arguments assert that “no necessity for such an
imputation can be given. It would have to be either (1) that after the
forgiveness of sin a man remains truly a sinner, his sin covered but not
destroyed; yet Scripture says that in justification sins are taken away,
destroyed, cleansed, made whiter than snow, purged, and cast into the depths
of the sea, so that though sought they cannot be found, while Christ's bride
is altogether beautiful and without spot; or (2) that our inherent
righteousness is not so perfect that it justifies simply and absolutely;
yet renewal in faith, hope, and love—which constitute the whole of inherent
righteousness—can be perfect here,” as he attempts to prove by many
arguments. Therefore, he concludes, imputed righteousness is unnecessary.

To all these I answer generally that the necessity of this imputation can
be considered in two ways: the necessity of faith, insofar as it must
necessarily be believed, and the necessity of the thing, insofar as this
imputation is absolutely necessary to our justification and salvation. We
assert both necessities of this imputation. For:

First, it must necessarily be believed by us, as is evident from our answer
to his first argument. Whatever God testifies in Holy Scripture must
necessarily be believed once it is revealed to us. “These things are
written that you may believe,” John 20:31. “Believe the prophets and you
shall prosper,” 2 Chronicles 20:20. We showed at length that those three
passages of Scripture clearly testify to this imputation of Christ's
righteousness for our justification. Therefore it is at least absolutely
necessary in this sense, namely as something to be believed.

Second, this imputation is no less necessary to our justification and
salvation as to the thing itself. Even if it were not necessary for the two
reasons Bellarmine lists and attempts to refute—which we will treat
shortly—his enumeration of causes is not sufficient, and its necessity can
be proved from others. For example:

1. From

[1679 printed p. 253 | IA leaf 261]

the fact that whoever is accounted righteous by another's righteousness
and satisfaction is necessarily justified through its imputation to him.
No other way can even be conceived: another's righteousness and
satisfaction, which ought to have been in and performed by him, are accepted
for him.

But we are justified by an alien righteousness, namely Christ's: “that I
may be found in him, not having my own righteousness which is from the law,
but that which is through faith in Christ,” Philippians 3:9. This must not
be understood, as the Romanists wish, of inherent righteousness produced by
the operation of Christ's Spirit, but of Christ's own righteousness,
according to the same Apostle in Romans 5:18: as “through one trespass,”
namely Adam's own, guilt came to all men unto condemnation, so “through one
righteous act,” namely Christ's own righteousness—or righteous deed, his
actual obedience—the free gift overflowed to all men unto justification of
life. Bernard beautifully says: “An alien righteousness is assigned to the
man who lacked his own”; Ambrose: “We hide beneath the precious purity of
Christ our firstborn brother, that we may bear testimony of righteousness
in God's sight”; and Augustine most clearly: “He was sin that we might be
righteousness—not ours but God's, not in ourselves but in him—just as he
was sin not his own but ours, not in himself but in us.” Another's
satisfaction cannot be credited to a guilty man unless the guilty man's
offense and guilt are credited to the man satisfying. The latter, righteous
in himself, is made guilty solely through another's offense credited to him;
likewise, the justified man, unrighteous in himself, is accounted righteous
solely through the imputation of another's satisfaction. The latter is
righteous in the same sense that his surety is unrighteous. Thus, if we
maintain that no one can in any way be righteous by another's righteousness,
we must assert that no one can gain benefit from another's satisfaction and
that no one can merit anything for another. As he rightly declares, “What
else is this but to demolish the foundation of all Christianity and at the
same time undermine the substance of all our consolation and salvation?”
Cardinal Contarini therefore says excellently: “I judge it entirely pious
and Christian to say that we ought to rely—as upon something stable which
certainly sustains us—upon Christ's righteousness given to us, and not upon
holiness and grace inhering in us,” and so forth. Neither Luther nor Calvin
could have stated anything clearer or more suitable to confirm our position
that an alien and external righteousness is required for our justification.
Since this external and alien righteousness cannot become or be called ours
except by imputation, this is the first ground of the necessity of the
imputation which Bellarmine denies.

2. The second is that our justification, if it does not consist entirely,
consists at least largely in the forgiveness of sins. Thus “to credit
righteousness” and “not to credit sin” in Romans 4:6 and 8, though distinct
and apparently contrary phrases, are nevertheless equivalent and say
precisely the same thing: the very rationale and nature of our justification
of which the Apostle speaks there consists in the imputation of
righteousness. This either is entirely the same as the non-imputation of sin
or at least always has it joined to itself. When God does not credit the
sins we possess, 2 Corinthians 5:19, he credits the righteousness we lack,
verse 21 and Romans 4:3, 6. Now,

[1679 printed p. 254 | IA leaf 262]

just as both phrases mention imputation alone in this matter, so the
forgiveness of sins, when obtained principally—as here—through our
Surety's satisfaction and merit, also occurs by imputation alone. If my sins
are forgiven because of Christ's satisfaction, that satisfaction comes to
me and in that sense becomes mine; but this can occur only by imputation. If
someone's debt is forgiven because his surety paid it, this happens because
the payment made by another is credited to him. On this point we already had
Bellarmine himself agreeing with us in book 2, chapter 7, *On
Justification*: “If they wished only to say that Christ's merits are
credited to us because they are given to us and we can offer them to God for
our sins, since Christ took upon himself the burden of satisfying for us and
reconciling us to God the Father, their position would be correct.” But this
is precisely our position and is therefore, by Bellarmine's own judgment,
entirely correct.

The necessity of this imputation for justification is therefore supreme,
because it is necessary for the forgiveness of sins: that our sins may be
entirely forgiven, that this forgiveness may continue, and that God may
freely accept our imperfect endeavors to glorify him.

I add only this: there is so great and necessary a connection between
Christ's satisfaction for the forgiveness of our sins and its imputation to
us that, although the Romanists and Remonstrants acknowledge the
satisfaction but deny its imputation to us, the Socinians consider the
matter more carefully. They know it has never been heard or could be
conceived that someone satisfied for another by paying or obtaining
forgiveness while such satisfaction performed by another was not credited
to him. Since they deny this imputation as much as the Remonstrants do, but
know very well that it cannot be separated from satisfaction, they dare
blasphemously to dismiss Christ's satisfaction together with its
imputation. See what this denial of imputation has finally come to!
