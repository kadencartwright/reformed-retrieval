# William Twisse, *Dissertation on Middle Knowledge* (1639)

## Book I — Part 51: Against Penottus, Book III, Chapter XVII, Concluding Arguments

[Book I, printed p. 94; BSB canvas 00120]

PENOTTUS.

“It is gathered evidently,” he says, “from the words of Thomas, 1, q. 14, art. 13, in these words:
‘Hence all things which are in time are present to God from eternity, not only for the reason that he
has the notions of things present before himself, but because his gaze is borne from eternity upon
all things as they are in their presence.’ Therefore,” he says, “according to Thomas the divine gaze
is borne upon the being of actual existence which a thing has in time in the nature of things.”

RESPONSE.

But, I say, you know perfectly well that things do not have actual existence in time from eternity.
Yet we are speaking about the divine gaze which was from eternity. The Dominicans indeed hold that
things have actual existence from eternity—not in time, to be sure, but in eternity—and they do this
in reliance upon these words of Aquinas. But you treat that eternal existence of things in eternity
as nothing. I, however, would say that the whole of time was before God from eternity in willed
being, and was thus an object of divine cognition; and that all things contained in time were in the
same way objects before God, so that God saw each of them as future for a definite time, as present
for a definite time, and as past for a definite time. In this way they were all present to God from
eternity, but only intentionally, not really; and not only in known being, but first in willed being
and afterward also in known being. Penottus attempts to prove his proposition from Scriptures freely
adduced: Genesis 1, “God saw the things which he had made.” That passage, however, concerns present
things only, not future things. Yet God does not go outside himself to contemplate the things he had
made, nor is he acted upon in knowing by the things themselves. By the same reason that, while the
time at which certain things are to be produced is approaching, he sees them as future, when that
time first arrives he sees them as present, and as soon as the time of their presence has ceased he
sees them as past. Genesis 18, “I will go down and see,” is equally foreign to the present purpose.
Such words are spoken in a human manner and concern not future but present things, as also when God
is said to search hearts. And who has ever heard the searching of a future heart attributed to God
from eternity? Yet this could suitably be done if the actual existence of future things were present
to God from eternity. I do not deny that it is present intentionally, namely in known being; but I
have never found that foreknowledge called a searching. Even when searching is attributed to God, it
is attributed only by a trope, so that the expression may be accommodated to our understanding,
since the knowledge by which God discerns the things inward to creatures is conformed to the image
of human searching. But after dispatching these matters quite superficially, Penottus promises that
the whole business will soon be settled by an evident argument.

PENOTTUS.

Either God at some time reaches by his gaze and the keenness of his intellect that being which a
creature has in itself, or he never does. If at some time, then always, for there is no change in God.
If never, then divine cognition is very imperfect and more imperfect than the cognition of a
creature. For a creature sees a thing in itself and experiences such a vision to be more perfect
than a cognition or vision of a thing in a mirror, picture, or exemplary image. The divine cognition
must be of this kind, or at least like it, if God does not see creatures in themselves. But so that
the force of this argument may be perceived better, let us formulate it in this way. Intuitive
knowledge which terminates at a thing as it is in itself is the most perfect knowledge of all.
Therefore, if it is possible, it ought not to be denied to God. But it is apparent in creatures that
knowledge of this kind is possible. Therefore, God beholds things in themselves, so that divine
cognition terminates at them as they are in themselves.

RESPONSE.

Certainly the vision of a thing in itself is more perfect than the vision of a thing in an image.
But take care not to think that the vision of things in God's essence is nothing other than the
vision of things in an image. The divine essence is the effective principle of things; an image is
not. And for that reason Thomas holds that even singular things are known by God. (In God, however,
this is to be seen, for ocular vision is not distinct from knowledge in God as it is in us.) “God,”
he says, “knows things other than himself through his essence, insofar as it is the likeness of
things or their active principle,” 1, q. 14, art. 11, in the body. The same author, art. 5, in the
body: “God sees himself in himself because he sees himself through his essence; but he sees things
other than himself not in themselves but in himself, insofar as his essence contains the likeness of
things other than himself.” Meanwhile, we by no means deny that the existence of all things was seen
by GOD from eternity, but as future; indeed, as existing outside their causes, namely in part in
willed being before God. Whatever things are, they have all of it from God's will. Therefore, thus
far they are also seen in themselves as existing, though not really but intentionally. For they
exist from eternity in no other way than intentionally. This knowledge in God is intuitive and far
more perfect than either our intuitive knowledge or an angel's. For angels cannot penetrate all
things by beholding them, but God has all things plainly before himself in willed being, and that
from eternity. Our intuition takes place by being acted upon by objects; divine intuition does not.
Grant that an angel's intuition is through concreated species; divine intuition is not. Without
doubt God beholds all things most perfectly, but absurdity results whenever his intuition is
accommodated to the little measure of our own. It is no less absurd to say that God beholds as
present in themselves things which are not present in themselves but only future. You will say:
when, then, will God behold the things themselves? Will he not do so before they actually exist? I
answer: divine knowledge of contingent things cannot be called intuition, accommodated to the mode
of our intuition, before the things actually exist in time. Yet when they exist in time, God does
not know them differently from the way he knew them from eternity, namely at home, before himself,
in willed being. But you will say: only when they first exist does he know that they actually are.
And who would expect him to know that they actually are before they are actual, unless someone wants
God, in knowing something, either to err or to invent? But you will say: therefore, when things come
to be, God knows something which he did not know before. I answer that this would certainly be so if
he received the cognition of things from outside. But since he knows all things in himself, including
the actual existences of all things, namely in willed being, it can hence be clear that, although the
things themselves outside God admit variation, God's knowledge is subject to no variation whatever.
For since whatever is willed by God is willed by him for a definite time, while God knows and beholds
for what time each thing has been willed, by that very fact he sees that each thing both will be
future for a

[Book I, printed p. 95; BSB canvas 00121]

time, and that to decree it is to decree both that it will be future for the preceding time, present
for the time for which it has been decreed, and past for the following time. Thus also one and the
same cognition contains all these things in an invariable manner, although the things subject to this
cognition admit variation. Therefore, to the form of the first argument I answer that God reaches
not only the being which a thing has in itself, but also the being which it will have or has had in
itself, although it is one and the same being. For from eternity God reached the real existence of
all creatures as future; in time he reaches it as present when first it is present; and afterward he
reaches it as past as soon as it has passed. That is, he knows it to be future as long as it is
future, knows it to be present as soon as it is present, and knows it to be past as soon as it has
passed. Will you, then, argue in this way: today God knows that the Roman Pontiff rules the Church of
God in an Antichristian manner; therefore from eternity he knew that the Church of God had been ruled
in an Antichristian manner? On the contrary, to know today that his rule is present is to have known
from eternity that his rule would be future; and to have known from eternity that his rule would be
future for this time is to know today that his rule is present. Likewise, to reach today the being
which a creature has in itself is to have reached from eternity the being which the creature was
going to have in itself; and after this being of the creature has passed, God will eternally reach
the being which the creature once had in itself. No change or shadow of turning in God results from
this.

PENOTTUS.

It had been said that this mode of vision cannot be posited in God, because such a vision depends
objectively upon the thing seen under the description of a moving object, and nothing is seen in
this way unless it moves the faculty through its proper species, which cannot be said of God in
comparison with creatures.

There is a valid objection against this reply, because an angel sees creatures intuitively, so that
the gaze of the angelic intellect truly and properly terminates at them; and yet, at least according
to the truer opinion of Thomas, to which all but a very few agree, an angel does not see through a
species received from things. Therefore, the divine intellect can do this much more through the
divine essence, which infinitely exceeds every perfection of any created species and in imitation
of which the angelic species itself is exemplified. The major proposition cannot be called into
doubt. For who can doubt that when the angel accompanied and addressed Tobit and seemed to eat and
drink with him, and when the angel took Habakkuk by a lock of his head and carried him to Babylon,
the angels saw them intuitively rather than only in a mirror, in an image, or in some other similar
way?

RESPONSE.

Intuitive cognition properly so called requires the object's actual presence, whether the beholder
beholds it through its species or through a concreated species, so that the object's presence
contributes to the intuition of it. But an object's actual presence contributes nothing to God's
cognition of it, although God's cognition, popularly accommodated to the image of our intuition, is
not called intuitive except on the presupposition that the object exists in the nature of things.
Nevertheless, it is most certain to the learned that God knows things in the same way through his
essence, whether they exist, do not yet exist, or are past—not insofar as his essence contains the
perfections of all species, for thus far he would behold only the species of things, but insofar as
he is willing and through his will is the effective principle of all singular things. Yet God does
not know them to be really present so long as they are future or after they have ceased to exist.
Meanwhile future and past things, no less than present things, are present to God in known being,
that is intentionally, not really.

PENOTTUS.

The second reason for proving the same point, and one which for me is demonstrative, is this: the
divine intellect extends as far in understanding as the divine will extends in willing and divine
omnipotence in causing. But the divine will extends not only to the being which a thing has in GOD,
but much more to the being which the thing has in itself outside its causes. Divine omnipotence in
causing likewise extends to the true being of the actual existence of things in themselves.
Therefore, the divine gaze too ought to extend to the same being of the actual existence of things
in themselves.

RESPONSE.

Let the reader see how diligently the Novarian acts here to impose upon himself, inferring identity
of meaning from identity of expressions even though the identity of expressions proceeds with no
agreement of sense. For he pretends that the extension of the divine will and omnipotence is the
same as the extension of knowledge, although everyone sees that foreknowledge, especially intuitive
foreknowledge, presupposes rather than makes the things to which it extends. The will and
omnipotence do not do so. By willing things which are by their nature merely possible, the will
constitutes them in willed being; it does not presuppose them. Likewise omnipotence constitutes the
things to which it extends in the being of actual existence; it by no means presupposes them. No one
should wonder if the will also causes the things willed, which previously did not exist, to exist
through omnipotence, though previously they did not exist. But it surpasses all reason for any
things to be seen to exist before they exist. We say that God sees all things in willed being before
they exist really. For it does not belong to vision to transfer a thing from potency to the act of
existence, but rather to presuppose that transfer. God sees the things he causes both before and
after their causation, but he sees them before himself and in willed being. Hence it no more follows
that God sees them as present than as future or past. It is commonly acknowledged that God embraces
future and past as well as present things by the knowledge of vision. If someone wishes God to
behold things as present when they are only future, why should he not proceed in his madness and add
that God sees things as past although they have not yet been present, and, after they have become
past, that God sees them not only as present but also as future, and that as eternally future?
Certainly both future and present things are outside their causes insofar as they have passed from
the condition of merely possible things into the condition of future things. To this extent not only
future but also past things are present to God, but only intentionally, not really. But, you say,
God sees things in themselves, and that from eternity, so that his knowledge terminates at their
actual existence. Yet as for the principle of seeing, God receives it only from his essence: insofar
as things are possible, from the essence considered simply, which eminently embraces the perfections
of all possibles; but insofar as they are future, he receives it

[Book I, printed p. 96; BSB canvas 00122]

from the essence as willing, decreeing, or determining itself to produce those things for a definite
time. Hence it follows that God sees all things in themselves, but only as future until the time
appointed for their production arrives; after it arrives, as present; and after the time of their
presence has passed, as past. Yet this will not be to know things in one way and another on the side
of the knower, but only on the side of the things known. The fiction that, on our opinion, God acts
blindly is exceedingly childish and more worthy of boys riding on a long reed than of Scholastics,
as though something would accrue to the divine perfection if only God had eyes. We do not doubt that
divine cognition terminates at the being of things. But before the determination of the divine will
it knows only possible being; before the execution of the divine will, only willed being; and after
the execution, it knows a thing actually to exist in itself. But how does it know it? Not in the
known thing itself, but in God himself as knower. For this statement of Aquinas will always hold:
“God sees himself in himself, because he sees himself through his essence. But he sees things other
than himself not in themselves but in himself, insofar as his essence contains the likeness of
things other than himself.”

[Marginalia: 1. p. q. 14. art. 5. in corp.]

Add, if you please, “or as their active principle,” from art. 11, in the body. Now at last let us
return from our digression to chapter 17 of the same third book.

PENOTTUS.

The first argument against the second proposition is that of Ferrariensis, book 1, *Against the
Gentiles*, chapter 66. If creatures are present to God only according to objective being and are not
coeternal with God according to their real existences, the reason by which Thomas proves that future
contingents are present to God from eternity would be trifling, since it proves the same by the same.

I answer that Thomas's reasoning is excellent, but its force was not perceived by the objector.
Thomas does not intend there to give only the reason why God knows future contingents, but the reason
why the certainty of divine cognition stands together with the contingency of things. The excellent
reason he assigns is this. He who sees the present being of a contingent thing's actual existence
can have certain knowledge of the contingent thing. From eternity God sees not only its future being
but also the present being of the contingent thing's actual existence. Therefore, he can have
certain knowledge of it while the contingency of the thing remains intact. He proves the minor from
the fact that God's knowledge is measured by eternity.

RESPONSE.

Certainly the discussion of Thomas at 1, q. 14, art. 13 tends in this direction, and elsewhere he
commonly teaches that God sees all things as they are in their presence. If that presence were only
objective, this opinion of Thomas would be plainly trifling. In the question just cited he also
explains the matter so that the presence of things according to actual existence precedes the divine
vision. “Just as,” he says, “one who goes along a road does not see those who come after him, but one
who beholds the whole road from a certain height sees all those passing along it at once.” I,
however, interpret this presence of things as their presence before God in willed being. For the
actual existence proper to all things was before God from eternity in willed being. Someone might
perhaps suspect that Thomas indulged his proposed comparison too far, illustrating the temporal
presence of successive things by a local succession which occurs at one and the same time, since it
is most certain that all parts of a place exist together, but the parts of time do not. Again, how
will you establish this consequence, “God's knowledge is measured by eternity; therefore from
eternity he saw the actual existence of all things,” except in the way it is established by the
Dominicans, namely by saying that all things existed in eternity from eternity?

PENOTTUS.

Secondly I say: grant that St. Thomas intended to prove from the presence of things that God knows
future contingents; he would nevertheless commit no begging of the question. To understand this, it
must be noted that an equivocation lies hidden in the term “presence.” This term can be taken as
signifying what is opposed to futurity or pastness, and it can be taken as signifying what is opposed
to absence. In his discussion Thomas uses the word “presence” not insofar as it is opposed to
absence, as the objector thinks Thomas uses it, but insofar as it is opposed to futurity. The
argument is this: one who sees not only the future being which a contingent thing has in its causes,
but also the present being which it has outside its causes, can have certain knowledge of that
contingent thing. God sees not only, and so forth. He proves the minor from the fact that God's
knowledge is measured by eternity. This is Thomas's discourse, in which there is no trifling and no
begging of the question.

RESPONSE.

This distinction of Penottus is of no value whatever. Although a thing's absence according to local
distance is distinguished from that thing's pastness and futurity, absence according to temporal
distance is by no means distinguished from pastness and futurity. Nor does Penottus wholly avoid
begging the question according to the form of argument which he devises here to attribute to
Aquinas; except that he wanders entirely beyond the bounds of the present undertaking. The
Novarian's former answer aimed to show that Aquinas proved from the presence of future things before
God not so much that God knows future contingents as that he knows them certainly, with the result
that every begging of the question is avoided. In this latter answer he goes further and undertakes
to show that Aquinas does not beg the question even if he concludes from the presence of things
before God only that future contingents were known to GOD from eternity. In putting this forward,
Penottus disgraces himself. For the outcome of his own argument amounts only to proving from the
presence of things before GOD not so much that knowledge of future contingents simply so called
belongs to God as that certain knowledge does. Whatever he may put forward, Penottus concludes
nothing other than that GOD can have certain knowledge of a contingent thing. But if the argument
is adapted to proving what Penottus undertakes to prove, namely that, although Thomas intended to
prove from the presence of things that God knows future contingents, he would nevertheless commit no
begging of the question, the argument Penottus supplies would at once disclose its own καχεξίαν—its
bad condition—and extreme incongruity. For it would proceed in this way: one who sees not only the
future being which a contingent thing has in its causes, but also its present being which it has
outside its causes, has knowledge of the contingent thing. God sees not only the future being which
the contingent thing has in its causes, but also its present being. Therefore, he has knowledge of
the contingent thing. In this

[Book I, printed p. 97; BSB canvas 00123]

argument, I say, what had to be proved in the conclusion is manifestly presupposed in the premises.
For it is presupposed in the premises that God sees the present being which a thing has outside its
causes. But this is, beyond controversy, to have knowledge of the thing, and specifically knowledge
in the genus of vision. It is equally beyond controversy that this is to have most certain knowledge
of the thing. Thus, wherever Penottus may turn, he can by no means escape begging the question.

PENOTTUS.

The second argument against the same conclusion is taken from the authority of Thomas, *Against the
Gentiles* 1.66, reason 6, where he teaches that all things which come to be in time coexist with
eternity in present being, and illustrates the point by the center and so forth. From the
coexistence with eternity of all things which come to be, he proves that all things are present to
God from eternity. Therefore, he is not speaking only about objective presence, and so forth.

I answer that, since Thomas's intention is established from so many passages, it is superfluous—to
say no more—to wish to bring him from the cited passage into support of the contrary opinion,
especially since he says nothing new there but persists altogether in the same account he had
handed down in 1, *Sentences*, and in the First Part.

II. Nevertheless, regarding that passage I say that, if Thomas's words are inspected, they carry
their solution with them. For he says that eternity coexists with all things which come to be in
each difference of time, just as an indivisible thing outside a continuum coexists with all the
parts of that continuum, and as the center coexists with all parts of the line. In this way it is
not necessary that things coexist with eternity before they exist in time. It is sufficient that
they coexist with eternity, and eternity with them, when they come to be successively in time.

RESPONSE.

Although Thomas's opinion has been established sufficiently, if anything from Aquinas should occur
to the contrary, it must either be tempered by a suitable interpretation or it must be admitted that
Thomas was ambiguous or changed his opinion. The passage in *Against the Gentiles* must be taken into
account especially because it is primary; and unless I am mistaken, Aquinas nowhere conducts
himself more precisely than when he argues in *Against the Gentiles*. As for your reply that
Thomas's words carry their own solution with them, I acknowledge that this is customary for you:
with a confident assertion you prepare the reader to obey your interpretation. You say that Aquinas
affirms that eternity coexists with all things which come to be in any difference of time, but that
it by no means follows that they coexist with eternity before they exist in time. But the argument
you proposed insisted that all things which come to be in time coexist with eternity in present
being, that is, that they coexist with eternity before they exist in time. Otherwise, it would have
proposed something no one does not know and drawn from it a consequence no one would concede. You
invert the proposition when you reduce it to this, as though Aquinas contended for nothing other
than that eternity coexists with all things which come to be in each difference of time. Yet if this
is understood of coexistence from eternity, the result will be the same, for from it there follows
that all things coexisted with eternity from eternity. And unless we interpret Aquinas in this
sense, what room will there be for the inference in Aquinas which you too acknowledge? “Whatever,
therefore, is done throughout the whole course of time, the divine intellect beholds in its eternity
as present.” It is therefore necessary for Aquinas to acknowledge such a presence of things
coexisting with eternity from eternity, from which he can conclude that God beholds from eternity
all things which come to be in every difference of time. For that a thing coexists with eternity as
present when it exists in time is as well known to every bleary-eyed man and barber as it was to
Aquinas; nor does it contain anything of weight by which an entrance may be opened to contemplating
future contingents from eternity. Nor can I sufficiently wonder at Penottus's phraseology when he
speaks of the coexistence of things “with eternity as present,” for this is not the phraseology of
Aquinas, even as Penottus himself reports it.

“Whatever exists in any part of time coexists with the eternal as present to it.” Aquinas does not
say, “Whatever exists in any part of time coexists with the eternal as itself present,” but “as
present.” Nevertheless, because whatever coexists with eternity must coexist with its whole—since
eternity has no parts—the Dominicans infer, and believe they do so according to Thomas, that it has
coexisted with eternity from eternity. I judge that this is in some respect true, and that outside
the things' causes, namely insofar as they coexist with God in willed being. This is in some respect
outside their causes, insofar as through the determination of the divine will they have passed from
the condition of possibles into that of futures. And this is for things existing in themselves to
be seen before God. For God sees them no differently when they exist in time: he sees them in
himself, not in themselves. Although the things themselves in themselves are the object of divine
intuitive knowledge, GOD sees those things in themselves no differently than in himself. For GOD is
not acted upon by objects according to his intuitive knowledge, as we are, but sees all things in
himself, whether future, present, or past, namely in willed being.

PENOTTUS.

Others take the third argument from the various authorities of divine Scripture and the holy
Fathers by which it seems to be indicated that future things already exist before God and that past
things have by no means passed away. These authorities were related above to prove the propositions
themselves. For they say that, just as one and the same instant of time corresponds to all places,
reaches all places however distant from one another, and reaches every duration of things however
diverse, so one and the same *now* of eternity can be present to many divisible durations. And just
as the indivisible soul corresponds to divisible parts of the body, so indivisible eternity can
correspond to divisible parts of time.

I answer that the authorities of the Fathers and Scripture must be expounded according to the sense
of the first and third propositions. The examples, however, commend the opposite. For just as an
instant of time does not reach places which do not exist in that instant, nor things which do not
exist in a place in that same instant, and just as the soul does not give life to parts of the body
which do not yet exist, so eternity does not coexist with parts of time or with things measurable by
time when those things do not yet exist in themselves.

RESPONSE.

But we have shown your first proposition to be vanquished. As for the third, namely the objective
presence of things before GOD from eternity, no one today, so far as I know, calls it into
controversy;

[Book I, printed p. 98; BSB canvas 00124]

the only question is whether Aquinas acknowledged another presence of things before God, as the
Novarian contends, but with an effort which in my judgment is altogether vain. I would nevertheless
deny that Aquinas acknowledged any real presence of things before God. For although he professes
that the divine intellect in its eternity beholds whatever is done throughout the whole course of
time, he immediately appends an exception in these words, as Penottus reports: “Yet what is done in
some part of time was not always existing.” Nevertheless, both according to the truth and according
to Aquinas, in my judgment, it can be proved that there is a presence of all things before God from
eternity in willed being. Hence God has beheld from eternity future as well as present things, and
past as well as future and present things. Nor can God otherwise be said to behold things in
themselves. But by entirely the same reasoning by which eternity does not coexist with parts of time
or with things measurable by time when they do not yet exist in themselves, it cannot be said that
God beholds things existing in themselves before they exist in themselves. For to behold a thing in
itself also connotes that the thing exists in itself and signifies that the thing so existing is
known intuitively by God. In this way, however, he beholds things in themselves in willed being
before they exist in themselves actually according to the measure of time. Nor does he behold things
in themselves differently either when they are present or after they become past. But I readily
recognize how little this philosophy suits Penottus's palate. The Dominicans will be able to digest
this doctrine much better than he can with his Jesuits, whose spirit the Novarian breathes
throughout, whatever he may put forward to the contrary. Thus it seems highly probable to me that
Penottus is merely play-acting. For his theology is everywhere disclosed by its own evidence to be
not so much Scholastic as Jesuitical.
