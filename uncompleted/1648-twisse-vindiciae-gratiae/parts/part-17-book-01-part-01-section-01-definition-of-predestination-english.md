THE VINDICATIONS
OF GOD'S GRACE, POWER,
AND PROVIDENCE,
BOOK ONE

PART ONE,

ON PREDESTINATION

SECTION ONE

PERKINS

Predestination is God's counsel concerning the final end of a human being, or his state beyond this temporal or animal life. For with respect to animal life we are equal, and this kind of life in God's counsel is only a preparation and step toward spiritual and heavenly life.

ARMINIUS

I now come to the book itself, which I shall examine a little more inquisitively and diligently. You will not take it badly if here and there, as though somewhat captious, I also bring certain more minute matters beneath the line. Why should it not be permissible to season a serious and solid conference about the truth, as it were, with a friendly and pleasant contest over more accurate treatment?

2. You begin with the definition of predestination, and rightly. But that definition does not seem suited to the predestination explained to us in Scripture. For the predestination treated in the Scriptures concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners; it is made in Christ; and it is directed toward blessings which concern not this animal life, to be sure, but the spiritual life—although a portion of them is communicated in this animal life, as is most evident from Ephesians 1. There, among the spiritual blessings to which we have been predestined in Christ, are numbered adoption as sons, verse 5; redemption through the blood of Christ and remission of sins, verse 7; and disclosure of the mystery of the divine will, verse 9. These good things are bestowed upon the predestined in this life. The Apostle speaks finely in Galatians 2 when he says that the life he now lives in the flesh he lives by faith in the Son of God, signifying that in this animal life he is a participant in spiritual gifts and lives a spiritual life from them.

3. But perhaps you did not intend to define accurately, but only to delineate for us some notion of predestination by a description. Though I grant that, there nevertheless appear to be many things in the description itself which must be noticed. For the word *counsel*, by which you intended to explain the genus of predestination, is not the genus of predestination but pertains to its efficient cause. A decree is established through counsel; that decree can suitably be established as the genus of predestination—if, at any rate, counsel by which he determines something can be attributed to God, as Scripture does in Acts 4:28 and Ephesians 1:11. What I say is evident from the passages cited. In the former, Acts 4, counsel is said to determine beforehand, or predestine, the things to be done; in the latter, Ephesians 1, God is said to effect all things, including the institution of predestination, according to the counsel of his will.

4. There is equality in this life between the godly and the ungodly with respect to external goods considered generally; among individuals, however, both the godly and the ungodly, there is a great difference. Indeed, it is so great that among those offended by that inequality it needs to be defended by an argument which reduces it to equality. Indeed, it is said of the godly and faithful that they are the most miserable of all human beings if in this life only they hope in Christ. 1 Corinthians 15:49.

RESPONSE

Far be it from us to take badly the diligence and care of any theologian applied to examining our doctrines. If they are gold or silver, surely the testing will be in no way harmful to us but profitable, since it will rub away any dross that clings to them and render the truth itself brighter and more splendid. But if they are hay or stubble, may God grant that it be reduced to ashes as soon as possible by the breath of his mouth. If anyone also wishes to be inquisitive in exploring minuter matters, whether piercing them through with a critical mark or chastising them with a stroke, let him see to it that he pleases himself; surely this ought by no means to displease us. For why should we not calmly endure others pointing out even the slightest blemishes in one another? By God's grace we shall indeed endure it. A friendly and pleasant contest over more accurate treatment has almost always been welcome even to the human Muses, whose doors were customarily called candid. Will it not be received in the same way by the divine and Christian Muses? God forbid.

2. The definition of predestination, you say, does not seem suited to the predestination explained to us in Scripture. What then? Does Scripture deny either that predestination is God's counsel or that it concerns a human being's final end? If it denies this, some passage in which it does so should have been indicated. But if the truth of this statement can stand with the truth of Scripture, although perhaps it may seem to you not to include fully everything Scripture teaches about predestination, and on that account can be said not to be sufficiently suitable, I do not understand why it should be called simply unsuitable. Yet it seems deficient to you on three grounds; let us therefore examine them one at a time. 1. The predestination treated in the Scriptures, you say, concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners. But where, finally, is even a trace of this found in Holy Writ? “Those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son”; “Those whom he predestined, he called.” Romans 8:29–30. “He predestined us to adoption.” Ephesians 1:5. “Predestined according to his purpose.” Ephesians 1:11. Nowhere in these passages is it either expressed or signified that predestination concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners. Again: “God did not appoint us to wrath, but to obtain salvation through Jesus Christ.” 1 Thessalonians 5:9. “From the beginning God chose you for salvation in sanctification of the Spirit and faith in the truth.” 2 Thessalonians 2:13. In these passages also it is neither expressed nor signified that predestination concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners. If this is gathered from the fact that those said to be predestined were sinners at the time the Apostle made those declarations: 1. It is in no way proved from this either that they were, or that they were considered as, sinners when predestination was made by God. 2. It is also true that God created us—us, I say, who are sinners—but it cannot be proved from this that we were considered by God as sinners when God created us. Then why did he not prefer to say that predestination concerns human beings insofar as they are faithful rather than insofar as they are sinners, since in both his public and private disputations he himself has defined predestination in this manner? “Predestination is the decree of God's good pleasure in Christ, by which he determined with himself from eternity to justify, adopt, and give eternal life to the faithful, whom he decreed to endow with faith,

[Liber I, Pars I; printed p. 36; BSB canvas 44]

to the praise of his glorious grace.” Add this also: those who establish that the object of predestination is the corrupt mass and, consequently, that none but sinners are predestined nevertheless do not think it necessary that sinners be mentioned in the definition of predestination. Evidence of this can be had from both our theologians and the Roman Catholics. Among the Roman Catholics, Aquinas speaks thus: “Predestination is a certain rationale, existing in the divine mind, for ordering some to eternal salvation,” question 23, article 2. Again in article 1, reply 3, he says: “To be predestined belongs to angels as it does to human beings, even though they were never miserable. For a movement does not take its species from the terminus from which, but from the terminus to which. As far as the nature of whitening is concerned, it makes no difference whether the one who is whitened was black, pale, or red. Similarly, as far as the nature of predestination is concerned, it makes no difference whether someone is predestined to eternal life from a state of misery or not.” Durandus likewise speaks thus in book 1, distinction 40, question 1: “The rationale, existing in God, for bestowing the helps by which someone attains eternal life is called predestination.” And Thomas Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 45: “Predestination is the eternal preparation by the divine will of final grace on the way and everlasting blessedness in the homeland for a rational creature.” Let us come to our own authors. After Kimedontius, *On Predestination*, chapter 2, had recounted certain definitions of predestination from Augustine—namely, that it is “the preparation of grace,” and likewise from the same author's *On Faith to Peter the Deacon*, chapter 35, that it is “the preparation of a gratuitous gift”—he has the following from his own opinion: “Predestination denotes God's eternal and firm purpose of bestowing grace and glory upon those whom he wills.” Tilenus also, *On Predestination*, number 14: “To predestine is nothing other than to order toward a definite end, whatever that end may finally be.” Much less would they think it necessary to establish that predestination concerns sinners insofar as they are sinners, or human beings insofar as they are sinners. For this would signify that God was moved to predestine human beings by consideration of sin. This is false not only generally, because no cause moving God to action is granted—indeed, according to Aquinas, no one was ever so insane as to assert it—but in the particular case it is foreign to all sound reasoning. It would follow that God was moved to predestine some to eternal life by consideration of sin, and that as though from condign or congruous merit. As for predestination to death, the indubitable sense of that limitation, “insofar as they are sinners,” is that God is moved by consideration of sin to predestine some to destruction, and that from congruity—and here in the highest degree, namely from condignity.

Furthermore, the phrase *insofar as they are sinners*, when it establishes the object of predestination, is ambiguous. It can be understood of sinners so denominated solely from the guilt of original sin. In this way very many of our authors are accustomed, with Augustine, to reason about the object of predestination, namely by establishing it as the corrupt mass and human beings bound by that common sin of origin. Or it can be understood of sinners so denominated not only from original sin, which is common to all, but from the individual sins proper to each, especially from the rejection of either legal or evangelical grace and from final perseverance in that rejection. In this way some of our authors form the object either of predestination or of reprobation, and in this sense Arminius forms for himself the object of reprobation. But where, I ask, does Holy Writ teach that predestination concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners in this sense? Indeed, even though sinners of this sort were the object of reprobation—which is indeed the opinion of Arminius, though notoriously false—with what effrontery could he assert that the predestination proposed in Holy Writ concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners in this sense? What then? According to Holy Writ, can only those whom God foresees will reject legal or evangelical grace and finally persevere in that obstinacy be called predestined? Here I appeal to the conscience of every Arminian. If someone, in order to rescue Arminius, should say that “insofar as they are sinners” in this passage signifies only sinners so denominated solely from original sin, see how it can be said with no reason whatsoever that this rescues the Doctor. For predestination is being treated generally, and it contains two species under itself: predestination to life and predestination to death, which is properly called reprobation. From this it will follow that if predestination generally concerns sinners insofar as they are so denominated solely from original sin, then every particular species of predestination—and specifically that which is to death and is called reprobation—also concerns human beings insofar as they are sinners so denominated solely from original sin. This will indeed cut the throat of Arminius's opinion, for he teaches that reprobation concerns sinners so denominated not solely from original sin but from rejection of grace and final persistence in their obstinate rejection of it. But if someone still intensely eager to rescue his teacher—as πολλοί μαθηταί κρείττονες διδασκάλων, “many pupils are better than their teachers,” though this rarely occurs among these men—should respond that “insofar as they are sinners,” just as it is established as the object of predestination generally, must also be understood generally, that is, as abstracted from both original and actual sin, but must be narrowed to its species whenever predestination to life or to death is treated particularly, he will not attain what he desires even in this way. For if reprobation, which is one species of predestination, narrows this formal object to the species of actual sin, it would follow that election, which is the other species, ought to narrow this common formal object to the other species of sin, namely original sin; and this cannot be admitted even according to the doctrines of Arminius. For although according to Arminius the formal object of reprobation is sinners insofar as they are sinners, the formal object of election according to him is actually not sinners insofar as they are sinners originally, or in Adam. Rather, sinners, whether actually or originally, insofar as they embrace evangelical grace through faith and persevere in it to the end are, according to Arminius, the formal and adequate object of election; just as sinners insofar as they reject grace and finally persevere in rejecting it are established by him as the formal and adequate object of reprobation. Yet this is almost always Arminius's habitual practice: to confuse everything with ambiguities so that he himself may hide more safely with his monstrous opinion. For this purpose he had seen that our theologians' disagreement in establishing the object of predestination was very serviceable to his arguments, some placing it in the intact mass and others in the corrupt mass. Yet this disagreement has thus far always existed not merely without loss of mutual communion and love but, in my judgment, without any contradiction and indeed with the greatest harmony, provided that each side understands the other rightly. I am confident that I shall demonstrate this at some point in these studies. Let us proceed to the remaining points.

2. The second ground of censure is that predestination is not said to have been made in Christ, although according to Holy Writ it ought to have been. Ephesians 1:4. But which of the Fathers, which of the Schoolmen, who ever before Arminius thought that this necessarily had to be expressed in the definition of predestination? I do not say this as though I were certain that no one except Arminius and his followers expressed it, but because I know of no one who asserts that it must necessarily be done—especially since Perkins is treating not predestination to life in particular but predestination in general. What then? Will predestination in general, which embraces under itself both election to life and reprobation to death, be said to have been made in Christ? Reprobation, therefore, was also made in Christ. But where did Arminius discover this mystery of Holy Writ? Not even in his own brain, I think. What is more, when he later corrects Perkins's definition of election, he proposes a definition of such a sort that from it only our justification, and not our election, appears to be made in Christ. The sense of the expression “in Christ” is disputed. Arminius wishes it to signify that Christ is the meritorious cause of election, which we deny. On this point, let the reader notice the following. 1. When Arminius proceeded to explain this, he offered as examples of the things Christ merited for us only grace and

[Liber I, Pars I; printed p. 37; BSB canvas 45]

glory, and we freely confess that Christ merited both for us; but we absolutely deny that he is the meritorious cause of election. His words in book 1, page 2, *On Election*, section 1, are these: “But that phrase, *in Christ*, denotes the meritorious cause by which grace and glory were prepared.” We readily grant this; but does it follow from this that Christ is the meritorious cause of election? 2. Even in the private disputations on the predestination of the faithful, in which he establishes Christ as the foundation of election in thesis 4, he explains it in such a way that from it Christ follows to be the cause only of the good things prepared by election, partly by efficacious communication and partly by his most worthy merit. These are his words: “We establish Christ as the foundation of this predestination.” His explanation of this follows: “And as the meritorious cause of those good things which are destined for the faithful by that decree. For the love by which God loves human beings precisely unto salvation, and according to which he precisely intends to give them eternal life, is only in Jesus Christ, the Son of his love.” Notice here that, although this is proposed as the reason for the preceding claim, according to Arminius nothing else follows from this love—which is indeed the love of election—than that Christ must therefore be established as the meritorious cause of the good things destined for the faithful by the decree of election. He does not say that he is the cause of the decree, but only of the good things destined for the faithful by the decree of election. Those things are grace and glory, as he himself explains in the following thesis, and grace of the sort which God graciously bestows upon us in this life, as he explains in the public disputation on predestination: specifically justification, adoption, the spirit of grace, and things of that kind. Finally, he wishes the same phrase, “in Christ,” to signify our existence in Christ through faith before election. Here notice a manifest contradiction. For if the phrase “in Christ” signifies that Christ is the meritorious cause, then it is equivalent to saying that God elected us on account of Christ; but if it signifies our existence in Christ through faith, it surely cannot be equivalent to “on account of Christ.” For one and the same particle cannot be employed in the same passage both properly and figuratively. If it is employed properly, then it is not employed figuratively; if figuratively, then not properly. But we have pursued these matters at greater length below and have treated copiously the question whether Christ is the meritorious cause of our election.

2. The third ground of censure is that, according to Holy Writ, predestination is directed toward blessings to be communicated in this life, such as adoption, redemption, and remission of sins. Perkins, however, omitted this. But let the reader consider how carelessly these claims are made. What then? Must predestination in general, which embraces reprobation just as much as election, be said to be directed toward these blessings? Or could Arminius have been unaware that Perkins was reasoning here about predestination in general? Or did he think it impermissible to employ *predestination* in this sense? This, then, should have been censured, without snarling so irrelevantly at his adversary. Yet this usage is also permissible, and on good authorities. Thus Augustine says in *Enchiridion to Laurentius*, chapter 100, that “God, being supremely good, makes good use even of the evil: to the damnation of those whom he justly predestined to punishment and to the salvation of those whom he kindly predestined to grace.” Arminius himself confesses that predestination, even when taken more strictly for the predestination of human beings, has customarily been employed in two ways: “At one time,” he says, “it is applied to both the elect and the reprobate; at another it is restricted to the elect alone, and then has reprobation as its opposite.” Holy Writ also speaks in the same manner, although not under the word *predestination* but under an equivalent term, as when Jude testifies that certain people are προγεγραμμένους εἰς κρίσιν—“written beforehand for judgment”—Jude 3; and the Apostle signifies that God appointed some to wrath. It is also utterly certain from Scripture that whatever comes from God's predestination happens in accordance with it. Acts 4:28. But even if it were so—namely, that Perkins here treated predestination in particular, meaning election as opposed to reprobation, although this is false—was he not permitted to delimit its object solely by its final end? Yet this has been placed in almost constant use among certain Schoolmen, I mean Aquinas, so that they distinguish two acts in God: one concerning the means, which they call predestination, and the other concerning the final end, which they wish to be election. Certainly in this lies the difference between the aforesaid Schoolmen and our theologians who treat predestination and consider it as distinct from reprobation. For the aforesaid Schoolmen wish election to be directed toward salvation, but predestination toward the means by which we attain salvation. They therefore also wish election, as a divine act concerning the end, to be prior to predestination, as a divine act concerning the means directed toward that end. Our theologians, however, embrace both these acts—which are on both sides acknowledged really to be one, differing by reason alone, as is also established on both sides—now under the name of election, now under the name of predestination. Thus this difference lies only in words, not in things.

3. But whether it was a definition or a description, you find in it not a few things to censure. You wish a decree to be the genus of predestination; but Holy Writ calls that very thing which you call a decree a purpose or counsel, as you yourself testify in the public disputation *On Predestination*, thesis 3. These are the words: “We establish a decree as the genus of predestination, which Scripture calls πρόθεσιν, ‘purpose,’ and βουλὴν τοῦ θελήματος Θεοῦ, ‘the counsel of God's will.’” Thus we have Arminius as an advocate for Perkins's cause and a betrayer of his own, which is accustomed to happen to those whose purpose is not so much to reconcile their own statements as to attack those of others. And indeed, in ordinary usage *counsel* is customarily employed for an act of the intellect joined with an act of the will, that is, for a considered volition or for a decree proceeding from counsel. In this sense the Apostle undoubtedly speaks about the immutability of the counsel in Hebrews 6:17. Likewise in Acts 13:36 David is said to have fallen asleep by the counsel of God, that is, by the will of God, in the way the old translator also rendered the passage and our English version has it. Hence there is also the saying, “The counsel of the Lord will stand.” Proverbs 19:21. And in Acts 27:42 it is said that the soldiers' counsel was to kill the prisoners; that is, the soldiers deemed it advisable and so willed to kill them. In the same way in Holy Writ, *God's knowledge* is customarily employed for the knowledge of approval, that is, knowledge joined with will; and πρόγνωσις, “foreknowledge,” also signifies God's decree. Finally, Arminius here presupposes that predestination is an act of God's will and not of his intellect. Yet he ought not to have been unaware—since he was sufficiently conversant with the Schoolmen—that Schoolmen of the greatest name dispute this matter, as Bellarmine could also have shown him in *On Grace and Free Will*, book 2, chapter 9. Schoolmen of better standing also wish it to be an act of the intellect, specifically Aquinas. But I acknowledge that, as far as the substance of the matter is concerned, the difference is very slight, since Aquinas professes that it includes the will to bestow grace and glory. They also assert this of predestination only as it is conceived as an act distinct from election, which they wish to be an act of the divine will by which God intends salvation for some. They wish predestination, however, to be concerned with the ordering of means fit for attaining salvation, and they say that ordering is formally an act of the intellect. See whether the whole matter can be reconciled by making a distinction. For means fit for salvation can be prescribed for ordering in two ways: either from duty—or rather from fittingness—that is, regarding the manner in which they can or ought fittingly to be ordered, and this is an act of counsel or intellect; or regarding the fact that they will be ordered in this or that way, and this is an act of decree or a determination of the will. Arminius next introduces a doubt about counsel, namely whether it can be attributed to God, and immediately resolves it by saying that Scripture does so. But whoever knows that the Word of God attributes this to God will never, I think, doubt whether it can be attributed to him. He may nevertheless still doubt in what sense it is attributed to God, which Arminius has not yet explained. Aquinas explains it thus: “The very rationale of things to be done is called counsel in God, not on account of inquiry, but on account of the certainty of cognition at which those who take counsel arrive by inquiry.” Question 22, article 1, reply 1, and First Part of the Second Part, question 14,

[Liber I, Pars I; printed p. 38; BSB canvas 46]

article 1, reply 2: “Counsel is attributed to God with respect to the certainty of the opinion or judgment, which in us arises from the inquiry of counsel; but inquiry of this sort has no place in God, and therefore counsel in this respect is not attributed to God.” According to this, Damascene says that God does not take counsel, for to take counsel belongs to one who does not know. The sum is that in God there is knowledge of everything to be done and the most absolute knowledge of its fittingness for the ends intended by him, but without consultation.

4. I readily grant the things which he rightly understood and reasoned about the inequality of the godly and the ungodly with respect to the advantages of the present life. I also have no doubt Perkins would have granted them. In this sense Perkins said that with respect to animal life we are all equal—not that every individual is always equal to every other, but because equality between them is not repugnant, that is, it is possible for them to be equal with respect to the present life and its advantages. In the same way the Church of God after Christ's ascension is called catholic, not because it is actually diffused throughout every part of the earth, but because it can be diffused throughout every part. Otherwise, it is most true that the ungodly sometimes—and, I add, very often—abound in the advantages of this life while the godly are destitute of them; that at other times, conversely, the evil are afflicted even in this world while the good obtain prosperous success in external affairs. This must be understood of the goods and advantages of the present life considered materially—for example, of health insofar as it is health, and of riches and breadth of reputation considered simply and without qualification. The ungodly sometimes enjoy these things, while the godly are deprived of them. Meanwhile, however, every condition of the godly is formally good insofar as by God's blessing it turns to the good of the godly; contrariwise, every condition of the ungodly turns to evil through God's curse. In this sense the Apostle's saying holds: “Godliness has promises of both the present and the future life.” 1 Timothy 4:8. Set forth what you have to say concerning the final end of predestination.
