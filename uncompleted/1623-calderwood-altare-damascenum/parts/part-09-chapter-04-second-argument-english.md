Second argument. That government which was employed in apostolic churches in
the apostles’ own times and was not rebuked by the apostles is without doubt
of apostolic institution. But the government of bishops with directive and
corrective authority over presbyters and the flock was employed in apostolic
churches in the apostles’ own times and was not rebuked by them. Therefore,
and so forth. If the proposition were to be proved, they would have to use
this middle term: whatever was employed in apostolic churches in the apostles’
own times and was not rebuked by them is without doubt of apostolic
institution. But many indifferent things were employed in apostolic churches.
Moreover, it is not enough that things being employed are not rebuked in order
for them to be of divine institution; they must also be approved. For many
things can be employed in churches while the apostles are unaware and are
occupied, as they say, in distant places. Finally, there is no doubt that
things employed while the apostles were alive, saw them, and approved them
were lawful. Nevertheless, they do not always institute the things they
approve. An apostolic institution does not follow a human invention but
precedes it. The Spirit teaches and directs the apostles, and they lead others
when they institute something that is to remain in the Christian church.
Downham compares the common government of presbyters to silver and the
superiority of the bishop to gold. Therefore, if the government of presbyters
had been employed in the apostles’ times—which our adversaries deny in
vain—would the apostles have rebuked it, seeing that it was silver? But let the
proposition pass, and let us pass to the assumption.

They say that with great agreement the authors testify that in the apostles’
age there were not only bishops but also a succession of bishops in the
churches of Rome, Alexandria, Jerusalem, and Antioch. Again they bring forward
Eusebius, who began his catalog with the apostles’ own age. At Rome, Linus,
Anacletus, and Clement; at Jerusalem, James the Just and Simeon the son of
Cleophas; at Antioch, Evodius and Ignatius; and at Alexandria, Mark, Anianus,
Abilius, and Cerdo were bishops while the apostles were alive, saw them, and
approved them.

*We have demonstrated,* says Tilenus, *that this, if not immediately
instituted by Christ himself, was at least done under the apostles and by the
apostles before they were taken away from human affairs. Mark discharged the
episcopate of the church of Alexandria and died in the eighth year of Nero. He
was succeeded by Anianus, and Anianus by Abilius; all these presided over that
church while the apostle John was still alive: Eusebius, book 2, chapter 24,
and book 3, chapters 13 and 21. Evodius sat at Antioch; after James the Lord’s
brother, Simeon sat at Jerusalem; and Polycarp sat at Smyrna while that apostle
whom I have named was alive and saw him.* Here Eusebius comes forward again,
testifying three hundred years afterward to what happened in the apostles’
age. We have already said how worthy of trust he is. On undertaking his
history he testifies that he entered an unfamiliar and deserted road. It must
be admitted that this happened to him especially at the very threshold. *But
it cannot easily be said how many, and which, of the apostles’ sincere
imitators are proved to have shepherded churches founded by the apostles,
except insofar as someone gathers them from Paul’s words,* says Eusebius
himself. *That interval from the final chapter of the Acts of the Apostles to
the middle of Trajan’s reign, during which Quadratus and Ignatius flourished,
can plainly be called `ἀδήλων`, “of uncertain things,” with Varro. In it
nothing certain about Christian affairs has come down to us except the very
few things touched upon incidentally by enemies of the faith—Suetonius,
Cornelius Tacitus, and Pliny Caecilius. To fill that gap, Eusebius drew without
discrimination `ἐκ τῶν ὑποθέσεων`, “from the hypotheses,” of some Clement or
other—not the learned Alexandrian `ὁ γραμματεύς`, “the scribe”—and from the
`τῆς πενταβίβλου`, “five-book work,” of Hegesippus, no better a writer,* as
Joseph Scaliger observed. Tilenus transferred this observation into his own
annotations while suppressing Scaliger’s name. *The history of those times,*
he says, *has great gaps and lacunae, which spurious Clements and other writers
of the same bran filled with tales drawn from their own brains and idle
dirges.* Likewise: *We warned above that there are great lacunae at the
beginning of ecclesiastical history. For from the end of the Acts of the
Apostles to Trajan’s times scarcely anything certain survives. Hence men with
nothing better to do seized the opportunity to venture and invent anything
they pleased—men of whom even the apostolic times were not devoid.* Downham
should be ashamed, then, of this assertion: *The history of Acts ends forty
years before John’s death; the remaining acts of the apostles were consecrated
to memory by Clement and Hegesippus.* What has been sung more repeatedly in
ecclesiastical history than that Peter remained at Rome for twenty-five years?
Yet Velenus refutes it with many arguments. Joseph Scaliger notes in his
annotations on the New Testament that the Jews had one metropolis of religion
and worship, Jerusalem, but three of polity: Jerusalem, Babylon, and
Alexandria. The Dispersion had two, an Asian one to which Peter writes and a
European one mentioned in John 7:35. Babylon was the capital of the Asian
Dispersion and Alexandria of the European. Peter writes to the Jews of the
Asian Dispersion from its own metropolis, Babylon. *Therefore,* he says, *if
Peter*

*was sent `ad περιτομήν`, “to the circumcision,” he appears to have passed his
life in the Asian `διασπορᾷ`, “dispersion,” if there is room for conjecture.
For no one with even a little refinement could believe in his coming to Rome,
his residence there for twenty-five years, and the supreme capital punishment
there.* Tilenus, following in Scaliger’s footsteps, writes the same thing:
*Whatever is affirmed about Peter’s coming to Rome and his death is supported
only by human testimonies—or indeed, if we wish to confess the truth, by that
of only one man, whom others followed `ἀβασανίστως`, “without examination,”
through excessive credulity. I mean Papias, whom even those who
trusted him in this matter acknowledged to have very little judgment and to be
a collector and disseminator of strange tales.* If Papias was the first
disseminator of this tale, that cloud of witnesses which our adversaries bring
forward presents us with only one witness. *For what was handed down from a
single witness and flowed from him to others possesses only the authority of
one individual testimony, even if the same thing is afterward affirmed by
countless others `μυριομυριάκις`, “ten-thousand times ten-thousand times.”*

The same Tilenus affirms that the apostles did not sit fixed in one particular
place but traveled throughout the world and taught all nations. He says in
particular that Peter’s office was to fulfill the ministry of the circumcision
among the Jews of the Dispersion; that he was assigned not to the citadel of
the Roman Empire but to the ministry of the circumcision; that the Jews of the
Dispersion were Peter’s own harvest; and that he preached in those regions to
which he accordingly also addressed his first epistle—ordinarily to Jews and,
outside his ordinary course, sometimes to Gentiles, while Paul, conversely,
went ordinarily to Gentiles and outside his ordinary course to Jews. *Thus
nothing is so fitting as to understand that designation*—namely, “Babylon” at
the end of Peter’s first epistle—*of the metropolis of the Chaldeans, in which
Peter, having received the apostolate of the circumcision, wrote that epistle.
He stayed chiefly there, where in the Jews’ enormous multitude he saw a most
abundant harvest for exercising his ministry.* Because Babylon figuratively
signifies Rome in Revelation, this perhaps led Papias and other ancients into
the error that it should be taken figuratively here, not properly. Therefore,
if the ancients who followed Papias erred about Peter’s coming to Rome and
residing there so many years, what judgment should be made of the rest? What
Thucydides said of the early Greek histories can be affirmed of the ancients—
Eusebius, Irenaeus, Jerome, and so forth: *What later men received from their
forebears they handed down `ἀβασανίστως`, without putting it to the test, as
though free from any need to examine it,* as Tilenus renders it from
Thucydides.

There is such diversity of opinions about Linus, Cletus, Anacletus, and
Clement, who are numbered after Peter in the catalogs of Roman bishops—about
who succeeded whom and in what order—that the accounts cannot be reconciled
in any way. Neither Irenaeus nor Eusebius anywhere mentions Cletus. Francis
Junius learnedly discusses the confused order of succession and so great a
diversity of writers. *The chief cause of the error,* he says, *was that at
first there was not one bishop in the churches, but many presided at once, who
were called bishops or presbyters by the apostles. Their*

*birthdays—that is, the days of their departure, martyrdom, and death—were
recorded in the church’s diptychs, and a solemn public commemoration was made
for their example and instruction. Thus Linus, Clement, Cletus, Anacletus, and
others were bishops or presbyters at Rome simultaneously, even at the time
when Peter and Paul are said to have been in the city. Linus followed their
departure four months later, after him Cletus, then Anacletus, and last of all
Clement, as we have shown from Tertullian, the Martyrium, Damasus, and the
breviary itself. But the calculation of those who make Anacletus Clement’s
successor is false. That this did not enter Damasus’s mind is proved most
plainly by the notation of years according to the consular fasti in which they
were crowned with martyrdom. Through the same cause the same error was offered
to Augustine and Optatus. The fact that Ignatius, Irenaeus, Eusebius, and
others do not even name Cletus rests on the same argument, because it was
unnecessary to name every celebrated presbyter or bishop of the Roman church,
provided the reckoning of the succession and times could stand. This was done
by naming Anencletus, who from the death of Linus, according to Damasus,
ministered in the Roman church almost until the time when Clement returned
from Greece. Wipe away those wretched correcting sponges, which prefer to
confuse all the authors’ narratives rather than yield to this statement drawn
from the truth: there were several bishops in the Roman church—that is,
presbyters—before the occasion of rising schisms produced the distinction of
bishops from presbyters.*

Linus, Cletus, and Anacletus, therefore, were merely presbyters or
bishop-pastors of the Roman church, not perpetual presidents, much less judges
and princely bishops. They governed the church together with several others
whose names are not recorded in the histories. In his vigorous and learned
dissertation, the most distinguished Gersomus Bucerus promises to surrender if
any reconciliation of so great a disagreement could be found which clearly
teaches that every one of those pontiffs presided alone over the Roman church
throughout the time of his alleged episcopate, in the manner of a bishop called
`διακριτικῶς`, “distinctively.” Therefore, three things must be observed in the
ancients’ writings on the succession of bishops. First, without discrimination
and examination they received many things from their forebears and handed them
down to posterity which can be reconciled neither with Scripture nor with one
another. On account of Papias’s authority or antiquity, many thought that Peter
had been at Rome. *This author of many tales was the first to write that Peter
had been at Rome. Hegesippus, Irenaeus, and others followed him on account of
his antiquity, and thus the opinion gained strength,* says Whitaker. Irenaeus,
as quoted by Eusebius, says that Papias was John’s hearer and Polycarp’s
companion, but Eusebius says that he was by no means a hearer of the holy
apostles or had seen them with his own eyes. He says, moreover, that Papias had
little judgment and handed down certain strange parables and things more like
tales than true histories. If Papias, a most ancient writer, is in Eusebius’s
own judgment an author of tales, the testimonies of the ancients must not be
accepted without examination and judgment. Second, in order

chapter 39.]

to trace tables of succession from the apostles’ own times and conform the
earlier ages to their own, when there were several bishops or presbyters in the
most celebrated churches, they selected one man more celebrated than the rest
for doctrine, authority, gravity of years, experience, zeal, piety, and
reputation, and inscribed him in the catalog. Third, the man they selected and
inscribed in the catalog they peculiarly and distinctively called “bishop”
according to the custom of their own age, although in the age in which he
flourished he was called bishop or presbyter without distinction, just as his
other fellow presbyters were. Indeed, even in his own age Irenaeus calls
presbyters or bishops without distinction when he writes about the apostles’
successors—unless we prefer to interpret Irenaeus as speaking about the true
and common succession of all presbyters rather than about that peculiar
succession devised to mark times in history, or to stop the mouths of heretics.
For the heretics could not trace from the apostles a series of celebrated
teachers handing heresies and errors down to posterity, whereas the orthodox
could demonstrate the succession of purer doctrine by the personal succession
of certain teachers who became distinguished in certain churches.

*It is necessary,* says Irenaeus, *to adhere to those who, as we have said,
guard the apostles’ doctrine and, together with the order of the presbytery,
display sound speech and conduct.* Likewise: *Therefore it is necessary to
obey those presbyters who are in the church, those who have succession from the
apostles, as we have shown, who together with the succession of the episcopate
have received the sure gift of truth according to the Father’s good pleasure.*
Likewise: *But when we appeal again to that tradition which is from the
apostles and is guarded in the churches through successions of presbyters,
those who oppose tradition say that, being wiser not only than the presbyters
but also than the apostles, they have found the pure truth.*

What has been said in general about the catalog of bishops can also be applied
to the bishops of the churches of Alexandria and Jerusalem. Joseph Scaliger
asks where the church of Jerusalem was, since the names of its bishops and a
certain, continuous succession have been handed down to memory until now. For
it is certain that it could not have remained at Jerusalem. The city was
utterly destroyed, so that not even a trace of it appeared. The plow was driven
over it by Musonius Tyrrhenus, prefect of the fortifications, so that not only
did it cease to be a city, but afterward no one was permitted to build even a
hut on its site once the plow had been driven over it. Yet Eusebius numbers
bishops down to the overthrow it suffered under Hadrian. Lest anyone think
there was no succession of the Jerusalem see, the ancients invented the claim
that it had not previously been entirely destroyed, but the Lord’s prophecy
and Josephus’s own history refute them, says Scaliger. In the series of bishops
of the church of Antioch there is almost the same diversity of opinions as
there is concerning the bishops of the Roman church. Some

refer Evodius, others Ignatius, as the first bishop. They were only presbyters
and colleagues of the church of Antioch. But because both were more renowned
than their other fellow presbyters, their successors, when compiling a
catalog, placed them both in their catalog in succession, though not in the
same order. What is more widely repeated about Ignatius than that he was led
from Syria to Rome to be thrown to the beasts? Scaliger nevertheless asks how
this could have happened, since it was the provincial magistrate who inflicted
punishment upon Christians. If anyone should say that he was a Roman citizen,
Scaliger replies that it was not lawful to throw a Roman citizen to the beasts.

Jerome indeed says of the church of Alexandria: *From Mark the Evangelist down
to the bishops Heraclas and Dionysius, the presbyters always selected one of
their own, placed him in a higher rank, and called him bishop.* But according
to Jerome himself, Mark was crowned with martyrdom in Nero’s eighth year and
therefore before the martyrdom of Peter and Paul. It follows, then, that the
episcopate was undertaken in certain churches while the apostles were still
alive. Joseph Scaliger observed that Jerome followed Eusebius in historical
matters, inserted many Eusebian things of his own, suffered from anachronisms,
and contradicted himself. Irenaeus says: *After their departure*—namely,
Peter’s and Paul’s—*Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, himself handed
down to us in writing the things Peter had proclaimed.* Thus that Alexandrian
episcopate did not arise while Peter and Paul were alive, much less while they
saw and approved it. Few of the apostles survived after Nero’s fourteenth
year, and fewer still after Mark’s martyrdom. For, as you see, Irenaeus relates
that the Gospel was written after the departure of Peter and Paul—that is,
after Nero’s final year—as all the papists hold. Jerome and others affirm that
he wrote the Gospel at Rome. He must therefore have spent some further years
at Alexandria and in Egypt. Hence Dorotheus and others extend Mark’s years to
the reign of Trajan. *But under Trajan’s reign,* says Dorotheus in his
*Synopsis*, *at Alexandria a rope was put about his neck. He was dragged from
the place called Bucolus to the place called “of the Angels” (or “of the
messengers”), and there in the month of April he was burned with fire by mad
worshipers of images and buried in Bucolus.* According to Dorotheus and others,
therefore, this account of Jerome’s did not happen even while John survived.

What if that episcopate of order at Alexandria was instituted by the
presbyters while John was alive but did not see it, since he was far from
Alexandria? Many things happened in the churches which were hidden from the
apostles and which they could know only from the reports of others. Could not
such a thing, then, have happened in some single corner of the world and
remained hidden from the one apostle? When John understood that Diotrephes had
aspired to primacy and had cast the brethren out of the church, he threatened
that when he came he would call his deeds to mind, epistle 3, verses 9–10. If
the apostles had anywhere perceived the seeds of evils, there is no doubt that
they would have rebuked them. The mystery of iniquity was at work in their

age. It is therefore no wonder if, while they were unaware, the foundation of
the primacy of Antichrist was laid somewhere. Moreover, that higher rank was
nothing other than a perpetual presidency of order. We are now disputing
about a primacy of authority, about a bishop who is not only president but
also prince—and this even if we grant, without conceding it, that Jerome was
not mistaken either about the fact itself or in assigning its date. It is
sufficiently clear that Jerome was mistaken in other things he related about
the church of Alexandria while following Eusebius.

Jerome writes that when Philo the Jew describes the Essenes he meant to
signify the manner of life the Alexandrian Christians led under Mark. *From
this,* he says, *it would be apparent that the first church of believers in
Christ was such as monks now strive and desire to be: nothing is their own;
among them there is no rich man and no poor man; estates are divided among the
needy; they devote themselves to prayer, the Psalms, doctrine, and continence;
and Luke relates that the first believers at Jerusalem were such.* A man who
relates that monks existed in the church of Alexandria under the emperor
Gaius—that is, in Philo’s age—might readily also affirm that bishops were there.
*Philo writes,* says Eusebius, *of how those to whom ecclesiastical ministries
are committed preside among them, including the diaconate and the presidencies
of the episcopate that stand above all.* Joseph Scaliger demonstrates in his
*Refutation of the Three Heresies* that Philo must be understood as speaking
of Jewish Essenes, not Christians.

Jerome says that the presbyters placed one man selected from among themselves
in a higher rank from the time of Mark the Evangelist. Therefore they must
also have placed Anianus in that higher rank. But from a fragment of an ancient
codex Gersomus Bucerus relates that Mark himself ordained Anianus when setting
out for Pentapolis. Some doubt whether Mark himself was ever at Alexandria,
because Papias was the first to relate that Peter was at Rome, from which they
say Peter sent Mark to Alexandria. *You see, then, how doubtful it is,* says
Parker, *that Jerome said there had been bishops at Alexandria from Mark,
since it is not yet sufficiently established at all that Mark himself was at
Alexandria. We received the Syriac version of the Testament, as Field proves
from Boderianus, from the Assyrians and Persians, and they say that Mark the
Evangelist handed it down to them. If that is true, it is probable that Mark
lived in Syria rather than Egypt.* We shall say more about Mark, Simeon, Linus,
Evodius, and James in the third argument. We have now seen by what testimonies
and witnesses, and how worthy of trust, they prove that bishops superior in
the authority of ordination and jurisdiction existed in the churches in the
apostles’ age. Let us also see which bishops they give us in sacred Scripture
as exercising directive and corrective authority over presbyters and churches
under the apostles.

Philippians 2:25. Those who succeeded the apostles in the government of
particular churches were called apostles, he says, as men

who possessed to a very great extent the same authority as the apostles and
apostolic men. *The men whom they once called presbyters and bishops they
afterward named apostles, but as time went on they left the name of apostolate
to those who had once been called apostles. Thus Epaphroditus was the apostle
of the Philippians, Titus of the Cretans, and Timothy of the Asians,* says
Theodoret. When Theodoret perceived that the word *bishop* had been common to
all pastors, he supposed or invented that they were called apostles in the
apostolic age in order to adorn the bishops of his own age, who were superior
to presbyters, with some more august title. For, as he thought, the apostles,
who were superior in rank to the presbyters—also called bishops—represented
his own bishops, or else the episcopal rank occupied a place very like that of
the apostles. Yet no testimonies of Scripture can confirm Theodoret’s

Ambrose understands bishops by the apostles in Ephesians 4 and 1 Corinthians
12:28. *The apostles,* he says, *are bishops. The prophets are interpreters of
the Scriptures, although in the earliest days there were prophets such as
Agabus and so forth. But now the interpreters are called prophets and the
deacons evangelists, as Philip was.* Let the reader consider how properly and
genuinely he applies those things to the state of the church of his own time.
Who will doubt that they are foreign to the apostle’s meaning? In sacred
Scripture the name *apostle* is taken either in the common sense for any envoy,
or in a narrower sense for Christ’s twelve chief disciples and for Matthias,
Paul, and Barnabas, who were admitted into the company of the apostles after
Christ’s ascension. *In the common usage of the Greeks,* says Casaubon,
*`Ἀπόστολοι` were certain men sent somewhere for the sake of conducting
business rather than delivering a message.* It is so understood in John 13:16.
Likewise: *So too Paul calls Epaphroditus, writing to the Philippians, their
`ἀπόστολον` in Philippians 2:25, as one sent to him by them to minister to him.*
Likewise: *It is certain that the designation “apostles” can be attributed to
no one except those whom I have named*—that is, the twelve, Matthias, Paul, and
Barnabas—*unless perhaps figuratively and equivocally*—that is, among the
fathers. In Acts 1:20 Peter calls the apostolate an `ἐπισκοπήν`, “oversight,”
but in the same speech the apostolate is also called a `διακονία`, “ministry.”
Were deacons therefore called apostles?

Serrarius observes from Epiphanius, Heresy 30, that among the Jews of Tiberias
there were men who held the second place after their patriarch and served as
his counselors. They were sent here and there throughout the provinces to
collect tithes, firstfruits, and similar things, and to correct what they saw
had been corrupted; and they were called apostles. Casaubon shows that the
Syriac word *Schelichim* was assigned to various ministers in the description
of Jewish rites. Thus when Paul calls Epaphroditus the apostle of the
Philippians, he means nothing other than their envoy, as the words that follow
indicate: *who supplied to me*

*the things I needed,* as though he had been sent to Rome by the Philippians
for this very purpose, to minister necessities to Paul.

What is said in 2 Corinthians 8:23 about the envoys of the churches illustrates
this passage. Titus and two others were sent to the Corinthians to collect the
contributions made for the relief of the church at Jerusalem. Those two
colleagues of Titus are called envoys of the churches—in Greek, apostles of
the churches. If someone exchanged the words and said *bishops of the
churches*, would he express the genuine sense of the words? They are therefore
called envoys of the churches *because they were individually delegated by
the churches to receive and convey their offering.* It is clear from verse 19
that they were selected by the churches to collect the beneficence of the
churches of Achaia, where it is said of one of the colleagues that he was
chosen by the votes of the churches as Paul’s traveling companion with the
beneficence that was to be administered; and from verse 24, where the apostle
says: *Therefore show them the proof of your love and of our boasting about
you, in the sight of the churches*—namely, the churches by which they were
sent. It is as though he said: *You will have all the churches as witnesses of
this piety of yours, in whose sight you conduct yourselves when you behold the
envoys chosen by their votes.*

Similarly, it is clear from chapter 2:25 that Epaphroditus was selected and
delegated by the Philippians to carry their beneficence to Paul: *who supplied
to me the things I needed*; and from chapter 4:19: *I have received everything
and abound; I am full, having received from Epaphroditus what you sent, a
sweet-smelling fragrance, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God.* They
admit that he was delegated to carry their benevolence, but they deny that he
is called their apostle on account of this particular and single embassy. It
does not seem probable to them that a designation so august, employed to
denote the sacred function of the apostles, would be imposed upon so obscure a
ministry or upon men who had been delegated. That ministry was not base and
obscure, since the apostle calls the Corinthians’ contributions and
beneficence a *grace*, and the Philippians’ a sweet-smelling fragrance. And in
the common usage of the Greeks and Syrians, human envoys were called
`ἀπόστολοι` and *Schelichim*, as we have shown above even from the Scriptures
themselves, John 13:16. In the same way the blessed and heavenly spirits are
designated by the name *angels*, although in ordinary Greek usage any
messenger or envoy is called an angel, as in James 2:25, where Rahab is
recounted as having received `τοὺς ἀγγέλους`, “those messengers,” and sent them
out by another way. Thus one must look to Greek usage in the case of Greek
words, not Latin usage. The Latins do not call envoys and messengers “angels”
or “apostles” in everyday speech, but it was familiar to the Greeks.

the churches. He commends them to the Corinthians with these titles, partly so
that the Corinthians would not doubt their honesty in a financial matter, and
partly so that their own contribution might be more abundant because the
envoys’ authority was greater. So too he calls Epaphroditus his fellow worker
and fellow soldier and commends him with other titles, as he customarily did
the men he sent to churches. Nevertheless, they are called envoys of the
churches with respect to the particular embassy we have described. Is it
likely that the churches wished to send their bishops away to Rome or
Jerusalem with their offerings? Who would conduct himself in the meantime as
ruler and judge, seeing that, as they themselves would have it, there was no
government of episcopal presbyters in the external forum? Why multiply words?
The English themselves in their latest translation render the Greek word
*apostle* as *envoy* in both 2 Corinthians 8:23 and Philippians 2:25.

Therefore none are called apostles except those chief men of whom we have
spoken, or, in the common sense, any envoy. But let us also grant that men
other than the principal architects of the church are called apostles. It
still does not follow that bishops were adorned with the title *apostles* in
the apostolic age. Indeed, that most august and especially distinctive title
of extraordinary and, above all, supreme men could not have been shared with
the ordinary pastors of churches without danger, for a sharing of names would
produce a confusion of things. Bishops should instead have been called
apostles once the apostles had been taken away, if, as they hold, bishops
succeeded them in external government. Yet they should not have been called
bishops, since that word was common to presbyters.

If Epaphroditus had been bishop of the Philippians, the apostle would not have
said, *I thought it necessary to send Epaphroditus to you.* He would instead
have said *to send him back*, if he were their bishop. For after he had
discharged his embassy and supplied the things Paul needed, he was bound by
duty to return to the Philippians, if he were bishop of that church. Yet he
appears not to have been about to return unless the apostle had thought it
necessary to send him. The reason why he sent Epaphroditus was *because he
longed for you all and was deeply distressed because you had heard that he was
ill*—that is, as the Syriac interpreter renders it: *He desired to see you all
and was anxious because he had learned that you had heard he was sick.* The
Philippians’ concern about Epaphroditus after they had heard of his poor health
sharpened Epaphroditus’s desire to visit the Philippians, who were so concerned
about him, although otherwise he would not have returned immediately.

The apostle says, moreover, that he would send Timothy *after,* he says, *I see
the state of my affairs*, 2:23; likewise verse 24: *I trust in the Lord that I
myself will also come to you soon.* But in verse 25: *I thought it necessary to
send Epaphroditus*, and so forth. The adversative particle shows that
Epaphroditus was sent because of a particular occasion, namely because Paul
could not yet send Timothy. Therefore, as Timothy was, so was Epaphroditus: a
fellow worker of Paul, sent here and there as need required, and consequently
an evangelist, not a bishop

[1623 printed p. 130 | IA leaf 142 | PDF p. 142]

as ruler and judge of the bishops and pastors of the Philippian church, of
whom chapter 1:1 speaks. The envoys of the churches mentioned in 2 Corinthians
8 were not bishops of the churches, for the churches could not properly, as I
have said, be deprived of their ordinary rulers for so long. Moreover, to call
two bishops—for the apostle speaks of only two—apostles of the churches would
be inept language. Not two but more bishops of the churches would have to be
called bishops of the churches. The apostle speaks not of two churches but of
several. Thus, if they were to be called apostles not because of the embassy
for collecting the Corinthians’ beneficence but because of some more august
function and ministry, they should then be called apostles of the churches
because they were sent to these and those churches as occasion required, not
because they were bishops of churches. For a bishop is bishop of one church,
not of very many, and two bishops cannot be called bishops of the churches,
especially when we speak either of many churches or indefinitely.

It is no wonder if evangelists were called apostles, since they either traveled
through regions with the apostles or were sent here and there by the apostles,
in the manner of apostles, to establish and confirm churches. But bishops are
attached to one particular church, which they ought not desert. The fathers
appear to have understood Epaphroditus to be an apostle in this sense—namely,
because he was an evangelist. Chrysostom says: *We send you your apostle, the
man who taught you.* Jerome says: *Because he himself had received the office
of apostolate among them.* Ambrose says: *For he was made an apostle by the
apostle when he sent him to exhort them.* None of these things proves that
Epaphroditus was the perpetual and ordinary pastor and ruler of the
Philippians; instead, they rather indicate the contrary. Yet these are the
fathers whom Tilenus names.

But to return to the point from which we began, I say that although
Epaphroditus and Titus’s colleagues were evangelists, they were not called
apostles for that reason. For if Titus’s colleagues were called apostles of
the churches by reason of the function by whose distinctive designation the
chief apostles are named, then they too were chief apostles. For the chief
apostles were also apostles of the churches, that is, sent to the churches.
Those who were called apostles of the churches could also have been called
apostles simply. If someone should say that the chief apostles were called
apostles of Christ, but evangelists apostles of the churches or of the
apostles, Casaubon will reply: *What shall we say of Baronius, who says that it
is the peculiar right of the one Roman pope to create apostles, something we
never read was done by the Lord’s apostles?* Therefore, just as they were
called apostles of the churches to which they were sent, so they should also
have been called apostles of the Christ who sent them. They were called
apostles of the churches in the common sense, that is, envoys of the
churches—specifically of those churches which chose them to collect the
beneficence of the churches of Achaia and carry it to Jerusalem, as we showed
above. The same judgment must be made about Epaphroditus,

[Marginalia: *Paraenesis*, chapter 5; *Exercises* 317.]

[1623 printed p. 131 | IA leaf 143 | PDF p. 143]

who was sent by the Philippians to minister to Paul the things he needed.
However it may be, bishops were never called apostles in the apostles’ times.
Do you suppose they would ever have refused so august a designation and
lowered themselves to the common title *bishop* if they had been adorned with
the title *apostles* in the apostolic age?

They also fashioned princely and judicial bishops under the apostles from the
angels of the Asian churches. Tilenus frames their argument in this way: *From
the epistles which John, inspired by God, wrote to the angels of the seven
Asian churches, a most compelling argument is constructed by which we prove
that rank was instituted in apostolic times. For to what end are the
individual angels or pastors of those churches alone addressed, praised,
rebuked, and admonished of their duty, if things done rightly or wrongly in
each church happened no more through their diligence or negligence than
through the common diligence or negligence of the presbyters? What could be
more unjust than, when a whole college constituted of presbyters equal among
themselves has sinned, for one of them—who possesses no authority of his own
above the rest—to bear the common fault of all and endure the rebuke alone?*
Likewise: *His epistle to the angel of Smyrna, whom not even your own men deny
to have been Polycarp, survives in Revelation 2:9. Although John censures and
finds nothing wanting in that one man, on your position he was at least worthy
of rebuke because he exercised the government of that church singularly; nor
should he otherwise have been adorned with singular praise, if everything in
that government was common to him and his colleagues and fellow presbyters.
Just as what is done amiss in an army by soldiers, centurions, and lower
commanders of ranks is not undeservedly imputed to tribunes and superior
officers, so whatever was done amiss in those Asian churches, not only by the
flocks but also by the presbyters, who in relation to the bishop are part of
the flock, would by no means be reproached to the one angel or president
unless he possessed a certain authority of his own for restraining both.*

Downham speaks similarly: *Does not Scripture testify to this everywhere when
the angel of the Ephesian church is commended because he tested and did not
tolerate those who said they were apostles and were not? Conversely, the angel
of Thyatira is rebuked because he tolerated teachers of the Nicolaitan heresy,
who are compared sometimes to Balaam and sometimes to Jezebel, because they
persuaded men to fornication and idolatry.* I acknowledge that some of our men
take the designation *angel* not `συλληπτικῶς`, collectively, but
`ἰδιωτικῶς`, individually, such as Reynolds, that most learned Englishman,
Theodore Beza, and others. But they interpret the angel only as a president or
`προεστῶτα`, not as a bishop, judge, and prince. In his conference with Hart,
Reynolds says that the presbyters constituted in individual churches were
bishops appointed by the Holy Spirit to attend to and feed the flock; and that
to accomplish this more readily by common counsel and consent they customarily
assembled more frequently in

[Marginalia: *Paraenesis*, chapter 10; chapter 4, division 3, at the end.]

[1623 printed p. 132 | IA leaf 144 | PDF p. 144]

one gathering and chose one president of the assembly and moderator of its
proceedings. Christ calls him the angel of the church in Revelation 2:1, and
writes to him the things he wished to signify through him to the rest. *And
this is the man,* he says, *whom the fathers afterward in the primitive church
called bishop.* Theodore Beza similarly says: *To the angel, that is, the
`προεστῶτι`, whom it was of course necessary to admonish first about these
matters, and through him his other colleagues and indeed the whole church. But
the episcopal rank later introduced into the church of God by human agency
cannot and must not be established from this. Indeed, it was not even
necessary for that office of the `προεστῶτος` to be perpetual, as the oligarchic
tyranny which arose from it—whose apex is the Antichristian beast—now at last
most certainly declares, to the ruin not only of the whole church but also of
the world.*

Serrarius writes as follows in *On the Rabbis* about the archisynagogue:
*Afterward he began to be called by the Jews Scheliach tsibbur, that is, prince
of the assembly, `ἄγγελος` or `ἀπόστολος τῆς συναγωγῆς`, of the congregation.*
Some think Christ alluded to this angel. But Casaubon interprets *Scheliach
tsibbur* as the apostle, not the angel, of the synagogue. *One is also found in
their rites who is called Scheliach tsibbur, `ἀπόστολος τῆς συναγωγῆς`, as
though you said “envoy of the synagogue.” Even today,* he says, *the synagogues
have their Schelichim, whom they hold to be cantors who send prayers to God on
behalf of the whole assembly.* Whatever his office was, it does not appear
that he was prefect of the synagogue. For there were several archisynagogues,
or prefects of the synagogue, in one synagogue, as is clear from Mark 5:22 and
Acts 13:15.

Others of our men interpret the angel collectively or indefinitely of several
men, such as Foxe, Perkins, Brightman, and others. *The letters are not sent to
some one angel but to the whole college of pastors, so to speak, all of whom
are comprehended by this common name. For there was not one angel of Ephesus
but several, nor was there any prince among them. This is plain from Paul, who
summoned the presbyters or bishops of Ephesus to Miletus, Acts 20:17, 28. He
would have spared the multitude if one of them had presided in authority over
the rest; or at least among his other admonitions he would have said one word
about obedience toward the chief bishop. But no principate of this sort had
yet arisen. It began only after the apostles had been taken away from human
affairs, except that Diotrephes gave some specimen of it.* The arguments that
follow induce me to accede to their opinion and interpretation.

1. The designation *angel* is attributed to all pastors and also to priests
and prophets in the Old and New Testaments: Judges 2:1; Haggai 1:13; Malachi
2:7; 1 Corinthians 11:10, as some interpret this last passage, including
Jerome, who interprets the angels of 1 Corinthians 11 as those who preside over
churches. Rabbi David Camius, according to Drusius, established two kinds of
angels: one of those who have their dwelling in the places above, and another

[Marginalia: on Revelation 2:1; chapter 31; *Exercises* 14, p. 311; Brightman
on Revelation 2:1; Drusius, *Praeterita* on Revelation 1:20.]

[1623 printed p. 133 | IA leaf 145 | PDF p. 145]

of those who are here in the lower places. Such, he says, are prophets or
priests. This designation is not attributed to them by reason of jurisdiction
but of embassy, for they are messengers and interpreters of God’s will. All
pastors are such interpreters. If Christ had wished to designate a particular
ruler or judge, he could easily have addressed him by the title *ruler*,
*judge*, or *president*. For those who rule others are customarily named by
some designation signifying their authority, not by one which denotes only an
office they share with others.

2. When Christ reveals the mysteries of the lampstands and stars, he says that
the seven lampstands are the seven churches, but he does not say that the seven
stars are the seven angels of the seven churches; rather, the seven stars are
the angels of the seven churches, Revelation 1:20. The number of angels is not
omitted without reason: it prevents us from supposing there was perhaps one
angel in each of the seven churches. Individual stars signify several angels
or pastors. The one two-horned ram in Daniel 8:3, 20 signified not one but
several kings of the Medes and Persians. The four horns in Zechariah 1:18
signified not four, but all the adversaries harassing Judah and Israel; and
each horn depicted several enemies. The five prudent virgins awaiting the
bridegroom represented not five but all the godly in a parable; and in the same
chapter, Matthew 25, the five servants to whom talents were entrusted signified
not five but more. Nothing is more usual than for one individual thing in a
sign to represent several things signified in parables, types, and prophetic
visions. Indeed, even if Christ had also assigned the number seven to the
angels when interpreting the mystery, it would still not follow that there was
only one angel in each church.

In chapter 8:2 the seven angels—those whom he had mentioned before—are said to
stand before the Lord. But Reynolds, who understands ministering spirits there,
says that the name *seven angels* signifies not precisely seven, but all the
angels by synecdoche. For there are not precisely seven angels who alone stand
before God, as is clear from Daniel 7, whatever the magi and cabalists may
prattle about seven angels standing before God’s throne. For the article
`τοὺς` added to the phrase, he says, indicates that mention is made of a known
thing which was discussed before; likewise, the added phrase *who stand*, of
whom he speaks, demonstrates it sufficiently. Now in chapter 7 all the angels
are mentioned and are said to stand around the throne; and in chapter 5 he says
there are ten thousand times ten thousand. *Therefore,* he says, *since it is
common to all God’s elect angels to stand before the throne, the name “seven
angels” appears to signify all God’s angels.* Likewise: *That the number seven
often signifies all, or at least signifies an indefinite number by a definite
one, is taught by the seven pillars of Proverbs 9, the seven pastors of Micah
5, and the seven eyes of Zechariah 3, but especially in those apocalyptic
mysteries: seven lampstands, seven lamps, seven bowls, and seven plagues,
Revelation 4, 5, and 15.* If anyone from Micah 5:5—*When the Assyrian comes
into our land and when he treads in our*

[Marginalia: *Censure of the Book of Apocryphal Predictions*, p. 64.]

[1623 printed p. 134 | IA leaf 146 | PDF p. 146]

*land, we shall set seven shepherds over him*—inferred that precisely seven
shepherds were appointed, he would not do so according to the prophet’s mind.
The two witnesses raised up by the Lord to prophesy in Revelation 11:3 were
not precisely two. You see, therefore, that even when a counting number is
given, the thing numbered must not always be taken precisely according to that
number.

3. Wherever there is a change from a singular number to a plural, it is
certain that the discourse concerns several, not one. *Yet she*—namely, the
woman—*will be saved by bearing children, if they continue in faith and love,*
1 Timothy 2:15. *The verb is placed in the plural number by the unanimous
agreement of all the codices,* says Theodore Beza. *Many deceivers have entered
the world who do not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh. This is
that deceiver and Antichrist,* 2 John 7. So in the epistles to the seven
churches the address is directed to several, as in chapter 2:24: *But I say to
you and to the rest in Thyatira.* “To you” means to the angels of the church
of Thyatira, for no one except the angel was previously mentioned in that
epistle, and these men are distinguished from the rest of the Thyatirans.
Therefore, when *to the angel* is said at the beginning of the epistle, it must
be understood indefinitely of any angel of that church, or collectively. This
is a customary synecdoche in Scripture even where an article is supplied.
*But the man who acts proudly, so as not to obey the priest continually
ministering there to Jehovah your God, or the supreme judge,* and so forth,
Deuteronomy 17:12. One must understand in this passage not one priest or judge,
but several by synecdoche, as is clear from verse 9: *And you shall go to the
Levitical priests or to the supreme judge who will be in those days.*

In Matthew 9:18 a ruler of the synagogue is said to have come to Jesus; in
Acts 18:8 Crispus is called ruler of the synagogue and in verse 17 Sosthenes
ruler of the synagogue, although there were several rulers of the synagogue.
`Ὁ σπείρων`, “the one sowing,” and `ὁ θερίζων`, “the one reaping,” John 4:37;
`ὁ κλέπτης`, “the thief,” John 10:10; and `ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός`, “the good
shepherd,” with the article repeated, John 10:11; and many similar phrases
occur everywhere, where it is sufficiently clear that the discourse is not
about a definite individual but indefinitely about several. And in this very
word *angel*, insofar as it signifies heavenly spirits, a synecdochic manner of
speech occurs, Psalm 34 and so forth: *The angel encamps.* In Acts 4:1, while
they were speaking to the people, the priests and the prefect of the temple
came upon them. Yet there were several prefects, as is clear from Luke 22:4:
*He went away and spoke with the chief priests and the prefects of the temple.*

4. In apocalyptic visions several preachers proclaiming the gospel are often
understood by one angel. Blasius testifies in his commentary on Revelation,
chapter 8, section 8, that according to the common mind of the interpreters—
Haymo, Richard of St. Victor, Ambrose Ansbert, and others—the seven angels with
seven trumpets signify not groups of seven but a very great number of
preachers, and that the first angel signifies the apostles, the second the
pastors of the second century, and so forth. Thus one angel will signify a
thousand or a thousand times a thousand. Therefore, just as the name *angel*
is taken collectively in

[1623 printed p. 135 | IA leaf 147 | PDF p. 147]

the other visions—whether you interpret it as preachers of the Word or as
ministering spirits—so it is also at the beginning of the book, where he spoke
in the singular number about each one separately, so that everything from the
book’s beginning to its end would agree. The ancients themselves interpreted
the angels of the churches sylleptically. Augustine—or the author of the
homilies on Revelation inscribed with his name—writes: *As for what he says,
“I have a few things against you, angel of Thyatira,” he says this to the
overseers of the churches who do not impose the severity of ecclesiastical
discipline upon wanton fornicators and others who act wickedly in any way.*
Gregory the Great writes: *Sacred Scripture often designates the preachers of
the church by the name “angels,” because they proclaim the Father’s glory.
Hence it is that John, writing in Revelation to the seven churches, speaks to
the angels of the churches, that is, to the preachers of the peoples.*

What they report about Onesimus as bishop of the Ephesian church and Polycarp
as bishop of the Smyrnaean church is of no importance. For although, to save
the historians’ credibility, I could answer that they were bishops of those
churches but not sole bishops, being so together with others, yet to confess
the truth, I value little the mutually conflicting reports of the ancients,
since the trustworthiness even of those who agree is not undeservedly doubted,
as I said above about Peter’s Roman pontificate. Others relate that Timothy
was bishop of the Ephesian church when Revelation was written; Ribera, Lyranus,
and Pererius follow them. Blasius leaves the question open and says it is
uncertain who that angel was. Ignatius’s epistles are spurious. Eusebius
testifies that Polycarp was crowned with martyrdom under Antoninus Verus, but
the Roman Martyrology puts it under Decius in the seventh persecution.
Revelation, however, as they hold, was written in Domitian’s fourteenth year.

Thus we understand the angels and stars as all pastors. If the stars signify
ministers, so also do the angels, for the seven stars are the angels of the
churches. But the stars in Revelation signify all pastors, not bishops alone,
unless someone should say that the dragon’s tail cast only bishops down from
heaven. Not only in Revelation but also in Daniel 12 preachers are adorned
with the commendation of stars. If anyone cannot be drawn into this opinion
that the angels should be taken collectively or indefinitely, let him abound
in his own sense. Nothing is thereby taken from the main controversy, even if
he follows the opinion of those interpreters who take the angel as one
individual person, but only as president and moderator. Let us therefore also
defend this opinion.

Why, they ask, is the address directed to one if there were several
colleagues? The papists say that when Christ said to Peter, *I will give you
the keys*, the keys were promised to Peter. Tilenus replies: *Christ indeed
promises the keys of the kingdom of heaven to Peter, but not to him alone;
rather, he promises them to Peter together with his companions.* When
Bellarmine had set down two kinds of representation, historical and parabolic,
Tilenus added a third: *Therefore there is a third way of representing
something,* he says, *when a man conducts the business not only in another’s
name,*

[Marginalia: homily 11; book 34, *Morals on Job*, chapter 4; *Controversies*
3, book 1, chapter 12.]

[1623 printed p. 136 | IA leaf 148 | PDF p. 148]

*but also in his own name, as a member of that body and `τὸ σύστημα`, “the
corporate whole,” whose person he bears.* The persons who were heirs or
legatees are described and named by notaries with no more or greater
circumstances than the person of Peter is in this text, says Cajetan. Yet
Tilenus replies that the promises were made not to Peter alone but to all his
companions in common. No particular person is designated here even by name.
We answer, therefore, that what was said to one president was said to all his
colleagues. *Peter did not bear their common person by virtue of his office.
This is evident because he was not yet appointed their pastor, nor was he
properly their head, except as the first among canons can be called the head
of the canons,* says the same Cajetan. I add that he held no primacy of order
by virtue of his office, and nevertheless what was promised to Peter is
understood to have been promised to all the apostles. Much more, therefore,
when an address about a common business and office is directed to the
president, it must be understood as also directed to his colleagues.

Moreover, it is plain from the very context of the epistles that they are
directed to several men, and that several bear the praise, and also the blame
where sin was committed. What else is meant by these threats, censures,
praises, and promises? *I will come against you and remove your lampstand from
its place. Behold, the devil is about to cast some of you into prison. But I
say to you and to the rest in Thyatira, whoever do not hold this doctrine and
have not approved the depths of Satan,* and so forth. And when it is said in
the singular, *You have labored for my name; you have not denied my faith; you
are neither cold nor hot,* is only the one man whom they wish to be bishop
praised or censured, and not the others? Why, then, are others punished or
adorned with rewards because of one bishop? *I will come against you and
remove your lampstand; I will come against you and fight against them with the
sword of my mouth; I will lay no other burden upon you.* Therefore, if the
statements made in the singular—*you have labored; you have not denied;
repent; you have kept my name*—must nevertheless not be understood as directed
to one individual person, why not also these: *that you cannot bear evil men,
and you have tested those who say they are apostles and are not, and have
found them to be liars; you permit the woman Jezebel, who calls herself a
prophetess, to teach*; and similar statements which seem to imply a certain
authority, since they are read in the same context as the preceding ones?

Yet in truth no authority in the external forum can necessarily be inferred
from these words. Did the Ephesian angel summon the false apostles, cite them
before his consistory, and excommunicate them by his superiority of authority?
Certainly those words indicate something else: *you were pressed by a burden,
and you endure.* *Not to bear evil men, to test those who call themselves
apostles, and to permit the woman Jezebel to teach* are things which belong
even to private Christians, who possess discerning judgment and ought to test
the spirits and oppose wolves, even though it is not theirs to judge them with
a judgment of jurisdiction. The following words show that these expressions
must be understood of the private and common authority of all:

[Marginalia: *On the Institution of the Roman Pontiff*, chapter 4; ibid.,
chapter 4.]

[1623 printed p. 137 | IA leaf 149 | PDF p. 149 | ends before the third argument]

*you hate the deeds of the Nicolaitans; you have there those who hold the
doctrine of Balaam; you also have those who hold this doctrine; you have not
approved the depths of Satan.* See, then, what it is *to bear, to test, and to
permit*. But let us also grant that these words must be understood of the
external forum. Did the bishop alone rule in that forum? *But I say to you and
to the rest in Thyatira, who do not hold this doctrine and have not approved
it,* chapter 2:24. “To you” means: “I say it to you who discharge the office of
president and to your colleagues.” For who else are to be separated from the
rest of the flock? Even if he had said, “to you and to the rest in Thyatira,”
one angel would not be contrasted with all the other Thyatirans as though he
alone had sinned. Instead, some Thyatirans who permitted Jezebel to teach
would be contrasted with others who did not hold this doctrine and did not
know the depths of Satan. Therefore, permitting Jezebel to teach did not
belong to one angel but to other Thyatirans too. If it occurred by way of a
voluntary exercise of jurisdiction, then several colleagues permitted it. If
only by approving and listening, then others from among the people also did
so. But when he says, *to you and to the rest in Thyatira*, he contrasts
colleagues with colleagues and the sounder part of the flock with its diseased
part. For it is plain from this verse that some men not only in the flock but
also in the presbytery did not persist in sound doctrine. If the Thyatiran
angel was only president, so too were the Ephesian and the others.

Thus it is evident from the contents of the epistles that not one but several
men are addressed, rebuked, admonished, praised, and censured, both from the
flock and from the presbytery. That the epistles are directed not to one but
to several is clear both from their contents and from the epiphonema appended
to them: *Let him who has ears hear what the Spirit says to the churches*; and
from chapter 1:11: *Write and send it to the seven churches that are in Asia.*
Hence some interpret the angel as the church, which I do not approve, because
then they would fuse the stars and lampstands into one. It is enough for us to
hold that the angel is only the president: the epistles are sent to the
churches; the fault is not cast upon one; one is not heaped with praises; one
does not bear the common fault of all or alone endure the rebuke; one does not
permit Jezebel to teach; and one does not test those who say they are apostles
and are not. Therefore, they gain nothing even though some of our men expound
the designation *angel* `ἰδιωτικῶς`, individually. Since the things they allege
about Archippus, Onesimus, and other apostolic bishops, as they hold them to
be, are not of such importance that I should linger in refuting them, let us
proceed to the third step.
