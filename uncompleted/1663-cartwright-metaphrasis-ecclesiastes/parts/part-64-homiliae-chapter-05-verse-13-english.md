VERSE 13. There is an evil that brings grief which I have seen under the sun:
wealth kept by its owner to his own harm.

VERSE 14. Or that wealth perishes in an evil undertaking, so that the son he

VERSE 15. Just as he came out of his mother's womb, he goes away naked again

VERSE 16. Therefore this too is an evil that brings grief: entirely as he came,

VERSE 17. Moreover, throughout all his days he eats in darkness, that is, with

The summary is to set before our eyes the evil that exists in the accumulation
of wealth, and to do so not only in these verses but also in chapter 6.

The parts are an explanation of the evil arising from the accumulation of
money: generally at the beginning of verse 13, and specifically in the rest of
the context.

VERSE 13. There is an evil that brings grief which I have seen under the sun:
wealth kept by its owner to his own harm.

Having explained the vanity found in an abundance of crops and produce, he
returns to the other side of the comparison, the gathering of riches or money;
he had placed the other side, concerning agriculture, before this one. Since
he was briefer in the first part, he attacks this insatiable desire for money
at greater length, because it is torn from human minds only with difficulty.
This was the reason Christ spoke so fully and forcefully when uprooting this
nettle from human minds. Matthew 6.

First he makes a universal pronouncement about it. It is not only vain for
establishing happiness in this life—which was also said about the produce of
the earth in verse 10—but additionally brings grief and sorrow to those who
devote themselves to increasing their money. Thus the person who greedily
heaps up money contracts grief along with the money and multiplies his grief in
proportion to the amount by which his money has increased. The miser, whom he
previously called a lover of money, therefore pays dearly for his own avarice,
by which his soul is wounded and pierced as by thorns and goads. Avarice is
therefore a kind of madness, when someone with eyes
open rushes headlong into the pursuit of money, which cannot be separated or
detached from a great evil. Whatever the precise nature of this is, however,
it is better discerned in its particular forms. He teaches from

two of them that one or the other commonly happens to the miser: either riches
bring destruction upon their owner, or they suddenly slip from his hand while
he is alive and looking on. In the verses now presented Solomon explains the
latter, intending to return to the former in chapter 6. He pronounces of both
that he has observed them happening to people under the sun.

In this he shows us a remedy against this plague of avarice: we should turn our
eyes toward these whirlpools of money and diligently inquire what their end
and outcome are. In this way the sad and fatal end of people of this sort will
make us abhor the love of money, and we will learn wisdom from the troubles of
others. Thus David, looking ahead to the end of the ungodly who flourish for a
time, preserved himself from the contagion of their ungodliness. See Psalm
52:6–9. Therefore, instead of keeping our eyes almost fixed upon gold and
silver, as we are accustomed to do, let us turn them toward the owners and
possessors of the money. Instead of fixing the mind's thought upon the
advantages money brings, let us extend our thought to the troubles it brings.
