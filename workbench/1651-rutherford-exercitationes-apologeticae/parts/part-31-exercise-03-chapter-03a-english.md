CHAPTER III

*The arguments of the Jesuits and Arminians against the predetermining
efficacy of grace are answered.*

The central question we have here with the Arminians is why this person is
converted and not that one: whether it is God’s absolute freedom, or rather
his justice, by which he deserts this sinner but does not desert the other who
wills to be converted, and instead converts him. Arminius speaks thus: *Let
someone ask* (he says) *why God deserts this one and takes up that one. The
answer is: because he wills. There is no cause outside him why he wills, but
there is a cause why he can will. That cause is sin—not effecting actual
desertion, but deserving it and making the sinner worthy of being certainly
deserted if God should will to punish him according to his merit, the willing
of which is left to his free choice.* And elsewhere thus: *Hardening and
blinding are announced to those who refused to obey God when he called.* Hence
sin on man’s part is established as the meritorious cause why this person
rather than that one is not converted; but on God’s part, not God’s absolute
freedom but justice is established as the cause. Therefore, according to our
opinion, if the

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkinian*.]

[1651 logical printed p. 478 | IA leaf 496 | PDF p. 496]

efficacy of grace proceeds from grace itself and not from free choice, God
will be the cause of nonconversion (as Arminius declaims), just as a man is the
cause of a building’s collapse when it is tottering and leaning toward a fall
and he does not place supports under it, or removes supports already placed.

2. But I would have it observed that a similar charge was made against
Pelagius: *These other propositions of Caelestius are now objected against
Pelagius: If by grace we do all things, when we are overcome by sin it is not
we who are overcome but God’s grace, which was either unwilling or unable to
help us in every way. And again he says: If it is God’s grace when we overcome
sins, then he himself is at fault when we are overcome by sin, because he was
either entirely unable or unwilling to preserve us.* Reply 1. Arminius says
that sin is the cause why God can will to desert a sinner who does not will to
be converted. But sin is the cause why God can will to desert a sinner either
by absolute power or by power restricted by justice. Arminius will not say the
first, for no one will say that sin is the cause why God can absolutely will
something. There is no cause outside God for God’s absolute volition; and
even supposing there were no sin, God can absolutely will whatever he
absolutely wills. Nor can sin be said to be the cause why God wills to desert
someone by a will restricted by justice, because then God would desert all
sinners. For no one who was not a sinner is converted from sin, and so God
would transgress against his own justice if he did not desert them all. But
observe, learned reader, the charming

[Marginalia: *On the Proceedings of Pelagius*, ch. 11; Augustine, *To the
Article*.]

[1651 logical printed p. 479 | IA leaf 497 | PDF p. 497]

deception of Arminius. He wants sin to be the cause why God could desert a
sinner—a meritorious cause, that is, not actually effecting desertion but
deserving it, certainly deserving it and certainly effecting it if God should
will to punish the sinner according to his merit. But if by the order of
justice the sinner deserves desertion, the question is why God is unwilling
to punish him according to his merit: either because he is not a sinner, which
destroys the hypothesis, or because God was absolutely unwilling. If it is
because he absolutely does not will to desert the sinner, then God wills to
punish not from justice but from absolute will, which Arminius will loudly
proclaim (I know well enough) to be ἀθεόλογον, unworthy of God. But perhaps he
will say that God is unwilling to punish this sinner according to his merit by
desertion because he punished Christ for him. I answer: say also that he is
unwilling to punish that sinner by desertion because he did not punish Christ
for him; and so Christ did not die or suffer punishment for the one who is
punished by desertion, yet according to Arminius Christ died for each and every
person. But he will say that God is unwilling to punish this man according to
his merit because he is in Christ as a believer, while he wills to punish the
other by desertion because he is not in Christ as a believer. Reply. Then by
his absolute will God cannot now punish an unbeliever by desertion; indeed, he
will not be just unless he punishes the unbeliever, nor will he be just if he
should will to punish the believer in Christ by desertion. 2. He maintains
that sin does not effect actual desertion but deserves it. Let that be so, but
either actual

[1651 logical printed p. 480 | IA leaf 498 | PDF p. 498]

desertion follows sin unavoidably, and thus he adds very badly and foolishly,
[*if God should will to punish the sinner according to his merit.*] What if
God were unwilling to punish according to merit? Then he will be a sinner and
not deserted, and therefore converted. But since to sin in the matter of
conversion is to place an obstacle before God as he converts, it follows that
the one who places an obstacle before God as he converts is converted, and
thus that a man who resists is converted. Or, if actual desertion follows sin
in a way that can be turned aside, it can come about that someone sins and
resists God as he converts, yet is not deserted and is therefore converted;
this is absurd and contrary to Arminius’s justice. 3. Arminius says that
hardening and blinding are announced to those who refuse to obey God when he
calls. It is clear from Arminius’s reply to Perkins in that passage that by
hardening he understands an act of divine justice by which the sinner is not
converted. And so Arminius charmingly wants nonconversion to be announced as
a penalty to those who are not converted. But for Arminius nonconversion
cannot be both penalty and sin. Hence it follows, as that most learned man
William Twiss observed most soundly, that since obeying God when he calls is
believing and repenting, God announces that he will not give faith and
repentance to those who do not believe and repent. What an absurd threat: If
you do not believe, I threaten you that I shall not give you the ability to
believe.

[Marginalia: Twiss, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius, Antiperkinian*,
book 1, part 1, section 3, p. 145.]

3. I would distinguish these four things here. 1. That there is in man a
natural unbelief and

[1651 logical printed p. 481 | IA leaf 499 | PDF p. 499]

obstinacy. 2. The failure to cure native obstinacy. 3. A voluntary love of
native unbelief, from which it comes about that someone actually resists God
when he calls. 4. Faith, which is a supernatural act. Natural unbelief comes
from us, and most writers teach that original obstinacy is the meritorious
cause of nonconversion. Because this is said by men of both great reputation
and equal learning, I do not dare condemn it without qualification. But if it
is the meritorious cause of nonconversion, all men deserve nonconversion, and
so by not converting some God would act contrary to demerit. Yet demerit,
properly speaking, is an evil that by the order of justice God not only can
inflict (since he can absolutely do everything that does not involve a
contradiction) but also ought to inflict. Hence they will be forced to say here
that by converting many God acts contrary to what he ought to do, which is
absurd. Their meaning here, therefore, seems to be that the τὸ *mereri*, the
meriting, is men’s being worthy of nonconversion on the hypothesis of the
divine good pleasure. The sense is that God could justly convert no one if it
had so seemed good to him. Therefore it is in no way due to merit that some
are converted and some are not. Thus God does not act here as just—for in that
case he would convert no one—but as acting from absolute good pleasure. But
Arminius in the *Antiperkinian* maintains that God cannot without injustice
deny someone efficacious motion, whether general or special, if that person is
compliant with grace. I therefore argue thus:

[1651 logical printed p. 482 | IA leaf 500 | PDF p. 500]

Arminius is speaking either of efficacious grace or of sufficient grace. Let
someone, then, be diligent in preserving the grace given. In that case God
cannot without injustice deny him efficacious grace, and so he will
necessarily be diligent in that act. By the same reasoning God cannot deny
him another efficacious motion, and so he will necessarily remain diligent;
thus either God can never deny him efficacious motion, and once diligent he
will never sin, which is absurd, or, if God denies efficacious grace to the
diligent person, God will be unjust and false in fulfilling his promises,
since he does not give efficacious motion to every diligent person. But if he
is speaking of sufficient grace, I say that God then inequitably and unjustly
denies efficacious motion to the diligent person by granting only a sufficient
motion that he foreknows will lack its effect, and thus justice is endangered.
For reason necessarily demands that if God contributes his motion here by the
order of justice, more be granted to the diligent person than to the idle and
sluggish. But it is certain that God sometimes gives efficacious motion to an
idle person, since otherwise he would never repent of his idleness; indeed,
God will grant nothing more here to any earnest and diligent person than to
the obstinate and hardened. For God cannot in justice deny sufficient motion
to the hardened and to all the idle, whether they are vigilant or not.
Otherwise a sinful act could not justly be imputed to them, as the Jesuits and
Arminians acknowledge. If they say that efficacious motion can be resisted,
they

[1651 logical printed p. 483 | IA leaf 501 | PDF p. 501]

contradict themselves. For actual compliance by the person who is moved
efficaciously is included in the essential concept of efficacious motion;
otherwise that motion would be sufficient, not efficacious. 2. I do not see
by what right, on the principles of the Jesuits and Arminians, God could have
given Adam, as he committed the first sin, sufficient and inefficacious motion
but denied him efficacious motion. For if God denied efficacious motion from
justice and from Adam’s demerit, he denied it because of a preceding sin. But
then two inescapable absurdities will press them: 1. There is a sin prior to
the first sin. 2. The same question about that earlier preceding sin must
continually recur without resolution. Consequently they will be forced to say
with us that God causes the willing in us not from a debt of justice but
according to εὐδοκίαν, good pleasure, and because it so pleased him,
Philippians 1:13; Matthew 11:26. 3. If they say that by a debt of justice God
deserted Adam when Adam first deserted him, let us examine this. For they
maintain that God was at hand and held out his motion ready for Adam if Adam
had willed. But what is this, *if Adam had willed*? God then efficaciously
causes willing in Adam provided that Adam himself had willed—how absurd! Next,
God cannot punish the first sinful act by desertion until that sinful act has
first actually existed. For by the order of justice sin is prior to punishment.
But if God’s efficacious motion did not

[1651 logical printed p. 484 | IA leaf 502 | PDF p. 502]

concur in the first sin, and God was only ready to furnish efficacious motion
but was unwilling actually to furnish it because man was unwilling to receive
that motion, it follows that the penalty exists simultaneously in nature with
the sinful act itself. Thus the penalty is not later than the fault, as equity
and the order of justice require; indeed, it follows that Adam simultaneously
sins and is punished by God. But the order of justice requires the punishment
to follow both the sin and the pronouncement of sentence. Perhaps they will
say that Adam’s free non-influence existed prior in nature to the denial of
divine motion, and therefore Adam’s sin of deserting God is prior to divine
desertion. But I answer that Adam’s free non-influence, at the moment in which
God’s non-motion first existed, is not sin in its complete being. For if
Adam’s sinful act is prior in nature to God’s non-influence, then God’s
influence is in no way the cause of a good act. For who is so dull-witted as
to say that a cause is by nature later than its effect? 4. It is certain that
Adam obeyed God for a time and performed acts of obedience. By what right,
then, could God not reward those acts in later acts by efficacious motion,
according to the principle Arminius presses as consonant with God’s equity?
After some pious acts of obedience, was God bound by justice to give Adam only
sufficient motion, which he foresaw would be in no way efficacious? Here they
will be forced to say that from absolute freedom and not from

[1651 logical printed p. 485 | IA leaf 503 | PDF p. 503]

justice God denied a motion that he foreknew would be efficacious, despite no
demerit of Adam. The same must be said about the fall of the angels. For our
present subject, we must reason in the same way about the nonconversion of a
man who was upright, pious, and humble continually up to the act of
nonconversion. By what right does God deny an efficacious motion to a humble
and upright man because of a free conversion not actively made, which does
not yet exist as a complete act? For God does not punish a sin that does not
yet fully exist. And why are pious acts of humility and preceding uprightness
not rewarded with efficacious motion according to foreknowledge? 5. But I
still do not see why Judas’s nonconversion rather than Peter’s has a
meritorious cause, nor how Judas’s obstinate placement of an obstacle can be
such a cause. For the obstinate placement of the impediment is Judas’s active,
obstinate nonconversion, and so the meritorious cause of Judas’s nonconversion
will be nonconversion. What? Will the same thing be its own cause? Alvarez
says here: *Because a man does not will to use exciting or sufficient grace,
he therefore is not converted.* Thus he casts privative or sinful
nonconversion upon the will of the man who does not will to use exciting
grace. But to use exciting grace is to obey God as he morally excites a man
to conversion, and thus to believe and convert oneself. Therefore the reason
a man is not converted is that he does not convert himself—how absurd! But he
casts the

[Marginalia: Alvarez, l. 102.]

[1651 logical printed p. 486 | IA leaf 504 | PDF p. 504]

negative lack of conversion upon God as not converting and as denying
efficacious grace. But for this William Twiss most deservedly thrashes Alvarez
at great length. 5. It is clear that the consciences of the Arminians waver
when they flee for refuge to God’s justice, for we heard above that Corvinus
acknowledged that he did not know the cause of the different outcome—why this
person is converted while that one, furnished with the same prevenient grace,
is not converted—and that it was not placed in free choice alone. Arminius
himself also strives to enter another way, but plainly with a wavering and
protesting conscience. *But* (he says) *this internal persuasion of the Holy
Spirit is twofold, one sufficient and the other efficacious. By the former,
the person to whom it is applied can will, believe, and be converted; by the
latter, the person to whom it is adapted wills, believes, and is converted.
The former is applied by the decree of providence, with certain foreknowledge
that it will be rejected by man’s free choice. The latter is administered
from the decree of predestination, with certain foreknowledge that the person
to whom it is adapted and applied will in fact will, believe, and be
converted, because it is so adapted as God knows to be congruous for
persuading and converting the one to whom it is applied.* There he denies that
sufficient grace is properly denied by the decree of reprobation, but says
that efficacious grace is denied by that decree; and he says this is from the
mind of Augustine, and perhaps even from the sense of Scripture. Yet Arminius
does not attempt to establish it by even a stroke or a tiny letter from
Augustine or Scripture, and he preferred to leave both himself and his reader
among hiding places and conjectures. But he asserts this far less

[Marginalia: Twiss, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius, Antiperkinian*,
book 1, part 2, digression 2, pp. 149–151.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkinian*, p. 665.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Conference with Junius*, proposition 15, pp. 563–564.]

[1651 logical printed p. 487 | IA leaf 505 | PDF p. 505]

hesitantly in his conference with Dr. Junius—indeed, rather dictatorially.
Thus Arminius does not simply maintain, but perhaps even maintains from the
sense of Scripture, that the reason this person rather than that one is
converted lies in free choice, according to the different manner of adapting
God’s grace, about which elsewhere. Therefore Arminius perhaps maintains that
an absolute decree of predestination and a congruous calling, not according
to the rule of justice but according to God’s absolute will, is no less
consonant with Scripture than the Arminian opinion. 6. If the Arminian calling
that arises from God’s will restricted by justice stands, God cannot fail to
convert Peter when he wills to be converted, and by the order of justice he
cannot convert Judas when he does not will to be converted, especially since
Judas’s final rebellion has not been expiated by Christ’s merit; and thus both
things could come about even against God’s will. Nor does he here have
mercy on whom he wills any more than God is just because he wills. For he is
just by necessity of nature, and by necessity of nature he will convert those
who will to be converted.
