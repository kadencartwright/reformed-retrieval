CHAPTER IV

*The manner in which contingency and the absolute decree agree; the arguments
of the Arminians, Suárez, Vázquez, Molina, Lessius, and Fonseca are called to
examination.*

[1651 logical printed p. 104 | IA leaf 122 | PDF p. 122]

1. It scarcely seems safe, especially for a novice, to descend into this
arena, unless it were better to suffer the loss and ruin of one's reputation
than of the truth. First, then, I wish four states of things to be observed.
The first is the state of nonbeing, or of what is simply impossible. God draws
no beings out of this state, because what is simply impossible is not an
object of omnipotence. 2. The second state is the most extensive, namely the
state of possibility, in which all future contingents, and all things both
necessary and contingent that will never be future, have being—that is, the
absence of repugnance to their being—in relation, namely, to God's omnipotence.
Even supposing that there had never been a decree in God to produce any beings
outside himself, all things that are now possible would in that case be
possible. For possibles are antecedent to God's will; in this manner all
contingents are antecedent to God's volitions. And, in the order of reason,
something is by nature possible before it is future, while being future adds
something to being possible, just as a species adds something to a genus. In
this sense contingent things, considered simply, can be or not be; and even
all the most necessary things can be or not be. But the third state of things
is the state of futurition, in which possible things receive future being from
God's decree. The possible, therefore, is the terminus from which; the future,
the terminus to which; and futurition the intermediate movement by which

[1651 logical printed p. 105 | IA leaf 123 | PDF p. 123]

a possible thing passes from the state of possibility to the state of
futurition. Accordingly, just as things are possible in relation to some cause
(otherwise they would be impossible, and this cause is omnipotence), so also
possible things are carried from the state of possibility to the state of
futurition, and are removed farther from nonbeing, through some cause. Now
this cause of futurition is either in a created being or in God. It
cannot be in a created being, for it would be either in the future being itself
or in another created being. It is not in the future being itself, because,
just as a present being does not have present being from itself, since it does
not have being simply from itself, and the past does not have past being from
itself, so neither does the future have future being from itself. For then it
would be both its own cause and both prior and posterior to itself, which is
absurd. Furthermore, since the future is from eternity, it does not have
future being from another created being, because there was no created being
from eternity that could be the cause of a future that is eternal. Therefore,
the cause of futurition must be in God. It is not in God's intellect, because
God does not make beings by understanding beings, since he also understands
nonbeings; nor is God's omnipotence the cause of the future, but only of the
possible. Therefore, it must necessarily be said that God's will, as freely
decreeing, is the cause of this passage from the state of possibility to the
state of futurition. The fourth state of things is the state of actualization,
in which things pass from the state of futurition to

[1651 logical printed p. 106 | IA leaf 124 | PDF p. 124]

the state of actualization; and the cause of this passage is the will in God,
armed with omnipotence.

2. I observe that the future being of that which exists, measured by God's
will, corresponds most exactly to the present being which that future thing
has and receives in time. Thus future being and present being are one and the
same being and differ only in the order of duration. Hence, the being that a
contingent effect has in time is nothing other than that very same future being
which it was to have from eternity through the free decree of the divine will.
And the possible never passes to the state of actualization except through the
intermediate state of futurition. Therefore, a contingent possible can in no
way pass immediately to the state of actualization, for then something would
be present which was not future from eternity. This is the same as some created
being's existing in actuality which was never in potency, and that is simply
impossible. The possible, therefore, is what can be or not be—that is, that to
which being is not repugnant. But a contingent possible is that which in fact
and in actuality can be or not be, not simply, but with futurition preceding.
Accordingly, although possibilities exceed actualities in number, yet the
number of future contingents and of contingent effects in actuality and in
fact is plainly one and the same, and the former do not exceed the latter even
by one.

3. From this I observe, third, that if it were posited, per impossibile, that
there were no absolute decree in God determining future contingents,

[1651 logical printed p. 107 | IA leaf 125 | PDF p. 125]

a contingent cause would not in fact determinately produce any effect other
than the one which from eternity had a futurition corresponding most exactly
to it. And a contingent effect that had no corresponding futurition could not
exist, because it is impossible for any being to be present which was not
future from eternity. Therefore, everyone sees that all contingent effects
which occur in time are in fact limited by eternal futurition. But if God's
decree, supervening upon that same futurition which corresponds to the
contingent effect that in fact comes about, determines it, the decree no more
overthrows the nature of a contingent cause than if the contingent cause alone
had determined itself antecedently to God's decree, eternal futurition having
preceded. Let human freedom, then, be inclined toward willing A, B, C, D, E,
and actually about to will C, not A, not B, not D, not E. The actual volition
of C alone had an eternal futurition corresponding to that volition of C. But
the volition of A, B, D, E had no futurition from eternity, nor could the will
actually will A, B, D, E unless the eternal futurition of that volition
preceded. For, according to our second observation, this is impossible. If,
then, God's absolute decree determines from eternity the will as freely
inclining and determining itself to will C—a volition to which eternal
futurition corresponded on the hypothesis that there were no absolute decree
in God—and that same decree does not

[1651 logical printed p. 108 | IA leaf 126 | PDF p. 126]

determine from eternity the will as freely refusing to will A, B, D, E, it is
entirely evident that God's absolute decree harms the nature of freedom no more
than if, per impossibile, there were no such decree in God. Therefore, let the
reader turn his eyes to the following diagram:

```text
God's absolute   ⎧ Able to    ⎧ A, a possible eternally future
decree            ⎪ choose     ⎨ B, a possible eternally nonfuture
                  ⎨            ⎩
                  ⎩ Choosing     C, possible and eternally future
                  ⎫            ⎧ D, a possible eternally nonfuture
Human freedom     ⎪ Able to    ⎨
                  ⎩ choose     ⎩ E, a possible eternally future.
```

4. The necessity from the hypothesis that freedom is now actually operating
and the necessity from the hypothesis of the absolute decree, although they
are necessities of different kinds with respect to the terminus and principle
from which, are nevertheless the same with respect to the terminus to which. I
demonstrate it as follows. These propositions are equivalent:

*People insofar as they are subject to God's absolute decree cannot will or
choose anything other than what they actually and in fact will or choose.*

*People in the actual exercise of freedom cannot will or choose anything other
than what they actually will or choose.*

Proof: those necessities are the same with respect to terminus and object
which remove the same indifferences of freedom. But the necessity from the
hypothesis of the absolute decree and the necessity from the hypothesis of
actual exercise remove the same—indeed, one and the same—indifference of human
freedom. Therefore, with respect to terminus and object they are

[1651 logical printed p. 109 | IA leaf 127 | PDF p. 127]

the same necessities. The major premise is certain from the opposition
between determination made by necessity and indifference. The assumption is
proved because the necessity of the decree removes indifference toward
willing A, B, D, E, just as freedom itself removes the same indifference and
no other. But in this our opponents both deceive and are deceived: they wish
it to flow from the force of the absolute decree that, of itself and with
respect to futurition, the free will would actually be inclined into this
corner and would be about to will A, B, D, E, but is twisted against its
innate power by a fatal decree to will C. We, however, utterly deny that human
freedom is twisted by the force of the decree, or by fatality, from that
original and native corner of its co-inclination to another corner, or to the
actualization of what is as it were another part of its potency. Rather, it is
actualized toward that same part which was to be actualized from eternity,
with eternal futurition preceding, and toward that very same part and no other
which human freedom itself would have embraced of itself, given, per
impossibile, that there had been no absolute decree in God and no providence
over things.

5. But I see that two things can be objected here. 1. They would say that here
I am now positing no other necessity in the free cause from the decree than a
concomitant necessity, one that leaves the free potency with complete
indifference, although in words I posit an antecedent necessity. For if God's
decree, when its executive power arrives in time, removes the same

[1651 logical printed p. 110 | IA leaf 128 | PDF p. 128]

indifference and particular potency which the actual exercise of freedom
removes, then, since actual exercise indifferently removes either this or that
potency, it follows that the decree removes either this or that indifference
and no other, and thus that free acts are not precisely determined by the
absolute decree. Reply. It does not follow. For although freedom in a most
free act removes either this or that potency, nevertheless it does not remove
both this and that. It removes only the one which was not to be actualized from
eternity and posits the one which was to be actualized and to which eternal
futurition corresponded. The decree, however, precisely determines that free
act and its futurition, since antecedently, with respect to the first vital and
elective motion, it places the free will in that vital situation which regards
the position of that natural inclination (so to speak) in which that same
particular potency is actualized—the one, and no other, which God from eternity
absolutely willed and decreed to be actualized, and which freedom, subordinately
and dependently upon God, wills to be actualized. Thus, suppose a certain
courtier secretly resolves, without the king's knowledge, to travel to a
distant country, and the king resolves within himself to send that same man as
an envoy to that country. The matter then succeeds according to both their
wishes: the one sends him by royal command, and the other freely makes the
journey there, although the king had pressed him by no such decree or command.
In this case, the king both absolutely determines that journey and the courtier
makes the journey most freely. So God absolutely

[1651 logical printed p. 111 | IA leaf 129 | PDF p. 129]

determines this free act and not another, and places freedom in that vital
situation in which it produces that free act and no other—the very act which,
with respect to eternal futurition, freedom was going most freely to produce.
Thus it rests with God's providence that such an act exists, not another, and
that there are not more or fewer; and it also rests with the will, but
subordinately and dependently, that such acts exist, neither more nor fewer.

6. It can be objected that if such a decree were not posited, there would be no
necessity of futurition, and true natural indifference would remain, because
the necessity of futurition is merely hypothetical and flows from created
freedom. For because created freedom is freely going to will this, there is
therefore an eternal futurition of such a volition. For, once it is posited
that a thing will be future, it must be future, just as a present thing must be
present. But in the order of reason antecedent to futurition, freedom is
absolutely indifferent toward willing this or that. Because, however, it is
going to will this and not that, eternal futurition therefore preceded. Reply.
1. Contrary to all sound reason, freedom freely willing in time is made the
cause of eternal futurition. For nothing temporal is the cause of what is
eternal, because a cause is prior to its effect, whereas the temporal is not
prior but posterior to the eternal. 2. We grant that the necessity of
futurition is only hypothetical, not absolute, but it is nevertheless an
antecedent necessity with respect to the free act. This suffices for us to
prove that antecedent necessity does not conflict with

[1651 logical printed p. 112 | IA leaf 130 | PDF p. 130]

a free act, unless it is a necessity by whose force freedom is torn or twisted
away from what it was naturally going to will toward what it was not going to
will. This is not true of necessity from the hypothesis of the decree. 3.
Since futurition is not mere nothing (for the future and the nonfuture differ
by something), they cannot assign any cause of the tendency from the possible
to the state of actualization other than God's free will. 4. Even in the order
of reason antecedent to futurition, a free potency is indeed indifferent to
refusing or willing the same thing disjunctively, but not to refusing and
willing the same thing conjunctively. But a disjunctive potency, in itself and
intrinsically, regards only one of the opposites. Thus by reason of the matter
the potency intrinsically regards one, while indifference only formally
implies that willing A belongs no more to freedom's natural manner of acting
than refusing A. Accordingly, the potency intrinsically regards only that
opposite which, through the positing of futurition, will tend from the state
of possibility to the state of actualization. And because God's decree
determines the potency to that same object in relation to which it will stand
in the order of reason subsequent to futurition, who does not see that here
the decree agrees perfectly with human freedom? God's dominion consists in
this: that he absolutely predetermines this act and not another, and that he so
places the will that, by its innate elective power and by a vital influx that
is indifferent

[1651 logical printed p. 113 | IA leaf 131 | PDF p. 131]

(as far as freedom's natural manner of acting is concerned), the will issues
into that numerically same object, and no other, which God precisely decreed.

7. As a fourth point, I also observe that human freedom has a twofold potency,
universal and particular. The former is essential to freedom: by its natural
manner of acting, freedom regards refusing no more than willing, choosing no
more than rejecting, or suspending its act. The latter is accidental, coming
to or departing from freedom while its essence remains intact. This
distinction of potency cannot be denied. Whoever denies the will's potency for
refusing, willing, or choosing in general denies the potency of freedom. But I
prove that there is a particular potency. Before Peter debates tomorrow, he is
in potency to debate tomorrow. But once tomorrow's debate has passed, such a
potency perishes, and he is no longer in potency for that debate, since there
is no potency for a past act. 2. A particular potency corresponds to every
particular act. But there are many particular acts in the will. Therefore,
there are also many potencies. 3. Just as the philosophers with very good
reason posit this twofold potency in prime matter, so with equal right there
are such potencies here. Hence, I say that God's decree does not remove
freedom's universal potency, since it is essential to it. But freedom's
particular potencies are determined by God from eternity. Proof. 1. All acts,
since they are determinate entities, are predetermined by God

[1651 logical printed p. 114 | IA leaf 132 | PDF p. 132]

through an absolute decree; otherwise they would occur outside God's
intention and would not be subject to providence. Therefore, particular
potencies are also determined. The consequence is proved because opposites
are multiplied in correspondence with the multiplication of their opposites.
2. These particular potencies are determinate beings; therefore, they are
absolutely predetermined by God. Otherwise they too would escape providence.
3. These potencies have determinate and individual being; therefore, they must
have such being from the first being. It follows from this that freedom is not
in particular potency for more or fewer acts than those which it actually
exercises, although it is in universal potency for all possible acts. But they
will say that the will is thus bound by the antecedent decree so that it cannot
do otherwise than it does. Reply. Notwithstanding the decree, by its universal
potency it can do otherwise and will, refuse, and choose things other than
those which it in fact wills, refuses, or chooses. But you will say that by its
particular potency the will cannot act or not act. Reply. It is no burden at
all to grant this, since the will itself most freely removes such a potency by
inclining itself into the act which, with respect to futurition, was to be
produced from eternity—a futurition which it had from God's absolute decree.
But because the will, by its free exercise, removes that same particular
potency which God's decree removes, freedom is no more harmed by the antecedent
decree than by its own actual exercise of freedom. But if God's decree

[1651 logical printed p. 115 | IA leaf 133 | PDF p. 133]

removed another particular potency, namely the potency for willing this,
while the will itself by that particular potency inclined toward refusing it,
then the antecedent decree would harm freedom. But this is falsely attributed
to us by our opponents.

8. They also object that necessity from the decree is not hypothetical but
absolute, and consequently they argue as follows that it overthrows freedom.
Hypothetical necessity is the necessity by which a thing otherwise contingent
or free cannot fail to be on the hypothesis that it is, and therefore it posits
the existence of the contingent thing. But the necessity of the decree does
not posit that the free act is already posited or exists antecedently to the
decree. On the contrary, because the decree is prior, the free act will
therefore necessarily be. Reply. 1. That definition of hypothetical necessity
is far too narrow and confined. For necessity is called hypothetical not only
when a contingent thing is posited as already existing, but when some
antecedent is posited from which, once it is posited, the contingent thing
will necessarily be future according to the manner of its nature—namely,
contingently. Thus free acts are necessary on the hypothesis of the divine
decree, which posits not that free acts already exist, but that by the force of
the decree they will exist most freely. The reason is evident, since necessity
from the decree posits that the free cause, by a vital and elective influx
arising from the judgment of reason, will issue into precisely such a corner
of opposition as it would freely issue into on the supposition that

[1651 logical printed p. 116 | IA leaf 134 | PDF p. 134]

there were no decree but that eternal futurition alone preceded, per
impossibile. I say that eternal futurition would precede *per impossibile*,
because it is impossible that there should have been any futurition of all
things from eternity except through God's free will. What follows? If the
futurition of all things had existed from eternity, then, against God's will
and contrary to it, the world and all beings which are now future would exist
necessarily, not freely, in relation to the divine decree. Thus God would have
created the world by a necessity of nature. By the same reasoning the
nonfuturition of all possible beings would have to be posited antecedently to
God's decree, and thus Jehovah produced all beings by a fatal
necessity, so that it was simply impossible for him not to produce them and
impossible to produce either more or fewer than have been produced. For what
is already future prior to, or contrary to, God's willing it to be future
cannot, even through God's omnipotence, not be future. For omnipotence cannot
make an identical proposition false or make the future not future. And here
let the reader behold the church's most wretched fortunes: see where we are
brought by the subtler sophisms of those who prefer people to be free rather
than God and meanwhile overthrow human freedom and turn it into a fate which
not even God can overcome.

9. But the enemies of grace shamefully go astray in this: from the ungrateful
and proud exaltation of the created will, they fail to recognize God's most
wise and omnipotent dominion over all created beings,

[1651 logical printed p. 117 | IA leaf 135 | PDF p. 135]

as though God could not penetrate into the depth of created freedom without
injuring its nature. Freedom hangs toward four corners; with respect to
eternal futurition it will actually and most freely issue into the third of
those four corners, and not into any of the remaining three. They suppose God
cannot antecedently, by his absolute decree, precisely determine it to that
third corner. We, indeed, if God were a finite agent, would readily grant that
he could not antecedently determine the free will precisely to one act while
passing over the other possibles. But they would grant us that God can
absolutely predetermine free acts if only we would grant them middle
knowledge, because through his infinite knowledge antecedent to his decree
God knows the futurition of contingent acts. They say this in words, however;
they do not prove it by the weight of arguments. We, on the other hand, say
that God, by virtue of his infinity and his absolute and wise dominion over
creatures, absolutely decrees all contingent acts and antecedently determines
the contingent cause toward that part of its original and radical
co-inclination toward which it will contingently determine itself. Unless this
is said, we cannot see or understand how he administers all things by his
infinite providence and wise dominion.

10. Accordingly, 1. the question is not whether free effects come about
contingently or whether they are contingent in themselves.

[1651 logical printed p. 118 | IA leaf 136 | PDF p. 136]

For contingent effects are contingent both essentially and antecedently to
God's decree, no less than a human being is an animal. 2. The question is not
whether they come about necessarily from God's decree, as though the decree
infused necessity into contingent effects, as Thomas Jackson speaks absurdly;
but whether they come about necessarily on the hypothesis of the absolute
decree—that is, after God has absolutely decreed that contingent acts are to
exist, whether they then come about in such a way that under that supposition
they could fail to occur. For God's decree does not make the cause itself
necessary by any necessity other than the one which freedom itself makes.
Thus the chief question is whether a free cause, insofar as it is subject to
the decree, can fail to act when it is determined to act through the decree.
For even as subject to the decree, it can by its universal potency fail to act
and can act contrary to that toward which it is determined, since the decree
in no way removes universal potency. It may therefore be said that, if
universal potency remains under the decree, then nothing impossible would be
posited if it were reduced to act, according to Aristotle's definition of the
contingent: *that which, though it is not necessary to exist, if it exists,
nothing impossible will result on that account.* Consequently, nothing
impossible would result if freedom, determined by the decree toward this side,
determined itself toward another and contrary side. Therefore, either such a
decree will be frustrated and rescinded, which must not be said, or such a
decree does not determine freedom precisely toward one side. Reply. The
potency of the free will is never reduced to act of itself and immediately,

[Marginalia: Aristotle, *Prior Analytics*, bk. 1, ch. 13, sec. 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 119 | IA leaf 137 | PDF p. 137]

but always by the mediation of a particular potency. Now a particular potency
could not be reduced to an act contrary to the act and decree determined. For
this conflicts with the hypothesis of the divine decree and indeed conflicts
with its own nature, because the decree determines the particular potency to
the act into which it will itself freely issue, and not into another. No sane
person would say that the will can fail to will what it is actually going to
will, once it is actually going to will it. For absolute necessity, or the
necessity of the consequent, as Antonius Andreae rightly defines it, is *the
necessity of the thing itself which consists in the essential dependence of
cause and effect, namely when a thing follows necessarily from an antecedent
cause.* But every contingent effect is from God's decree in such a way that it
nevertheless could have failed to be. For Peter's volition is not from God's
decree as light is from the sun or heat from fire, just as that volition of
Peter is not in such a manner from Peter's freedom. I therefore always
maintain that God's decree precisely determines the created will toward the
side toward which it will freely determine itself. But you will say that God's
decree does not for that reason determine free potency toward the side toward
which the will can determine itself; rather, toward whatever side the will
determines itself, it could have determined itself toward the contrary side.
Reply. 1. It is ridiculous to say that God's decree determines the will toward
the side toward which it can incline. For it can incline itself toward all
possible acts, nor does freedom itself determine itself to possible acts. 2.
Toward

[Marginalia: Antonius Andreae, distinction 5, *Metaphysics*, question 4.]

[1651 logical printed p. 120 | IA leaf 138 | PDF p. 138]

whatever side the will may determine itself, supposing that it determines
itself to an act contrary to the one which God's decree has now determined
from eternity, I then say that the antecedent decree ought to have bestowed
eternal futurition upon that possible act, by which it would be raised from
the state of possibility to the state of futurition. 3. If we speak strictly,
God's decree, since it is an immanent act in God, posits nothing in freedom and
therefore does not determine it. God's determination, however, is a transient
and temporal act. Nevertheless, we say that the decree determines freedom
because God absolutely decreed that the will should determine itself in time
when God's executive power arrived. From this I first say that these
propositions are false: 1. God decreed that contingent effects should come
about necessarily. 2. God decreed that free acts should not be able not to be.
These are true: God decreed that contingent effects should come about in such
a way that they could fail to come about, namely, when the supposition of
eternal futurition and of the divine decree is offered. 3. God decreed that
precisely those free acts, and no others, should exist which freedom freely
elicits in time. Hence, let these propositions also be observed. 1. Peter will
believe because God decreed that he should believe most freely through the
motive power of efficacious grace. The same must be said of unbelievers in a
sound sense, as we shall explain later. 2. Because Peter is going to believe,
God therefore decreed that he should believe freely. This is true, but from
effect, not from cause, because God decreed antecedently

[1651 logical printed p. 121 (partial) | IA leaf 139 | PDF p. 139]

to Peter's volitions the act toward which Peter was, with respect to eternal
futurition, going freely to determine himself. And this is the property of
wondrous wisdom and omnipotence: that God should penetrate so deeply into the
nature of a contingent cause that he draws out that actualization of natural
co-inclination, and only that one and no other, which the contingent cause
contingently desires to have drawn out. In our homeland we shall see these
things more luminously and clearly; meanwhile, we believe them.
