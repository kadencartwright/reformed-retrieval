QUESTION XIII.

THE FOURTH COMMANDMENT.

Is the first institution of the Sabbath in the Fourth Commandment, and is the
commandment partly moral and partly ceremonial? We deny the former and affirm
the latter.

[Marginal heading: The etymology of the word Sabbath.]

I. To make this most vexed question concerning the Sabbath easier to
understand, certain things must first be stated, both concerning the
etymology of the name

[Printed p. 69 | Internet Archive PDF 97 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and concerning its various meanings. The true origin of the word—for there is
no need to recount the empty and absurd conjectures of various writers—is to
be sought nowhere else than from the verb שבת, that is, *to cease* or *to
rest*. It is referred either to the *existence of a thing*, when it ceases to
be and fails, as is said of the manna (Josh. 5:12) and of joy (Isa. 24:8), or
to the operation of an agent, when he ceases to work. It is used for *he
rested* and *ceased from work*, which is the proper meaning in this passage
and is indicated in Genesis 2:3, when God is said to have blessed the seventh
day because בו שבת, *on it he had rested*. Therefore that day is called שבת
and יום שבת. The Greeks render it by παύειν, ἀναπαύειν, and καταπαύειν, and
the noun שבת by ἀνάπαυσιν, as Josephus does in *Against Apion*, book 2, and the
Septuagint more than once. Elsewhere they retain the Hebrew word, as do the
New Testament writers, who refrained from translating it as a thing very well
known and so that it might be distinguished from profane and ordinary rest,
as was done with very many other Hebrew words: *Hosanna, Pascha, Immanuel*,
and so forth.

[Marginal heading: Its various meanings.]

II. The *use and meaning* of the name is manifold. The proper and primary
meaning, on which all the others depend and which is understood here, is that
in which it is used for every seventh day of each week. This arose from the
event, because, when the creation of things had been completed, God ceased
from creating new species and willed that this day should thereafter be
appointed for human rest in memory of the event, so that on it human beings
would not only cease from bodily and profane works in imitation of God but
also devote themselves to divine worship. From this, for the same reason, all
the solemn feasts of the Jews are designated by the name Sabbath, even though
they do not fall on the seventh day (Lev. 23:32 and elsewhere), because they
were celebrated in nearly the same manner as the weekly Sabbath.

Third, the first and last days of any feast that lasted several days are
called a Sabbath, because both were equally solemn. To this belongs the
δευτερόπρωτον Sabbath in Luke 6:1, the “second-first.” It was so called either
because it came next after the solemn Passover and was ἀπὸ τῆς δευτέρας
πρῶτον, *the first from the day after the Passover*, in the reckoning of
Pentecost, as the great Scaliger maintains; or because it was the final day of
the feast, which, as it was the later and second Sabbath in order, was equally
first or great in dignity with the first day of the feast. That first day was
solemn because of the public assembly gathered on it, so that you might call
the last day the first repeated a second time. This explanation pleases our
Beza and others more.

Fourth, Sabbath is also taken by synecdoche for the week itself, which the
seventh day closes. In Luke 18:12, νηστεύω δὶς τοῦ σαββάτου means “I fast
twice in the week,” and in 1 Corinthians 16:2 μία σαββάτων is used for the
first day of the week.

[Marginal heading: Three kinds of Sabbath: temporal, spiritual, and eternal.]

III. Again, three kinds of *Sabbath* are observed in Scripture: temporal,
spiritual, and eternal and heavenly. The *temporal* Sabbath is that which God
prescribed to believers under the Old Testament. This in turn was either
*annual*: every seventh year, in which he commanded the Israelites to leave
the land uncultivated so that it too might have its rest (Lev. 25:2), and the
forty-ninth year, which was the year of Jubilee and a Sabbath of weeks of
years (Lev. 25:8); or *monthly*: the new moons, or the first day of each
month; or *weekly*, which is the subject here. Second, the *spiritual* Sabbath
consists in the rest of conscience enjoyed by believers and in ceasing from
the works of sin, to which they ought to apply themselves throughout the
whole course of life, as Hebrews 4:1, 3 indicates. Third, the *eternal and
heavenly* Sabbath is that in which, received into heaven and most perfectly
freed both from sins and from the labors and troubles of this life, we rest
eternally in God. Hebrews 4:9 speaks of this: “There still remains a Sabbath
rest for the people of God,” namely, the heavenly rest under which eternal
happiness is customarily represented.

[Marginal heading: The first question, concerning the origin of the Sabbath.]

IV. Two chief matters concerning the Sabbath are asked and contained in this
question. The former concerns the first origin or institution of the Sabbath;
the latter concerns its nature and use, or what kind of commandment it is,
whether moral or ceremonial. Both matters are disputed among theologians and
even today are debated with great heat of mind. Here, in a few words and
subject to the judgment of the more learned, we shall disclose what seems to
us must be held.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

V. As to the former, the question does not concern the author of the Sabbath.
Everyone agrees that its institution must be referred to God alone. Rather,
it concerns the beginning and origin of this institution: whether it is to be
derived from the very infancy of the world or referred only to the
promulgation of the law on Mount Sinai. Most of the Reformed embrace the
former position; see their catalogue in Walaeus and Rivet. Some others follow
the latter. Although each position rests upon reasons that are not to be
despised, and the disagreement is not fundamental, we nevertheless judge the
first to be truer and more consistent with the words of Scripture, and we
also adopt it, relying especially upon the following arguments.

[Marginal heading: The institution of the Sabbath before the law is proved.
1. From Genesis 2:3.]

VI. First, immediately after creation was completed, God is said to have
blessed and sanctified the seventh day: “And God blessed the seventh day and
sanctified it, because on it he had rested from all his work which God had
created” (Gen. 2:3). These words seem clearer than to be bent toward the
opposing position by any distinction. For God cannot be said to have blessed
and sanctified the seventh day except by the institution of the Sabbath. That
day was blessed for no other end than that it might be consecrated to the
worship of God in memory of the divine rest from the works of creation. God
is therefore said to have blessed and sanctified it because he blessed it by
sanctifying it, separating it from profane and common use and dedicating it
to divine worship, so that it might thereafter be sanctified by human beings
through public exercises of piety and the solemn worship of God.

VII. In vain it is objected, first, that Moses said these things by
anticipation in order to show the *equity* of the commandment, not its
*beginning*, so that God is said to have sanctified this day not when he
ceased from his works but when he gave the manna or gave the law through
Moses. We answer that although Scripture sometimes uses anticipation, one
must never resort to it unless necessity compels. Here no necessity appears
that recommends an anticipation, and many reasons prevent its admission.

First, just as God is said properly and historically, not proleptically, to
have rested from his works on this seventh day, so he is said to have blessed
and sanctified it. These matters are set forth in one continuous account and
must be understood of the same seventh day of creation, not another similar
day, as is also indicated by the demonstrative article ה repeated three
times. Second, on the assumption of an anticipation, the sense of Moses’
words will be this: after completing the work of six days God rested on the
seventh day; therefore, after two thousand four hundred and fifty-three years
he blessed a similar day and sanctified it for sacred uses. Everyone sees how
harsh and forced this is. Third, no solid reason can be given why Moses, in a
simple historical narrative of events that had occurred two thousand years
earlier, would have wished to insert something that was done only in his own
time. It would plainly have tended to obscure rather than explain the history
he was composing, while not adding even the slightest indication of that
anticipation.

VIII. Second, it is objected that this sanctification and blessing must be
understood with regard to intention and appointment, not execution. We
answer: God is said to have blessed the day in the same manner in which he
rested on it. But he did not rest only

[Printed p. 71 | Internet Archive PDF 99 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

in intention, because he intended to do so, but in fact, because he ceased
from his works. Second, the appointment was not first made on this day but
from eternity. On this argument God could be said to have sanctified the days
of Passover and Pentecost because he then had in mind that he would sanctify
them. Third, it is objected: “Although God then blessed the seventh day, it
does not follow that he then prescribed the observance of this day to Adam.”
God does not immediately command everything that he blesses to be kept;
blessing and sanctifying are actions of God, not ours, while the commandment
concerns human actions. We answer: since the blessing and sanctification were
not made with respect to God but to man, it is evident that God sanctified
this day—that is, separated it from common use and dedicated it to sacred
use—for no other end than that it should be religiously observed by man.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Exodus 16:23, 25.]

IX. Second, in Exodus 16:23 the Sabbath is mentioned as already known and
solemn among the Israelites before the very promulgation of the law, in the
giving of the manna. They are commanded to gather a double measure on the
sixth day, by which they might be fed on the sixth and seventh days, the
latter being the Sabbath of the Lord. “This is,” he says, “what Jehovah has
declared: tomorrow is rest, a holy Sabbath to Jehovah. Bake what you will bake
today,” and in verse 26: “For six days you shall gather it, but on the seventh
day, the Sabbath, there will be none”; and in verse 29: “Consider that God has
given you the Sabbath. Let each person remain in his place; let no one go out
from his place,” namely, to gather manna. “Thus the people rested on the
seventh day.” This could not be said unless the Sabbath had already then been
instituted and commanded by God.

Nor should it be objected that it was instituted only here, when the Lord
says that *tomorrow will be the Sabbath*. For however it is read—whether
“tomorrow is,” as most interpreters translate it, or in the future, “tomorrow
will be,” as some others render it, neither of which is in the Hebrew—this
does not denote the first institution of the Sabbath, but only its
confirmation and an exhortation to observe it properly. Nor ought so solemn a
day to have been instituted so obscurely; it ought to have had a solemn and
clear declaration of the divine commandment.

[Marginal heading: 3. From Exodus 20:8.]

X. Third, the words of the fourth commandment, “Remember the Sabbath day, to
sanctify it,” clearly indicate that this law was not first given then, but was
only renewed after it had, as it were, fallen into disuse, so that they would
apply particular care in this matter and would not afterward consign this
commandment to oblivion, as they had done before. The following words confirm
this, for they repeat the reason for the institution given in Genesis 2,
concerning God’s rest and the blessing and sanctification of the seventh day.
This sufficiently indicates that the Sabbath was instituted when that reason
had its place. Hence Calvin says on Exodus 20:11: “From this passage it is
elicited by a probable conjecture that the holiness of the Sabbath preceded
the law. And certainly what Moses related before, that they were forbidden to
gather manna on the seventh day, appears to have been drawn from received
knowledge and practice. And since God delivered to the saints the rite of
sacrifice, it is not credible that the observance of the Sabbath was omitted.”

[Marginal heading: 4. From Hebrews 4:3, 4.]

XI. Fourth, in Hebrews 4:3–4, when the apostle is about to prove that a
Sabbath rest—that is, a rest into which believers will enter through faith—
still remains for the people of God, while unbelievers are to be excluded
from it because of unbelief, he shows that the words of Psalm 95 which he
quotes, “I swore in my wrath, They shall never enter my rest,” must
necessarily be referred to it. For Scripture speaks of only three Sabbath
rests of God: first, after the work of creation; second, after the people were
brought into the land of Canaan; third, with respect to the spiritual and
eternal rest of believers and the blessed. The words of David cannot be
referred either to the first rest of God, into which he entered from the
beginning and which he

[Printed p. 72 | Internet Archive PDF 100 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

prescribed to human beings by his example, or to the second, which was
procured for them by Joshua’s introduction of the people into the land of
Canaan, because both had long since passed. They must therefore necessarily
be understood of that third rest promised to believers in the gospel through
grace and to the blessed in heaven through glory.

Thus the sense of the words, which otherwise seems intricate, is plain: “For
we who have believed enter into God’s rest, just as he said, Therefore I swore
in my wrath, They shall not enter my rest.” Although the words of the Psalm
are threatening, they nevertheless include a promise by consequence, just as
in the commandments of God every injunction includes a prohibition and every
prohibition an injunction. Lest anyone say that these words must be referred
to the rest of God which occurred after the work of creation and of which
Genesis 2:3 speaks, he adds καίτοι ἔργων, and so forth, “although his works
were completed from the foundation of the world,” to indicate that this
cannot be understood of that rest of which Moses speaks there, because it had
long since passed. Or, lest it be referred to the rest of Canaan, he adds in
verse 8: “If Joshua had introduced them into rest”—namely, that true and
saving rest in which happiness consists—“he would not afterward have spoken
of another day.” Hence he concludes in verse 9: “Therefore a Sabbath rest
remains for the people of God,” that is, a rest different from the preceding
ones.

XII. Nor should it be replied here that the rest of which the apostle speaks
in verses 3 and 4 has nothing in common with the Sabbath instituted by God,
because it must be understood of God’s rest, that is, a rest proper to God
himself, not to human beings. For in this passage God’s rest does not exclude
but necessarily includes the rest or Sabbath of human beings. No reason could
be given why the apostle should except that rest from the laying of the
world’s foundations if it pertained in no way to human beings and if, under
God’s rest, he did not understand the rest which God prescribed to human
beings by his example. Second, if the Sabbath of human beings is not included
here, it will follow that Paul omitted the Sabbath of the Jews. He could not
do so without prejudice to the argument he was employing, because it could
have been objected to him that there was another Sabbath rest besides that on
which God rested and that which the Israelites enjoyed after the possession
of Canaan—namely, the ordinary weekly Sabbath.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the religion of the fathers.]

XIII. Fifth, the same point is confirmed by the *piety and religion* of the
ancient fathers. Since it belongs to natural and perpetual law that certain
times be set apart for the solemn worship of God, it cannot be that Adam and
the holy patriarchs did not have some sacred and solemn days on which the
worship of God was performed and on which they remembered God’s benefits in
the creation of heaven and earth. If there were sacred days then, it is
reasonable that this day instituted by God was observed rather than any other
days of which Scripture says nothing. It is replied in vain that they could
have had other times besides this day. Since this is said nowhere, it is
rejected with the same ease with which it is proposed. Nor can other times,
which are mentioned nowhere, be invented without rashness while that time is
passed over whose sanctification before the law is mentioned so expressly.

[Marginal heading: 6. From traces of the Sabbath among the Gentiles.]

XIV. Sixth, among the Gentiles there are by no means obscure records both of
the number seven in general as sacred and of the recurring seventh day as
regarded sacred by the Gentiles. This custom seems to have been derived not
so much from the practice of the Jews, whose religion was hateful to the
Gentiles, as received from an ancestral tradition, πατροπαράδοτον, and handed
down to them by long use. Hence Clement of Alexandria says in *Stromata*,
book 5, ἀλλὰ καὶ τὴν ἑβδόμην ἱερὰν, οὐ μόνον οἱ Ἑβραῖοι, ἀλλὰ καὶ οἱ Ἕλληνες
ἴσασι: “Not only the Hebrews but also the Greeks recognize every seventh day
as sacred.” He proves this from Hesiod, Homer, Linus, and Callimachus, among
whom ἑβδόμη, the *seventh day*, is called ἱερὸν ἦμαρ, a *sacred day*. Hence
Philo calls it ἑορτὴ πάνδημος, a *feast of the whole people*, pertaining
equally to all nations. So too Josephus says in *Against Apion*, book 2,
chapter 10: “There is no city,” he says,

[Printed p. 73 | Internet Archive PDF 101 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

“of the Greeks anywhere or of the barbarians, nor any nation, to which the
custom of the seventh day on which we rest has not reached.” In Tibullus,
book 3, Elegy 3, the day of Saturn is called a *sacred day*. See more in
Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book 13, chapter 13.

XV. Although God is said to have made the commandment concerning the Sabbath
known to the Jews through Moses, with an emphatic summons to attention also
placed before it (Exod. 16:29; 31:13; Ezek. 20:12; Neh. 9:14), it does not
follow that it had not already been instituted from the beginning. Not only
what is first instituted is said to be given, but also what is renewed if it
has perhaps been effaced or neglected, or what is promulgated clearly and
distinctly in a new and more illustrious manner. Thus in Psalm 147:19 God is
said to have given his statutes and judgments to Israel and not to have done
so to any nation—not absolutely and simply, because the Gentiles bear the
work of the law written in their hearts (Rom. 2:15), but in a certain respect
and comparatively, with regard to the revelation made in the Word. The
Sabbath is said to have been made known in the same manner as the law,
commandments, and statutes are said to have been commanded through Moses, for
these things are joined in Nehemiah 9:14. Yet no one can deny that this law,
namely the moral law, had been given before, as had various ceremonial
statutes and commandments in the institution of sacrifices and circumcision.

XVI. Nothing prevents things given generally to all from being given
specially to some, with an emphatic distinction and indeed as a sign of their
sanctification, if things once given generally and omitted or forgotten
through negligence are afterward restored to some and commended for special
reasons. This is what occurs here. Therefore, although in its first
institution the Sabbath had uses common to all and a general end—namely, to
celebrate the memory of creation, contemplate the works of God, and exercise
the public worship of God—nothing prevents it from having special ends with
respect to the Israelites. Just as the rainbow, which was otherwise a natural
sign, was made a sacramental sign of a thing which it had not indicated
before, although it had existed, so what was the sign of one benefit could,
with the addition of a new blessing and institution, be a special sign to
those for whom the benefit was enlarged. In this sense, in Exodus 31:13 and
Ezekiel 20:12, the Sabbath is said to have been given to the Israelites as a
special sign, so that by the true worship of God they might be separated from
the other nations, especially the Egyptians, although otherwise it was a sign
common to all.

XVII. Although in the state of innocence Adam, as just and holy, ought to have
worshiped God daily and contemplated his works, this does not prevent him
from having been obliged to have some stated day on which he would render
solemn and public worship to God. On it he would cease, not indeed from works
of righteousness and praise, which ought to have been continuous, but from
his daily labors in cultivating paradise, to which by God’s prescription he
also ought to have applied himself, although without the trouble and weariness
that later came through sin.

XVIII. Although no express mention is made in the lives of the patriarchs of
their observance of the Sabbath, it does not follow that it was altogether
unknown or unobserved by them, because the narrative is concise and need not
contain everything that pertained to them. It was enough for the Holy Spirit
to touch upon matters that served his purpose, namely, to confirm the promises
made to them concerning the blessed Seed and to trace their genealogy for
knowing the truth of the history. Thus no mention is made of the Sabbath being
observed in the time of the judges and Samuel, but one would wrongly infer
from this that it was not observed. Second, we do not read that the patriarchs
observed any stated times for the public worship of God, and yet their piety
forbids us to doubt that they had certain sacred and solemn days consecrated
to God’s worship. No days

[Printed p. 74 | Internet Archive PDF 102 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

could have been more suitable than the weekly seventh days, which for a
special reason had
been blessed and sanctified by God for man’s rest after the pattern of God’s
rest.

[Marginal heading: The second question, concerning the morality of the
Sabbath.]

XIX. The second question treats *the morality of the Sabbath*: whether the
Fourth Commandment, by which the sanctification of the Sabbath is decreed, is
moral and perpetual, or only ceremonial and established for a definite time.
Three principal positions of theologians occur here: two extremes and a third
middle position. The first maintains that the commandment is simply moral and
perpetual. The Jews follow it, and of old the Ebionites, Cerinthians,
Apollinarians, and others called by the common name *Sabbatarians* joined
them. They wished the seventh day to be sanctified now no less than formerly,
and Eusebius notes that they were condemned as heretics by the ancient church
(*History*, book 3, chapter 21). Nor are those far removed from them who even
today contend that this commandment is absolutely moral, like the other
commandments of the moral law, and therefore must be observed perpetually.

The second position asserts that it is merely ceremonial and therefore was
wholly abrogated through Christ. The ancient Manichaeans, and today the
Anabaptists and Socinians, hold this position, wishing it to have been so
abrogated that it pertains in no way to Christians. In chapter 1, question
69, the Racovian Catechism answers that “it has been removed under the New
Covenant, like the other ceremonies.” Volkelius says in book 4, chapter 14:
“The fourth commandment of the Decalogue concerning the Sabbath, which was
formerly given to the Israelites, is ceremonial, not moral, and moreover
pertains in no respect to the discipline of Christ.” Nor are others lacking
whom this position pleases.

The third, middle position holds that this commandment is mixed, that is,
partly moral and partly ceremonial: moral as to its *substance*, namely, the
necessity of divine worship at a definite and stated time, but ceremonial as
to its *circumstance*, namely, the particular determination of the seventh
day. This is received among the orthodox, and we shall demonstrate its truth
in three propositions.

[Marginal heading: First proposition: the Fourth Commandment is not wholly
moral.]

XX. First: *The Fourth Commandment is not in every respect moral and
perpetual*. This is proved both against the Jews and against Christians who
still urge its absolute morality. First, against the Jews, from the nature of
*moral things*: moral precepts strictly so called belong to the law of nature,
conform to the image of God and to the notions of good and evil, honorable
and shameful, have their principle in nature itself, and of themselves
contribute to good morals; therefore, they belong to eternal and immutable
law. But the determination of the seventh day is not such a thing, since,
whether considered ἁπλῶς, *simply*, in itself, it is neither good nor holy, or
considered *comparatively*, it is no better than the determination of another
day except by the authority alone of the one commanding.

Second, this is proved *from the commandment itself*, in which the
determination of public worship for the seventh day is prescribed. Since this
is merely a circumstance of time, it is for that very reason to be considered
mutable and thus ceremonial and positive, not moral. Third, it is proved
*from the end of the commandment*, because among other ends it also had the
character of a *sign* and a *type*, which for that reason had to be abrogated
in its own time. It had the character of a sign when it is called the sign of
the covenant entered between God and the Israelites (Exod. 31:13; Ezek.
20:12, 20, and elsewhere), a sign not only *indicative of present grace*, but
also *sealing future grace*: not only that Jehovah was the God of the Jews,
but also that he was the God who sanctified them. It had the character of a
type because it is called, with the other ceremonies, a *shadow of things to
come* (Col. 2:16–17), representing the twofold rest which believers obtain in
Christ. The first is *spiritual*, both in the rest of conscience from the
terrors of divine wrath and in ceasing from our evil works (Matt. 11:28; Rom.
5:1; Heb. 4:3; Isa. 58:13–14). Hence the spiritual worship of the New
Testament is customarily described by the Holy Spirit’s style, which
generally delights to express the mysteries of the gospel in legal terms, by

[Printed p. 75 | Internet Archive PDF 103 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the celebration of the Sabbath (Isa. 56:2; 66:23). The second is *heavenly*,
namely, rest not only from all sins but also from all the labors and miseries
of this life (Ps. 95:11; Heb. 4:10; Rev. 14:13). Fourth, the prophets often
number the Sabbaths among the other feasts and ceremonies of the Jews,
although these belonged to merely ceremonial and positive law and were
observed only from and on account of the commandment. Therefore, the
commandment concerning the Sabbath is akin to them in some part of itself.

XXI. Second, against Christians: First, all ceremonial and typical things, as
such, were taken away through Christ; therefore, so was what is ceremonial in
the Sabbath. Christ testifies that something of this kind exists in it when
he places it in the same order as other legal ceremonies, such as the
institution of the showbread and the priests’ λειτουργία, or ministry, in the
temple (Matt. 12:3–5). Indeed, the Sabbath is there included by him under the
name of *sacrifice*, and of a *sacrifice* contrasted with *mercy* (vv. 7–8).

Second, the Lord testifies in Mark 2:27–28 that “the Sabbath was made for man,
not man for the Sabbath, and the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.” Its
sanction is therefore of another nature than the other precepts and is
subject also to the Son of Man, who has the power to abrogate it with respect
to what is merely positive, which cannot be said of the others.

Third, the apostle expressly teaches this in Colossians 2:16–17 when he says:
“Let no one judge you in food, a part of a feast, a new moon, or Sabbaths,
which are a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ’s.” Here, first,
he joins the observance of Sabbaths with other ceremonial things as something
homogeneous with the *distinction of foods, feast days*, and *the new moon*.
Second, he calls them all σκιὰν τῶν μελλόντων, “a shadow of things to come,”
where the words ἅ ἐστι are not so much διακριτικά, *distinctive*, as
αἰτιολογικά, *causal*, so that the reason may be given why Sabbaths should no
longer be observed: because they *are a shadow of things to come*. Third, he
opposes Christ to them as the body
in whom and through whom the thing signified was exhibited.

It is replied in vain that the weekly Sabbath is not understood, but various
other Sabbaths observed among the Jews which were typical. First, this
gratuitously assumes what must be proved, and the apostle’s words do not allow
it. Since they are general and treat Sabbaths in the plural without any
qualification, they must extend to all; it is not ours to restrict what the
apostle did not restrict. Second, feasts are distinguished here from Sabbaths.
Third, principally those Sabbaths are understood which were decreed by the
Fourth Commandment, recurred frequently, and whose observance the Jews and
Sabbatarians demanded among the first Christians. Yet it cannot rightly be
concluded from this that the Sabbath is therefore merely ceremonial, as those
of the opposing position argue, because what is ceremonial in a certain
respect and therefore mutable need not immediately be such in every respect,
as will be proved more fully afterward.

Fourth, from the absurdity: if this commandment is in every respect moral and
perpetual, then even today under the New Testament we are bound to the precise
observance of the seventh day, nor could it have been transferred to the
Lord’s Day.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXII. Although the commandment concerning the Sabbath is contained in the
Decalogue, it does not follow that it is moral in every respect, because the
Decalogue is not only a compendium of the moral law. In it is also given the
foundation of the whole Mosaic law, whose three parts were moral,
ceremonial, and forensic. Hence, in addition to the moral law chiefly
delivered here, the root of all the ceremonies in which divine worship then
consisted is also given in the first table, just as the foundation of the
Jewish polity and its judicial laws is given in the second. Therefore, the
*thing commanded* and the *circumstance of the thing* must be distinguished
here. The thing commanded is moral; the circumstance of the thing is
ceremonial or at least merely positive.

[Printed p. 76 | Internet Archive PDF 104 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XXIII. The Sabbath is called ברית עולם, an *everlasting covenant* (Exod.
31:16–17), not by reason of an absolute *perpetuity*, but one that is
*comparative and periodic*, because of the long duration of its use in the
Old Testament. Thus the word עולם is often taken for a memorable duration
that is nevertheless limited by the nature of the thing. In this manner it is
attributed to circumcision (Gen. 17:13), the showbread (Lev. 24:8), the
offerings of firstfruits (Num. 18:19), and the priesthood (Num. 28:23), and
עולם is commonly used of the time of the legal economy up to the Messiah.
Indeed, it is also used for a shorter span, namely the time until the Jubilee:
in Exodus 21:6 the servant is said to serve *forever*, that is, until the
Jubilee.

XXIV. From the fact that the law concerning the Sabbath was given before the
fall so that the day might be sanctified by man after God’s example, it is
rightly gathered that in its principal part it is moral. But it does not
immediately follow that it is absolutely and simply such. For not everything
God commanded or forbade Adam is morally good or evil of and in itself, as is
evident even from eating of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

XXV. The immutability of God and the constancy of his decrees do indeed cause
things which belong to God’s natural law or which he has decreed forever also
to be perpetual and never to change. But the same account does not apply to
things of positive law, which are instituted only for a definite time. Such
was the Mosaic Sabbath with respect to the circumstance of time and the
manner of observance prescribed to the Jews.

[Marginal heading: Second proposition: the Fourth Commandment is not merely
ceremonial.]

XXVI. The second proposition: *The Fourth Commandment is not ceremonial in
every respect*. The reasons are as follows. First, it is a commandment of the
Decalogue, in which the moral law is contained; consequently, no commandment
in it is merely ceremonial. Second, its end is moral: both the primary end,
namely, the public and solemn worship of God consisting in the consideration
and meditation of his works and in public exercises of piety, which cannot be
performed without the determination of a certain stated and solemn day
dedicated to that worship; and the subordinate end, namely, cessation from
servile work for some relief of servants and animals. Third, the duty
prescribed is moral, namely, the sanctification of the Sabbath by separating
that day from others and consecrating it to holy uses, namely, exercises of
piety and worship toward God and charity toward one’s neighbor. Fourth, the
reason and motive of the commandment, drawn from God’s example proposed to us
for imitation—namely, his rest on that day and his sanctification of it—is
moral, and therefore we cannot be conformed to it except by the performance
of the corresponding moral duty.

XXVII. Fifth, the nature of the commandment concerns things that befit a
rational nature as such and agree with natural principles and right reason.
For just as a rational creature is bound to worship God not only with the
internal worship of the mind but also with the external worship of the body,
and not only privately but also publicly in the communion of saints, so right
reason teaches that nothing is more fitting than to consecrate to God—who has
freely given us our being, everything we possess, and the whole of time
itself—a stated and definite time in some fitting cycle for worshiping him
publicly and celebrating his praises.

Sixth, this commandment is never read to have been entirely abrogated in the
New Testament. Indeed, since Christ confirmed the whole moral law as
pertaining to Christians and binding them at every time, he is for that very
reason considered to have confirmed this commandment too. And since certain
days have always been sanctified and dedicated by the Christian church for
public assemblies and the solemn worship of God, it is abundantly evident
from this that the observance of this commandment was regarded as moral and
belonging to perpetual law. Otherwise, if the commandment were merely
ceremonial, it would be no more lawful now to observe any Sabbath

[Printed p. 77 | Internet Archive PDF 105 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

than to restore after their abolition the other ceremonies which God abrogated
under the New Testament. Everyone sees how absurd this would be.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXVIII. The commandment concerning the Sabbath must be considered in two
ways: either *absolutely and in itself*, with respect to the *substance* of
the commandment; or *in relation to the Mosaic economy* of the Old Testament,
with respect to the precise *circumstance of time* and the special reasons and
ends for which it was given to the Israelites. Although in this latter
respect it is called a *sign* of the dispensation of that particular covenant
(Exod. 31:13; Ezek. 20:12), and therefore has something ceremonial, it does
not follow that it is a mere ceremony or that the commandment considered in
itself pertained only to this economy.

XXIX. Although the command concerning a solemn *place* of worship was
ceremonial, it does not follow that the command concerning a solemn *time* is
likewise ceremonial, because there is a manifold difference between place
and time in this matter. First, observance of time is mentioned in the
Decalogue, but not determination of place; therefore, they are not equal
circumstances of religion. Second, the necessity and usefulness of a certain
place for worship is only physical and accidental to religion, because it
makes no contribution to advancing religion whether God is worshiped in the
open air, in a basilica, in an underground place, in public, or in private.
But the necessity of time, whether undetermined or determined, is far
greater, because it is moral and theological, whether we speak separately of
the total portion, duration, or frequency, and much more if we speak of them
together. For there is no doubt that a greater portion of time in exercises
of religion, whether with respect to duration, frequency, or both, contributes
to progress in piety, and that, other things being equal, the one who expends
such time in sacred worship is more pleasing and acceptable to God than one
who does so more rarely and briefly.

Third, a determination of time is necessary for sacred exercises, because it
is impossible for us to transact worldly business at one and the same time
and simultaneously exercise the worship of God. Therefore, lest worldly
affairs hinder us from devoting ourselves to divine worship for as long and
as often as is required, such a determination is absolutely necessary. But
nothing like this can be said with respect to place, because in one and the
same place, though at different times, we can worship God and transact worldly
business; and if some place is determined, worship need not occur there if no
determination of time is made.

XXX. Although the weekly Sabbath, considered with respect to the Mosaic
economy and its ceremonial adjuncts—namely, the precise observance of the
seventh day and its scrupulous and laborious observance prescribed in the Old
Testament—was a shadow of things to come and is rightly said to have been
abrogated under the New Testament, it does not follow that the Sabbath itself,
considered in itself and absolutely, is likewise ceremonial and therefore to
be abrogated. Paul speaks in Colossians 2:16–17 of the Sabbath in the former
sense, in which the pseudo-apostles urged it, but not the latter. In the same
manner he speaks of *food and drink*, not *absolutely and simply*, because it
would then follow that no moral commandment about food and drink exists and
that gluttons and drunkards are no more to be condemned now than those who
observe new moons. Rather, he speaks *in a certain respect* and comparatively,
with regard to the dietary law which prescribed a distinction of foods, a law
now removed in the New Testament.

XXXI. Although the institution of the Sabbath with respect to the
determination of a particular day does not flow from God’s nature or rest
absolutely on God’s primary natural right, but depends upon his will and thus
rests rather upon his positive right, it does not follow that it cannot be
moral and perpetual by reason of a secondary right natural to us. What is
positive to God can be natural to us, as was seen before.

[Printed p. 78 | Internet Archive PDF 106 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

[Marginal heading: Third proposition: the Fourth Commandment is partly moral
and partly ceremonial.]

XXXII. The third proposition: *The Fourth Commandment concerning the Sabbath
is partly moral and partly ceremonial*. The ceremonial element can be viewed
in three things. First, *in the designation of the seventh day*, which, since
it was changed in the New Testament, had to be ceremonial. Second, *in the
sanctification of that day*, in its strict and rigorous observance from
evening to evening: negatively, through cessation from every servile work,
according to which it was lawful neither to kindle a fire, prepare and cook
food, walk, nor make a journey; and positively, in the performance of various
ceremonial works, partly those common to other days, such as the continual
sacrifice and circumcision, and partly those proper to this day, namely, its
special sacrifices for the whole church, two year-old lambs and two tenths of
fine flour mingled with oil (Num. 28:8–9), and the showbread, which had to be
set out in the temple on every Sabbath (Lev. 24:7–8). Third, *in the typical
significance of this day*, to represent the grace of sanctification and, from
it, spiritual and heavenly rest in Christ, of which Paul speaks in Colossians
2:16–17.

XXXIII. The *moral* element likewise consists in three things. First, in the
*appointment of public assemblies* for worshiping God at a certain definite
time and day. Second, in *the sanctification of the day itself*: negatively,
through ceasing from the works of one’s ordinary calling so that one may have
leisure for sacred meditations and divine worship, though works of *charity
and necessity* are excepted; and positively, through the solemn and public
worship of God in the congregation of the church. Third, in the *relief of
servants and animals*, which God willed to be considered, lest they be worn
out by unbroken labor and so that the servants themselves might also devote
themselves to sacred things.

[Marginal heading: Whether the determination of one day in seven pertains to
the morality of the Sabbath. State of the question.]

XXXIV. Here, however, a new question arises concerning this *morality*, which
is also debated among the orthodox: whether by the force and analogy of this
commandment there pertains to it not only the determination of some time and
an undetermined day for public worship, but also the designation of one day
in seven in a weekly cycle, which, recurring in its circuit each week, must
necessarily be observed. On this matter, first, the question does not concern
the *fittingness* of the thing, whether it was fitting and agreeable to reason
that it be designated in this way. Everyone agrees that one day in seven
could rightly have been instituted for the worship of God and was not
instituted without various weighty reasons. Rather, the question concerns its
*necessity*: whether by the force and analogy of the commandment it was
absolutely necessary from the nature of the thing that it be arranged in this
way.

Second, the question does not concern the *moral in the strict sense*, which
is natural to God, as founded in his nature and holiness and flowing from his
image, whose contrary he cannot command without contradiction or dispense
with. In this sense everyone agrees that the designation of such a day is not
natural to God, and that God was free to select one day out of seven, eight,
or ten for his worship and was not bound to one side of the contradiction by
any necessity of his nature. Rather, it concerns the *moral in the broad
sense*, which, although positive on God’s part, is nevertheless natural to us
insofar as it agrees with rational nature and man’s relation and duty toward
God.

XXXV. Although the matter is debated problematically on both sides while the
bond of faith and charity remains intact, and great men who stand for the
negative position do not lack weighty reasons to defend their view, we
nevertheless do not deny that with others we incline toward the affirmative
and that the reasons brought forward for it seem more solid to us.

XXXVI. First, from *the nature of the thing*. For since it is natural and
moral

[Printed p. 79 | Internet Archive PDF 107 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

not only that man worship God publicly with some external worship, and indeed
in the communion of saints and a public assembly, but also that some time be
set apart for this use, it must likewise be natural that some solemn
determination of that time be made, both with respect to frequency and
duration. This can be made by none other than God, the supremely wise author
of worship and time. Therefore, since in the law he willed to define that day
which in his wisdom he knew to be most fitting and suitable for this end,
leaving six days to man and reserving for himself only one in the weekly
cycle, anyone who now wished to revoke that day and substitute another in its
place would in some manner exalt himself above God and boast that he was
wiser than God. This would be evidence not only of the greatest rashness but
also of intolerable pride and profane impiety.

XXXVII. Second, if by the force and analogy of the commandment it is not
rightly gathered that one day in seven is to be consecrated to divine worship,
no definite number or cycle of days can be limited by any divine commandment,
since no other number is mentioned anywhere in any other commandment.
Everyone sees that this is absurd. Nor does it help to object that it is
enough if by the force of the commandment a day sufficient for exhibiting
gratitude to God is gathered. For the commandment does not treat only a
sufficient day but one day in seven, which is not ours to change.

XXXVIII. Third, in changing the seventh day the apostles nevertheless retained
the weekly cycle, so that they selected one day out of seven for public
worship. They would not have done this unless they had recognized it as
invariable and moral. And no one has ever been found among Christians who
dared attempt any change here. Nor should it be said that this was done from
Christian liberty and prudence. Although the change from the seventh day to
the first was made from Christian liberty, it does not follow that retaining
one day in seven was likewise absolutely free and positive, depending on
their mere discretion.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXXIX. The determination of one day in seven no more conflicts with Christian
liberty than does the designation of a definite and stated day for divine
worship. Whether one is selected from seven, eight, or ten, some distinction
is always preserved. Therefore, when the apostle teaches that the distinction
of days has been removed under the New Testament (Rom. 14:5–6; Gal. 4:10) and
charges it as a fault against the Galatians that they observe days and months,
this must be understood of a ceremonial distinction of days, such as obtained
among the Jews under the Old Testament, where it constituted part of divine
worship and had to be observed laboriously and rigorously; or such as
obtained among the Gentiles, who considered some days in themselves holier
and more fortunate than others and therefore customarily distinguished them
as black and white, favorable and unfavorable. Otherwise, if every observance
of days is condemned absolutely and simply, it will now be lawful to observe
no solemn day at all for worshiping God, nor could Paul have prescribed to
Christians that they assemble and make collections on the first day of every
week (1 Cor. 16:2).

XL. Although believers are bound to worship God on every day of the week, and
thus our whole life ought to be a continuous Sabbath, it does not follow that
no stated day in the week is to be consecrated to God. It is evident that
public worship cannot be performed daily, both because of the weakness of the
flesh and because of the necessity of animal life, which requires various
human labors for its preservation. Thus the Sabbath rest of this life is
distinguished from the heavenly, which will be perpetual and constant,
because we will rest from all our labors, having been freed from all the
miseries and necessities of this life.

[Printed p. 80 | Internet Archive PDF 108 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XLI. Although by the force of the commandment one day in seven must
necessarily be observed as a moral and perpetual duty, it does not likewise
follow that the observance of the seventh day decreed by this commandment is
moral. For the precise determination of the seventh day is entirely free and
accidental to worship. Whether worship is rendered to God on the seventh day,
the sixth, or some other day of the week, it is the same provided that he is
worshiped. But the determination of one day is necessary and contributes
chiefly to the worship of God, for we do not judge that God is fittingly and
sufficiently worshiped unless one day in the week is consecrated to him.

XLII. That Calvin was not opposed to this position can clearly be gathered
from his sermons on the Ten Commandments. In the fifth sermon he says: “If we
were as fervent in the worship of God as we ought to be, one day in the week
would not have to be selected, but we would have to assemble daily without a
written law. Since, therefore, our weakness is so great, let us acknowledge
that this polity was given not only to the Jews, so that a definite day might
be had for assembling, but also to us, and that this is common to us with
them.” Likewise in the sixth sermon: “When it is said, ‘Six days you shall
work,’ the Lord shows that it should not seem burdensome to us to dedicate
some definite day to him, since he grants us six in place of one.” Near the
end he adds: “God does not deal with us according to his highest right, but is
content if we consecrate one day to him, or if that day serves us throughout
the whole week.”

XLIII. Nor can it be gathered that he had another view from book 2 of the
*Institutes*, chapter 8, section 34, when he says that “he does not so dwell
upon the number of seven days as to bind the church to its servitude.” His
purpose there is not so much to condemn the designation of one day in the
week for public worship, which he proves expressly enough elsewhere, or to
refer it to a merely human and free polity, as to censure the Jewish
superstition of those who Judaized in its observance. To this belong the words
that follow: “Thus vanish the trifles of the false prophets who in former
centuries imbued the people with a Jewish opinion, bringing forward nothing
else than that what was ceremonial in this commandment—which they call the
fixing of the seventh day—has been abrogated, while what is moral remains,
namely, the observance of one day in the week. Yet this is nothing other than
to change the day as an insult to the Jews while retaining in the mind the
same holiness of the day.”

It is certain that here he does not criticize any of the Reformed, with whom
he never had any dispute on this matter, but certain Roman Catholics and
scholastics. They thought they had sufficiently protected evangelical liberty
if they taught that fixing the seventh day had been abolished as ceremonial,
while in the meantime they taught that one day in seven must be observed in
the manner in which the Jews observed their Sabbath. Hence he adds that “those
who cling to their constitutions surpass the Jews three times over in the
gross and carnal superstition of Sabbatarianism.”

XLIV. *Peter Viret*, Calvin’s colleague, follows the received position and
expounds it fully in dialogue 5 on the Ten Commandments. Nor does *Beza* hold
otherwise on Revelation 1:10. He maintains that “the Fourth Commandment
concerning the sanctification of every seventh day was ceremonial with
respect to the day of the Sabbath and the legal rites; but with respect to
the worship of God it is a commandment of the immutable moral law and
perpetual in this life,” and so forth. And afterward: “Therefore, the
assemblies of the Lord’s Day are of apostolic and truly divine tradition, yet
in such a way that the Jewish cessation from every work should by no means be
observed, because this plainly would have been not to abolish Judaism, but
only to change it with respect to the day.”

The position of *Martyr* is the same in *Commonplaces*, class 2, chapter 7,
paragraph 1: “Let us omit here the mysteries of the number seven, and so
forth, and let us attend only to this, that one day out of every seven must be
reserved for God.” And afterward: “Just as it is perpetual and

[Printed p. 81 | Internet Archive PDF 109 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

eternal that, as long as the church acts upon earth, sustenance is owed to
its ministers, and so forth, so it is stable and firm that one day in the
week be devoted to divine worship,” and so forth. So *Bucer* says in *The
Kingdom of Christ*, book 1, chapter 15: “Certainly this too is our duty: to
sanctify one day in the week publicly for religious exercises, and so forth.
Who, therefore, does not see how beneficial it is for the congregations of
Christ that there be one day in the week so consecrated to sacred religious
exercises that no other work may lawfully be performed on it than assembling
in a sacred gathering?” and so forth. *Zanchi* says in chapter 19 on the
Fourth Commandment: “The commandment is moral insofar as it commands that we
consecrate one day out of seven to the external worship of God,” and so
forth. *Fayus*, disputation 47 on the Fourth Commandment, thesis 17; *Junius*,
*Observations on the Polity of Moses*, chapter 8; *Parcus* in the *Catechism*;
*Alsted*, *Catechetical Theology*, book 3, chapter 6; and not a few others
hold the same position.
