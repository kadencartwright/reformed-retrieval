## QUESTION XII

Is it lawful in oaths to use amphibological equivocations and mental
reservations?

We deny it, against the Roman Catholics, and especially the Jesuits.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

I. To understand the state of the question, observe, first, that the question
does not concern the *rhetorical use* of equivocations. For it is agreed that
rhetoricians frequently use them in the figure called antanaclasis and πλοκή,
and in the trope of irony, as in the familiar epigram:

*Who would deny that Nero was born from the stock of Aeneas?*

*The former took away his mother; the latter lifted up his father.*

In this manner the same word is often used in different senses in the same
passage of Scripture (John 1:10; Mark 8:35). Nor does the question concern
*logical use*, which often occurs in disputations and other probative
discourses, πειραστικοῖς, instituted for the purpose of exercising minds and
fortifying them against sophisms. Rather, it concerns *theological* and
*ethical use* in giving testimony, in a judicial proceeding, or in a case of
confession: whether, besides grammatical or logical ambiguity—which often
arises either from a twofold sense of words, from the construction of a
sentence, or from the varied arrangement of circumstances—another,
theological ambiguity should be admitted under some pretext of qualification,
by which we profess one thing with the mouth and keep another shut within the
breast, so that the questions of judges may be evaded and the truth concealed.

II. The question does not concern a *stated qualification*, whether explicit
or implicit, nor an *understood qualification* that can readily be gathered
either from the common agreement of the speakers or from other circumstances
set before them. For no one denies that such logical qualifications or
restrictions, which are elicited from what has been stated or are assumed and
understood on both sides, are lawful and free speech from lying and perjury.
Rather, it concerns a qualification assumed only and understood by the one
who speaks, which produces either verbal or real equivocation. It is *verbal*
when the very words employed have an ambiguous sense, and the swearer takes
them in the sense that he wishes to conceal from his hearers so that they may
take them in another sense. Thus if someone, when asked, “Is Peter at home?”
were to answer, “He is not” (*non est*), that is, “He does not eat” (*non
est*). It is *mental* when the very words employed have one simple sense but
are changed into another through a tacit restriction or interpretation that
the speaker reserves within his own mind.

To this belongs the reply of Francis of Assisi. When he was asked which way a
certain murderer who had passed by him had gone, he put his hands into his
sleeves and answered that the man had not passed that way, understanding that
he had not passed through those sleeves. In this manner most Jesuits maintain
that a priest, when asked whether he is a priest, can answer that he is not a
priest if he is asked either by one without judicial authority or has a
reasonable cause for

[Printed p. 64 | Internet Archive PDF 92 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

concealing the truth, provided that he understands either that he is not a
priest of Baal or that he is not a priest in order to say this to the other
person.

III. The question therefore comes down to this: whether *equivocation* and
amphibology—not rhetorical or logical equivocation arising from the
indifferent signification of words, the construction of a sentence, or the
comparison of circumstances, but *ethical* equivocation composed for the
purpose of deceiving, which reserves in the mind a qualification that has not
been stated in any intelligible manner, nor assumed and understood on both
sides, but is reserved inwardly only by the one who speaks—can have a place in
a judicial proceeding, a case of confession, or the common conversation of
human beings, and excuse from lying and, if it is confirmed by an oath, from
perjury. The adversaries affirm it; we deny it.

IV. The teachers of equivocations were once the ancient heretics. The
*Basilidians* were infamous for them, for they taught that faith was to be
concealed or denied by perjury so that martyrdoms might be avoided (Irenaeus,
book 1, chapter 23; Eusebius, *History*, book 4, chapter 7). After them the
*Priscillianists* followed the same course. Augustine says of them in *On
Heresies*, chapter 70, that in order to conceal their defilements and vile
acts, they had these words among their dogmas: “Swear, perjure yourself; do
not betray the secret.” Also well known is the κυβεία, or sleight, of Arius by
which he eluded Constantine’s examination concerning the Nicene faith
(Nicephorus, *History*, book 8, chapter 51).

In the preceding century the Jesuits, teachers of every perfidy, recalled the
same dogma as though from the underworld. Although they bring it forward not
willingly but almost against their will and keep it among the secrets of their
Society, they nevertheless do not conceal it in their writings and
sufficiently confirm it by their deeds. This art is openly defended by
Cardinal Toledo, *Priestly Instruction*, book 4, chapter 21; Suárez, volume
*On Virtue and the State of Religion*, tractate 4, book 3, chapters 10–11;
Lessius, *On Justice and Right*, chapter 47, doubt 6, section 35; Emmanuel Sá,
*Aphorisms*, under the entries “Oath” and “Lie”; Sánchez, *Decalogue*, book 3,
chapter 6; Valentia on II-II, question 110; and many others: Azorius,
Maldonatus, Parsons, and Eudaemon-Johannes in his *Apology for Garnet*, where
he argues this cause expressly and at length.

To all these Garnet himself must be added. He demonstrated this art by his
practice and defense before the judges, as can be seen in the proceedings
against the English traitors published by Camden and in King James’s *Apology
for the Oath of Allegiance*. And all these are from the Jesuit order. With
them agree the canonist Navarrus, chapter 12 of the *Manual*; Bañez on II-II,
question 62, article 2, doubt 8; the *Gloss of Canon Law* on chapter *Ne quis*,
Cause 22; Vincentius Candidus in *Moral Disquisitions*, disputation 26,
article 3, doubt 1; and many others.

V. Although Becanus cries out that injury is done to him and his associates,
who teach that one must speak and answer sincerely, candidly, and with all
equivocation excluded, it can nevertheless readily be gathered that he
quibbles in his usual manner and plays with equivocation. For he maintains
that an accused person is not bound to confess the truth if the judge does not
observe the legitimate process of law in his interrogation. A judge is
considered not to observe the order of law when he asks about a hidden crime
concerning which there is neither a report against the accused, nor competence
in the judge, nor complete proof. In such a case Becanus does not consider the
accused to lie if he denies having committed the crime—provided, that is, the
crime is still hidden—as though the order of law depended upon the estimation
of the accused himself.

VI. It must nevertheless be acknowledged that not a few vigorously and
openly reject and oppose this impious dogma. Such are Malderus, *On the Abuse
of Mental Restrictions*, chapter 4; Sepúlveda in his short work *On the
Manner of Giving Testimony*; Paulus Sylvius, theologian of Douai; and
especially Joh. Barnesius the Benedictine. Barnesius published a particular
treatise against equivocations with the approval of the Theological Faculty
of Paris, in which he

[Printed p. 65 | Internet Archive PDF 93 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

proves his doctrine by the agreement of Thomas, Scotus, Gerson, Lombard,
Cajetan, Soto, and all the scholastics of better repute.

VII. The orthodox steadfastly follow this position. For although Becanus, in
his *Manual*, book 5, chapter 21, so that the abominable doctrine of the
Jesuits might for a while be withdrawn from the readers’ eyes by spreading
this as a kind of cuttlefish ink, calumniously ascribes to us that “we
strenuously use this art and by calumny charge them with what we ourselves
do”—and Brochmannus follows him in his *System of Theology*, treatise *On the
Law*, chapter 6, question 4—it is nevertheless evident from the constant
teaching and practice of our theologians that they themselves strenuously lie.

Nor is the calumny supported by what both men bring forward from Beza: that in
his confession in 1556 he asserted that “the body of Christ is truly and
really present in the Supper,” and so forth. For we admit the true and real
presence and reception of Christ by the faithful in the Supper, although we
reject a corporeal and Capernaite presence.

[Marginal heading: The unlawfulness of using equivocations in oaths is proved
from Hebrews 6:16.]

VIII. Our reasons are as follows. First, an oath ought to be the end of every
controversy, according to the apostle (Heb. 6:16). But where equivocation is
present, disputes are multiplied rather than ended, since nothing is evident
from which the other party may gather whether the matter is conducted in good
faith. Nor is the distinction between a *competent* and an *incompetent judge*
of any force here, as though one must not equivocate before a judge proceeding
juridically but may do so before an incompetent judge who does not proceed
juridically. For faith must be kept even between enemies, and one must
consider not *to whom* but *by whom* one has sworn.

And although in some case, as Thomas acknowledges in II-II, question 69,
article 2, it is not only lawful but also praiseworthy “to keep silent about
the truth where you are not bound to bring it forward; yet,” he says, “it is
lawful for no one in any case to put forward a falsehood.” For, as Lombard and
the canonists say concerning an oath in *Sentences*, book 3, distinction 39,
by whatever artifice of words anyone swears, God, who is the witness of the
conscience, nevertheless takes it as the one to whom the oath is sworn
understands it. In the same way, a simple assertion must be judged not from a
deceitful reservation but from the agreement of the statement uttered with
the matter proposed by the questioner, since it is directed to him. Nor does
it matter whether one addresses a competent judge, an enemy, or a friend. For
just as a lying document does not borrow truth from its reader, neither does a
lying assertion borrow it from any hearer.

[Marginal heading: 2. Because Scripture condemns this.]

IX. Second, Scripture openly condemns this art when it rebukes those who speak
peace to their neighbor with their mouth but lay snares in their heart (Jer.
9:8), and those who walk in craftiness (2 Cor. 4:2). Hence it commands the
renunciation of τὰ κρυπτὰ τῆς αἰσχύνης περιπατούντων ἐν πανουργίᾳ, “the hidden
things of shame, walking in craftiness,” and commands that *by the
manifestation of the truth we commend ourselves, not only before God, but also
to every human conscience*.

They object in vain that they do not permit equivocation indiscriminately and
without discrimination, but only when a just necessity of concealment so
requires, in order that, while answering truly, one may nevertheless escape
the conscience of the person who has no right to learn the secret. For they
gratuitously suppose that a just necessity can arise for employing an
equivocation that cannot exist without a lie. Although, therefore, they do
not wish it to have a place either in matters of faith or in contracts by
which prejudice may come to others, nor in moral affairs, as in the common
practice of life when truth is required among friends, but only in a
*necessary defense* to avoid some great harm or to obtain a good of no small
importance, elsewhere they extend it further “to any other act of utility or
virtue,” as Garnet says, where “a mental reservation is judged expedient and
carefully contrived,” as Zanchez contends. “In the cause of religion in a time
of persecution, anyone can, and perhaps is bound,” Parsons says, “to use
equivocation.” Moreover, in other matters, through this singular doctrine, as
Navarrus says, *we can*

[Printed p. 66 | Internet Archive PDF 94 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*avoid innumerable sins*. On this principle, according to them, it will be
lawful to equivocate whenever and as often as one pleases, as Mason rightly
shows from their various cases in *The New Art of Lying*, chapter 3. Everyone
sees how dangerous this would be in the church, the commonwealth, and the
mutual dealings of individuals.

[Marginal heading: 3. Because it is opposed to divine, natural, and civil
law.]

X. Third, this is opposed to divine, natural, and civil law: to *divine law*,
which forbids doing evil that good may come; to *natural law*, because it is a
false signification, in conflict with nature and joined with the purpose of
deceiving; and to *civil law*, because, if this is posited, conversations,
oaths, contracts, and dealings will be suspect, and nothing will be safe and
secure.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because it deceives the magistrate.]

XI. Fourth, it is wicked to deceive the magistrate and evade his questions
with ambiguous answers, as Jesuitical equivocations do. For in this way they
deceive God, who presides over judicial proceedings conducted in his name (2
Chron. 19:6), and thus all the judgments of the magistrate are dissolved,
since through equivocation the truth cannot be investigated, controversies
cannot be decided, the guilty cannot be punished, and the innocent cannot be
acquitted.

[Marginal heading: 5. Because mental reservation is a grave sin.]

XII. Fifth, mental equivocation cannot be employed without the gravest sin.
First, it is a direct and manifest lie, because one thing is concealed in the
breast and another uttered with the mouth, and this with the intention of
deceiving. The witness’s words run contrary both to his mind and conscience
and to the thing itself. One who says that no lie is involved here because the
equivocator utters a falsehood not from a desire to deceive but from a desire
to preserve himself will not escape. For “one who thinks one thing and says
another deceives the other person and intends to deceive,” says Toledo
(*Institutes*, book 8, chapter 54). He does not utter it in this way except to
produce a different opinion in the other person’s mind, and that is to
deceive.

Nor does it help that this is said to be done for the purpose of escape. For
although the primary end may be that you escape danger, the intermediate end
is that you escape by deceiving. A robber will say that he commits robbery not
to despoil the traveler but to provide for his own necessities. But the judge
will reply: I do not condemn you because you provided for yourself, but
because you willed to steal in order to do so; for evils must not be done that
good may come.

Second, the nature of an oath is violated by this pretense. An oath is the
most solemn bearing of testimony to others, which cannot occur when we explain
nothing certain by our words, but something fictitious and feigned. Third, it
is a grave injury against God to bring forward his testimony, even
fictitiously, to confirm a false matter. Fourth, in this way a door is opened
to fostering every lie. For, as Azorius says, nothing is so false that it
cannot be freed from every lie if we retain something unspoken in the mind at
our own discretion. We can easily deny that we have whatever is requested of
us, understanding, of course, “to give it”; and we can deny that we did or saw
whatever we have done or seen, in the sense, of course, “so as to tell it to
others.”

Nor should it be answered that a single composite and complete statement, in
which there is no falsehood, is formed from the vocal statement and the mental
restriction, and that it is lawful for a person to compose his statement from
vocal and mental terms. For although such a mixed statement could in some
manner be admitted when a believer speaks with himself or with God alone, who
perceives whatever is in our mind just as well as if it were expressed in
words, it nevertheless cannot have a place in testimony given before human
beings, because a mental reservation testifies nothing to the hearers.
Whatever it may be, therefore, the testimony does not cease to be a lie and,
if it is confirmed by an oath, perjury, since what is signified in the vocal
statement of the testimony conflicts with the witness’s conscience.

[Marginal heading: 6. Because verbal equivocation is unlawful.]

XIII. Sixth, verbal equivocation is no less unlawful in an oath: both because
it testifies to nothing certain, without which an oath is at least vain, and
because it misleads and deceives

[Printed p. 67 | Internet Archive PDF 95 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the hearers, which is opposed to the nature of an oath. Moreover, because an
oath of this kind confirms a lie, it is manifest perjury from the nature of
the thing and the swearer’s very intention, and is a profanation of the divine
name. Nor is the character of perjury or lying removed if an injury is done to
us or we are questioned in a less than juridical manner, as though it were
right for us to look to our own good provided that we speak ambiguously. For
no injury inflicted upon us by human beings can furnish a just cause for
doing injury to the divine name, as occurs in lying and more gravely still in
perjury.

[Marginal heading: 7. It conflicts with the example of Christ and the saints.]

XIV. Seventh, the example of Christ, the apostles, and the martyrs is
especially relevant here, for they are never read to have used equivocations.
For although Christ sometimes remained silent when interrogated, in order to
testify that he laid down his life voluntarily, nevertheless, when he answers
the high priest and Pilate, he speaks openly (John 18:19), and is said to have
made the noble confession (1 Tim. 6:13). The apostles act in the same way in
Acts before Jews and Gentiles. And the martyrs, when brought before the
tribunals of unbelieving judges, neither evaded questions nor answered
ambiguously.

[Marginal heading: 8. It is pressed by various absurdities.]

XV. Eighth, the adversaries’ position is pressed by various most grave
absurdities. For if it is lawful to make an openly lying statement true by
adding a qualification that anyone may devise in his mind as he pleases, it
will follow, first, that God gave laws against lying or perjury in vain, since
there is no one to whom such a mental qualification could not repeatedly
occur. Second, the devil is not the father of lies and never lies, since such
a reservation can never be lacking to him. Third, the martyrs were not only
fools, but also unjust to the truth and to themselves, since everyone,
especially when life is in danger, is bound to use every lawful defense by
which he may extricate himself. This could easily have been done according to
this Loyolan hypothesis. Fourth, every basis for trust in judicial
proceedings, covenants, and contracts is removed, whether they are public
between princes or states or private in any dealings whatsoever, if a mental
reservation lies hidden that overturns and renders everything doubtful.

Since, therefore, this στρεψοδικοπανουργία, or law-twisting knavery, is
burdened by so many and such absurdities, it is abundantly clear how
reasonably not only the orthodox but also the more judicious Roman Catholics
have hitherto rejected and opposed so foul and monstrous a fiction.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XVI. It is one thing *to keep silent about something true*, and another *to
say something false*. It is not a lie when the truth is hidden by silence,
but when a falsehood is brought forth in speech. Thus Augustine responds to
the Priscillianists, who excused lying by the example of the patriarchs, in
*Against Lying*, chapter 10. Abraham could keep silent about something true
when he did not say that Sarah was his wife (Gen. 12:18), because this was not
precisely asked of him, nor was it necessary that he disclose it of his own
accord. But he said nothing false when he called her his sister, because,
“Truly,” he says, “she is my sister, the daughter of my father” (chapter
20:12).

Furthermore, examples of the saints are recorded in Scripture but are not
immediately commended; one must live by laws, not examples. Hence Augustine
says: “When we read these things in the Holy Scriptures, let us not believe
that, because we believe they were done, they also ought to be done, lest we
violate the commandments while indiscriminately following examples”
(*Against Lying*, chapter 9).

XVII. Promises and threats that have a tacit condition do not favor
Jesuitical equivocation. For although the condition is tacit and not
expressed, it was nevertheless not unknown to those to whom they were
proposed.

[Marginal citation: Jonah 3:4.]

Thus Jonah’s threat of destruction made to the Ninevites included a
qualification known to everyone, *unless they should repent*. The Ninevites
themselves understood this and therefore repented.

Likewise, when Samuel professes that he had come to Bethlehem to sacrifice to
the Lord (1 Sam. 16:5), he says something

[Printed p. 68 | Internet Archive PDF 96 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

that he was going to do and that he actually did, but did not further disclose
what he was about to do, because there was no need.

XVIII. Christ does not use equivocation in John 7:8 when he says that he was
*not going up to the feast*, although he had nevertheless tacitly resolved
within himself to go up, as he did a little later. For he does not deny
without qualification that he will go up, but only in a certain respect with
regard to a definite time: Ἐγὼ οὔπω ἀναβαίνω, “I am not yet going up,” because
his appointed time, or the hour fixed for going up, had not yet come, as he
immediately adds. Therefore, he postponed his ascent; he did not deny it.

Likewise, in Mark 13:32 he denies without qualification that he knows the day
and hour of judgment, without any mental reservation, but he understands this
according to his human nature, by which he is the Son of Man.

[Marginal citation: Luke 24:28.]

Christ’s action by which he made as though he would go farther while
accompanying the disciples traveling to Emmaus implies no equivocation, but a
concealment of his purpose, which belonged to prudence, not equivocation. “The
Lord intended to proceed farther,” says the Jesuit Barradius (volume 4, book
8, chapter 12), “if he were not detained by the disciples.” “Truth feigns
nothing,” says Ribera. “If they had not detained him, he would undoubtedly
have passed on and gone farther.”

XIX. An *investigation* for eliciting the truth is one thing; a *subterfuge*
for disguising a lie is another.

[Marginal citations: 1 Kings 3; John 6:6.]

The first is confirmed from Solomon’s
practice in the case of the prostitutes and from that of the Savior with
Philip, whom he questioned about the loaves, “testing him, for he himself knew
what he was going to do” (John 6:6). Hence it is evident that tests differ
widely from equivocations, and that prudently testing someone or concealing a
purpose is as far removed from lying as those who defend a lie are removed
from Christian sincerity. In making a test it is lawful to conceal the truth,
but it is not lawful to lie, deceive, or perjure yourself so that you may test
more safely.

XX. Physicians’ feigned stories for soothing the delirious always have an
understood qualification that is very well known from usage, so that it is
clear from the matter at hand and the end they propose that the physicians do
not feign in order to deceive but only relate fictitious things in order to
care for the sick person’s infirmity. An explanation added at the conclusion
of the affair, not the perfidious imposture of equivocation, remedies
everything.

XXI. When it is asked what must be done when a prince or one’s country is in
danger, or to preserve the chastity of one’s wife or mother, or to escape in
one’s own danger the savagery of an attacking tyrant, or to turn a robber and
murderer away from an innocent traveler, no sure and just subterfuge for
escape can be found in equivocation, which cannot occur without sin.

Rather, let Firmus, bishop of Thagaste, be heard in Augustine’s *On Lying to
Consentius*, chapter 13. When questioned about a man sought by the officers,
he said, “I will neither betray him nor lie.” And let the woman on the rack be
heard in Jerome’s *To Innocent*: “I do not therefore wish to deny it lest I
perish, but I therefore refuse to lie lest I sin.” We must therefore do what
belongs to our duty, leaving the rest to the providence of God, which knows
how to provide for the innocent without the defense of sin.
