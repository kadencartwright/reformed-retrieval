Verse 7. The devastation of the wicked will cut apart those who have refused to exercise judgment.

In יגור (*dissecabit*, will cut apart) there is a metaphor transferred from civil public punishment, by which malefactors were subjected to the saw, to God’s horrible vengeance upon the wicked.

The purpose is to deter the wicked from injustice toward their neighbor by the severity of divine punishment.

Once a sin has been committed, people, provided they are safe and sheltered from the Magistrate’s sword or axe, count as gain whatever advantage or pleasure they have obtained from deeds unjustly done. In this, after the manner of beasts, they look only to that by which their senses are moved and which is present, directing their mind little or not at all to that which follows. They do not fear God, whom they do not behold with their eyes. Because they do not apprehend by their senses his sword—though it is polished and sharpened, and indeed by God’s sentence steeped in their blood in heaven and, as the Prophet speaks, made drunk—and because it has not yet been brought to their throat, they do not dread it. Because the prison of hell and the demons who are its lictors are unseen, they regard them as sport and jest. But their wickedness will cost them no less than if a most severe Magistrate exacted the punishments for their perpetrated crime. Indeed, it would have been far better to have felt the avenging hand of men than, by experiencing God’s severity, to fall into the hands of the living God.

Therefore, the Holy Spirit, foreseeing that there would be a great multitude of sinners among men and that it would advance into unbridled license, beaten back by no barriers of laws, prepares a remedy in this Proverb for so great an evil when he pronounces that violation of one’s neighbor will be avenged by God. By this very fact, the Prophet in this passage holds out before them the saw or axe of God the supreme Judge, with which he exacts punishment from evildoers, its edge turned toward their faces, as is customarily done to those condemned to death. Therefore he teaches that they must not only be punished with death but with a death exceedingly ignominious. For thus we read at 2 Sam. 12.31 that the Ammonites, who had broken public faith by violating ambassadors, were subjected to punishment; see Dan. 2.5 and 3.29; see Luke 12.46.

From this the distinction between God’s judgments upon strangers and upon his own is evident. For he chastens his own with a pruning hook, as a farmer prunes and trims his vines; strangers, however, he subjects to saws and axes. He moves the former with a light arm, but drives the latter violently with exerted strength. He reduces those to desolation and waste; these

[1632 witness | PDF p. 462 | printed columns 908-909]

however, he grazes gently. He cuts those out by the roots so that they may never grow again, but he cuts into these in such a way that they quickly put forth shoots again and rise to greater height.

And thus far concerning the effect; the cause follows, because they refuse to exercise justice.

Scripture generally desires the testing and examination of faith to be undertaken in those duties which are directed toward one’s neighbor rather than those which are directed immediately toward God, because in those hypocrisies it is sustained with greater difficulty, as was said before.

It is evident in the eyes of all that people are generally deterred from evil deeds only by fear of punishment, and that the hope of impunity is a great enticement to sin. But injuries inflicted upon one’s neighbor and visited with punishment by no human laws are infinite in number. For what punishment imposed by the laws do refusal of obedience to parents, sword fights or combats, adulteries, fornications, drunkennesses, oppressions, frauds, lies, and so forth have? And among so infinite a number of duties, for the recounting of which a day would not suffice, if you except the crime of treason, murder, and the kind of secret theft, what such great deed and enormity, expressed in act or words, is there against which human laws establish anything more severe?

It must not be passed over that he says, *because they refuse to exercise*, and so forth. Therefore, however well they speak and even profess that they execute right, unless they execute judgment they will pay the penalties. Then, if they are liable to God’s judgment for this reason, that they refuse to execute judgment and right, much more must they be dealt with as guilty if they admit that which is contrary to right and judgment. By this it is declared that the devastation and destruction of the wicked are summoned by themselves, although they are accustomed to refer the cause of their own destruction to God.

Verse 8. A man in his way [a variable man] is at once changeable and alien; but the pure man is upright in his work.

In הפכפך (*volubilis*, changeable) there is a metaphor drawn from light bodily gesticulation and transferred to signify levity and inconstancy of mind.

The purpose is to exhort us to sincerity of mind, once levity and inconstancy have been rejected.

One who is variable and inconstant in his pursuits and practices is displeasing to God. He is one who turns himself into every form, like a Chameleon or Proteus; who has a double heart, Ps. 12.3 and James 1.8, as one who directs his tongue with one heart but retains the meaning of his mind within himself with the other. He is also called *bilinguis*, double-tongued, at 1 Tim. 3.8. Of this kind are those who in divine worship follow that which is best adapted to their own honor, advantage, or pleasure; who change their religion and faith at a single syllable from the Prince; and who hold one opinion while standing and another while sitting. The godly are their opposites: from their firmness the Apostle calls them ἑδραῖοι, steadfast, at 1 Cor. 15.58; and at Col. 1.23 he also contrasts with these flighty people those who are τεθημελιωμένους, founded, and ἀμετακινήτους, immovable. Of this kind likewise are those who are flighty, changeable, and color-shifting in their duties toward their brethren: now they love, now they hate; today they are friends, tomorrow enemies; today they look upon you with a serene countenance, tomorrow with a grim one. Finally, they are those who are not constant in their particular calling: now they wish to practice trade, now agriculture.

In the law the Lord appears to wish to fortify his people against such changeableness by certain ceremonies enacted concerning this matter, Deut. 22.9, 10, 12. In this passage, however, by the first simile he illustrates its disgrace. For just as bodily gesticulation and turning this way and that is customarily censured—as the ridiculous agitation of David’s bodily members before Achish is described by our proverb, “He looketh 9. wayes at once.” Hence it is also confirmed in Solomon’s Proverb that a fool is known from his gait—so inconstancy of mind and

changeableness most justly incurs the censure of everyone.

Next he deters us from this vice by the estrangement joined to it—understand: from God and men. For God also hates those who are exceedingly unlike him and who depart from him in order to approve themselves to men, and the godly pursue them with hatred, Ps. 26.4, 5. This argument is illustrated from opposites, because, namely, one who walks straight and leads an even and, as it were, single-colored life is pure.

This Proverb therefore refutes the ancient Priscillianist heresy. They thought it permissible to deny the truth in words, provided that they thought rightly in their mind; this error has revived in our age. Hence those who falsely put Nicodemus forward in their own defense are also called Pseudo-Nicodemites.

We must beware, however, lest through the appearance of constancy in an opinion once adopted we fall into obstinacy, a vice no less hateful to God than that leaping levity of which we have already spoken. Especially liable to this vice are those who, although they possess great ability, also think more magnificently of themselves than is fitting, as well as those who have very great power and authority. For each of these kinds of people thinks that they concede very much from themselves and from their reputation if they should afterwards cast away an opinion they once seized. Hence also that axiom appears to have arisen, τὸ κακὸν καλῶς κείμενον ἀκίνητον, “an evil well established is immovable”; but in the Philosopher, unless I am mistaken, it is δυσκίνητον, “hard to move.” See Daniel and Esther concerning the unalterable laws of the Medes and Persians; likewise that statement of Pilate, “What I wrote, I wrote”; indeed, even of David, who, once the injustice was known, was unwilling entirely to rescind and break the most unjust sentence he had passed against Mephibosheth. Carefully avoiding these snares of Satan on both sides, therefore, let us hold to a middle way, so that we are tenacious only of that which is proved to be just and godly, but ready to move away from those things which we have rashly undertaken.

Verse 9. It is better to dwell in a corner of the roof than to dwell with a woman of contentions and in a shared house.

In אשה (*muliere*, with a woman) there is a synecdoche of the genus, namely *woman* for *wife*. See what was said before on 19.13.

The purpose is to sharpen people’s caution in the choice of spouses.

The latter part of the verse declares that not just any woman is understood in this passage, but only a wife, since it denotes only one with whom a man is so joined in fellowship that he cannot be separated from her. For a maidservant, if she is insolent and refractory, can be driven from the house. Indeed, even a daughter, if she is disobedient to her Father and does not amend after being chastised, must be brought before the Magistrates and condemned to death, Deut. 21.18, and so forth. Therefore this passage must be understood of that union and fellowship which, once entered, cannot be dissolved unless adultery is committed—or, rather, which is not altogether dissolved, because when she commits adultery, she then ceases to be a wife. Hence, when Solomon at 19.13 establishes a comparison between a wicked son and a quarrelsome woman, he teaches, not obscurely, that this latter evil is more grievous. Therefore it must be noted in passing from this Proverb that the bond of marriage is by far the tightest, since it cannot be severed by so great and deadly an evil, and how even under the law the divorces of the Jews for any cause whatsoever and for a slight offense were unlawful.

Since the evil of a contentious wife is taught by the Proverb, the first question is who, after all, is the woman who ought to be regarded as contentious. Is it one who flies at her husband, lays violent hands upon him, or joins battle with him? Away with monsters of this kind rather than women, who must be restrained more by a stick than imbued with precepts and sound counsels. But in this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 464 | printed columns 912-913]

passage those women are especially designated who are proud, since those same women are always contentious; Prov. 13.10. Moreover, what kind of woman this is becomes known from the opposite virtue of a dutiful wife. The distinctive virtue of a Christian wife is proposed at 1 Pet. 3.4: a spirit of gentleness and meekness. Its effect and fruit is declared in the following verse, namely, that they present themselves as obedient to their own husband in lawful and honorable matters. Therefore those women must be regarded as contentious who do not obey their husbands in lawful and honorable matters; who are quarrelsome, as at chapter 19.13; who, although they are one flesh with their husbands, nevertheless are not one will with them, as though one tongue, hands, and feet; but who will something other than their husbands, hate those whom their husbands love, and love those whom they hate.

The evil of this disobedient woman is illustrated by a comparison with a lesser evil, namely that it is better to dwell in a corner of the roof than with her in a house, though it is spacious. The roofs of the Jews were flat by law, and there was a floor laid and placed beneath the feet upon which one could stand firmly and without danger. Thus by this form of speech he teaches that it is better to dwell in the open air, where one would be exposed to winds, storm, cold, heat, and rain, than to dwell in one house with a contentious wife. When, however, he says *in a corner*, he further magnifies the evil of a contentious wife: it is not only better to dwell in the open air, but even to dwell in a very narrow place in the open air, than in one house, though exceedingly spacious, with a contentious wife. For one who dwells in the open air sometimes receives pleasant and delightful air and enjoys the light of the sun, which cheers a person; but a contentious woman is like a continual dripping, Prov. 19.13.

Here someone objects: “Why, then, should I not separate myself from her, since it is better to lead one’s life alone than with a contentious woman?” I answer that *good* in this passage is not taken for that which is joined with duty, but only for that which has less inconvenience and trouble. It is not, however, lawful to forsake one’s duty through flight from and disgust at inconvenience.

From this it is evident what power sin and a woman’s departure from duty possess. For at Gen. 2 God had pronounced that it was not good to lead a solitary life without a woman’s help and companionship; but a woman’s insolence brings it about that it is preferable for a man to be alone than to be joined to a woman. God will certainly never allow such an inversion of the divine ordinance to go unpunished.

From this let women learn that it is not enough if they preserve themselves chaste unless they also render obedience to their husbands; and that they are not a help to their husbands, as they were appointed by God to be, but rather an impediment, unless, and so forth.

Next young men and young women together are taught to take great account in the choice of spouses, and to concern themselves not so much—as is customary—with beauty, dowry, and ample revenues as with piety and a well-formed upbringing. For a spacious house and landed estates will not help them so much as wickedness will grieve them and bring trouble. Although men determine within themselves that, provided they obtain women who are well endowed, those women will easily be brought back to godliness and duty.
