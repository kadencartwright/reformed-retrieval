## Part 19 — First body, Proposition XVIII

PROPOSITION EIGHTEEN.

SECTION ONE.

ARMINIUS. First, it follows from the foregoing that punishment cannot justly be prepared, by a
pure act of divine good pleasure, for those passed over, on account of foreseen sin which, because
of their just preterition and abandonment in nature, they necessarily had to commit.

JUNIUS. To the first. 1. It is denied that Adam was passed over by God in common and left in nature.
Rather, by the measure of nature he was merely left to himself in the single, natural deed which
lay within nature alone; and he was diligently forewarned by God and instructed by God as if by an
express agreement. 2. It is denied that, because of that preterition, he necessarily had to commit
the sin. For this would have been so if there had been a necessitating habit or a passible quality
in human nature; but his power was free toward either alternative, and his will was contingently
borne toward this or that. Therefore the sin did not have to be committed necessarily. He
accordingly committed it contingently—as Scripture and the consensus of the church have always
taught—by virtue of nature’s free power, which belongs to the will. For the wise man rightly says
in Ecclesiastes 7, “This I have found: God made man upright, but they have sought out many
reasonings.”

ARMINIUS. The former argument rests upon the supposition of the definition by which preterition is
defined in your theses. It reads as follows: “Preterition is an act of divine good pleasure by
which God determined from eternity to leave certain creatures in their own nature and not to
communicate to them the supernatural grace by which their uncorrupted nature might be confirmed or
their corrupted nature restored, for the declaration of the freedom of his goodness.” But that
nature is one which has no supernatural or divine power, as stated in Thesis 10 of the same
disputation. For those passed over are left in the same nature and considered in the same manner
from which, and as considered in which, those predestined are raised up. Those left in such a
nature, which has no supernatural or divine power, cannot perform a law that cannot be performed
without supernatural grace. Therefore punishment cannot justly be prepared for them on account of
a sin of this kind committed against a law which they could not perform.

2. Accordingly, the first thing denied appears to me to be beside the point. For we are not dealing
with the manner in which Adam was left to his nature and entrusted to himself. For Adam’s
abandonment at that time in the hand of his own counsel belongs not to the decree of preterition but
to the providence by which God acts without distinction between the predestined and the reprobate,
and so forth.

3. I answer the second denial. From abandonment in a nature which has no supernatural or divine
power, a sin which cannot be avoided without supernatural grace must necessarily be committed by
one so abandoned. The will is indeed free, but not with respect to that act which cannot be either
performed or omitted without supernatural grace, just as the same will is not free with respect to
the act by which it wills the universal good—indeed, its own good. The reason is that in

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 72 | BSB canvas 84]

man there is a passible quality inclining toward that forbidden act and ready to move the will to
consent to the same act.

4. The negative act of God which preceded human sin belongs either to providence or to reprobation
or preterition insofar as the latter is distinguished over against providence. It does not belong
to reprobation. 1. The act of reprobation concerns some human beings, not all, for not all human
beings are reprobate. 2. If sin arises from an act of reprobation, or not without it, then only some
human beings sin and some do not. Namely, those concerning whom God exercises his negative act of
preterition sin, while those concerning whom he does not exercise the same act do not sin. But all
sinned; therefore sin did not arise from that act. 3. If sin arises from the negative act of
reprobation, it now follows that Adam and all human beings in him are reprobate. For Adam and all
human beings in him sinned. 4. Conversely, if human sin arises from the negative act of
preterition, then from the affirmative act of predestination, which must be opposed to it at the
same moment, comes man’s perseverance in the good, at least with respect to that single act. But no
man persists in the good in which he was created from the affirmative act of predestination.
Therefore. 5. Those to whom God once, by the negative act of reprobation, denies efficacious aid,
he finally denies efficacious aid; otherwise the reprobate are not reprobate. But he does not
finally deny efficacious aid to all human beings, for then all would be reprobate. Therefore the
act by which efficacious aid was once denied to all human beings is not an act of reprobation.

5. Nevertheless, some negative act of God preceded human sin, for otherwise man would not have
sinned. Therefore that act belongs to providence. Here two things must be considered. First, sin
does not arise necessarily from that negative act, but while that act stands it can either occur or
not occur. For providence ordered man to eternal life and furnished means sufficient and necessary
to attain it, leaving the free use of those means to man’s choice and not willing to hinder him.
From this negative act of God, who was unwilling effectually to prevent sin—to which the opposite
act exists—it follows that man was actually able to sin, not that he actually sinned. But because
God, by the infinity of his knowledge, saw that man would fall at a definite time, the fall
therefore occurred infallibly with respect only to that foreknowledge, not with respect to any act
of God’s will, whether affirmative, and so forth.

6. Second, it must be considered that God’s providence does not make a precise distinction among
human beings insofar as they are elect and insofar as they are reprobate. Consequently, that
negative act of God concerns all human beings commonly and universally, without any distinction
between elect and reprobate. From these things I conclude: because this negative act which preceded
sin belongs not to reprobation or preterition but to providence as distinguished over against it,
it follows that God did not by an act of preterition concern himself with human beings outside sin,
or as considered not yet sinners. For no negative act of preterition preceded this negative act of
providence, whether in order or in time. Nor did any other act of preterition intervene between
this act of providence and sin.

7. There is no need here to prove that man sinned not necessarily but freely. That is not disputed.
What had to be taught was that, once this preterition was posited, man nevertheless sinned freely
and not necessarily.

RESPONSE. 1. It is false that, because of this preterition and so forth, they necessarily had to
commit sin. For they did not have to commit it by duty, nor did they commit it necessarily in fact;
rather, once preterition—or the decree to permit sin—was posited, they were indeed going to sin,
but they were going to sin freely.

2. As for Arminius, one cannot help wondering by what reasoning he first says that Adam was not
passed over and afterward, namely in the second place, supposes that he was passed over, when he
says, “It is denied that, because of that preterition, he necessarily had to commit the sin.” But I
resolve this difficulty as follows and free Junius from contradiction. Preterition as defined by
Junius in his theses appears to be only the preterition of reprobation. In angels this consisted in
the denial of supernatural grace by which their uncorrupted nature would be confirmed; in human
beings, however, it consisted in the denial of supernatural grace by which their corrupted nature
would be restored. Junius therefore rightly denies that Adam was passed over in common or left in
a nature which had no supernatural power—for he has declared more than once already that Adam was
equipped with supernatural gifts. Yet Adam was left to himself in the single, natural deed which
lay within nature alone. For it lay within nature either to pluck the fruit or not to pluck it, to
taste or to refrain from tasting. But the preterition which Junius sets forth in the second place
is merely the permission of sin, not reprobation, and, unless I am mistaken, it is a typographical
slip. It seems that he should have said, “It is denied that, because of that permission”—not
“because of that preterition”—“he necessarily had to commit the sin.”

3. We come to Arminius. Although the preterition defined by Junius is the preterition of
reprobation with respect to angels, as regards the first part of the definition, nevertheless we
do not deny that Adam had thus far been passed over in common insofar as his uncorrupted nature was
by no means confirmed by God through efficacious grace. But in that case “nature” must not be
understood in the same way as when the preterition of reprobation is under discussion. For that
description of nature looks only to human beings with respect to their restoration. Although,
according to the opinion of Junius, man as the object of predestination and reprobation was
considered in one respect as constituted in natural endowments, Junius nevertheless did not mean
that Adam was created in a nature of such a kind that it possessed no supernatural or divine
power. Whether these things cohere and agree is another question; as the matter arose, we have
already explained what occurred to us. Yet this was the opinion of Junius, as is clear from the
foregoing. Therefore Junius meant both that the moral law was performable by Adam through the
grace with which he was imbued and that the particular law forbidding him the fruit of the tree of
knowledge was performable apart from grace. Arminius therefore falsely supposes that, according to
the opinion of Junius, the law given to Adam was not performable by Adam. These claims proceeded
from the preterition defined by Junius but wrongly understood by Arminius.

2. If Junius had merely said that Adam was left to himself, Arminius would have answered
inadequately, as usual; but since he expressly denies that Adam was passed over by God and left in
nature, his statements are by no means beside the point. “We are dealing,” Arminius says, “with
abandonment in nature which belongs to the decree of preterition.” Let it be so. Junius meant the
object of this decree—among other considerations—to be man considered in his purely natural
endowments, and all human beings in Adam as sharers in his primeval state. “But,” you say, “they
were not thus left in a nature which possesses no supernatural or divine power.” Nor did Junius
mean that the nature of Adam, or of human beings in Adam, was of that kind. Rather, although they
were created in Adam with supernatural gifts, he meant that God considered them only as in their
purely natural endowments. For it is not necessary that consideration of everything belonging to
man be required to constitute a suitable object of predestination and reprobation.

3. Junius does not deny the divine gifts infused into Adam, but he denies that consideration of
those gifts pertains to constituting a suitable object of predestination and reprobation.
Therefore it does not follow that Adam necessarily had to commit the sin. And Junius has already
made it sufficiently clear—with Arminius acknowledging this—that Adam was imbued with supernatural
grace before the fall. Meanwhile, it is true that he was left in such a way that, notwithstanding
the supernatural gifts by which he could sufficiently avoid every sin, it could be said to have
been impossible for the sin to be avoided, just as it was impossible for Christ’s bones to be
broken, although the soldiers did not lack

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 73 | BSB canvas 85]

ample power to crush Christ’s bones. This was not because Adam lacked all supernatural grace but
only because he lacked efficacious grace. Accordingly, by reason of the supernatural grace which
Adam possessed, Adam’s will was sufficiently powerful to perform God’s law; and therefore, when he
transgressed, he freely transgressed God’s law. Thus it is false that there was in man a passible
quality inclining toward that forbidden act and ready to move his will to commit the same act. For
an inclination of this kind—namely, toward the forbidden act—was not natural to man. Arminius,
however, learned from his Jesuits that it was natural, “being evil” (*kakon ōn*). And even if it had
been natural, Adam was still so supported by the supernatural grace which Junius more than once
attributes to him before the fall that Adam’s nature, by its first constitution, was fitted for
obedience rather than defiance.

4. When Arminius, in his usual fashion, distinguishes the negative act into an act of providence or
of reprobation insofar as reprobation is distinguished over against providence, it is just as if
someone said that every living thing is either an animal or a man insofar as man is distinguished
over against animal. But it is absurd, I say, to distinguish a species over against its genus or a
part over against the whole. So far as I know, moreover, all learned men declare predestination to
be either a subjective or an integral part of providence. For they establish the difference as
follows: providence directs all creatures, not merely some, and directs them toward all their ends,
not only natural but also supernatural. Predestination, however, concerns only rational creatures,
directing them toward their ends which are not natural but supernatural. But let that go; let the
distinction proceed. We deny that this negative act of God belonged either to providence or to
reprobation. For it could belong, and did belong, equally to both predestination and reprobation,
and consequently it was also an act of providence. For every act of predestination and reprobation
is also an act of providence, although not every act of providence is an act of predestination or
reprobation. We nevertheless say that it also belonged to reprobation, though not to reprobation
alone, but rather to predestination in general insofar as it embraces both reprobation and
election. The reason is that election is the decree to confer the grace by which someone rises
again from sin. Reprobation is the decree to deny the grace by which someone rises again from sin.
Therefore God’s act by which he permits sin in all serves the execution of both election and
reprobation. And because God wills to manifest his glory in this manner, he must will to permit sin
in all—sin of such a kind that, in accordance with God’s decree, all are conceived and born in sin.
From this you may see and observe how Arminius’s arguments droop and altogether come to nothing,
since they rest solely on their author’s ignorance and lack of knowledge of the truth. “The
negative act of God which preceded sin,” he says, “does not belong to reprobation, because the act
of reprobation concerns some human beings, not all.” Let us pass over how absurdly he disputes
about a single negative act preceding Adam’s sin, as if many more had not preceded it. But to the
argument. From this it follows only that the act did not belong to reprobation alone. We do not say
that it belonged to reprobation alone, but also to election—that is, to predestination in general
insofar as it embraces both election and reprobation. 2. Likewise, to the second, we say that the
sin of all served the execution of predestination in general, for it was necessary both that the
former should be saved from mercy and that the latter should be condemned from justice. 3. To the
third, it follows only that Adam and all in him were predestined, either to life or to death. 4. To
the fourth. It is false that it follows from the act of predestination that man would persevere in
his first state. For the act preceding sin did not belong to reprobation but was the permission of
sin, serving equally the execution of both election and reprobation—that is, of predestination in
general, whether to life or to death. 5. God finally denies efficacious aid to those to whom he
denies it by an act of reprobation. This is true of the special act of reprobation opposed to the
act of election. But the negative act of God which preceded sin is not the special act of
reprobation opposed to the act of election. It is an act common to both and serving equally the
execution of both.

5. If it is an act of providence, then it is an act either of predestination generally or,
specifically, of election and reprobation, because providence concerned with man and his
supernatural end is called predestination. Certainly that negative act—so that we may cease
contending about words—was the act of permitting Adam’s sin. From it, I acknowledge, sin did not
arise necessarily but freely. Yet sin certainly arose in such a way that, while that permission
stood—namely, by the denial of efficacious grace—it was impossible for it not to arise, though it
arose freely. That is, it was impossible for it not to arise freely. In the same way, once God’s
decree that Christ’s bones should not be broken was posited, it was impossible for them not to
remain unbroken, and to remain unbroken freely; for without doubt the soldiers freely refrained
from breaking Christ’s bones. Likewise, once God’s decree to create the world was posited, it was
impossible for the world not to be created. What, then? Was it therefore created necessarily?
Nothing less; it was created freely. Therefore it was impossible for it not to be created in the
way in which it was created—that is, most freely. But you will say that whatever occurs in such a
way that it cannot occur otherwise occurs necessarily. I answer: certainly whatever occurs in such
a way that it cannot occur otherwise absolutely does occur necessarily. But what occurs in such a
way that it cannot occur otherwise, not absolutely but from a supposition, by no means occurs
necessarily. “Providence,” Arminius says, “ordered man to eternal life and furnished means sufficient
to attain it, leaving the free use of those means to man’s choice.” These things are true of
providence insofar as it is limited to predestination, not insofar as it is distinguished from
predestination, as the wakeful Arminius dreams. For insofar as God predestined that his glory be
made known in the human race by way of sparing mercy and punitive justice, it was necessary for the
execution of this counsel both that sufficient means for attaining eternal life be granted and
that efficacious grace for using those means suitably be denied. Meanwhile, the denial of that
efficacious grace neither hindered human freedom nor annulled what God had ordained. Observe in
passing, however, that Arminius incidentally insists upon a falsehood: that an act of willing
effectually to prevent sin would have been unfitting. He supplies no reason to establish the claim.
And why would it have been more unfitting in the case of man than in that of the elect angels?
Again, “the act finally preventing sin,” he says, “would have belonged to predestination.” You
silently suggest that the act of being unwilling effectually to prevent sin belongs not to
predestination but to providence. Yet we have already proved that it was an act of predestination
insofar as predestination embraces election and reprobation, and by that very fact an act of
providence; for every act of predestination is an act of providence, though not conversely. It is
utterly false that the act of sin occurred infallibly with respect only to God’s foreknowledge and
not with respect to any act of his will. It also occurred infallibly with respect to an act of his
will. I prove it as follows, though Arminius, by that authority which is his, is accustomed to enter
the scholastic arena to dictate rather than to argue. Before every act of divine foreknowledge
called knowledge of vision—such as the knowledge of future things—things passed from a possible
state into a future state. For they cannot be foreknown as future unless they have already been
made future from among

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 74 | BSB canvas 86]

possibilities. Arminius acknowledges this. Although it seems more probable to me that these things
should be joined rather than distinguished by moments of nature, our reasoning will nevertheless
retain its force. From where, then, do possible things become future things? Not from their own
nature, for by their own nature all things outside God are merely possible. They therefore have
this from elsewhere: either from an act of the intellect or an act of the will. Not from an act of
the intellect; for by foreknowing future things God does not make them future, but rather supposes
them to be so. Therefore they become future from an act of his will. Again, the sin in question was
a sin of commission and therefore had an underlying natural act, namely the act of eating. But it
is false that the act of eating, however sinful, occurred infallibly only with respect to divine
foreknowledge and not with respect to any act of God’s will. For unless God had decreed that he
would be the efficient cause of that act of eating, he would never have foreseen it as future. It
therefore had necessarily to occur—upon the supposition, namely, both of God’s foreknowledge and of
God’s will decreeing to effect the underlying act and to permit its wickedness by denying the
efficacious grace for avoiding that sin. Thus the distinction Arminius invents between occurring
infallibly and occurring necessarily is rotten. For not even infinite knowledge can foreknow
things which are not future; nor are they future because of foreknowledge. Rather, foreknowledge
must presuppose an act of the will by which things which in themselves are merely possible pass
from possibilities into future things. The distinction between them is only formal, while they are
entirely inseparable. For not only does what occurs necessarily occur infallibly; what occurs
infallibly also occurs necessarily, precisely because it occurs infallibly.

6. Providence does not discriminate, but neither does predestination; and that negative act belongs
to providence only because it belongs to predestination. For providence concerned with rational
creatures and their supernatural ends is predestination itself. Here, however, I understand
predestination insofar as it embraces election and reprobation. Consequently, the act belongs no
more to reprobation than to election, nor was God by it more concerned with human beings not yet
sinners through an act of reprobation than through an act of election, since it served equally the
execution of both. Nor, when I say that it was an act of election, was I required to suppose that
it was also an act of preservation in the good. For since election is the decree to confer human
salvation from sparing mercy, not preservation in the good but the permission of evil is found to
be especially suitable for executing this counsel.

7. Arminius introduced this entire philosophizing about that negative act as though it were an act
of reprobation—when it is merely the permission of sin—only at his pleasure, so that, by instituting
a longer dissertation upon it which would nevertheless come to nothing, he might frustrate the
reader’s expectation. The reader was waiting to see how Arminius would prove that, according to the
opinion of Junius, Adam’s fall occurred necessarily. Yet Junius was so far from denying that the law
given to Adam was performable by him through grace that he went further and affirmed that it was
performable by nature. In refuting this Arminius has nowhere yet acquitted himself successfully,
but has conducted himself as though his mind were entangled like birds hindered by birdlime from
flying away. It was therefore by no means difficult for Junius to teach that man sinned freely,
since he sinned in a matter whose performance did not at all exceed the powers of nature. Yet it
does not therefore follow that he did not sin necessarily—namely, upon the supposition both of
God’s will decreeing that sin should occur while he permitted it and of God’s foreknowledge. But
Arminius has not yet been able to digest the fact that this necessity, said to arise from a
hypothesis, agrees perfectly with freedom absolutely considered, unless he should at the same time
abandon his cause. Yet boldly and vigorously he does not so much attack it as rant against it at
the top of his lungs but without substance. Doubtless the Christian commonwealth will fare very
well once the fancies of a haughty doctor are thrust upon us as principles either of philosophy or
of theology.

SECTION TWO.

ARMINIUS. Second, the punishment ordained for them is spiritual. But spiritual punishment cannot
be ordained for those who would depart from their origin unless, conversely, a spiritual reward
had been prepared for those who would persist in their origin. Yet a reward of this sort was not
prepared for such persons. For they were able to persist in their origin by the powers of nature
alone, but spiritual happiness cannot be acquired by those powers.

JUNIUS. To the second. The word “also” must be added to the proposition, in this manner: “the
punishment ordained for them is also spiritual.” For punishment of both kinds, namely bodily and
spiritual, was instituted for them, as Scripture attests. But the assumption which says that a
reward of this sort was not prepared for them in common if they should persist in their origin is
denied. For it is altogether clear that it was proposed to them, if they should persist, by the
covenant of nature and their ordination to grace; it was likewise signified by the symbol of the
tree of life and promulgated by the threat of death. For what? “Death,” and so forth.

But in order to prove this assertion you add, “For they were able to persist in their origin by the
powers of nature alone.” This is denied. They were able to persist by those powers only in natural
matters, by no means in matters ordained to grace. But you will say that the argument based on
Thomas’s supposition is certain, for he wished man in the cause of election to be considered in his
purely natural endowments. We answer, first, that this does not touch us, who affirm that man was
considered by God in common in the cause of election. Second, although Thomas sometimes speaks in
this way, he must be understood rightly, because the expression is used equivocally here. For the
principle of election is one thing—although we explained the same Thomas’s mind on that matter in
Proposition Six—and Adam’s condition when he fell into sin is another. From all Thomas’s writings it
is clear that it did not even enter his mind in a dream that Adam was then in his purely natural
endowments. For how could one think this who everywhere openly cries out that man was created in
supernatural grace, and who expressly maintains this very thing against the Master of the
Sentences? Therefore the supposition is false, and so forth. Man could not persist in his condition
and state by the powers of nature alone, nor acquire spiritual happiness by them; for spiritual
happiness is not the wages of laborers but the inheritance of children in Christ, given through
grace, not earned by labor.

ARMINIUS. The second argument also rests upon a supposition which I seem to have taken from your
Theses. It consists of two parts. The former is: supernatural happiness cannot be acquired by the
powers of nature alone. The latter: the law given to Adam could be performed by the powers of
nature alone. The former part is true. The latter is in your theses: man is left in a nature which
has no supernatural or divine power. Yet he was still able to perform the law; otherwise God is
unjust, since he imposes upon a creature a law which cannot be performed. From this I concluded
that a spiritual punishment ought not to have been assigned to the transgression of that law when
no supernatural or spiritual reward was promised for its performance. But no supernatural reward
was promised for the performance of a law performable by the powers of nature alone; otherwise
nature will be able to acquire the supernatural. Therefore a spiritual punishment ought not to
have been assigned to the transgression of that same law. I subsume: but according to your meaning,
as I perceived it, the law given to Adam was performable by the powers of nature alone. Therefore a
spiritual punishment ought not to have been assigned to it. Yet a spiritual punishment was
assigned to it. Therefore it was assigned unjustly. Whether these things follow from your theses
or not, I shall no longer inquire, because you now openly say that a supernatural reward was
prepared for our first parents if they should persist in their origin. I therefore bid this
argument of mine to stand.

From what you say I further conclude as follows in support of my opinion. What has been prepared
for all human beings, under the condition of obedience which they were able to perform from the
gift of God granted or to be granted to them by the ordinance of grace, cannot be denied to some
human beings by God’s certain and determinate decree unless their disobedience has been foreseen.
But eternal life was prepared for all human beings under the condition of obedience which they
were able to perform. Therefore, for human beings, denial is not

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 75 | BSB canvas 87]

possible unless their disobedience has been foreseen. Therefore all were considered as sinners by
God when he passed them over, and consequently they were not considered in common. I touch
Thomas’s opinion only as it is explained in your theses. But allow me to ask Thomas to prove that
God passed over man considered in the state of integrity, in which he possessed not only natural but also
supernatural gifts. I confess that spiritual happiness is the inheritance of children, but it
would have come to those persisting in the primeval state by a different covenant from that by
which it comes to those believing in Christ. For it would have been given to the former by the law
of works; it is given to the latter by the law of faith.

RESPONSE. Behold a wretched disputant! One might suppose that Arminius did not properly know the
elements of logic, since he has not yet learned even to construct a valid syllogism. For the
syllogistic forms proposed in his proposition do not sufficiently agree with the syllogistic forms
repeated in his rejoinder, nor do the individual forms taken separately have any agreement with a
truly syllogistic method. Above all, he suffers from a confusion of words; the words he confuses are
“spiritual” and “supernatural.” For although he relies only upon the word “spiritual” in the
proposition, nevertheless when he reaches the rejoinder he uses “supernatural” more frequently and,
on the side of the reward, is almost content with that term. On the other side, however, he
preferred to call the punishment spiritual rather than supernatural, for he did not venture to
call it supernatural. Yet when philosophizing about the reward on the other side, he preferred to
call it supernatural rather than spiritual. The reader easily understands what a supernatural
reward is—namely, eternal life. But Arminius did not venture, conversely, to call eternal death
supernatural. Therefore he preferred to call the punishment spiritual. Yet he did not explain the
reason for this designation, nor can I easily discern whether he meant eternal death to be denoted
by “spiritual punishment” or not. Conversely, what he means by “spiritual reward” is again obscure,
and Arminius did not explain it, except that he generally calls the same reward supernatural. That
scarcely lessens the obscurity, especially since he never calls the corresponding punishment on
the other side supernatural. Yet “supernatural” suits his purpose better than “spiritual,” since he
wishes to conclude from it that performance of the law—to which a supernatural reward is
attached—was supernatural, that is, performable only by supernatural grace.

So much for Arminius’s grammar; let us come to an examination of his logical method. Here nothing
sound at all appears, for nowhere does a valid syllogistic form emerge. Three propositions are
suggested in order. The two at the extremes are categorical, the middle one hypothetical. They
cannot be combined into one syllogism but are better fitted to form two; at least, they are wholly
sufficient to complete the premises of two syllogisms, from which conclusions will flow of their
own accord—but, unhappily, conclusions charging headlong against one another. Let the first
syllogism be: “Spiritual punishment cannot be ordained for those who would depart from their origin
unless, conversely, a spiritual reward had been prepared for those who would persist in their
origin.” This proposition is clearly resolved into the following formally hypothetical
proposition: “If spiritual punishment was ordained for human beings who would depart from their
origin, then a spiritual reward was ordained for human beings who would persist in their origin.”
But spiritual punishment was ordained for human beings who would depart from their origin. Both
premises are set forth by Arminius, from which the following conclusion flows: therefore a
spiritual reward was ordained or prepared for human beings who would persist in their origin. This
conclusion, I say, clearly follows from premises set forth most expressly by Arminius. Nevertheless,
in the madness with which he was equipped to confound scholastic method, he immediately attaches a
directly contradictory proposition in this form: “But a reward of this sort”—that is, a spiritual
reward—“was not prepared for such persons,” that is, for those who would persist in their origin.
It therefore appears that a second syllogism must be constructed as one more suitable to Arminius’s
reasoning, in the following form, retaining the same hypothetical major premise: “If spiritual
punishment was ordained for human beings who would depart from their origin, then a spiritual
reward was ordained or prepared for human beings who would persist in their origin. But a reward
of this sort was not prepared for such persons.” From these premises the following conclusion
readily flows: therefore spiritual punishment was by no means ordained for human beings who would
depart from their origin. This conclusion flatly contradicts the proposition of Arminius which he
first recited as the authoritative opinion (*kyrian doxan*): “The punishment ordained for them was
spiritual.”

In order to reconcile these things from a consideration of what Arminius declared in his
rejoinder, it must be observed that, when Arminius writes that the punishment ordained for those
departing from their origin was spiritual, he pronounced this as though from his own oracle and
from his own opinion. But when he subsumed, “A reward of this sort was not prepared for such
persons”—that is, for those who would persist in their origin—he pronounced it from the opposing
opinion of Junius. This he gathers in the following manner from the statements he immediately
attaches: “For those who were able to persist in their origin by the powers of nature alone, no
supernatural reward was prepared. But Adam and all in him were able to persist in their origin by
the powers of nature alone”—understand, according to the opinion of Junius, although Arminius did
not indicate this at all in the proposition but discussed it as though it were his own opinion.
“Therefore no supernatural reward was prepared for them.” But he wished to overturn this with an
argument duly formed as follows: if spiritual or supernatural punishment was ordained for those
departing from their origin, then a spiritual or supernatural reward was ordained for those
persisting in their origin. But spiritual punishment was ordained for those departing from their
origin. Therefore a supernatural reward was ordained for those persisting in their origin. From
this conclusion he wished to proceed to show that man could not perform the law given by God by the
powers of nature alone—that is, apart from supernatural grace. This can be framed as follows: a law
for whose performance a supernatural reward was ordained was not performed naturally but
supernaturally—that is, not by the powers of nature but through supernatural grace. But a
supernatural reward was prepared for the performance of the law given to Adam. Therefore its
performance was not natural but supernatural—that is, it had to be performed not by the powers of
nature but through supernatural grace. This, therefore, is Arminius’s final endeavor in this
argument, which he completed so unsuccessfully. He appears to have intended this: therefore Adam
was not destitute of the supernatural grace through which he was able to perform God’s law.

But it has already been shown both that Arminius wrongly formulated even this state of the third
question and that it does not agree with his own declaration of opinion to the Estates. What of the
fact that none of us affirms that Adam lacked supernatural grace through which he could render
obedience to God—sufficient grace, I mean? We say only, together with Arminius, that efficacious
grace was denied to him. Nevertheless, in the argument which he produced with difficulty rather
than brought fully to birth, there are several things of little strength. The proposition, “A law
for whose performance a supernatural reward was prepared is not performable by the powers of
nature,” is weak. If the question concerned merit, we would immediately concede it. But we do not
merit eternal life even through supernatural grace. And who would deny that it lies with God to
reward natural obedience, if he should so will, even with eternal life? That hypothetical
proposition too—“If spiritual punishment was ordained for those departing from their origin, then
a spiritual reward was ordained for those persisting in their origin”—has little strength. For God
is not bound to reward every act of obedience, though he may punish any disobedience at his
pleasure. But grant that all these claims hold: what do they have to do with Junius? He was so far
from maintaining that, through any

preterition or abandonment, Adam lacked the powers to perform God’s law that, on the contrary, he
maintained that the particular law given to Adam was performable by nature.

2. Few points in Junius call for notice. The first is that he distinguishes between natural matters
and matters ordained to grace, saying that the former, but not the latter, are performable by the
powers of nature alone. He has already pressed this distinction repeatedly, but I have not yet
grasped with what coherence he does so, nor in what sense matters are said to be ordained to grace
and for that reason by no means performable by nature. What of the fact that the members of a
distinction ought to be opposed, and consequently that matters ordained to grace ought by no means
to be regarded as natural matters? And who understands what it means to be ordained to grace when
natural things are excluded? Again, this very phrase “ordained to grace” sounds somehow
“mysterious” (*mystēriōdes*) in my ears. I fear that Junius himself did not sufficiently grasp what
he meant.

As for Junius’s supposition that, according to Thomas’s opinion, man in the cause of election was in
his purely natural endowments: I wish Junius had done us the favor of indicating the passage in
Aquinas from which he drew this. I confess that I have not yet discovered that Thomas adopted such
an opinion. I would gladly undertake a new investigation of the matter if I did not despair of
success, since I suspect that Junius ascribed this opinion to Thomas by relying on conjectures
rather than testimonies, without thus far communicating to us any evidence at all. Perhaps he came
to this suspicion from what Thomas writes at 1, q. 23, art. 1, ad 3: that it makes no difference to
the concept of predestination whether someone is predestined to eternal life from the state of
misery or not. But this is to support his conjecture with a slender prop.

3. Let us come at last to Arminius’s rejoinder. Here again the water sticks in his throat, and he
shows himself miserably perplexed and entangled. He sweats to frame arguments according to his
pleasure and accomplishes nothing. See how he links syllogisms. “Man is left in a nature which has
no supernatural or divine power”—this he takes from Junius. To it he adds from his own store: “Yet
he was still able to perform the law; therefore a spiritual punishment ought not to have been
assigned to transgression of the law when no spiritual or supernatural reward was promised for
its performance.” This conclusion has no agreement at all with the preceding propositions. Rather,
the syllogism would proceed as follows: man is left in a nature which has no supernatural or
divine power. Yet he was still able to perform the law. Therefore performance of the law was
something divine or supernatural. We have already said what seemed necessary for reducing these
arguments to a more suitable form. We have also shown upon what invalid foundations the entire
argument rests and how incongruous a comparison it frames between spiritual punishment on the one
side and supernatural reward on the other. But at last, having discovered that according to
Junius’s opinion a supernatural reward was appointed for performance of the law, Arminius bids his
argument to stand and turns to inventing a new one. “What has been prepared for human beings under
the condition of obedience cannot be denied to them unless their disobedience has been foreseen.
But eternal life was prepared for human beings under the condition of obedience. Therefore it
cannot be denied to them unless their disobedience has been foreseen.” I have contracted the major
premise into a few words so that judgment concerning it may proceed more freely, especially since
the added propositions serve only to dull the reader’s attention and make him less prepared to
undertake its examination.

I therefore answer that the major premise is utterly false, and an obvious demonstration which
plainly convicts it of falsehood lies ready to hand. Eternal life was proposed to the angels under
the condition of obedience; salvation is proposed to human beings today under the condition of
faith and good works. But can eternal life and salvation therefore be given to them only after
their obedience, faith, and good works have been foreseen? What then prevents election and
reprobation from being based upon works, even against the Apostle’s will? Perhaps you will say—and
truly—that salvation is denied only after evil works have been foreseen, because the denial of
salvation occurs only on the day of judgment or the day of death. But according to that
interpretation, I say, not only foreknowledge of disobedience but its actual existence precedes the
denial of salvation. For in this sense the denial of salvation is the same as damnation. Yet no
sober person denies that damnation occurs after disobedience, and that not only in God’s
foreknowledge but in the nature of things. Arminius, however, is dealing with the denial of eternal
life through God’s decree, not through the execution of the decree. You will reply further: from
this it follows at least only that reprobation does not occur except from foreknowledge of
disobedience. But Arminius does not say that election occurs only from foreknowledge of obedience.
Certainly he does not say it, but it is just as if he had said it in so many words. For by the same
form of argument it is proved that the election of human beings does not occur except from
foreknowledge of faith and good works, in this manner: what has been prepared for human beings
under the condition of faith and good works cannot be granted to them except from foreknowledge of
their faith and good works. But eternal life was prepared only under the condition of faith and
good works. Therefore it cannot be granted except from faith and good works foreseen. Here too you
might retort that in fact eternal life is granted to no one except those devoted to good works.
Precisely, because the granting of eternal life is one thing and the decree to grant it another.
Likewise it is denied to no one except those guilty of evil works. I speak respectively of evil
works and good, not merely foreseen but actually preceding.

Why, then, does Arminius dwell only upon foreknowledge? Because the question concerns not so much
the granting or denial of eternal life—about which no one doubts—as the decree to grant and deny it.
Why, then, did Arminius make no mention of the decree in his argument but only of the execution of
the decree? Because he preferred to play the impostor, mingling and confounding everything, rather
than the legitimate disputant. For if he had expressly constructed his argument concerning the
decree, it would immediately have appeared that he makes election depend not only upon foreseen
faith but upon foreseen works. Thus he used the expression “denial of eternal life,” signifying not
so much the decree as its execution, in which he could range more freely and plausibly and impose
upon his readers more effectively. Perhaps, given his superficial training in logic and scholastic
method, he imposed not only upon his readers but upon himself. From this, however, he concludes
that human beings were therefore considered as sinners by God when he passed them over. He ought
rather to say, “by God when he denied them blessedness,” for the argument proceeded concerning
denial. But denial is opposed to a granting which occurs in time. Meanwhile, if the argument
proceeds concerning preterition, we have shown that by an equal argument it can be concluded that
human beings were considered by God when he elected them not only as believers but also as persons
conscious of good works.

We have already said what had to be said about the opinion of Thomas which Junius ascribes to him.
When Arminius asks Thomas to prove that God passed over man in the state of integrity, allow me to
answer and undertake the proof myself, whatever may be the case concerning Thomas’s opinion. If
preterition is understood as the denial of efficacious grace for avoiding sin, it is evident that
Adam and all in him were thus passed over by God in the state of integrity. Again, if it is
understood as the denial of efficacious grace for avoiding the sin on account of which someone
will be condemned, it is likewise evident that some were passed over in Adam in this manner—namely,
all who are condemned on account of original sin. But if it means the decree to pass over, whether
with respect to the granting of efficacious grace for salvation or the granting of glory, I do not
easily believe that Aquinas placed the state of integrity before this decree. Certainly, we read
that at the Council of Trent theologians of the highest reputation—Thomas, Scotus, and others—held
that before creation God, from the mass of the human race, elected some to glory by his sole and
mere

mercy and prepared for them efficacious means by which they would be brought to salvation.
Arminius acknowledges, however, that reprobation and election were made at the same moment. The
inheritance would have come to God’s children who persisted in the primeval state by a different
covenant from that by which it comes to believers in Christ. I acknowledge that this is truly
said, but it could hardly be stated less pertinently. For the question between us is not how it
would have come to them, but how it was decreed. We say that, however it would have come, it would
nevertheless have been decreed only from God’s mere and pure good pleasure. Thus all the
distinctions Arminius pursues on this point are beside the matter and of a sort whose absence would
by no means be unbecoming to Arminius as a weighty disputant.
