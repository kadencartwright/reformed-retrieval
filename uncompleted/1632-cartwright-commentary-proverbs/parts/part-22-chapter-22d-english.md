Verse 8. One who sows iniquity will reap trouble, and the rod of his fury will be consumed.

In זורע (*seminator*, one who sows) and יקצר (*metet*, will reap) there is an allegory, by which it is declared that punishment will befall each person according to his wickedness, just as bad seed produces a harvest corresponding to itself. In שבט (*virga*, rod) there is a metonymy of effect for the cruelty which a tyrant exercises with a rod; and by synecdoche a rod is also put for every instrument and means of oppression. In עברתו (*furoris sui*, of his fury) there is a metaphor from waters passing over their banks.

The purpose is to show that those who tyrannically abuse their riches and power will perish by that cruelty of theirs.

The comparison is exceedingly commonplace and very familiar to Scripture: just as farmers receive a good or bad harvest, a full or meagre one, according to the character of their sowing, so people will carry away in both the present life and the future a reward corresponding to their deeds. This is the only difference: the person who makes a bad sowing receives the same thing in the harvest, but the wicked person receives the punishment of wickedness, not wickedness itself.

Hence it appears how certain and how just is the punishment which God will inflict upon the wicked, and consequently how false and empty is the hope of those who, after spending the whole course of life wickedly, nevertheless dream of salvation. This is no less empty and ridiculous than if a farmer who had never ploughed or harrowed, and had brought either no seed or rotten seed into the field, should nevertheless expect a plentiful and good harvest.

This punishment of the wicked is illustrated by another comparison. For just as it often happens that one who strikes another person with a rod is himself beaten with that same rod if he falls upon a stronger man who wrenches it from his hands, and just as one who beats another with a rod sometimes breaks the rod if he has done it with force applied, so these rich men and creditors who abuse their riches for cruelty and oppression fall upon God, who is stronger than they. He strikes them with the same scourge with which they bloodied others, or takes away the powers which were the ungodly instruments of oppression. They indeed look no farther than the poor whom they oppress; they do not consider God, their avenger.

Verse 9. The man who is good of eye will himself be blessed, when he has given from his bread to a poor man.

In טוב עין (*bonus oculo*, good of eye) there is a twofold synecdoche:

[1632 witness | PDF p. 495 | printed columns 974-975]

one of the part, by which through a kindly eye he understands also a good ear; the other of the whole, by which through a good eye he designates a single kind of goodness in the eye, namely, that by which a person is made readier to give through looking. In לחם (*pane*, bread) there is also a synecdoche of the part for every kind of sustenance, together with clothing and even money itself, by which these and the other supports of life can be procured.

The purpose is to add a spur toward liberality, especially toward the poor.

Who is good of eye appears from Matt. 20.15 and Prov. 23.6, where one who is kindly, or beneficent, and who gives more than he is bound by law or agreement to give, is opposed to the evil-eyed person, or to the person who envies another's good. Therefore, the man who is good of eye is one who, moved inwardly and in his mind by compassion, so affects his eye that by its gaze his mind too is rendered readier than before to help the needy. For just as a mind disposed toward giving affects the eye by its goodness, so the eye, striking back upon the mind, kindles it to greater kindness. By this we are taught that the just and proper use of the members of the body, flowing from a rightly constituted and affected mind, flows back toward the fuller adornment of the mind. The man who is good of eye is aroused to beneficence both by the sight of the poor man himself and by the sight of the gift which he is going to confer upon him. But the evil-eyed person, first, is not moved to compassion by looking upon the poor man; or if he is moved a little, nevertheless, when his eye turns toward whatever it is by which the poor man might be restored, what would be spent upon him seems too much.

Hence it is clear that our liberality ought to be exercised cheerfully, promptly, and with an open countenance. It would contribute very greatly to this if we remembered that God will look upon us with a kindly countenance if we look cheerfully upon the poor.

Afterward he assigns the chief object of liberality, namely, the poor and needy man. For although liberality is exercised upon guests, strangers, and neighbours by receiving them, nevertheless the surest indication of true liberality is that someone has relieved the poor and needy, Luke 14.12, 13, 14. See Isa. 58.7 for the things that should especially be conferred upon the poor. Add to this that we must give from our own possessions and must not snatch from these people what we would give to others.

There follows the reward of liberality: that he will be blessed. Here, because he does not expressly indicate the person who will bless, it is gathered that this blessing extends more widely. He will be blessed by all the godly, who will give thanks to God and pour forth prayers for him; he will also be blessed by the poor themselves, or, if perhaps they are unmindful of the benefit received, their naked loins and empty bellies will nevertheless bless him, as is stated at Job 31.20. Finally, he will be drenched by God himself with eternal blessings, 1 Tim. 6.18, 19, and with momentary blessings, 2 Cor. 9.9, 10. Moreover, God's blessing will overflow upon his whole household, 2 Tim. 1.16, as is sufficiently and abundantly established by the examples of the widow of Zarephath and the Shunammite woman, who received Elijah and Elisha.
