QUESTION XVI.

Was John’s baptism essentially the same as Christ’s baptism? We affirm,
against the papists.

I. Although this question may seem less necessary today, when no one is
baptized except with the baptism administered by the apostles, nevertheless,
because it is customarily disputed sharply by the papists, something must also
be said about it briefly, so that the truth of orthodox doctrine concerning
baptism may be illustrated more fully.

II. Although the controversy about John’s baptism is commonly divided into
several parts—the first, whether it was instituted by God; the second, whether
it had the promise of grace annexed to it and the grace of salvation was
dispensed through it; the third, whether those baptized by John were to be
rebaptized—it can suitably be reduced to one general question concerning the
essential identity of the two baptisms. We affirm this identity; the papists
deny it.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the papists.]

III. The Council of Trent explains its position in this way in session 7,
canon 1 on baptism: “If anyone says that the baptism of John had the same power
as the baptism of Christ, let him be anathema.” Bellarmine explains this in
three propositions in book 1, *On Baptism*, chapters 19, 20, and following:
(1) John’s baptism was not the same as Christ’s baptism. (2) It did not have
the same power. (3) After John’s baptism, Christ’s baptism necessarily had to
be administered. In this he follows the opinion of the scholastics. Lombard,
*Sentences* 4, distinction 2, book 8, maintains that “John’s
baptism washed bodies but did not cleanse from sins.” Salmerón says that
John’s baptism differed from Christ’s baptism in form, words, and the fruit of
grace. Others maintain that it was only a prelude to grace and a rudimentary
baptism leading to repentance.

[Marginalia: And of the orthodox.]

IV. We acknowledge that some difference must be established between John’s
baptism and Christ’s, but an accidental difference with respect to
circumstances and degrees, not a substantial and specific difference with
respect to essence; a difference with respect to the manner of manifestation
and the measure of gifts, but not with respect to the thing itself. First,
with respect to the manner of manifestation: John’s baptism signified neither
Christ as distantly absent, as did the legal ceremonies of the Old Testament,
nor Christ as already dead and raised, as does our baptism, but Christ as
coming and about to die. With respect to the measure of gifts: John’s baptism
was not administered with so great an abundance of light and spiritual gifts,
because the Holy Spirit was communicated more sparingly while Christ had not
yet been manifested, nor did so great an abundance of gifts make its waters
fruitful, if it is permissible to speak this way. These were more abundant in
the apostles’ baptism, in which there was often a greater and more conspicuous
presence of the Holy Spirit and a greater abundance of gifts, and which was
not limited to the Jews, within whose bounds John’s baptism was confined, but
was also

[1847 vol. III printed p. 341 | IA leaf 375 | PDF p. 375]

common to the Gentiles. Yet whatever this difference may have been, we judge
that the grace of the remission of sins and regeneration was sealed equally
in both, and therefore that the power of each was the same with respect to
essence.

[Marginalia: The identity of John’s and Christ’s baptism is proved. 1. From the causes.]

V. The reasons are as follows. (1) Each baptism agrees in its essentials and
in all its causes: in its efficient cause, God, who instituted that baptism
(John 1:33; Luke 3:2–3; Matt. 21:25); in its material cause, both external,
namely the element of water (Matt. 3:11; John 1:33), and internal, namely the
promise of grace concerning the remission of sins and regeneration (Matt.
3:7–8; Luke 3:3; Acts 19:5); in its form, which is both the attestation and
sealing of the promise through water and the rite of washing, and the joining
of the promise itself with this element and its rite (Mark 1:4; Luke 3:3); and
in its end, which is the confirmation of the baptized concerning God’s grace,
their separation from unbelievers, and their reception into the assembly of
the church (Acts 2:38). Hence Tertullian rightly says, “There is no difference
between those whom John dipped in the Jordan and those whom Peter dipped in
the Tiber.”

[Marginalia: 2. From the effects.]

VI. (2) The identity of the effect proves the identity of the cause. But the
effects of John’s baptism were the same as those of Christ’s baptism, namely
repentance (Mark 1:4; Acts 2:38), remission of sins (Luke 3:3; Acts 22:16),
faith in Christ (Acts 19:4; Mark 16:15–16), the Holy Spirit with respect to his
ordinary gifts (Acts 18:25 and 2:38–39), and separation from unbelievers
(Matt. 3:6–7; Luke 3:7; Acts 2:38–39).

[Marginalia: 3. From the identity of Christ’s baptism and ours.]

VII. (3) Christ’s baptism ought not to be different from the baptism of
believers: both because Christ is the Head and believers are the members; and
because he had to sanctify in his own baptism the use and sacrament of our
baptism; and because baptism is a symbol of the unity of believers in one
mystical body (Eph. 4:5), not only with one another but also with Christ the
Head (1 Cor. 12:13; Gal. 3:27); and because his circumcision was the same as
the circumcision of the Jewish people. Nor should the adversaries be heard
here when they contend that Christ cannot be said to have been baptized with
his own baptism. Their first reason is that he would have been baptized in his
own name. But nothing prevents Christ as man, with respect to his human
nature, from having been baptized in his own name as God. Their second reason
is that the remission of sins and the washing of regeneration, which are
conferred in his baptism, cannot be attributed to Christ without injury to
him. But although he did not receive baptism to seal the remission of sins and
regeneration for himself, he had to receive it as our Surety, in order to
consecrate baptism in himself and seal that grace to us. The same difficulty
presses upon John’s baptism, in which both benefits are sealed. Although
Christ had no need of regeneration, there were nevertheless other reasons why
he received baptism after circumcision: namely, to teach that the power and
sanctification both of the Old and of the New sacraments depended upon him
alone as the foundation of both covenants and the Savior of both peoples, the
circumcised and the baptized. Bellarmine absurdly affirms that it would be
insulting to Christ if he were held to have received the same baptism as we
did, because it is not fitting for a commander to receive the mark of his own
army but to impress it upon others. As if it were shameful for a leader to go
before others not only by command but also by example. As if it were more
honorable for the commander to bear his servant’s mark than his own, since
that baptism was John’s, not Christ’s.

[Marginalia: 4. From the parity between John’s baptism and the Word he preached.]

VIII. (4) The power and efficacy of John’s baptism ought not to have been less
than that of the Word preached by him, since there is a perpetual relation
(σχέσις) between the Word and the sacraments. But the Word preached by John
concerned spiritual grace to be conferred through Christ and was the beginning
of the gospel (Mark 1:1; Matt. 3:2; Luke 1:77). Therefore his baptism was also.
It cannot be said, 1. “The consequence does not

[1847 vol. III printed p. 342 | IA leaf 376 | PDF p. 376]

hold from doctrine to sacraments, because heretics can have true sacraments
though they do not have true doctrine.” For the sacraments of heretics cannot
be true except insofar as they retain the true doctrine of baptism in its
essentials. Nor can it be said, 2. “John taught nothing about the resurrection
of Christ and therefore did not have the same doctrine.” For although he did
not teach it expressly, he nevertheless intimated it sufficiently, though
more obscurely, when he called Christ the Lamb of God who takes away the sins
of the world, namely by a sacrificial offering, since he could not have
accomplished this unless he had been raised. Add that, since John acknowledged
him as the beloved Son of God (Matt. 3:17), who received the Spirit without
measure and faith in whom obtains eternal life (John 3:36), by that very fact
he had to believe him to be the Prince of life and the Author of eternal
salvation.

[Marginalia: 5. Because it is the baptism of the gospel.]

IX. (5) John’s baptism is the baptism of the gospel or of the New Testament,
for the beginning of the gospel is expressly placed in John’s ministry (Mark
1:1), and John’s baptism testified concerning Christ as already manifested
(Acts 19:4–5), and all the prophets and the law had to continue until John
(Matt. 11:13) and cease in him. But according to Paul, the baptism of the New
Testament is one (Eph. 4:5).

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

X. It is called John’s baptism not because he was its principal author, since
he is expressly said to have been sent by God for this purpose (John 1:33) and
his baptism to have been from God (Matt. 21:25), but because he was its first
administrator. It is also so called in relation to Christ’s baptism
administered through the apostles, insofar as John did not abound in the same
visible gifts of the Holy Spirit as they did. But this does not prevent its
institution from being divine. Hence Alexander of Hales says in part 4,
question 6, that baptism “was indeed instituted by John in respect to
ministry, but by God in respect to authority and command.”

XI. The sayings in which John distinguishes his baptism from Christ’s, as when
he is said “to baptize with water, but Christ with fire” (Matt. 3:11), do not
detract efficacy from it or distinguish the baptisms, but the baptizers. They
distinguish the persons within the order of efficient causes and establish
Christ as the principal author of grace and John as the ministerial cause,
through whose ministry Christ was freely efficacious in bestowing the Spirit.
In this way Paul distinguishes the external ministry of the apostles from the
internal power of God acting (1 Cor. 3:6): “I planted, Apollos watered, but God
gave the increase.” Add that the verb *to baptize* is clearly used in diverse
ways. It is attributed properly to John, but metaphorically to Christ for the
visible outpouring of the Holy Spirit made on the day of Pentecost, as Christ
himself explains (Acts 1:5): “You will be baptized with the Spirit not many
days from now.” This became clear from the event itself (Acts 2), and Peter
confirms it (v. 16).

XII. John’s baptism not only disposed toward repentance and the remission of
sins, but also sealed that remission. Just as he preached the law and the
gospel in his ministry, so also he sealed the evangelical promises by his
baptism. Nor, if he is said “to have prepared the way of the Lord” by his
ministry, does it follow that he did not teach faith in Christ and the
remission of sins. This is expressly indicated in Acts 19:4, where he is said
not merely to have commanded those who came to him to believe that Christ was
going to come, as the Socinians would have it, but to believe in Christ—“in
the one coming after him, so that they might believe” (εἰς τὸν ἐρχόμενον μετ’
αὐτὸν ἵνα πιστεύσωσι). He invited them to faith not in the future but in the
present, although its object was future, just as the fathers believed with
present faith in the Christ who was to come.

XIII. If the express invocation of the Trinity was not present in John’s
baptism, it was nevertheless implicit in the faith in Christ that he
commanded, which necessarily involves the Trinity. And if, when the heavens
were opened, the entire Trinity visibly manifested itself at Christ’s baptism,
according to the saying, “The Father in the voice; the Son in the body; the
Spirit in the dove,” faith in and worship of the Trinity cannot be said to
have been absent from John’s

[1847 vol. III printed p. 343 | IA leaf 377 | PDF p. 377]

baptism. Add that if John’s baptism was from God the Father, Son, and Holy
Spirit, there is no doubt that John also baptized in the name—that is, in the
confession and invocation—of the Trinity. For he could not baptize in the name
of anyone other than the one who sent him to baptize.

XIV. Acts 19:4–5 does not prove that the Ephesian disciples, who had been
baptized with John’s baptism, were rebaptized by Paul. For the words of verse
5, “and when they heard, they were baptized” (ἀκούσαντες δὲ ἐβαπτίσθησαν), are
not words in which Luke narrates what followed Paul’s discourse to them.
Rather, they confirm Paul’s discourse to those Ephesians, in which he teaches
that those who had received baptism from John had been baptized in the name
of Christ and therefore had no need of a new baptism. Thus Paul is introduced
as speaking: “John indeed baptized with the baptism of repentance, telling
the people that they should believe in the one who was coming after him; but
those who heard him [ἀκούσαντες δὲ, in place of οἱ δὲ ἀκούσαντες]”—John, that
is—“were baptized in the name of Jesus,” namely by John himself. Thus this
speech of Paul instructs those to whom it is directed about the baptism they
had received; it in no way teaches that they were rebaptized. That the passage
can best be explained in this way is clear from the force of the particles
*men* (μὲν) and *de* (δὲ). Although they are often expletive and are used
alone, they never occur together in a discourse without being adversative.
Therefore, unless we wish to tear Paul’s discourse apart, we must acknowledge
that these words, “but those who heard” (ἀκούσαντες δὲ), are not Luke’s words
but Paul’s as he addresses the Ephesians. Luke continues Paul’s words in verse
6 and connects them with verses 3 and 4 by the conjunction *kai* (καὶ),
namely narrating that Paul, upon hearing that they had been baptized by John,
laid hands on them, as in Acts 8:14, 17.

XV. But even if we granted that the Ephesian disciples were baptized again,
the difference between the two baptisms with respect to species would not
follow from this, but only a difference with respect to degree. That degree
was more perfect in both knowledge and efficacy after Christ’s institution
than at the time of John the Baptist, when it began to be administered. Thus,
because they had previously been baptized by John into the Christ who was
coming, after Jesus had been manifested, died, and been raised, they could be
washed “in the express name of the Lord Jesus,” who had now come. Accordingly,
although no specific difference between the two baptisms is granted, there is
nevertheless such a distinction that a suitable reason can be given why those
baptized by John could have been rebaptized, but not those baptized by the
apostles: both because apostolic baptism was accompanied by an abundance of
spiritual gifts; because it was instituted immediately by Christ and was
performed expressly and explicitly in Christ’s name; and because the
sacrament of initiation could vary according to the different ages of the
faithful. For some believers believed in Christ as one who was to come; others
in him as coming, who was on the way; and others in him as one who had come.
Circumcision was instituted for the first; John’s baptism for the second; and
Christ’s baptism for the third.

XVI. It is gratuitously assumed that the multitude of Jews baptized by the
apostles on the day of Pentecost had previously been baptized by John. (1)
Not each and every Jew was baptized by John, but only many, and nothing else
can be gathered from the hyperbolic expressions in Matthew and Mark. (2) Among
those thousands baptized by the apostles (Acts 2), many had gathered from
every nation under heaven—Medes, Parthians, Arabs, and others—whom no one would
say had been baptized by John. (3) If the adversaries’ reasoning holds,
Christ’s baptism could be proved repeatable. For it is clear from John 4:1–2
that Christ’s disciples baptized many more than John. Therefore, if it is
credible that some of those thousands of people whom the apostles baptized on
the day of Pentecost had previously been baptized by John, it is much more
credible that, from the same thousands, some had been baptized by Christ’s
disciples, which the papists nevertheless deny.

XVII. Although John’s ministry belonged to the first foundations of the
church and the ministry of the apostles to the church’s full constitution, it
does not follow that John’s ministry was not efficacious for salvation. For,
according to the angel’s testimony (Luke 1:16), it turned the hearts of the
children of Israel to the Lord their God; and its preaching of the doctrine
of repentance and the remission of sins could not have been other than
efficacious for salvation, since these two things comprise the whole gospel.
