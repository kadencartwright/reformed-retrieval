## A Scholastic Disputation on Divine Providence

## Chapter I

### What the providence of God is

1. *Providence* in Latin takes its name from seeing, and in Greek it is
*pronoia*. In Hebrew, as in Latin, the word is transferred from sense to
understanding, as the most learned Dr Rivetus notes. It denotes not so much an
act of the understanding as an affection and care of the will. Hence יראה:
“The Lord will provide himself a lamb,” and “The Lord has looked upon me.”
Indeed, by the word למשפטיך, *Lemishpath*, God’s counsel and ordering are
expressed: “By your ordering all things continue.” So the Fathers teach.
Damascenus defines providence as “care directed toward existing things.” Nor
do Aristotle, Aquinas, and Boethius disagree.

*[Marginal references: What providence is. Rivetus, Theological Disputation 1,
thesis 2; Genesis 22:8; Genesis 29:32; 1 Samuel 16:1; Ezekiel 20:6; Psalm
119:91; Damascenus; Aristotle, Ethics 2, chapter 12; Aquinas 1, question 22,
article 1; Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy 4, prose 6.]*

Some writers, not improperly, include three acts of God within providence: 1.
foreknowledge; 2. purpose or predestination; and 3. government or
administration. Certainly neither a parent exercises providence over his
children nor a shipmaster over a ship if the latter merely sees and beholds
what happens to the ship, and the former what happens to his children,

[PDF p. 54; printed p. 2]

but does not also take solicitous care—the one of the ship and the other of
his children.

2. Providence is an act, or care, of God by which, according to his eternal,
most wise, and most free counsel, he sees, preserves, and governs each and
every thing insofar as he sees it to be capable of being ordered by God; and
he irresistibly directs them to their own ends, but especially to the ultimate
end, namely God’s glory, in accordance with the natures of all things, though
sometimes beyond or contrary to those natures.

*[Marginal references: What providence is. 1 Corinthians 9:9; 1 Peter 5:7;
Hebrews 13:15; Deuteronomy 32:10; Hosea 11:3; Psalm 91:1–2; Romans 11:36;
Psalm 104:35; Matthew 10:29; Ephesians 1:11; Proverbs 16:4; Hebrews 1:2.]*

The genus in this definition is an act, not a counsel. For providence is not
formally counsel, but a certain continued creation of things and external
administration which connotes the immanent acts of knowledge and counsel. It
is not, however, an act of the intellect alone, but also of the will and
power; and insofar as it is restricted to God’s holy children, it embraces
God’s singular favor toward his own.

The difference is taken from the four causes. The matter, or object, is
twofold. Its material object is *all beings*, because God generally takes care
of all things, as the Holy Spirit says; and it is *individual things*, for God
does not disdain even the smallest particulars. Its formal object is all and
each of these things insofar as they can be ordered to God’s glory. Hence I
exclude two things from this object: 1. God himself, the ultimate end; and 2.
things that are simply impossible, for neither the latter nor the former can
be directed to the ultimate end.

I say “insofar as God sees them to be capable of being ordered.” Thus I wish
both things which actually exist and things which are merely possible to be
included within this object. For it must be credited to providence that many
possible things never become actual, that there are not many worlds but one,
and that plagues, famines, and wars are restrained. Things which can be
preserved by God and things which can be annihilated by him also fall under
this object.

The efficient cause is God himself, who works all things according to his
counsel. The end is God’s glory: “For God made all things for himself,” as the
Wise Man says. The form includes three acts of God’s providence: 1. the
preservation of things; 2. cooperation with all second causes; and 3. the
irresistible direction of all things to their ends.

Now God’s preservation is not bare permission, in the way that the Hebrew
midwives preserved the male children alive; nor is it an internal causation by
God, as matter preserves form by receiving it and form preserves matter by
actualizing it. Nor does God preserve things by nurturing them through
friendly qualities, as a physician preserves a sick person. Rather, this
preservation is an influx and penetration of divine omni-

[PDF p. 55; printed p. 3]

potence, by which the power of God, intervening between pure nonbeing, or pure
nothing, and the thing, preserves it from annihilation; and annihilation is
properly opposed to this preservation. For all things tend toward their
maternal *nothing*, not from any displeasure toward being, but from sheer
indigence, unless the preserving power of God intervenes, “which upholds all
things by his powerful word,” as Scripture says. Augustine, Gregory,
Chrysostom, and likewise Thomas, Durandus, Bonaventura, Suarez, and Gregory of
Valencia teach more about these matters.

*[Marginal references: Augustine, On Genesis 4, chapter 12; Gregory, homily 17
on Ezekiel; Chrysostom, homily 19 on Ephesians; Thomas, Against the Gentiles 3,
chapter 66.]*

There are more fervent and laborious disputations on God’s cooperation with
second causes, especially with the created will in acts of sin, and on the
direction of all things to their ends.

*[Marginal references: Durandus 1.2, distinction 44, question 1; Bonaventura
1.1, distinction 37, question 1; Suarez, Metaphysics, volume 1, disputation 21,
section 3; Gregory of Valencia, volume 1, disputation 8, question 2, point 1.]*

## Chapter II

### What the nature of God’s permissive will and of his will of good pleasure and will of sign is; the Remonstrants and Arminians are summoned to take sides

Our doctrine about God’s providence concerning sin cannot be made plain and
accessible to everyone unless we first say something about the nature of the
divine will and these distinctions.

One will of God is the will of good pleasure, or the decreeing will, which is
also called hidden; another is the will of sign, which is called approving,
approbative, or revealed. The will of good pleasure is called hidden, not
because it is simply not revealed to us, but because it is not revealed by a
word of command, promise, or threat as our duty. Nevertheless, it is disclosed
to us by the event, and from Scripture we know that there is such a will in
God. For in Scripture God says that he neither willed nor decreed what did not
occur, such as the breaking of Christ’s bones; and what did occur, such as the
division of Christ’s garments, he says that he decreed.

Again, one will of good pleasure is efficient. By it God decreed either to do
something through his immediate omnipotence—the creation of the world was
decreed and willed from eternity by this will—or to effect something through
rational creatures. Thus, that Peter should hear the Gospel and be converted
to faith in Christ was willed from eternity. Another will of good pleasure is
called permitting, or permissive. By it God decreed that sin should be
committed by a rational creature with his permission, but by no means with God
as its efficient cause or author.

[PDF p. 56; printed p. 4]

2. These two wills, the will of good pleasure and the will of sign, are not
contrary or contradictory, as the Jesuits and Arminians charge us with
teaching. For God often wills the same thing both by the will of good pleasure,
because the thing itself occurs, and by the will of sign, because he commanded
by his holy law the thing that occurs. The conversion of the Gentiles to faith
in Christ is an example. God both decreed that conversion from eternity—for
otherwise it would not occur—and commanded that conversion by the word of the
Gospel. Therefore it is one and the same will of God which is called the will
of good pleasure and the decreeing will whenever it is disclosed to us by the
event or nonevent, and is called the will of sign insofar as it is commanded
or forbidden in God’s Word.

*[Marginal note: God can will the same thing both by the will of good pleasure
and by the will of sign.]*

Nevertheless, they differ in such a way that God wills certain things by the
will of sign—such as the repentance of the reprobate, the Savior’s not being
killed, and the faith of Judas the traitor—which he by no means willed or
decreed by the will of good pleasure, since such things never occur in the
nature of things. And God wills by the will of good pleasure and has decreed
to occur certain things which he can by no means will by the will of sign,
such as the sale of Joseph and the killing of Christ. These things do occur
and therefore cannot occur contrary to, beyond, or apart from God’s decree and
will of good pleasure.

Unless God’s ever-watchful providence is to be banished in the fall of so many
most noble creatures—apart from which, nevertheless, “not even a sparrow falls
to the ground or a hair falls from the head”—God did not will the sale of
Joseph or the killing of the innocent Savior by the will of sign. That is, he
by no means approved it as a just and good thing; indeed, he detested it as
unjust and evil. *[Marginal reference: Matthew 10:29–30.]*

Hence, 2. the object of the will of good pleasure is a being which occurs in
the nature of things and which will certainly have actual existence or
nonexistence, whether good or evil, whether consonant with the will of sign or
approval, or dissonant and contrary to it, provided it is considered under the
aspect of its occurrence in the nature of things, or insofar as it occurs. But
the object of God’s will of sign or approval is that which is morally good and

[PDF p. 57; printed p. 5]

honorable; the object of his disapproval is that which is unjust and evil,
whether or not it occurs in the nature of things.

And, 3. from an affirmative will of good pleasure the inference to the event
or existence of the thing at last in its own time is valid. For example: God
decreed by his will of good pleasure the devastation of the Chaldeans by the
Medes. Therefore, the Medes will necessarily devastate the Chaldeans. Again,
God decreed by his will of good pleasure that Christ’s bones should not be
broken. Therefore, Christ’s bones were necessarily not broken. All such
inferences are valid because the occurrence or nonevent of the thing very well
implies God’s will or unwillingness of good pleasure.

But such inferences cannot validly be drawn from the will of sign. For
example: by his will of sign and approval God did not will, but prohibited,
Joseph’s sale by his brothers. Yet it does not follow, therefore, that such a
sale did not occur and was never done. It follows only that the nonsale of
Joseph is right and just. Again, God willed and commanded Cain’s repentance,
but it by no means follows, therefore, that Cain repents. For by no art of
logic can the event or nonevent of a thing be soundly concluded from the will
of sign or approval. Such a will establishes nothing about the occurrence or
nonevent of things, but only about the rightness or wrongness of things and of
our actions.

And although this will of sign is violated by wicked people, God’s will is not
thereby resisted, as if that which God willed and decreed to occur did not
occur. For God in fact never decreed and never willed that the obedience of
the reprobate should occur. He commanded them to obey, but God’s command does
not signify to us God’s will concerning such an event, but only God’s will
concerning the moral rightness of the thing.

4. By the will of good pleasure God determines human acts and their existence
or nonexistence. But the will of sign determines nothing at all about
existence or nonexistence, or about the occurrence or nonevent of things. It
only determines the moral regulation of human acts, whether those acts occur
and exist or do not occur and never exist. And as far as the essence of the
will of sign is concerned, God approves with equal moral complacency the final
obedience of Judas the traitor and the obedience of the Apostle Peter. But by
the will of good pleasure he wills Peter’s final obedience to occur and exist,
and according to the gracious decree of election he gives the efficacious
grace necessarily required for the actual existence of Peter’s obedience. By
contrast, according to the contrary decree, God decreed from eternity

[PDF p. 58; printed p. 6]

to deny the grace necessarily required for the obedience of Judas the traitor,
and he never willed that obedience by the will of good pleasure or the
decreeing will.

3. Hence this thesis must be carefully observed and proved: the will of sign
formally reveals to us the moral regulation of acts, but it does not reveal to
us that God intended or decreed that those acts should occur and exist. It
reveals only that, if such acts exist, God requires in his will of sign and
revealed will that they conform to God’s law. This is proved as follows.

*[Marginal note: The will of sign does not reveal to us that God intended or
determined the existence or nonexistence of our actions, but only their moral
agreement with God’s revealed will, whether our obedience exists or does not
exist, or the actions are thus regulated.]*

1. In his law God did not formally decree that our moral actions should exist
or not exist, nor did he determine this with himself from eternity, as, God
favoring, I shall demonstrate. He only commands the regulation and ordering of
acts and prohibits their disordering. Now ordering and disordering properly
make us obedient and blessed, or disobedient and miserable; and the ordering
or moral regulation of our actions is the true fulfillment of the will of
sign, while irregularity is the violation of that will.

Hence God’s Word, which is the sole sign of the will of sign, requires us to
elicit acts simply consonant with that will of sign or revealed will. But when
commanding repentance and faith to the reprobate, for example, God did not
determine that their repentance and faith should actually exist. The existence
or nonexistence of things is determined by God’s will of good pleasure, not by
this will of sign. By the will of sign God only says what is morally pleasing
to him and what is not. By this will he says to Judas the traitor, “I command
and will your final obedience”; that is, “I approve it and regard it as morally
pleasing and acceptable, whether that obedience actually exists or not.”

Just as the law of an earthly prince is not enacted for the purpose that
people should simply cultivate society, but on the hypothesis that society is
cultivated, so that they may order their life civilly and according to the
rule and standard of good laws, so the formal use of God’s Word revealing the
will of sign to us is to illuminate, teach, and direct “what is the good,
pleasing, and acceptable will of God,” and how our acts, on the hypothesis that
they are elicited and exist, may be formed according to God’s Word so that
they are pleasing and acceptable to God and, in Christ, worthy of reward.
*[Marginal reference: Romans 12:2.]*

2. The Law, as distinguished from the Gospel, is the revealed will of sign to
us who are now enslaved to sin. But God’s Law as such does not reveal to us
that there is an eternal decree of God’s good pleasure that we, thus enslaved
to sin,

[PDF p. 59; printed p. 7]

should perform works of the kind that the Law in this condition requires.
Therefore some will of sign does not reveal to us that God decreed and
intended that we should do what God so wills. The major premise is certain,
because the Law equally binds sinners and the innocent. The minor is proved:
if God in his Law intended such legal obedience, “Christ died in vain.”
*[Marginal references: Romans 3:20–21; Galatians 3:10–13.]*

3. The law of nature is the will of sign for those nations that have heard
nothing at all, or little, about Christ. But such a law does not reveal to
those nations that God intended and decreed that they should keep the law of
nature perfectly. This is impossible apart from the grace of Christ, which is
denied them by God’s eternal decree. That law merely reveals that God approves
the regulation of acts, whether those regulated acts exist or not.
*[Marginal references: Romans 11:20–21.]*

4. The law of nature is the will of sign even for the devils. But such a law
by no means reveals that God intends the devils to obey and perform acts
conforming to the law of nature. This is absurd, for then God would have to be
said to will the salvation of demons. Therefore the will of sign, in requiring
acts of obedience from us, does not reveal to us that God intended and decreed
that those acts should exist, but only that they should conform to God’s Law
and be good and morally upright, whether they exist or not.

*[Marginal note: The devils now sin against the will of sign.]*

Nor may the Arminians say that the devils no longer sin. 1. Scripture says
that they sin. 2. It is certain that the devils are rebuked; but why are those
who cannot sin reproved? 3. God either approves the devils’ crimes—which is
blasphemous—or disapproves them, and so they are sins. For God’s will is not
purely negative toward the moral acts of a rational creature, to say nothing
of how absurd it is that God should not hate the devils’ crimes, which upright
and godly people detest. *[Marginal references: John 8:44–45; 1 John 3:8;
Genesis 3:14; Job 2:2–3; Matthew 4:10.]*

5. Whatever God decreed and intended actually to exist does actually exist.
But the actual obedience which the Arminians and Jesuits say that God wills
and decrees in the revealed will of sign never actually exists in most
people. Therefore God never intended and decreed that this obedience should
occur. The major is proved (for Scripture and experience confirm the minor):
Scripture argues from the negation of the event to the negation of the decree,
and from the affirmation of the event to the affirmation of the decree.
Therefore whatever God decreed actually to exist does actually exist. The
antecedent is proved: God willed to kill Eli’s sons and decreed their
destruction. Therefore Eli’s sons did not listen to their father’s voice. In
the same way Christ’s bones were not broken. *[Marginal references: 1 Samuel
2:25; John 19:36.]*

[PDF p. 60; printed p. 8]

Therefore Scripture was fulfilled, and consequently God’s decree that
Christ’s bones should not be broken was fulfilled. The division of Christ’s
garments was foretold and hence decreed; therefore that division occurred.
Nothing is more frequent in Scripture than this: “This occurred; therefore
Scripture was fulfilled, and therefore God’s decree expressed in that
Scripture was fulfilled.” Likewise, “This does not happen; therefore the
Scripture predicting that this would not happen was fulfilled.” Or, if you
prefer, let the inference be framed thus: this was foretold in Scripture and
therefore decreed; therefore it occurred. “Jehovah spoke of this as future;
this has happened. God spoke of this as not future. Therefore this is future.
Therefore this is not future.” Almost countless valid inferences of this kind
occur in the sacred page. Hence whatever God willed and decreed to be done
always occurred and is done. What God did not will and did not decree does not
occur and is not done. Since, therefore, what God wills by the will of sign
very often does not occur, it necessarily follows that God never willed and
never decreed that it should occur, but only that God approved its good moral
quality, hated and forbade its evil quality, whether or not those acts
existed.

*[Marginal references: Matthew 27:35; Matthew 2:5, 17, 22, 23; Matthew 8:17;
Matthew 13:14; Matthew 26:31; Matthew 27:35; John 12:37–38; Acts 4:27–28; Acts
15:16; Acts 13:46–47; Romans 9:32–33; Romans 11:8; Galatians 3:16–17; 1 Kings
11:32, compared with 1 Kings 12:24; 2 Kings 22:38; 2 Kings 9:24–25; Isaiah
14:24–25.]*

From this I draw the following corollaries. First, the Gospel, insofar as it is
the bare will of sign, has the power and activity of the Holy Spirit no more
closely joined to it than does the Law. But insofar as the Gospel is equipped
with God’s power and works faith in the hearts of the elect, it is a sign of
the will of sign and something more. For it also includes God’s gracious
decree to convert those whose hearts the Gospel reaches when divinely equipped
with such power.

2. The revealed will of sign, as such, teaches, illuminates, directs, and binds
people to regulate their acts, whether those regulated acts actually exist
and occur or not.

3. God’s approving the obedience of Judas the traitor, Pilate, and Herod,
while permitting them to sin according to his eternal counsel and denying
them the grace required for obedience and for abstaining, in a praiseworthy
and proper manner, from the abominable killing of the Savior, imputes no
hypocrisy or double character to God. Indeed, there would be no hypocrisy even
in a man, for the speech is in no way contrary to the meaning of his mind.
Thus Christ, who is God, willed and approved the gathering and obedience of
the Jerusalemites to himself as a thing intensely dear and morally pleasing
to him, just as a hen cares for her chicks, warming and nourishing them by
spreading her wings, although the Jerusalemites

*[Marginal note: A corollary from the distinction between the will of good
pleasure and the will of sign, and from the differences between the members of
the distinction.]*

[PDF p. 61; printed p. 9]

treated the Savior’s gentle invitation with utter indifference. Meanwhile he
denied them that grace which would most sweetly have procured their entirely
free obedience without impairing their liberty. This is an invincible
argument that God decreed that their obedience should never exist.

The Arminians object. 1. “God not only approves the repentance of the
reprobate as something becoming to a person and owed to himself by that
person, but also commands it to the reprobate, and therefore inwardly desires
and wills that it actually be performed.” I answer by denying the consequence.
For nothing follows from God’s gentle invitation other than a serious and
vehement approval of repentance, whether or not it is actually performed. The
command certainly adds to God’s approving will a revelation of the duty
required from the reprobate, but it by no means adds God’s decree by which he
wills that obedience actually to be performed. We deny this, and it was what
had to be proved. *[Marginal reference: Remonstrants’ Synod, article 1, page
57.]*

They object 2. “If God invites people to obedience, earnestly entreats them,
is grieved and sorrowful, and is moved with inward pity because people harden
themselves against obedience, then God not only approves the repentance of
the reprobate but also desires that it be performed. The former is true;
therefore so is the latter, for sorrow over an act not performed is a sign
that God willed it actually to be performed.” I answer by denying the major.
For no more can God’s decree and intention concerning the performance of that
act be concluded from God’s entreaties, grief, and sorrow over obedience not
being performed than we can infer from these that God has true human passions
of the kind Homer imagined in his Jupiter.

*[Marginal note: What kind of will of God is expressed by God’s invitations
and entreaties to obedience and his sorrow over obedience not performed, and
how these are attributed to God by anthropopathy.]*

1. Only this follows: from his most holy nature God so intensely approves the
obedience of the reprobate as a thing morally pleasing to him from the heart.
People similarly desire things from the heart, and when they fail to obtain
them they grieve and are saddened at having unhappily fallen short of their
wishes. In all other respects the likeness of this anthropopathy limps.

2. At most, this proves that God’s will of approval loves existing obedience
with an intense act. That is not against us. God approves obedience whether it
exists or does not exist. Does it therefore follow that he decreed from
eternity that obedience should exist? It does not.

3. They object: “Repentance can be approved by one who neither commands it to
another nor wills it by a command. But God commands repentance to the
reprobate. Therefore God does more than approve the repentance of the
reprobate.” I answer by conceding the whole. For this is in no way against us,
because

[PDF p. 62; printed p. 10]

a command is not only approval of the thing commanded but also an
authoritative revelation of the thing commanded as a duty which the person
upon whom the command is laid is bound to perform.

*[Marginal note: How God approves existing obedience which he nevertheless
does not will or decree to exist.]*

They object 4. “God wills obedience by the will of sign insofar as he praises
and rewards it. But he neither praises nor rewards obedience unless it
actually exists. Therefore God wills by the will of good pleasure that
obedience actually be performed.” I answer, 1. by denying the major. It is not
because God praises and rewards obedience that he therefore wills and
approves it; rather, on the contrary, because he approves it, he therefore
praises and rewards it.

2. Further, God stands in one relation to obedience and in a very different
relation to existing obedience. As Lawgiver God commands obedience and loves
and approves it by the will of sign. But as universal Administrator of the
world, he punishes disobedience or graciously forgives it to the sinner in
Christ. He can neither punish nor forgive disobedience considered in itself
and not existing. Likewise, he rewards only obedience that actually exists.
As Governor of the world he thus draws a manifestation and illustration of
the glory of vindicatory justice, grace, and mercy from obedience and
disobedience actually existing in the nature of things. God cannot do this as
Lawgiver insofar as he approves and commands obedience whether or not it
exists. It is therefore denied that God wills and approves obedience insofar
as he also rewards it. He certainly rewards obedience according to the will
of good pleasure, but he loves and approves it by the will of sign.

They object 5. “If God does not will that the obedience which he commands by
the will of sign should exist, but merely approves it, then those who sin
contravene no will of God and do not do what God did not will to be done.
There is no opposition here between what the sinner does and what God wills to
be done. But the latter is false, for sin is the transgression of the divine
will. Therefore,” and so forth. I answer: it is not absurd that sin is not a
violation of the will of good pleasure. “For who resists his will? Whatever
Jehovah wills, he does. He has formed his counsel, and who will make it void?”
Rather, sin properly consists in a person’s willing and doing the contrary of
what Jehovah approves as good, and doing what did not ascend into God’s heart
to command. *[Marginal references: Romans 9; Psalm 137:6; Isaiah 14:27;
Jeremiah 7:31; 19:5.]*

*[Marginal note: God’s will of good pleasure is either efficient or
permissive.]*

4. The will of good pleasure is twofold: one is efficient, by which God
determines to effect something either by himself or through creatures; the
other is permitting, or

[PDF p. 63; printed p. 11]

permissive, by which God effects nothing himself but permits another to act.
The permitting will denies two things and affirms one. 1. It denies God’s
efficiency, for what God permits he does not effect as author or efficient
cause. 2. What God permits, he does not will as a moral cause, nor does he will
it by any act of an approving will; it therefore denies God’s will of sign.
But the permitting will includes an act of will decreeing that an act be done
or exist by another who deviates and is permitted to sin. God favoring, it
will be demonstrated that this includes a positive act.

Nor should the papists cry out here except against their own, not against us.
Let them hear their own Cuneus: “The will to permit sins includes two things.
One is the purpose not to prevent the sin. The other is the purpose not to
elicit efficacious help or a determination to the opposite act—that is, to
avoid the sin. Add also that God has a decree to concur with the real,
positive act of the will, which also is sometimes a free act elicited by the
will.” *[Marginal reference: Cuneus, Various Disputations, folio 1, number 1.]*

And Thomas Bradwardine says: “With respect to anything whatsoever, God’s
permission is also his actual will. God has some act of will with respect to
anything whatsoever that is permitted, and not an act of nilling; because
then it would not occur, and so would not be permitted,” and so forth. Indeed,
“God’s being supremely actual and perfect is not free but absolutely
necessary.” Therefore God does not have from eternity a suspended will toward
possibilities that will never be future. Hence I infer that he also does not
have a suspended or negative will toward sins which are possible futures.
*[Marginal references: Thomas Bradwardine, On the Cause of God, book 1,
chapter 33; Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 15; Ruiz, volume On Predetermining
Providence, tractate 2, section 5, disputation 12.]*

Diego Ruiz says: “Sometimes permission is predetermined through motives and
circumstances from which the sin is infallibly inferred.” Molina, Suárez,
Vásquez, Valentia, and Bellarmine prove the same thing unanimously and with
one voice: “God employs such a concurrence and such motives with such
circumstances, by which he wills the sin to be permitted, which he knows will
infallibly be committed.” *[Marginal references: Molina, part 1, question 14,
disputation 18, member 2; Suárez, book 2 On Helps, chapter 3, number 4;
Vásquez, part 1, disputation 95, chapter 1, number 68; Gregory of Valentia,
part 1, question 23, point 2; Bellarmine, On the Loss of Grace, book 2,
chapter 14.]*

Soto, John Major, and Vega prove “that some temptations are so vehement that
in them God nevertheless denies special grace, without which it is impossible
to avoid sin.” Add to these Richard, Gabriel, Stapleton, Ledesma, and the
Salmanticenses: “From the will to permit sin, sin follows infallibly, and
indeed by virtue of such a permissive will.” Bañez teaches the same with his
fellow Dominicans. “The actions of those,” says Ledesma, “who crucified Christ,
if considered in relation to those most wicked ministers, were most grievous
sins; but in relation to Christ, who sacrificed himself by means of those
actions, they were holy actions

[PDF p. 64; printed p. 12]

and religious, and according to that relation they could be from God.”
*[Marginal references: Soto, book 1 On Nature and Grace, chapter 1, to 2;
Major, book 2, distinction 25, question 1; Vega, Short Work on Justification,
question 12; Richard, book 1, distinction 38, question 5, sole article;
Gabriel, book 2, distinction 25, article 3, sole question; Stapleton, On
Justification, book 2, chapter 13; Ledesma, On the Helps of Divine Grace 12,
question 29, doubt 1, conclusions 2–4; Domingo Bañez, part 1, question 14,
article 13; Michael Rispolis, book On Predestination, page 62; Alvarez, On
Helps, book 3, disputation 24.16, number 16.]*

Why then, Jesuits—why, Becanus, Suárez, Bellarmine, Vásquez, and Lessius—do you
shout that “the Calvinistic God is the author of sin”? Calvin, Beza, Luther,
Zwingli, and our writers have taught nothing here except what your Ledesma,
Bañez, and Cuneus, and above all the mouths of the Jesuits, have advanced and
taught. What does Rispolis say about this? “By permission we understand the
withdrawal of help by which that evil use would not have occurred, and this
withdrawal of help is called permission because in this withdrawal a man is
left in his natural passive freedom, which necessarily inclines toward evil.”

Diego Alvarez says: “God effects by a predetermining will all acts good from
their object which are done in time, and those which from their object are
not evil insofar as they are acts, although otherwise in the individual they
are sins because of an evil circumstance.”

In the same place: “All Thomists, with one or another exception, teach this
conclusion.” Again: “Before foreknowledge or foresight of the determination of
the created will, God determined from eternity, by an absolute or consequent
will, not to give supernatural helps to those who in fact do not receive
them—if the expression ‘not to give’ is taken to signify a mere negation of
help, or an absolute will not to give such help.” This is what we teach about
God’s permissive will, of which, however, more will be said in confirmation of
our position.

## Chapter III

### The new fiction of middle knowledge

Whether middle knowledge preserves God’s providence in the government of
contingent things. The evasions of the Jesuits, Hieronymus Fasolus, Rodrigo de
Arriaga, Diego Ruiz, Pedro de Arrúbal, Vásquez, Gamachaeus, and others are
examined.

*[Marginal note: The Jesuits’ fiction of middle knowledge is exploded, and our
law of God’s providence concerning future contingents is set forth.]*

Disputations are full of discussion about God’s immanent acts, namely his
knowledge and will. Jesuits, Arminians, and others of that kind establish
“middle knowledge” as the single moderator and arbiter of disputes, which
reconciles the immutability of the divine decree with acts of free will.

[PDF p. 65; printed p. 13]

Our position is that absolutely all free acts occur and actually exist in the
nature of things because God from eternity absolutely and immutably decreed
that so many and such contingent and free acts should exist in the nature of
things. Thus God’s free decree is the first and highest cause of all
contingent things that are future either absolutely or conditionally. And so
God from eternity and absolutely decreed the free repentance or nonrepentance
of the Tyrians as dependent upon the sight of miracles as a condition. He did
so by an absolute act of his decree which, if we consider the decree itself,
depends upon no condition outside God in the creature. Unless we say this,
serious absurdities necessarily follow.

1. Our free acts will not depend causally upon the free decree of divine
providence. Instead, these acts and all things that happen contingently—indeed,
even necessarily, for the rationale of them all is the same—will be future
apart from and before every act of the divine will or foreknowledge. This is
fortune itself anticipating God and his will, care, providence, wisdom, and
omnipotence. How so?

2. Our faith, hope, all the consolations of Christians, patience, fervor in
prayer, and gratitude toward God would have to be resolved ultimately, not
into God’s supreme dominion or his absolute and independent will as the first
and highest cause, but into fortune and the fortuitous connections of
contingent things and creatures.

3. Our adversaries’ position is that so many and such free acts of disputing,
walking, and speaking—and, in supernatural matters, of believing, praying,
hoping, praising God, and so forth—exist in the nature of things, not because
God had decreed that so many and such acts would occur, but because the
creature and created freedom so willed and arranged it; or because, before
every act of God—as if the supreme Creator and Inspector of the world did not
administer all things by his wise counsel and providence—such actions, orders
of things, conditions, and circumstances are to be posited and will be future
while God does not yet know, will, or care, and thus while God sleeps (may the
blasphemy be far from us) and does not govern.

4. From this it follows that all things which will be future by the operation
and ministry of second causes are future, or have their futurity, without God
and without any act of almighty God. This overturns all providence. And if
there is no providence, there will not even be a God. Or else God will govern,
care, and exercise pro-

[PDF p. 66; printed p. 14]

vidence concerning their futurition, their entrance into the world and into
being, and the class and number of those beings and things, which nevertheless
are future before every act of divine government. And if they are future
before every act of God’s government, why should they not also actually exist
apart from every action or care of that same providence? This is
contradictory.

Before we enter the ocean of this dispute, it may be asked, 1. whether the
conditionally future contingent which our adversaries fabricate as the object
of middle knowledge is a future being or a possible being. I shall answer
briefly: the repentance of the Tyrians is considered in two ways.

*[Marginal note: The object of middle knowledge is a being actually future,
and how it is a future being including only possibility.]*

1. It is considered in itself, absolutely, apart from every condition, and
prior to every determination of God’s will, as the Jesuits consider it. In
this way it is only a possible being and not a future one. For what never
occurs in time was never future from eternity; but the repentance of the
Tyrians never occurs. Therefore such repentance is only something possible
and never passed beyond the limits or state of merely possible beings. Such
an object and such repentance are known by the knowledge of simple
intelligence.

2. But this repentance clothed with God’s eternal decree, whose office it is
to give either absolute or conditional futurition, is truly future under this
condition—namely, if the Tyrians see miracles—and is something more and beyond
what is merely possible. For what our Savior says will be future if such a
condition is posited will most certainly and unalterably be future if that
condition is posited. But Christ, whose knowledge and prediction cannot be
mistaken, says that the Tyrians would practice penitence and repentance if
they saw the signs. Therefore such repentance, under the reduplication of
that condition, is something future and not merely possible.

*[Marginal note: The decree concerning future contingents is absolute in
itself.]*

2. It is asked whether this decree of God is conditional or absolute. I
answer: with respect to its object the decree is improperly and figuratively
called conditional; but the decree in itself and simply is absolute. For,
apart from every condition suspending the decree, God absolutely, completely,
and irrevocably decreed the actual connection—although the connection is free,
depending merely upon God’s free constitution rather than upon the nature of
the things connected, since repentance and the sight of signs cohere with one
another merely contingently—between the repentance of the Tyrians and the
sight of miracles. And this con-

[PDF p. 67; printed p. 15]

nection, actually complete and absolutely decreed by God, exists even though
the connected extremes themselves never have futurition.

But Hieronymus Fasolus, the Neapolitan Jesuit, sweats in vain to prove that God
knows conditional future contingents. No one, so far as I know, denies that.
The question is whether God infallibly knows conditional future contingents
before every decree of the divine will, for this is what we deny.

There are weighty writers who reduce middle knowledge to the knowledge of
simple intelligence by this argument: the object of middle knowledge is a
possible being, for hypothetical futurition belongs to a possible being as
such. Indeed, of every possible being it is truly said, “This being will be
future if God wills,” and God knows this to be true of every possible being.
Therefore middle knowledge is reduced to the knowledge of simple
intelligence.

*[Marginal note: The object of middle knowledge is a possible future being;
in itself it is nothing other than the knowledge of simple intelligence.]*

But I answer: every possible being can become future when this condition is
posited, namely, if God wills it to be future. Yet a conditional future is
truly future when the condition is posited, while what is formally possible
and such as possible is not future; otherwise it would already have passed
from the state of possibility to the state of futurition. *[Marginal note: A
future is possible, not future.]*

That the object of middle knowledge is not formally possible but future is
proved by the fact that the object of middle knowledge has determinate truth,
as its patrons teach. In fact, the repentance of the Tyrians if they see
miracles is determinately future. But what is formally possible and such as
possible is not determinately future, for what is formally possible stands
indifferently toward being future or not future and formally abstracts from
both.

2. The Discalced Friars of the College of Salamanca, following Soto’s rules,
observe that the conjunction “if” is taken in three ways: 1. inferentially, as
“If he is a man, therefore he is an animal”; 2. promissorily, as “If you serve
Peter, I shall give you a reward”; 3. conditionally, as “If the Tyrians see
the signs, they will repent.” But I make two observations here. *[Marginal
references: College of Salamanca, Course of Theology, tractate 3, volume 1,
On the Knowledge of God, disputation 6, doubt 4, figure 41; Soto, Dialectical
Summulae, chapter 8, lecture 1.]*

1. A fourth meaning may be added, when “if” denotes mere coexistence without
any connection of the extremes, as “If the pope reads, the Turk will laugh.”
For instruction’s sake we call such propositions “disparate.”

2. “If” is very often taken inferentially and in the first way, although the
consequence is sometimes good and necessary, as “If he is a man, he is an
animal,” and sometimes bad and not necessary, as “If you serve Peter,
therefore I shall give you a reward.” If the promise is human, the consequence
is bad and

*[Marginal note: How uses of the conjunction “if” are distinguished in this
dispute.]*

[PDF p. 68; printed p. 16]

not necessary, because every man is fallible and can violate his pledged
faith. If it is a divine promise, the inference is entirely certain,
necessary, and good, because it is incompatible with God’s being essentially
truthful that he should break his pledged faith.

3. But to the point: I prove that this middle knowledge overturns rather than
preserves God’s providence. 1. The certainty of divine knowledge cannot stand
with this knowledge. Therefore providence is overturned by this knowledge.
The antecedent is proved. Disparate propositions such as “If the pope reads,
the Turk will laugh” and “If the staff is in the corner, it is raining now”
are not known infallibly by God prior to God’s decree. Therefore neither are
conditionals of this kind: “If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent.”

*[Marginal note and references: Middle knowledge overturns God’s providence.
Lessius, On Efficacious Grace, chapter 19, figure 3; Arriaga, Logical
Disputation 14, section 6, figure 104; Fasolus, on Thomas, volume 1, question
19, article 13, doubt 13, figure 235; Ruiz, on Thomas, disputation 61, section
1, figures 17–18.]*

The antecedent is proved by the testimony of our adversaries. The Jesuit
Lessius says that such propositions posit nothing; Arriaga and Fasolus say
that they are only possible and do not possess determinate truth; and Ruiz
says that all such propositions are false and their contradictories true by
purely natural knowledge. I assume this. But conditionals prior to God’s
decree, such as “If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent,” posit
nothing. For apart from God’s decree there is no determinate truth in such
things. They are not true in themselves, since, as is evident, they are not
necessarily true because the extremes cohere entirely by accident. Nor are
they contingently true except possibly, since no cause connecting the extremes
with each other exists on the side of the thing when no decree of God is yet
posited.

*[Marginal note and references: The object of middle knowledge, as they
fabricate it, is uncertain and unknowable except as possible. Arrúbal, volume
1, question 13, article 13, disputation 45, question 5, figure 25; Arriaga,
Logic; Curiel against version 4, On Wisdom 1, page 176.]*

Pedro de Arrúbal and Arriaga reply that the rationale is not the same because
there is no connection at all in disparate propositions. “Once the condition
is posited, namely, if Peter is tempted, it is contradictory that Peter should
neither determinately sin nor determinately not sin. Therefore one side is
determinately true and the other false, and one who predicts this when the
thing stands so predicts the truth. Therefore it was true before the
prediction.”

I answer. “The rationale of these and those certainly appears to be the same,”
says Alfonso Curiel. Nor do I say that the rationale is altogether equal, for
there is a certain motive force in the sight of signs for producing repentance
in the Tyrians, while the existence of a staff in a corner has no force at all
for producing rain. But as concerns metaphysical certainty, or the unalterable
inevitability of repentance, the rationale is altogether equal and the same
in both. For the repentance of the Tyrians follows from the sight of miracles
no more inevitably and unalterably than rain unalter-

[PDF p. 69; printed p. 17]

ably follows from the existence of a staff in the corner. Fasolus, a patron
of middle knowledge, admits this. For the meaning of “If the Tyrians see the
signs, they will repent” is not that the Tyrians will repent determinately and
unalterably because they see the signs. The sight of signs is not the cause
that determines repentance; besides being false, that would be plainly
Pelagian. In the same way, rain does not occur unalterably from the existence
of a staff in the corner. *[Marginal reference: Fasolus, on Thomas, volume 2,
question 14, article 13, doubt 13, point 2, figure 250.]*

2. To Arriaga’s point I say: once it is posited that the staff is in the
corner, it is contradictory that rain should neither determinately occur nor
determinately fail to occur, just as, once temptation is posited, it is
contradictory that Peter should neither determinately sin nor determinately
not sin. The same contradiction in both places weighs upon our adversaries as
much as upon us.

But they will say that “If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent” is
determinately true through the bare coexistence and conjunction of repentance
and the sight of the signs, because these two cohere as the moving condition
and the conditioned thing. Hence, Arrúbal says, “There is no knowledge in God
of future contingents unless they are determined with all their circumstances
and conditions. For this is not determinately true: ‘If Peter asks John for
alms, he will receive them.’ But this is determinately true and therefore
knowable before God’s decree: ‘If Peter asks for alms from John, who is rich
and liberal, in the proper manner and time and with the remaining proper
circumstances, he will then receive them.’” *[Marginal reference: Arrúbal,
volume 1, question 14, article 13, disputation 46, chapter 1, figures 1–2.]*

But I answer: likewise, if all the circumstances are proper and the
conditions determined, and the proper time is posited in which the existence
of the staff in the corner and the rain coexist, this will be determinately
true: “If the staff is in the corner, it is raining.” But the determinate
truth before God’s decree of “If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent”
is the very point in question, and we deny it. For God, by the free decree by
which he denied efficacious grace to the Jews, brought it about that this was
true: “If the Jews see Christ’s signs, they will not repent.” Could God, I ask,
by such a decree have brought it about that this also was true: “If the
Tyrians see Christ’s signs, they will not repent”? Certainly this cannot be
denied.

2. Propositions about a future contingent, even before they are determined by
determinate circumstances, are just as truly contradictory, according to the
positions of our adversaries, as they are after all the circumstances have
been determined. Therefore in this way there will be middle knowledge in God
even before future contingents are determined, contrary to Arrúbal’s
assertion.

3. By this reasoning, therefore, there will be no knowledge in God

[PDF p. 70; printed p. 18]

of “If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent” unless all the
circumstances are determined, and thus unless the will of the Tyrians has
first been moved and bent toward repentance by the motion and action of
efficacious grace. But who, I ask, doubts that God certainly knows these
propositions to be true after the predicate has in fact agreed with and
inhered in the subject? Indeed, in this way angels, human beings, and even
bleary-eyed people and barbers can know determinately that Peter will receive
alms from John, who is rich and liberal, if he asks for them with all the
proper circumstances. For one of those circumstances is that John’s will has
been efficaciously moved to give the alms. But this efficacious motion
presupposes God’s decree. If, however, the Jesuit does not include among the
determinate circumstances John’s will operating in second act and John
actually giving the alms, then I say that, prior to God’s decree, John is able
both not to give and to give the alms even if all the circumstances have been
determined. For even when all the circumstances are determined in this way,
they always leave John to his sheer liberty and the indifference of his will
toward giving and not giving the alms. Consequently there is no metaphysical
certainty here, which is the point in question.

2. I argue thus. Knowledge which removes God’s wisdom and justice does not
preserve but overturns providence. Middle knowledge does this. Therefore
middle knowledge does not preserve but overturns providence. The major is
certain; the minor is proved. All wise and just connections of things come
from God’s wise and just will as their cause. Therefore such connections
cannot precede the acts of God’s wise and just will. An effect is not prior to
its cause. Accordingly all these connections would exist in themselves, in
the nature of things, and be determinately true before the wise and just
decree of the divine will: “If the Chaldeans plunder Job, Job will submit
humbly and patiently to God; if the patriarchs sell Joseph, Pharaoh will
gloriously exalt him; if Judas betrays the Savior, Judas will devise death for
himself with a noose; if Peter prays, Peter will believe intensely.” Acts of
the holy, wise, and just divine will shine in these connections. Therefore
these connections cannot precede the acts of the divine will and knowledge,
nor can they possess determinate truth in that case unless God’s justice and
wisdom are altogether absent and asleep in such things. I add that

*[Marginal note and reference: Arminians 2, section 4. Middle knowledge
removes God’s wisdom and justice.]*

[PDF p. 71; printed p. 19]

a condition is often a means with respect to the conditioned thing. Thus the
hearing of the Word is a means to faith; the sight of signs is a means to the
repentance of the Tyrians; and by God’s ordering the killing of the Savior is
a means to the redemption of the human race. But these wise and just chains
of things are real, good, and holy beings. Therefore they cannot exist in the
nature of things and possess determinate truth unless they come from the first
holy, first just, and first wise being, such as God’s free will—unless God’s
justice is entirely overturned and wisdom banished.

But here the adversaries make two replies. 1. They say, “These chains do not
precede God’s conditional will, although they do precede God’s absolute will,
and there is nothing absurd in this.” I answer: it is entirely false that
God’s conditional will is the cause of such connections. God does not say, “I
will the hearing of the Word to be a means to faith on this or that
condition.” For no condition can be given without an infinite regress. The
hearing of the Word and faith are connected without any condition. The
connection itself is not formally conditional, nor is God’s will which
connects them conditional; it is entirely absolute, which supports our case.

*[Marginal note: God’s will concerning conditional futures is absolute, not
conditional, as the anti-predeterminists dream.]*

For the connection “If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent” is not
conditional, since a condition cannot be assigned without absurdity. Rather,
formally it is one of the things connected, not the connection itself, that
depends upon a condition. That is, the repentance itself is conditioned and
the sight of signs is the condition; and this will of God connecting them is
not conditional. For God wills absolutely that these extremes be connected,
and in no way conditionally.

2. Fasolus replies: “The created will entirely determines itself to either
alternative, and a decree of God is certainly given concerning the connected
extremes. But we ask for a third decree concerning the connection itself, for
God does not say, ‘I will that Judas be converted if he were called in such a
manner.’” *[Marginal reference: Fasolus, on Thomas, volume 2, question 14,
article 13, doubt 14, figure 265, and doubt 16, figure 275.]*

Answer: here the Jesuit yields the field. Job’s patience is connected with the
distresses and calamities which he suffers from wicked people, and the
conversion of Judas or Peter depends upon such a calling, either 1. by its own
nature; 2. by God’s free and just will; 3. by the impulse of fortune; or 4. by
fate. It does not depend upon nature, for murmuring against God in adversity
is more natural than patience. If nature were the cause of the connection,
all who are oppressed would patiently submit themselves to God, which is
utterly false. Nor is there a natural bond between such a calling and the
obedience of Judas,

*[Marginal note: All connections of things, whether absolute or conditional,
are decreed by God.]*

[PDF p. 72; printed p. 20]

between prayer and the activity of faith, or between Joseph’s imprisonment
and exaltation. If fortune or fate reigns here, God’s providence collapses.
Therefore these connections depend solely upon God’s will.

Indeed, the reason why the godly humble themselves under the persecutions of
tyrants is, as they say, “because Jehovah so willed.” Why do they thank God
when strangers and people otherwise hostile in disposition toward them answer
with cheerful and pleasant things, wish them well, love them, and pursue God’s
godly servants with benevolence? The godly give the reason: “They wish us
well, favor us, smile upon our wishes, and grant what we ask because this is
the gracious will toward us of our heavenly Father.” Hence I think it is
evident to anyone of sound judgment that these connections depend upon God’s
just and wise decree.

“If the wicked afflict Job, by God’s free decree he will submit himself. If a
godly man entreats his enemy as a suppliant, he will obtain favor and grace.”
Whence does this come? Certainly from God’s decree. “If Lydia hears the Word,
the Lord will open her heart.” Whence does this come, if not from God’s wise
decree? “If the disciples ask the owner of the donkey for the donkey for the
Lord and Savior, they will return from its owner with their wish fulfilled.”
Whence does this come? Everyone knows that Jehovah decreed it thus from
eternity. It is certainly blasphemous to assert that connections so wise,
gracious, and just emanate causally from no decree of the divine will but are
tossed about by blind fortune.

But anyone can understand that the Jesuits posit conditions here merely to
deceive the people. For if “if” here denotes no logical connection, as Fasolus
says, but merely the moral and free influence of the antecedent upon the
consequent, then prior to God’s decree this is just as determinately true:
“Peter will be converted,” as this: “Peter will be converted if he is called
in such a manner.” Indeed, according to Jesuit dogmas, Peter’s being converted
if he is called in this way, and Job’s being patient if afflicted by evil men,
are no more from God’s free will and decree than rain’s occurring because a
dog barks in a hall. According to the Jesuits, no will of God has any place in
such connections. *[Marginal reference: Fasolus, on Thomas, volume 2, question
14, article 13, doubt 13, point 1, figure 251.]*

5. But Vásquez wonderfully paints and fabricates in these conditionals an
objective coexistence of the extremes without an inferential and logical
connection. He says: “The truth of a conditional proposition consists in a
good inference, for it is at once a consequence and a proposition. Its truth
insofar as it is a proposition is its goodness insofar as it is a consequence.
Therefore if the conditional consequence is not good, as ‘If the Turk is
dead,

*[Marginal reference: Vásquez, on Thomas, disputation 67, question 14, article
13, chapter 3, figures 15, 17, 25.]*

[PDF p. 73; printed p. 21]

therefore Peter married,’ that proposition will be neither true nor false.
When, therefore, we say of Peter, ‘If Peter were anticipated by this congruous
thought, he would do this,’ either we assign there a cause with which the
effect is connected, or we do not. If the former, that part is true; but if we
do not assign a cause, it will be false because there will be no good
consequence, since there will be no connection between that congruous thought
and what Peter would do. Thus both contradictories will be false. For the
propositions to be true and the consequence good, that union need not possess
necessary truth; the truth and goodness of the consequence is sufficient, and
because a consequence once good is always good—in these conditionals, ‘If the
Tyrians see the signs, they will repent,’ the consequence is not necessary
from the nature of the thing but only in fact, as a consequence from singular
matter follows from the antecedent as from a cause, although it could fail to
follow. If the same congruous thought remains entirely the same and the will
does not put it away, the antecedent can never be true and the consequent
false.” *[Marginal reference: figure 26.]*

*[Marginal note: An objective coexistence metaphysically certain and grounding
God’s infallible knowledge concerning every inferential connection is a
dream.]*

I answer, 1. prior to every decree of God to give efficacious grace, “If the
Tyrians see the signs, they will repent” is not a good consequence, but rests
upon an utterly false and Pelagian dogma: “The Tyrians see the signs;
therefore they will repent.” Therefore, on your view, it will be neither true
nor false, any more than “The Turk is dead; therefore Peter married,” for it
is Pelagian and contrary to experience to say that miracles produce
repentance.

But in “If Peter were anticipated by a congruous thought, he would do this,”
if we assign a cause with which the effect is connected, the consequence is
good but plainly identical in reality. That is, the consequence is good if it
is good. Who does not see how foolish the Jesuits show themselves to be in
this matter? For if repentance follows from the sight of miracles, who denies
that it follows?

2. This is a monstrosity of logic: “A consequence not necessary from the
nature of the thing, but only in fact.” Aristotle, far more correctly,
recognizes no legitimate consequence unless the conclusion follows of
necessity from the premises. For if the consequent follows from the antecedent
only in fact, then it can fail to follow. The consequent is therefore true,
but not because of or by the logical force of the antecedent. Thus the
consequence is bad, and consequently if bad once, always bad—just as the
Jesuit rightly says that a consequence good once is always good. The Jesuit
rightly says what Aquinas also says: “The truth of a conditional proposition
consists in the goodness of the consequence.” But this is no consequence at
all,

*[Marginal reference: Aquinas, part 1, question 14, article 13, reply 2.]*

[PDF p. 74; printed p. 22]

prior to every decree of God: “The Tyrians see the signs; therefore the
Tyrians will repent.” What Diego Ruiz says is astonishing: “Although the
inference is not affirmed, provided that at least the coexistence of the free
act with the hypothesis is affirmed, the argument from the affirmation of the
hypothesis to the affirmation of the free act will be valid.” *[Marginal
reference: Diego Ruiz, on Thomas, disputation 68, section 9, figure 21.]*

But this is to say that such propositions are inferential and are not
inferential, for to anyone who knows Latin, an argumentative proposition is
inferential. But should we, I ask, be grateful to Vásquez for saying, “If the
same thought remains entirely congruous and the will does not put it away, the
antecedent can never be true and the consequent false”? For certainly if the
same congruous thought remains and the will never puts it away—that is, if the
Tyrians are congruously moved in such a way that they actually repent—then
without doubt they actually repent. That is: “The Tyrians repent; therefore
the Tyrians repent.” A congruous motion essentially includes the effect;
otherwise it is not congruous but incongruous motion, as the Jesuits teach.

3. In contingent matter, a simply contingent consequent in no way follows
from a simply contingent antecedent.

4. In conditional futures there are true enthymemes and legitimate
syllogisms. Therefore propositions about them are truly argumentative. The
consequence is certain. The antecedent is proved: “The Samaritan woman asks
Christ for water; therefore Christ gives her living water. The Jews will see
Christ’s miracles; therefore the Jews will not necessarily repent.” Again:
“If David flees from Keilah, the Keilites will not deliver him into Saul’s
hands. But if David consults God concerning flight, David will flee from
Keilah. Therefore, if David consults God concerning flight, the Keilites will
not deliver him into Saul’s hands.”

*[Marginal note: Scriptural consequences from a contingent and merely
possible antecedent to a possible consequent are valid because of the
predetermination of the divine decree, not because of the nature of the things
themselves.]*

It must always be noted that such propositions concerning conditional futures
are always argumentative or inferential, not from themselves, but by the
force of the divine decree or determination. Therefore, whenever Holy
Scripture uses consequences of this kind, the consequence is valid not from
the simple connection of the terms, but from God’s free constitution and
predetermination. For this consequence would be plainly ridiculous and empty:
“The Tyrians see the signs; therefore the Tyrians will repent,” if, by an
impossibility, we posit no decree and no providence in God which connects these
extremes with each other. For in themselves and from the nature of the thing,
the repentance of the Tyrians and the sight of the signs in no way cohere
unalterably with one another. If there were from themselves a connection
between these two, and indeed a necessary connection which as a foundation
and certain ob-

[PDF p. 75; printed p. 23]

ject possessed entity-level certainty grounding the infallibility of divine
knowledge, then whether or not God existed and whether or not God’s providence
existed to govern human affairs, the Tyrians would repent if they saw miracles.
There would exist in nature a certain and immovable providence in the things
themselves, from themselves and from their own nature, apart from God’s free
decree and entirely without any providence. But this would be either fated
fortune or a fate anteceding and constraining all God’s providence, which is
plainly most absurd.

3. Argument. Knowledge which overturns the chief act of divine providence,
namely God’s concurrence with the free will and thus with all second causes,
does not preserve providence but overturns it. Middle knowledge overturns the
chief act of providence. Therefore, and so forth. The minor is proved.

*[Marginal note: 6. Middle knowledge overturns all of God’s concurrence with
second causes.]*

Either God says, “Before my decree I foreknow that the will will go forth into
such an act; accordingly I await the determination of the created will,
prepared to concur with it,” or he says, “If the will is placed in such an
order of things and is thus anticipated, and if I furnish my concurrence in
second act, Peter will freely will or do this.” But neither can be said.
Therefore this knowledge removes God’s concurrence with second causes. The
major is certain; the minor is proved in parts.

1. The first cannot be said because God cannot foreknow that such a free act
will determinately occur at that sign at which he awaits the determination of
the created will. As Lodovico a Dola says, “At that sign a co-principle
essentially required for that act is lacking.” For at that sign the created
will and God’s concurrence, which causally concur in the free act, are still
supposed to be indifferent principles of the free act. *[Marginal reference:
Lodovico a Dola, Four-Part Disputation on God’s Concurrence, part 2, chapter
3, section 3.]*

God cannot foreknow that the free act will determinately occur when the
co-principles of that free act can still either exist or not exist. Otherwise
it would follow that God foreknows the free act will determinately occur, and
yet it can happen that such an act never occurs, and thus God’s knowledge will
be fallible. The reason for the consequence is that a will physically
indifferent can both refuse and will, and God, who from his absolute and free
dominion over all created acts “does whatever he wills in heaven and on
earth,” can refuse to concur. For God does not foreknow that he will concur
with the free created will except because, prior to his act of knowledge, he
willed and decreed to concur.

If the second is said, namely, “I foreknow,” says God, “that Pe-

[PDF p. 76; printed p. 24]

ter will believe if I so bend his will and furnish my concurrence in second
act,” then because God’s concurrence essentially includes the free act of
created liberty, God would only be saying, “I therefore foreknow that Peter
will believe if only he believes, so that my knowledge cannot be mistaken.”
But even idiots, uneducated people, and indeed demons certainly know that Peter
will believe if he is going to believe.

Philip Gamachaeus tries to answer this argument. “The conditional decree,” he
says, “which rests upon middle knowledge, should not be expounded here as ‘I
shall act if the man wills to act,’ but rather as ‘I shall act because it is
infallibly future that the man will will to act in such a state.’” In the same
place he says that “God’s efficacious will does not simply precede the man’s
assent, but rather accompanies it.” *[Marginal reference: Gamachaeus, Doctor
of the Sorbonne, on Thomas, question 19, chapter 7.]*

But I dissolve the argument thus. Either one of two contradictorily opposed
effects cannot be from both God’s will and the human will when those two wills
are opposed. For absolute dominion over a free act, effecting that the act
exists rather than does not exist, or the contrary, cannot in any way belong
to two agents tending toward contradictory acts. Yet, according to the
adversaries, God often wills that an act exist which the human will altogether
refuses, and conversely. For suppose that His Most Serene N., King of Great
Britain, and the King of France contend over taking an island, so that one
violently wrests it from the other, while the contrary wills of the kings
remain. It involves an everlasting contradiction for each king to obtain full
and free dominion over that island. For if the island is under the power of
one, it must be taken from the other; therefore dominion over the island does
not belong to each.

*[Marginal note: God’s dominion over the free acts of the creature is removed
if God’s concurrence depends upon the concurrence of an indifferent creature.]*

Therefore God must say, “If the man wills to concur in willing, I shall
concur,” or the man must say, “My will shall elicit this free act if God
concurs with my will.” And when God wills the free act to occur and the man
refuses, or conversely—which happens not infrequently—neither God nor the man
will possess full dominion over the act. God must therefore say, “I shall act
if the man wills to act.”

*[Marginal note: 7. Middle knowledge destroys all futurition of things,
because it posits all future things apart from God’s will.]*

4. Argument. Future and nonfuture things either are future and nonfuture from
themselves and apart from every will of God, or only causally from God’s will.
If the former, it appears contradictory that future things could have been
nonfuture and nonfuture things could have been future. Thus all things are
necessarily future and none contingently future. For whether God refused or
willed, there would be a connection between the sight of signs and the
repentance of the Tyrians,

[PDF p. 77; printed p. 25]

and it would not be within God’s power to make this proposition false. God
could not cause its falsity by his free action, because, according to the
Jesuit-Arminians, this connection is a real being prior to every act of the
divine will. Nor could God make this proposition false by a necessary action:
“If the Tyrians see the signs, they will repent.” It is evident to anyone that
this is absurd. It would then be impossible for God to give repentance to the
Tyrians if they did not see signs, or to deny repentance if they did see
signs. But this needs no refutation.

I add: God knows conditional futures by this knowledge either formally or
only materially. The former is not knowable by God, because it is false that
the Tyrians determinately repent because they see signs; they can see the
signs and never repent. If God knows these things only materially, then they
are just as inevitably future when the condition is removed as when it is
posited, and are contingents future absolutely, not conditionally. But the
Jesuits will not admit this, for here they always distinguish between
contingents future absolutely and contingents future conditionally, although
Gamachaeus concedes it. *[Marginal reference: Gamachaeus, on Thomas, part 1,
question 19, chapter 2.]*

But if every future thing is future from eternity and never begins to be
future, as that great man Thomas Bradwardine proves, then I infer: if a future
thing is future from itself and not from God’s will, a present thing is also
present from itself and a past thing past from itself. *[Marginal reference:
Bradwardine, On the Cause of God, book 1, chapter 13.]*

For if this is false, as the Jesuit-Arminians maintain—“The conditional
futurition of the Tyrians’ repentance is from God’s free will”—then this will
also be false: “The actual existence of the Tyrians’ repentance is from God’s
free will.” For according to sound logic, it is impossible that anything be
present from God’s free will unless its futurition was also from God’s free
will. Whatever exists in time from God’s will was itself future from eternity
by that same will of God.

That a future thing never began to be future is proved thus. Suppose, on your
view, that a future thing began two hundred imaginary years before the
creation of the world. Three hundred imaginary years before it, one of these
was true: “The beginning of the future thing is future,” or “The beginning of
the future thing is not future.” If the former, then by the same reasoning the
thing beginning to have futurition was future before it was future, which is
absurd. But if “The beginning of the future thing is not future,” then
something will be true in the present—namely, at the beginning of the two
hundredth imaginary year before creation—which was not fut-

*[Marginal note: No future thing began to be future.]*

[PDF p. 78; printed p. 26]

ure in the three hundredth year, which is absurd. From this it follows that
God begins in this year to know and will what he previously did not know and
will, and there will be mutability and increase in God’s infinite knowledge
and will, which is blasphemous. If the conditional futurition of the Tyrians’
repentance never began to exist but was from eternity, then from eternity that
futurition depended upon God’s free will. Therefore such futurition never
existed prior to God’s free will.

5. The argument is this. If this knowledge is posited, the certainty of
providence perishes. Therefore this knowledge does not preserve providence.
The antecedent is proved. If this knowledge is posited, the certainty of
predestination perishes, because prior to every dominion of the divine good
pleasure and prior to every free decree of God, these possess determinate
truth: “If this many, namely 1,440 elect persons, are called in this way and
their wills are finally bent in this way through such and such motives, they
will finally obey God. And if this many reprobates, say 166,700, are called in
this way and their wills affected by such motives, they will finally resist
God when he calls.”

*[Marginal note: 8. Middle knowledge destroys all decreeing judgment of divine
providence and also of election and reprobation.]*

Hence, on the supposition of this new knowledge, it is more than certain that
the avoidability or inevitability of the conversion or nonconversion of all
mortals precedes God’s decree. Therefore God cannot elect, convert, and save
more people than those who are in fact converted. For before God freely
decrees that they should believe, it is inevitably and determinately future
that this many, neither more nor fewer, will finally believe and be saved.
Consequently God’s free dominion has no weight, and God’s will has no power at
all to determine that this many, neither more nor fewer, and thus neither
later nor sooner, should be converted. Let the reader see how unhappily Molina
extricates himself from this argument in volume 1, on Thomas, question 23,
article 4, section 5, disputation 1, member 11, page 322, conclusion 6.

6. Argument. Knowledge which necessarily implies that the patience of the
saints, thanksgiving, zeal and ardor in prayer, and trust in God do not flow
from God’s gracious will, but that all these exist before God graciously
decrees that they should occur, does not preserve providence. Middle knowledge
necessarily implies this. Therefore, and so forth.

*[Marginal note: 9. Middle knowledge overturns all religion, patience,
thanksgiving, zeal in prayer, trust, and hope.]*

The minor is proved. Suppose all these possess determinate truth before every
gracious and free decree of God: “If the Chaldeans despoil Job, then Job will
patiently submit himself to God. If Esau should meet the patriarch Ja-

[PDF p. 79; printed p. 27]

cob and do him no injury, then Jacob will pray ardently and give thanks to
God. If Saul attacks David with weapons in order to kill him, then David will
place his trust in God.” If, I say, all these possess determinate truth prior
to every decree of God, then the patience of the saints, thanksgiving, ardor
in prayer, and trust cannot flow from any decree of God. For what is prior to
the decree cannot flow from God’s decree as an effect flows from a cause. But
patience does not exist at all except on the hypothesis of an inflicted evil,
nor gratitude except on the hypothesis of a benefit conferred. Hence it is
evident that these most excellent virtues are not from God’s gracious will,
which is absurd, because both the conditions and the conditioned things would
precede all God’s knowledge and decree.

7. Argument. What is inferred from a merely possible supposition is only
possible, since even when the supposition is posited it is only possible. It
therefore does not possess determinate truth and is not the object of God’s
infallible knowledge. But the object of middle knowledge is inferred from a
merely possible supposition, and is only possible even when the condition is
posited, and in no way necessary apart from God’s decree. Therefore such an
object does not possess determinate truth, and so this knowledge does not
preserve God’s providence.

*[Marginal note: 10. The object of middle knowledge is a possible future that
does not possess determinate truth.]*

The reason for the major is that a future never follows from a possibility,
just as a species or something less extensive can never be inferred from a
genus or something more extensive. “It is an animal; therefore it is a lion”
does not follow. Therefore the determinate futurity of the Tyrians’ repentance
cannot be inferred from the fact that they see signs, since the sight of the
signs is only possible and repentance after seeing the signs is possible,
merely indifferent, and can never occur.

Herice says that “the scholastics did not know it.” Granado and Fonseca say
that “something new is always devised by the more recent writers.” The College
of Salamanca says that “the Council of Trent, which occurred in 1545, taught
nothing about it, although there would have been the greatest need to do so if
it had not been a fiction.”
