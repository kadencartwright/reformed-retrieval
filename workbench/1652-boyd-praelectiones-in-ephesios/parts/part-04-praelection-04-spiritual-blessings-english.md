[Marginalia: Lecture IV.]

We have before us, hearers, that notable general proposition which is
afterwards unfolded more fully by the Apo—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 49 | Caput I p. 7]

—stle through its parts and explained more distinctly in the former part of
this chapter. For the sake of more
distinct teaching, we proposed that two things in it should be considered: the
form in which our Apostle sets it before us—namely, the form of thanksgiving
or doxology, that is, the giving of thanks, into which the Apostle bursts at
once at the very threshold of his epistle with a truly remarkable affection
of piety, although he is a prisoner and detained in bonds at Rome (since this
was discussed fully enough above, there is no reason to repeat it here)—and
the matter, or material parts, which remain for us now to examine in it.
[Marginalia: Transition.]

We have already spoken about the author of this blessing, namely God the
Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, while considering who it is whom the Apostle
blesses here, that is, to whom he gives thanks with his whole heart. Therefore
we shall weigh only these three things in order within the matter of the
proposition itself: (1) the blessing itself proposed in general; (2) where, or
in what place, that heavenly Father blessed us; and finally, in whom he
blessed us. You see that all these things are distinctly comprehended in these
words. From them we shall endeavor to derive some observations with such
brevity that, if possible, some time may remain for writing down the things
which we have already taught in their proper place.

[Marginalia: Threefold division.]

As for the first, “who blessed us with every spiritual blessing”: since I
remember explaining above what it is for God to bless us and how greatly it
differs from that blessing by which we pursue his holy name with grateful
hearts, I do not press that here. We add only this by way of further
elaboration, that is, for the sake of more exact teaching, so that the epithet
by which the Apostle marks and designates the benefits of God the Father in
Christ when he calls them *spiritual blessings* may be understood more
clearly. There are three kinds of blessings, that is, of benefits, with which
the elect and faithful are adorned, since every good which we enjoy in this
life as well as the future is God-given or God-sent.

Some goods belong to the body and in some respect are common to man and the
brute animals themselves, such as those that concern vegetative and sensitive
life. Others are proper to human beings but common to the regenerate and
unregenerate, such as those that properly concern the human mind or chiefly
have their seat in it, that is, in the rational soul. These include all the
arts, both the refined and liberal arts and the lower and mechanical ones,
although the latter are more necessary than the former for sustaining life;
learning and knowledge properly so called, and thus the whole of human
philosophy; and political, moral, and economic virtues. We assign to this same
order of blessings all the foundations, aids, instruments, supports, and
occasions of these things—for example, natural aptitude itself, judgment,
memory, ingenuity, books, resources, schools, and infinitely many other such
things. The entire consideration of the natural and animal man, that is, the
unregenerate man, rests in these things and moves within these bounds and
limits, within the barriers of this wretched life. He is wholly unable to pass
beyond them or to rise higher in hope or thought to the future life, shut up
as he is in the darkness of sin and a blind prison. To this are referred all
those goods “divided threefold,” as Aristotle, that most acute observer of
natural men, puts it in book 1 of the *Nicomachean Ethics*.

[Marginalia: First member. Three kinds of blessings. 1. 2. Exclamation.]

See within what miserable confines, within what a narrow boundary, this
corrupt and sinful nature is restrained! It does not recognize that third kind
of spiritual blessings of which the Apostle makes mention here. I mean those
goods which are ordained only to the kingdom and life of God, to that eternal
and heavenly life. Their nature is such that not only are they themselves
truly good and true blessings, but they alone sanctify the use of those other
goods for us and render it saving. Apart from these blessings and without
their fellowship, the other goods generally turn to the greater ruin of those
who possess them and frequently become occasions of more grievous
condemnation. This comes not from the nature of the goods themselves but from
the malice and depravity of human nature.

[Marginalia: 3.]

These are benefits proper to the faithful and regenerate. They do not come to
us from nature, not even in the smallest part, but are wholly from grace. Nor
do they concern the present life, as the former goods do, but are referred to
that future and glorious life. The Apostle therefore judged that he must make
mention of them here among the Ephesians, and thus among all the faithful. So
far as he can, he is always accustomed to draw human minds away from present
things and from the care, pursuit, and anxiety of this life, to which all are
by nature excessively inclined; to lead them up to heaven itself; and to draw
them to love that heavenly life.

But see, I ask, how far he is from speaking sparingly, meagerly, or feebly
about these blessings. Instead, as soon as he makes mention of them, he
enlarges his mouth together with his heart: “He blessed us,” he says, “with
every spiritual blessing.” In this way he commends, extols, and magnifies all
the more to them those riches of divine grace. He indicates that divine
beneficence withheld from them nothing necessary for eternal life, salvation,
and blessedness, but poured down from heaven upon the saints and faithful the
whole supply of heavenly grace, as it were, from a full horn.

[Marginalia: With every blessing.]

For in Romans 8 he argues most solidly and sweetly: “He who did not spare his
own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how will he not also graciously give
us all things together with him?” And just as there is no end or boundary in
the love of God and Christ—for, as his beloved disciple says in chapter 13 of
his Gospel, “having loved his own who were dwelling in the world, he loved
them to the end”—so also there is no measure or restriction in his grace and
blessing. He whom God has once begun to attend with his kindness in Christ he
never forsakes and never will forsake until at last he renders him in heavenly
places laden with that eternal weight of exceedingly excellent glory.

“Jehovah God is a sun and shield,” says the royal Psalmist in Psalm 84.
“Jehovah gives grace and glory and withholds no good thing”—that is, as the
Apostle speaks here, no spiritual blessing—“from those who walk in
integrity.” Therefore, as he closes that psalm, he rightly exclaims—or closes
by exclaiming—and let us exclaim together with him: “O Jehovah of hosts, how
blessed is the man who trusts in you!”

[Marginalia: Romans 8:32. John 13:1. Psalm 84:11–12.]

The Apostle himself will teach us one by one in what follows what those
spiritual blessings are in particular. There is therefore no reason to taste
them before their proper place. Let us instead pass to the second member of
the matter described above, in which the Apostle says that God blessed us “in
the heavenly places”—“in the places above heaven,” as some have translated
it—that is, in that glorious place which far surpasses all these visible
heavens not only in position and height but also in glory, light, splendor,
and blessedness.

Here it must be held, hearers, that all the orthodox do not explain this
phrase in the same way. Some refer it to the blessings themselves, as though
it had been added only as a further explanation of the preceding word in this
manner: “He blessed us with every spiritual blessing,” namely, with hea—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 50 | Caput I p. 8]

—venly things or heavenly benefits. On this reading “with every spiritual blessing”
and “in the heavenly places”—spiritual blessings and heavenly things—are
entirely the same in reality. In this way, they say, the Apostle denotes that
he means those spiritual blessings which concern heavenly blessedness and are
therefore proper to the faithful and elect, rather than things for the use of
the present life which are also common to the unregenerate and ungodly.

[Marginalia: Second member. First of various expositions.]

This exposition is indeed devout and orthodox. Yet somehow my mind inclines
more to the following one, because the expression “in the heavenly places” is
referred to heavenly places. The first exposition seems somewhat harsh
because of the change in gender and number in the explanatory phrase that is
added. Moreover, the Apostle takes this same phrase in the same sense below:
both in verse 20 of this same chapter—God “placed Christ at his right hand,”
he says, “in the heavenly places,” that is, in heaven—and in verse 6 of the
following chapter—he raised us up together and seated us together “in the
heavenly places” in Christ Jesus. Since those phrases are plainly understood
of a place, and the noun *place* must necessarily be supplied in them, it
certainly appears that this same expression should be taken no differently in
the present verse.

[Marginalia: Second exposition.]

We should consider instead how the Apostle can say here that we have been
blessed in heavenly places, that is, in that glorious heaven in which Christ,
King and Lord, now sits at the right hand of God the Father. In what sense or
on what account does this suit us, who still dwell on earth and are so far
distant and removed from those heavenly places? Lest I delay you longer,
receive the answer briefly in this way. We may be said to be blessed by God,
that is, endowed or presented with spiritual gifts and benefits, in heaven.

First, this is on account of God himself who blesses. Residing in heaven as on
his glorious throne, he bestows these spiritual and saving gifts upon the
elect and faithful, and upon them alone. This not only wonderfully commends to
us the dignity and incomparable worth—that is, worth above every price—of
these spiritual goods, because the benefits are not earthly and temporal but
eternal, heavenly, and truly divine. It also opens to us their first fountain
and origin.

[Marginalia: Note the common proverb. 1.]

For just as it is commonly said of wedlock or marriage that it is contracted
and arranged in heaven but consummated on earth—that is, it is first destined
and ordained by divine providence before it is actually entered upon by human
beings—so no one was ever truly blessed on earth whom God had not first
blessed in heaven by the firm decree of his counsel and the immutable and
irrevocable determination of his most free good pleasure.

Second, this is on account of our Lord Jesus Christ and his glorious person,
who according to his human nature resides in heaven, laden with every glory
and blessing. He does this not only in his own name but also on behalf and for
the sake of his mystical body, that is, of all the elect, although they still
dwell on earth. The following phrase, “in Christ,” wonderfully proves and
confirms this account. It indicates that we are laden with every spiritual
blessing in heaven “in Christ,” that is, in the very person of Christ as our
head and mediator. In both his own name and the name of us all, he entered
that heavenly inheritance and there was filled with glory and blessing. This
glory and blessing is not contained and enclosed in him alone but flows
abundantly into all of us, his true members. It is like that ointment poured
upon the head of Aaron the high priest which was diffused and dripped not
only onto his beard but also to the very edge or hem of his garment.

[Marginalia: 2. Mark this reasoning carefully. Psalm 133:2.]

In a word, his blessing is ours. Since it is in heaven, where he himself also
resides, where, I ask, will we be said to be blessed by God if not in heaven,
where our blessed head is? This is especially so because the Apostle himself
will speak so plainly and emphatically in the following chapter, saying in the
same sense that God the Father made us alive together with Christ, raised us
from the dead together with Christ, and seated us together in heaven in that
same Christ—or, so to speak, caused us to sit together. None of these things
yet belong to us except in the person of Christ himself. In him we already
live as in our head, have been raised from the dead, and reign and triumph in
heaven even while fighting on earth. He has done all these things for us, and
we are united to him by such a close, ineffable, and indissoluble bond—indeed,
in him we are as one mystical person with him, of which he is the head and we
the body.

Finally, we are also called blessed in heaven on account of the completion of
all spiritual blessings, which is ultimately reserved for us in heaven. It is
reserved in such a way that we now enjoy it through faith and even now hold
and possess it in hope, so that whatever we expect on that glorious day may
be said already to have been obtained by us, according to the customary
expression of the Holy Spirit in the Scriptures.

[Marginalia: 3.]

It is therefore evident on what account we are said to be blessed in heaven,
whether we regard the source and first author of every blessing, namely God
himself, glorious and blessed forever, who resides on his heavenly throne and
distributes those goods to us; or Christ our head, received into heaven and
now reigning there gloriously, in whom is the fountain and fullness of all our
blessings; or finally the supreme perfection and consummation of those same
blessings, their ultimate summit and culmination, which we shall at last enjoy
in heaven.

Once these things are understood, less labor will now need to be expended in
explaining the third item that follows, namely, in whom God the Father endowed
us with every spiritual blessing in heavenly places. The Apostle adds “in
Christ.” I say, *in Christ*, not so much insofar as he is the eternal Son of
God the Father as insofar as he was appointed our Mediator from eternity,
ordained as our head, and in the fullness of time exhibited to us in the human
nature which he assumed.

[Marginalia: Third member.]

Considered in this way, Christ in turn stands in two relations to God’s
benefits. First, passively, so to speak, insofar as by the dignity of his
person and office he receives in himself those benefits of God the Father as
his own. Thus he is a receiving subject and, as others say, a kind of passive
matter or material cause of our salvation, which consists wholly in his death
and blood, in that propitiatory sacrifice of his, as the matter of every
salvation and blessing. We see this consideration of Christ our Mediator and
High Priest expressed everywhere by this phrase among the Apostles,
especially Paul, by which we are said to be blessed in Christ, holy in Christ,
just, reconciled to God, gracious and acceptable to God, and so forth. That
is, they regard us as grafted into Christ through faith and, so to speak,
incorporated into him, for the sake of Christ, his merit, and his favor.

[Marginalia: 1.]

Second, this same Christ stands actively or effectually toward these same
benefits. Having received them from the Father and had them poured into his
own person beyond every measure, he freely bestows them upon us, who have
deserved nothing of the kind and indeed could never deserve them, but have
rather deserved every wrath and hatred. In this respect Christ is customarily
presented as another giver and distributor of those same goods from the
Father and as their efficient cause, second in order—

[Marginalia: 2. Through Christ.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 51 | Caput I p. 9]

[Typographic repetition at the page turn: scan 51 repeats the final words
“deserved … In this respect Christ … efficient cause, second in order” from
scan 50; the repeated matter is normalized once above.]

—within us.

Our Apostle ordinarily expresses this consideration of Christ the Lord in
this way when he is accustomed to pray for all goods for us—grace, peace, and
so forth—from God the Father and from our Lord Jesus Christ. He also does so
when he declares that through Christ we are endowed with some heavenly
benefit by God the Father, as immediately below in verse 5: “that he might
adopt them as sons for himself through Jesus Christ.” For the Father is said
to do something through the Son in this way, namely, as an agent or efficient
cause second in order.

Both senses are entirely true, and devout and orthodox is the opinion of
those who explain “in Christ” here as “in the material cause of our
salvation,” since in him are the fulfillment of the law, expiation of sins,
wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, our redemption, and thus, in a word,
our whole salvation. On this account, “in Christ” here would mean the same as
“on account of Christ’s merit,” or “in respect and consideration of Christ.”
For example, it would be as if a king, having received a notable benefit from
one of his subjects, or having experienced the most ready obedience of his
beloved son, should not only love him uniquely but also love all his friends,
clients, relatives, and household in him—that is, out of regard and favor for
him—should hold them dear in his son, and should attend them with every favor
on his account.

[Marginalia: Comparison.]

Yet the point we touched on above certainly seems to be something closer and
more effectual which the Apostle indicates by this phrase in the present
passage: namely, that “we are blessed in Christ.” That is, he means not merely
that our persons are blessed through and on account of Christ and his merit,
but rather that we are blessed in the person of Christ himself as members in
their head, since his blessing, life, and glory are ours. Thus Jehovah himself
is called *tsidqenu*, that is, “our righteousness,” by the prophet in Jeremiah
23. In a word, we are blessed in him not merely because his blessing flows
into us through his merit and is communicated to us on his account, but
because he himself, our head, is now laden with every blessing, just as in him
and together with him we have already risen, already lived again from the
grave, and already sit and have been placed in heaven.

[Marginalia: Jeremiah 23:6.]

From these things as now explained arise various heads of observations, only
some of which we shall touch upon in passing. First, you see how the Apostle
undoubtedly alludes to the promise made to Abraham, namely, that “all the
families of the earth would be blessed in his seed.” He thereby explains that
promise more clearly and distinctly and teaches which seed is meant, namely,
Christ the Redeemer; then what blessings or what kind of blessings are meant,
namely, not those carnal and earthly ones which under the pedagogy of the law
were more fully inculcated upon the ancient people as types, shadows, figures,
and even certain pledges of heavenly things, but those spiritual blessings
which are far better and more excellent by infinite degrees, since they
concern eternal life, glory, and salvation; and finally to whom alone that
promise belongs, namely, not to every individual among the Gentiles without
distinction, although no race of men is excluded from it, but only to the
faithful and elect, either already grafted into Christ by living faith or to
be grafted into him in their own time.

[Marginalia: First observation. Genesis 22:18.]

Second, note in passing that notable expression of the holy language by which
God’s benefits are customarily called *blessings* and God is said to bless
human beings when he benefits them. He does this by his powerful and effectual
word alone, by which, the Apostle says, he calls the things that are not as
though they were and by calling causes things that formerly did not exist to
exist. By this word, says the royal Psalmist, “he established the heavens: he
spoke, and all things were made; he commanded, and whatever exists came into
existence.”

This teaches us not only to observe the power and efficacy of the divine word,
by which, just as he created all things from nothing in the beginning, he is
also effectual through his word in renewing, illuminating, making alive,
healing, and purifying us when he deigns to support its preaching and hearing
with the power and efficacy of his Spirit. It also serves to confirm our faith
in God’s Word, so that we may learn to depend upon it wholly, to be rooted and
founded in it even amid temptations, and firmly to rely and rest upon its
promises through faith, together with that same royal Prophet. Having
experienced in himself both the certainty and efficacy of the divine word, he
inculcates this same thought three times in a single psalm, Psalm 56: “In God
I will praise his word. In God I trust; I will not fear what flesh may do to
me. In Jehovah I will praise the word.”

[Marginalia: Second observation. Psalm 33:6, 9. Psalm 56:5, 11.]

Third, if God blessed us in Christ, we certainly were not blessed in
ourselves, but rather cursed and children of wrath. Nor did we have anything
in our whole nature that could have attracted or drawn out that blessing of
God, much less deserved it. Not even our misery itself rendered us worthy of
pity, since it had been contracted voluntarily by our own fault. Therefore
our whole salvation and blessing is purely free. Indeed, that eternal
inheritance to which we are called in Christ is nothing other than the
Father’s blessing given to sons out of fatherly grace, not wages owed to hired
laborers for their works. The Savior himself will disclose this gracious
foundation of salvation in that final and sweetest sentence: “Come, blessed
of my Father,” and so forth. He declares that we are to be given the heavenly
inheritance because by pure grace we were received as sons by God the Father
and adopted “in Christ, the uniquely beloved, only-begotten, and firstborn
among many brothers.”

[Marginalia: Third observation. Confirmation. Anticipation.]

The testimonies concerning good works which he afterwards adds prove the
Father’s blessing, not as causes of salvation, but as effects, marks, fruits,
and testimonies—known and manifest to all—of faith and of the divine blessing
begun here within us. They are therefore especially suited to demonstrate the
rectitude of that judgment.

Finally, it is neither rash nor without reason that, when setting forth this
spiritual blessing, the Apostle added the phrase “in the heavenly places.” He
did so to lift our hearts, thoughts, affections, and desires from these lands
and earthly things to heaven itself, so that although we dwell on earth in the
body, we may nevertheless dwell in heaven in mind, where we have our glorious
head and in him the treasure of all our blessing, the inexhaustible fountain
of all our life, grace, joy, and glory. Therefore, unless we are plainly
senseless and mad, we must surely also have our heart in heaven, where we have
our head, homeland, treasure, heavenly inheritance, stored-up hope,
unfading and incorruptible crown, and, in a word, eternal blessedness.

[Marginalia: Fourth observation.]

Therefore let us conclude this third discourse of today in the words of the
Apostle himself to the Philippians and Colossians: “Let our *politeuma*, our
commonwealth and manner of life, be in heaven, where that heavenly city also
is. Let us therefore seek the things above, where Christ is seated at the
right hand of God—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 52 | Caput I p. 10 (through Lecture IV close)]

—let us care for the things above and not earthly things, if indeed we have
died with Christ and risen with him.” For then we may certainly apply to
ourselves that same most sweet argument of the Apostle which follows in the
same place: “Our life is hidden with Christ in God. But when Christ, who is
that life of ours, has been revealed, then we too will be revealed glorious
with him.” Meanwhile, to him with the Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit,
be all honor and glory forever. Amen.
