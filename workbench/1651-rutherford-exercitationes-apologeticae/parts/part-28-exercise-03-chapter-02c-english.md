13. *Matthew 11:25. I thank you, Father* (says *Christ*), *Lord of heaven and
earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and revealed them to
little children. 26. Yes, Father, because so it was well-pleasing before you.*
From this I argue as follows: the efficacious cause why the little children
are enlightened and the wise are not is God's good pleasure, on account of
which Christ gives thanks to God alone, as to the one upon whom being actually
enlightened rather than not enlightened depends. Otherwise, if the fact that
this man is enlightened while another, furnished with equal prevenient grace,
is not enlightened depends upon man's free cooperation, thanks would have to
be given to man, not to God. The Arminians say that it is from this man's

[1651 logical printed p. 434 | IA leaf 452 | PDF p. 452]

humility that he is enlightened, and from another's proud pursuit of wisdom
that he is not enlightened, and that God's decree is that good pleasure.
Answer 1. It is certain that Cassian and Faustus handed down this interpretation
drawn from Pelagius. The Arminians avoid the word *merit*, but hand down the
thing itself. Indeed, like the papists, they taught that God's grace is
required for the very essence of merit, as Prosper and Fulgentius observed.
2. Let this, then, be God's decree and good pleasure: “I decree that all should
be converted, provided that they are little children.” And its contrary will
be, “I decree that none should be converted if they are wise.” No such decree
exists in all Scripture. For in that case all the humble would be converted
and none of the wise. But this latter claim is false: *1 Corinthians 1: God
chose not many noble, not many wise.* Therefore some wise people, though not
many, are converted, and thus God himself would rescind this decree, which is
absurd. 3. If by “little children” he here understands those humble in habit,
this decree cannot but be rescinded, because habitual humility does not
undeviatingly determine the will to conversion, and so many humble people are
not converted. But if by being a little child he understands actually
assenting to God as he reveals, this is certainly nothing other than actually
being converted. Yet being actually converted cannot be a prerequisite
condition in conversion. And so, granting that being a little child determined
God's will to convert this man and not that one, God would, by the necessity
of his justice and truthfulness,

[1651 logical printed p. 435 | IA leaf 453 | PDF p. 453]

undeviatingly convert this man and not that one. Thus the efficacy of grace
would have to be referred back to God's determining will, not to human free
choice. 4. This good pleasure distinguishes not only the proud from the humble,
but also divides the whole human race into two classes, verse 27: *Nor does
anyone know the Father except the Son, or he to whom the Son wills to reveal
him.* This distinction, therefore, is referred directly not to the wills of
the wise and the little children but to God's will, which reveals the Father
to whom he wills. 5. There is no ground on which Christ gives thanks to the
Father for a bare decree, but because he revealed Christ to his disciples and
little children, not to the princes of this world who were eminent in rank and
wisdom. 6. Grevinchovius denies that the revelation of faith is treated here
(*unless* (he says) *perhaps you take faith for the object of faith*). But the
doctrine of faith was revealed not only to little children but also to the
wise. Otherwise, how could the wise have hated and disdained this doctrine?
Who would hate something not revealed? But if by “reveal” they here mean to
enlighten in second act, then it is necessary that the Savior is speaking here
about the generation of faith, and habitual humility will thus be the cause
of the efficacy of this revelation, which is absurd. 7. If the wise and the
little children are the formal object of this decree, then those to whom this
mystery is revealed will always be less worthy and worse than those to whom it
is not revealed. Arminius, however, will concede this only reluctantly, for he
acknowledges that God provides unequal grace to equals,

[Marginalia: *Against Ames*, p. 52.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, disputation 16, thesis 7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 436 (misprinted 463) | IA leaf 454 | PDF p. 454]

but equal grace to unequals—indeed, that he applies greater grace to those who
are worse. Consequently he reveals this mystery to the less good and does not
reveal it to those who are better and humbler. Thus this decree would still be
rescinded.

14. *All whom the Father has given me come to me*, John 6:37. Likewise, *all
who have heard and learned from the Father come to me.* Now all logicians know
that propositions concerning every instance are necessary. Hence those given
by the Father and taught by the Father necessarily come to Christ—that is,
they believe. But no necessitation comes from free choice; therefore it comes
from God and his grace. For since necessity is a mode of being, it arises from
the causes from which the being itself arises. But there are no causes here
except grace, the Word, and created freedom. They will not say that necessity
flows from the last two; it must therefore flow from God graciously moving.
If those given and taught by the Father come to Christ in such a way that, by
the indifferent determination of human freedom, they can fail to come, then
this contradiction could be true: “Some who are given and taught by the Father
do not come to Christ.” Thus Christ—away with blasphemy—would have spoken
falsely. Corvinus's escape here is subtler: by [*hear*] (he says) and [*learn*]
is denoted a certain disposition effected in us through grace, by which it
comes about that we do what is commanded, that is, that we come to Christ.
Answer 1. If a grace is meant by which it comes about that we do what is
commanded, his doctrine should be

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 46, fol. 18.]

[1651 logical printed p. 437 | IA leaf 455 | PDF p. 455]

that grace moves the will undeviatingly. If it moves deviably, I still infer
this to be true: [*Some who have heard and learned from the Father do not come
to Christ.*] But the sophist wished to conceal the matter and wants a certain
disposition effected through grace to be denoted here, by which it comes about
that we do what is commanded. Yet whether through this grace it comes about
that we act necessarily or contingently, deviably or undeviatingly, he leaves
the reader—whom he strives to make sport of—to guess, lest he betray a bad
case. 2. More subtly than solidly, Corvinus wishes those who heard and learned
from the Father to be understood as those who heard and learned in such a way
that they actually come to Christ. Thus the Savior would have said, “All who
actually come to Christ actually come to Christ.” This foolish question was
never at issue between Christ and the people of Capernaum. Rather, the question
was why some come to Christ and others do not, as if any mortals had posed to
Christ the question whether those moved by the Father in such a way that they
actually come to Christ actually come to Christ! Is the cause why intense cold
of the air, when it actually causes ice, actually causes ice? Is Corvinus,
actually erring with Arminius, the cause why they obstinately champion Arminian
errors? What more foolish thing could have been said? 3. This is the Savior's
inference: “All will be taught by God,” as it is written in Jeremiah 31:34 and
Isaiah 54:13; therefore they will come to me. But according to Arminius and
Corvinus, Christ would have acted the sophist

[1651 logical printed p. 438 | IA leaf 456 | PDF p. 456]

(may the Lord grant better). “All who will be taught by the Father in such a
way, let them come to me; therefore they will come to me.” Understand it like
this: “All will come to me; therefore all will come to me.” But if Christ's
antecedent stands in the Arminian sense, all will be taught by God while their
indifferent freedom remains, by which it can happen that they either come to
Christ or do not come. Christ's consequent is then utterly false. Indeed, I
would then be allowed to declaim with them in this way: “All will be taught by
God; therefore they will not come to Christ.”

[Marginalia: *Against Perkins*, p. 177.]

15. *But Arminius, in the manner of a shipwrecked man, says this: The Holy
Spirit* (he says) *so acts upon free choice as he knows to be suitable and
congruous, so that it is moved certainly and infallibly. I would have the
Father's drawing understood in the same way.* So say Fonseca, Suárez, and
Valentia. But answer 1. Fonseca, in the place cited, figure 9, says that *a
congruous calling in its essential concept includes the obedience of the one
called.* And Suárez, in the place cited, folio 9, says that *to learn is the
same as to believe, and to come to Christ and to believe are altogether the
same.* Thus it still means: *All who come and believe come to Christ and will
believe.* Suárez, however, says that *God, foreseeing every angle of a free
capacity with respect to moral infallibility, infallibly determines freedom;
but he denies that it is determined by God with respect to metaphysical
infallibility, such that it is impossible to fail, because the physical power
to resist always remains.* Answer 1. But what follows then? That power is
either dormant—and thus on your way of philosophizing freedom is removed and
its use and exercise impeded, which must not be said—or it is not dormant, and
thus an antecedent determination stands together with freedom, which in our
doctrine you

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, bk. 6, ch. 2, question 5, sect. 15.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Divine Grace*, bk. 3, ch. 11, fol. 10.]

[Marginalia: Valentia, vol. 2, disputation 8, question 3, p. 14.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Divine Aid*, bk. 3, ch. 10, fol. 7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 439 | IA leaf 457 | PDF p. 457]

censure. 2. On the Jesuit's principles, an inclination equally indifferent to
unwilling and willing is not an object of infallible knowledge, nor can a
determinate act flowing from that inclination be known except in God's
determining decree—which Suárez will never admit. For infallible knowledge
presupposes in itself a knowable object that is necessary either in itself or
in another that determines it. For all metaphysicians teach that certainty of
mind apart from all certainty and necessity of being is impossible. Therefore
there is no disputing against those who deny first principles. 3. If the
metaphysical power of freedom can fail, nothing absurd will be posited if it
actually fails, as Suárez and Vásquez philosophize elsewhere against absolute
decrees. Consequently, that moral persuasion must determine the will fallibly,
not infallibly. But if he means—as is certainly the Jesuit's mind—that this
moral determination includes foreknowledge of the free cooperation of human
choice, he plays childishly and does not argue. It is as though God should be
said morally to determine human freedom because he foreknows that it will
determine itself freely. Who would attribute determination to God or to the
reason morally applied by God, when all the determinative causality there is
proceeds neither from God nor from the reason—since each leaves the freedom
of metaphysical indifference intact—but from human freedom itself? He is
certainly a disputant worthy to be hissed out of the University of Coimbra and
from the

[1651 logical printed p. 440 | IA leaf 458 | PDF p. 458]

Peripatetic school, believe me. Yet Bellarmine states plainly that the efficacy
of conversion in John 6:44–45 is attributable to the excellence of the teacher,
not to the zeal and industry of the disciple—but not consistently with his
own doctrine. If this is true, Bellarmine's congruous calling will be banished
from theology.

16. *1 Corinthians 4:7. Who has separated you? Or what do you have that you did
not receive? But if you received it, why do you boast as if you had not
received it?* But if, of two men furnished with prevenient grace as seven,
this man believes and renders grace efficacious in second act and in the
outcome, while the other does not believe but resists, then this man has
actually separated himself and can boast over the other in his own free act.
Nor is it necessary that he should give thanks to God for his conversion any
more than the man not converted. Paul, by contrast, attributes the praise for
his conversion to God: *1 Timothy 1:17. To the eternal, immortal, and invisible
King.* *Revelation 5:9. Those glorified offer praises to the Lamb because* (they
say) *you were slain and redeemed us to God by your blood out of every tribe
and tongue and people and nation.* Now if other nations and peoples, endowed
with equal prevenient grace, were unwilling to believe, while these freely
believed with only the same grace and separated themselves from the others,
free choice making the grace of redemption and calling efficacious in the
outcome and in second act, then they owe thanks for their salvation to
themselves rather than to Christ. For all logicians know that effects produced
in this object and not in that one must not be attributed to a cause acting
indiscriminately

[1651 logical printed p. 441 | IA leaf 459 | PDF p. 459]

upon this object and that one. I suppose that the sun imbues all the flowers in
a garden equally with its light and warmth, and this flower flourishes while
the others wither. Would I rightly say to the flourishing flower, “O you who
are more blessed than the others, you owe more to the sun than the others do”?
For I would thereby imply that the sun did not illuminate the other flowers
with light and warmth but blessed only this one, which is plainly false.

Answer 1. Suárez says, *it is not expedient to boast in one's own things.*
Valentia says that the converted person *can boast, not in the Lord alone, but
both in the Lord and then in himself.* Fonseca says that *men can boast in
themselves, but that it is safer* (as Chrysostom and Ambrose say) *not to boast,
because of the danger of pride and vainglory.* The Arminians at the Hague
Conference, p. 333, deny that all boasting is evil. Corvinus, *Against Tilenus*,
ch. 5, p. 136, says that a man can be said to separate himself just as Scripture
says that a man sanctifies, purifies, gives life to, and saves himself. Answer
1. Whoever calls into question or dispute whether men can boast in the Lord is
mad, as Scripture says in 1 Corinthians 1:30–31. But the question is whether
one may boast in what we have received as though we had not received it. Yet
according to our adversaries' doctrine, the converted man, furnished with
prevenient grace as eight, just as the unconverted man is also furnished, can
boast in the free act of his active conversion, which he no more has from God
than the unconverted man does. 2. Their wish to boast both in ourselves and in
the Lord is intolerable pride: it is for us

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Divine Aid*, bk. 3, ch. 20, fig. 14.]

[Marginalia: Valentia, vol. 2, disputation 8, question 3, p. 40.]

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, bk. 6, ch. 2, question 5, sect. 9.]

[1651 logical printed p. 442 | IA leaf 460 | PDF p. 460]

to divide glory with God contrary to the Scriptures. *Psalm 115:1. Not to us,
not to us, but to you, Jehovah, be glory.* 3. Since they maintain that boasting
in ourselves is not safe, they must confess that they tread a less safe road
to eternal glory while we tread a safer one. As far as we are concerned, let
them enjoy that dangerous road. 4. Scripture says that men sanctify, purify,
and liberate themselves as God's instruments and as free agents subordinated
to predetermining grace; but Scripture nowhere says that man is the cause that
separates himself from the number of the reprobate who are outwardly called
just as he is.

[Marginalia: Answer 2. Suárez, *On Divine Aid*, bk. 3, ch. 15, fol. 8.]

Answer 2. Suárez and with him that servile declaimer Grevinchovius say, as do
the Greeks, “For who discerns you?” That is, “Who judges you, or on what ground
do you reckon yourself worthy of greater praise or honor, since human judgment
is fallible?” Answer. But then the Apostle's argument against vainglory has
neither head nor tail. For whether man or God is the judge of the gifts by
which some excel others, if man has the gifts from himself he can boast in
himself—which the Apostle rebukes there.

[Marginalia: Answer 3.]

Therefore 3. Suárez adds in the place cited, while admitting the received
interpretation, namely that the Apostle speaks of the cause of distinction and
difference between the holy and the unholy. *But that occurs in two ways. In
one way, as it is referred to God alone; in another way, as it is referred to
God as the principal author, but not the only one. In the former way—that is,
according to the order of intention or divine predestination—but not according
to execution. For the fact that God so distinguished between two men that he*

[Marginalia: In the same place, fol. 9.]

[1651 logical printed p. 443 | IA leaf 461 | PDF p. 461]

*chose this one and not that one, and prepared a congruous calling for this
one and not for that one, was due to the divine will alone. But the fact that
this man actually attains glory and is sanctified while the other does not is
not due to the divine will alone, but also to the human will and cooperation,
as the Council of Trent said. Thus far he.* Answer 1. The Apostle's meaning,
therefore, will be: “Who distinguished you in eternal election?” What could be
more forced? Does the Apostle have a syllable about predestination? Did it
enter the minds of the conceited Corinthians that they had predestined
themselves from eternity? Jesuit, were you not ashamed to write such fictional
interpretations? 2. The Apostle is treating of gifts and charisms formally
received by man from God; he does not touch the question of the cause why God
predestines and efficaciously calls. Why say more? The question concerns acts
and gifts in man; the Jesuit dreams that it asks about God's acts. 3. If free
choice together with God distinguished this man from that one with respect to
the execution of the decree, why does the Apostle say, “Why do you boast?”
Indeed, it is permissible to boast in the free choice that formally
distinguishes Peter from Judas.

4. The Jesuit adds: if those words, [*Who discerns you?*], were spoken because
God through physical predetermination moves this man to a supernatural act and
not another, by the same reasoning it would have to be said in every morally
good act that God alone distinguishes the one who acts from the one who does
not act; and the same would also have to be said in evil acts. Answer 1. There
is nothing absurd in this; indeed, it is

[1651 logical printed p. 444 | IA leaf 462 | PDF p. 462]

necessary that this man wills because God determines him to will, while this
man does not will—even in acts of nature underlying sin—because God does not
determine him. For God bends the king's heart wherever he wills; a man's steps,
the tongue's answer, the killing of a traveler by an axe that apparently fell
by chance, and the outcome of lots are from God absolutely determining these
things. 2. But in supernatural acts he distinguishes the one who acts from the
one who does not act in a far different way, because for these acts God
contributes both a supernatural habit and a special influx of determining
grace. In acts of nature or evil acts, however, he bestows only the common
predetermination upon the powers implanted at creation. Thus the separation is
in no way equal in the two cases. For by the special grace and good pleasure by
which we are predestined to glory, God separates us from others in supernatural
acts; but in acts of nature the separation occurs by common providence. Thus
the Jesuit falsely supposes that we posit the same physical predetermination in
supernatural and natural acts, which we do not teach. 3. This absurdity will
truly burden the Jesuit, who posits a congruous and a non-congruous motion in
all free acts, especially since he posits God's physical influx as equal in
all—that is, collateral, indifferent, and general.

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 33, fol. 46; 25, p. 501.]

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 219.]

[Marginalia: Marcantonio, *On the Ecclesiastical Republic*, bk. 7, ch. 11, no.
121.]

Answer 4. Corvinus, Grevinchovius, and Marcantonio de Dominis, Archbishop of
Spalato, say that faith or conversion is not treated here (these are

[1651 logical printed p. 445 | IA leaf 463 | PDF p. 463]

Corvinus's words), but such gifts as those on account of which teachers boasted
among themselves, namely languages, eloquence, and wisdom, which depended
simply upon God's pure gift; and that freedom of the will contributes nothing
to whether anyone lacks those gifts or has them more sparingly, whereas in the
distinction of a believer from an unbeliever this freedom contributes
something. Answer 1. Augustine, Chrysostom, Cyprian, the Council of Orange,
canon 6.3, and the Council of Milevis held in Africa in the time of Honorius
and Arcadius expounded it in this way. 2. We do not stand upon human authority.
It is indeed true that, by negation, the conversion of man is not treated there,
nor do we press the text in that sense. But from the universal major, [*Who
discerns you? Why do you boast?*], the Apostle infers the conclusion that one
must not boast in gifts divinely bestowed. And he intends it to be universally
true that no one distinguishes himself, not even in gifts of grace bestowed.
From this we infer that we must boast much less in ourselves concerning the
gift by far the most excellent of all and proper to the elect—namely, saving
conversion to God—in which far greater praise and much more eminent glory is
owed to God than in the bestowal of gifts which God is sometimes pleased to
grant even to the reprobate. 3. It is utterly false that human freedom
contributes nothing to whether one lacks these gifts or has them more
sparingly. For Apollos's freedom in reading the Scriptures contributed to the
fact that he excelled in eloquence and knowledge of the Scriptures.

[1651 logical printed p. 446 | IA leaf 464 | PDF p. 464]

Otherwise, Paul would exhort Timothy to read the Scriptures in vain. *1
Timothy 4:13 and verse 14.* He does not want him to neglect the gift conferred
upon him through prophecy. 4. Nothing is more false than that the Apostle
speaks only of those gifts or of no others. Indeed, he speaks of all apostolic
gifts by which some excelled others and were the object of vain admiration to
the Corinthians, so that some regarded Paul, others Cephas, and others Apollos
as their teacher of the faith, as is clear from chapter 1, verses 13–14;
chapter 2, verses 1–3; chapter 3, verses 20–23; and chapter 4, verses 1–4. And
the Apostle wholly removes all ground for boasting in the free exercise of
apostolic gifts when he requires that they be regarded as God's stewards and
requires faithfulness, in which everyone sees that freedom is used. He also
prefers the Apostles to false apostles by their endurance of many afflictions,
namely disgrace, hunger, thirst, and nakedness, verses 10–12. In these
sufferings the freedom furnished with divine grace that separated them from
false teachers shone forth. 5. It is also false according to the Arminian
school that human freedom contributes nothing to whether anyone has those
gifts more sparingly. For who denies that, if the Apostles grew sluggish, God
would diminish or entirely remove their wisdom, eloquence, and gift of
prophecy, just as he takes away the talent from the lazy and idle servant; and
that God increases those gifts in those who labor diligently, since Paul by
God's grace labored more diligently than all the others, *1 Corinthians
15:10*?

[1651 logical printed p. 447 | IA leaf 465 | PDF p. 465]

5. The answer of Marcantonio de Dominis, Archbishop of Spalato, is far more
honest than the others: *in the case of ordinary men* (he says) *there is
nothing absurd in the saved owing no more to God than the damned.* Answer.
The others are forced to admit this. But whether the man who wrote this
deserves to be called a horse or a theologian is not easy for me to determine.
And then those who have already been condemned to the torments of hell can
give thanks to the Lamb who redeemed them out of all nations no less than
those who stand before God's throne. Behold the theology of the damned and a
new song!

[Marginalia: Archbishop of Spalato, *On the Ecclesiastical Republic*, bk. 7,
ch. 11, no. 122.]

Answer 6 is Corvinus's: *I confess* (he says) *that the exact account of the
causes of this different outcome* (conversion and non-conversion) *is unknown
to us; I likewise acknowledge that the same diversity is not to be sought in
free choice alone.* Answer 1. Acknowledge, then, the Arminian error: the exact
account of election and reprobation is not this man's faith and that man's
unbelief. Yet to your Arminius this predestination is the foundation of
Christianity—alas, a fragile foundation, even in Corvinus's judgment!—the
subject matter of the gospel, indeed the gospel itself. Yet this gospel of the
Arminians is wavering and doubtful to Corvinus, in my judgment easily the most
learned of them. Yet before now the Arminians always deemed it most judicious
to derive the account on God's side from God's justice, which converts the one
who is willing and does not convert the one who places an obstacle, and on our
side from human choice. But now

[Marginalia: *Against Molina*, ch. 45, fol. 133.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Declaration of His Opinion*, p. 119, Bor. ed.]

[1651 logical printed p. 448 | IA leaf 466 | PDF p. 466]

this account is not exact. How wavering and suspended is the Arminians' faith!
2. The reason why, of two furnished with equal prevenient grace, this man is
converted and the other is not does not lie in free choice alone; therefore it
lies either in free choice and God together or in God alone. If in God, then,
because Judas's active non-conversion is sin, the account and cause of sin will
have to be sought in some way in God. The Arminians will not bear this. And if
the reason is in any way elsewhere than in free choice alone, is it in fortune?
In fate? In a constellation? Good God, all these are absurd. If in God, then it
is either in justice—but this cannot be said, for then the cause on man's side
would lie in free choice alone, which Corvinus denies—or it is not in God's
mercy or goodness. For what goodness, what mercy—or goodness, or mercy—is it
that Judas is not converted? If it lies in God's freedom, why do you wish the
cause of this different outcome to be unknown? But confess, I ask: is this
cause not sufficiently known? “I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy.”
He is convicted and does not dare to confess himself convicted; he agrees with
us but is ashamed.

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 211; p. 229.]

Answer 7. Grevinchovius says two things here. 1. There is nothing here in which
free choice can boast. For there are two things in free choice: 1. that it
effectively produces its own act; 2. that it determines itself to acts; and
each is from God. 3. He says that calling is the fountain of conversion and of
our faith, and that this is from God alone,

[1651 logical printed p. 449 | IA leaf 467 | PDF p. 467]

but that the actual application of the remission of sins to Peter rather than
to Judas is the consummation of conversion. Answer. He wishes the fact that
the will effectively produces an act and determines itself to the act to be
from God. But how? From God as Creator, my good man. Yet Pelagius said this,
confounding nature and grace. In this way willing will be from God just as
running is in a horse and motion toward the center in a stone. Is this your
grace? If you mean that the will's determination of itself to the work is from
God, your conscience declares this false. What is it to determine? Is it not
to remove indifference to unwilling and willing? Yet you wish God always to
leave the will to its indifference; otherwise he would overthrow freedom if
he removed indifference. But do you mean that God brings it about that the
will itself removes indifference? Yet I say that for the will to remove
indifference to unwilling or willing is for the will to will and not to be
unwilling, or to be unwilling and not to will. But if God does not effect
willing except in this way, because he brings it about that the will can be
unwilling or willing, God would thus be said to remove indifference because he
does not remove it but leaves the will to indifference. 2. Conversion, as it
is a supernatural act in us—in which sense we are now disputing about it—is,
according to the Arminian, an effect of divine calling; but the cause of the
comparative conversion of Peter rather than Judas is Peter's choice alone,
not God's calling, prevenient grace, or cooperating grace. 3. The same thing
was taught by

[1651 logical printed p. 450 | IA leaf 468 | PDF p. 468]

Pelagius: the beginning of conversion is from God's grace alone, because the
law and divine sayings are from God alone. Augustine, *On the Proceedings of
Pelagius*, book 1, chapter 11. It is objected that in his third chapter
Caelestius wrote that God's grace and aid are not given for particular acts,
but are in free choice or in the law and teaching. Grevinchovius places grace
in the calling through law and gospel. Augustine, *On the Grace of Christ
against Pelagius and Caelestius*, chapter 7: *For God helps us* (Pelagius says)
*through his teaching and revelation, when he opens the eyes of our heart,
when he shows us things to come so that we are not occupied with present
things, when he lays bare the devil's snares, and when he enlightens us by the
manifold and ineffable gift of heavenly grace.* And in the same work, chapter
41: *But he establishes that the very aid by which he says the natural ability
is assisted consists in the law and teaching, which he confesses is revealed
to us by the Holy Spirit, on account of which he concedes that he is to be
adored.*
