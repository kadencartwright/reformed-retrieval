CHAPTER XXXVIII.

The General Stains Are Washed Away.

Certain general stains must now be washed away, though very briefly, because
there is a separate place to speak about most of them.

The first is that the English tribunals, both civil and ecclesiastical, have
condemned the ministers. To this nothing else need be answered except that
saying of Gelasius: “An unjust sentence ought to burden no one.” Nor is there
reason for anyone to be disturbed by the common infamy spread from here among
the people. Rather, one must call to mind what Gregory says about Paul: “The
Teacher of the Gentiles—

[1638 control | printed p. 126 | BSB canvas 136 | bsb10507786_00136]

—testifies that in preaching he runs through ill repute and good repute, and he
also says, ‘as deceivers, and yet true.’”

The second concerns the slightness of the cause, an objection which is not
new. For Emperor Maurice once objected to Gregory “that he was stirring up a
contention in the Church about a frivolous matter, since he disputed only over
the name Universal Bishop.” But what Gregory could answer while attacking the
Episcopate of Constantinople, we can now answer while contending against the
equally unlawful English Episcopate: “Some frivolous things are harmless, and
some frivolous things are harmful.” Yet who would call frivolous what belongs
to God and what disturbs the whole Church? Therefore, again with Gregory:
“The cause is not mine but God’s; the whole Church is disturbed by the
invention of a certain pompous expression.” Again: “You ought not to say that
the same cause is nothing, because if we bear this with equanimity, we corrupt
the faith of the universal Church.” And so also do we corrupt its unity,
concerning which Cyprian says: “We ought firmly to hold and vindicate this
unity, especially we Bishops who preside in the Church, so that we may also
prove that the Episcopate itself is one and undivided.”

Our Jewel brings these words forward against the Pope’s primacy, but they also
hold against the English Episcopal primacy. The fact that Cyprian here uses the
name Bishop generally for anyone who presides over a Church—as Jewel himself
interprets it—is proclaimed by the matter itself and will be demonstrated in
its own place. The other passage of Cyprian, cited together with it by Jewel,
demonstrates the same thing: “The Church is bound together by the glue of
priests who mutually cohere with one another.” What follows, then? The unity of
the English Church is preserved not by the presidency of one Bishop but rather
by the concord of all the pastors, as though by a kind of glue. Since that
concord flourishes most among equals, the English ministers must now cultivate
it in such a way that together they defend the one and undivided ministry of
all—as Cyprian advises—against the English Bishops’ usurpation.

The third stain remains. In it, some think the ministers are considerably
prejudiced by the fact that their present contention is directed not against
outsiders but against their own brothers. Yet I say that the promise in
Revelation 14:13 belongs to them: “Blessed are those who in the last times
suffer against the Beast, just as much as those who suffer against pagan
Emperors in the early Church.” For the goodness of a cause is the measure by
which God is accustomed to measure the patient endurance of his servants. If
the inequality of the enemies produced an inequality, the martyrs killed by a
Pope professing the Christian name would not be so blessed as those killed by
pagan Emperors who were strangers to that name. What is more, the English
ministers now suffer from their brothers for nothing other than what is Papal.

I will add still more. In proportion as the contest which God’s servants are
compelled to sustain against brothers is greater, so it is always accustomed
to contain a richer harvest of glory. This is what that declaration of the
Holy Spirit seems to suggest: “Here is the endurance of the saints,” Revelation
14:12—as if he had said: “Here is a test of Christian fortitude all the
more evident because the contest must now be undertaken not against pagans but
against professing Christians.” Let us look—

[1638 control | printed p. 127 | BSB canvas 137 | bsb10507786_00137]

—to Christ, the author and perfecter of our salvation, who was moved not so
much on account of open enemies as domestic ones, such as Judas was—just as
David once was on account of Ahithophel—Psalm 55:12–13. Those who are now
conformed to him can truly say to them with Cyprian: “It is no dishonor for us
to suffer from brothers what Christ suffered, nor is it glory for you to do
what Judas did.”

Here belongs what Christ writes to the Church of Smyrna in Revelation 2:9.
Ephesus and the primitive Church, its antitype, endured the gravest calamities,
but from pagan men, which was some alleviation of the pain. Smyrna, however,
and the Church which was its antitype, extending from Constantine to Gratian,
endured all the worst things from its own people—from its own, I say, who
professed themselves to be Jews and genuine Christians. Hence the Holy Spirit,
when writing to these Churches, omits threats and consoles as much as possible.
He does so with a consolation which can deservedly strengthen those now
fainting in England: “I know your affliction,” though your enemies cry out that
it is just; “I know your poverty”; the swollen despise you, the proud contemn
you, “but in my sight you are rich. Do not fear what you are about to suffer,”
for all these things too, however much inflicted by Jews, however much by those
who wish to be regarded as orthodox, will turn into a reward for you.

Since this is so, let those who now suffer in England not grow weary in spirit,
but let them proceed further. At every step let them recall manly courage, as
that Spartan mother did, and Christian courage, which is far better than
Spartan. Let them further increase in strength according to those words of
John: “Let him who is righteous be made righteous still,” Revelation 22:11.
Finally, let them preserve their garments undefiled by the English
subscription. For in this way they will walk with their Christ in white, just
as those in the Lutheran Church, the antitype of Sardis, preserved themselves
undefiled from subscription to the Book of Concord, Revelation 3. And this is
the third corollary which arises from both the holiness and the necessity of
reformed discipline: the ministers in England who suffer for it are to be
praised, not condemned, to be crowned rather than afflicted with punishments.
