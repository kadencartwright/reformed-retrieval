### Chapter II. On the Certainty of Righteousness

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *Certainty is a certain firmness and immutability of truth. It is
twofold: one kind belongs to the object and is the immutability of the thing;
the other belongs to the subject and is the firmness of our assent to the
thing. The former certainty arises either from the necessity of the thing
itself or from a determination made, if the thing is not necessary. The latter
certainty arises partly from the means by which some thing is commended to us,
and partly from the soundness of the understanding and the affection of the
will.*

Protestant reply. There is no reason for us to contend greatly about these
things. Yet it is useful to observe: I. Certainty properly and essentially is a
mode or affection of cognition; this is that certainty which Bellarmine calls
*the subject's.* Improperly and by participation, however, it is attributed to
everything which is moved infallibly by a cognitive power, as Thomas has it in
2.2, question 18, article 4. The certainty of the subject is not only in the
assent of the intellect but also, by participation, in the affections of the
will—in joy, hope, and so forth. III. Things absolutely necessary have in
themselves a certain immutable firmness which exceeds the notion of certainty.
IV. Not all certainty of the subject arises from persuasion, soundness of the
understanding, or affection of the will. Those things do not apply to divine
certainty, nor to that certainty which is found in blessed men from vision, or
sometimes in wayfarers from inspiration—indeed, not even to the ordinary
certainty of the intellect concerning common principles.

2. Bellarmine. *One kind is evident certainty, namely of those things which are
seen in some way. Its first degree is held by the first principles of things;
the second, by conclusions deduced from them; and the third, by those things
which are perceived by the bodily senses, which is called the certainty of
experience. Another kind is obscure certainty, namely of those things which
are held by faith alone or by opinion. Its first degree is held by divine faith,
under which falsehood can by no means lie; the second, by the certainty of human
or moral faith; and the third, by the certainty of conjecture and opinion,
which renders a man secure and removes anxiety, yet does not cast out all
fear-*

[Printed p. 342 | Google Books PA342 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*fulness.*

Protestant reply. In the former kind of certainty, experience is wrongly
restricted to the bodily senses. For the soul also has its own experience and,
as it were, sense: he who senses that he knows has knowledge. The faithful
taste how good the Lord is. In the second: I. The certainty of faith is called
light in the Scriptures. Therefore, although it does not have the evidence of
the thing or of reason, nevertheless, because it has the evidence of
revelation, it ought not simply to be called obscure. II. Just as Bellarmine
made two degrees within the certainty of evidence—one of principles and the
other of conclusions deduced from them—so also in the certainty of faith he
ought to have observed a similar distinction between things immediately
revealed and conclusions deduced from them. Properly speaking, no conjectural
certainty is given; only by comparison are very probable conjectures called
certain. III. That which does not cast out fear cannot with foundation or
reason render a man justly secure.

3. Bellarmine. *If the state of the question is established without fallacy or
ambiguity, it will be this: Whether, without special revelation, anyone ought
or is able to be certain by the certainty of divine faith—to which falsehood
can by no means be subject—that his sins have been remitted.*

Protestant reply. The *certainty of divine faith* is not placed in this
statement without all fallacy and ambiguity, because Bellarmine does not
distinguish between the degrees of this certainty with respect to the
principles which are immediately revealed and the conclusions evidently
deduced from them, nor between the certainty of faith in itself and that which
is from faith, whether in apprehension or in hope. But with these things
understood, we admit this state of the question and will defend the affirmative
part against the negative part of the Pontificians.

#### Bellarmine's arguments

4. Bellarmine. Proverbs 20:8. *Who can say, “My heart is clean; I am pure from
sin”?*

Protestant reply. This proverb is rightly explained by 1 John 1:8: *If we say
that we do not have sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.* It
is explained thus by Solomon himself in 1 Kings 8:46: *There is no one who does
not sin.*

5. Bellarmine. Ecclesiastes 9:1. *There are righteous and wise men, and their
works are in the hand of God, and yet a man does not know whether he is worthy
of hatred or love, but all things are kept uncertain for the future.*

Protestant reply. I. That last clause, *all things are kept uncertain for the
future,* does not appear in the Hebrew text. II. Whatever is contained in these
words concerning knowledge of love and hatred is referred

[Printed p. 343 | Google Books PA343 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

to external things. For it is manifest—and the Pontificians will not deny it—
that many can know themselves to be worthy of hatred. Thus Bellarmine shortly
afterward says: *sinners can easily know themselves to be worthy of hatred, not
of love.*

Bellarmine. 1. *Solomon speaks only of the righteous.*

Protestant reply. I. In Bellarmine's judgment, therefore, he speaks only of men
unknown both to themselves and to the whole world. For whose benefit, then,
does he preach about such men? II. It is evident from the words immediately
following that Solomon also speaks of unrighteous men: *all things happen alike
to the righteous and the wicked,* and so forth; these contain the reason for
the preceding assertion.

Bellarmine. 2. *Solomon's reasoning is as follows: If the righteous could know
with certainty whether they are loved by God or not, they would know it chiefly
from God's benefits. But God's benefits are common to the righteous and the
wicked, and all things happen alike to the righteous and the wicked. Therefore
the righteous cannot know with certainty whether they are loved by God or not.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine is inept here twice over: both because he
invents a frivolous argument whose foundation—*that God's love is known chiefly
from external benefits*—is manifestly false, and because he assigns so foolish
an argument to the wisest Solomon. II. If all those things from which God's
love is chiefly known happen alike to the righteous and the wicked, then the
righteous man can have not even any probable conjecture, much less any certain
conjecture which renders a man secure and removes anxiety, concerning God's
love; yet the Pontificians do not dare to deny this.

Bellarmine. 3. *How, then, is what follows in the next words true: all things
are kept uncertain for the future?*

Protestant reply. This is not true, nor does it follow in Solomon except in the
corrupt translation. If this were true, what Bellarmine afterward affirms would
be false: *sinners can easily know themselves to be worthy of hatred, not of
love.* If sinners can easily know this, then I. not all things are uncertain
among them; II. if sinners can easily know that they are worthy of hatred, then
those who cannot easily know themselves to be hated by God can certainly
conclude from that very fact that they are not sinners, and consequently that
their sins have been remitted.

6. Bellarmine. Job 9:12, 15, 21, and chapter 31:14. *Job displays doubt
concerning his righteousness.*

Protestant reply. Job could not doubt that the perfection of inward
righteousness was not his, since in chapter 7, verses 20–21, he confesses his
sins and asks for remission. If he doubted concerning some of his works,
whether they were fruits worthy of repentance, this is no wonder and does
nothing for the question. Even if his mind had hesitated concerning God's
favor in the hour of a most grievous tempta-*

[Printed p. 344 | Google Books PA344 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tion, it does not follow from this that he could not and ought not to have
been certain of the remission of his sins.

7. Bellarmine. Psalm 19:12. *Who understands his faults? Cleanse me, O Lord,
from hidden faults.*

Protestant reply. The argument from these words must be as follows: *He who
does not understand all his faults cannot and ought not to be certain that his
sins have been remitted.* But if this consequence holds, it follows that not
even then was David certain of the remission of his sins, when Nathan told him,
*Jehovah has put away your sin,* 2 Samuel 12:14. For it is not credible that at
that moment he understood each and every one of his faults.

8. Bellarmine. 1 Corinthians 4:4. *The Apostle says that he knows nothing
against himself, and yet that he is not justified in this.*

Protestant reply. I. These are not the words of a man who doubts, but of one who
simply denies: *but I am not justified in this.* Bellarmine could therefore
with greater plausibility prove from this that Paul was not justified than
that he doubted about it or was uncertain. II. In establishing the state of the
question, Bellarmine exempted from the number of uncertain righteous men those
who *have a special revelation* concerning the remission of their sins. Surely,
if there ever were any such men, this ought not to be denied of Paul, the chief
Apostle, who was caught up to the third heaven and had a *surpassing abundance
of revelations.* In chapter 8 Bellarmine affirms that this was revealed to I
know not what petty saints—Anthony, Galla, and Francis. It was therefore only
fair that he honor Saint Paul in the same way, 2 Corinthians 12:3. The
Apostle's meaning is manifest: namely, that he was very well conscious of his
faithfulness in dispensing the gospel, but that he did not thereby arrogate to
himself a pronouncement that he was immune from every defect in the Lord's
judgment, from which he awaited the praise of sincerity and faithfulness.

9. Bellarmine. *Without living faith and serious repentance no one is
justified. But no one can be certain, especially by the certainty of faith,
that he has living faith and serious repentance; for no testimony of this kind
concerning our faith and repentance in particular is found in any Scripture.
This is plainly a theological demonstration.*

Protestant reply. If this theological demonstration were sound, it would
destroy every use of theology. For in no Scripture is there a testimony
concerning Muhammad, or concerning his Quran in particular, that they ought to
be condemned. Can we therefore not be certain by the certainty of faith that
those abominations are to be detested? In no Scripture is it found that this or
that heathen or Muslim cannot be saved without conversion and faith; can there
therefore be no certain faith that faith is necessary for such a particular
man

[Printed p. 345 | Google Books PA345 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

in order that he be saved? In no Scripture—indeed, not even in any council or
papal sentence—is it found that the particular codex Bellarmine used when
citing Scripture contained the word of God. Was Bellarmine therefore uncertain
whether the things he alleged were the word of God or not? Bellarmine ought not
to have been ignorant that particulars of this sort are evidently known to us
by sense, observation, and experience, and that in faith the sealing of the
Holy Spirit is also added; but a conclusion rightly deduced from premises of
which one is a matter of faith and the other has certain evidence is certain by
the certainty of faith.

10. Bellarmine. *Scripture—Daniel 4:27, Joel 2:12, Jonah 3:9, Acts 8:22—
indicates that it is uncertain whether one who repents will obtain remission:
“Who knows?” “Perhaps,” “if perhaps.”*

Protestant reply. No uncertainty of remission before God and in the
consciences of those who truly repent is indicated, but the grievousness of the
sins to be remitted together with the possibility of remission, although it
might be difficult. In the Prophets a certain uncertainty may also be indicated
concerning the averting of some public calamity. If these phrases indicated
uncertainty that remission would be obtained by those who truly repent, they
would render all God's promises doubtful.

Bellarmine. *They have regard to the uncertainty of our disposition.*

Protestant reply. In men such as Simon Magus was, this can be conceded. But
those who have living faith and true repentance have another disposition. It
is evident that some singular consideration inhered in the person of that
magician, by which it came about that the promise of grace was proposed to him
in a manner in which it is proposed to no one else in the whole New Testament;
and Bellarmine argues most absurdly from what was said to him singularly to all
the faithful. Yet Dionysius the Carthusian delivers two other interpretations:
*Peter says “if perhaps” to intimate how very grievous the sin was; or (as some
say) because he saw in the Spirit that Simon would not repent.*

11. Bellarmine. *Scripture often warns us not to be excessively secure, as
though we were certain of righteousness already obtained.* Proverbs 28:14; 2
Corinthians 10:18; Philippians 2:12; 1 Peter 1:17; 2 Peter 1:10.

Protestant reply. Scripture indeed warns us not to be excessively secure, but
it does not place excessive security in our certainly apprehending God's grace
and the remission of sins in Christ.

Bellarmine. 1. *If anyone knew by the certainty of faith that he was righteous,
he could surely commend himself, contrary to the Apostle,* 2 Corinthians 10:18.

Protestant reply. He who knows himself to be righteous in Christ, not in
himself, by mere grace, can commend nothing under that name ex-*

[Printed p. 346 | Google Books PA346 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*cept God's grace in Christ. For commendation is due to the cause, not the
object.

Bellarmine. 2. *If certainty of calling is to be sought through good works,
then surely certainty of justification is also. But the former is true,* 2
Peter 1:10. *Therefore.*

Protestant reply. We concede the whole.

Bellarmine. 3. *If certainty of justification depends upon good works, then a
man is not certain of justification as soon as he is justified.*

Protestant reply. We neither say that all are certain immediately, nor that
those who are certain are so certain that they cannot become more certain; nor,
finally, do we say that the certainty of the faithful depends simply and
entirely upon good works.

Bellarmine. 4. *Therefore certainty of justification does not depend upon faith
alone.*

Protestant reply. It does not arise from faith alone as a sign and confirming
argument, but it does arise from faith alone as an apprehending cause.

Bellarmine. 5. *Our justification is therefore not certain by the certainty of
faith, which is infallible, but by the certainty of works, which is conjectural
and imperfect certainty.*

Protestant reply. With those premises overthrown, this conclusion lies fallen.
Faith itself is strengthened by works, yet it remains faith, not conjecture. In
the same way the certainty of faith does not cease to belong to faith and to be
infallible because it is confirmed by other means. The certainty of knowledge
is in some way perfected by experience, yet it does not thereby cease to be
the certainty of knowledge.

12. Bellarmine. *Nothing can be certain by the certainty of faith unless it is
either immediately contained in God's word or is deduced from God's word by an
evident consequence. But this particular proposition, “This or that man is
truly justified,” is not immediately contained in God's word, nor can it be
gathered from God's word by an evident consequence. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. It can be gathered from God's word by an evident consequence.

Bellarmine. 1. *The reasoning by which anyone might conclude from God's word
the certainty of faith concerning his own righteousness would have to be this:
God's word testifies that all who are truly converted to God and seriously
repent obtain the pardon of sins and grace. But my true conversion and
repentance are evident to me. It is therefore certain to me by the certainty of
faith that I have received pardon and grace. But the assumption of this
argument is not only false but also impossible unless there is a revelation.*

Protestant reply. This reasoning is not proposed in sufficiently good faith,
because it makes no mention that God's word testifies that *everyone who
believes* receives grace or remission of sins, although Scripture everywhere
ascribes this to faith, and even

[Printed p. 347 | Google Books PA347 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

knows how to give faith the chief place. Yet insofar as faith is comprehended
in conversion, we admit this very form of reasoning. We affirm not only that
the assumption is possible and true without special revelation, but also that
it lies in the duty of all the faithful to render it certain and firm for
themselves.

Bellarmine. 2. *The heart of man is perverse and inscrutable; who will know
it?* Jeremiah 17:9.

Protestant reply. Other men indeed have no access to know another's heart; but
*the spirit of man which is in man knows the things of man,* 1 Corinthians 2:11.
No Pontifician, unless insane, will deny that a man knows something of his own
heart and could know more if he applied due attention. Bellarmine said above
that the sinner can easily know himself to be a sinner. The inscrutability of
the heart therefore does nothing to prevent the believer from knowing his own
faith.

Bellarmine. 3. *We see in actual fact that many are deceived who think they
have what they do not have.*

Protestant reply. I. If this reasoning had any force, it would destroy all
certainty of knowledge and sense and would summon from the underworld the
Pyrrhonian sect of Skeptics who know nothing. For we see in actual fact that
many are deceived who think they know what they do not know and see what they
do not see.

Bellarmine. 4. *Who is certain that he does not labor under some gross
ignorance?*

Protestant reply. Bañez answers in 2.2, question 6, article 2, at the end:
*Culpable error does not destroy faith if it does not possess an obstinacy
which the believer is certain that he does not have.*

Bellarmine. 5. *There are no clearer signs of true conversion and grace already
obtained than works of love, and especially if anyone lays down his life for
God; but this very thing can be a fallacious sign.*

Protestant reply. I. Medina in 1.2, question 112, article 5, and Bañez in 2.2,
question 6, article 2, expressly deny what Bellarmine here assumes as conceded:
*that the chief argument of faith is to seek death for it.* Rather it is *to
believe all the things which the Roman Church proposes, so that one is prepared
to deny with the intellect all experience of the senses and all other knowledge
rather than the Church's dogmas.* And if they had put the word of God (as
Christians ought) in place of the Roman Church, their opinion would be truer
than what Bellarmine assumes. II. Other signs more closely joined to grace are
given. “Certainly,” says *Alexander of Hales, part 3, question 61, member 7,
question 3,* “a man can know by experience, or experimental knowledge, that he
has grace. These experiences are light, peace, and joy. For when these three
things are in a holy soul, it is a truly certain experience that the soul has
grace. Likewise, a well-disposed taste

[Printed p. 348 | Google Books PA348 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

cannot fail to know the sweetness joined to it; therefore a well-disposed soul
cannot fail to sense the divine sweetness joined to it. But the sense of divine
sweetness is through grace; therefore a well-disposed soul cannot be ignorant
that it has grace.” To these we add, as the principal cause of certainty, the
testimony of the Holy Spirit, Romans 8:16. III. These arguments are not
fallacious where they are truly present. Thus Alexander of Hales in the same
place says: *In this the holy soul differs from the heretical soul: the holy
soul truly has these things when it has grace, but the heretical soul does not.
The joy of the faithful soul differs from the joy of the unbelieving soul as
much as the joy of imagination or of one dreaming differs from the joy of one
awake.*

13. Bellarmine. *No one is bound to believe certainly by the certainty of faith
that his sins have been remitted. For an article of that sort is contained in
no creed of the faith.*

Protestant reply. I. Every believer is bound to believe by the certainty of
faith what God has promised to believers. But God has promised believers that
their sins will be remitted, Acts 10:43. Therefore every believer is bound to
believe by the certainty of faith that his sins have been remitted. II. We are
bound to believe many things which are expressed in no creed other than in
Scripture. III. It is contained in the Apostles' Creed: *I believe the remission
of sins.*

Bellarmine. 1. *It signifies that baptism unto the remission of sins is given
in the Church.*

Protestant reply. It indeed signifies this, and by consequence that all true
believers baptized by grace have the remission of their sins. Furthermore, the
man who truly professes that faith is a believer. From these things it
necessarily follows that the man who truly professes that faith is bound to
believe that his sins have been remitted.

Bellarmine. 2. *If we had an article of faith of this sort, it would follow that
every man who sins mortally immediately becomes a heretic. For one who sins
voluntarily cannot believe himself to be righteous unless he wishes
deliberately to believe what is false.*

Protestant reply. I. Medina in 1.2, question 112, article 5, does not fear to
concede in some manner what seems so absurd to Bellarmine: *The believer is
bound to believe that he has divine faith; if he doubts concerning this, he is
a heretic. This,* he says, *is our opinion.* We, however, deny the consequence,
and that for various reasons. For I. not everyone who fails to believe what
ought to be believed is a heretic. II. Not every sin mortal in its nature makes
the sinner unable to believe that his sins have been remitted. Bellarmine saw
this, and therefore he chan-*

[Printed p. 349 | Google Books PA349 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ged the term and denies that such a man can believe *himself to be righteous,*
namely with inherent righteousness. III. If sin actually bringing death is
understood, then we say that such a sinner is not bound at that time to believe
that his sins have been remitted. He does not err in faith by not believing it;
rather, he would err if he did believe it.

14. Bellarmine. *It is not expedient that men should ordinarily have certainty
concerning their own grace, for it might beget pride.*

Protestant reply. What is said concerning virtue is still better said
concerning this certainty: if it is genuine, its nature is such that no one
uses it badly, although very many abuse a false appearance of it.

15. Bellarmine. *It was revealed to Saint Anthony, Saint Galla, and Saint
Francis that their sins had been remitted. But to what purpose was this, if
this knowledge were common to all believers?*

Protestant reply. These fables are rejected with the same ease with which they
are related. Yet nothing prevents what all believers ought and are able to know
from being revealed extraordinarily to a few; so it was revealed to Paul that
he persecuted Christ, although all who persecuted the Church ought and were
able to know this.

16. Bellarmine. *Certain most perfect men trembled no little on account of this
uncertainty.*

Protestant reply. As though we called into doubt that the best Christians
sometimes suffer grievous temptations and dreadful desertions!

17. Bellarmine. *Although almost all the heretics of this age boast of this
certainty, nevertheless very many of them are deceived on their opinion, while
on ours they all are.*

Protestant reply. By this splendid reasoning some atheist could prove that
there is no certainty at all of true religion.

#### Our arguments

18. Protestant argument. John 14:17. You know him because he remains with you
and will be in you; and verse 20: In that day you will know that I am in the
Father, and you in me, and I in you.

Bellarmine. 1. *From the former passage it is gathered only that those who know
the Holy Spirit by faith do not know him without his grace and aid.*

Protestant reply. Indeed, it is openly gathered that Christ's disciples know
the Holy Spirit through his grace, aid, and indwelling. Otherwise nothing would
be affirmed concerning believers any more than concerning the world, nor would
any reason for the promise made to Christ's disciples be contained in these
words. Yet they are so clear that they cannot be denied except by the greatest
impudence. “Knowledge,” Cajetan says, “is signi-*

[Printed p. 350 | Google Books PA350 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*fied from experience within themselves: for God also grants this, that we may
know the things which have been given us by God,” 1 Corinthians 2. “It is
intimated that it is proper to the elect to know the Holy Spirit within
themselves.”

Bellarmine. 2. *In the second testimony the Lord speaks of the blessed
knowledge which will be after this life.*

Protestant reply. Toledo answers well: *This is indeed a great cause of
consolation; yet now he was applying remedies near at hand to the disciples'
present sorrow concerning his departure, and was not referring their vision to
that last day, since he would also furnish it much earlier. For although all
time is little when compared with eternity, nevertheless, when compared with
their grief and sorrow, the delay was very great and must not be called small.*

Bellarmine. 3. *If anyone contends that what the Lord says is fulfilled even in
this life, the answer will be ready: to know that we are in the Son and the Son
in us is not to know that we are righteous, but that Christ is the head of the
whole Church; for thus Christ is in us, that is, in the Church.*

Protestant reply. Such answers are always ready for sophists. But what
consolation would it be to anyone to know that Christ is in the Church if at
the same time he knows nothing of his being a living member of that Church, so
that Christ is also in him? John interprets those words thus in his first
epistle, chapter 3, verse 24: *By this we know that he dwells in us, namely from
the Spirit whom he gave us.* Does the Spirit who dwells in the Church but not
in us give us this knowledge? Bellarmine held his readers in too much contempt
when he thought they could be deceived by so gross a scheme.

19. Protestant argument. Romans 8:15–16. You have received the Spirit of
adoption, by whom we cry, Abba, Father; he testifies with our spirit that we are
children of God.

Bellarmine. 1. *The Holy Spirit testifies to this through the experience of a
certain sweetness and inward peace, which does not beget certainty except
conjectural certainty.*

Protestant reply. I. Believers do not make a conjecture when they cry, *Abba,
Father. The name Father is repeated,* Cajetan says, *to signify the firmness
and certainty of confidence.* II. God's testimony deserves the most certain
faith, and it produces such faith where it is received, not conjectural
opinion. *From this testimony,* Cajetan adds, *we clearly see that we ought to
believe that we are children of God.*

Bellarmine. 2. *The testimony of the Spirit is nothing other than the very
prayer by which we say, Abba, Father. But that it is the Holy Spirit who moves
us when we say this is known to us not from divine faith but only from
conjectures, which can be fallacious.*

Protestant reply. I. The testimony of the Spirit is manifestly distinguished
from that prayer. For we must first be rendered more certain that we

[Printed p. 351 | Google Books PA351 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

are children of God before we can invoke God confidently as Father. II. We know
by divine faith that the Holy Spirit moves those who pray as they ought, Romans
8:26. III. The presence and testimony of the Spirit is a seal that seals and a
pledge that confirms, Ephesians 1:13–14; 2 Corinthians 1:22. It therefore does
not depend upon fallacious conjectures.

20. Protestant argument. Romans 8:36. I am certain that nothing can separate
us from the love of God in Jesus Christ.

Bellarmine. *He does not mean the certainty of divine faith, but a certain
persuasion and confidence for the producing of which conjectural certainty
suffices. This is evident from the Greek word* πέπεισμαι.

Protestant reply. The contrary is evident. I. From the preceding question,
which has the force of the firmest and most effectual assertion: *Who will
separate us?* II. From the enumeration of nearly all evils, which he made with
amplitude and force of speech to demonstrate the immovability of confidence.
III. From that boasting in verse 37: *in all these things* ὑπερνικῶμεν, *we are
more than conquerors.* IV. From the use of the Greek word where it is applied
to denote the spiritual confidence which believers have concerning those
things which pertain to their own salvation, Romans 4:14; 2 Timothy 1:14; and so
forth. V. Bellarmine himself, in book 5, chapter 7, in order to defend by great
names that some confidence can be placed in good merits *which are known to be
truly such,* brings forward the examples of Nehemiah, David, Hezekiah, Esther,
and the Apostle Paul himself. But if Paul together with the others knew that
their works were truly good and even meritorious, then they surely knew that
they had been justified by God's grace—something which Bellarmine was not
ashamed to deny in this place concerning Paul, John, and the others. But either
he changed his opinion together with the scene, or he thought that certainty
of grace ought to be conceded only insofar as it could contribute to placing
confidence in merits, not to the infallible application of God's promises.

21. Protestant argument. 1 Corinthians 2:12. We have received the Spirit who
is from God, that we may know the things given to us.

Bellarmine. *The Apostle is not speaking about knowledge of God's benefits
which pertain in particular to this or that person, but about knowledge of the
benefits which God has prepared for his elect. We have this knowledge through
the Spirit of God, who has revealed them in the Scriptures.*

Protestant reply. I. The Apostle does not speak of the Spirit in the
Scriptures, but of the Spirit in us—of the *anointing which we have received,*
1 John 2:27; of the Spirit through whom God's love is poured out in our hearts,
Romans 5:5. II. The benefits which God prepared for the elect pertain in
particular to this or that

[Printed p. 352 | Google Books PA352 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

elect person. Bellarmine therefore invents an opposition between things that
are not different. Thomas confesses that it can be understood that the Spirit
of God is given to the saints so that they may know spiritual gifts which those
who do not have the same Spirit do not know, according to Revelation 2: To him
who conquers I will give hidden manna, which no one knows except him who
receives it. Cajetan is clearer: *The Apostles had certain knowledge that faith,
hope, love, and other gifts freely given had been given to them by God.*

22. Protestant argument. 1 Corinthians 11:28. Let each man examine himself,
and so forth.

Bellarmine. *This examination does not require the certainty of faith, but such
certainty as we can obtain from the examination of our own conscience.*

Protestant reply. This examination requires that each person, by sifting his
own conscience according to the rule of faith, find himself approved before God
according to God's judgment, so that he may worthily eat the Lord's body. Such
certainty, however, is from faith unto faith.

23. Protestant argument. 2 Corinthians 1:12. This is our boasting, the
testimony of our conscience, that with simplicity and godly sincerity, and so
forth.

Bellarmine. 1. *The testimony of conscience does not furnish infallible
certainty.*

Protestant reply. Not every testimony indeed, but such testimony of such a
conscience as could furnish boasting to the Apostle.

Bellarmine. 2. *We also boast concerning those things which we trust that we
have or hope that we will have, although not without fear.*

Protestant reply. If doubt is understood by fear, this is false. For spiritual
boasting is a fruit of divine faith, Romans 5:2.

24. Protestant argument. 2 Corinthians 13:5. Examine yourselves, whether you
are in the faith.

Bellarmine. *The Apostle speaks of Christ's presence among the Corinthians,
not according to grace that renders acceptable, but according to the power,
providence, and miracles which he worked among them.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine passes over as though not seeing those first words
on which our argument rests and by which his interpretation is refuted:
*Examine yourselves, whether you are in the faith; test yourselves. Do you not
recognize your own selves? He warns them to examine themselves concerning
their faith,* as Thomas himself observes.

Bellarmine. 1. *The Apostle did not wish to affirm that all those men were
righteous at that time.*

Protestant reply. He wished all of them to examine themselves as to whether
they were righteous.

Bellarmine. 2. *In the preceding words the Apostle speaks about the power of
Christ.*

[Printed p. 353 | Google Books PA353 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. Christ's presence through grace and faith agrees very well
with that power, 1 Corinthians 10:5. The phrase *to be in the faith* can signify
nothing other than to be in a state of grace; nor are those said *to examine
and test themselves* who consider only external deeds. Thomas is therefore
compelled on this passage to concede that *we know with certainty that Christ
is in us,* but he most ineptly adds, *according to the intellect, not according
to the effect,* as though the Apostle were stirring Christians only to an
examination of the intellect, or as though Christ dwelt in the intellect of the
faithful and not in the heart. And Thomas's master, *Alexander of Hales, part 3,
question 61,* shortly before the end, being convicted by this testimony,
confesses that *grace can be known,* but most ridiculously distinguishes
between *the thing and the rationale of known grace.*

25. Protestant argument. Ephesians 3:12. Through whom we have confidence, or
boldness, παρρησίαν, and access in confidence through faith in him.

Bellarmine. *Certainty begets boldness, but that certainty is not concerning
our righteousness, but the truth of the mysteries of faith.*

Protestant reply. The Apostle expressly says that this certainty is *through
faith in Christ.* But the same Apostle teaches everywhere that this faith is
imputed for righteousness.

26. Protestant argument. In chapters 3, 4, and 5 of John's first epistle,
believers are said to know, to recognize, and to have testimony in themselves
concerning God's grace.

Bellarmine. 1. *Those words, “we know” and “we recognize,” do not necessarily
include the certainty of divine faith, for we are said to know those things
which we gather from signs and conjectures.*

Protestant reply. I. They include the certainty of faith in chapter 3, verse 5:
*You know that he was manifested to take away our sins;* verse 16: *We know the
love of God;* and chapter 4, verse 16, where John himself explains thus: *we
have known and believed.* II. The same John in chapter 5, verse 13, so joins
these two things—*that you may know you have eternal life, and that you may
believe in the name of the Son of God*—that he openly testifies that the
knowledge believers have concerning their own salvation includes the certainty
of faith.

Bellarmine. 1. *Those statements are general, not particular; for they signify
that those who love the brothers have been translated from death to life,* and
so forth.

Protestant reply. They indeed signify this as a proposition, under which they
also expressly assume: We love the brothers, and so forth. From this they infer
the conclusion: Therefore we have been translated from death to life. John
speaks of himself together with those like him, who have testimony within
themselves.

Bellarmine. 3. *John trusted with good hope that he was one of those who love
the brothers.*

[Printed p. 354 | Google Books PA354 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. This alone sufficiently shows how desperate this cause of
the Pontificians is: in order to defend it, they deny that the most holy
Apostles, after the abundance of the Spirit and of all grace had been poured
out upon them, could answer with certainty the question which Christ proposed
to Peter when he was still a novice: *Simon, son of Jonah, do you love me?* Yet
Peter even then answered (undoubtedly truly): *Certainly, Lord, you know that I
love you.* Cajetan was ashamed to pronounce so abjectly concerning the
Apostles or other first Christians. Paul, as Cajetan says on Romans 5, together
with the other Apostles and the justified who persevered in the beginning of
the Church, had received the Holy Spirit with so great an abundance of
supernatural gifts that they experienced within themselves the love of God
poured out in their own hearts.

Bellarmine. 4. John 5:10 *does not speak of the testimony of our justification,
but of the testimony of Christ's divinity.*

Protestant reply. John himself answers in verse 11: *But this is the testimony,
that God has given us eternal life,* and so forth.

27. Protestant argument. 2 Peter 1. Labor to make your calling and election
certain.

Bellarmine. 1. *The Lutherans wish their certainty to be prior to works, not
subsequent to them.*

Protestant reply. With Scripture we wish it to be both prior and subsequent.

Bellarmine. 2. *Certainty which arises from works cannot be the certainty of
faith.*

Protestant reply. This is true of that certainty which arises only from works,
but not of that which also arises from works, or is confirmed by them.

Bellarmine. 3. *The Apostle is speaking not so much about certainty on the part
of the subject as on the part of the object.*

Protestant reply. It is enough for us if he speaks of both. But it is evident
from the testimony which follows that the Apostle speaks of the certainty of
the subject: *If you do these things, you will never stumble,* and so forth.
Indeed, we cannot render our calling certain in itself unless we render it
certain to ourselves, because in calling God's goodwill concerning our
salvation is signified to us.

28. Protestant argument. The most holy men praised in Scripture—Abraham,
David, Paul, and others like them—were certain of God's grace through faith.

Bellarmine. 1. *Perhaps by a special revelation they merited to be rendered
certain of the grace which they found before the Lord.*

Protestant reply. I. By this sort of *perhaps* all arguments in any cause could
be evaded. II. If they were certain of grace from special revelation, then, in
the judgment of the Pontificians, that cer-*

[Printed p. 355 | Google Books PA355 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tainty would not have been the certainty of faith. Thus Bañez in 2.2,
question 1, article 1, doubt 3: *Things revealed to particular persons,
although supernatural, do not pertain to the assent of Catholic faith or of the
theological virtue, but only those things which are known to the Church and
proposed by her. But concerning Abraham and David it is evident that they were
certain of God's grace through faith.* III. Bellarmine alleges this revelation
without any certainty either of faith or of reason, and indeed contradicts
himself. For earlier, in chapter 9, he denied that either Paul or John, the most
holy Apostles, had any certainty concerning God's grace other than conjectural
certainty. But concerning David and the Prophets, Medina in 1.2, question 112,
article 5, rightly observes that, having obtained the remission of sins, they
frequently and repeatedly trust and hope, as is read everywhere in the divine
Scriptures, but that this must not always be referred to divine revelation.

Bellarmine. 2. *When Scripture commends the saints and praises their
righteousness and virtues, it renders us certain, for whom that was more
useful, rather than them.*

Protestant reply. I. We do not say that every Scripture which commends the
saints rendered those already-dead saints certain of God's grace. Bellarmine
therefore sophistically denies what he affirms, lest he seem to have answered
nothing at all. II. When Scripture renders us certain concerning the certainty
which the saints had, it does this so that we may walk in the footsteps of the
same faith, Romans 4:12.

20. Protestant argument. Certainty of grace and salvation rests upon the truth
of God's promises.

Bellarmine. 1. *The promises are general.*

Protestant reply. From a general proposition and an assumption rightly posited,
a special and personal conclusion is deduced.

Bellarmine. 2. *These promises can indeed be applied to individuals in
particular, but then they are conditional, not absolute.*

Protestant reply. Faith is the condition; therefore, when faith is posited, the
promise applied particularly ceases to be conditional and becomes absolute.

20. Protestant argument. Scripture attributes to faith πληροφορίαν, the
fullest certainty, Romans 4; Hebrews 10 and 11; James 1; just as it does to hope,
Hebrews 6.

Bellarmine. 1. *The remission of sins, or the justification of each individual,
does not pertain to faith properly so called.*

Protestant reply. The very sound of these words is so opposed to the apostolic
phrase that it is astonishing Bellarmine did not mitigate their dreadful
harshness in some way. But because this matter was debated above, for the
present it is enough to observe that the Pontificians refer the following
conclusions to faith properly

[Printed p. 356 | Google Books PA356 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

so called: that this or that most vain pope cannot err; that the Council of
Trent cannot err, because these general propositions are matters of faith among
them: A legitimate successor of Peter cannot err. A legitimate council
approved by the pope cannot err. Yet neither are these propositions in
Scripture, nor can the assumptions—this man is a legitimate successor of Peter;
this council was legitimately assembled—be evident. How unjust toward God's
grace, therefore, are those who, although this proposition is in Scripture,
“Whoever believes is justified,” and the assumption “I believe” is no less
evident to many than that they exist and live, nevertheless deny that the
conclusion “I am justified” pertains to faith?

Bellarmine. 2. *Hope not only does not require, but cannot even bear, absolute
certainty on the part of the intellect. For what we know with certainty that
we will have, we do not properly hope for, but simply expect.*

Protestant reply. The vanity of this reasoning would have some force among the
heathen, for whom hope is the name of an uncertain good, but it has no place
among Christians versed in Scripture. For in the Scriptures of the Old
Testament hope and expectation are not even distinguished by words, and in the
New Testament they are actually confounded. Romans 8:23–25: *awaiting adoption,
for we are saved by hope. If we hope for what we do not see, we await it with
patience.* Philippians 1:20: *According to my earnest expectation and hope.*
The definition of hope in Lombard 3.26 and Thomas himself, 2.2, question 18,
article 4, is of this sort: *Hope is the certain expectation of future
blessedness.* The Pontificians do not deny that while Christ was on earth he
had absolute certainty of intellect concerning the future glory of his body,
and yet the same Thomas in 3, question 7, article 4, does not hesitate to affirm
that *Christ had hope with respect to the immortality and glory of the body
which he had not yet obtained.*

31. Protestant argument. That doctrine which teaches perpetual doubt,
hesitation, and wavering is very absurd.

Bellarmine. 1. *There is no Catholic writer who teaches that men ought
perpetually to doubt and hesitate concerning their reconciliation with God.*

Protestant reply. I. There is enough absurdity in the fact that the
Pontificians teach that men must perpetually doubt concerning perseverance and
election. II. Bellarmine himself in chapter 4 teaches that *all things
concerning God's hatred and love are kept uncertain for the future,* but we
must doubt concerning things uncertain. III. The same Bellarmine in chapter 5
sets Job forth as the example, rule, and balance of the faithful in the
knowledge of their justification; and yet he affirms concerning Job that he

[Printed p. 357 | Google Books PA357 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*displayed doubt.* And in chapter 6 he teaches that uncertainty concerning the
remission of sins is indicated by the phrases *perhaps, if perhaps, who knows,*
which carry the most uncertain doubt before them. And in chapter 8 he contends
that there are no signs of grace except common and *fallacious* ones; such
signs certainly do not remove doubt.

Bellarmine. 2. *This opinion indeed does not remove all fear, but it removes all
anxiety and hesitation, and even doubt itself, if he is called doubtful who
does not dare assent to either side.*

Protestant reply. He doubts concerning grace who cannot give thanks to God from
a consciousness of grace, but no Pontifician can do this according to
Bellarmine's opinion. It would be vain to contend about words. But surely a man
condemned to death who has no way of escape except through pardon cannot fail
to be anxious until that pardon is established for him. To know nothing
concerning our eternal state is an anxious matter.
