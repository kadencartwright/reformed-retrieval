2. Let this be the second foundation. This question, which we now have in
hand, can best be understood, and ought to be settled, from the general aim of
the passages in which it is treated and from the genuine state of the
controversy between the apostle and those opponents whom he undertakes to
reprove and refute. For Marcus Antonius in Cicero's *On the Orator* rightly
thinks that nothing helps a prospective speaker equally with looking as
frequently as possible to the subject about which he is to speak. Hilary also
rightly said in book 4 of *On the Trinity* that the understanding of things
said must be taken from the reasons for saying them. So too Ambrose—or whoever
it was who wrote those commentaries on the Pauline epistles—has the following
in the preface to his commentary on the Epistle to the Romans:

“The beginnings of things must be sought, so that knowledge of the things
themselves may be had; only then can the rationale of the case more easily be
explained, if its origin is learned.” Rightly

[1679 printed p. 91 | IA leaf 99]

indeed. For once the harbor we wish to reach has been established, it will be
easier to judge the manner of sailing. And this must be considered here all
the more because Bellarmine, in *On Justification*, book 1, chapter 19,
supports his opinion from the apostle's aim in the Epistles to the Romans and
the Galatians. Do not therefore weary, hearers, if the remainder of this
lecture is spent on historical rather than polemical material, so that from
it we may borrow light not only for the matter we are now treating but also
for the entire dispute concerning justification—Paul's as well as our own.
Consequently, for us to resolve this controversy more happily, two things
must be investigated with greater care. 1. Who were the opponents against
whom the apostle argues so laboriously, especially in the Epistles to the
Romans and Galatians? 2. What was τὸ ζήτημα, the question, or τὸ κεφάλαιον,
the main point? What, I ask, is the thing that he strives so earnestly to
prove and confirm? Here a many-sided disagreement arises among interpreters
as well as disputants, and in some cases a plainly hostile contradiction.

1. First, as concerns the opponents, or those who held wrong opinions, whom
he had either to refute or to instruct more correctly, they were of two
kinds. They either opposed the law to the gospel, or combined it with the
gospel.

The former kind included both Gentile philosophers and Pharisees, together
with other Jews imbued with their leaven, all of them the most hostile enemies
of the gospel. Both groups opposed the law to the gospel and works to faith
in this respect: they wished a man to be righteous and thus justified by
works without the faith and grace of the gospel. For this reason they not
only treated that faith and grace with neglect and contempt, but also hated
and persecuted it most fiercely with fire and sword.

The philosophers and other more reputable Gentiles were so swollen with
pride in their knowledge and virtues that Christ was foolishness to them.
They foolishly supposed that the law of nature had prepared for them an
entrance to righteousness and happiness. Against them the apostle acts in
Romans 1, 1 Corinthians 1, and Colossians 2.

The Jews, “nursed on Moses,” and relying upon Moses and his entire law,
stumbled at the stone of offense. Excluding faith in Christ, they sought from
works of the law that legal righteousness alone (Romans 9:31–32; 10:3). The
apostle repeatedly censures them in that epistle, as also in the Epistle to
the Galatians, in Philippians 3, and elsewhere. These, then, were the people
who opposed the law to faith and to the gospel. They extolled the law and
works in such a way that they wished evangelical faith in Christ to be
entirely excluded, regarding it as ridiculous and utterly unfit for a
sinner's justification.

[Marginalia: Acts 21:20.]

Meanwhile, however, there were others who did not think that the law should
be opposed to the gospel. They considered faith in Christ supremely necessary
for justification and salvation, but in such a way that they wanted the law
and its works to be joined with the gospel and faith in this business. Of
these Ambrose says: “Those from among the Jews who believed, yet thought
wrongly about Christ, said that the law must be kept, as though salvation were
not complete in Christ.” These too were of two kinds, namely:

[Marginalia: In the preface to Romans.]

1. The authors and seducers of this opinion were the pseudo-apostles—Gnostics,
as some think, or Simonians, or whoever else they were. Concerning them and
the beginnings of this heresy we read as follows in Acts 15:1: “Certain men
who came down from Judea taught the brothers and said, Unless you are
circumcised according to the rite of Moses, you cannot be saved.” In verse 24
the apostles also have these words in their synodical letter: “Since

[Marginalia: 2 Corinthians 11:13.]

[1679 printed p. 92 | IA leaf 100]

we have heard that certain men who went out from us”—they went out not only
from Judea, as verse 1 says, but from the fellowship of the apostles, and for
this reason Ambrose called them “believers”; thus they did not execrate faith
in Christ like unadulterated Jews, but rather professed it—“since we have
heard that certain men who went out from us have troubled you with words,
unsettling your souls, and saying that you ought to be circumcised and keep
the law, though we gave them no command, it seemed good to us,” and so forth.
Paul everywhere pursues these men sharply, especially when they had now
ascended to a higher pitch of obstinacy. He calls them “pseudo-apostles” and
ministers of Satan (2 Corinthians 11:13, 15), dogs, evil workers, and the
concision (Philippians 3:2), and strikes them with an anathema (Galatians
1:8–9). He argues against these men especially, as though against bewitchers
(Galatians 3:1) and seducers of the faithful, and attacks them with still
greater force.

2. Second, very many simpler Christians tasted of this leaven. They were
either ensnared from the beginning by the deceit and cunning of these men, or
else seduced and carried away after they had been imbued with true faith. For
this gangrene, just as it is now found everywhere, had then also crept far and
wide. Apart from almost the sole exception of the church of the Thessalonians,
of which we read nothing of the kind in Paul, it had invaded the rest either
nearly or altogether. It is not so evident that the church at Ephesus had
been sprinkled with this infection, since it is not scourged by name for it
in the apostle's epistle to that church. Yet something of the kind seems to
us to be intimated, since in chapter 4, verses 3 and 13, he exhorts them so
strongly to “the unity of the faith,” and diligently warns that they should
“no longer be children, tossed about and carried around by every wind of
doctrine, ἐν τῇ κυβείᾳ τῶν ἀνθρώπων, ἐν πανουργίᾳ πρὸς τὴν μεθοδείαν τῆς
πλάνης—in the trickery of men, in craftiness directed toward the scheming of
error” (verse 14). Writing to Timothy, bishop of the Ephesians, he also
mentions τῆς ἑτεροδιδασκαλίας, heterodox teaching (1 Timothy 1:3; 6:3). He
had certainly warned earlier in Acts 20:29 that “savage wolves, not sparing
the flock,” would enter among them after his departure, and indeed that men
would rise up from among themselves “speaking perverse things, to draw away
disciples after them.” We should suppose that this occurred at least when, in
Revelation 2:4, Christ charges this church with having lost its first love.

As for the Philippians, although we do not read that they were completely
torn apart by these dogs, we do read enough in Philippians 3 to show that
they were assailed. In both epistles to the Corinthians we find them battered
excessively by this ram; and that the Colossians were as well is evident from
Colossians 2. But Paul deals with this matter more expressly in the Epistles
to the Romans and Galatians. For, as Jerome says in the preface to Galatians,
“elsewhere the question was debated partially and in passing, as it presented
itself to one treating another matter, and was barely touched; but in these
two epistles the cessation of the old law and the introduction of the new are
specifically contained.”

He deals more gently with the Romans, as Ambrose and Peter Martyr understand
it, because from the cradle of their Christianity they had drunk milk not
entirely ἄδολον, pure, but somewhat mixed with this poison. Bucer does not
approve this, but he is even more displeased with the Roman Catholics on this
account, since they maintain, together with the more ancient fathers, that
Peter was the founder of that church. Whatever may be true of that point,
there is no reason to doubt that a great crowd of Jews had already flowed
into Rome. They came partly from those who had previously been transported
there by Pompey, and partly because Judea now obeyed the Romans and had been
reduced to the form of a province. Among others, we should judge that many
believers had also migrated there, as is evident concerning Priscilla and
Aquila. This was not without controversy concerning the legal ceremonies,
which emissaries of the Pharisees endeavored the more to inflame, to the
combined dishonor and harm of Christian charity and faith.

Romans.]

But the apostle marvels at the Galatians, presses them, and rebukes them more
severely. First he marvels that they are “so quickly transferred into another
gospel,” after deserting the one who called them into the grace of Christ
(Galatians 1:6). Afterward he rebukes them still more sharply as ἀνοήτους,
foolish or “badly deprived of their holidays”: “O foolish Galatians, who has
bewitched you, that you should not obey the truth?” (chapter 3, verse 1). “The
word ‘foolish’ is filled with great anger, or with great concern; and not the
word ‘foolish’ alone, but, so to speak, even the entire epistle.” So
Chrysostom speaks on this matter, and afterward explains at length the quite
just cause of this great anger. Its sum comes to this: having already been
freed by Christ from the burden of the law, they had been bewitched by the
poisons of the pseudo-apostles and wished still to serve the law (Galatians
4:9).

And so at last the first inquiry is sufficiently settled: namely, who were
the opponents whom the apostle undertook either to refute or to instruct
better? Yet by way of a concluding flourish I will add this to the first
inquiry:

The apostle's deliberate purpose was not so much to contend with the first
kind of opponents, though he censures them at times—whether the philosophers
and other unbelieving Gentiles, or unadulterated Jews, sons of the Pharisees,
who now opposed the law to the gospel armed not with arguments but with force
and weapons, and were so far from acknowledging Christ and faith in him that
they persecuted them in open warfare. Rather, he argues against the second
kind of opponents, who combined the law with the gospel. Although they
professed Christ, faith, and justification through faith, they nevertheless
wished to stitch works of the law onto faith in order to obtain that
justification.

Such were the seducing pseudo-apostles who had arisen either from the Jews or
from the Gentiles. They urged this point above all, and for that reason the
apostle pursues them more sharply.

Such too were certain faithful Christians who had been seduced, who had
permitted themselves to be subjected voluntarily to this yoke of the law and
wished others to be subjected as well. On this account he rebukes them both
seriously and, at times, severely.

Once this foundation, which is indeed most firmly founded, has been laid, the
Roman Catholic fiction mentioned earlier falls utterly to the ground: that
the apostle denies justification only to works preceding faith and performed
solely by the powers of nature and free choice without the grace of Christ,
while they assign justification to works flowing from faith and grace and
assert it with the greatest effort. Thus they weave precisely the same fabric
as the pseudo-apostles and those Christians originally seduced by them,
against whom the apostle rises so vigorously. For they both agree on this
point—the people of that former time and our Roman Catholics today: in
constituting justification they combine the law with the gospel. Neither
group wishes the law and works without faith to be able to justify, but both
contend that the law and works justify, provided only that they are joined
with faith. For them, justification consists of works with faith; for the
apostle, it is “by faith alone apart from works” (Romans 3:28). According to
their principles, faith and works are so joined in the business of
justification that faith holds only the place of a prior disposition, while
works and the infusion of grace hold the status of the material and formal
cause. According to the apostle's teaching, faith itself bears only the
status of an instrument, while works, even those proceeding from faith, have
no place in the very rationale of justification, though they have the fullest
place in the person who has truly been justified. The apostle, I say, does
not argue here so much against Gentiles and Jews as against pseudo-apostles
who boasted of faith in Christ and against true, faithful Christians deceived
by them, yet trusting in their works, which they wished to stitch onto Christ
and faith in him.
