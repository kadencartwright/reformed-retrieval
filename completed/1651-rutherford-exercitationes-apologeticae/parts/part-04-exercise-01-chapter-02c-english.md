20. “To justify” is taken either actively, for God's judicial act, or passively
or terminatively, insofar as it terminates upon the believer. In the former
sense it is an internal and immanent act in God, not one passing over into an
external subject. Acts of this kind are election and reprobation, which possess
their whole completed being before the elect, the reprobate, or the justified
either begin to exist, live, and believe or do anything good or evil. In this
sense faith is not the instrument of justification, for no one by believing
causes God to be unwilling to punish sins or to will to love us unto eternal
life.

But “to justify,” terminatively and passively, is to declare righteous a person
who is alive and actually believes. This is done by a judicial act terminating
upon the conscience of a guilty sinner who has been summoned before the
fearsome tribunal of God the judge and convicted of sins. In this forensic
proceeding the sinner is absolved; he actually perceives and apprehends the
declared absolution, and with a trusting reliance

[1651 logical printed p. 52 | IA leaf 70 | PDF p. 70]

rests upon Christ, who now holds out the manifestation of that sentence. Yet
the expression “to justify” (*τὸ justificare*), in this customary scriptural
form of speech, does not posit in God a new volition now beginning in time, as
the Arminians prattle together with their Socinus. Rather, it posits an
intimation now made to the conscience of God's eternal volition.

I prove it as follows. A person is not justified from eternity, because a
person does not exist from eternity. Sins are not remitted from eternity to a
person who believes, because that person does not exist from eternity. For in
this sense justification and remission are not limiting terms. Second, when a
king pardons a courtier's crime of treason, does he also pardon the courtier's
heirs and children who are not yet born? Those not yet born are not actually
capable of either the king's anger or his favor, but only potentially. The
accused stands in the same position in this matter.

The Arminians will therefore say that here we take justification to mean the
sense and knowledge of justification and remission. Consequently, “people are
justified by faith” has the same force as saying that people are justified only
when they believe—that is, that they then perceive themselves to be justified,
although they had already been justified. Sicilian trifles and quibbles! To be
justified is more than to perceive oneself justified. For, first, it is the act
of an absolving God terminating upon the conscience of a person summoned and
drawn before the tribunal of the fearsome judge; before this instant that act
did not terminate upon the conscience. Second, by this act God assures the
conscience of the summoned person who relies trustingly upon Christ that a full

[1651 logical printed p. 53 | IA leaf 71 | PDF p. 71]

expiation of all sins has now been made in Christ. Third, the sinner himself,
by an act of trust, rests upon Christ as the sufficient Savior of believers.
But an act of God terminating upon us cannot be the bare perception of that
act. What sane person whose head possessed even slightly more sobriety would
argue in that fashion?

21. Therefore, although sins are remitted from eternity in the mind of God, a
person is nevertheless not justified from eternity—that is, declared righteous
in his own conscience when he is summoned before God's tribunal as righteous
in Christ. For although God's act of justifying connotes God's immanent and
eternal act, that act is nevertheless not the whole and complete formal nature
of the sinner's justification about which Paul in the Epistle to the Galatians
and Scripture speak.

Formally, then, for God to justify is for him actually to declare, by a judicial
act, that the accused who trembles before the judge has an eternal absolution;
and now, for the first time and never before, the effects of the divine
displeasure against sin cease by virtue of the divine promise, by which Christ,
all his benefits, an actual right to God's kingdom, and the dignity of adoption
are promised to the believer.

You will say: Are not the effects of divine wrath against sins removed from an
elect person before he believes? For if the punishment inflicted upon an elect
person before he believes is satisfactory, God punishes the sins of the elect
once in Christ and once in the elect themselves before they believe.

I answer: Certainly, before an elect person believes, God's wrath against him
and all the effects of wrath

[1651 logical printed p. 54 | IA leaf 72 | PDF p. 72]

toward his person have ceased, and this on account of Christ's merit. Yet this
does not happen by virtue of that chief gospel promise—“He who believes in
Christ does not come into condemnation”—for the effects of God's wrath against
the elect person's sin are never removed by virtue of that promise until the
person actually believes. Hence an elect person has no consolation and no
peace of conscience before fulfilling the condition of the covenant of peace,
that is, before believing.

Therefore, although even before believing the elect person is not subject to
God's wrath because of Christ's merits and satisfaction, and although on
account of that satisfaction he is given the grace of baptism and the gospel,
and even the Holy Spirit by whose power he eventually believes, nevertheless,
while he does not yet believe, he does not possess these benefits by virtue of
the covenant of grace and that special gospel promise—“He who believes has the
Son; he who believes will be saved.” Thus, in this sense, the sins of an elect
person who does not yet believe are not yet remitted, and God's wrath is not
yet removed. That is, the effect of divine displeasure against sin is not yet
taken from him in the special manner in which it is removed by virtue of the
gospel promise that requires faith, which is the fulfillment of the covenant.

Consequently, the elect person who does not yet believe has a right *in*
Christ, but not a right *to* Christ, since, as one who does not yet believe, he
does not know whether he is a covenant member to whom the promises belong as
they belong to an heir and son of promise. But one who believes has both a
right in Christ and a right to Christ. In him Christ has fulfilled the new
covenant and, as

[1651 logical printed p. 55 | IA leaf 73 | PDF p. 73]

the covenant's surety of faith, Hebrews 7:22, by virtue of God's promise and
truthfulness—which is a certain justice of God—the effects of God's wrath are
removed according to the special manner of a perfectly equitable covenant.
This is for sins to be remitted and not imputed, according to the scriptural
expression in Psalm 32:1–2 and Romans 4.

It is therefore one thing for a person to be justified in Christ from eternity
and another for a person to be justified in Christ in time according to the
gospel covenant and compact. I therefore say that sins are remitted through the
act of believing because faith apprehends Christ, not as a bare object—for in
that way the assent of faith apprehends Christ and the history of the gospel—but
as an object given and actually offered by God as our righteousness. Thus faith
embraces, kisses, and receives Christ insofar as he is given and promised, John
1:12 and Hebrews 11:13, so that, according to the new covenant and by virtue of
God's truthfulness, Christ is the believer's righteousness. Hence there is also
a special covenantal immunity, so to speak, from the effects of God's wrath and
from sin's condemning force.

Here I wish this to be noted: faith does not instrumentally produce even
eternal and immanent justification and remission of sins; rather, it
presupposes these as an object accomplished long ago. In this sense the
argument of Bellarmine, Socinus, and the Arminians against us proceeds. We agree
with them that no act produces its own object, and that sins are not remitted
through the act of faith but had already long before been remitted. Yet
covenantal justification,

*[Source note: The witness prints “Bellarmimi” for “Bellarmini.”]*

[1651 logical printed p. 56 | IA leaf 74 | PDF p. 74]

or federal remission—which I have now explained—is an effect of faith, and
faith is naturally prior to that justification. This covenantal justification
does not add anything real to God's eternal volition not to punish the sins of
the elect in the elect themselves but in Christ. Nor is it bare knowledge or a
perception of justification. Rather, it establishes in the believing elect
person a new immunity from condemnation. I say “new,” not with respect to the
substance of the thing, since even before they believe the elect are always
immune from condemnation in and because of Christ's death, but new with respect
to the manner of the thing. For this immunity is the removal of the effects of
divine wrath according to the special gospel compact which, by God's grace,
they fulfill when they believe. Before the act of believing this removal was
entirely dispensatory, arising from God's free constitution; the present
removal rests upon God's truthfulness and the special compact revealed in the
gospel. The former comes from God's absolute freedom, the latter from God's
just will.

*[Marginalia: *Apology*, chapter 10, fol. 108.]*

22. Hence it is easy to answer what the Remonstrants object against our
doctrine in their *Apology*. They declaim magisterially against confidence in
special mercy. First, the object of faith is the promise of mercy, and that
promise is universal just as the preaching of repentance is universal, as the
Augsburg Confession acknowledges. But the special mercy of the elect, or mercy
flowing from election, is not universal and common to all but is the peculiar
mercy of the elect. Therefore such special mercy is not the object of faith. I
answer: The promise of

[1651 logical printed p. 57 | IA leaf 75 | PDF p. 75]

mercy is called universal in that Augsburg Confession because it is proposed
indefinitely to everyone in the church; but in itself and with respect to the
divine intention it is in truth peculiar to the elect.

Objection 2. That special mercy cannot be promised; therefore its promise is
not the object of faith. The premise is proved: such mercy belongs peculiarly
to the elect by force of the absolute decree of election long before it is
promised.

I answer: Our theologians do not teach that special mercy is formally promised
in the gospel. Rather, it is promised with respect to its effects and is said
to be promised because effectual grace, faith, remission, and eternal glory,
which flow from that special mercy, are promised to the elect. It is not absurd
for the effects of grace to be promised after we are already in grace according
to the gracious decree of election.

Objection 3. Faith is not the application of the remission of sins. The proof
is this: The remission of sins is a wholly pure act of the divine mind or will,
a judicial act immanent in God and not passing into a subject outside God. Who
could understand an act of God that is perfectly immanent in God being applied
by us?

I answer: It can readily be understood that all God's immanent acts—by which
God decreed to call, justify, and glorify us—are applied to us when faith
reveals those acts to us through the gracious effects wrought in us. Second,
properly speaking we believe what is revealed in the Word, but we also believe
God's internal and immanent decrees *a posteriori*, though not *a priori*, and
we thus apply to ourselves what we believe, although

[1651 logical printed p. 58 | IA leaf 76 | PDF p. 76]

special application seems to me to be an effect, not the essence, of justifying
faith.

Objection 4. The applying act is either an efficient cause, an antecedent
condition of special mercy, or merely coexists nakedly with it. If the first,
mercy will be the effect and later than the application, and therefore not its
object, since the object of application is prior to application itself. Yet the
suggested coexistence is empty. Therefore—

I answer: If we speak properly, the act of application is an instrumental
cause, not of mercy itself, but of mercy's gracious effects. It is consequently
neither an efficient cause nor a prerequisite condition. We deny that faith
causally moves God's will in any manner so that he wills to have mercy upon us.
For God's merciful will is God himself as merciful, and God has no cause outside
God himself; if the cause of his being were outside him, he would be a creature,
not God.

Objection 5. It is absurd for faith—that is, the application of special
mercy—to be prescribed by God to people under a promise of special mercy or
remission of sins.

I answer: First, it is not absurd but evangelical for God to command the
application of special mercy's effects under the promise of eternal life. Who
among us asserts that the grace of election is promised to us? That would
greatly please the Arminians, who wish faith to move God's will to elect people,
so that their promise is: Believe, and you will be elect in Christ's blood.
Second, it is their invention that God is moved to

[1651 logical printed p. 59 | IA leaf 77 | PDF p. 77]

the immanent acts of remitting sins and electing by our faith, which is
blasphemous.

Objection 6. A determinate act, such as election, cannot fail to produce in
God's will another gracious and determinate effect distinct from justification.
But that act can only be the act of remitting and saving. Special mercy must
therefore be formally included in election. Consequently, by the very fact that
God elected a sinner to life, the divine will was determined to remit the
sinner's sins. For in Calvin's school it is atheological and Socinian to say
that God wills eternal life for a sinner by a determinate will without the
remission of sins.

I answer: First, from the fact that election produces the act of remitting and
saving, it does not follow that this act is essentially included in election.
Second, God's will was not determined to remit by the very fact that he elected;
rather, it will necessarily be determined. For there are two different
determinations of willing—namely, the willing of glory and the willing of the
remission of sins. God's will is first determined to save as the end and then
to remit as the means. This is not atheological in our school. Yet, if we speak
strictly, one act of willing in God is not the cause of another act. By the very
fact that God elects people to life, he wills to give Christ and to remit—that
is, not to punish on account of Christ—so that he may confer the glory he
intends in a manner consistent with his justice. It is not because

[1651 logical printed p. 60 | IA leaf 78 | PDF p. 78]

a will intent upon destruction because of sin is changed into a will to save
by faith as an external cause, as the Arminians dream.

*[Marginalia: *Apology*, p. 112.]*

Objection 7. Justification is an act of God which God accomplishes wholly and
solely within his own mind, since it is nothing but the volition or decree by
which he wills at some point to remit sins and impute righteousness to those
who believe. That is, by it he wills not to inflict upon them the penalties
their sins deserve, but to treat them as righteous and reward them. How, then,
can this act of God properly be called an effect of faith, and of faith insofar
as it is an instrument? Surely by believing we would cause that act of God;
what could be more absurd?

I answer: First, those who wish justification to be an immanent decree must
call it an eternal decree. For all God's immanent acts are in reality God
himself acting and are therefore coeternal with God. But when, with their
customary craft, they say that justification is a decree by which God wills at
some point to reward believers, they make sufficiently clear their judgment
that this immanent decree begins at some time. They thus posit at some time—that
is, in time, when people believe—a new and temporal willing in God that is
subject to arising and perishing as a person believes and repents, or does not
believe and falls back into grave sins. They are consequently compelled to
maintain that God's decrees are both temporal and mutable. Second,
justification taken terminatively is not formally a decree but a covenantal act
of declaring the believer righteous. Third, for us to cause by believing that
God wills or decrees

[1651 logical printed p. 61 | IA leaf 79 | PDF p. 79]

from eternity is absurd. The Remonstrants nevertheless admit this when they
teach that God elects people from eternity because they believe. Yet it is not
absurd but orthodox to say that by believing we cause God to declare and
pronounce us righteous in Christ to our consciences. By believing we thus cause
the effects of God's wrath and displeasure to cease in the manner most fitting
to the gospel covenant. In this sense we believe faith to be the instrument of
justification—not because faith as a work is part of our righteousness, but
because by faith we embrace Christ, whom God has given to us as righteousness.

They call it mere nonsense to say that faith, as though it were a hand,
apprehends Christ's righteousness or draws remission to itself, while they call
knowledge, assent, or application an instrumental action. But, say the
Remonstrants, since, once faith is performed, we obtain the remission of sins
from God's promise in no other way than if faith had been given by God as an
instrument for obtaining the remission of sins, why should it be called an
instrument of justification?

I answer: First, none of us has called faith an instrument with respect to
God's act of justifying. But with respect to passive justification terminating
upon our conscience, we call it an instrument in this sense. We do not mean
that faith by any instrumental causality produces Christ's righteousness—those
who charge us with that are prattling—or that it draws Christ's righteousness
to itself by a real act as a poor person's hand draws an alms to itself, for
every analogy limps. We mean that, once the condition of faith is fulfilled by
God's grace and thus

*[Marginalia: *Apology*, fol. 112.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 62 | IA leaf 80 | PDF p. 80]

the covenant of grace is fulfilled, we are declared righteous by the gospel
promise, and the effects of divine wrath cease by the gospel compact. Hence,
in the scriptural expression, we receive (*λαμβάνομεν*) God's promises by faith,
Galatians 3.

Thus covenantal justification is not God's bare estimation accepting us as
though it established nothing in us. Although it does not expel a disposition
from us and introduce a new disposition of habitual grace, as the papists
dream, it nevertheless establishes something real in us: namely, that we have
forsaken our own righteousness and cling firmly before the judge to Christ's
righteousness for the remission of sins. It also establishes another real
being in us, namely, God's act of justifying terminating upon our conscience.
By that act we leave the court just as a debtor, whose debt has been paid by a
surety, leaves it with an assurance that the debts have been paid, like a
certificate of absolution from Christ.

*[Marginalia: Remonstrants’ *Apology*, fol. 113.]*

Objection 8. Strictly speaking, it is not true that Christ's righteousness is
imputed to us because of Christ's merit and obedience, unless we say that
Christ's righteousness is imputed to us because of Christ's righteousness,
which is absurd. Episcopius holds the same. Our righteousness, therefore, is
not properly Christ's righteousness—still less his active or passive
righteousness—but that which Christ merited by both of them and which we obtain
through Jesus Christ alone. For properly speaking, Christ's righteousness is
not what is imputed; rather, that which is imputed to us

*[Marginalia: Episcopius, disputation 22, *On Justification*, thesis 5.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 63 | IA leaf 81 | PDF p. 81]

to believers is itself righteousness. Hence Arminius himself, *On
Justification*, article 19, asks whether it may correctly be said that Christ's
righteousness is graciously imputed as righteousness.

But I answer: First, the inference by no means follows: because Christ's
righteousness is imputed to us on account of Christ's merit and obedience,
therefore Christ's righteousness is imputed on account of Christ's
righteousness. For Christ's obedience is not formally his righteousness but
only causally; it is the ground and cause of Christ's righteousness. Because
Christ obeyed most fully unto death on the cross, he therefore rose as the
surety of faith, righteous and discharged from the debt. On this account God
reckons us discharged from the debt and righteous. This is for Christ's
righteousness to be imputed to us, which is strictly true.

For these three things are subordinated here. First, from absolute freedom God
reckons Christ's death as the same as if we had died. Second, by obeying unto
death Christ acquired a righteousness belonging to a surety. Third, because of
such obedience Christ is righteous in his capacity as surety, and we are
righteous in him. Nor can it correctly be said that Adam's sin is imputed to us
on account of Adam's sin.

*[Marginalia: Arminius, *Articles to Be Examined*.]*

Second, we do not say that Christ's righteousness is imputed on account of
Christ's merit. Rather, we say that what Christ merited for us by his death,
namely Christ's righteousness, is ours by divine imputation. For Christ's
righteousness simply considered is not ours, but his righteousness as a surety
or guarantor is ours. Third, the Arminians incline toward saying that Christ's
righteousness is ours not formally but effectively: namely, that by his death
Christ merited that our

[1651 logical printed p. 64 | IA leaf 82 | PDF p. 82]

act of believing and good works should be regarded by God the judge as our
righteousness. They say this very craftily, lest they be compelled to admit
that Christ died in our stead, place, or person, and so maintain instead that
he merely died for our benefit. They can thereby allow everyone for whom Christ
died to be condemned eternally and pay the penalties of their sins,
notwithstanding God's justice and the fact that Christ paid the penalties for
them. They share this view with the papists. Thus Francis Suarez the Jesuit
says: “Christ's merits are not given to us so as to justify formally, but so
that they may be the price by which we purchase the righteousness by which we
are formally justified. In the same way, one who gives another person the
price with which to buy clothes is said to clothe him, not formally, but
effectively and morally, as is evident.”

*[Marginalia: Suarez, *On Divine Grace*, book 7, *On the Sanctification of
Man*, chapter 7, section 30.]*

Objection 9. They do not wish all sins to be remitted in justification. “Was
the price,” they say in the *Apology*, “fully paid for all the elect's sins,
whether already committed or yet to be committed, or not? If not, how can you
wish that price to be a true price? If it was, what remains for the elect but
to sleep securely from then on?”

I answer: First, Christ's merit is a true price, although it does not purchase
God's grace or favor as though God were changed from angry to benevolent. It is
a price because, once it is given, God can actually do good to sinners in a
manner consistent with his justice. Second, it is a price paid for sins yet to
be committed, nor is that any reason for them to indulge in sleep and security.
For such security, arising from the corrupt disposition of the flesh, is an
abuse of the remission of all sins, not remission's genuine offspring,

*[Marginalia: Remonstrants’ *Apology*, chapter 18, fol. 200.]*

[1651 logical printed p. 65 | IA leaf 83 | PDF p. 83]

and we believe that such security cannot befall a regenerate person. For in
the gracious covenant's promise God has expressly promised such a person that
he will put the fear of himself into his heart so that he will not depart from
God, Jeremiah 32:40.

Third, it is well that the Arminians wish some sins to be remitted to an elect
person and others not, and sins already committed not to be imputed while sins
yet to be committed are imputed. But since God foreknows those sins yet to be
committed, does he not at the same time hate the same person unto death and
love him unto eternal life? That is to posit contrary wills in God.

Fourth, the Arminians sufficiently expose themselves when they wish the act of
justifying, which above they called an immanent decree in God, to be a temporal
act coeval with the acts of free choice. This is to imagine God more mutable
than Proteus, fixing, unfixing, establishing, and rescinding so many volitions
and decrees over the hours and years.

Fifth, imputed sins either have been expiated by Christ's blood and God's wrath
satisfied for them, or they have not. If they have, then notwithstanding God's
imputation of the sins the elect person is in favor with God, and a true price
has been paid for the imputed sins. But what justice is there in charging as a
debt what has been fully paid? If they have not, then the later faith of the
elect person causes God not to impute sins for which God's wrath has not been
satisfied. This will be remission without the shedding of blood, or faith will
be a ransom (*λύτρον*) for those sins, which is absurd. Sixth, this is precisely
what the Arminians wish: that God did not obtain for the elect

[1651 logical printed p. 66 | IA leaf 84 | PDF p. 84]

a full remission, but that Christ merely died for them conditionally, if they
should believe. Yet this overturns their own doctrine, or else makes our faith
add something to Christ's satisfaction to complete it, as though it were
insufficient in itself. Seventh, it follows that no one is truly justified
before death, for God will always impute sins yet to be committed. Scripture
contradicts this, testifying that in this life we have peace from justification
and access to God through Christ, Romans 5:1–2, and the remission of sins,
Ephesians 1:7 and Colossians 1:13–14.

*[Marginalia: Remonstrants’ *Apology*, same place, fol. 200.]*

Objection 10. Either this price paid for the elect is accepted and paid without
a condition, or it is accepted only with a condition. If without a condition,
what need is there for application or faith? What is the formal effect of
application? If it is fully paid and accepted with a condition, how has it been
fully paid for the elect any more than for the reprobate? Indeed, what kind of
benefit is suspended from a condition?

I answer: First, this argument will also torment the Arminians, who wish Christ
to have died absolutely and therefore to have paid the price of redemption for
everyone. But then what need is there for faith? If the price was paid under
the condition of faith, then Christ died only for believers, which they deny.
Nor could it be paid under the condition of faith. For then, if we who now live
after Christ's death had not believed—which Arminius holds possible—Christ
would not have died for us, and thus not for all mortals;

[1651 logical printed p. 67 | IA leaf 85 | PDF p. 85]

indeed, if faith is the condition of the price's having been paid, faith should
have preceded the payment of the price. Christ therefore should not have died
until all mortals finally believed, which is absurd. And if Christ's death
depends upon faith as a condition, then if some people did not believe, Christ
should not have died for them. Thus he has not yet died for everyone.

Second, the Arminians here intimate that Christ died absolutely for everyone,
as they teach elsewhere; yet by divine acceptance the price was paid by Christ
and accepted by the Father only in the name of those who believe. If this is
what they mean, then they miserably tear Christ's death away from the price,
and Christ's death becomes a ransom (*λύτρον*) merely because it is death. But
Christ did not give the price of redemption for those who do not believe.
Therefore, even supposing that they believed, their faith would help them
nothing. In that case they could not have been redeemed and consequently could
not have been saved, since the price of Christ's blood was not given for them.

But if they mean that faith causes the acceptance by which God accepts Christ's
death for the believers themselves, then the written charge and sins are not
blotted out before we believe. Yet Paul says in Colossians 2:15 that these were
blotted out by Christ as he hung upon the cross, and therefore before we—who
now live after Christ's death—believe. Moreover, we are graced and accepted by
God in the Son of his love, Ephesians 1:6, and in Christ's blood, Hebrews
9:13–14, not in the act of faith.

Third, we say that the price was paid for the elect without a condition in
order that it should be paid, but not without the condition of faith in order
that it should be applied;

[1651 logical printed p. 68 | IA leaf 86 | PDF p. 86]

application is therefore necessary for covenantal justification. Fourth,
according to our doctrine that price is not an empty benefit because it is
suspended from the condition of faith. For we do not acknowledge the Arminian
potestative condition which they have drawn from the jurists, one that stands
open to being or not being. Christ's death would indeed be empty under such a
condition. Rather, the fruit of the price that was given is suspended from the
condition of faith, which God works in us unfailingly according to his good
pleasure alone.
