Verse 16. Take his garment who stands surety for a stranger, and take a pledge from him who stands surety for foreigners.

In זר (*alieno*, for a stranger) and נכרים (*peregrinis*, for foreigners) there is a metaphor taken from an outsider and a foreigner and transferred to all those of whom no certain and established knowledge of this kind is available to us, such that we should constitute ourselves sureties for them, or they should put themselves forward among us as sureties for strangers; just as elsewhere זלה stands for every woman with whom intercourse is illicit, however well known to us, or even related to us. Likewise, in בגדו (*vestem ejus*, his garment) there is a synecdoche for all domestic goods.

The purpose is to arm us against rash suretyship. See 6.1–5, 11.15, and chapter 17.18.

Thus far in Solomon’s Proverbs he has deterred people from rash suretyship. Now he discloses the means by which they may guard themselves against rash suretyship: namely, if they accept a pledge from the person for whom they stand surety, so that they may provide for themselves against accidents and dangers that arise. Then, in the preceding Proverb, warning was given in general that no one should rashly stand surety for anyone. In this place he designates in particular a certain kind of people from whom a person should shrink in the business of suretyship, namely, those who have grown accustomed to stand surety for others. For if such a person pays the debts for which he has interposed himself as surety, he must be regarded as already exhausted; but if not, he should nevertheless be suspected from his very readiness to stand surety. Yet what he commands—that the person’s garment be taken—seems improper, since provision was made by law that a garment must be restored before sunset, Exod. 22.25. Response: the garment must be returned at sunset only when the person lacks other garments by which he may protect himself from the cold of the night. Of this kind are all those things by which life is sustained, Deut. 24. Therefore the Prophet commands that a pledge should altogether be accepted from such a person in this place, and indeed that his garment should be taken rather than that the creditor should fail to provide for himself and his own security. Next, it is the rashness of the person himself who, being placed in such difficulty that he does not have two garments with which to cover himself, nevertheless chose to stand surety for another. He will pledge his faith and bind himself to you by every sacred thing; but do not rest content with these unless you have accepted a pledge with which you may satisfy the creditor.

Therefore by this Proverb the Prophet dissuades from suretyship with a new argument drawn from an accompanying disadvantage: one who stands surety will not easily find a surety for himself if ever he is pressed.

But if it is not safe to transfer the debts of others to ourselves, much less is it safe to make the sins of others—and, as it were, their debts, which are far more harmful than the former ones—our own. Yet we do this whenever we participate in the sins of others, 1 Tim. 5.

Verse 17. The bread of falsehood is sweet to a man; but afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel.

In לחם (*panis*, bread) there is a synecdoche for all human sustenance. Likewise, in פיהו (*os ejus*, his mouth) and חצץ (*scrupo*, gravel) there is a synecdoche for the whole person and every harmful thing. In שקר (*falsitatis*, of falsehood) there is a metonymy of the adjunct. There is also another synecdoche in חצץ, the singular for the plural.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 441 | printed columns 856-857]

The purpose is to deter people from fraudulent gain.

At first it may seem improper that he calls bread acquired by fraud sweet, since bread is the same whether obtained by honest labour or by fraud. But by this formula of speaking the Prophet intimates the monstrous perversity of the human disposition, to which no food is so sweet and savoury as that which is obtained by theft or by circumventing another person. To this pertains the statement at Prov. 9.17 concerning stolen waters and clandestine bread which are sweeter than other things acquired lawfully and securely. A notable example of this wickedness exists in Israel, who, despising manna—a most sweet food and desirable for many reasons—coveted the onions of Egypt and other foul things which were then unlawful for them. Nor do we find this unusual or new in our own times. For so plainly does it happen that people of abandoned and ruined wickedness dare openly profess that a variety of pastures makes fat calves, that they prefer adultery to lawful marriage. There are also not a few who will not hesitate to say that game caught at night and by stealth is more delicate and pleasant than that which is obtained justly. Nor are there lacking people who, after outmanoeuvring their neighbour in the conduct of commerce and taking profit from another person’s loss, will profess that they will drink one more wine-jar than they otherwise would have drunk if that fraud had not intervened.

In the latter half, the Holy Spirit blunts that former judgment of the flesh and sets its contrary in its place, saying that the mouth of the person who seeks sustenance by fraud must be filled with small stones. In this he seems to allude to bread made from flour into which small stones are mixed during grinding; while a person is eating they strike his teeth and break them. By this single synecdoche he embraces all those losses and disadvantages which come upon a person from this wickedness.

When he says “afterward,” he teaches that those things which are obtained by fraud turn out just as foods and drinks do which at first please the palate but afterward, in the very act of swallowing, are of an unpleasant and hateful taste, especially those which offend the stomach or belly. On this matter there is an excellent passage at Job 20.15. Yet there is this distinction: there the nature of sin is discussed universally, whereas in this place fraud alone is understood specifically. For he teaches that sin is like a delicate food which the sinner retains in his mouth and on his palate with the greatest pleasure and, so that he may keep it there for a long time, turns this way and that in his mouth. Yet afterward, when it descends into the belly, it pricks him and does not allow him to be at rest until he has, as it were, vomited it up. With this agrees what is celebrated in our Proverb: “Sweet meat must have sower sawce.” A notable example of this matter is drawn from the Israelites, to whom the flesh of quails was sweet and pleasant; but afterward their mouth was filled with small stones. For while they were still eating, the wrath of God invaded them and destroyed an immense multitude. The survivors were grieved by their death, since the children of one, the wife of another, and the husband or brother of yet another had been taken away by death. See also the history of the Amalekites who plundered the city of Ziklag, 1 Sam. 30.16.

And though God’s judgment may be silent outside, nevertheless in one moment God can erect within a person the tribunal of his own conscience, which may torment him by night and by day and never permit him to be at rest until through despair he has cast himself down to hell.

Verse 18. Let every thought be established by counsel; and wage war with prudent counsels.

In מלחמה (*bellum*, war) there is a synecdoche of the part for affairs of greater importance.

The purpose is to draw us away from rashness and exhort us to act advisedly in all things, especially those which most greatly concern us.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 442 | printed columns 858-859]

At Prov. 19.2 there is a precept not unlike this one, and one which brings a singular light to the interpretation of this Proverb. Something may likewise be drawn from 29.20 which is akin to this argument.

First, indeed, it must be observed that it is not enough for someone to set before his own eyes and hold as resolved those things which are honourable and praised unless he has also considered the reasons and means by which the things designed and begun within himself may be advanced and perfected. For there are very many who, setting excellent things before their eyes and determining in their mind to accomplish them, so trust in these sound and holy designs of theirs and in God’s grace for prospering them that meanwhile they neglectfully pass over the things which God has appointed beforehand for accomplishing them—just as though holy purposes had feet or wings to run or fly of their own accord—with the result that they are left destitute of every aid for running and flying. They act as though there were no obstacles in these matters, outside us, within us, in others, in Satan, and in the world, which oppose themselves to holy thoughts and designs. Of this kind are those who resolve to enter upon a better manner of living but who, because they are not constant hearers of God’s word, do not earnestly pray to God, and do not withdraw themselves from wicked companionships and association with ungodly people, fall away from those thoughts and designs of theirs. Nor is this surprising, since they contribute no more to accomplishing the matter well than a farmer who, resolving within himself and within his own mind upon a plentiful harvest, neglects everything which serves to advance it.

Therefore, in order to advance and perfect holy undertakings, vain confidence in ourselves, by which we are accustomed to trust in ourselves and our own wisdom, must first be far removed. Nothing is more adverse than this alone to the proper conduct of affairs, Prov. 3.6–7.

Next, it is expedient to acquire knowledge of the affairs to be conducted, without which we must necessarily wander in darkness like blind people, Prov. 19.2.

Afterward we must beware of rashness both in words, Prov. 29.20, and in deeds, 19.2. For the headlong person errs just as much as the person who does not know what must be done. From this it is readily evident that someone can possess knowledge of a matter to be conducted and nevertheless wander away through rashness from the goal which he proposes, because he observes neither the time, the place, the person, nor other things of that kind.

Moreover, we must beware of affections and disturbances of mind which carry us away headlong—envy, hatred, love, anger, and so forth—which are the worst counsellors in conducting affairs.

Once these reefs, which are adverse to conducting affairs prosperously and rightly, have been avoided, we must consider what must be done next so that the things rightly proposed may attain a happy outcome: namely, that there is nothing so slight or contemptible which we undertake that it should not be directed by counsel in order that it may reach the desired end. See chapter 3.6–8. Those merchants yield in the marketplace and collapse in credit who neglect small sums in their accounts. Plainly, the same thing seems rightly to be feared also by those who do not bring each and every thing back to examination. How true this is appears from the Apostle’s statement to the Colossians at 3.17, where he teaches that all words and deeds ought to be joined with invocation of the divine name. See also Psalm 128.1, the eighth. This must be diligently noted against frauds and cunning counsels which are sold under the name of wisdom. See Jeroboam’s cunning counsel.

In matters of greater importance, moreover, of which Solomon has proposed one kind here, he teaches that prudent counsel must be employed. This is not because there is any sound counsel which is not prudent, but because he warns that counsels more mature and elaborated must be employed in conducting these matters. By the same token he warns not only that we ourselves should

consult God in the word, in the Prophets, and in prayer, which we read was done by David and other godly Kings (I add that it was even done by the ungodly Ahab, who consulted his false prophets about war), but that we should also seek the counsel of others for that matter. No one, however endowed with a gift of wisdom, ought to refuse this, since Solomon, the wisest King of all who had ever lived before him or would live afterward, also had his more sacred senate and prudent counsellors, whom he even employed to retain the peace which had been promised to him by God.

But if considered counsel is needed for waging this external and bodily war, how much more is it needed for waging spiritual war, where the enemies are more dreadful and that for which the contest is waged is much greater.
