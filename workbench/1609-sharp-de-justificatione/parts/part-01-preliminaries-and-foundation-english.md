## A Treatise on the Justification of Man before God

Since we are about to debate the chief article of the Christian religion, we
shall first briefly set down the summary of orthodox doctrine as a foundation,
and afterward discuss the controversies between us and the Roman Catholics.

### Meanings of the word

The word “justification” has eight different meanings.

1. It signifies acknowledging and praising someone’s righteousness and
equity, as in Luke 7:29 and 35.

2. It signifies parading an empty righteousness and seeking a reputation for
righteousness, as in Luke 16:15.

3. It is taken metonymically for the law itself, which is the standard of
righteousness and justification, as in Psalm 119:5.

4. It signifies promoting oneself and others toward increase in righteousness
by teaching and instructing, Daniel 12:3; Revelation 22:11.

5. The word “justification” is transferred from forensic to sacred usage and
signifies acquitting an accused person and pronouncing him innocent, as in
Exodus 23:7. It is therefore opposed to condemnation in Proverbs 17:15; Romans
3:26 and 8:33; Acts 13:38–39; Luke 18:14.

6. It signifies being constituted righteous by the adoption of an inhering
righteousness. In this sense it is not found in Holy

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 14 | image 15574986 | printed p. 12]

Scripture, though the Roman Catholics use it in this sense—but most
inappropriately, because it signifies not justification properly speaking but
sanctification, the effect of justification.

7. It signifies declaring before other people that a person who has been
justified by the righteousness of faith is righteous, James 2:21.

8. It signifies making righteous, namely by the imputation of Christ’s
righteousness, as in Romans 4:5: “God justifies the ungodly,” that is, he makes
him righteous by the imputation of Christ’s righteousness, and afterward
pronounces him righteous.

### Righteousness and justification are twofold

Justification derives its name from righteousness. Hence, as righteousness is
twofold, so is justification.

1. Legal. 2. Evangelical. Legal righteousness—I am speaking of created
righteousness—is conformity with the law in the person who is therefore called
righteous according to the legal covenant: “Do this and you shall live.” It
existed in Adam before the fall, exists in the angels, existed in Christ as
man, and will exist in the glorified in eternal life. But after the fall it has
not existed, and will not exist in this life, in anyone except Christ. Hence,
since justification is the application of righteousness, or the pronouncement
that a person is righteous on account of full observance of the law, and since
this righteousness cannot fall to us on account of the sins inhering in us, no
one can be justified by this righteousness.

Legal righteousness comes about either through obedience, through punishment,
or through both. The first is proper to the angels and to Christ as man, and
will be proper to the blessed after this life. The second belongs to the
damned. The third is proper to Christ as mediator.

Legal righteousness can be distinguished by its subjects as either the
righteousness of a person or of a cause. It is the righteousness of a person
when a person conforms to the law, which does not befall us in this life. The
righteousness of a cause exists when the cause which a person—whether or not
perfectly righteous—maintains is righteous. This is the righteousness about
which

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 15 | image 15574987 | printed p. 13]

David often speaks in the Psalms, and it is called the righteousness of a good
conscience.

Evangelical righteousness is conformity with the law through another. It is
the fulfillment by Christ of the law which we violated, imputed to us by God
through faith. Hence evangelical justification is the application and
imputation of evangelical righteousness, by which, from God’s grace and on
account of Christ’s merit, we are fully acquitted from the transgression of the
law and its punishment and are pronounced righteous before God.

### Causes of justification

The causes of evangelical justification are as follows. First, the efficient
cause, which is either principal or instrumental. The principal cause is
twofold, internal or external. The principal internal cause is what moves God
within himself to justify us. It is not foreseen works or faith, nor an
evaluation of things present, but God’s good pleasure alone (εὐδοκία), as in
Romans 3:21, 24; Ephesians 1:9; 2:8; Titus 3:5, 7; Isaiah 43:25.

The principal external cause is Christ’s merit and satisfaction, the cause
which moves God to pronounce us righteous, as in 1 Peter 1:19; 1 John 2:2; and
the passages cited above.

The instrumental efficient cause is twofold, internal or external. The
internal is twofold, apprehending or sealing. The apprehending cause is faith,
as in Romans 3:28; the sealing cause is the Holy Spirit, Ephesians 1:13. From
his operation comes the internal effectual calling, when he effectually moves
the hearts of the elect to acknowledgment of their misery, to seek deliverance,
and finally to believe in Christ.

The external instrumental cause, which is generally joined to the internal,
is the preaching of the Gospel, through which the external calling takes
place.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 16 | image 15574988 | printed p. 14]

The external and internal calling differ as follows. First, the preaching of
the Gospel effects the former, while the effectual operation of the Holy
Spirit effects the latter. Second, in relation to the external calling a person
does not behave only passively, because he attends sacred assemblies and hears
the word. In relation to the internal calling, however, a person behaves
entirely passively if you consider its beginning, because he cannot prepare or
dispose himself in any way to receive it.

The material cause, which is called the meritorious cause, is the satisfaction,
merit, and righteousness of Christ. Considered as inhering in a subject, it is
outside us and in Christ alone, its proper subject. Yet it is ours and in us
insofar as it is apprehended by faith. Therefore we are said to become the
righteousness of God in him, 2 Corinthians 5, the last verse.

The formal cause is the imputation of Christ’s satisfaction, merit, and
righteousness, and the forgiveness of sins. For faith unites us to Christ, and
on account of this union God imputes Christ’s merit and satisfaction to us and
reckons it ours, as though by our own obedience we had fulfilled the law and
paid the punishments due for our sins. 1 Corinthians 1:30; Romans 3:24 and 4:9;
2 Corinthians 5:19; Luke 18:14.

The end is twofold. The first and ultimate end is the glory of God and Christ,
Ephesians 1:6 and 2:7; Romans 3:26. The second and proximate end is the
salvation of the elect, Mark 16:16; Ephesians 2:8.

Legal righteousness and justification differ from evangelical righteousness
and justification: first, in their subject, because legal righteousness
inheres in the person who is called righteous by it, while evangelical
righteousness inheres in Christ alone and does not inhere in the person who is
called righteous by it; second, in their efficient cause, because the efficient
cause of legal righteousness is the person in whom it exists, while the
efficient cause of evangelical righteousness is Christ alone; third, in their
end.

### Definition of the thing, and explanation of the definition

Justification is an act or judgment of God the judge, by which,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 17 | image 15574989 | printed p. 15]

from his sheer grace and on account of Christ’s merit, he acquits believers of
all sins and imputes Christ’s righteousness to them, to his own glory and the
salvation of believers.

Five things must be maintained in explanation of this definition and the
debate which follows.

First, justification is an indivisible act performed once by God, at the very
first moment when we begin to believe. It has no increase of degree or parts,
as sanctification does, although with respect to our sense and application it
has degrees in accordance with the degrees of faith, as in Psalms 32 and 50.
For there is a twofold application of Christ’s merit in justification: first,
by God, once and fully, in which respect it is perfect at the first moment;
second, by believers through faith, which is more or less perfect according to
the measure of faith.

Second, two things must be considered in this indivisible act of
justification. They must indeed be distinguished, but in no way separated.
First is the imputation of Christ’s righteousness, by which God makes Christ’s
righteousness—that is, his obedience and satisfaction—ours, regards and
reckons it as ours, as though it had been rendered by us, so that we may appear
before God’s tribunal clothed and covered with it. Thus, before he pronounces
us righteous, he makes us righteous. In this respect “to justify” signifies
“to make righteous,” as in Romans 4:5.

Second is acquittal, or the forgiveness of sins, by which God the judge
pronounces innocent the believer who is clothed with God’s righteousness and
liberates him both from the guilt and the punishment of sin. In this respect
“to justify” signifies “to pronounce righteous,” as in Romans 8:33. First, we
are rendered conformable to God’s law, and so lawlessness (ἀνομία) and
unrighteousness are covered. Second, we are free from the liability and
punishment which the law threatened, and every debt is removed. Justification
is frequently named in Scripture from

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 18 | image 15574990 | printed p. 16]

either one of these two things.

Third, the word “impute” in this debate does not signify “to give,” “infuse,”
or “introduce,” but “to regard,” “reckon,” “number,” or “credit.” It does not
have a fictitious sense, as the Roman Catholics claim when they assert that we
teach justification by a fictitious righteousness. The verb λογίζειν—that is,
“to impute”—which the Apostle uses eleven times in the debate concerning
justification in Romans 4, and more often in Galatians, is a metaphor drawn
from settling accounts. Thus, to impute something to someone is to regard or
reckon a person as one who did what he did not do. The word is read in this
sense also in Mark 15:28; 2 Timothy 4:14; Philemon 10; Romans 8:26.

Imputation is twofold. One kind is real or legal, when the thing itself, owed
by right, is paid by the debtor himself, and what has been paid is entered into
the account as received, so that the debtor is thereby actually released.

The other kind is gracious, when the debt is paid not by the debtor himself
but by another—namely, a surety—and is therefore regarded by the creditor as
payment. Thus, with respect to us, the imputation is gracious, since we were
not able to pay; but with respect to Christ the surety, it was real.

Fourth, the righteousness of Christ imputed to us is twofold, active and
passive. The active is the perfect fulfillment of God’s law, which was perfect
in Christ whether you consider affirmative or prohibitive commandments,
Matthew 3:15 and 5:17; John 8:29; Philippians 2:8. The passive consists in all
Christ’s sufferings, and especially the experience of divine wrath and eternal
death, and finally natural death. Thus, by acting and suffering, he most
perfectly made satisfaction to the Father

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 19 | image 15574991 | printed p. 17]

for us, and both kinds of obedience are imputed to us for righteousness.

Fifth, all and only those who believe are justified, for we apprehend Christ’s
satisfaction and merit by faith. These propositions—“we are justified by
faith,” “through faith,” “from faith,” “faith is imputed for righteousness,”
and “we are justified by faith alone”—have the same sense, as is evident from
Romans 3:22, 25–27, 30; 4:5; 9:9, 30; Galatians 3:14; Philippians 3:9; Luke
8:5; Ephesians 2:8, 10. They do not mean that we are justified by the merit or
worthiness of faith; by faith insofar as it is a work of the Holy Spirit in us,
an infused quality, or an act of our mind; or by faith insofar as it is joined
with love. Rather, by a figurative, synecdochical expression, the instrument is
put for the thing apprehended by the instrument, or faith is put for faith’s
object. We are therefore not justified by faith considered absolutely, but
correlatively. Hence the propositions “we are justified by faith” and “we are
justified by works” are set in contradictory opposition, as in Romans 3:27–28;
Galatians 2:16. For the proper and sole act of faith in justification is to
apprehend and apply Christ’s righteousness by faith.

### Effects of justification

First, adoption. As soon as the elect are acquitted of sin through
justification, they are adopted into the right of God’s children, Romans 8:14;
Ephesians 1:5.

Second, sanctification. For all who are justified are so governed by the Holy
Spirit that they do not walk according to the flesh but according to the
Spirit—that is, they flee evil works and perform good ones, Romans 8:1. By
these works of the Spirit, as fruits of faith, justification is demonstrated.

Third, peace of conscience, concerning which see Romans 5:1. For as soon as we
are justified, God sends a joyful

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 20 | image 15574992 | printed p. 18]

messenger into the hearts of the elect concerning the judgment rendered. He
does so through his Holy Spirit, who makes us certain of God’s grace and thus
liberates us from fear of condemnation and turmoil of conscience, in which the
height of human misery consists, as in Romans 8:9, 15. This peace of conscience
is greater or less according as the sense of justification is intensified or
diminished.

Fourth, confidence in God.

Fifth, certain persuasion that prayers are heard, Romans 8:23.

Sixth, hope, or expectation of promised good things.

Seventh, certainty of salvation.

Eighth, patience and endurance of the cross, Romans 5:6.

Last, glorification itself.

### Justification and sanctification differ

Justification must be carefully distinguished from sanctification because the
Roman Catholics confound the two.

They agree, first, in the principal efficient cause, for God the Father in
Christ through the Holy Spirit is the author of both; second, in their end and
purpose.

They differ, however, first, in form. Justification is the imputation of
Christ’s righteousness and acceptance on account of it; sanctification is the
bringing of the justified from impurity of life to purity through the Holy
Spirit. Second, in their proximate efficient cause: justification is from
Christ alone, while sanctification is from the Holy Spirit. Third, in effect:
justification acquits us in God’s judgment, but sanctification does not.
Fourth, justification is an indivisible act, but sanctification is perfected
successively and by degrees. Fifth, justification is solely a benefit freely
given, but sanctification is our service; for they differ as cause and effect.
