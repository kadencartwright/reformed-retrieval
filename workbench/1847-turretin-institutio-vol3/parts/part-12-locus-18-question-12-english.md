QUESTION TWELVE

ON THE TRUE MARKS OF THE CHURCH

Is the truth of doctrine which prevails in some assembly, or conformity with
God’s word through the pure preaching and profession of the word and the
legitimate administration and use of the sacraments, a mark of the true
visible church? The affirmative is maintained against the papists.

I. After treating the church’s nature, properties, and adjuncts, order
requires us to discuss its marks. This question pertains to its external
condition and is of the greatest importance in religion. Since salvation
cannot be obtained except in the communion of the true church, and many glory
in this holy name who lack its reality, it is highly important to have a
thorough knowledge of its true marks. We may thereby distinguish Christ’s
true sheepfold from wolves’ dens and the genuine society of godly Christians,
to whose communion we are called, from the conventicles of heretics that we
must avoid. Thus we may know which assembly we must join in order to attain
salvation. Since the question can be divided into two parts—the first about
the true marks which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 75 | IA leaf 109 | PDF p. 109]

are asserted by us, and the other about the false and counterfeit marks which
the papists force upon us—we shall pursue each distinctly and now treat the
first.

[Marginalia: What marks are.]

II. By *marks* are commonly understood certain external signs that fall under
the senses and through which one arrives at knowledge of a hidden thing. The
Greeks call these *marks*. Some are only probable and plausible, called
*probabilities*. Their nature is to indicate a thing in some fashion by a
probable but by no means necessary rationale—for example, those taken from
external and accidental adjuncts which clothe and accompany the thing itself.
Others are necessary and essential, called *proofs*, which certainly and
infallibly indicate the thing under investigation, as smoke indicates fire
and breathing indicates life, because they are taken from the thing’s essence
or its inseparable properties. The discussion here concerns marks of the
latter order, not the former.

III. Various authors require various things for a true mark. Some require
that it be essential rather than accidental, proper rather than common,
certain, clear, and perceptible rather than doubtful and obscure. Others,
such as Bellarmine, require that it be proper, better known, and inseparable.
We judge that only two things are required, to which the rest are easily
referred: it must be proper and it must be better known. If it is proper, it
is also necessary, essential, and inseparable; if better known, it is evident
and perceptible.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

IV. 1. The church can be considered either with respect to its internal and
mystical condition, as invisible, or with respect to its external condition,
as visible and instituted. Its marks can therefore be discussed in different
ways. As invisible, it is considered for discerning the true elect and
believers. In this sense it has as marks faith, hope, and love, implanted
through effectual calling. From them each person is certain of his own calling
(2 Peter 1:10), and through them he renders it at least probably certain to
others (Matthew 5:16; James 2:18). But these marks are not being treated here.
As visible and according to the form of its external gathering and union, its
marks are sought in this question: What are the marks and characteristics by
which the true visible church, which believers must join for salvation, may be
distinguished?

V. 2. Nor is the question about the marks of the Christian church in general,
for the profession of Christianity sufficiently distinguishes it from pagans
and other unbelievers. It concerns specifically the marks of a particular
visible church, so that we may distinguish the orthodox and purer church from
the heterodox and heretical, abandon the latter, and enter the former’s
communion. Here a twofold association of Christians must be distinguished.
One is general, resting upon the profession of Christianity and comprising
the Apostles’ Creed and baptism as the tokens of Christianity. This can indeed
suffice to constitute a baptized Christian, but not to obtain salvation with
certainty, since it is often liable to various fundamental errors both in
faith and worship. The other is special, in a communion that possesses purity
of the word and sacraments, unmixed with any heresy or idolatry, in which
salvation can be obtained. This latter communion is properly at issue here.
The question is not how a society of Christians may be distinguished from the
assemblies of pagans, Turks, and other unbelievers, but how, among the various
assemblies that profess Christ’s name, the true and orthodox may be
distinguished from false and heretical assemblies unworthy of the name of the
true church.

VI. Although some verbal diversity occurs among the orthodox in assigning the
marks of the true church, there is agreement on the matter itself. Whether
there is said to be one mark—namely, the truth of doctrine and conformity with
God’s word—or several—the pure preaching of the word with the legitimate
administration of the sacraments, to which others add the exercise of
discipline and holiness of life, or obedience rendered to the word—the matter
comes to the same thing. Where truth publicly prevails, love and holiness also
flourish after their manner. Nor can the pure

[1847 vol. III printed p. 76 | IA leaf 110 | PDF p. 110]

word of God be preached anywhere without the sacraments also being
legitimately administered there and the discipline prescribed by God’s word
prevailing and flourishing, since these two flow from God’s word and are its
appendages.

VII. The following must also be observed about these marks. 1. There are
different degrees of necessity, and some marks are more necessary than
others. The pure preaching and profession of the word stands in the first
degree of necessity, because without it the church cannot exist. The
administration of the sacraments does not possess an equal degree of
necessity. Although it depends upon the former, it can nevertheless be absent
for a time, as was seen in the Israelite church in the wilderness, which
lacked circumcision. The same rationale applies to discipline, which pertains
to preserving the church’s condition, but when it is removed or corrupted the
church is not immediately removed.

2. These marks possess a certain latitude, so that they admit various degrees
of purity, sometimes more perfect and sometimes less perfect, according as
they approach more or less nearly to the rule of Scripture. They accordingly
show the church to be either purer or more impure. This latitude must not be
extended so far that fundamental errors are tolerated, but only blemishes and
lighter errors. Thus a society which fosters capital errors that overthrow
the foundation of salvation cannot retain the name of a true church; yet a
church which errs somewhere in doctrine does not immediately lose the name of
a true church. Although it can no longer be called a pure church, it does not
cease to be a true church. Hay and stubble do not immediately deprive an
assembly of the dignity of a church, provided they are not laid down as the
foundation, according to the apostle’s rule (1 Corinthians 3:12).

3. The church can be considered either as already constituted or as yet to be
constituted, and either in a pure and uncorrupted condition or in an impure
and partly corrupted condition. The present question about its marks is
framed with respect to the former condition, not the latter. 4. The church’s
opinion must not be assessed from the private opinions of its exacting critics
and prelates, who, carried away by the goad of disputation, often rush to
extremes which the church either does not understand or does not approve, but
from its publicly received and retained doctrine and practice.

VIII. When the truth of doctrine and conformity with God’s word, or the
sincere preaching and observance of the gospel, is said to be the proper mark
by which the church is known, the other marks are not excluded but included.
Whether you consider God’s voice, it is the word; the faith of human beings,
it concerns the word; life and obedience, they are the fruit of the word;
good order, it comes from the word; or the sacraments, they are seals and
appendages of the word and the visible word. Thus wherever we turn our eyes,
the true criterion of the church is the divine word. It is consequently
called a banner, scepter, light, norm, and balance, by whose guiding star and
rule all things are to be examined. A mark can be spoken of in two ways:
either with respect to its efficient cause, namely God, who uses it to mark
the true church; or with respect to its receiving subject, when, having been
received by hearers, it produces fruits of faith and godliness by which it is
known.

[Marginalia: It is proved that the truth of doctrine is a mark of the church.]

IX. It is proved that such a mark of the church is true and essential. 1.
From Scripture, which by this characteristic distinguishes true Christians
as members of the church from false ones. “My sheep hear my voice and follow
me” (John 10:27). Here Christ proves that the unbelieving Jews are not of his
sheepfold—that is, do not belong to the true church—because they do not hear
the Shepherd’s voice. “You are not,” he says, “of my sheep, as I told you,”
adding this reason in verse 27: “My sheep hear my voice.” Thus those who do
not hear Christ’s voice are not of his sheepfold, while conversely those who
hear and follow it truly belong to that sheepfold and are its members. What is
true of the individuals must also be true of the whole church composed of
them.

Nor should it be objected here (1) that “a mark of the sheep, not of the
church, is proposed, and we are taught who the elect are, but not

[1847 vol. III printed p. 77 | IA leaf 111 | PDF p. 111]

where the church is.” Both things are necessarily contained here, and one
depends upon the other. The sheep of Christ, or who the elect are, cannot be
known without thereby knowing the church which consists of the sheep and the
elect and where it is. If the church is the flock of sheep, and the sheep are
none other than those who hear Christ’s voice, then wherever Christ’s voice
is heard, there are Christ’s sheep, and consequently the true church must be
there.

(2) Nor should it be objected that “it cannot be a perceptible mark, because
this hearing, to be true, must be of the heart and not of the body.” This
hearing must indeed take place with the heart, but so that it also manifests
itself outwardly, both through external teachableness and profession of the
word and through actual obedience to and following of Christ. Although this
teachableness does not produce infallible certainty concerning others, but
only moral certainty from the judgment of charity, since its sincerity cannot
be certainly evident to us, it nevertheless furnishes an indubitable argument
both with respect to individuals—because one who is persuaded that he hears
Christ’s voice thereby knows that he is Christ’s disciple and a member of the
church—and with respect to the whole assembly, because where Christ’s voice
resounds and is heard, the true church cannot fail to be there.

X. 2. To the same point belongs Christ’s statement, “If you remain in my
word, you will truly be my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the
truth will set you free” (John 8:31–32); and in verse 47, “Whoever is of God
hears God’s voice”; and in chapter 14:23, “If anyone keeps my word, my Father
will love him, and we will come to him and make our dwelling with him.” Here
the keeping of Christ’s word and commandments is proposed as a mark of his
true disciples and as the means of obtaining his presence in their midst.
Where Christ dwells with the Father, the true church cannot be denied to be
there, since it is his house and temple.

This is confirmed from Matthew 18:20, where Christ promises his presence in
the midst of those gathered in his name. Since Christ’s saving presence has a
place only in the true church, and it is promised to those gathered in
Christ’s name—that is, those who come together by his authority to preach and
hear his word—there is no doubt that the church where believers assemble in
Christ’s name is true. It cannot be said that “this passage indeed
demonstrates where Christ is, but not where the church is.” Christ cannot be
found without the church in which he dwells and which is his body, for it
cannot be separated from him.

XI. 3. The same is proved from Acts 2:42, where the character of the apostolic
church is proposed through perseverance in the apostles’ doctrine,
fellowship, and the breaking of bread, and so forth: “The disciples continued
in the apostles’ doctrine and in fellowship and in the breaking of bread and
in prayers.” Thus the pious exercises of the primitive church are noted, each
one being a mark by which the church of Christ was distinguished *a
posteriori* from the Jewish synagogue and other assemblies of unbelievers.
Three are mentioned as principal: the preaching and hearing of the word,
prayers, and communion in the Eucharist, which is described
synecdochically by “the breaking of bread,” as in Acts 20:7. Thus “in
fellowship and in the breaking” is put by hendiadys for “in the fellowship
of the breaking,” as in Virgil, “we pour libations from bowls and gold,” that
is, from golden bowls. Just as the apostolic church was distinguished by
these marks, so it must be known by the same marks today. Therefore, wherever
there are the apostles’ doctrine and the legitimate use of the sacraments and
prayers, it is certain that the true church of Christ exists.

XII. 4. The rationale for distinguishing the true church from the false must
be the same as that for distinguishing the false church from the true and
false prophets from true teachers. This rationale is nothing other than the
falsity of doctrine and its disagreement with God’s word (Isaiah 8:20;
Deuteronomy 13:1–2; Luke 16:29). Hence Christ, speaking of false prophets,
says, “By their fruits

[1847 vol. III printed p. 78 | IA leaf 112 | PDF p. 112]

you will know them” (Matthew 7:16), not only with respect to their morals and
life but chiefly their doctrine, as is gathered from Luke 6:45. John likewise
wishes the spirits—that is, teachers—to be tested to see whether they are from
God (1 John 4:1). If you ask for the norm of the test, he himself supplies it
in the following verse from the truth of doctrine: “Know God’s Spirit by
this: every spirit that confesses that Jesus has come in the flesh is from
God; every spirit that does not confess that Jesus has come in the flesh is
not from God.” More clearly, he says in his second epistle, verse 9, “Everyone
who departs and does not remain in Christ’s doctrine does not have God; he who
remains in the doctrine has both the Father and the Son”; and in verse 10,
“If anyone comes to you and does not bring this doctrine, do not receive him
into your house.”

Paul confirms this when he pronounces anathema upon anyone who would preach
something beyond what was preached: “If I or an angel from heaven should
preach something beyond what was preached, let him be anathema” (Galatians
1:8). He wishes not merely that whatever is foreign to the gospel be rejected,
but that anathema be pronounced upon anyone who should dare to bring such a
thing forward, whoever he may be, whether an apostle on earth or even an angel
from heaven, than whom nothing after Christ can be more illustrious or
venerable. Paul thus excludes the most imposing marks of authority and the
greatest miracles that might be pleaded, such as an angel’s descent from
heaven. If the presence of an angel or the authority of an apostle cannot
obtain our faith when it is opposed to the gospel, how much more must the
authority conferred by local or personal succession be reckoned as nothing,
since such successors cannot be considered greater than the apostles? Again,
if the apostles wished the doctrine of the gospel to be the rule by which
true or false teachers are known, how much more must this be so today, when
nothing infallible remains to us except Scripture?

XIII. 5. What always belongs only to the church and to the whole church must
be its essential and specific mark, by which it is distinguished from all
other assemblies, not only of unbelievers but also of heretics. The truth of
doctrine which shines forth in the preaching of the word and the
administration of the sacraments is such a mark. The church alone is God’s
house, the pillar and foundation of the truth (1 Timothy 3:15); it alone is
built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets (Ephesians 2:19–20);
it alone has the seals of the covenant (Matthew 28:20; 26:28; Acts 2:42;
Genesis 17); it alone possesses the word and is distinguished by it from
other assemblies (Psalm 147:19; Deuteronomy 4:6). These privileges do not
belong to it only for a time, but always and perpetually until the
consummation of the ages (Ephesians 4:11–12). Therefore it is rightly
inferred that where the preaching of the word and administration of the
sacraments exist, there is the church; and conversely, where the church is,
there are the preaching of the word and administration of the sacraments.

XIV. 6. That by which the visible church is constituted, gathered, and
preserved, so that when it is present the church is present and when it is
removed the church is removed, is also its proper and essential mark. No mark
is more certain than one taken from its cause and inseparable property. The
preached and received word is such a thing (1 Corinthians 4:15; Ephesians
2:19–20; 5:26; 1 Peter 1:23; James 1:18; Matthew 28:19–20), for it
constitutes, preserves, and nourishes the church, so that when it is present
the church is present and when it is removed the church is removed. Hence the
removal of the lampstand, or ministry of the word, brings the destruction of
the church after it (Revelation 2:5), and the cessation of prophecy brings
the scattering of the people (Proverbs 29:18): “Where there is no vision, the
people are scattered.”

XV. 7. The fathers support us. Tertullian says in *Prescription Against
Heretics*, chapter 21, “That must undoubtedly be held which the church
received from the apostles, the apostles from Christ, and Christ from God,
and so forth.” Speaking of heretics in chapter 32, he says, “Their doctrine
itself, when compared with the apostolic doctrine, will pronounce by its own
difference and opposition that it has neither an apostle nor

[1847 vol. III printed p. 79 | IA leaf 113 | PDF p. 113]

an apostolic man as its author, and so forth.” In chapter 37 he introduces the
church speaking in this way: “I am the heir of the apostles; as they provided
in their testament, as they entrusted to faith, so I hold.” He says later,
“How are heretics strangers and enemies to the apostles except by the
difference of the doctrine which each of them has brought forward or received
according to his own judgment against the apostles?”

Chrysostom says in *Homily 33 on Acts*: “A Gentile comes and says, ‘I wish to
become a Christian, but I do not know whom to join. There are many conflicts,
dissensions, and disturbances among you. I do not know which doctrine to
choose or prefer. Each says, “I speak the truth.” I do not know whom to
believe, since I am ignorant of the Scriptures and each side makes the same
claim.’ Indeed, this is greatly in our favor. For if we said that we believed
reasonings, you would rightly be disturbed; but since we receive the
Scriptures, they are simple and true, and it will be easy for you to judge.
If anyone agrees with them, he is a Christian; if anyone fights against them,
he is far from this canon.” And in *Homily 6 on Matthew*: “Where faith is,
there is the church; where faith is not, there is no church.” And in *Homily
16 of the Imperfect Work on Matthew*: “When heresy, which is Antichrist’s
army, prevails, there is no test of the church except through the Scriptures
alone.”

The author of the commentary on the Psalms under Jerome’s name says on Psalm
133: “The church does not consist in walls, but in the truth of doctrines.
The church is where the true faith is. Fifteen or twenty years ago the
heretics possessed all the walls of the churches here, but the church was
where the true faith was.” Ambrose says in *On Luke*, book 6, chapter 9:
“Faith, then, is commanded to be sought first in the church. If Christ dwells
in it, it is undoubtedly to be chosen; but if an unbelieving people or a
heretical teacher deforms the dwelling, communion with the heretics is judged
to be avoided and their synagogue to be fled.”

Augustine says in *On the Unity of the Church*, chapter 3: “Let us not listen
to ‘I say this’ and ‘you say that,’ but let us listen to ‘the Lord says this.’
There are certainly the Lord’s books, to whose authority we both assent,
which we both believe and both serve. There let us seek the church; there let
us discuss our case.” And in chapter 10: “I have the most manifest voice of
my Shepherd commending the church to me and expressing it without any
ambiguity. I will count it against myself if I should wish to be seduced and
led astray by human words from his flock, which is the church itself, since
he especially admonished me by saying, ‘Those who are my sheep hear my voice
and follow me.’ See, his voice is clear, open, and heard. How will one who
does not follow him dare to call himself his sheep? And so forth.”

He says in chapter 16: “No one reaches salvation itself and eternal life
unless he has Christ as Head. But no one can have Christ as Head unless he is
in his body, which is the church. Just as we must recognize the Head himself
in the holy canonical Scriptures, so we must not seek the church in the
various rumors and opinions, deeds and words, and visions of human beings,
and so forth. Let them demonstrate his church, if they can, not in the
speeches and rumors of Africans, not in the councils of their bishops, not in
the writings of any disputants, not in deceitful signs and wonders, and so
forth, but in the prescription of the law, the predictions of the prophets,
the songs of the Psalms, the words of the Shepherd himself, and the preaching
of the evangelists, that is, in the authority of all the canonical books of
Holy Scripture.”

Thus he says in chapter 2: “The question between us and the Donatists is where
the church is. What, then, shall we do? Shall we seek it in our words or in the
words of its Head, our Lord Jesus Christ? I think we ought rather to seek it
in his words, for he is the truth and knows his body best.” Many more things
to the same point are read there, which we omit for brevity’s sake. Thus in
*Epistle 166*: “In the Scriptures we have learned Christ; in the Scriptures
we have learned the church. We hold these Scriptures in common; why should we
not retain both Christ and the church in them?” Vincent of Lérins, as Sixtus
of Siena notes in *Bibliotheca Sancta*, book 6, annotation 104, sets down the
authority of the Scriptures as the first rule for distinguishing the true
church from the heretical.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 80 | IA leaf 114 | PDF p. 114]

XVI. 8. Not a few papists agree with us here. Bellarmine places holiness of
doctrine among the church’s marks, and in *On the Church*, book 3, chapter 2,
defines it “by the profession of the same Christian faith and communion in
the same sacraments.” Elsewhere in book 4 he concedes that “when Scripture is
received and speaks clearly, and a question about the church arises, the
church can then be judged from Scripture as the better-known thing.” Thus in
*On the Marks of the Church*, chapter 2, while responding to Augustine’s
statements affirming that the church must be demonstrated from Scripture, he
admits that “Scripture teaches what the marks of the church are.” From this it
is inferred no less evidently than necessarily that Scripture is not only a
mark of the church, but also the chief and primary of all marks, since its
other marks are known from and through Scripture itself.

Driedo says in *On Various Ecclesiastical Dogmas*, book 4, chapter 4, “The
church must be sought and known from the Scriptures.” Cassander acknowledges
in *Consultation*, article 7, that “the marks of the church are the doctrine
of the gospel and the use of the sacraments.” Stapleton says in *On the
Principles of Faith*, book 1, chapter 22, “The preaching of the gospel is a
proper and very illustrious mark of the catholic church, and so forth.” In
*Relecture on the Principles of Faith*, controversy 1, question 4, article 5,
he concedes that “the church of Christ is known by wise and spiritual people
through sound doctrine and the right use of the sacraments.” Gregory of
Valencia says in volume 3, disputation 1, question 1, point 7, section 18, “We
confess that Christ’s church can lack neither the truth of doctrine nor the
legitimate use of the sacraments, and that those among whom these things are
wholly preserved constitute the true church.” Others confess the same.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XVII. It is one thing falsely to attribute the church’s marks to oneself and
glory in them, and another truly to possess them. The former is a question of
fact; the latter, of right. The false boasting of heretics who claim the marks
for their assemblies ought not prejudice believers’ certain persuasion,
because judgment about the marks must be made from the truth of the matter,
not the dreams of the sick or the opinion of the riotous. Nor can it be said
that our marks are common rather than proper because heretics or schismatics
attribute them to themselves, (1) any more than one could say that God’s
covenant is common to the rejected and cut-off Jews with Christians because
they glory in it, or that justice of cause belongs both to plaintiff and
defendant because each boasts of it. (2) By the same *ad hominem* rationale,
the papists’ marks would have to be rejected, because not a few besides them
attribute such marks to themselves, including antiquity, unity, holiness of
doctrine, and the like. (3) Indeed, there can be no marks of anything in the
world which some shameless and lying sophist will not arrogate to himself.
Who does not know that the devil wishes to pass himself off as God, that the
prince of darkness transforms himself into an angel of light, that Antichrist
claims Christ’s name for himself, and that a prostitute feigns a matron’s
attire?

XVIII. It is one thing to know individually who the elect are, and another to
know where they are and in which assembly they are found. Our marks do not
aim to reveal the former to us, but only the latter, which suffices for us to
know which assembly we must join. In a city it is not necessary to know
distinctly and certainly who the true and faithful citizens are who obey the
laws from the heart. It is enough to know which commonwealth possesses such
laws.

XIX. Although the pure preaching of the word does not always prevail in the
church, it does not follow that this mark is separable from the church and is
therefore falsely called a mark. This purity must be understood with a
certain latitude. The church does not immediately cease where purity ceases
in some degrees, provided it does not cease entirely. Purity must exist in
fundamentals for the church to be true, although various errors can prevail
in it otherwise. From these it can contract various degrees of impurity,
which, although they take away from it the name of a pure church, do not take
away the name of a true church so long as the foundation remains safe and
unharmed. Pure preaching of the word and purity of the church proceed step
for step.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 81 | IA leaf 115 | PDF p. 115]

If the former is pure in every respect and free from error, the latter will
also be pure; but if errors are mingled with the former, the church’s
condition begins to be corrupted, yet it does not immediately cease to be a
true church until the foundation is attacked.

XX. Although the dispensation of the word and sacraments is among the
church’s goods and endowments, they are nonetheless its marks, since the one
does not conflict with the other. In earthly affairs, possession and use of
things are a mark that ownership has been transferred, and the benefit does
not therefore cease to be a fruit or good. Thus the mark of the covenant
sanctioned by God with the church is the word, as its authentic instrument,
sealed with the seals of the sacraments. From their legitimate dispensation,
most abundant fruits return to their possessors.

XXI. It is one thing to be better known by nature and another to be better
known to us. Scripture is by nature better known than the church, because it
is the church’s principle and foundation; hence the church cannot be known
certainly and infallibly except from Scripture. The church is better known to
us than Scripture by a confused and inchoate knowledge, because it is the
means and instrument that leads us to Scripture and delivers it to us. Thus
Scripture and the church render mutual services to one another, but the
authority belongs to Scripture and the ministry to the church. The church
shows Scripture by its ministry and *a posteriori*, as an effect shows its
cause and a lampstand its light. Scripture shows the church by its authority
and *a priori*, as a cause shows its effect.

XXII. Perron objects in vain that “doctrine cannot be a mark of the church,
neither doctrine that is not disputed, because all parties agree about it and
so it is a mark not of distinction but rather of communion; nor disputed
doctrine, because the case is before the judge and no decision can be made
except through the church.” The response is as follows.

1. We do not say simply that doctrine is a mark of the church, but doctrine
insofar as it conforms to Scripture, which is a principle received among
Christians. If there were no rule for deciding controversies, or it were so
obscure that it could scarcely, if at all, be known, I admit that disputed
doctrine could not be a mark. But we have a canon in the word according to
which the godly can easily be taught the truth of conformity with the rule.

2. Undisputed doctrine, such as the Lord’s Prayer, the law, and the Apostles’
Creed, can decide disputed doctrine according as it agrees with or differs
from it. Thus the affirmative articles about which we agree are the rule of
the negative articles about which we dispute, just as the straight is the
index of itself and of the crooked. If Christ is our Mediator and Advocate,
he must therefore be the only one, because he admits no partner. If the
sacrifice of Christ’s cross is propitiatory, there can be no place for
another. If Christ is the Head of the church, the pope therefore cannot be,
because these things are incompatible.

3. If a mark ceases to be a mark because an opponent raises a dispute about
it, all the marks brought forward by our opponents are imperiled, because
each of them can be disputed.

XXIII. He does no better in supposing that infallible conclusions of faith
and decisions cannot be made except through an infallible means, which cannot
be human reasoning because it is fallible, nor private inspiration because it
can often be deceptive, but only the authority of the church, which God gave
us as an infallible interpreter. For, 1. the infallibility of the object, or
the dogmas, is wrongly confused with the infallibility of the subject, or the
human intellect. The dogmas have absolute infallibility, but the human
intellect properly has no infallibility, although it has a certainty in its
operation which does not deceive. Nor must what is by nature fallible always
actually fail, for otherwise there would be no certainty of knowledge, though
such certainty does exist. Therefore, the means that leads us to knowledge of
an infallible dogma need not itself immediately be infallible; it is enough
that, when rightly applied, it does not deceive. Thus the human mind—not
alone, but enlightened by the Holy Spirit—can be such a means by which truth
is distinguished from error. In this sense Paul says that the spiritual
person judges all things (1 Corinthians 2), and John says that the anointing
teaches us all things (1 John 2),

so that there is no need for the believer to be subjected to any
ecclesiastical tribunal in order to know doctrine. There is no apostle, nor
even an angel, and much less any pontiff or council, that is not subjected to
this examination by Paul’s oracle (Galatians 1:8). Nor, if fanatics falsely
boast of their inspirations, does it follow that a believer cannot be
certainly persuaded concerning his own inspiration, just as a sane person
does not cease to know certainly that he is in possession of himself and
reasons well even though an insane person attributes the same to himself.

2. The cardinal wrongly confuses the internal means and organs of knowledge
with the external object when he compares reasoning, inspiration, and the
authority of the church. Reasoning and inspiration are internal means and
organs by which we arrive at knowledge of the truth, but the church’s
authority is an external means that stands as an object which proposes the
truth. If the first two means are fallible, they will be equally so with
respect both to the church and to Scripture, and they will be no less capable
of error in receiving the decisions of councils than in judging the doctrines
of the Scriptures.

XXIV. It cannot be said that the common people and peasants are incapable of
examining doctrine and therefore need other perceptible marks suited to their
understanding. The discussion here does not concern the examination of every
doctrine and all the questions that can be raised about it, but only doctrine
necessary for salvation, in which the essence of faith consists. This is
clearly set forth in Scripture and can be perceived by every believer.
Otherwise the psalmist would say in vain that God’s law gives wisdom to the
simple (Psalm 19:8), and Paul would say in vain that Timothy had known the
Holy Scriptures from childhood, which can make him wise for salvation (2
Timothy 3), and that “the spiritual person discerns all things” (1
Corinthians 2:15). Nor is it any less difficult for the common people to grasp
the church’s marks which the papists bring forward and ascertain their truth
than to examine doctrine, as will be proved later.

XXV. Although a mark need not be a thing’s essential form or specific
difference, this does not prevent it from being a mark. Indeed, no more
certain mark can be given, since form gives being to a thing. Nor is it an
obstacle that the forms and differences of things are generally hidden,
whereas a mark ought to be perceptible and external. Natural and corporeal
things, which fall under the senses and whose marks must consequently be
external and perceptible, stand on one rationale; spiritual and moral things,
which fall under the intellect, on another. Such is the church in question,
whose being is moral and spiritual. It is not considered here simply as an
assembly of persons united among themselves through the external acts of
religion, but through true faith in Christ and sincere administration of the
sacraments. This truth of faith and purity of divine worship are discerned
only by the intellect through comparison of doctrine with the word.

XXVI. When the church is shown through doctrine, this is no more explaining
the same by the same than when the thing defined is explained through its
definition. Although a definition agrees with the thing defined and does not
differ from it in reality, it is nevertheless clearer and more explicit than
the thing defined which it makes known, as when I call a human being an animal
endowed with reason, or a grammarian one who knows or teaches grammar. Nor can
this be called begging the question, because the thing is explained through
its own form and difference.

XXVII. Although an unbeliever and heretic can arrive at a confused and
obscure knowledge of the church before knowledge of doctrine, he can never be
certainly and infallibly persuaded concerning the church’s truth and its
purity or impurity unless the purity or impurity of the doctrine upon which
the church is founded is first known. Material knowledge belongs to sense and
does not effect a demonstration, but no formal knowledge can be given unless
the form is known and it is proved that this form inheres in this matter.

XXVIII. A mark is considered either in itself and in the abstract, or in the
concrete insofar as it is applied to some subject. Thus a prince’s seal is
considered either in itself, with respect to its nature and the use for which
it is intended, or with respect to its application to letters or things that
must be sealed. In the former sense, the church’s mark is in Scripture,
because Scripture is the rule, norm, and canon of all truth. In the latter,
this mark is impressed upon the church through its profession of doctrine and
practice of divine worship conforming to Scripture. When the question
concerns the mark of the church insofar as it can be distinguished from other
societies, the mark is not understood in the former sense but in the latter,
by reason of conformity with the word.

XXIX. Since it is evident from what has been said that the truth of doctrine,
or conformity with God’s word, is in the thesis the true and genuine mark of
the true church, it is not difficult to infer in the hypothesis which is the
true church we must join to obtain salvation. Is it the present Roman church,
which retains in faith and worship so many capital errors and idolatries
utterly opposed to God’s word, or our church, which is content with God’s word
alone? More must be said about these matters in what follows.
