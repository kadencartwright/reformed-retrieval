## QUESTION VIII

### How did Joseph have two fathers, Jacob according to Matthew and Eli according to Luke?

In the Lord’s genealogy no question has been more vexed by ancients and moderns alike, whether Roman Catholics or Protestants. Celsus, Julian the Apostate, Porphyry, and others from the pagan school; Faustus the Manichaean and many from the ranks of the heretics, in order to undermine the credibility of the evangelists Matthew and Luke, used to urge an open contradiction: namely, that Joseph is said by one to be the son of Jacob, and by the other the son of Eli. Many answers may be seen among both the Greek and Latin fathers, and more numerous and lengthier ones among more recent writers—our own Gomarus, Vossius, Spanheim, Grotius, and others, as well as the Roman Catholics Maldonatus, Cornelius a Lapide, Suárez, Baronius, and others. Our judgment on the whole matter is this. If we rest in the common answer, which is general, the mouths of all who bark here against sacred Scripture will be shut quite sufficiently; but if we think we must descend further into particulars, there is scarcely anything solid beyond conjectures on which we can plant our foot.

The answer I call common, solid, plain, and, if I may so speak, satisfactory though more general, is this: there is nothing absurd in one son’s having two fathers of different kinds, so that one is a natural father and the other a legal father; the former begets the son, while the latter adopts the child begotten by another, according to the laws, into all the rights of sons. Thus Manasseh and Ephraim were the natural sons of Joseph alone, but by adoption they were Jacob’s legal sons in all the rights of sons, just like Reuben, Judah, or any others begotten by Jacob. Moses had Jochebed alone as his natural mother, but in addition Pharaoh’s daughter, by whom he was adopted as a son, became his legal mother. Obed, David’s grandfather, had Boaz alone as his natural father, but also had as his legal father Mahlon, the former husband of his mother Ruth, to whom her later husband Boaz raised up offspring according to the law after his death. Augustus Caesar was by nature the son of Octavius alone, but by Roman law was made the adoptive son of Julius Caesar, his great-uncle, namely the brother of his grandmother by his mother Atia. Nothing is more frequent, both among the Jews and among every nation, than for one man to have two fathers, one according to nature and the other according to law; indeed, all adopted sons must have two fathers of this kind. This solid answer most fully stops the mouth of everyone who barks at Joseph’s two fathers, for this has prevailed in every age, everywhere on earth, among all nations, both by law and by custom, without anyone ever contradicting it.

Nothing further is required to beat down our opponents’ insolence and to resolve the question without any difficulty. Yet when the meddlesome curiosity of learned men will not stop here, but descends to determine the particular methods and special avenues of legal sonship, many immediately entangle themselves in such snares of difficulties that—

—not only do some depart from others, but after much dispute the same men depart from themselves by retracting their own opinions. Although the most judicious men still consider the case undecided—whether their earlier or later judgment is more consonant with the truth—Gomarus, and Spanheim after him, certainly prefer the earlier opinion of both Augustine and Piscator to the one each embraced in his retractions.

We indeed think that most learned opinions here are not so absurd that they cannot be defended with tolerable probability, but none of them is so demonstrable as to compel anyone’s necessary assent, since even their clearest and most certain claims scarcely pass beyond the limits of conjecture.

The first and most widely received of these is that of Julius Africanus. In his letter to Aristides, cited by Eusebius in book 1, chapter 7 of his *History*, he teaches that he received his opinion from the Lord’s kinsmen called *desposynoi*, whom we suppose to have been Christians; why else would they have boasted of bearing the Lord Jesus’s name, kinship, and family? Africanus makes Joseph not merely Eli’s legal son but descends to the particular mode of his sonship, identifying the law laid down by Moses in Numbers 36:7 and Deuteronomy 25:5 concerning levirate marriage, peculiar to the Jews. By it, when a brother died without children, lest his memory perish among God’s people and his inheritance be alienated to outsiders—whether to another tribe or to another family of the same tribe—his brother was ordered to marry the dead man’s wife and to attribute to his brother the first son born from her, although the child was his own by nature, so that he might succeed to all his rights as a legitimate son. Africanus says he learned from the *desposynoi* that this happened in Joseph’s case: Joseph’s fathers Jacob and Eli were brothers; Eli married and died without children, but Jacob married Eli’s wife according to the law and by her had Joseph, who was Jacob’s natural son but his brother’s legitimate son. Africanus further says that he learned from the *desposynoi* how Jacob and Eli had become brothers. The mother of both was Estha—a woman, Greek *gynē Estha*, from the Hebrew *ishah*, as Casaubon correctly notes, though Aquinas wrongly read *Jesca*. We could readily forgive that blemish in Aquinas’s most barbarous age, when Greek was not understood and Hebrew was not read; but one is disgusted by the perversity of the Jesuits a Lapide and others, who in this light of learning choose to persist in an obvious error and even today write *Jesca*. Melchi, a descendant from Nathan’s family, had married this Estha and by her had the son Eli. When he died, Matthan from Solomon’s family married his widow Estha, and she bore him the son Jacob. Thus Eli and Jacob, although born of different fathers and different families, became maternal half-brothers, and the right of levirate marriage belonged to them as true brothers.

If Julius Africanus did not devise this opinion, he was at least the first to spread it, as Suárez teaches in his discussion of Thomas, part 3, question 27, article 1, disputation 2, section 3. It is not true that Justin Martyr wrote the same thing before Africanus. We acknowledge that Justin was some years older than Africanus, but the treatise *Questions of the Gentiles to the Orthodox*, in which the present opinion is found, cannot be Justin’s and must be much later than both him and Africanus, since Origen and the Manichaean heresy are expressly mentioned in it, as Bellarmine himself acknowledges in *On Ecclesiastical Writers*. Almost all the Greek and Latin fathers followed Africanus’s opinion. Augustine long pursued a different path because Africanus’s writings reached him late; but as soon as he read Africanus’s letter to Aristides, he retracted his former opinion and confidently followed Africanus with the rest, as he professes in book 1, chapter 7 of his *Retractions*. The opinion is indeed rejected as wholly useless and improbable in *Questions on the Old and New Testament*, but learned men of every party have long agreed that although this work appears among Augustine’s writings, it is not Augustine’s.

More recent theologians, especially the scholastics after Aquinas, part 3, question 31, follow the same path. Some Protestants indeed took a different path. For this reason the very learned Jesuit Maldonatus, on Matthew 1:16, rises up not only against Calvin and Beza by name in his margin, but also against the whole Protestant race as heretics, atheists, men weary of the old gospel, and fabricators of a new one. Certainly, if it had occurred to the angry Jesuit that in abandoning Africanus here the Protestants had as their leaders, companions, and adherents the chief Roman Catholics—indeed, the chief Jesuits—he would have restrained his undeserved fury against us. Suárez, in the place just cited, professes that he stands with that party and affirms that Cajetan, Jansenius, the Carthusian, Arias Montanus, Guaguerus, Galatinus, Driedo, Cano, Soto, Erasmus, and others agree with him. Cornelius a Lapide joins them and cites several of his own writers on Luke 3:23. Among our writers Gomarus, in chapter 5 of *On Christ’s Genealogy*, argues against Africanus’s opinion and cites Aretius, Chemnitz, Illyricus, Chytraeus, and others on his behalf; but the most learned Spanheim attacks Africanus more clearly and accurately than anyone. Let us now examine his arguments, taken from Suárez, Gomarus, and others, with a few of his own added, as they appear in part 1, doubt 22, paragraph 28 of his *Gospel Doubts*.

—yet nothing prevents human credence from being given to the history handed down by Africanus, as to many other narratives in ecclesiastical history. Nor are the particular opinions of Spanheim or others here any more written down and expressly stated in Scripture than Africanus’s.

Second. Africanus’s account, he says, is inaccurate, because it makes Melchi, Estha’s husband, Eli’s immediate father and third in descent from Joseph, whereas Luke makes Melchi fifth from Joseph and establishes Eli’s father not as Melchi but as Matthat, who was Levi’s son and Melchi’s grandson. Reply. I acknowledge that this is a serious argument worthy of careful consideration. I answer first that, granting Africanus suffered a lapse of memory here by writing Melchi for Matthat, an error in this readily corrected circumstance does not undermine the substance and foundation of the opinion. That foundation is that Jacob and Eli were true brothers, one Joseph’s natural father and the other his legal father; that they had a common mother and different fathers, one from Solomon’s line and the other from Nathan’s. Whether Eli’s father was Matthat or Melchi indeed affects the truth of sacred history but has little, if anything, to do with the present opinion, for it is preserved equally well through Matthat as through Melchi. Second, whatever error exists here in striking Levi and Matthat from Christ’s genealogy should be imputed not so much to Africanus as to either the age or place in which he lived. I certainly think Spanheim argues rightly and forcefully against the excessive boldness of Cordesius, canon of Limoges, which Hugo Grotius rashly appears to excuse, if not defend, on Luke 3:23. Cordesius insists that copyists inserted an error into Luke’s original text when in verse 24 they inserted Matthat and Levi between Eli and Melchi, and he orders it corrected by deleting those two names. This conclusion of Cordesius must be banished by everyone who professes the perfection of sacred Scripture. It cannot be denied, however, that these two names were omitted in various ancient manuscripts of the best fathers, as Vossius showed at length before Cordesius on page 22 of his *Disquisition on Christ’s Lineage*. Spanheim’s replies to the testimonies of Irenaeus, Ambrose, Jerome, Eusebius, and other fathers certainly scarcely weaken the premise of Cordesius’s argument, namely that the sacred copies of many fathers did not read Matthat and Levi at Luke 3:24. But whatever may be said of the premise, with Spanheim we most willingly condemn the inference: that today’s sacred text should be corrected to match that reading of those ancients, since Matthat and Levi are read distinctly in every manuscript copy now extant. Third, following Bede and Euthymius long ago on Luke 3, most who follow Africanus’s opinion today easily break the force of this argument by correcting Africanus’s error concerning the person. For Melchi read Matthat; or, as Bede has it, Matthat had two names and was also called Melchi. Spanheim and Gomarus are compelled to assert the same thing, without any Scripture, of his son Eli, whom they wish also to have been called Eliakim or Joakim. In this way Spanheim’s most serious argument becomes trivial and nothing.

Spanheim’s third argument: although by Africanus’s account the right of levirate marriage would have belonged to Jacob and Levi [the printed text reads *Levi*] as true brothers, no right of this kind applied to their fathers, Estha’s husbands. Although they were from the same tribe, their families were separated by a great interval, one coming from Solomon’s house and the other from Nathan’s; moreover, a widow among the Jews was bound to marry her husband’s nearest male relative. Reply. No one ever claimed a right of levirate marriage in Estha’s marriage—that after Melchi died once Jacob had been born, Matthat married Estha. That happened by the parties’ free choice. Nor did any law or custom among the Jews, or any other nation, compel a widow who had borne an heir, and a son at that, to her former husband to marry her husband’s nearest male relative.

Fourth. No good reason can be given why Luke traced so long a line of the descent of Joseph’s legal family, when Joseph was only Christ’s supposed father, while omitting Mary’s line, although she was Christ’s true mother. Reply. 1. This argument is very slippery: to deny or doubt a fact because its reasons and advantages cannot immediately be shown. 2. A host of ancient and modern writers who assert that each evangelist terminates his genealogy at Joseph gives many reasons for this divine fact, which there is no need to repeat. 3. Everyone denies that Mary’s genealogy is omitted, but holds that it is included in the family of Joseph, her nearest male relative. 4. Spanheim wrongly rejects the reason offered by Pseudo-Justin. Although it does not apply to the whole series, it is enough if it is suitably applied to the last degrees of the series, for which alone it was intended. It seems to do that beautifully: for how could Christ’s being given to Joseph as a legal son from his wife Mary, without any act of his own, be more suitably illustrated than by the similar case—though it does not correspond in every respect—in which Joseph himself was likewise given by his mother to her legal husband Eli, without any act of Eli’s, according to the laws of Israel?

Besides these four, Spanheim presents nine other arguments against Africanus’s opinion in paragraph 12 of the same *Disquisition*. I shall touch on only two of them, the seventh and ninth, passing over the rest because they either coincide with the former arguments or are of less importance. The seventh is this. When according to the laws of levirate marriage one son has two brothers as fathers, one as natural father and the other as legal—

—father, those two brothers have one father, and their son has one and the same grandfather. But nothing like this appears in Joseph, because his two fathers Jacob and Eli not only have different fathers but entirely different ancestors all the way to David. Reply. The argument holds for paternal half-brothers, but by no means for those who are only maternal half-brothers, such as Africanus supposes Jacob and Eli to be.

The ninth is this. Africanus proposes his opinion in timid and hesitant language. Reply. Among the wise, modesty has never harmed an argument, nor has bombastic boldness helped one. Africanus and all his followers, as Spanheim acknowledges, profess that they regard their opinion as more suitable than all others for solving the present knot. Spanheim’s great words about the absurdities of others and his own demonstrations, along with other rhetorical flowers of the same flour, certainly do not make his different opinion any more suitable.

From what has been said, I think, it is sufficiently clear that the objections brought by more recent Protestants or Roman Catholics against the opinion of Africanus and the ancients are not so weighty that it must necessarily be abandoned. Nor do the moderns bring demonstrations for their particular determinations that impose on anyone the necessity of assent. Spanheim offers two methods that greatly please him, especially the second. Let us now consider whether this pleasure arises more from mere choice than from any solid reasons.

Both methods agree that Luke does not recount Joseph’s genealogy but Mary’s. Once this is granted, there will be no appearance of contradiction between the evangelists, for in Matthew Jacob is Joseph’s father, while in Luke Eli is not called Joseph’s father; rather, Eli himself and his ancestors all the way to Nathan must be referred to Mary alone, whose true and natural father Eli was. They explain this in two ways. First, Joseph is called Eli’s son because he was his son-in-law. Second, the parenthesis in verse 23 is extended to all these words, “who was, as was supposed, the son of Joseph,” so that Eli is connected in no way with Joseph but with Christ alone, Eli’s grandson through his daughter Mary.

Spanheim supports the former by various reasons in paragraph 24 of the place cited above, all of which Baldwin and Walaeus excerpt word for word on Luke 3:23. This opinion has the impostor Annius as its author, as Suárez rightly observes, and Erasmus before him. John Calvin rejects it—I shall not say with anger, but nevertheless, as is his habit, very gravely—for this particularly weighty reason, among others: it excludes Christ from the line and succession of Solomon and all the kings of Judah. Gomarus and Spanheim reply that Calvin acknowledges Solomon’s line became extinct in Ahaziah, but this has nothing to do with the present matter. What concerns Calvin here is not Christ’s natural succession but his legal succession to the kings of Judah. No room whatever remains for it if Mary is referred by Luke’s line to Nathan in such a way that she had no right in Joseph’s natural ancestors, the kings of Judah named by Matthew, and therefore Christ had no lawful share in David’s throne or Judah’s scepter. Although Spalato affirms this, almost all Roman Catholics and Protestants deny it, affirming that Shiloh, Jesus Christ through his mother Mary, was not only David’s son according to the flesh but also the lawful successor of Solomon and the kings of Judah.

Spanheim’s first reason for his former method, which he professes to take from Galatinus, is this. All spouses are one flesh; therefore one spouse’s parent can rightly be called the other’s parent, and consequently Mary’s father is rightly called Joseph’s father. Reply. In chapter 12 of his seventh book Galatinus brings forward so many foolish fables from “Rabbonu Haccadosh” and “Rabbi Haccanas,” entirely fictitious writers, as Casaubon rightly notes, that he has lost all authority on this subject. As for the argument, it does not follow that because a son-in-law can in some sense, and sometimes is, called his father-in-law’s son, the matter therefore stands so at Luke 3:23. There is no inference from possibility to actuality, especially here and now, above all since the foundation of the inference has plainly been removed. These spouses certainly never became one flesh; here there is an exception to the general rule. Although every marriage was instituted so that spouses should be one flesh, in the marriage of Mary and Joseph it was expressly ordered otherwise by the will of the supreme lawgiver.

Spanheim’s second reason from Galatinus is Genesis 37:35. Jacob’s daughters-in-law are called his daughters, for otherwise Jacob had only one daughter, Dinah. Therefore Joseph, Eli’s son-in-law, can be called Eli’s son. Reply. There is no necessity in the inference, as has already been said, unless from what happened once or twice you conclude that the same thing not only can happen but always and necessarily does happen. Moreover, Gomarus concedes that the premise can be denied, because Jacob’s “daughters” may properly be explained as his granddaughters, namely his sons’ daughters, as well as his daughters-in-law.

The third argument is from Ruth 1, where Naomi’s daughters-in-law are called her daughters. Reply. In that same chapter they are called not simply Naomi’s daughters but also her daughters-in-law three times. Thus, if the matter stood so in Luke, the case would be settled—if Joseph were called Eli’s son-in-law either there or anywhere in Scripture. Gomarus confirms the preceding argument from Esther 1, where Esther is called Mordecai’s daughter. Reply. Spanheim prudently neglected this confirmation, for the ready answer was that the manner in which Esther was Mordecai’s daughter is expressly stated there: she was his paternal cousin, whom he raised with a father’s affection—

—toward her.

Spanheim’s fourth argument. In ordinary speech every son-in-law is called his father-in-law’s son. Therefore the person called Eli’s son in Luke 3:23 was his son-in-law. Reply. It is an open non sequitur from particular cases to a particular case. In Scripture the designation “son” often arises from the affection that a superior has, or ought to have, for an inferior. Thus Joseph calls Benjamin his son, Joshua likewise calls Achan his son, David calls himself Nabal’s son, and the prophets’ disciples are often called their sons. Were they therefore natural sons, sons-in-law, or grandsons?

Fifth. Matthew says that Jacob begot Joseph. Luke, however, does not say that Eli begot Joseph but that Joseph was Eli’s son. Reply. The only thing this purports to prove is that Joseph was not Eli’s natural son but was his son in some manner other than nature. The question, however, is quite different—namely, whether Joseph was Eli’s son-in-law—and nothing of the kind is proved by this argument. 2. The author of this argument is Africanus, whom Gomarus and Spanheim reject here. 3. Augustine, who borrowed this very argument from Africanus, acknowledges that it is not sound. Certainly Grotius in his writings, and Gerard Vossius before him, forcefully demonstrate that the Greek verbs for begetting, child-producing, and the Hebrew *yalad* are properly used of both legal and natural generations, and that adoptive fathers are properly called begetters just as natural fathers are. Thus Julius Caesar is called the begetter of Augustus, and Augustus the begetter of Germanicus, by classical authors; and in sacred Scripture the legal sons of legal fathers are often called their seed, stock, or offspring. But what seems to me the principal point and to settle the matter completely—though both Grotius and Vossius omitted it—is that God is often said to beget his adopted sons, and they to be regenerated by him. Thus the argument from the word “beget,” although Gomarus and Spanheim repeat it until they are hoarse, appears to contribute nothing whatever to the present conclusion.

The other opinion is that the parenthesis in Luke 3:23 must be extended beyond the words “as was supposed” to the following words also, “the son of Joseph.” In this way Eli will be referred to Christ alone as his grandson, and by no means to Joseph as his son-in-law, since Joseph is included in the parenthesis and therefore excluded from every relation made in that passage. I find this opinion cited by Suárez and not disapproved, but rejected by Maldonatus and a Lapide; it is nevertheless approved by our writers Junius, Gomarus, and Diodati and extolled above all by Spanheim. I offer the following considerations to show why I judge it less necessary than the former opinions and indeed less probable.

First. This invention comes, at least in large part, from the author of *Questions on the Old and New Testament* in volume 4 of Augustine, who was the first to refer Christ immediately to Eli. But this author, as Gomarus himself acknowledges, is an impure Arian, and for that reason all his inventions please me less.

Second. Just as this corrupt author refers Christ immediately to Eli, so he also refers the same Christ immediately to Joseph, who stands below Eli; indeed, he refers him immediately to each and every person above Eli all the way to Adam, which both Gomarus and Spanheim profess to be a foolish and absurd invention.

Third. Extending the parenthesis so that Joseph is included in it corrupts the original text, for in all Greek manuscripts and printed editions the parenthesis contains nothing but these words alone, “as was supposed.”

Fourth. Suppose the punctuation of the original text could be changed at the pleasure of scholars today. I do not think this should be conceded, lest too broad a door be opened for curious and perverse minds to twist many passages of Scripture away from their genuine purpose according to different arrangements of punctuation. In any event, the subject matter does not admit this extension of the parenthesis, for in this way the substantive noun “son,” which governs all the following genitive cases—son of Eli, son of Matthat, son of Levi, and so forth—would be excluded not only from that verse but from all those that follow. The word “being” and every other word enclosed in the parenthesis is excluded from the rest of the sentence, which is normally and necessarily complete without them.

Furthermore, if the word “son” must be supplied in this way—“Jesus, being the son of Eli”—how harsh is this either to hear or understand? According to this supplying of the word, the text would have to be read, “Jesus, being the son (as was supposed, the son of Joseph) of Eli”—an extremely harsh and obscure expression. To extricate himself from this, Gomarus invented an entirely new meaning of the adverb *hōs*. He does not wish, as every mortal before him did, to explain *hōs* here as “as,” but adversatively as “while”: not “as was supposed” but “while it was supposed.” I do not wish to examine the passages he cites where he thinks *hōs* should be accepted in this new sense, since Spanheim himself does not depart here from the received usage, nor, so far as I understand, does anyone else follow Gomarus here.

I add this. If the participle “being” is joined with the preceding “began,” as in the Antwerp Polyglot, the Old Latin, and many other Bibles—though not in our common editions or the recent London Bibles—extending the parenthesis will produce an entirely crippled sense, for there would be no noun to govern the genitive “of Eli” or to connect Jesus with Eli.

—the opinion would require, as Gomarus rightly notes, an expressed verb answering to “was supposed,” namely “was, however,” or something similar, so that it would read in this manner: “Jesus Christ, as was supposed, was Joseph’s son; he was, however, Eli’s son.”

The eighth argument against this opinion is that, like the preceding one, it excludes both Mary and Christ according to the flesh from Solomon and all the kings of Judah, and from every right to David’s throne or scepter. In this scheme that right would belong to Joseph alone, and by no means to Mary.

The ninth places Mary in a family too distant from Joseph’s. As Spanheim himself counts, David was the forty-first ancestor from Mary. Grant that in David’s day David’s family had been reduced to very few, and Jesse’s root to a few withering branches. It is nevertheless scarcely credible that the number was so very small that no one more closely related to Mary could be found than Joseph, whose ancestry did not unite with hers until they ascended more than forty degrees to David himself.

I shall stop with the tenth. This opinion makes the first degree in Christ’s genealogy unlike all the rest. In all the others there is a connection of son with father; in the first, of grandson with grandfather—Jesus with Eli. I acknowledge that in Matthew’s line not only is a grandson joined with his grandfather but also a great-grandson with his great-grandfather. This is done both to preserve Matthew’s express scheme of three groups of fourteen and because sacred Scripture elsewhere plainly teaches that those whom Matthew calls sons were really great-grandsons or descendants in the third degree. Nothing like this appears in Luke, and nothing similar should be affirmed without necessity.

Spanheim offers the following arguments for his opinion. First, nothing in the text is forced by it, but everything flows plainly. Reply. I have just said how everything is forced by it and how both the words and the sense oppose it.

Second. Parentheses of this kind are customary, and sons are frequently put for grandsons. Reply. These are either assumptions of the point at issue or say nothing against us. Who doubts that parentheses are very frequent in every kind of discourse? But extending parentheses beyond the bounds of the original texts, in order to confuse both words and sense, is not customary and appears in no approved author. It is readily granted that a grandson may sometimes stand for a son, but no one will grant it in a catalogue where, in more than eighty degrees, a son never stands for a grandson, unless convinced by an effective argument, and none appears here.

Third. Although the Syriac interpreter does not mark the parenthesis, he nevertheless reads, “and he was supposed to be the son of Joseph.” These words cohere as if enclosed in a parenthesis and cannot be separated. Reply. If Spanheim wished to stand by the judgment of the Syriac, Arabic, or Ethiopic interpreter, his case would immediately fall. For all of them, as may be seen in the recent London Bibles, refer Joseph to Eli as his father more expressly than either the Greek or Latin. The fact that the words “and he was supposed to be the son of Joseph” cohere does not help the matter, for the words cohere no less with our parenthesis. “Jesus was (as was supposed) the son of Joseph” and “Jesus was supposed to be the son of Joseph” are one and the same. The whole question, however, is whether Jesus, with Joseph passed over, is referred immediately to Eli, or whether Joseph is referred to Eli as Eli is to Matthat. We affirm that the Syriac and all the other eastern interpreters do the latter most expressly, as will be evident to anyone who looks.

Fourth is the authority of some Protestants and Roman Catholics. Reply. All these appear merely to have followed Junius, as they did more than once elsewhere. But if the matter were to be decided by authorities, far more and greater men undoubtedly stand for the former opinions than for this new and final opinion of Spanheim.

I shall vex this question no further. I end as I began: the general answer concerning a natural and legal father is satisfactory. If one descends to greater particularity, nothing well-founded and nothing necessary is found; conjectures and probabilities alone fill the whole page. Among them the opinion of the ancients is not so absurd that it cannot be defended suitably enough, nor have the positions of any of the moderns yet been so demonstrated that many objections cannot be raised, on account of which any sensible person would claim the freedom to suspend judgment.
