##### On the Limbo of the Fathers

30. Bell.: *That the souls of the godly were in heaven before Christ's
ascension is a position altogether unheard of in the Church of God.* Prot.
Even if this position were unheard of, it ought not on that account to be
rejected by the Roman Catholics, who defend various positions entirely
unheard of for six whole centuries after Christ, as our Jewell has clearly
demonstrated against Harding in twenty-seven articles. 2. What was heard in
former times about the highest heaven is not of much importance in this case,
unless it is demonstrated that godly souls were detained in a subterranean
hell. 3. Nevertheless, it is not unheard of, either in the Scriptures or in
the Fathers, that before Christ's advent the souls of the godly were in
Paradise, in Abraham's bosom, in the kingdom of heaven, and in a blessed
state. The Scriptures also speak of certain ones *existing in the heavens*
when they were reconciled through Christ's blood, Colossians 1:20.

31. Bell.: *Genesis 37. Jacob and Joseph went down to hell.* Prot. That is,
they died, as was proved above.

32. Bell.: *Luke 16. There was only a chasm, nothing solid, between the place
of the condemned and Abraham's bosom. Both souls were therefore in the same
abyss, although far apart from one another.* Prot. There is absolutely nothing
solid in this sophistry. To say nothing of Bellarmine's departure here from
the common interpretation, *chaos:* 1. Something solid is often found within
a hollow—as the earth is situated within the vault, or as it were the hollow,
of heaven.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 113 | printed p. 103 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

2. Neither Abraham nor the condemned are said to have been in the chasm; the
chasm was between them. It therefore also follows that they were not in the
same abyss. 3. If nothing solid lies between the hell of the condemned and the
limbo of the Fathers, how was the fire of hell restrained so that neither its
flame nor its smoke ascended all the way to that limbo, to the Fathers' great
distress? A miracle must certainly be invented here—and gratuitously, without
any necessity arising from the matter. 4. *Chasm is not taken properly here,*
says the Roman Catholic Jansen on this passage, *but metaphorically signifies
an impassable crossing.* Or, as Cajetan puts it, *an inaccessibility secured
on both sides.* Or, as the Jesuit Emanuel Sa notes, *the greatest interval and
an obstructed way.* More particularly, Jansen adds, it signifies *God's most
firm decree forbidding the mingling of the one group with the other.* Or, as
Cajetan expresses it, *the immutability of each state, established and made
firm.* On this passage Maldonatus cites Chrysostom, Augustine, Bede,
Theophylact, and Euthymius as holding the same position.

33. Bell.: *1 Samuel 28:13–14. Samuel's soul ascended from the earth.* Prot.
It was not Samuel's soul, which could not be summoned by magical arts as this
shade admits about itself in verse 15, but a deception of Satan.

34. Bell.: *1 Peter 3:19. The spirits of the righteous are said to have been
in prison.* Prot. It is not righteous spirits who are designated there, but
rebels, verse 20.

35. Bell.: *Zechariah 9:11. The saints are said to be prisoners in a pit in
which there was no water.* Prot. 1. Abraham's bosom cannot be understood by
this pit, because in that pit there was no water—that is, there was great
misery without remedy, as appears from Genesis 37:24 and Jeremiah 38:6. But in
Abraham's bosom there is abundant water of refreshment and consolation, Luke
16:25, so much so that the condemned rich man asked for water from that bosom,
verse 24. Bell.: *The depth of the Babylonian captivity cannot be understood
here by the pit, because these words contain an address to Christ, and those
who had been prisoners are said to be delivered in the blood of the
covenant.* Prot. 1. It is evident from the use of the feminine gender that
this speech is directed not to Christ but to the Church.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 114 | printed p. 104 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Arias Montanus observed this as well. 2. They are not said to have been
delivered by blood, but rather covenanted by blood, in view of the history
found in Exodus 24:8.

##### On Christ's Descent

36. Bell.: *Psalm 107:16. He broke the bronze gates and shattered the iron
bars.* Prot. Bellarmine himself answers for us: *This passage does not prove
that Christ truly descended into hell. For it is certain that, according to
the letter, it treats of deliverance from Egypt.* Bell.: *It is nevertheless
understood in this way by Hilary and Ambrose, in a mystical sense.* Prot. If
some applied the same expression to Christ, a new mystical sense is not
thereby proved. Furthermore, Bellarmine laid down this rule in *On the Word of
God,* book 3, chapter 3: *Effective arguments must be sought from the literal
sense of Scripture alone.*

37. Bell.: *Matthew 12. The Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth.*
Prot. 1. It is sufficiently well known that in the Scriptures *heart*
metaphorically denotes any midpoint, or a part of the midpoint, lying between
extremes. Thus Tyre is said to be situated in the heart of the sea, Ezekiel
27:4, although it was not far removed from the shore. In this sense Christ was
in the heart of the earth while he was in the grave. So Rabanus, in Thomas's
*Golden Chain:* *Jonah was in the belly of the sea creature for three days
and nights, and Christ in the grave.* So Jansen on this passage: *Jonah
prefigured Christ for three days and nights in the grave.* So the Jesuit
Emanuel Sa: *Some doctors understand “in the heart” to mean in the middle, or
within—that is, the grave.* Chrysostom also gives a distinctive reason why
this expression is understood in this way: *He did not say “in the earth,”
but “in the heart of the earth,” both to indicate the grave and so that no one
might suspect merely the appearance of death.* Scripture everywhere, as well
as the Apostles' Creed, also opposes Christ's resurrection to his death and
burial for three days, 1 Corinthians 15:4. Bell.: *Christ's grave appears to
have been above the earth.* Prot. It does not so appear to those who attend to
these scriptural expressions: *It was hollowed within a rock; a very great
stone was rolled against its entrance; in John, he stooped down to look in;
Peter went down.* Bell.: *Just as Jonah was alive in the belly of the sea
creature, so something living ought to have been in the heart of the earth.*
Prot.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 115 | printed p. 105 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Thus Christ's body too ought to have been in hell—and an animate body, also
clothed rather than naked, and an intact rather than crucified body—because
Jonah was in that condition in the belly of the sea creature, if likeness is
required in every respect. But when Justin Martyr, question 64, was asked how
the dead Christ could have been prefigured by the living Jonah, he answered
that *death is not compared with death, but a stay of three days with a stay
of three days.*

38. Bell.: *Acts 2:27. You will not leave my soul in hell.* Prot. We answer,
1. in Peter's own express words in verse 31: *David spoke of Christ's
resurrection.* Christ, moreover, rose from death, verse 24, and with respect
to his body—which is what is properly raised—from the grave. 2. By a manifest
inference from verse 29: the place and state in which Christ was not left was
one and the same as that in which David is said to have been left—otherwise,
as they say, there would be four terms in the Apostle's syllogism. But the
state in which David was left was nothing other than death, and the place
nothing other than the grave, verse 29. 3. We also argue from the force of the
expression itself and the aim of the Psalm from which it is cited: not to
leave is to deliver; to deliver is to deliver from evil and misery. But Christ
suffered no misery in the limbo of the Fathers, but in death and the grave.
Bell. 1. *ψυχὴ signifies nothing but the soul properly, and ᾅδης nothing but
hell.* Prot. If these points are granted, nothing will follow from them unless
he also shows that words in the Scriptures are employed only properly.
Nevertheless, it is utterly false when he says that ᾅδης never signifies the
grave. For, besides what has already been said about this matter, it can
signify nothing else in Psalm 141:7 according to the Greek interpreters:
*διεσκορπίσθη τὰ ὀστᾶ παρὰ τὸν ᾅδην: the bones are scattered—not beside the
lower regions, but beside the grave.* Genebrard himself renders *Sheol* there
as *grave.* Although ψυχὴ also properly signifies the soul, it can
nevertheless be taken synecdochically for the person and metonymically for the
body, just as the Hebrew word *Nephesh* is frequently employed in Scripture.
Bell. 2. *Even if the body could be understood by the soul, it is not thereby
immediately proved that it ought to be expounded that way in this passage.*
Prot. We do not adduce this to prove that point,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 116 | printed p. 106 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

but to answer the objection. It nevertheless proves that, because the word
*soul* is employed in this passage, the soul itself in its essence is not
necessarily understood unless other reasons compel it—something Bellarmine
does not show. Bell. 3. *The soul is distinguished here from the flesh when it
is said that neither was the soul left in hell nor did the flesh see
corruption.* Prot. The soul is no more distinguished from the flesh in verse
31 than it is from God's Holy One in verse 27 and Psalm 16:10. That both
designate one and the same thing in reality appears from Acts 13:35, where one
is put in place of both, and from Acts 2:20, where only one thing is affirmed
of David so that both expressions may be affirmed of Christ in verse 31.
Bell. 4. *Soul is never taken for a corpse, since in a corpse there is the
privation of life; nor can we say that life is in the grave, since a lifeless
body is there.* Prot. 1. A little later Bellarmine himself expressly admits
that in Genesis 37 and Numbers 19:11 *soul* signifies body and flesh. A body
without life, such as is denoted in Leviticus 21:11 and Numbers 19:11, is a
corpse. 2. Life can be said to be in the grave in the sense in which *glory*
is said to dwell *in the dust,* and *life* to be trampled *in the earth,*
Psalm 76. Bell. 5. *Usage does not admit a synecdoche in which one part is put
for another part.* Prot. It is enough for us if the soul as a part is put here
for the whole—that is, for Christ, who with respect to his body was in the
grave.

39. Bell.: *There is a great difference between Nephesh and ψυχὴν. For
Nephesh, without any trope, signifies not only the soul but also an animal and
even a body. Hence it also almost always has the adjective “living” joined to
it, since it signifies something indifferent to living and not living. But
the Greek word ψυχὴ, and the Latin “anima,” does not have such a general
signification. Furthermore, soul is here opposed to flesh: neither soul nor
flesh.* Prot. Bellarmine here shows that he has been reduced to desperate
straits: in order to break through, he has no regard for what he says or what
sort of thing it is. For 1. he says there is a great difference between
*Nephesh* and ψυχὴν, although throughout the Old Testament the seventy
translators—with the exception of six or seven passages—

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 117 | printed p. 107 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

consistently rendered that Hebrew word with this Greek word. Nor could anyone
after them find either another Greek word which corresponded so aptly to that
Hebrew word, or another Hebrew word which equaled that Greek word. 2. He says
that *Nephesh* signifies soul, animal, and body without any trope, although in
reality there is no Hebrew word which has several significations without a
trope, nor is there such a word in other languages unless something of the
kind happens by chance—much less can part and whole, subject and attribute, be
signified by the same word without a trope. 3. He does not shrink from saying
that *Nephesh almost always has the adjective “living” joined to it,* although
this is found very rarely, except in the creation account; and there too, in
the translation of the Seventy, the Greek word ψυχὴ occurs with the same
addition, just as it does in the Apostle, 1 Corinthians 15:45, ψυχὴν ζῶσαν.
4. He dreams about *Nephesh* what all logic denies—namely, that it signifies
something indifferent to living and not living. For living and not living are
contradictories and therefore encompass in their scope all beings and
nonbeings; they cannot have anything indifferent or third. 5. He insists that
the particle *neither* necessarily implies an opposition, although in six
hundred passages it is added as a harmonious reinforcement, as in Hebrews
13:5: *I will by no means leave you, neither will I forsake you.*

40. Bell.: *Romans 10:7. Who will descend into the abyss? That is, to bring
Christ back from the dead. This shows that after death Christ was in the
abyss, which is extremely far from heaven—that is, in the deepest parts of the
earth.* Prot. 1. *Into the abyss* in Paul is one and the same as *across the
sea* in Moses, Deuteronomy 30:13. But *across the sea* does not signify hell;
therefore neither does *into the abyss.* 2. To ascend into heaven and to
descend into the sea or abyss means nothing other in this passage—as in Moses,
Deuteronomy 30:11—than vainly to seek a hidden or distant thing. 3. Paul
himself, in his application, applies it to bringing Christ back from the dead;
he says nothing about limbo. 4. In that expression, *to descend into the sea
or abyss,* attention is paid not to the place's distance from heaven but to
its distance from us. To go into heaven and the sea is to search far and wide.

41. Bell.: *Ephesians 4:9. He descended into the lower parts*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 118 | printed p. 108 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*of the earth.* Prot. That is, into the lower part of the world, which is the
earth. That this is the genuine sense appears from the Apostle's argument: he
proves this descent from the statement in the Psalm that he ascended on high.
But a descent into the limbo of hell cannot be proved from an ascent, because
he could at least have ascended from the earth on high even if he had never
been at the center of the earth. Bell. 1. *The lower part of the earth
signifies one part of the earth which is lower than the others.* Prot. It
signifies one part of the world which is lower than the others, Isaiah 44:23.
Cajetan also takes it this way in this passage: *More plainly, “of the earth”
is understood as an apposition determining “the lower parts.” For the lowest
part of the world is the earth, in distinction from the lower parts of
heaven, which are in the air.* Bell. 2. *What follows supports us: “that he
might fill all things”—that is, that he might be present and visit all parts
of his kingdom.* Prot. 1. The filling of all things in verse 10 is referred
not to Christ's descent but to his ascent. 2. *To fill all things* means, as
Cajetan and Emanuel Sa judge, *all things that were promised and written
concerning him;* or rather, according to the context, it means—as Dionysius
the Carthusian has it—*that he might fill every kind of human being, that is,
some from every kind of human being, with the gifts and graces of the Holy
Spirit.*

42. Bell.: *1 Peter 3:19. In which, coming, he preached to those spirits who
were in prison, who had once been unbelieving while they awaited God's
patience in the days of Noah. And 4:6: For this reason the gospel was preached
even to the dead, that they might indeed be judged according to man in the
flesh, but live according to God in the spirit.* Prot. There is nothing in
these words from which Christ's local descent into hell to lead the souls of
the Fathers out of limbo can be proved. For 1. the prison treated here
signifies the place and state of those who are kept in chains of darkness for
condemnation, 2 Peter 2:4. It is therefore not Abraham's bosom. 2. The
preaching mentioned tended toward repentance and conversion, for this is what
it is to be judged in the flesh and to live in the spirit, 1 Peter 4:6, just
as in 1 Corinthians 5:5 the same end of excommunication is declared through
*the destruction of the flesh and the life of the Spirit.* But after this
life there is no place for repentance and conversion in Abraham's bosom. 3.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 119 | printed p. 109 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

The separated soul did not discharge the office of preaching—such an office
of the soul is read of nowhere—but the Spirit of God and of Christ performed
this through Noah, *a preacher of righteousness,* as Moses indicates in
Genesis 6:3 and Peter himself explains in his second epistle, chapter 2, verse
5. 4. The persons to whom Christ is said to have preached through the Spirit
were unbelieving, disobedient, or rebellious, not righteous as the Fathers in
Abraham's bosom were. The sense therefore is that Christ, speaking through his
Spirit in the prophets, preached in former times, in the days of Noah, to
people who were then living as unbelievers and are now, on account of that
unbelief, condemned in the prison of hell. Bell. 1. *The common position of
the Fathers is to the contrary.* Prot. 1. We do not have every passage of
Scripture rightly and accurately explained by the Fathers, much less by their
common position and agreement. Otherwise, Cajetan says in his preface to the
books of Moses, *all hope of expounding Holy Scripture would be taken away
from later writers, except by transferring it, as they say, from a book into
a notebook.* 2. Augustine and Bede, as Bellarmine admits, depart from the
common interpretation and support us. To these can be added Jerome on Isaiah
45, and among more recent writers the Gloss, Lyra, Hugh, Dionysius the
Carthusian, and Thomas Aquinas himself. 3. The remaining Fathers agree neither
with Bellarmine nor among themselves in their exposition of this passage.
Bell. 2. *When Christ's Spirit is distinguished in opposition to the flesh,
it does not appear capable of signifying anything but the soul.* Prot. 1. In
Romans 1:3–4, Christ's Spirit, opposed to the flesh, signifies the divine
nature, not the human soul. In Hebrews 9:14, the same Spirit of Christ,
opposed to blood, denotes the same divine nature, not the human soul. In 1
Timothy 3:16 there is precisely the same opposition between Christ's Spirit
and flesh. Nor in truth can the other opposition agree with the expression of
Scripture, which by Christ's flesh everywhere signifies his whole human
nature. 2. What is said here cannot be understood of the soul, as Augustine
observes, because Christ is said to have been made alive by the Spirit, which
Paul rightly explains of divine power in 2 Corinthians 13:4. But it cannot in
any way be expounded of the soul, which neither was made alive nor had the
power to make the dead body alive. Bell.: *The soul is said to have been*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 120 | printed p. 110 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*made alive because it remained alive. For what is not killed is said to be
made alive,* 1 Samuel 27:9, 2 Samuel 8:2, Acts 7:19. Prot. 1. In these passages
the word Peter employs, ζωοποιηθεὶς, which presupposes death according to the
Apostle himself in 1 Corinthians 15:28, is not found, but other verbs which
signify preserving alive, ζωοποιεῖσθαι and ζωογονεῖσθαι. 2. None are said to be
preserved alive unless they are liable to and in danger of death, but the soul
is immortal by its nature. 3. The opposition in the text is between *being
put to death and being made alive.* Just as being put to death is not being
preserved in death, so being made alive is not being preserved in life.
Bell. 3. *The phrase “coming, he preached” cannot be taken of the divinity
except improperly, while in verse 22 it is taken properly.* Prot. 1. For this
objection to have force, Bellarmine ought to have proved that the word *having
gone* is always employed properly, or at least is so employed in this passage.
But nothing occurs more frequently in the Scriptures than for God to be said
improperly, by anthropopathism, to descend, ascend, come, or go here and there.
The soul itself is not properly said to go, πορεύεσθαι. 3. When Christ is
properly said to have gone in verse 22, it is understood of his person
according to the human nature, not of the soul alone. Nor should it seem
strange to Bellarmine that the same word is employed properly and improperly
in the same chapter, since he himself contends in *On Purgatory,* book 1,
chapter 5, that in one and the same sentence the term *fire* in 1 Corinthians
3 is taken once for judgment and once for true purgatorial fire. Bell. 4. *By
the spirits who were in prison the living cannot be understood except through
affected impropriety and obscurity. Add that in chapter 4, verse 6, the gospel
is said to have been preached to the dead.* Prot. 1. The Greek text does not
say *who were in prison,* but the participle οὖσιν is understood, and the
present tense properly denotes *existing in prison,* or those who *are now in
prison.* 2. There is no affected impropriety or obscurity in it if people
whose souls are in hell are called spirits existing in prison. 3. Just as in
chapter 4, verse 6, the gospel is said to have been preached to the dead not
when they were dead, but while they were in the flesh, *that they might be
condemned in the flesh,* so in this passage living people are called spirits
existing in

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 121 | printed p. 111 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

prison not while they were living but after death. For living people are
called spirits in prison by the same reasoning by which those living in the
flesh are called dead. Bell. 5. *If this passage is understood of preaching
done in the days of Noah, it is not apparent why the narrative was inserted.*
Prot. Although that fullness of spirit and speech exists in Peter's writings,
so that the sequence of his arrangement does not always appear except to a
highly intelligent and attentive reader, the continuation in this passage of
the same discourse he had previously begun concerning patience in doing good
and bearing evil can nevertheless be readily observed. For this narrative
sets forth Christ's patience, Noah's patience, the happy outcome for those who
possessed their souls in patience, and the destructive outcome for those who
abused the patience of God and men.

Prot. Augustine refutes the Roman Catholic explanation of this passage in
letter 99 with various arguments. 1. There is no reason why Christ would
preach in hell only to those who were unbelieving in Noah's time. 2. There was
no church in hell to be gathered by Christ's preaching. 3. Abraham's bosom,
in which the Fathers were, was exceedingly far removed from hell. Bell. 1.
*Nor is it apparent why Peter says that Christ preached in the days of Noah
rather than in the days of Abraham.* Prot. We have just shown a sufficient
reason: this example from Noah's days was especially suited to Peter's
argument concerning patience. Bell. 2. *Christ preached in hell to all the
good spirits, but those who were unbelieving in the days of Noah are expressly
named because there was greater doubt whether they were saved or not. Peter
indicates that this was true of some of them.* Prot. 1. Good spirits and
unbelieving spirits in the prison of hell are plainly opposed. 2. There was no
greater doubt whether Noah's contemporaries were saved or not than there was
about the two hundred and fifty and the many others who perished with Korah,
Dathan, and Abiram in Numbers 16, or about the children of Israel with whom
God was not pleased but whom he laid low in the wilderness on account of
their sins, 1 Corinthians 10:5. 3. If Peter had wished to indicate that some
of them repented at the end of their life,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 122 | printed p. 112 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

he would not have described them only in terms of disobedience and prison. 4.
The repentance of the dead treated in chapter 4, verse 6, is not an antecedent
reason for the preaching of the gospel, but follows as its effect: *Therefore
the dead too were evangelized so that they might be condemned in the flesh.*
But Bellarmine says that *they were condemned according to the flesh* in the
sense that *they were considered condemned by human judgment because their
bodies had been killed by the waters.* This is refuted in the same way from
Peter's own words. For Peter intends the condemnation of which he speaks to
be the end of the preaching—*therefore, so that*—which no one will affirm of
human judgment or estimation. Peter also says that they were to be condemned
in the flesh, while Bellarmine says that they were condemned by human judgment
because they had perished in the flesh. Who will reconcile these statements
with one another? Bell.: *Christ's preaching in hell was not for conversion,
but only for the announcement of great joy.* Prot. This invented preaching,
then, is not the preaching of which Peter treats in either chapter 3, verse
19, or chapter 4, verse 6, because no mention is made there of great joy
announced, but of preaching directed toward condemnation and life.

43. Bell.: *Christ's descent into hell is confirmed by the tradition of the
Fathers.* Prot. A descent such as Bellarmine defends cannot be confirmed from
the common agreement of the Fathers, as our Parker has clearly demonstrated.
For 1. various Fathers understand by the descent into hell nothing other than
the common state of death. 2. They describe the descent as penal and as a part
of humiliation. 3. They often call the deliverance of people from hell a
deliverance from the guilt and judgment of impending condemnation. 4. They
attribute this deliverance not to a local descent of the soul, but to the
suffering of death. 5. From those who teach a local descent of the soul,
nothing certain can be gathered either as to its destination or as to the end
and use of such a descent. Bellarmine also hesitates so much in defining these
things that he rightly renders his whole defense—not to say his conscience—in
this case suspect. For he says the following.

44. Bell. 1. *Christ did not descend into hell solely for the beatification of
souls, for that could have taken place without the descent, but also so that
he might remain in that place of souls*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 123 | printed p. 113 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*for three days, and for other causes as well, chapter 15.* 2. *Christ's soul
was at once in hell and paradise, whether the word paradise is taken
metaphorically or properly—that is, for an orchard. For it was not impossible
for God to bring it about that Christ's soul was in two places at once. Ibid.*
3. *It is indeed probable that the soul of Christ the Lord descended to all
the places of hell. Chapter 16.* 4. *Christ's descent conferred two great
benefits on the souls of the righteous: 1. essential beatitude; 2. a leading
out from that prison and a conducting into heaven. He furnished them the
first immediately and the second not long afterward. Ibid.* 5. *For the first
benefit there was no need of the descent, since it is the divinity which
properly beatifies, and it is everywhere. Ibid.* 6. *It is not credible that
any souls of infants who died with original sin were saved. Ibid.* 7. *It
would not be an error if someone said that all the souls in purgatory were
then delivered from purgatory by special grace, because the Lord is not bound
to the sacraments or to our merits. Ibid.*

Prot. 1. If the souls of the Fathers could have been beatified without
Christ's local descent into hell, then that descent was not necessary and is
therefore not the descent of which the Fathers speak. For Irenaeus, cited by
Bellarmine in chapter 14, says that in his descent Christ *observed the law of
the dead, so that he might be the firstborn from the dead.* Origen says that
he *descended for the salvation of the world.* Eusebius says that, *summoned
by the law of love,* he descended. Damascene says, *so that light might shine
on those who were under the earth in darkness, just as the sun of
righteousness rose upon the earth.* Tertullian says, *so that he might make
the Patriarchs and Prophets possessors of himself.* Hilary says, *the Lord did
not refuse the descent for the completion of a true man.* Philastrius says
that *by Christ's descent into hell they had merited pardon.* Gregory says
that *he descended into hell for man's redemption.* But Bellarmine gives no
certain reason for the descent except that Christ might remain there for
three days, although he has not denied that Christ could have remained in
paradise and did also remain there. According to Bellarmine, therefore, he
descended only so that he might descend, and remained there for three days
only so that he might remain there.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 124 | printed p. 114 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

2. If Christ's local descent was neither necessary for human salvation nor
can be defended without inventing so great a miracle—that by God's
omnipotence one and the same soul was properly and locally at once in heaven
and on earth—it is certainly not credible. For Bellarmine himself teaches in
this same chapter 15 that *the essence of a soul, since it is a particular and
finite thing, is necessarily present in one definite place and not another
unless it moves from place to place.* This is also Bellarmine's argument in
the same place against Durandus: *If he descended into hell only by his
effect, then at the same time Christ's soul was in many places at once.* He
quite plainly intimates that this can take place only by effect, but not
properly or in actual fact.

3. If it is probable that Christ's soul descended into all the places of
hell, including that of the condemned, then it is surely not probable that
Christ descended into hell without necessity merely to remain there. For to
remain in the fire of Gehenna which torments souls was not desirable for
Christ's soul. To this is also added what Medina testifies on Thomas, part 3,
question 52, article 2: *All theologians and all Christians, whose common
sense cannot be fallacious, hold that Christ's soul with respect to its
substance did not locally enter the hell of the condemned.* He also says that
this truth is effectively proved from 2 Corinthians 6, where it is said that
light has no fellowship with darkness, and he affirms that such an entrance
would be unseemly for Christ.

4. The local presence of Christ's soul did not confer essential beatitude on
the righteous Fathers. 1. Christ was the Lamb slain from the beginning of the
world, Revelation 13:8, so that the power of redemption through him was
equally efficacious in the faithful of every age for justification,
sanctification, and glorification. 2. When Durandus contends that the souls
necessarily ought to have been beatified at the very instant Christ died,
Bellarmine himself answers in chapter 15 that, once the price of redemption
was paid, they indeed ought to have been beatified *immediately,* but that
this *immediately* could be a certain very short period of time. But since in
God's reckoning that price of redemption was paid from the time the promise
concerning the woman's seed was made, and was effective before God as though
paid, and consequently

the souls of the faithful ought to have been beatified *immediately,* I hope
Bellarmine will not say that this *immediately* could have been after four
thousand years, as he necessarily has to say if Adam and Abel had to await all
beatitude until Christ's resurrection. For all who believe in Christ have
eternal life, John 5:24—essentially, that is, and incipiently. 3. It is
expressly said of the faithful before Christ's advent that in life they had
spiritual joy, John 8:56, and beatitude, Romans 4:3, 6, and after death peace,
that is, according to the Hebrew expression, blessedness, Isaiah 57, and
comfort set in opposition to the misery of the condemned, Luke 16:25. 4.
Christ's soul does not properly beatify by its presence, but the divinity
does, as Bellarmine admits.

5. Nor did that local presence confer on the souls of the righteous a leading
out from prison. For the centurion's faith, which did not depend on Christ's
local presence, is praised in Matthew 8. And in Matthew 27:52, immediately
upon Christ's death, many bodies of saints are said to have risen together
with their souls. Add also that the souls of the righteous could equally have
been led out of their prison—if they had one—without Christ's local presence,
just as other souls, if we believe the Roman Catholics, are ordinarily led
out of the prison of purgatory without such a leader going before them.

6. If it is not an error to say that at that time all souls were delivered
from purgatory by special grace, there is certainly no reason why those should
be charged with error who say that no faithful people, at least from that time
onward, go into purgatory. For the Lord's grace is just as special today as it
was formerly, and he is no more bound now to the sacraments or to our merits.

7. By the same special grace, the way into heaven lies open to the children
of the faithful who die without baptism, because the Lord is not bound to the
sacraments.
