“Nor can anyone say,” Bradwardine argues, “that God propositionally foreknows
that Antichrist will exist through Antichrist himself, since Antichrist himself
is as yet nothing in himself and does, or causes, nothing through himself.”

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 18.]

In the same way, Peter's free volition of A rather than his nilling of it—which
was future before the world was created—and conditional futures antecedent to
every decree of God are mere nonentities. Therefore they cannot even
objectively terminate infallible divine knowledge.

“Nor can it be said,” Bradwardine argues, “that God foreknows future things
through their futurition. For then, it appears, future things would be the
cause, or causes, of divine knowledge, contrary to what was established above.
Moreover, if that futurition is nothing, it causes absolutely nothing; but if
it is some reality distinct from God, one argues as before: that futurition was
itself caused by God at some time, in accordance with the foregoing. How, then,
did God know beforehand that it would be future? And how did he know beforehand
that those future things would be future, or that the future thing would be
future? Furthermore, that futurition makes the thing future either contingently
and indeterminately, or necessarily.-*”

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, place cited, book 1, chapter 18.]

[Marginal summary: From Bradwardine: God foreknows all future things in his
will.]

[Chapter XIII; printed page 154; PDF page 205]

*But if it does so necessarily and determinately—if it makes the thing future
contingently and indeterminately, how does God know through it determinately
and certainly that the thing will be? Does not that futurition then exist
determinately, and does it not follow from it determinately and necessarily
that the thing will exist? How, then, can it be avoided that everything which
happens happens by necessity, and absolutely nothing contingently? But if that
futurition is posited in God, it appears above all that it must be placed in
the divine will, since this is the cause of the futurition and determination of
all future things, as the principal intention is established from item 14 of
what follows in this work. Nor is it reasonable to suppose that God knows
future things through his essence alone, with his will removed. For the essence
by itself is indifferent, equally and indeterminately able to represent to the
divine intellect that Antichrist will exist or will not exist, just as either
can equally be and be known by God. Therefore, by itself and without some prior
determination, it represents neither. Similarly, one must not suppose that God
knows future things through his intellect alone, or that the intellect of God
determines the divine essence to reveal this or that future thing, since that
intellect is of itself indifferent to either of the contingent opposites, just
as God's essence itself is. Moreover, the intellect alone is not practical in
this way when his will is removed, as appears from items 80 and 90 of this work.
Furthermore, since the intellect with the will set aside is a necessary
principle, as is evident, and as corollary 10 of this work shows, every future
thing would be necessarily future and foreknown by God; indeed, an equally
balanced contingent would perish. Nor can the intellect be determined, as
some wish, by the infinity and immensity of its knowledge. For even when the
immensity of his knowledge is posited, God can still indifferently know that
Antichrist will exist or will not exist, just as Antichrist can indifferently
exist or not exist. Therefore he will know neither without another prior
determination which limits or terminates. Again, the determination by which God
is determined to know something is naturally prior to that knowing, or to the
knowledge arising from such determination. But God is determined to know that
Antichrist will exist by the infinity of his knowledge. Therefore God's
infinite knowledge is naturally prior to the very act of knowing that
Antichrist will exist. And since the former includes the knowledge of this
proposition—‘Antichrist will exist’—it follows that the knowledge by which God
knows that Antichrist will exist naturally precedes itself.”

From these and similar arguments this most learned scholastic—than whom, with
the possible exception of our own John Duns Scotus, I find scarcely another
among the ancients who is earlier, none more subtle, and none more solid; and
who among the Jesuits has no disciple at all,-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 155; PDF page 206]

*and who by right ought and is able to yield to none in intellectual
acuteness—concludes that contingent futures, whether conditional or absolute,
are known in no medium except God's free decree alone. For the depth of God's
knowledge, to which the Jesuits retreat ignorantly—to avoid saying
maliciously—whether construed as infinite intellect, God's essence, or an idle,
confused, general permissive decree: none of these can apprehend as determinate
something which is indifferent and indeterminate toward either alternative,
once the antecedent and determining decree is set aside. Nor does an antecedent
decree remove elective indifference, because it does not necessitate (1) by
coercing or applying force, (3) by physically necessitating as fire heats and
the sun illuminates, or (2) by removing the intellect's judgment or internal,
elective spontaneity. Hence we steadfastly deny the Jesuits' principle, which
they have never proved: “Whatever determines a free will to one outcome
destroys freedom.” On that reasoning the created will itself, when freely
acting in this way, would destroy freedom, because according to the Jesuits it
freely determines itself to one outcome. “But it does this,” they say, “in a
way congruent with the nature of freedom.” We reply to the Jesuits that God's
antecedent decree does not properly determine a free will to one outcome, but
posits a future determination which will occur most freely. Likewise, what
they call God's physical predetermination—more truly, something acting in the
manner of a physical cause, or rather according to a very dissimilar
analogy—moves and bends the free will in a way most congruent with the character
and nature of freedom, in the same manner in which the will moves itself with
respect to a bending that does not coerce, violate, or destroy freedom at all,
although plainly not in the same manner within the class of a vital cause and
elective agent.

[Marginal summary: Determination of a free will to one outcome does not injure
or remove freedom.]

Hence it is easy to answer what Ruiz, among other points, adduces against
predetermination more laboriously and subtly than solidly and learnedly. It
seems to be a support for middle knowledge, but a rotten one. For he supposes
that God's physical predetermination conflicts with a contract, promise, oath,
or vow. There are three kinds of contract.

[Marginal reference: Didacus Ruiz, *On God's Knowledge*, disputation 46,
sections 1–3, pp. 400–401, etc.]

[Marginal summary: Ruiz maintains—but in vain—that physical predetermination
conflicts with the nature of a promise, vow, and oath.]

1. “A farmer promises to work in the field only at those times in which God
will physically predetermine him to work, and on the hypothesis of
predetermination. But this is not a promise, because something necessary is
promised in vain; and on the supposition of predetermination-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 156; PDF page 207]

*it is absolutely necessary. This is confirmed: if someone promised to come
to another person's house only at the time when he was running or walking
toward it, he would be judged to employ material unsuitable for an obligation.
This is further confirmed because, according to Thomas, what is absolutely
necessary either to be or not to be can in no way fall under a vow. It would be
foolish for someone to vow that he will die or that he will not fly. But it is
absolutely necessary to follow physical predetermination by cultivating the
field when God predetermines one to do so, because there is no freedom there
except in God to withhold or apply predetermination, not in the person. And
whenever the one making the promise omits something which he promised in the
contract to do, physical predetermination to that work was not given to him.
Therefore the contract does not obligate someone to do anything except on the
supposition that physical predetermination to it is given.”

[Marginal reference: Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae*, II–II, question 88,
article 2.]

2. “The second mode is that the farmer promises to cultivate the field only
at those times in which God has not physically predetermined him to cultivate
it. But neither can this mode fall under a contract, oath, or vow. (1) It is
absolutely impossible, even by God's absolute power, to cultivate the field at
those times in which God does not predetermine one to cultivate it. (2) It is
intrinsically evil, injurious to God, and presumptuous concerning one's own
powers to attempt to do something without God's concurrence, which is
absolutely necessary for such an operation. (3) No one would keep the
obligation of his contract, as follows. The observance which presupposes
physical predetermination is not the contract's material on the present mode;
but when physical predetermination is absent, there is no observance, which
alone had been promised, as is now presupposed.

3. “The third mode is that the farmer promises to cultivate the field at any
time, whether God has physically predetermined him to cultivate it or has not.
But neither is this material capable of obligation. For parts which, taken
separately, are incapable of an obligation, promise, or contract are not made
capable merely by being taken together, unless their necessity, impossibility,
or uselessness is removed through the conjunction. But to cultivate the field
when God predetermines one to it is always necessary. Therefore obligation to
it through a promise is always useless. This is confirmed: if the actual
cultivation of the field, precisely considered insofar as God grants or denies
his determination, is not material capable of obligation, then it follows that
it is capable of obligation in no other manner except insofar as it includes
God's physical determination. Thus-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 157; PDF page 208]

*the farmer promises that God will in fact and actually grant him perpetual
physical predetermination to farm, and that, once this is posited, he will
continually cultivate the field. But since predetermination is an act belonging
to another and depending on God alone, no one can promise it, for no one can
promise anything except what lies within his own control.”

[Marginal reference: *Stipulatio illa*, 83, Digest, *On Obligations*.]

I answer, first: this argument militates not so much against us, who maintain
that predetermination is necessary for all acts, as against the apostle James
and the Holy Spirit. For one who promises or resolves to go into a city (James
4:15), and who does not promise and resolve to make that journey ἐὰν ὁ Κύριος
θελήσῃ, “if the Lord wills,” makes one of the following promises. Suppose a
covenant is made among merchants about a journey into the city for purposes of
trade. He promises to make that journey (1) on the hypothesis that God wills
the journey actually to occur at this determinate time; or (2) on the
hypothesis that God does not will it to occur; or (3) that he will journey
there whether God wills the journey to occur or does not will it. If the first,
the defenders of physical predetermination say, with the apostle James, that
the godly promise a journey into the city on this hypothesis: “If the Lord
wills and has decreed that this journey occur at this time.” This introduces
no absolute necessity from positing God's decree or an enacted physical
predetermination, but only the kind of necessity called qualified and
hypothetical. It is the same necessity by which whatever exists, when it
exists, must exist. Thus, when God wills Peter to dispute at this time and moves
and bends him to dispute at this time, Peter must dispute. This is merely that
Aristotelian necessity by which a thing must exist when it exists. But by what
means, Jesuit, will you prove this to be an absolute necessity and utterly
incapable of being the material of a contract, vow, covenant, or oath, like the
case in which someone vowed that he would be mortal or would not fly? You will
fail in the proof and the water will stick fast for you—this is supremely
necessary, I think. If God moves the king's heart to execute Joab the murderer,
as in Proverbs 21:1, does Solomon execute the murderer by absolute necessity?
Or does Solomon perform a free and praiseworthy act of justice, his will having
been moved to this act by God as God wills, not as King Solomon himself wills?
Certainly, on Ruiz's account, a king impelled and determined by God performs no
free act—for without doubt Proverbs 21:1 cannot be explained otherwise than of
predetermination in the manner of a physical cause-*

[Marginal summary: Didacus Ruiz's argument fights against the apostle James
and Scripture, which says: “We will do this if the Lord wills.”]

[Chapter XIII; printed page 158; PDF page 209]

*—except by a qualified necessity. Solomon promised his dying father David
that he would punish Joab the murderer (1 Kings 2:5–6, 28–31), and did so under
the pious and religious hypothesis of James 4:15 and as the Lord spoke through
him: if indeed the Lord by his grace bent and determined Solomon's heart to so
praiseworthy an act of justice. Nor did David promise by absolute necessity,
or vow, something which lay in another's power—namely God's alone. For if all
the promises of mortal people were made piously and religiously, they would
necessarily be made with a virtual, and even express, hypothetical submission
to God's decrees and to his providence as the determining and first moving
cause of all things, actions, covenants, and promises: “If the Lord wills; if
he supplies concurrence”—and who, even an atheist, denies that God is perfectly
free to withhold it? Does it offend the Jesuit that people must subject their
promises to the command and direction of God, in whose hand are all our ways?
Is it not right to acknowledge Jehovah in all our ways as governor and guide?
But it troubles and vexes the Jesuits for angels and human beings to be subject
to God; they prefer the dominion of freedom to reside with the creature.
Nevertheless, it follows perfectly well that violators of contracts are guilty
and ought to be punished whenever they omit what they promised to do, even if
divine predetermination or the Jesuits' collateral concurrence is absent. For
this absence is not the moral cause of the violated promise. Rather, the free
choice of the covenant-breaker—which contributes its virtual and entirely free
consent to that absence—is the proximate and sole culpable cause of the broken
covenant.

[Marginal summary: All promises of mortals are tacitly conditional: “if the
Lord wills.”]

2. Nor does the farmer promise to cultivate the field at times when God does
not physically predetermine him to cultivate it, unless with a rash and
inconsiderate purpose he intends to do such a thing. This often happens to
people who do not acknowledge God as guide and master in their ways: they
resolve to go into the city for trade but do not resolve to do it “if the Lord
wills,” as James 4:15 says.

(3.) I wonder with what face the Jesuit asserts that “Peter's promising to work
in John's garden if the Lord contributes his physical predetermination is the
same as someone's promising to go to another person's house on the hypothesis
that he was running or walking toward the house.” They differ by the whole
breadth of heaven. God's predetermination is in no way simply the same thing as
the act of working in the garden, nor is the action of God as first cause-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 159; PDF page 210]

*and our free action the same in the way that going to another person's house
and running to that house are the same. 4. Nor is it true that attempting to do
something without God's predetermination is intrinsically evil and an
excessive presumption upon one's own powers, unless God's will that he refuses
to join his predetermination to such an act has been revealed. But where has
this been revealed? It becomes clear to us only from the event. Indeed, it is
not intrinsically evil for the disciples to attempt to adhere to Christ and
confess him, even though it was revealed to them, according to the Holy
Spirit's prophecy (Zechariah 13:7; Matthew 26:31), that they would culpably
stumble when the Savior was arrested and crucified, and would deny him. Christ
foretold and revealed, according to the prophecy of Zechariah 13:7 (Matthew
26:31), that God would deny either indifferent and collateral concurrence or
predetermining concurrence to the act of adhering to Christ and confessing him
before people. For that act did not occur in the real world; rather, the
contrary act of abandoning the Savior was infallibly foretold both by the
prophet Zechariah and by Christ. Was it therefore intrinsically evil or
injurious to God to attempt the act of adhering to Christ and confessing him?
On this account, attempting to obey God and endeavoring to fulfill the
disciples' obligatory duty would be intrinsically evil—nothing could be more
absurd or blasphemous. This flows from ignorance of the will of good pleasure
and the will of sign, as though the will of good pleasure were our rule for
what is injurious to God and intrinsically evil and for what is pleasing,
right, and lawful to him, whereas our rule is the revealed will of sign. Hence
the Jesuits and Arminians, together with the Socinians, are forced to admit two
contradictory wills in God—the very thing they falsely ascribe to us.

5. Against the Jesuits we teach that we little mortals, in whose hand and power
our ways do not lie, can promise, covenant, vow, or swear that we will do
nothing unless the Lord wills, grants us the further enjoyment of life—which
we are not ashamed to say lies by no means in our power, however loudly the
atheistic Jesuits protest—and contributes his predetermination to what we
promise to do. God's predetermination is no more the thing promised by us than
the further enjoyment of life or light. It is instead a pious condition,
graciously granted, under which we promise to do something. We are bound to
say, in James 4:15: “We will go into such a city, remain there a year, trade,
and make a profit, if the Lord wills and we live.” They do not promise-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 160; PDF page 211]

*that they possess dominion over God's will or over the enjoyment of their own
life for a year. For God's will concerning this journey belongs just as
exclusively to God and lies just as far outside the poor merchant's control as
God's predetermination is an act lying within God's domain alone, not within
human control. So far is the godly merchant, in promising his associate and
trading partner that he will come to the city to buy goods from him “if the
Lord wills and he lives,” from arrogantly promising or grandiosely ascribing
to himself dominion over God's will and the further enjoyment of his life,
that, on the contrary, he piously and religiously submits himself, his journey
to the city, his trade, and the enjoyment of his life to God's wise will and
supreme dominion. He says only that he will go to the city and buy goods there
if the Lord wills and grants predetermination for the journey; but if the Lord
does not will it, denies predetermination for departure to the city, and breaks
the slender thread of his very fragile life, he will rest most devoutly in the
divine counsel. For every promise, covenant, vow, decree, and oath of creatures
must be made under the pious condition of the divine will, predetermination,
and providence of the God in whom we live, move, and have our being (Acts 17).

2. Let me turn this weapon irrefutably against the head of this Goliath. For
this futile kind of argument strips God of all dominion and of fidelity and
truthfulness in his promises. I therefore apply these three modes of promising
to God. God promises the free generation of his Son from David's seed, the
free obedience of the Gentiles and their entirely free incorporation by faith
and adoption into his church, and finally the free walk in God's commandments
promised according to the tenor of the covenant of grace (Deuteronomy 30:6;
Jeremiah 31:32–36; 32:39–40; Ezekiel 36:26–27; Isaiah 54:1–3, etc.). God
promises that he will procure all these things: (1) either when people will
freely consent to God's universal indifferent concurrence. And thus, in
promising that he will congruently move people to these free acts only at
those times when they will join their created will's own free, vital
determination to God as he concurs, God promises something absolutely
necessary, as the Jesuit reasons. For on the supposition that God congruently
moves the will to a free act, it is absolutely necessary that the will,
congruently moved by God, follow God's congruent motion as something
essentially efficacious; and whatever exists, when it exists, must-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 161; PDF page 212]

*exist. For, according to the Jesuit's doctrine, there is just as necessary and
intrinsic a connection between a free act and congruent motion as, according
to our doctrine, there is between a free act and God's predetermination.

2. Or John promises to perform all the free acts of which we speak only at
those times when God joins the Jesuits' universal and simultaneous concurrence.
But that is to say: John promises to work in Peter's garden only at those times
when he cannot fail to work. For it is just as absolutely necessary for John
to work on the hypothesis that universal concurrence has actually been granted
as it is on the hypothesis that physical predetermination has actually been
granted. The Jesuits will never show a relevant difference. Finally, the
Jesuits devote themselves wholly to taking away and wresting from God all
freedom in promises, covenants, the covenant, and an oath: God's promises are
made dependent upon the creature's free concurrence, and God promises that he
will do this if the creature wills, or that he will do it only at those times
when the creature supplies its free influence. In this way everything Didacus
Ruiz wrongly alleges against God's predetermination can easily be answered.
But this denial is middle knowledge's one rotten support.

My foundation—that God has decreed conditional futures although the connected
terms never existed—is firm from Scripture. Jeremiah 38: “If you indeed go out
to the princes of the king of Babylon, your soul will live, this city will not
be burned with fire, and you, your people, and your household will be safe.”
This means nothing other than that all who obey God will be saved. Yet that
king did not go out to the princes of Babylon and was not safe, but a captive
and deprived of his kingdom; nor were the people or the household safe.
Nevertheless, God's will was determined from eternity to that connection
between the obedience of the king and people of Judah, their due submission to
the king of Babylon, and the safety of Judah's king and people. This is also
proved by all promises of life proposed to the reprobate and all threats made
to believers. These contain God's most actual and determinate will and good
pleasure to save all who obey and punish all who do not believe if they fulfill
this or that condition. In most cases that condition has never been fulfilled,
nor has the reward ever been given or the punishment inflicted. Nevertheless,
God has voli-*

[Marginal summary: The promises and threats of both law and gospel prove that
God has a will concerning connected conditional futures, although the
connected terms never existed.]

[Chapter XIII; printed page 162; PDF page 213]

*tion of the most actual kind concerning the connection between the terms.
Examples are these: “If Cain does well, he will be accepted by God” (Genesis
4); “If Judas believes, he will be saved”; “If Peter persists in
impenitence, he will be condemned.”

Indeed, the Discalced Carmelites of Salamanca say: “In this testimony of Isaiah
14, God boasts of the efficacy and power of his decree: by the very fact that
he is the one who decreed it, no one can invalidate it. But if that decree were
infallibly efficacious because the future event was foreseen, God would have no
greater ground for boasting than the creature decreeing that very thing.
Indeed, the creature could similarly boast that, when it decreed such a
devastation and employed the means to it, no one could resist the foreseen
future effect.”

[Marginal reference: the Discalced Carmelites of Salamanca, theological
course, volume 1, tractate 3 *On God's Knowledge*, disputation 8, question 3.]

This appears to me to be Thomas's express opinion: “Therefore, by knowing his
essence, God knows everything to which his causality extends; and it extends to
the operation of the intellect and will.” And he says: “Since being and
understanding are indistinct in God, he must cause things through his
knowledge, with his will nevertheless joined to it.” And in the reply in the
same place: “It must be considered that a natural form, insofar as it is a form
remaining in that to which it gives being, does not signify a principle of
action, but does so insofar as it has an inclination toward an effect.
Similarly, an intelligible form does not signify a principle of action merely
insofar as it is in the one understanding, unless there is joined to it an
inclination toward an effect, which is through the will. For since an
intelligible form is related to opposites—for the same knowledge is of
opposites—it would not produce a determinate effect unless it were determined
to one by appetite, as is said in *Metaphysics* 9. But it is manifest that God
causes things through his intellect, since his being is understanding. Hence
his knowledge must be the cause of things insofar as it has the will joined to
it; and God's knowledge, insofar as it is the cause of things, is commonly
called knowledge of approval.”

[Marginal reference: Thomas Aquinas, *Contra Gentiles*, book 1, chapter 28.]

Reply to objection 1: “For if certain things are future, it follows that God
foreknew them; yet future things are not the cause of God's knowing. Instead,
it must be said, as above, that God is the cause of things through his
knowledge. God's knowledge extends as far as his causality extends. Thus,
because God's active power extends not only to forms from which the account of
the universal is taken, but also even to matter, God's knowledge must extend to
singulars which are individuated through matter. Since he knows things other
than himself through his essence insofar as it is the likeness of things or
their active principle, it is necessary that-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 163; PDF page 214]

*his essence be the sufficient principle for knowing all things made through
him, not only universally but also singularly.” Add the conclusion of article
13: “Since God knows all things which exist not only actually but potentially,
both his own and the creature's, he knows from eternity, simultaneously and
infallibly, all contingent futures as they are in themselves, according to the
state in which they actually exist.”

[Marginal reference: Thomas Aquinas, question 14, article 8, conclusion in the
reply.]

[Marginal summary: According to distinguished scholastics, God foreknows
conditional futures in his decree.]

Álvarez and the theologians of Salamanca cite Augustine, Hilary, Richard,
Scotus, Hervaeus, Ledesma, and Estius in support of the claim that God knew in
his will what he decreed to do. Yet the Jesuits—whether they have written
commentaries on Thomas I do not know; volumes broad enough, that much I
know—admit that God decrees to bring about all absolute contingents. Oviedo,
Arriaga, Fasolus, and many other Jesuits also admit that absolute and
conditional contingents have the same relevant character in this dispute over
middle knowledge, as I cite them.

[Marginal references: reply to argument 1 in the same place, article 11,
reply; Salamanca College, theological course, volume 1, tractate 3,
disputation 8, question 3; Álvarez, *On Aids*, disputation 10.]

For, first, God acting through his will is the most universal cause of all
beings with respect to their substance and every mode; otherwise free acts
would not be beings by participation.

2. Since God is the most efficacious cause, he accomplishes by command
whatever he wills, and because he wills—not because the creature wills—and no
one resists his will (Romans 9). Augustine demonstrates with two invincible
arguments that this is true in free agents: “For he is called omnipotent for no
other reason than that (1) he can do whatever he wills, and (2) the effect of
the Omnipotent's will is not hindered by the will of any creature.” For in that
case a human being would certainly do whatever he willed, and no one—not even
God—could resist his free will, as one absolutely able to act or not act. For
no one can destroy created freedom, and not even omnipotence can resist it;
because once everything is posited, including God's omnipotent will striving
against it, the creature can act or not act. What a proud little creature this
is!

[Marginal references: Augustine, *On Predestination*, book 26, chapter 10;
Hilary, *On the Trinity*, book 9, chapter 4; Richard, book 1, distinction 38,
article 1, question 5; Scotus, book 1, distinction 39, question “vinc.” (as
abbreviated); Hervaeus, in the same place.]

3. The entire reason why God knows all possible things is his infinite power.
Therefore the entire principle in which God knows every willable future thing
and every rejectable nonfuture thing will be that same power determined by his
most wise counsel. For all future things participate in being and knowability
in this cause.

[Marginal reference: Ledesma, *On Aids*, disputation on knowledge of future
things, doubt 3, solution 4, argument.]

4. God acting through his will is the most perfect, absolutely independent,
and sovereign cause of all future things, with so irresistible a force upon
effects that all free acts are future because a created or uncreated will-*

[Marginal references: Estius, *Sentences* 1, distinction 38, question 7;
Augustine, *Enchiridion to Laurentius*, chapter 95.]

[Chapter XIII; printed page 164; PDF page 215; before Chapter XIV]

*wills them to be future. If the former, the free creature will be the first
and independent cause. If the latter, the point at issue is established.
Suárez does not solve the problem by saying that God's absolute will is not the
efficacious cause of the futurition of free acts. For the kind of knowledge by
which God knows that Peter would consent on the hypothesis that he were placed
in these or those circumstances presupposes in God a will by which he wills to
grant concurrence. I answer: it is true that a will to grant concurrence is
presupposed, but what kind of concurrence is it? It is such as the created will
wants—toward nilling or willing at the poor creature's command—not such as
appears absolutely fitting to God, nor such as God would will and choose. On
the Jesuits' account, then, there is not presupposed in God a will to concur
toward willing rather than nilling, but only a confused will toward either
willing or nilling, according to what pleases not God but the created will.”
