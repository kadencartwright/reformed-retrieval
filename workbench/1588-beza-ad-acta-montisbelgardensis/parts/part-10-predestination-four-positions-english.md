## Preface to the Treatment of Predestination

There formerly were, and still are, four principal positions among theologians concerning predestination, which is the chief part of the doctrine of God’s providence. The first belongs to both ancient and recent Pelagians, who say that the cause of destination, whether to life or death, must be sought in the human being. For God offers salvation to everyone, and people can either accept or reject it when offered, the natural human will being flexible toward either side. Therefore, according as God foreknows who will accept salvation and who will reject it, he destines the former to salvation and the latter to death.

The second belongs to those who judge that the grace of salvation is offered without exception to all who were lost in Adam, none of whom God wills to perish. Since he knows that by their own corrupt nature they will reject that grace through unbelief, by extraordinary mercy he therefore excepts certain people from that ruined mass. Freely and from his mere kindness—not from any foreseen faith or good works of theirs, since he himself bestows faith and a good will upon them—he freely justifies them in Christ, whom they embrace by faith, and glorifies them. But the rest, left in their perdition because of their foreseen stubbornness and its fruits, he justly condemns.

The third belongs to the Semi-Pelagians, who agree with those just described except that they establish in human beings some remnants, however weak, of free choice toward either side. They attribute reception of offered grace partly to God’s mercy and partly to the human will prepared by itself. They also hold that works following faith are, as they say, meritorious. From there comes that most offensive distinction, which did not please even all the Scholastics, between merit of congruity and merit of condignity.

The fourth and final position belongs to those who teach that the cause why some—namely, the elect—are saved and others—namely, the rejected—are condemned is clear and manifest if by “cause” one means the way by which God saves those destined for salvation and likewise destroys those assigned to eternal death: namely, mercy in Christ and through Christ in those who are saved, but corruption and all its fruits in those who perish. Yet as to the distinction itself—why from eternity, by that will which precedes all causes in order rather than by that will through which he executes each decree in its time, God determined to save some by mercy and justly destroy others—they judge there to be no cause other than God’s will. Its depth is not to be searched by us but adored, lest we rush over a precipice. They also demonstrate that it does not follow that God is the author of any evil or of the destruction of the reprobate.
