## Part XLIV: “Sufficiently” and “Efficiently”

[Part II | PDF page 219 | logical source scan 218 | printed page 217 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

For the statement “Christ died sufficiently for the sins of all human beings, but not efficiently,” although true in a right sense, is nevertheless expressed very harshly and ambiguously, no less barbarously. The word FOR declares either the Father’s counsel according to which Christ suffered, or the effect of his passion, or rather both; neither pertains to any but the elect. Yet it must not be denied that this offering is of such value that it could have satisfied even for infinitely many worlds, if there were more worlds and all their inhabitants were endowed with faith in Christ—much more, then, for every individual in one world without exception, if God willed to have mercy on them all.

But Christ’s propitiation, of which John speaks here and which he says was furnished for the whole world—or Christ’s sacrifice itself—has two parts that cohere inseparably and were foreshadowed by the rite of the ancient sacrifices: first, that solemn intercession expressed in John 17, which, as he hastened to his own slaughter, he offered not for his own sins, as those legal high priests did, but for ours; then his very offering of himself upon the altar of the cross. Who could say that Christ willed to offer himself for others than those for whom this supreme priest of ours, at once priest and victim, prayed to the Father, without impiously tearing apart these most closely joined and inseparable parts? For whom does he testify that he prays? First, for those dearest disciples whom the Father had given him; then, he says, “for those who will believe in me through their word.” “But I do not pray for the world.” And, “They are not of the world, just as I am not of the world.” As our adversary is compelled to confess on page 545, by “world” he means those opposed to believers.

The distinction he finally advanced between SUFFICIENTLY and EFFICIENTLY is a mere evasion and does not relieve my adversary in this fiction. From our entire disputation it is clear that the question between us was not whether Christ’s redemption is saving for every human being or only for believers. Apart from the insane Origenists, who dreamed that even the demons would finally be saved, who among Christians ever raised that question? Rather, two matters of the greatest importance have been and remain disputed between him and me. First, whether by his eternal and immutable decree God determined to make, and in his own time does make, has made, and will make until the end of the ages, from the same clay both vessels for honor and vessels for dishonor—the latter certainly through judgment,

[Part II | PDF page 220 | logical source scan 219 | printed page 218]

the former through mercy—which necessarily reach the end appointed for them, so that he may be glorified eternally in both. I affirm this; Andreae denies it. Second, whether God, as Andreae affirms and I deny, always willed every individual without any exception to be saved, and therefore sent his Son to suffer for every individual, disclosed that grace to every individual from the world’s creation, and gave every individual the power to receive it; or whether, as I maintain and he denies, just as God immutably determined from eternity to destroy those whom he pleased through just causes inhering in them, no less than to save by mercy others whom he likewise pleased—although they too were in themselves worthy of destruction—so also in his own time he presented his Christ, their head, formerly promised through the prophets, only for those of his whom he would save. To them alone he truly opens the heart so that, taught outwardly through the external ministry and inwardly through his Holy Spirit by effectual calling, and justified through faith, he may finally glorify them with eternal life. The rest he partly deems worthy of no disclosure whatever of his gospel; others, mixed with his saints through external calling, he hardens through their own fault so that they finally plunge into eternal dishonor and destruction, for which they were destined.

Since these were the questions between us, why could he seem to have changed the question, as sophists do, and introduced that distinction between SUFFICIENCY and EFFICIENCY, except to cover his monstrosities with this applied cosmetic? He even dared to write in just so many words—what wickedness!—and call it eternal and immutable truth, that Christ SUFFERED NO LESS FOR THE CONDEMNED, was crucified and died, and satisfied for their sins, than for the sins of Peter, Paul, and all the saints.

And lest anyone doubt that this man holds that Christ truly and actually expiated the sins of every individual human being without any exception, although afterward through their unbelief they do not receive the fruit of that expiation, behold his own express words from page 548, accompanied in the margin by this proposition, “Unbelief alone condemns human beings”: “They are not condemned”—that is, adjudged to eternal destruction—“because they sinned; otherwise all the elect would also be condemned, since all sinned and lack”—translate rather “fail of” or “are destitute of”—“the glory of God. But they are condemned because they refuse to embrace Jesus Christ by true faith, because through faith they do not apply to themselves the benefit of Christ’s death, who died for them and satisfied for their sins, as Christ openly testifies when he says, ‘This is judgment, that the Light came into the world and the world loved darkness more than light,’ John 3. Likewise John 16: ‘The Holy Spirit will convict the world of sin because they do not believe in me.’” I ask you, Christian reader: before now has anyone ever dared so shamelessly to introduce into the Christian church a teaching so false, so mon—

—strous, so abominable, and even to adorn it with the designation “eternal and immutable truth”?

Besides what I have now so often shown—that every human being cannot be charged with unbelief unless one posits the claim so ridiculous as not even to need refutation, that every individual is at least outwardly called to the gospel’s grace—what madness is this, to deny that anyone is condemned because of sins, yet make all who are condemned guilty of unbelief? Is unbelief not a sin, I ask? Indeed, it is so great a sin that Christ, discussing it not as though it alone were sin, but as though almost the greatest of all, says, “If I had not come and spoken to them, they would have no sin,” John 9:41. Yet that they were contaminated by infinitely many other vices is shown at least by his addressing these same people in John 8:7: “Let the one among you who is free from sin cast the first stone at her.” And in John 3:36, speaking of this very unbelief, he did not say, as our Augustine somewhere beautifully observes, that God’s wrath comes, but that God’s wrath remains upon him.

If Andreae should answer that he does not deny unbelievers abound in other sins too, but only that they are condemned because of those sins, he certainly fights with himself. For if Christ’s death is expiation for their sins; if Christ satisfied for them no less than for Peter, Paul, and all the saints; if that Lamb gave himself to take away the sins of every individual without exception, it necessarily follows that those sins have been blotted out, since the blotting out of sin depends inseparably upon expiation and satisfaction necessarily abolishes every guilt. But if he nevertheless admits that his unbelievers have sins besides unbelief—since stubbornness against the gospel is certainly never without both preceding and subsequent sins—by what appearance of reason will these sins count for nothing before God’s tribunal, when even one and the least of them deserves eternal death? What does the Holy Spirit more frequently and terribly reproach in those sacred books than the multitude of sins? “Sins,” says the Lord, “have placed a division between me and you.” Is unbelief alone, then, the cause of condemnation? And why do even all the holiest people pant in daily prayers for the remission of sins—indeed, each person more ardently the holier he is—if unbelief alone condemns? Who of sound mind ever separated the expiation of sins from their true, real, and actual blotting out? And since all Christ’s saving benefits are apprehended with Christ himself by faith alone—among them the freely granted propitiation of sins is of such importance that blessedness is sometimes defined by this benefit alone—how can Christ truly be said to have expiated the sins of unbelievers? I mean unbelievers who persist in unbelief all the way to condemnation. Otherwise, God at whatever moment he pleases makes himself found, as Isaiah says, by those who did not formerly seek him. As for

[Part II | PDF page 222 | logical source scan 221 | printed page 220]

the things Andreae used as a pretext for this truth of his—so indubitable among all godly doctors, eternal, and immutable—against the “absurd, horrendous, and manifestly impious things,” as he himself speaks on page 549, that Beza teaches about Christ’s death by denying that he died, whether by the Father’s decree or in effect, for all sins and sinners, that is, every individual without any exception: it is fair that I answer these too.

First, he says, “If those to be adjudged in eternal judgment were condemned because they sinned, all the elect would also be condemned. For all sinned.” I ask, what stupidity is this? Let there be two sick men bedridden with the same disease, lethal in itself but curable by a skilled physician. The physician assists one; the disease kills the other, either because he lacks the physician or because he fails the physician. Who, perhaps excepting this one disputant, would deny that the man died from that disease because both suffered from the same disease? But if Andreae, discussing foreknowledge, had reasoned thus: “If the foreseen corruption of original sin and its fruits, namely sins, had provided God a cause for destining some to damnation, all would be destined to damnation,” the argument would be firm and solid, since the Lord could foresee or find nothing different in any sons of Adam. But as firm as that argument is, so futile and even ridiculous is this one. God gives to the one what he did not find in him, and thus expels what he did find in him; in the other he leaves what he found. You ask why? Because it so pleased God. But why did it please him? O depth! Meanwhile neither has any ground for complaint, since the former has life from the gratuitous remedy, while death came to the latter only from the disease, which neither the sick man wished removed nor the physician willed to remove.

As for John 3:19, that passage neither pertains to every individual without exception, especially those of all ages, nor excludes the remaining sins. Indeed, Andreae should consider why he suppressed the final portion of that verse, namely these words: “Because his works are evil.” They declare one of the chief causes of stubbornness against the truth, namely, that because human beings are evil, as their whole life testifies, they refuse to be reproved by the light. Yet it would be astonishing if, when unbelief is added to the other sins, no account were therefore taken in condemnation of the other works of the flesh, whether preceding or subsequent. Nevertheless, those who practice these things, says the apostle in Galatians 5:21, “will not possess God’s kingdom,” because, says the same apostle in Romans 1:32, “they are worthy of death.” Nor is the other passage from John 16 to be explained differently. It indeed marks stubborn unbelief as the sin by which every excuse is especially taken from rebels, but not as though God will therefore take no account of the other sins, even those preceding.

We just answered this observation too. At the end Andreae no longer says simply, as before, that human beings are not condemned because they did not sin, but adds the restriction,

[Part II | PDF page 223 | logical source scan 222 | printed page 221 | ends before Matthew 11 item]

“in such a way that they can no longer be helped by any means.” He does rightly and speaks truth; yet he would have acted much more rightly had he openly withdrawn that teaching of his, as he promised in the colloquy he would do if convicted of error on page 547. Who would have resisted him if he had spoken this way in the colloquy itself? And what drove him only now to add this restriction, except the testimony of a convicted conscience?

But I say again and profess before the whole church of God that it is false, blasphemous, and impious to say that Christ—whether regarding God’s counsel or regarding the effect—suffered, was crucified, died, and satisfied NO LESS for the sins of those to be condemned and adjudged in eternal judgment than for the sins of Peter, Paul, and all the saints, as Andreae was not ashamed to affirm in just so many words orally and in writing. As for that distinction between SUFFICIENTLY and EFFICIENTLY, which I do not deny when rightly understood, I showed in the immediately preceding reply that it is conceived in harsh and ambiguous words and has nothing to do with the question debated between us.

But what if even that restriction does not sufficiently relieve our adversary? Thus unbelief supervening upon remissible sins appears to be posited as wholly irremissible, and consequently, according to our adversary’s teaching, human beings are condemned through it alone, because unbelievers can no longer be helped by any means. I indeed confess that if anyone persists in rejecting the gospel all the way to the end, it is altogether finished for him, since salvation belongs only to believers. But this is not peculiar to unbelief, for the same must be thought of any sin of which a person does not repent to the end. Then, if this was our adversary’s meaning, why did he not express it? Furthermore, who granted him that no hope of repentance and salvation remains for those who formerly, whether for a short or a long time—for who besides God could define that time?—resisted the gospel not only through ignorance or contempt but even obstinately, while God had not yet softened their hearts? Thus indeed the one who, when called, had proudly refused to obey his father afterward went into the vineyard. Otherwise unbelief would be the same as the sin against the Holy Spirit. So far is this from being true that, on the contrary, only those sin against the Holy Spirit without any hope of pardon who, having been so taught by the Holy Spirit that they knew and acknowledged the truth, afterward knowingly attack that very truth in the Devil’s manner, as did Julian the Apostate.
