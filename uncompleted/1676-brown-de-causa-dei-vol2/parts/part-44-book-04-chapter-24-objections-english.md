# CHAPTER XXIV

## The opponents' objections are resolved

Our opponents contend with many arguments against the truth now abundantly
confirmed, and they leave no stone unturned to make it manifest that this
fourth precept is not moral but ceremonial. The reasons by which they attempt
to prove that this precept is ceremonial will be called to examination later.
Here it is fitting to examine certain other reasons by which they wish to
prove that this precept is not moral.

*Objection 1.* This precept is nowhere mentioned in the New Testament.
Therefore it is not moral.

*Response 1.* The force and authority of a moral law does not depend upon its
being mentioned in the New Testament. A law derives all its authority and
force from the Lawgiver, not from any book in which it is written, nor from
the time at which it is mentioned. 2. Things that are more plainly commanded
and more fully explained in the Old Testament are not always mentioned as
distinctly and extensively in the New Testament as other things about which
perhaps some controversy had arisen, or which were being perverted, or had
fallen out of use, or of which mention is made for some other special reason.
3. That this inference is empty is evident from the fact that not all moral
matters are mentioned in the New Testament. Where is the express prohibition
of the worship of images condemned in the second precept of the Decalogue?
Where is the prohibition against marriage between a brother and his uterine
sister? I pass over other prohibitions of this kind against marriage within
the forbidden degrees. 4. Since the law of God written in the Old Testament
binds us just as much as the law of God written in the New Testament,

[Printed p. 255 | IA PDF 263 | IA leaf 263 | IA decausadei02brow]

all the precepts of the Old Testament that have not been abrogated or made
obsolete in the New Testament will also hold us bound. But it has not yet been
proved from the New Testament that the fourth precept of the Decalogue has
been abrogated. 5. This precept was confirmed and ratified under the New
Testament, as is evident from what was said above and will be still more
evident from what will be said.

*Objection 2.* This fourth precept specifically enjoins observance of the
seventh day in order, or observance of the last day in the weekly cycle. This
is not moral, however, because that day is now changed.

*Response.* It is conceded that observance of the seventh day was consequently
commanded by this fourth precept, because the precept that commands observance
of the Sabbath designated and determined by God commands observance of the
seventh day when and so long as that seventh day is the Sabbath instituted by
God. But when this particular day is changed, it does not follow that the
precept itself, which enjoins observance of the Sabbath determined by God, is
also changed. When the Passover could not be observed in the first month,
Numbers 9:9, 10, neither the precept itself concerning celebration of the
Passover nor the duty according to that precept was taken away. It is false,
however, that the substance of the fourth precept concerns observance of the
seventh day in order and primarily enjoins it, because the substance of the
command is contained in these words: *remember the Sabbath day, to sanctify
it*. This Sabbath day is no more closely related to the last day of the week
than to the first. It can be said no less of the first than of the last, but
can be said equally suitably of either whenever it seemed good to God to apply
the Sabbath to this one or that one.

*Objection 3.* A positive precept is not moral. But the fourth precept
concerning sanctifying the seventh day is such a precept. Therefore, and so
forth.

*Response 1. To the major premise*, by making a distinction: a purely positive
precept is not moral and perpetually binding. But there is a positive precept
that is also called moral, or moral-positive, as is evident from what was said
above in Book I, the last chapter. 2. The whole argument can be conceded, for
only this will be concluded from it: therefore the precept concerning
observance of the last day of the week is not moral. We readily concede this,
for we do not say that the moral element of the fourth precept is that the
seventh day in order be observed, but that the seventh day in number, or one
out of a cycle of seven days, be observed. The fourth precept concerning
observance of the Sabbath on the seventh day in number is not simply positive,
but rather moral-positive. Indeed, when the terms are rightly explained, it
is moral-natural, as we showed above in Book III, Chapter XXVIII.

*Objection 4.* This precept flows merely from God's free good pleasure, has no
intrinsic rectitude, and is only a law of commandments consisting in rites,
Ephesians 2:15; Colossians 2:14. Therefore it is not moral.

*Response.* We showed long ago that many things are positive with respect to
God, or flow from his free determination, which nevertheless are moral and
natural to us. Such is this precept concerning the Sabbath, as was abundantly
proved above. 2. This precept has as much intrinsic rectitude and morality as
certain other undoubtedly moral things possess, such as the command not to
contract marriage within certain forbidden degrees, and not a few other
things that pertain to the second table of the Decalogue, as was also shown
above. 3. It is asserted without proof, and will never be proved, that this
fourth precept is a precept consisting in rites. Nor will it be proved that
all precepts positive with respect to God are of that kind. But there is no
reason to repeat here what has been said repeatedly.

[Printed p. 256 | IA PDF 264 | IA leaf 264 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Objection 5.* If the fourth precept is moral according to the meaning of its
words, it was implanted in Adam. Yet at most it was given to him after he had
been created, Genesis 2:3. Moreover, then the seventh day in order would never
have been changed by Christ.

*Response.* Were the precepts concerning the contracting of marriage between
one man and one woman, concerning conjugal duties, and other things of that
kind dependent upon the institution of marriage also given to Adam after his
creation? What then? Are they merely positive on that account? Or can they not
be said on that account to have been natural to Adam and nevertheless also
positive with respect to God? Where, then, is the force of this argument? 2.
We are not speaking here about the seventh in order, because the substance of
the fourth precept does not require observance of that seventh day, but
something far different, namely, the sanctification of a day in the weekly
cycle.

*Objection 6.* All precepts whose observance is necessary under the gospel
are either precepts of nature, precepts of the gospel, or precepts of the
church. But the fourth precept concerning the Sabbath of the seventh day is
none of these. Therefore, and so forth.

*Response 1.* We do not regard precepts of the church as such as moral. 2.
There are other precepts that, although not natural to God himself but
positive, are nevertheless natural to us. They were given from the beginning
to bind absolutely all human beings and have never been abrogated. We maintain
that the precept concerning the Sabbath is such a precept. 3. The assumption
speaks about the Sabbath of the seventh day, that is, the seventh in order,
about which we are not speaking.

*Objection 7.* All divine laws whose observance is necessary to salvation
under the gospel are inscribed on the hearts of the faithful. But the precept
concerning the Sabbath of the seventh day is not so inscribed. Therefore, and
so forth.

*Response 1.* Here too we can concede the conclusion, because it does not
count against us. For, as has been said, we are not speaking about the Sabbath
of the seventh day in order, which the assumption of this argument concerns.
2. The *major premise* is not universally true, because the opponent himself
will acknowledge that use of the New Testament sacraments is necessary by
necessity of precept, yet these things are not inscribed on the hearts of the
faithful. There are also other precepts positive with respect to God whose
observance is necessary even under the New Testament. 3. Experience teaches
that the precept concerning observance of the weekly Sabbath is inscribed on
the hearts of the faithful, at least of some. And perhaps there are others who
are either badly educated and instructed or carried away by prejudices, and
who acknowledge absolutely no moral element in the fourth precept. Yet the
opponent himself will not say that the matter is therefore so. 4. Was the
precept against polygamy inscribed on the hearts of the patriarchs? Or because
they declared by their practice that this precept was not written on their
hearts, must it therefore be said that this precept was not moral? 5. Godly
and upright people also disagree among themselves about the full meaning of
certain other precepts, which nevertheless do not on that account cease to be
moral. 6. Is that promise of the new covenant limited only to natural
precepts, which are already inscribed in some fashion on the hearts of all?
And is this fourth precept excluded, which was inscribed on the stone tablets
no less than the others, and to which inscription the prophet seems to allude?
7. It is moral to observe a determined solemn and sufficient time, and this is
consequently inscribed on the hearts of the faithful. Therefore it will be
moral to observe the fourth precept, and this is also inscribed on the hearts
of the faithful, because that precept contains precisely this determination,
namely, of one day in the weekly cycle.

*Objection 8.* Obedience to this precept is not morally possible for all
nations, because it enjoins observance of a particular day distinguished from
the others by its own morning and evening, yet in some regions there is no
such distinction of days.

*Response 1.* What, is it morally possible for us sinners to observe any moral
precept rightly? 2. The fourth precept properly requires the seventh part of
our time to be sanctified to God, with due qualifications. Every nation,
however, knows how to distinguish and divide time in this way. Even those
nations that do not see the sun for some months have nights and days
analogically: that is, a time for working and a time for sleeping; and they
can calculate days and nights by the stars. Furthermore, regions of this kind
have few inhabitants. 3. Were the Jews not formerly bound to observe the
Sabbath even if perhaps staying for some time in regions of this kind? 4. What
if the Lord's Day is observed even today in regions of this kind? But we dealt
with this objection above in Book III, Chapter XV, pages 588, 589.

*Objection 9.* A precept of which Christ, insofar as he is the Son of Man, is
absolute Lord, and with obedience to which he dispensed, is not moral. But the
fourth precept concerning sanctifying the Sabbath is such a precept, as is
evident from Matthew 12:8 and John 5:8, 9.

*Response 1.* Christ himself observed the ceremonial law, and as long as he
was alive he granted no one the power to violate it. 2. He was indeed Lord of
the Sabbath in such a way that he could change the day of the week and
transfer the Sabbath to the first day of the week. Nevertheless, as Mediator
or Son of Man, he was not absolute Lord of the fourth precept in such a way
that he could transfer the weekly Sabbath to one day out of a number of ten or
four days. 3. The deed in John 5:8, 9 was not a violation of the Sabbath. See
the preceding volume, pages 706 and 728.

*Objection 10.* A precept over which anyone has power in a state of necessity
is not moral. But the Sabbath precept is such a precept, Matthew 12:8.

*Response 1.* The preceding objection supposed that Matthew 12:8 was to be
understood of Christ as Mediator, but this one wishes it to be understood of
any human being. 2. The *major premise* is true if it is understood of what is
moral-natural, but not if it is understood of what is moral-positive. 3.
Properly speaking, no mortal is lord over divine laws in any case of
necessity. In certain cases of necessity, however, the Lord, from his sheer
grace, dispenses with the observance of certain laws. In such cases there is
no violation of the law properly so called, but the lesser yields to the
greater. 4. This objection does not touch the moral element of the fourth
precept.

*Objection 11.* A law that was made for man, and not man for it, is not moral.
But the fourth precept of the Decalogue is such a law. Therefore, and so
forth.

*Response.* This objection is similar to the preceding one, and the same
response can be applied. Only this much can be inferred from it: the precept
concerning the Sabbath is not moral-natural. But it will not be concluded
from this that the precept is not moral-positive. Whatever could be inferred
from this against the present obligation of the precept concerning observing
this or that Sabbath in this or that particular case, nevertheless nothing
can be concluded from it against the fourth precept or against what is moral
in the fourth precept.

*Objection 12.* Sabbath rest yields to works of mercy and necessity. Therefore
that precept is not moral.

*Response.* The inference is invalid. Acts of praying and preaching can here
and now yield

[Printed p. 258 | IA PDF 266 | IA leaf 266 | IA decausadei02brow]

to works of that kind, yet it by no means follows from this that they are not
moral duties. 2. Although Sabbath rest here and now yields to acts of mercy
and necessity, what follows from this against the substance of the fourth
precept? In what case does that rest yield to works of this kind?

*Objection 13.* A moral precept admits no change. But this fourth precept has
been changed, for the seventh day is changed into the first day of the week.

*Response.* This fourth precept has not been changed with respect to its
substance, for it requires that one day in the weekly cycle be consecrated to
God, and this has not been changed. For even when the seventh, or last, day
was changed into the first day of the week, the substance of this precept
remained whole and unimpaired, because one out of seven is always observed,
which the substance of this precept requires. Indeed, by that change the
substance of the fourth precept was confirmed and ratified. 2. Furthermore,
the opponents themselves concede that it is moral for some particular day to
be observed. This moral requirement always remains, even after this change.
Why should not what we call the moral element of this precept remain as well?
3. Moreover, a moral precept can admit some change, as did the seventh
precept, notwithstanding which it was lawful for Adam's sons to marry their
sisters.

*Objection 14.* That time of worship which is prescribed by the fourth precept
is in itself indifferent and only a means of worship, not worship itself or
something moral.

*Response.* A time determined by God's command is no longer indifferent, and
observance of a solemn time is a part of worship, as we proved above in Book
II, Chapter XV.

*Objection 15.* Rest is not love for God, nor is there in it that expression
of love for God which is found in the other precepts of the Decalogue.
Therefore this precept is not moral.

*Response.* Honor for parents is not formally love for God. Nevertheless,
when it is rendered according to the precept, an expression of love for God
is made in it. So it is also in observance of the fourth precept, or in
sanctification of the Sabbath. 2. This objection supposes that nothing other
than that external rest is required in the fourth precept. This is false,
however, as will appear later.
