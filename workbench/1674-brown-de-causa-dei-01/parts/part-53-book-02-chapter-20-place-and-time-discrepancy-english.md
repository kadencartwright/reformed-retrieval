CHAPTER XX

How Great the Disparity Between Place and Time Is in Relation to Religion Is
Demonstrated

Having premised in the preceding chapter certain points concerning the nature
of Place and its Usefulness and Necessity in relation to Religion, we now
proceed to demonstrate the falsity of that oft-repeated assertion concerning
the equality of Time and Place with respect to Religion. We shall show briefly
how manifold the distinction between them is, whether we speak of Indeterminate
Time or of Determinate Time.

And first, if we speak only of *Indeterminate Time,* it can be shown in many
ways that *Place* is not to be compared with it in relation to Religion. For,

1. We saw above that *Indeterminate Time* is something of the greatest
importance, notably conducive and useful—even Ethically and Theologically—to an
action which admits of degrees, such as Religion, whether we speak separately
of the *Total Portion,* the *Duration,* or the *Frequency,* and much more if the
discussion concerns all these things conjointly. But nothing of this kind can be
affirmed concerning *Place,* whether with respect to its entire proportion, any
of its dimensions separately, all of them conjointly, or finally with respect
to *where.* For as far as advancing Religion is concerned, it makes no
difference whether we worship God in a subterranean Place, a basilica, or the
open air, provided that the place is of such a kind as to cause no inconvenience
to our life. Therefore *Place and Time* are not equal circumstances in Religion.

2. From the greater and more abundant fruit of Religion in any person, we can
easily infer that he has spent a large portion of Time in the exercises of
Religion, whether with respect to duration, frequency, or both. But nothing of
this sort can be said of *Place.* For no one can infer from anyone's progress in
Religion and piety that he worshiped God here and not elsewhere—in a larger,
more ornate place and not in a subterranean place, some cell, the woods, or a
field,

[1674 logical printed p. 369 | IA leaf 387 | PDF p. 387]

or in a Temple. Therefore *Place and Time* are not equal with respect to
Religion.

3. When one of two otherwise equal people surpasses the other in piety and
bears greater fruits, it is not difficult to infer that he spent a larger and
more frequently recurring portion of Time in Religious exercises than the other
spent. But nothing of this sort is established concerning *Place.* For from the
greater or lesser fruit of one person or another, no one can infer that this man
worshiped God here in a common, plain, and minimally ornamented place, while the
other did so elsewhere in a basilica or in a very large and very ornate Temple,
as is evident. Therefore the difference here is very great.

4. Thus, other things being equal, we can judge progress by the proportion of
Time applied to the exercises of Religion and say that the one who has spent a
greater portion of Time in pious exercises has advanced further in religion
than the one who spent less. And likewise we can predict with certainty that
the one who dedicates a twice-greater portion of Time to divine things will bear
greater fruits. But concerning *Place,* no such thing appears capable of being
said. Will anyone say that the person who practiced worship in a larger place
made greater progress in true piety than the one who did so in a narrower Place?
Or can we predict with certainty that, with respect to fruits, this person who
worships God and pursues the exercises of piety in this or that place, whether
larger or more ornate, will far surpass another who performs Religious duties
in a different place that is somewhat dark or less splendid? Therefore *Place
and Time* differ even with respect to Religion.

5. We said above that a person pursues Religion with greater love when of his
own accord he consecrates a greater portion of time to divine things and is
occupied more frequently in the exercises of Religion; and, conversely, that a
person is ill-disposed toward Religion when, although he could if he wished, he
nevertheless refuses to spend so great a portion of Time upon sacred things.
But can anything similar be said of *Place?* Can a greater affection for
Religion be inferred from someone's wishing to worship God in some larger
Temple and not in a private room? Superstition can certainly be inferred from
it, but not true piety or devotion of mind; nor can they be inferred from his
returning more frequently to the same place or changing places from time to
time. The difference between *Place* and *Time* is therefore evident.

6. That Christian is wise who redeems Time for Religion and who takes as much
time as he can from other earthly business and spends it upon sacred things,
whether with respect to *Continuance* or *Frequency.* But if someone preferred
to worship God in a larger place rather than a narrower one, what frugality
would be evident there? How could he be said to redeem *Place?* What wisdom is
evident in choosing the largest or most ornate place, or in frequenting one and
the same *Place,* unless perhaps accidentally? As it is said, *Time alone is a
proper object of honorable avarice.* Therefore *Time and Place* differ greatly,
even with respect to Religion.

7. God commands that we invoke and worship him more frequently; and those who
celebrate him more frequently and for longer, and who give themselves to pious
exercises, are praised more by God, if other things are equal. But this cannot
be said of *Place.* For God nowhere commands that we praise him in a more
celebrated place rather than a less celebrated one, nor that we frequent some
particular place considered in itself and not another. Under the New Testament,
those who practice divine worship in a basilica are not praised more than those
who do so in a narrower Temple. Therefore *Place and Time* are not to be
equated.

8. The one who worships God more frequently pleases him more than the one who
does so more rarely, and the one who does so longer

[1674 logical printed p. 370 | IA leaf 388 | PDF p. 388]

on each occasion pleases him more than the one who does so more briefly,
provided other things are equal. But who will say that the person who worships
him in this place rather than another, considered in itself—that is, the one who
worships him in a more spacious rather than a narrower place—pleases God more?
Or who will say that a person is more acceptable to God because he practices
the worship of God more frequently rather than more rarely in this or that
place considered in itself? Therefore *Place and Time* are not equal
circumstances of Religion.

9. The mind's inward devotion and true piety and holiness move, urge, and impel
a person to dedicate a greater portion of Time to divine exercises, both with
respect to *Duration* and to *Frequency.* But true piety and pure holiness of
mind will never move, urge, or impel a person to frequent this *Place* and not
that one, considered in itself, or to worship God in this place but not another,
considered in itself. Therefore the difference between *Place* and *Time* with
respect to Religion is manifest.

10. The one who spends a greater portion of Time in religious pursuits, both
with respect to *Continuance* and to *Frequency,* will enjoy greater inward peace
and tranquility of conscience than the one who spends less, other things being
equal. But this cannot be said of *Place.* For the person who worships God in a
larger place will not enjoy greater true peace and tranquility than the one who
does so in a smaller place—I do not concern myself with a false peace founded
upon superstition—and so forth. Who, therefore, will say that *Time and Place*
are equal circumstances of Religion?

We see, therefore, how greatly even *Indeterminate Time* differs from *Place.*
And we show as follows that *Determinate Time* also differs from *Place* in many
ways.

1. We proved above in Chapters VI and X that it is necessary to Religion that
some *Time* be determined. Can anyone demonstrate a similar necessity for the
determination of *Place?* Does the law of Nature require that some place be
Theologically determined for the performance of religious duties? What
Religious and Moral necessity can be shown here? Is not one place as convenient
for religion as another? Can we not worship God just as well in the open air as
in a roofed house? Therefore no Theological or Moral necessity is apparent for
determining some *Natural Place* for religious use. Thus *Place and Time*
differ with respect to Religion.

2. We also saw above that nature itself at least *initially* determines *Time*
with respect to *Duration and Frequency,* so that Time applied to Religion
cannot or ought not be shorter with respect to *Continuance,* nor recur more
rarely with respect to *Frequency.* But nothing of this sort can be said of
*Place.* When someone prepares some building for Religious uses, nature,
Theologically speaking, does not determine anything about its proportion even
*initially.* It does not say that a place dedicated to Religion must be at least
so long, broad, or high. Nature, Physically speaking, will indeed initially
determine those dimensions and require that it be at least long, broad, and
high enough to hold all who are accustomed to assemble there to worship God and
allow them to breathe there, and so forth. But it says nothing beyond this. As far as
our soul and Religion itself are concerned, it says absolutely nothing about
the dimensions of *Place.* Therefore are *Place and Time* equal circumstances
of Religion?

3. Everyone is bound to observe the *Time* which nature at least initially
determines with respect to

[1674 logical printed p. 371 | IA leaf 389 | PDF p. 389]

*Continuance and Frequency,* as is evident from the foregoing, because otherwise
he would sin against the Law of Nature. But when some house has been built and
assigned to pious uses, the law of Nature does not require everyone to observe
the same place precisely because that place has been assigned to Religion. I
acknowledge that every private person is bound to frequent Ecclesiastical
assemblies held in the appointed place; but this is not on account of the Place,
but of the assembly. For if the Ecclesiastical assemblies were held elsewhere,
he would be bound to leave that Place and join the assembly in another place no
less than in the former one. Therefore *Place and Time* differ even as far as
Religion is concerned.

4. It was proved above that Religion would be ruined if some sufficient Time
were not determined for everyone. Can anything similar be demonstrated
concerning *Place:* namely, that all Religion would perish unless some
sufficient place were determined—Theologically, that is, in relation to God's
acceptance, the good of our souls, and the peace of our consciences? What, then,
do the Adversaries mean by saying that *Place and Time* are equal circumstances
of Religion?

5. It is necessary that *Time* be determined for Religion because it is
impossible for us to conduct worldly business and practice the solemn worship
of God simultaneously at one and the same Time. But nothing of this sort is
established concerning *Place,* because at different times we can both worship
God and conduct earthly business in one and the same Place and where, as is
evident. Therefore *Place and Time* are not equal with respect to Religion.

6. The determination of Time is required for religious exercises because many
people are excessively averse to religion; for once the determination is made,
even the unwilling are compelled and impelled. But a determination of Place is
not therefore necessarily required. For once this is made, the excuse of the
unwilling is not removed, nor is any remedy applied to their aversion. Once Time
is determined for the public worship of God, it follows that those who live near
one another assemble in any nearby and convenient place, and that others living
farther away come to them. But from a determination of *Place* alone, nothing
concerning worship follows until some determination of Time is made. Once *Time*
is determined for solitary worship, it is unnecessary to make any mention of
*Place,* for it makes no difference whether that worship occurs in a narrow or
larger house, or in a field. But once *Place* is determined, unless *Time* is
also determined, God might never be worshiped in that Place. Therefore it is
manifest that *Place and Time* are not equal adjuncts of Religion.

7. *Time* must be determined because of the interruptions caused by worldly
things, which would otherwise impede us from worshiping God continuously for as
long and on as many repeated occasions as is required. But once Time is
determined both with respect to *How Long* and to *How Often,* those impediments
are removed. This cannot be said of *Place.* For once *Place* is determined,
those impediments are not removed, and those interruptions are not forestalled.
A place can be assigned and, if no determination of *Time* is made, may never be
applied to worship, because the worship of God can always be practiced
elsewhere. Therefore religion is not safeguarded through the determination of
*Place* alone as it is safeguarded through the determination of *Time* alone.
It is therefore absurd to say that *Place and Time* are equal circumstances of
Religion.

[1674 logical printed p. 372 | IA leaf 390 | PDF p. 390]

8. Because people lack the wisdom to make, without much deliberation, a
perpetual distribution of *Time* between secular and spiritual exercises, it is
therefore required that some determination of *Time* be made in relation to
Religion. But it is not therefore required that some determination of *Place*
be made because people lack the wisdom to assign places to the business of body
and soul. For no Moral or Theological Wisdom is needed here, and the reason is
that the same individual *Place* can serve each use, as is evident. Therefore
*Place and Time* are not equally necessary, or equal circumstances in Religion.

9. Furthermore, because not everyone can rightly distribute the entire portion
of Time between *Duration* and recurring *Frequency* together, the determination
of *Time* is therefore necessary. But this cannot be said of *Place.* Therefore
there is an immense distinction between *Place* and *Time* in relation to
Religion.

10. The greater the determination of Time with respect to *Duration,* provided
our necessary earthly business can bear it, the more the glory of God and our
salvation are advanced when that determination is observed. But this cannot be
said of *Place.* For in the smallest chapel, provided it can contain all those
who worship God together, the glory of God and the salvation of men can be
advanced just as much as in the largest cathedral Church. And the glory of God
or our salvation no more requires us to frequent this largest Place than to
assemble in another, smaller one to celebrate God's praises together. But one
Hour is not equal to an entire day. Therefore *Place and Time* differ greatly
with respect to Religion.

11. The greater the determination of Time with respect to the *Frequency* of
Recurrence, provided some Time is reserved for performing our necessary
business of life, the more the glory of God and the salvation of men are
advanced when this determination is observed. This cannot be said of *Place,*
because the glory of God and the salvation of men no more require us always to
frequent one and the same place than to practice Religion in various and
distinct places. For as far as Religion is concerned, it makes no difference
whether we always worship God in one and the same place or in different ones.
But the matter does not come to the same thing whether we observe one day in
the cycle of a week, a month, or an entire year, as everyone knows. Therefore
*Place and Time* are not equal.

12. It does not belong to man to make a determination of Time in relation to
Religion, and it is dangerous to entrust this to people, as is evident from the
foregoing. But no danger would threaten Religion if people determined *Place,*
because, once the general rules of convenience are observed, it makes no
difference which place is assigned. It is therefore evident that *Time and
Place* differ greatly with respect to Religion.

13. There is no certain rule according to which people can judge and determine
a *Time Sufficient and Necessary* for Religion in such a way as to satisfy their
own and others' consciences abundantly, both concerning the *Sufficiency* and the
*Necessity* of the determined Time, as is evident from the things said above.
But there is a certain rule evident to everyone according to which they can
judge concerning a *Sufficient and Necessary Place.* For no spiritual Necessity
or Religious Sufficiency must be sought here; the mere convenience of the
*Place* for receiving everyone and repelling injuries from the air and sky

[1674 logical printed p. 373 | IA leaf 391 | PDF p. 391]

is sufficient, and so forth. No one with a sound head can be ignorant of what
this convenience is. For those who know what is required to render a place
convenient for civil uses cannot be ignorant of what is required to render a
place suitable for hearing sermons, and so forth. Therefore those who assert
that *Place and Time* are equal circumstances of Religion are not to be heard.

14. There is some *Principal-Solemn, Necessary, and Ordinarily Sufficient Time,*
as we showed above. But no one will say that there is some *Principal-Solemn,
Necessary, and Ordinarily Sufficient Place.* Therefore *Place and Time* are not
peers or equals in Religion.

15. In the determination of *Principal-Solemn Time,* if this power belonged to
man, there would be reason to fear that some would sin by excess and others by
defect; that the former would impose a burden that could not be borne, at least
in the judgment of certain Adversaries, while the latter would give countenance
to the profane, because according to the Adversaries there is no certain rule by
which the judgment must be made. But no such danger appears in the determination
of *Place.* For if the *Place* is merely suitable for hearing and for practicing
the other duties of piety, the Religious will have nothing to object, nor will
others have anything about which to lodge a complaint. Hence it is also evident
how great the difference between *Time and Place* is as far as Religion is
concerned.

16. By many arguments above we proved that the power to determine
*Principal-Solemn Time* belongs to God alone and not to men. But no argument can
prove that the power to determine the *Place* for practicing the worship of God
belongs to God alone. Therefore *Place and Time* differ greatly in relation to
religion.

17. No one can determine a *Time* for another person's solitary worship in such
a way as to bind his conscience to observe it. Nor can anyone determine a Time
for himself for such a use in such a way that he can observe it constantly,
since every day so many impediments or distractions which he cannot prevent can
arise. Yet such a Time is necessary to Religion and, as was said above,
*Principal-Solemn Time* ought also to include it. Hence it is sufficiently
established that the power to determine *Principal Time* belongs to God alone.
But as far as *Place* is concerned, no such necessity appears for determining a
place for private worship, since it makes no difference where this worship is
practiced, provided the person is alone, whether in a room or outside. And it is
evident that a solitary place is commanded, if possible, when solitary worship
is commanded, but conversely that solitary worship is not commanded whenever a
solitary place is commanded, since in a private place someone can perform both
carnal and spiritual business. Hence it is also evident how greatly *Place and
Time* differ and how absurd it is to say that they are equal adjuncts of
Religion.

18. There is a precept in the Decalogue concerning the observance of *Time,* but
there is no precept concerning the observance of *Place.* Hence it must be
concluded that *Place and Time* differ greatly and are not equal circumstances
of Religion.

19. Although all *Time* and *Place* originally belong to God, and no Place or
Time properly belongs to us except by divine indulgence, he has indulged and
freely granted us six days for performing our business and reserved for himself
one after the six working days. But as far as *Place* is concerned, he gave the
earth

[1674 logical printed p. 374 | IA leaf 392 | PDF p. 392]

to the children of men, *Psalm 115:16,* and now under the Gospel he reserves no
place for himself as he had done under the Old Testament. Therefore it is
evident that *Time differs greatly from Place.*

20. We saw above that a divine blessing is needed for any Time less than the
whole to be rendered *Sufficient* for Religion. But God blesses only his own
institutions and determinations, and he blessed the Time determined by himself,
*Genesis 2 and Exodus 20.* A Place, however, can in no way of itself advance the
good of our souls, since every *Place* is equal, provided it is suited to the
body. Consequently, it is not to be expected that God will annex his blessing
to this place and not that one. Nor did God ever bless any place for the sake of
religion except typically. Hence it is also clear that *Place and Time* are not
equal adjuncts of Religion, but differ greatly from one another.

21. We proved above that the observance of *Principal-Solemn Time* is a part of
worship and that this Time is properly sacred. But this cannot be said of
*Place,* for there is now no sacred place. Nor was a place ever regarded as
sacred Morally, but only Typically in former times. Synagogues are nowhere
called sacred places; indeed, not even the Temple's courtyard is called sacred.
Therefore *Place and Time* differ even with respect to Religion.

22. The Time Necessary to Religion, or Principal-Solemn Time, is pleasing and
acceptable to God, as we saw above. But there is now no *Place* which can be
called specially pleasing and acceptable to God for the sake of Religion, and
in this respect to be preferred above others. Therefore *Time differs greatly
from Place,* and they are not equal circumstances of Religion.

23. There is some Time now under the New Testament which is distinguished by a
special title and called the *Lord's Day,* and which thereby regards Christ in a
special manner. But we read nothing similar said concerning Place. Therefore
*Place and Time* differ.

24. The same Determination of Time with respect to *duration and Frequency* can
be observed by all the inhabitants of the earth. Indeed, the same beginning of
Time can also be observed, except for that difference which the diversity of
meridians would cause. Thus, at almost one and the same Time, absolutely all
people can worship God, whether publicly or privately. But it is impossible for
everyone to assemble in one *Place,* nor is it probable that everyone could
assemble together in places similar in every respect to practice worship. And
even if it were granted that all places built for holding public assemblies
would have the same dimensions, who would say that this is probable? Yet how
great the disparity between *place and Time* is even in this respect is evident
to everyone.

Therefore from these Thirty-four differences it is more than sufficiently
evident that *Time and Place* are not equal circumstances of Religion, whatever
our Adversaries may say. Thus far concerning these things.

The End of Book II.
