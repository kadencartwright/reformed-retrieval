Verse 10. Do not move the ancient boundary, and do not enter the field of the fatherless.

Verse 11. For their redeemer is strong; he himself will dispute their cause with you.

In יתומים, “of the fatherless,” there is a synecdoche of the part. In גאלם, “redeemer,” there is a metaphor transferred to God from men who, by reason of kinship, have the right to redeem another person's property.

The purpose is to deter men from inflicting injury upon the afflicted.

The first part of the first verse was discussed above at chapter 22, verse 28. In its latter part he indicates that boundaries are removed for this purpose: to invade other people's possessions. For boundaries are like walls, stone fences, embankments, and hedges, by which people's possessions are separated and distinguished from one another. Accordingly, someone who moves boundaries has already resolved to seize another person's property.

But when he specifically forbids anyone to enter the fields of the fatherless, he appears to censure the wickedness of very many people who, although they desire to scrape everything together for themselves, nevertheless refrain out of fear from touching the possessions of the more powerful, and plant their foot upon the possessions of the poor and those without protection, from which they think they cannot be dislodged. But verse 11 meets these beastlike men, who without any exercise of right reason are so uncontrollably carried away toward only what is present and before their eyes. This was discussed above at chapter 22, verse 23.

Nevertheless, something new appears in this verse, possessing great elegance. For a comparison is drawn from the law written in Leviticus 25:22, 24 and following, where it is taught that the land of Canaan belonged to God, not to the Israelites, and therefore no one was permitted to sell his estate. But if someone, compelled by poverty, had sold it and did not have the means to redeem the field or house, especially a country house, the person most closely related to him by blood was to recover it from the buyer's hand by paying the price the seller had received. But if even the kinsman could not redeem it, it was nevertheless to return to its original possessor in the year of jubilee. Looking to this custom, the prophet teaches that God is the defender of the fatherless, who will recover from the oppressor's hand the property unjustly taken from them. Therefore let oppressors consider another court, another tribunal, another judge, and advocates other than those they see with their eyes. Thus, if it is sound counsel not to contend with someone more powerful, we must take care again and again not to enter into judgment with God, who is at once most just and most powerful. No one readily oppresses a prince's servant; but who, then, will injure with impunity those who are not so much servants as sons of the King of kings? Such indeed are the fatherless and widows, provided they are believers.

### Translator's note

The Latin preserves the printed *Qoud* in *Qoud si*, understood as “but if” (*Quod si*), and the nasal abbreviation *aliū* for *alium*, “another.” “Redeemer” here is the kinsman-redeemer who recovers alienated property; Cartwright explicitly transfers that legal image to God's defense of the fatherless. The Hebrew headwords are retained as printed.
