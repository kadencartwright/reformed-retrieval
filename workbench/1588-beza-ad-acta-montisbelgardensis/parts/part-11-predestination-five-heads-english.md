## Part XI: Predestination—Five Preliminary Heads

First, they begin the distinction concerning people who in due time will be born—some to salvation, others to destruction—from the foreseen fall of the human race in Adam. But I ask: did God the maker not first, in the order of causes, determine within himself what that most excellent of all this world’s works would be and to what end he would make it, but only after foreseeing a defect in his work ordain what he wished to follow from it? Not even a leaf falls unless the Creator’s will precedes; all the hairs of our head are numbered; and the outcome of cast lots is from God, as Scripture testifies both against the Epicureans who deny providence and against the Peripatetics who establish only universal providence. Shall we then think that the will of the first human beings inclined itself to evil voluntarily and without God impelling them—which we both acknowledge—in such a way that the event happened with God unwilling, idly watching, or ignorant?

How much more correctly does Seneca speak, although he gravely erred concerning Stoic necessity, as we will say in its place: *That God always wills the same thing is the praise of his constancy; that he brings into effect whatever he decreed accords with his omnipotence; that his will is joined with power is a symmetry worthy of the ruler of all things.* Certainly, if not merely the fall of man, however spontaneous and voluntary, but even the smallest thing is withdrawn from God’s eternal decree and from his will—which, although sometimes hidden from us, is always just and to be adored by all—the foundation of his providence, by which he ordained every single thing from eternity, collapses.

The second head is that it does not appear true to say that the grace of salvation, which is certainly placed in Christ alone, is offered to every human being without exception, and consequently that foreseen stubbornness is the cause why God destined some to wrath. Even if we conceded what we have refuted elsewhere, that infants born from the godly embrace Christ by faith in their mothers’ wombs, what will become of so many godly infants who die before any use of reason? Indeed, what of so many myriads of adults who depart this life and perish outside Christ before they have learned the least thing about him by hearing? Finally, what of so many peoples and nations whom, before Christ’s advent, God—as the Apostle says in Acts 14:16—permitted to walk in their own ways toward destruction? He permitted it, as Augustine rightly concludes, not unwilling but willing. And what of those whom long ago, because the gospel was despised in past ages, he deserted and still deserts today?

Thus the same Paul assigns the entire human race to God’s just wrath not because of rebellion against the gospel, whether present or future, but because they despised God’s truth—that is, natural knowledge—and did not obey their conscience’s reproofs. Therefore not even the outward call to Christ which strikes the ears alone is universal, that is, common to every individual. Nor does it follow that only the elect are called: many are called, but few chosen; and, as Augustine rightly says, no one believes without being called, but not everyone who is called believes—

—This is true because tares are and will remain mixed with wheat in the outward church until the end of the ages. Therefore the gospel’s voice is proclaimed promiscuously and indefinitely—not to everyone, but to those whom God sees fit, including persons destined for wrath—not for salvation but for judgment; not that their hearts may be divinely softened, but that they may be hardened through their own heart. On this matter, Christian reader, see if you wish and consider carefully what Augustine writes in *Against Julian* 5.3.

Third, if what that opinion establishes were true—namely, that God wills no human being to be condemned, although not every individual is saved—then either his will is frustrated or the outcome of God’s will depends upon the human will. The efficacy of the divine purpose would thus be placed in human rather than divine power. Likewise, if God does not will to condemn those whom he condemns, force is surely applied to him, and of such a kind as rules over his will. But if he willingly condemns those whom he formerly willed to be saved, then he changes his judgment at human discretion. All these things are plainly far removed from God. I have already partly shown, and will show again in its place, how God does not mock human beings even if he does not draw everyone called, and indeed calls them so that, hardening themselves, they may fill up their measure. Likewise, although, as Augustine correctly writes, God’s will is the necessity of things—not Stoic but Christian necessity—I will explain hereafter why no blame for the destruction of the reprobate therefore resides with God.

Fourth, if their position holds, besides the grave error of confusing the causes executing the decree—that is, the condemnation of certain persons—with the cause for making the decree itself, a double absurdity is established. One is that in those who are condemned the foreseen causes of condemnation precede God’s decree to condemn them, although the order of causes, even in God, requires us to say that he first determined what he would do and afterward ordained the intermediate causes by which he would do it. The other is that, although the same knowledge or faculty pertains to contraries, according to the position I oppose, foreseen faith and its fruits are rightly and deservedly subordinated against the Pelagians to God’s decree concerning the salvation of those upon whom he wills to have mercy; but in the contrary case, foreseen corruption—or, as Andreae prefers, unbelief and stubbornness—precedes God’s decree itself concerning their condemnation to destruction.

Fifth, throughout this treatment they appear to me to confuse two most weighty questions concerning this subject. One asks what predestination itself is and of what nature, and concerns its causes and effects. The other debates the disclosure of predestination and its application to our use. These are plainly different. The former begins from the supreme principle which ordains all subordinate causes, namely God’s eternal purpose, which must be adored rather than searched. The Apostle prescribes this method in Romans 8:29–30 and throughout chapter 9, descending from the highest to the lowest.

But when the disclosure of this decree and application of this doctrine to particular persons are treated, the entire mind must be fixed—as the same Apostle does in Romans 8 through verses 29–30—upon Christ preached and offering himself to us in Word and sacraments. We ascend to him from effects, that is, from the good works which the Spirit of adoption works in the saints, to faith, and from faith to the hearing of the Word confirmed by the addition of sacramental promises. In Christ alone God’s eternal purpose concerning our adoption and salvation in Christ—hidden before that calling—is most certainly manifested to us by the Holy Spirit, who through the voices of gospel and minister gives clear and resounding testimony to our spirit.

We must wander neither from Christ nor from that outward call or inward drawing. Yet in that most grievous temptation concerning particular election, faith is sometimes so powerfully assailed in many ways by distrust and the sense of committed sins that it threatens ruin. Paul therefore leads us to that supreme principle, God’s eternal and immutable decree, so that, with the anchor of hope fixed in the truth and immutable firmness of his good pleasure, by which from eternity he determined for his own sake and not ours to have mercy upon his people, our heart—such hearts necessarily belong to those who have ascended from the lowest effects to Christ and overcome every temptation—may say with the Apostle: “If God is for us, who is against us?” “Who will bring charges against God’s elect?” and, “No created thing will separate us from God’s love, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.”

The elect’s full assurance therefore struggles within them, is agitated in the most grievous temptations, many examples of which are set before us especially in the Psalms, and sometimes comes near shipwreck, but it is never wholly submerged. The plank of repentance always supports it opportunely even in the midst of shipwreck. To this pertains, “Let him who stands take heed lest he fall”—not because true faith can fall away as concerns God’s decree, but because the means must be chosen by which the righteous person never falls so as not to rise again. So too Paul’s exhortation: “God’s firm foundation stands, having this seal: The Lord knows those who are his”; but, “Let everyone who names Christ’s name depart—

—from unrighteousness.” So also Peter’s admonition that we make our calling and election firm—that is, in ourselves.

The decree of reprobation does not possess an equal disclosure, because God, at times of his own choosing, very often ultimately prefers sinners who stand farthest from his kingdom and approach nearest the precipice of damnation—so that his goodness may shine all the more splendidly—not only to hypocrites but even to some who had tasted God’s good gift, as happened to Judas the traitor. Hence Christ says, “Tax collectors and prostitutes go before you,” and, “Wisdom was justified by her children”—namely by tax collectors and sinners, though despised by the Pharisees. Famous too are the examples of the sinful woman and the hanging thief, Christ’s saying concerning those called at the eleventh hour, and many similar cases. We must therefore despair of no one, even at the final breath, but invite everyone to repentance from God’s Word.

As God wills those fainting under sin’s burden to be raised by his ministry, and the faithful minister’s office tends to this, so those who please themselves in their sins must be terrified by preaching the law. If necessary, by setting before them that terrifying decree of eternal reprobation against the impenitent, they must be driven as it were to hell—not that they might perish there, but that, awakened instead, they may escape, as the Apostle says, the devil’s snare in which they are held captive.

I wished to preface these things, Christian reader, so that everyone may understand the matters at issue and the extent of our disagreement. I am ready to follow the judgment of learned and truly godly theologians devoted to the church’s truth and peace, especially in so grave a question, so remote from carnal sense, if the Lord should ever grant that this controversy too be determined in a properly and Christianly assembled council, in whose midst the Son of God, our Savior, sits as judge through his Word.

But come: let us now hear the theses proposed by each side.
