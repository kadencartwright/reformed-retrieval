# William Twisse, *Dissertation on Middle Knowledge* (1639)

## Book I — Part 41: Digression III, Section IX, Argument 6 and Vásquez's Objections

[Book I, printed p. 79; BSB canvas 00105]

Sixthly: It would follow that contingent futures are also known with certainty by an angel. For the
duration of an angel's substance, which is called *aevum*, is wholly simultaneous; therefore it
embraces simultaneously all things which will occur afterward, and all things exist simultaneously
for the angel.

NAVARRETE.

The sixth argument requires us to examine how future things coexist with an angel's substance and
with his operation and cognition. On this matter the disciples of St. Thomas, together with
Cajetan, answer that although an angel's substance is measured by *aevum* and contains all time as
one—and consequently all things which are future in time are present to that substance—angelic
operations are nevertheless measured by discrete time. Consequently, intellection does not attain
things which are future in time, nor are those things objectively present to such intellection. This
solution is true and must be maintained. But Vásquez argues against it in the third chapter cited.

I. First, from the real presence which things possess with respect to God in eternity, it follows
that they are objectively present to his intellect. Therefore, if things are really present to an
angel, they will also be objectively present. The antecedent is proved: we do not say that things
are known by God as present because they are objectively present to his intellect, for this would
prove the same by the same—namely, that things are known with certainty as present because they are
known with certainty.

II. Secondly, he argues specifically against Cajetan. In Cajetan's doctrine the intellection by
which an angel knows himself is measured by *aevum*, just as the angel's substance is. Therefore
things future in time are also present to this intellection in objective being.

III. Thirdly, the difficulty is increased if we speak at least of an intellection measured by a
single instant which lasts through one hour and coexists with all parts of that hour. It follows
that through it the angel simultaneously knows all the things which will occur in that hour.

Navarrete proceeds to meet these objections as follows. To understand the preceding solution it
must be noted that, because in God substance and operation are entirely the same, if the substance
is measured by eternity every operation is measured by the same measure. Thus, by the same real
existence by which things exist as present to God, they are also objectively present to his
intellection, provided that the character of presence is preserved in the manner due to each: to
the divine essence according to real presence, and to intellection according to objective presence.
Although presence in objective being is radically derived from real presence, nevertheless,
formally speaking, the measure of eternity in the act of the divine intellect requires immediate
presence in objective being.

Secondly, it must be noted that, because the entire perfection of divine intellection is derived
from the divine essence and no perfection is derived from the things known, the entire character of
the measure in that intellection is derived from things insofar as they are present in that same
essence. The perfection of angelic intellection, however, is also derived through its relation to
the things understood insofar as they exist outside the angel's intellect and essence. Hence the
perfection of the measure which is in an angel's essence insofar as it coexists with temporal future
things is not communicated to that angel's intellection; and on the side of the object which the
angel beholds outside his own essence, the intellection receives succession.

The first argument is therefore answered as follows. Although the character of the measure of
eternity in divine cognition is radically taken from the divine essence and from the real presence
by which future things are present to that essence, formally it is taken from a certain indivisible
coexistence with respect to all intelligible things according to the manner of the intellect itself.
Since nothing is called present to an intellect unless it is objectively present, the said measure
immediately requires that objective presence. This does not prove the same by the same, but explains
what presence the measure of eternity immediately requires in intellection.

[Book I, printed p. 80; BSB canvas 00106]

RESPONSE.

Previously you had said that the measure of eternity in the act of the divine intellect formally
requires immediate presence in objective being. Here you say that the measure of eternity in divine
cognition is formally taken from a certain indivisible coexistence with respect to all intelligible
things according to the manner of the intellect itself. Anyone may judge whether this later
explanation does more to illuminate the former statement or to obscure it. I acknowledge that the
formal character of a thing's existence in objective being differs from the character of its real
existence, for in objective being it exists only under the aspect of being knowable. But by the very
fact that it exists in any manner, it is posited as knowable as an existing thing, at least by the
divine intellect. The same must also be said with respect to the angelic intellect. Just as, by the
very fact that something exists in time, it is knowable intuitively by an angel, so by precisely the
same reasoning, by the very fact that something exists in angelic *aevum*, it must be posited as
intuitively knowable by the angel.

NAVARRETE.

The second argument is answered by saying that the intellection by which an angel knows himself is
measured by *aevum* insofar as it regards that special object, namely the essence and nature of the
same angel, but it is not measured by *aevum* of itself. The angel's essence is therefore measured
by *aevum* without any limitation and thus coexists with all things future in time. But the
intellection by which an angel knows himself is first limited by such an object, and the measure of
*aevum* consequently belongs to it under that limitation. Thus it does not coexist with all things
future in time; rather, by that indivisible and simultaneous cognition the angel knows only whatever
he can know about his own substance.

RESPONSE.

Granted that angelic cognition is measured by discrete time, nothing in this account prevents
angels from knowing all things which exist in time, although they do so by knowing one after
another. By the same reasoning nothing prevents them from knowing things which are future, although
they cannot know them simultaneously but one after another. It appears more than astonishing that
angels cannot see the things posited as existing before their very feet, namely in angelic duration
itself.

NAVARRETE.

The third argument is answered by saying that an angel's instantaneous cognition which coexists
with a part of our time is of itself and intrinsically determined to a special object. Thus, if the
angel is to know another object, that cognition is changed. When it is asserted that all things
which coexist with a cognition according to its proper measure can be attained through it, this must
be understood provided the intellection does not of itself imply limitation to one object. If it
does imply this, then properly speaking that one object alone coexists with it, and not other
objects.

RESPONSE.

Yet this limitation of angelic cognition to some one object is at the angel's pleasure. Although
while considering that object he cannot contemplate anything else, nevertheless, since it lies
within the angel's power to behold any of those things which are really present to him and are
therefore knowable, nothing will prevent him from abandoning the contemplation of one and devoting
himself to another. He will behold future things, since they are really present to him in angelic
duration, just as he beholds things present in their own temporal duration. Again, if you know that
future things are really present to an angel in *aevum*, then without doubt the angels themselves
know this. But if they do not know what those things are or how they are disposed, angels fare just
like blind human beings who know that many things are present to them but, because they are deprived
of sight, cannot behold what those things are or how they are disposed.

I note in passing that Navarrete's first distinction, which he judged must be prefaced to understand
the preceding solution of the argument—namely, the distinction concerning the identity of substance
and operation in God—was proposed by Navarrete to no purpose whatsoever.

To the arguments of Vásquez.

God knows all things insofar as they are in his essence without receiving perfection from the
things themselves. Therefore the coexistence of things in eternity is irrelevant to their
infallible cognition; it is only necessary that the things themselves be in the divine essence as
in the proper object of divine cognition.

NAVARRETE.

To this, he says, the answer is evident from what has been said. The argument proves that the
certainty of divine cognition in itself and intrinsically is not taken from that presence of things.
It does not prove, however, that insofar as among known things one is called the ground of another,
that presence is not also the ground of certainty.

RESPONSE.

This explanation, according to which he holds that the certainty of divine cognition must be drawn
from the real presence of things in eternity, is entirely new. For at folio 329, page 2, at the end
of the first column, he had said only that this certainty is drawn from the real existence of things
in eternity, not *a priori* but *a posteriori*. Here, however, he says that this certainty is drawn
from things known insofar as one is the ground of another. Lest he appear to wander beyond the
bounds while abandoning the presence of things in eternity as the ground of the certainty of
divine cognition and considering things thus far only insofar as one is the ground of another, he
therefore adds that this presence is the ground of certainty—or at least that nothing contrary to
it has been advanced by this argument of Vásquez. But I say that to consider things insofar as one
is the ground of another has nothing in common with considering their real presence. For this their
presence not in real but only in knowable being is sufficient. Moreover, existence in eternity and
cognition by God are attributed not only to those things of which one is the ground of another, but
to all other things as well.

He argues against the assertion of certain Thomists that this presence is necessary for future
things to be known intuitively by God. Antichrist, for example, now possesses nothing more than
another possible thing which will never exist. But such a thing is not known intuitively by God;
therefore neither is Antichrist.

[Book I, printed p. 81; BSB canvas 00107]

NAVARRETE.

Antichrist now—that is, in relation to this instant—possesses nothing more than possible things
which are not future. Yet in relation to eternity, which indivisibly encompasses all future things,
even now he possesses something more.

RESPONSE.

These distinctions concerning the relation of things to this instant and to eternity are
deliberately introduced by Navarrete to ruin the reader's judgment, lest it be freely applied to
deciding between the truth and the error which departs from it. If at this instant of time
Antichrist possesses nothing by which he differs from purely possible things, it is false that he
really existed in eternity from eternity. But at last let Navarrete not fear to affirm that
Antichrist now possesses something more than a possible thing possesses, namely insofar as he exists
in eternity, for this does not belong to possible things. Come then, let us subject this something,
whatever it may be, to examination. If a future thing now possesses something more than a merely
possible thing, this something more is either substantial, accidental, or both. You will say both,
because substances as well as accidents exist in eternity, and substances do not exist without
accidents. Therefore, I say, they existed in eternity from eternity. Since future things are
infinite—and categorically so, according to your opinion—it follows not only that many other things
besides God existed from eternity, but that an actually infinite number of things existed from
eternity according to their real being in eternity. Yet according to Aquinas an actually infinite
number of things cannot be given.

Again, if they possess real being in eternity from eternity, they possess it either from their own
nature, from God, or from some third source. They do not possess it from their own nature, for by
their own nature they are merely possible and consequently no more exist in eternity than merely
possible things do. Nor do they possess it from God. For if from God, then from God creating, God
knowing, or God willing. They do not possess it from God creating, for then it would be from God
creating either in eternity or in time. It is not from God creating from eternity, since creation
did not occur from eternity; nor is it from God creating in time, since an effect cannot be prior—
and infinitely prior—to its efficient cause. Nor is it from God knowing, both because God's
knowledge constitutes things only in known being, which is intentional and not real being, and
because Navarrete concedes that the existence of things in eternity precedes God's intuitive
knowledge. Finally, it does not arise from God willing, since God's will constitutes things only in
willed being, which is intentional rather than real being.

Secondly, if it is from God willing, then it is either from God's willing that things exist in
eternity, his willing that they exist in time, or both. If it is only in both ways, then God's merely
willing that they exist in time does not entail that they exist in eternity. But you insist that it
is necessary from the nature of eternity that whatever exists, or will exist, in time has already
existed in eternity from eternity. Perhaps you will say that it occurs in both ways insofar as one
necessarily follows from the other. Therefore, I say, either from God's willing that something
exist in time it is necessary that he also will it to exist in eternity from eternity, or from his
willing that something exist in eternity from eternity it necessarily follows that he wills it to
be made at some time. Neither can be maintained. For if God's willing that something be made in time
entails his willing that the same thing exist in eternity from eternity, it would follow that
because God wills a thing to be temporal, he therefore wills that same thing to be eternal.

Secondly, the relation which the will to produce a thing in time bears to the will to produce it in
eternity is the same as the relation which the production of the thing in time bears to its
production in eternity. Therefore, if the will to produce the thing in eternity follows from the
will to produce it in time, the production of that same thing in eternity likewise follows from its
production in time. But this is impossible: that which is later would be the cause of that which is
prior—and prior from eternity. Again, God's willing something to be made in eternity does not entail
that he wills the same thing to be made in time. For whatever God wills to be made in time, he wills
to be made according to some definite difference of time. But to will something to be made in
eternity no more implies that the thing will be future according to one difference of time than
another, because indivisible eternity possesses no differences corresponding to those of time.
Secondly, there appears to be no consequence if one says: God wills something to be present to him
in eternity; therefore he wills that same thing to be present at some time in time. Add that, before
these Doctors, it was never heard that God had decreed some things to be present in eternity. It
therefore appears that one should by no means say that God's willing something to exist in eternity
caused things to exist in eternity from eternity. Rather, one must say that this comes from God's
willing that they exist at some time. Yet even this appears wholly improbable. For to be willed to
exist in time is itself to be future; but from God's willing something to be future in time, it does
not follow that it is present in eternity, and that from eternity.
