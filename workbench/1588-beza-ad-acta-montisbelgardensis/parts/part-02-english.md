## Theodore Beza’s Preface to His Response to the Acts of the Montbéliard Colloquy Published at Tübingen

Part II.

Andreae inserted a long narrative at this point, adding, in his accustomed fashion, some things to his own replies and, on the other hand, taking not a few things away from mine. It was certainly quite unnecessary, since we nevertheless consented to continue the colloquy. But he plainly wished to seize this opportunity both to brand us—as though, distrusting our cause, we had fled from the disputation—and publicly to disseminate his extremely serious accusations with which he then assailed us: a sure indication, of course, of the peace among the churches which he had procured.

The matter stands as follows. When five whole days and more had been uselessly consumed in that utterly inept mode of disputation, Andreae, no longer sustaining the role of a disputant or of the other party, but addressing us in the name of the most illustrious prince himself, unexpectedly requested that predestination be disputed next. We, on the other hand, modestly asked leave of the most illustrious prince to depart. First, in the letters by which he had summoned us, he had stated no other reason for this meeting than that unhappy controversy about the Lord’s Supper; nor had our cities sent us for any other reason. Second, it appeared that this extremely weighty question could not be debated publicly in that assembly, with opposing opinions produced on each side, without causing offense to some people; nor could it be concluded in a short time, whereas, with the feast of Easter now approaching, we could not conveniently be absent from our churches. We added, however, that we were prepared, if it pleased his Highness, to set forth privately before him our position on this matter also, confirmed by testimonies of the divine Word;

[Part II | PDF page 6 | logical source scan 5 | printed page 4]

and, if Andreae proposed any theses to us, to carry them home and prepare there at leisure a considered reply to them. Thus the conference of that day, March 25, was concluded.

On the following day, however, when we met again and presented a petition, we partly repeated the same reasons for our refusal of a more extensive conference and partly requested that, since we agreed upon the principal and essential heads of the doctrine of the Lord’s Supper—namely, the signs and the things signified, both against the transubstantiators and against those who establish bare signs—and since there was sufficient agreement about the useful reception of Christ’s body and blood by faith alone, we should henceforth sincerely attest our mutual brotherhood, all bitterness being removed and those party names abolished. In this way an entrance would be prepared toward fuller agreement concerning the mode of the presence and the eating of the unworthy.

We and likewise the hearers were then ordered to withdraw for a little while. The Württemberg theologians, however, remained with the most illustrious prince and deliberated together with him—an altogether novel example. Shortly afterward we were recalled, for the deliberation was very brief, and heard Andreae once again sustaining a double role and discoursing at length in his usual fashion. This was the substance of his words.

The most illustrious prince had made no mention in his letters of the other three heads—Andreae in fact added two of them for the first time even then—because he had not understood that we disagreed about them too. The prince was indeed prepared to spare neither labor nor expense in applying a remedy to these evils. If it did not please us to confer about these three as well, Andreae said that it nevertheless seemed expedient to him that the theses about these heads, written by us, should be read aloud in the hearing of everyone and afterward carried home by us, as we had requested, so that we might reply to them when and as it seemed good to us. These things, then, he said in the name of the most illustrious prince,

[Part II | PDF page 7 | logical source scan 6 | printed page 5]

to which he afterward added of his own whatever he wished. As the matter itself shows, he feared that, if we did not consent to the public recital of his theses, he would lose what seemed to him an excellent opportunity to bring us into universal odium on these heads also. How partly absurd and partly manifestly distorted in another direction were the things which he then said against us, no less bitterly than shamelessly, I would have the reader learn from his published writing. I have nevertheless thought it good to submit their substance here, adding a refutation to each one.

1. *The doctrine of predestination is not merely useful but necessary, and it is therefore requisite that hearers also be rightly instructed about it in sermons.* Who does not know that? Let Andreae show, if he can, either that this part of Christian doctrine has been neglected by our men in writing or preaching, or that it has been treated more rightly and accurately by his men than by ours.

2. *Unless this doctrine is rightly explained according to the analogy of faith, a window is opened to the life of the Epicureans.* This is entirely true. But do those teach according to the analogy of faith who manifestly, either with the Pelagians, establish the foreseeing of faith and good works as an antecedent cause of the eternal decree of election, or necessarily refute themselves when explaining the other member of the divine decree, fearing nothing from those thunderous words of Paul, “Before they had done anything good or evil”? I send them away to Luther’s school.

And do those commend an Epicurean life who maintain that God’s decree is indeed immutable, because no change can befall God, but meanwhile teach that things which God has joined by an indissoluble bond must never be separated? That is, the intermediate causes by which his decree is executed must never be severed from his eternal and infallible decree of election or reprobation, to be executed in its proper time: in the elect, the causes of salvation, namely faith embracing Jesus Christ and his Spirit acting in the elect; but in the reprobate, the causes of condemnation, namely corruption and its rotten and death-bearing fruits.

3. *Third, when that doctrine is badly explained, it gives occasion for despair*

[Part II | PDF page 8 | logical source scan 7 | printed page 6]

*to consciences afflicted and tempted by Satan.* I agree. But which people heal wounded consciences with sure and appropriate remedies—both the consciences of those who have never yet been called even outwardly by the preaching of the Word, or have not yet apprehended by any faith the salvation offered in the gospel, and the consciences of those whose faith has been lulled to sleep either by negligence or by some very grave fall?

Is it not those who apply to them these most firm divine consolations: that some are called and given faith even at the eleventh hour; that outward calling also, unless the failure lies with us, is a pledge of the divine will to save us; that the doors of the merciful God must be knocked upon, even if they do not open at the first repeated blows; that the effect of baptism, although sometimes delayed by the fault of the baptized until their last breath, nevertheless cannot become empty for those who repent; that those to whom God’s mercy is offered through the ministry of the gospel by God’s singular will perish not simply through sin but through unbelief; and, finally, that true faith is given irrevocably to the elect, and therefore, although it sometimes appears entirely extinguished, it is not lost, but must be rekindled by prayers and by applying both earnest detestation of sin and the promises of the truthful God?

Or do those not provide far worse for troubled consciences who recall fallen sinners to the first principles of faith and hope, yet take what learned theologians have written about a temporary interruption of the Holy Spirit’s energy and of his manifested efficacy and extend it to the loss of all grace itself? After bidding consciences return to the truth of God’s Word and to the full assurance of faith, they sever the promise of perseverance from the gift of faith as though it were not irrevocable. From this necessarily follows uncertainty about election itself and a shaking of the foundation of faith. Thus they themselves immediately overthrow the very thing by which they wish to raise the fallen and upon which they wish those who stand to lean.

4. *The doctrine of predestination ought not to be banished to the schools in such a way that there is no need to set it before the rude and unlearned people.* We exclude neither this nor any other head of our religion from the pulpits of lecture halls or from the teaching chairs of churches. But who denies that the method of teaching and disputing on each side differs from that of setting doctrine forth in public sermons and applying it to the use of hearers—or, as Paul speaks, of rightly dividing it? Thus too the offices of pastors and doctors are distinct in the house of God.

[Part II | PDF page 9 | logical source scan 8 | printed page 7]

Besides, what wise person would not in this matter prefer the Apostle’s counsel and example—giving milk before solid food to those who are still infants in Christ—to following this so-confident doctor, who thinks the space of one hour sufficient for explaining the mysteries of so difficult and manifold a question? “No prudent person,” Christ says, “puts new wine into old wineskins.” How much less wise, then, is one who thinks first about putting the finest wine into vessels still fouled by dregs and sour wine, instead of thinking about cleansing and preparing them?

I also marvel that it could enter Andreae’s mind that so difficult a doctrine, one so contrary to human reason, ought to be set before the rude and unlearned, when he thinks so great an account must be taken of those whom he calls weak—even after the gospel has now been restored in Germany for seventy years and more—that he judges that images, which are true and manifest tinder for idolatry, should not even now be readily removed from churches. At least let him hear Luther discussing this matter in the preface to the Epistle to the Romans: “The old Adam must first be mortified, the sense of the flesh worn down, and those nursing in Christ must first grow up before they drink this unmixed wine. For here too there is a certain infancy of its own, which meanwhile needs milk until it becomes accustomed to solid food.”

5. *We, whom Andreae calls the opposing party, teach concerning predestination that by an eternal, hidden, and immutable decree God does not will the greater part of humanity to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth.* But what if God had decreed to save absolutely no one, but to sentence all human beings who would ever be born once for all to eternal punishment, just as he did all the evil spirits who fell? Would Andreae say that God would thereby have done anyone an injury, without his own conscience reproving him? God is certainly bound to the human race by no more necessary bond than to all the animals upon whose slaughter he has permitted human beings to live. He is not therefore to be proclaimed cruel to beasts but beneficent to us, with thanksgiving, as the Apostle admonishes us.

[Part II | PDF page 10 | logical source scan 9 | printed page 8]

Therefore, that from eternity God decreed to single out some from the human race—people utterly lost by their own fault alone—and destined them for gratuitous salvation in Christ is a signal act of grace. That he determined from eternity to afflict the rest, left in their sins, with just punishments befits a just judge. This divine decree cannot be denied without also abolishing the decree of election, that is, the foundation of our faith and salvation. For no one who takes everyone can be said to choose; it is the one who determines to receive some and reject others. Neither of these can exist without the other.

Again, since this decree is divine, it is necessarily eternal and indeed immutable, unless with notable blasphemy we suppose either that God adopted some plan in time, or that he decrees uncertainly the things he decrees, or that he is mutable. What they teach about foreknowledge is empty, as though it either preceded that supreme decree in order or were separated from it. On the contrary, if you consider the order in which things exist, something must first be decreed to be future before God foreknows it as future. Unless this is granted, we shall fall into one or both of these blasphemies, which say that things exist either of themselves or by chance. Some people think that it follows from this that God is the author of evils, but that is the worst possible inference, as will be explained in its proper place.

We call that decree hidden for two reasons. One is that it is not for human judgment to determine definitely and without exception which individual persons that dread decree concerns. Christian charity instead commands us to hope well even of the greatest sinners and to offer prayers for them before God, although otherwise it must be maintained universally that whoever dies outside Christ is a vessel of wrath.

[Part II | PDF page 11 | logical source scan 10 | printed page 9]

The other reason is that the causes by which this decree is executed—both the salvation of the elect and the destruction of the reprobate—have been revealed to us. The cause of the former’s salvation is Christ, apprehended by faith, freely justifying the elect. The causes of the latter’s condemnation are the propagation into the entire human race of guilt and corruption derived from our first parent—to which infants themselves in their mothers’ wombs are also subject—and afterward the rotten fruits of that corruption. But why God decreed to choose and save these people in Christ, to leave others outside Christ and finally to condemn them justly, and indeed why these rather than those, is an inscrutable cause to us: namely, his will alone, hidden from us in very many matters, as Augustine says, but always just.

Therefore those gravely wander in this mystery who do not distinguish this eternal decree from its execution, which occurs in time, and falsely establish foreseen faith and good works in those who are chosen, but foreseen unbelief and sins in those who are left, as an antecedent cause of the decree. Those, however, sin more gravely and more absurdly who rightly reject the former against the Pelagians but embrace the latter, perversely fearing that if they maintain otherwise the cause of the reprobate’s condemnation will be attributed to God. Yet the rationale common to contraries is necessarily the same. Just as Paul, speaking of Jacob, says that he was loved—that is, destined for the love which would follow in its proper time—while he was not yet born and before he had done anything good, so also he says of Esau that he was hated—that is, destined for hatred and destruction—likewise before he was born and had done anything evil.

“Therefore,” Andreae says, “God does not will all people to come to the knowledge of the truth, though the Apostle affirms it.” On the contrary, he does will it, if in keeping with the Apostle’s meaning you understand ALL not as every individual within the classes but as all classes of individuals, together with all learned and capable interpreters and as the sense of the passage itself demands. Otherwise, let Andreae teach us whether some can come to this path of truth when God is unwilling and opposed,

[Part II | PDF page 12 | logical source scan 11 | printed page 10]

or whether God can will that they come to it and yet they do not come. I know that he will deny the former; but whichever he asserts, God will equally be judged not to be God—that is, to be powerless—because either he will thus not will what nevertheless occurs, or he will will what nevertheless does not occur.

There is no basis for anyone to object here that what God wills without condition necessarily occurs, and likewise what he wills without exception not to occur does not occur, while the outcome of those things which God conditionally wills or does not will depends upon fulfillment of the condition, of which kind is the matter now in question. That is, God wills ALL to come to the knowledge of the truth, but under the condition, “If they believe.” Though this possesses some appearance of truth, it is spoken most absurdly.

First, the firmness of the divine counsel is made dependent upon the human will or other intermediate causes, which cannot be done without blasphemy. On the contrary, all intermediate causes conspire with the determination of the first cause: contingent causes voluntarily, but necessary causes necessarily. Next, how will this condition, “If they believe,” be required of those who do not possess the power to fulfill it? The whole exception is therefore either empty, or a free choice to believe or not believe—that is, one flexible toward either part—will be attributed to everyone. It will be attributed either by nature, which is purely Pelagian, or by grace, which the matter itself proves utterly false and which is further demonstrated as follows.

The truth about which we now inquire is certainly not that whose traces remain in the human mind and which, the Apostle says, people suppress in unrighteousness. It is the saving truth revealed to us in the gospel, which flesh and blood do not know and about which the natural person cannot even think. Since this is so, if God wills ALL—that is, every human being universally and without exception—to come to salvation, as these men interpret the Apostle’s passage and others like it, it follows necessarily that this truth, effectually joined with the capacity to believe, must be instilled in the minds of every individual either by an extraordinary inspiration or by the ministry of the preached Word.

[Part II | PDF page 13 | logical source scan 12 | printed page 11]

I judge that no one of sound mind can affirm either alternative. At least the many thousands of infants outside the Church who perish either in childbirth itself or before the use of reason would have to be excepted from their number. The same judgment must be made about by far the greater multitude of peoples who, it is evident, have died before, and die daily, without hearing even the name of Christ and the gospel.

It remains, therefore, that the term “all” must by no means be understood of individual human beings. Rather, it signifies that, without regard to age, sex, condition, or nation, the Church which we call catholic is divinely chosen and gathered from every kind of people. Therefore, whoever God decreed from eternity not to dignify with the knowledge of his truth—doing no one an injury at all even if he destroyed every individual universally, since we are all conceived and born in sins—will never come to the truth and will not even wish to come to it, whether he is outwardly called or is not called. On the other hand, whoever God determined from eternity to save through his truth will either be saved from the womb, as one born in the Church, comprehended in the covenant, and given to Christ; or in his own time he will be drawn by the Father to Christ—that is, as Augustine rightly and aptly interprets Christ’s saying, “From unwilling he will become willing.”

Yet Andreae testifies that he condemns this doctrine, although it is confirmed both by the common notions concerning God, by almost innumerable testimonies of Scripture, and finally by invincible arguments, as “erroneous and false, depriving troubled consciences of all consolation and utterly abolishing the use of the whole ministry of the Word and sacraments.” But it is well that these are not thunderbolts belonging to Almighty God—to whom eternal praise and glory be given both as the most merciful Savior in Christ of his elect and as the most just avenger of the sins of the reprobate outside Christ—but Andreae’s sooty flashes. I pray that the same God, the Best and Greatest, give him more knowledge and less self-confidence.

6. *We deny that baptism is the washing of regeneration, but say that it is only its*

[Part II | PDF page 14 | logical source scan 13 | printed page 12]

*seal and confirmation.* Beside these words a new observation is placed in the margin: *The sum of Theodore Beza’s doctrine of baptism.* On the contrary, among Andreae’s many other utterly false calumnies this is not the least. For what Christian has ever denied that baptism is the washing of regeneration, which the Apostle testifies in express words? But just as, in Andreae’s judgment, the one who denies that Christ’s body is in, under, or with the bread—indeed, that it is present everywhere in its substance and is eaten by anyone at all with the mouth itself—abolishes the Lord’s holy Supper and denies that the bread is the Lord’s body delivered for us, so he supposes that baptism is not held to be the washing of regeneration by one who does not attribute at least in some measure to the water of baptism and the outward washing what could not without impiety be granted even to angels—namely, the work of the Holy Spirit alone. This will be explained more fully in its proper place, God willing.

7. *Not all infants who are baptized are regenerated, but innumerable baptized children are condemned eternally by God’s hidden decree.* At least Andreae should except adult catechumens who are hypocrites. If he acknowledges this, as he is compelled to do, come, then: from what passage of Scripture, or relying on what argument, will he say that the condition of infants is better than that of adults? And why should it be less permissible to think that some are baptized without benefit than that the Lord’s holy Supper is taken by some unto destruction?

If Andreae objects with what he himself contended during the disputation—namely, that all infants offered for baptism in the Church possess true faith, because regeneration presupposes the faith by which Christ is embraced—how will he prove concerning all baptized children what he does not dare affirm concerning all adults? But even if for now we grant him this, what will become of those who afterward clearly demonstrate by their wicked life that they have not been regenerated? It is certain that there are all too many such people, Christians in name and nothing less in reality, just as formerly there were far more Jews circumcised in the flesh but in reality enemies of Christ. Andreae will doubtless answer that they afterward fell away from faith and regeneration. But if that is true,

[Part II | PDF page 15 | logical source scan 14 | printed page 13]

then neither is our election certain nor is there full assurance of faith. When these are removed, where is the hope, where the consolation, of Christians? But more fully about these matters in their proper place.

So that this calumny may be entirely clear, we have learned neither to think nor to speak in this way about baptized people, whether children or adults. On the contrary, we teach that complete baptism is always divinely offered to those rightly baptized, whether they believe or do not believe. Although it is not necessarily presumed that those born in the Church are children of God and of the promise—for, as the Apostle says, they are not all children because they are Abraham’s seed, but only those who are children of the promise are reckoned as seed—nevertheless this is presumed probably and by no means without reason, while God’s secret counsels are left with him. Accordingly, the adoption of the covenant is sealed in them; this is afterward discerned more certainly in adults from the effects of the Holy Spirit. If these things do not satisfy Andreae, are we therefore profane? Does Beza deny that baptism is the washing of regeneration, rather than Andreae himself being caught in an entirely manifest calumny?

8. “There is also a controversy between us,” Andreae said, “about the demolition of images: whether images which appear to furnish an occasion for idolatry ought to be removed from sight before they are removed from the mind. There is no controversy that images openly exposed for idolatry ought to be abolished.” An excellent controversy indeed, and one befitting a veteran doctor of theology: whether idolaters are to be reckoned among the weak; whether one heals them who thinks that the tinder of this idolatry should be retained; whether the commandment of the first table concerned only the Jews, which Luther and Brenz wrongly affirm but Andreae rightly denies; likewise, whether in the second commandment God superfluously prohibited not merely the adoration of images but also their making for the sake of any religion; and thus whether God forbade only the worship of images as gods, while by no means forbidding that he himself be worshiped in any images; and whether those acted rightly or otherwise who, roughly four centuries after the apostles’ preaching, first introduced pictures and finally statues also into religious places, although they gave no thought to the idolatry which soon followed.

[Part II | PDF page 16 | logical source scan 15 | printed page 14]

If images are not to be removed at once by a Christian magistrate, as Andreae affirms, Beza asks what period he wishes assigned to this weakness, and whether sixty years and more ought not to have sufficed for removing images from the minds of idolaters. He asks further how it can be judged, more readily than from manifest kneeling and gestures of that kind, which statues people misuse for worship, and thus which images ought long since to have been abolished in very many German churches. Finally, Beza asks, if Andreae speaks sincerely, why he has not removed crosses and the idols of crucifixes; indeed, why he has himself either arranged for them to be restored somewhere after their removal or permitted their restoration, since no idols render people more prone to idolatry.

9. “There is also a controversy whether the true preaching of the gospel and use of the sacraments can be celebrated in those churches in which the papists practiced their idolatry, or whether they should instead be overthrown. You sustain great odium on this account, because in previous years so many churches in Gaul and Belgium were razed to their foundations.” Pure calumnies, and recounted by Andreae at that time and place for no other reason than to satisfy his desire to brand us. It is not credible that anyone could be so egregious a liar as to invent that the French ever doubted that God’s true worship should be restored in the papists’ own churches. Among the things which they always urged most strongly before the royal majesty was that some churches be conceded to them; and wherever our men gained the advantage, scarcely anything was requested more strongly by the papists than that their churches be restored to them. Nor can I think otherwise about the Belgians. Andreae cannot be ignorant that throughout all Switzerland the Word of God is taught and the sacraments administered nowhere but in those churches. I will reply in its proper place concerning the violent overthrow of churches, of which Andreae would gladly make us the authors or counselors against his own conscience.

These things, then, he said. I concealed my answers, lest I stir the fire, and asked again to be dismissed.

[Part II | PDF page 17 | logical source scan 16 | printed page 15]

With the prince’s gracious leave I replied that the public recital of Andreae’s theses did not seem equitable to us, lest the suspicion enter the hearers’ minds that we were conscious of holding those doctrines which he had already charged against us and which he was doubtless going to repeat in his theses.

At this, once again with equal shamelessness and sustaining the role of the most illustrious prince, after saying many things about his Highness’s piety and zeal—about which assuredly none of us doubted—what reproaches did he not hurl against us? If his words are to be believed, “in asserting his doctrine he produced the clear, open, and manifest Word of God, while we produced nothing except arguments of human reason and human glosses. This ill-fated and destructive conflict concerning the Lord’s Supper and Christ’s person arose from no one but us. They have always retained, and still retain, the plain sense of the words of Christ’s testament, whereas we corrupt Christ’s testament and twist his clearest words into an alien sense.” In short, what did he not say?

Then, not fearing to add mockery to this insult, he said, “We shall devote ourselves to public peace and tranquility. We are earnestly and deeply affected by the misery of the French churches. We pray diligently for them, and shall pray as long as we live, and we promise that as far as our part is concerned we shall never fail them.” Who here would believe you or anyone of your faction, Andreae? Are you not the man who shortly before this very colloquy published that most poisonous writing in which, following Brenz’s testament, you sounded the trumpet against the French who embraced the gospel, not merely as most destructive heretics but also as seditious people rebelling against their magistrates? Are you not the man who dared to call us Mohammedans in this very colloquy? Are you not those from whose mouths nothing is heard daily but the names Zwinglians and Calvinists, and whose chief concern in Christian religion is to expose us to the hatred of everyone as corrupters of Christ’s testament, as blasphemers against Christ’s own person, and as people who make God the author both of crimes and of the evils of condemnation? Is it not customary for you to call so many most holy

[Part II | PDF page 18 | logical source scan 17 | printed page 16]

martyrs—who sealed the truth of the gospel with their blood while you rested safely in your heated rooms—martyrs of the devil? And from where, I ask, comes this sudden change? But it is well, and we give thanks to God, the Best and Greatest, that among most of the devout princes of Germany—including, far be it from us not to count the most illustrious prince of Montbéliard, celebrated for undertaking an embassy on our behalf all the way to Paris—truth, piety, and equity possessed more weight than all your demagogic clamors. Through these at last, as I hope, your stratagems will be disclosed, and both the goodness of our cause and our innocence will become manifest.

But to return to the matter: when Andreae had thus taken his friendly and courteous leave of us, and we had departed so as to prepare to leave on the following day, we nevertheless learned the next day that Andreae and Osiander were singing a triumph as victors, as though we had fled from them, and that the most illustrious prince himself bore this sudden breaking off of the conference most indignantly. We did not think that we ought to depart in his displeasure. Finally, we feared that a departure of this kind might harm the French exiles, who in great number had fled to Montbéliard and been most kindly received by the illustrious prince. Therefore, although we foresaw sufficiently where the remaining proceedings would end, we nevertheless changed our judgment in the morning, greeted the most illustrious prince, and consented to the ensuing continuation of the conference. We added only the condition that, because the feast of Easter was near, the disputation should not be prolonged further; and thus at last we proceeded once more to hear Andreae’s declamations.

## Theses of the Württemberg Theologians Concerning Papist Churches, Images, Musical Organs in Church, and Their Reformation

### Matters Placed Beyond Controversy

I.

Churches dedicated to divine worship, so that in them the Lord’s Word may be taught and heard and the sacraments distributed and received, must, when they have been defiled by idolatry, be cleansed according to the Lord’s Word.

### Response to the Theses Concerning Images

To I. WE AGREE.

### Dr. Jakob Andreae’s Marginal Annotations, Briefly Replying to Beza’s Theses Presented After the Colloquy Had Ended

1. It was therefore frenzy, not zeal—or at least zeal not according to knowledge—that in many places in Gaul and Belgium they demolished a very great number of immense churches and razed them to their foundations.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Marginal Annotation

It is clear, first, that not everyone who conducted himself in this way by destroying images on private authority must indiscriminately and immediately be judged insane. A serious zeal to detest idolatry cannot proceed from madness. The whole previous life and sincere confession of the divine name amid the flames demonstrated that not a few were led by this zeal. Next, it is evident that in not a few places in Belgium the magistrates’ commands preceded those acts of destruction.

In France, however, I acknowledge that sin was committed in these matters not merely through imprudence but also through misdirected impulse and in various other ways, as commonly happens in wars. Yet, following Lutheran hyperboles, I would not call all who failed to observe the proper measure madmen, bloodthirsty people, and murderers. I know certainly that in this way injury would be done to many truly godly people who sinned not from perverse but from imprudent zeal. Indeed, when under Constantine a certain zeal of this kind for God carried Christians so far against pagan idols, the ancient bishops did not condemn as madmen or people guilty of some crime those who happened to be killed by the pagans during the overthrow of idols. They determined only that they should not be numbered among the martyrs. Canon 60 of the Synod of Elvira.

[Part II | PDF page 20 | logical source scan 19 | printed page 18]

The overthrow of the churches themselves is less capable of excuse and certainly is not approved by me. Nevertheless, as I know well and as the cities themselves afterward testified, in most places it occurred neither by violent impulse nor rashly. Rather, the very location of those churches necessarily required it in that most sorrowful war into which our people were driven for the sake of protecting their safety against the violators of public peace.

But if, Andreae, all those whose zeal while war raged was not thus far according to knowledge are once for all madmen to you because they did not take the proper account of time and order, by what name will you call those who, not while war raged but in settled peace, persuaded Ulrich—the most illustrious prince and duke of Württemberg of blessed memory, the present duke’s grandfather—to turn the church of the Tübingen monks into a stable? I saw this while present, but never heard it criticized by you.

### Thesis II of the Württemberg Theologians

*Images, whether painted or sculpted, by which sacred histories and subjects are represented, are a matter indifferent.*

### Antithesis II of Theodore Beza

We acknowledge that painting and sculpture have great use in civil affairs, which are not at issue here. Experience itself shows, however, that their use for representing sacred histories, although in itself indifferent, does more harm than good if they are introduced into sacred places, because of the mind’s supreme propensity toward idolatrous worship. Nor did those money changers, sellers, and buyers lack a plausible pretext from ancient custom, priestly authority, and other reasons.

Andreae says, “We do not at all refer God’s command concerning prohibited images to the ceremonial laws.” Then let him settle the matter with Luther and with his own Brenz. For in discussing on Exodus 20 this very prohibition against making and worshiping images, Brenz says: “This is simply prohibited in the Mosaic polity, and it was not permissible publicly to establish images in the Church even if they were neither WORSHIPED nor ADORED, just as it was simply prohibited for anyone to eat pork. This too is a part of the ceremonial commandment which pertains only to the Mosaic polity, as do circumcision, the Sabbath, and so forth.”

What, then, of Luther? In treating Deuteronomy 4, where by the same command Israel is ordered to destroy both the makers of images and the images themselves, he runs entirely beyond bounds. Turning from the rash iconoclasts—whom, although exaggerating, he rightly rebukes—to the general question whether images ought to be removed in any manner, Luther says: “Since it is established that among Christians the impious must not be killed with the material sword,” and that this part of the law has been abrogated as merely ceremonial and temporal until Christ, “it follows that images also must not be removed by any necessity, since they belong to the same part of the law.”

Yet soon afterward he restrains himself somewhat: “Although I also,” he says, “do not greatly love images and would wish them not to be set up in a church, I do not consider only the fact that they are worshiped, which I think occurs more rarely, but that confidence in the work is placed in their value and appearance, as though by this work some service were rendered to God. Meanwhile the expense is wasted, and everything spent upon them ought to be spent for better uses in the necessity of the brethren.”

Thus Luther, agreeing with Brenz that the command to remove images—and therefore also against making them for the sake of any religion or sanctity—is part of the ceremonial law. Yet I am compelled to find much else wanting in these words. First, Luther’s opinion that the worship of images is rare and is not the principal cause of that divine prohibition is refuted not only by the Lord’s own express words and the rebukes so often pressed by the prophets, but finally by experience itself, which even today meets everyone’s eyes. Next, as concerns the expense and confidence in the work as though holy, from where does this arise except from the very fountain of idolatry? Images are regarded as sacred things, although God willed that not even living saints or angels should be honored with outward religious adoration, much less any mute and dead images.

Since this can occur not only in public sacred places but also in private houses—indeed, scarcely any house of superstitious people lacks private sacred images before which they prostrate themselves and invoke saints or Christ himself—I would not rashly approve even Luther’s words in the same place: “I cannot condemn unassuming painted images in a private house.” The most sorrowful examples of Micah in Judges 17 and Gideon in Judges 8:27 teach—

[Part II | PDF page 33 | logical source scan 32 | printed page 31]

—with how much prudence and circumspection even historical paintings of this kind must be used, lest Satan find a crack by this means as well.

To 4.

To that stale argument from the two cherubim seated upon the Lord’s ark in the sacred building—an argument Andreae drew from the filthy pools of those who defend idolatry—we replied that they were placed in the sacred place by God’s command in such a fashion that the people could no more misuse them for idolatry than the ark itself, since they were placed in the innermost sanctuary of the temple beyond people’s sight. What does Andreae answer? “It makes no difference,” he says, “that the people did not see those images. The question is whether it is lawful, by their example, to paint or sculpt images.” As though any of us condemned painting or sculpture in itself!

But if Andreae is dealing with things which pertain in any way to religion and conscience, I reply boldly and without exception in God’s own words: it is unlawful not only to adore any kind of image but even to make it for any sacred use. Since sacred places in particular are destined for holy worship alone, it is shameful and intolerable—to use Epiphanius’s words—for any such thing to be seen in Christian churches. I affirm that this is how the command must be explained, how the Lawgiver himself understood it, and how the true Jewish and the pure Christian Church judged it. Andreae cannot deny this without inexcusable impudence. His claim that it made no difference whether the Israelites saw those images does not even need refutation among people of sound judgment.

To 5.

Andreae produced another argument drawn from the images of lions and oxen by which certain vessels and furnishings of the temple were supported according to God’s command. Why does he not also produce the pomegranates and lily flowers, and the cherubim woven into the tabernacle’s curtains in embroidered work? All this is absurd. At the command of God, who by no means disagrees with himself, those things were made and placed so that they were mere ornaments of the objects and could no more be misused for any worship of God than the vessels and furnishings themselves. How, then, do these things pertain to our subject?

To 6, 7, and 8.

We said it would have been better if the bronze serpent fashioned in the wilderness at God’s command had never been exposed to everyone’s eyes once its typological use had ended. We also said that Christian princes in our times ought to have imitated, and still ought to imitate, the example of the most holy king Hezekiah in utterly abolishing it. Andreae replies that the sin did not consist in that serpent’s being exposed to everyone’s eyes, since by looking upon it the godly could confirm their faith every day; rather, blame for the idolatry must be attributed to negligent or even idolatrous priests.

Andreae is gravely mistaken if he thinks that in the time of Moses or in those holy times the serpent was placed either in the tabernacle or in the Lord’s temple. Moses or the godly kings would never have done so contrary to the Lord’s—

[Part II | PDF page 34 | logical source scan 33 | printed page 32]

—command, and history does not explain where that serpent stood in Hezekiah’s time. It is probable, however, that after the people plunged headlong into every kind of idolatry, they misused this image somewhere with a peculiar religious devotion, just as they did very many memorials of the holy fathers. What could the Jews have done here that Christians have not eagerly rendered to their crosses and crucifixes?

If Hezekiah is proclaimed worthy of the highest praise because, not content to arrange that the people be better instructed, he did not spare that image, though fashioned by divine command and preserved through so many centuries as a memorial of so great a miracle, what excuse can Christian princes now offer for their negligence in abolishing once and for all those crosses and images of the Crucified in particular? These scarcely entered Christian sacred places in the fifth century, yet gradually became defiled with every impurity of dreadful idolatry—to pass over innumerable other idols which godly men cannot even look upon without horror.

To 9.

Andreae says, “The spiritual depiction of Christ mentioned in Galatians 3 does not conflict with an outward picture.” I grant that it does not conflict in itself, but it is incongruous whenever such things are made or possessed for the sake of any sacred use, even apart from adoration and worship. No vigilance can prevent this incongruity, especially if the images are set up in places ordained for the worship of God alone, as the experience of so many centuries has proved. For this reason Brenz, as I said before, rightly teaches that “in the sacred Mosaic polity it was not permissible publicly to set up images in the Church even if they were not adored.” Who does not see that this commandment, contrary to Brenz’s own opinion, must be observed no less diligently in the Christian Church?

Unless perhaps we dream that Christians are less inclined to idolatry than the Israelites formerly were, although Christians now have a much more plausible pretext than the Jews once did, before whom only the gross idols of pagans were set; and unless we imagine that the Christian Church has had, or now has, more diligent pastors than Moses, Aaron—whom even the people drew away from his duty—Joshua, Phinehas, and finally all those prophets who pressed upon the Israelites that commandment of the Lord. Even if we grant that so obedient a people and such diligent teachers can exist, what prudence is there in laboring to prohibit an abuse while tolerating and not removing the occasion—or rather, the most immediate enticement—for introducing it, directly before their eyes?

To 10.

Nor do we condemn in themselves paintings of sacred histories or prophetic visions, especially those by which the sacred text itself can be illustrated and better understood. But what should be done with paintings which, because they are false, also deceive their viewers? What should be done also—

[Part II | PDF page 35 | logical source scan 34 | printed page 33]

—with things rightly and honorably delivered in Holy Scripture but represented so shamefully and obscenely by painters? Suppose someone places before our eyes Potiphar’s wife and Joseph solicited by the adulteress; or Bathsheba or Susanna bathing naked and disfigured with the dress of a prostitute; or represents the incest of Amnon or Absalom, or the things narrated in Ezekiel 16. “We judge,” Andreae will surely say, “that all such pictures must be abolished.” Would that they were all abolished and removed not only from churches but from everyone’s eyes. Yet if these are rightly judged worthy of complete abolition, why not much more those which, if retained in churches, the experience of so many centuries shows cannot fail gradually to entice people, almost against their will, even into a fouler fornication?

Finally, since the difference between entirely inanimate things such as all paintings and sculptures and the realities presented in sacred histories or visions is as great as that between body and shadow, I would by no means wish bare shadows to be set forth for viewing, from which viewers may imagine whatever they please. Rather, diligent care should be taken that the body be joined with the shadow—that is, the Lord’s own Word, through which he instructs us by our ears and understanding, should be joined with the pictures.

### Thesis VII of the Württemberg Theologians

*The rule prescribed by Paul concerning not offending the weak must be carefully observed, and idols must first be plucked from people’s minds by the Lord’s Word before they are removed from their eyes, and so forth.*

### Antithesis VII of Theodore Beza

Effort must indeed be made gradually to pluck idols from the minds of the idolaters themselves. But we deny that so foul a disease of superstition must in turn be nourished. Nor do we see how it is not nourished in the minds of those before whose eyes objects stand displayed by which they are moved to retain it.

### Andreae’s Marginal Annotation

If sound gospel doctrine is delivered to the people, is there not greater danger from haste than from delay and a longer postponement in removing images? Idols must first be removed from people’s minds by God’s Word before they are removed from their eyes. In this matter a definite time neither can nor ought to be determined.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Marginal Annotation

Having condemned—and rightly so, not with regard to the act in itself but to its circumstances—the tumultuous casting down of idols by private impulse, Andreae devotes this whole thesis to excusing magistrates who delay in removing them. He places two reasons beneath his position. The first is that, according to the Apostle’s teaching, the weak must not be offended. The second is that there is greater danger in haste than in delay and a longer postponement, whose definite duration neither can nor ought to be determined. I affirm that Andreae gravely and ruinously errs on both points and draws his followers into the same error. For—

[Part II | PDF page 36 | logical source scan 35 | printed page 34]

—as concerns the first reason, it is entirely certain that the Apostle is dealing neither with pagans only somewhat converted to Christ nor with any manifestly idolatrous worship. He is dealing with Jews who, not through obstinacy and an evil conscience but through ignorance, tenaciously retained the Levitical worship instituted by God, because they had not yet learned that the freedom acquired for us through Christ had come and that the ceremonial law, whose substance was Christ, had been abrogated. This is established not only by the fact that the Apostle mentions not simply the weak but the weak in faith. When he speaks about perverse Jews, he calls them the mutilation and dogs, whom he commands not to be received but avoided and to whom he did not yield even for a moment.

When dealing with profane idolaters, moreover, the Apostle is so far from teaching that the saints may connive at their unlawful rites that he denies that those who feast with them in an idol-temple have anything in common with Christ. Even in private society with pagans, he does not permit the saints to eat meats sacrificed to idols—which otherwise, as regards that circumstance, are indifferent—to the offense of those who so abominate idols that they reject idol-offerings without exception. How, then, do Andreae’s arguments pertain to our subject?

Although I do not classify the papists, from whom we rightly separated, either with Jews who fell from the covenant for a time, with Turks who are neither in nor from the Church, or with those ancient peoples with whom God never made a covenant of peace, nevertheless, as regards the idolatry with which we are now dealing, I place them in the same rank, though not equal in every respect. And as regards idolatry itself, how unjust is his comparison with Levitical worship or with the ceremonial law delivered by God? It is precisely like comparing Christ with Belial and light with darkness. By what reasoning, conscience, or shame, then, does Andreae compare what is said about the weak in faith with people manifestly profane in this respect, and compare a worship formerly not only tolerable but expressly commanded by God with that most foul fornication?

Andreae will therefore say, “Must nothing be conceded to those who have not yet been brought so far away from papist idolatry that they sufficiently perceive all their errors? Do you not judge that idols must first be plucked from their minds by God’s Word before being removed from the churches and from their sight?” I judge that here too the pastors’ office must be distinguished from the duty of godly magistrates. It is the pastors’ duty to drive Satan out, both within and without, by those spiritual weapons alone. Nothing further is lawful for them, except when by an extraordinary—

[Part II | PDF page 37 | logical source scan 36 | printed page 35]

—movement of God—which is exceedingly rare and must not rashly be drawn into a precedent—they are impelled. Pastors should therefore diligently teach their magistrates that they must strive, by the authority and power divinely entrusted to them, either to cleanse true and pure religion within their domain if it has been polluted, or to defend and preserve it pure if it has already been cleansed. They should instruct private persons both publicly and individually, beginning with milk and afterward offering solid food, as the Apostle practiced among the Corinthians; and they should renew the old wineskins before pouring new wine into them, lest both wineskins and wine perish, as our Teacher warns.

I also grant that godly magistrates must take care to follow in some fashion the footsteps of diligent and industrious pastors in restoring true religion. Yet they must not by their indulgence encourage those who delay, but instead, as Augustine often presses, must consider Christ’s words, “Compel them to come in.” Finally, the prudence of the flesh must not rule here; rather, the zeal and wisdom of God must govern. This wisdom will prescribe not only what is to be done but how and by what progression it is to be done, since because of the variety of circumstances nothing specifically certain about this matter seems capable of being prescribed or done.

The people are commanded first to offer peace—that is, peaceful submission—to hostile cities, and to destroy only those who refuse peace. Does Andreae therefore think that their idols had to be tolerated until they were plucked from their minds? But this, I grant, rests upon a special reason. When Asa and all those godly kings restored God’s worship at the prophets’ admonitions, after overthrowing the idols and adding capital punishment, did they wait until the apostates consented to the destruction of their idols?

By all means let doctrine go first, let admonitions go first, and let everything go first which can recall minds turned from the true God to true worship, just as those godly kings above all arranged for God’s Word to be read and explained before the whole people. Yet especially in matters clear and manifest in themselves—such as the fashioning and placing of every image for any religious use and every kind of inward or outward veneration, so expressly prohibited and condemned by all the testimonies of the pure Church—to wait until hypocrites consent, the superstitious agree, and the sluggish, pleased with their own idleness, testify that they have awakened: what is this except, after the example of Horace’s rustic, to wait until the whole river flows away while its spring continually supplies water; and, under the pretext of avoiding an untimely offense to people, piously of course and with great charity to neglect the insults inflicted upon God himself?

Although rashness must therefore be condemned, nevertheless, contrary to Andreae’s opinion—he is gravely mistaken here as well—there is more danger in one day’s delay than in accelerating the matter by an entire month. But why is this discussion needed now? Let us grant—

[Part II | PDF page 38 | logical source scan 37 | printed page 36]

—that images could have been tolerated for thirty or forty years while image-worshipers in Germany were instructed. If they had then been removed, how many of those who had seen them standing would now be offended, when they would never have seen them in churches or elsewhere? And what shall be said of those places where things once removed have been restored? As though God’s worship depended upon images which the whole pure ancient Church lacked for so many centuries, knowing neither crosses nor crucifixes and judging it unlawful to see an image of a hanging man in a sacred place; as though true religion would be imperiled unless images were preserved. Are these not rather the evasions of people who, in order to defend their own negligence by the errors and failures of others, cover themselves against the rain with a wet sack, as the old proverb has it?

### Thesis VIII of the Württemberg Theologians

*As concerns organs and musical instruments, God’s manifest Word remains: “Praise the Lord with strings and organ. Praise him with well-sounding cymbals. Praise him with cymbals of jubilation.” The examples of the most godly kings—David, Solomon, Hezekiah, Joshua, and others—testify to the same, whose instrumental music in the temple was not reproved by the Holy Spirit but commended.*

### Antithesis VIII of Theodore Beza

It is established that this was no insignificant part of Levitical worship, instituted by God as its author, not by human decisions. Otherwise it would necessarily have to be instituted in all Christian churches, if this command of God pertained to them.

### Andreae’s Marginal Annotation

Instrumental music was indeed employed in Levitical worship, but the godly kings who instituted it did not therefore possess an express special command of God, since it was neither instituted nor observed by Moses. Consequently, the testimonies drawn from Holy Scripture by the Württemberg theologians for retaining instrumental music in churches pertain to everyone. Those who remove it from churches and temples as though prohibited by God do wrong.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Marginal Annotation

Whom shall we believe: Andreae, who charges David and the other godly kings with the crime of self-willed worship as though they possessed no express command to employ instrumental music in Levitical worship, or rather the Holy Spirit, who convicts Andreae of both supreme audacity and shameful ignorance? For the Holy Spirit speaks as follows in 2 Chronicles 29:25: “Hezekiah stationed the Levites in the house of the Lord with cymbals, harps, and lyres according to the command of David, Gad the king’s seer, and Nathan the prophet, because through the hand of the Lord this—”

[Part II | PDF page 39 | logical source scan 38 | printed page 37]

“—command came through the hand of his prophets.” There is no doubt that nothing was sung in the temple, either by the bare voice or with instruments, which was not heard by all the hearers; but the form of that music is now entirely unknown to us. It is established, and sorrowful experience teaches, that it was a part of ceremonial worship which must not rashly be introduced into Christian churches.

Although, therefore, we by no means condemn the right use of music, especially when appropriately adapted to singing God’s praises, I judge that those who abolished from the churches that symphonic music—whether instruments are joined to the voice or not—in which ears, not souls, are fed while understanding nothing that is sung, acted wisely. We see that by this means all God’s worship has degenerated into little songs. Content with grave, godly, and religious singing by the voice alone, they removed such music from public assemblies.

The ancient Christians certainly sang hymns in their morning gatherings before dawn, as Pliny the Younger testifies, in a language and music understood by all. Writing on this subject, the author of the *Christian Questions and Responses*—though falsely attributed to Justin, since Origen, who lived later than Justin, is cited in it, yet still very ancient and learned—says in question 107: “The use in churches of songs performed with instruments of that sort and other things suited to infants has not been received; simple singing remains in them.” It may also probably be inferred that the ancient churches were content with what is called Gregorian chant or another of that kind, which Gregory restored as it gradually fell into disuse. For the instrument called by antonomasia the organ, which alone the papists customarily use in their churches, was, according to competent witnesses, first introduced with that musical rite by Pope Vitalian around the year of the Lord 660 and, according to Aventinus, constructed in France in the year 878. Theodoret attributes something of this kind to the Meletians in book 4 of his *Heretical Fables*. Let Andreae’s zeal rely as long as it wishes upon these authorities and upon his Osiander, the architect of organs; I require a different inspiration.

## We Judge the Following Dogmas Not to Agree with Holy Scripture

I.

*The gospel cannot be taught in a church in which prohibited and idolatrous rites have been celebrated.*

II.

*It is not lawful to celebrate the Lord’s Supper upon an altar which formerly served the abominable papist mass.*

[Part II | PDF page 40 | logical source scan 39 | printed page 38]

III.

*A wooden table must necessarily be established in churches in place of a stone altar, upon which the Lord’s Supper is celebrated, although Christ, together with his disciples, did not use a table but reclined on the ground according to the custom and practice of the nation.*

IV.

*The abolition of images must be entrusted to the people, although it is committed to the piety and wisdom of kings, princes, and the ordinary magistrate of each place.*

Jacobus Andreae, Doctor.

Lucas Osiander, Doctor.

### Theodore Beza’s Response to the Dogmas

We judge that these pertain neither to us nor to any Christian churches. We nevertheless say this: although it is in itself indifferent whether a stone structure which they call an altar or a common wooden table is used in the Lord’s sacred Supper, it is improbable that Satan could ever have changed the sacrament of the Lord’s Supper into that dreadful sacrifice of offering Christ anew if Christian churches had lacked altars. Would that the designation “sacrament of the altar” had never been employed.

### Andreae’s Marginal Annotations

1. Below in the colloquy it is copiously demonstrated that these dogmas were spread and put into practice by Zwinglians and Calvinists.

2. The Lord’s Supper and the sacrament produced altars, not altars sacrifices. More on this will follow in the conference itself.

Theodore Beza, minister of the Genevan Church.

Abraham Musculus, minister of the Bernese Church.

Peter Hübner, professor of Greek in the Bernese academy.

Antoine de la Faye, minister of the Genevan Church.

Claude Aubery, doctor and professor of philosophy in the Academy of Lausanne.

Montbéliard, March 29, 1586.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Marginal Annotations

I replied, and still reply, that these are pure calumnies and putrid lies, which Andreae nevertheless was not ashamed to affirm would be copiously—

[Part II | PDF page 41 | logical source scan 40 | printed page 39]

—demonstrated by him, and indeed as though they were dogmas spread by those whom he calls Zwinglians and Calvinists and completed in actual practice. Let those demonstrations, which certainly exist nowhere and cannot exist, therefore be read. The distinguished demonstrator merely recites the tale or history of some count or other who, apparently moved not by religion but ambition, twice against the wishes of a prince who shared jurisdiction with him in a certain church, arranged for a stone altar to be demolished and a table built in its place. A splendid proof indeed: the action of one ambitious or perhaps poorly instructed man of our confession, which Andreae calls Zwinglian and Calvinist, must be regarded as a dogma—indeed, a dogma spread and put into practice by Zwinglians and Calvinists.

How could this count have thought that the gospel could not be taught in a church where prohibited and idolatrous rites had been celebrated, when he merely exchanged a stone altar for a wooden table? And if we wished to imitate Andreae’s slander, why could I not conclude by an equal—that is, no less inept—argument that the prince of the Augsburg Confession who, after the wooden table had twice been overthrown, caused the stone altar to be restored, thought the Lord’s Supper could not lawfully be celebrated upon a wooden table but necessarily required a stone altar? What if I went still farther and called this a Lutheran or Württemberg dogma? Everyone would rightly spit upon me as a most mendacious slanderer.

But of what can Andreae be ashamed? Not content to smear us with so shameless a calumny, shortly after narrating that history he pleasantly mocks us: “We did not propose these dogmas,” he says, “because we thought you held the contrary opinion, but because your churches have everywhere acquired a bad reputation under this name”—with you and your kind, Andreae, as their authorities, of course—“and therefore it is useful for everyone to know that you by no means approve that confusion.” Tell me, then: how can you have said just before that these were Zwinglian and Calvinist dogmas and that you had demonstrated this copiously? Why undertake to demonstrate what you yourself do not believe? It would be exceedingly unjust to judge our position on these and the other heads of Christian faith, not from our confessions and our words, writings, and, by God’s grace, deeds, but from some statement or action of this person or that, or from the mouth of a most shameless slanderer.

To 2.

I remarked in passing that, although I do not condemn stone and fixed altars in themselves, it would nevertheless have been better for wooden tables to have been retained according to ancient custom, because Satan, as most crafty, seized from those altars no slight occasion for gradually transforming the sacrament of the Lord’s Supper into a sacrifice, even an expiatory one, and certainly a dreadful pollution. “On the contrary,” Andreae says, “sacrifice produced altars; altars did not produce sacrifice.” This is false. For although in that happier age the Christian churches used wooden tables even after the love feasts had been abolished—

[Part II | PDF page 42 | logical source scan 41 | printed page 40]

—upon which offerings were placed before being deposited in the altar itself, which was hollow, as Biclus testifies in reading 15, with a monument to this still preserved at Rome in the Lateran church, those tables were abusively and commonly called shrines and altars. Indeed, those wooden tables remained in Egypt under Athanasius, as appears from Ischyras’s slander, and in Africa under Augustine, as can be established both from other passages and because in question 101 of the *Questions on the Old and New Testaments* he testifies that carrying the altar is the deacon’s duty. Yet it can be proved from Gregory of Nyssa’s oration on baptism that stone altars were established in some places many centuries before any mention was made of that pollution. Sylvester in the time of Constantine the Great is thought to have introduced this, as Gerson among others testifies in book 4 against Floretus.

But come, let Andreae be forgiven this ignorance of history, as also his dream that Christ reclined with his disciples upon the ground and did not use a table. That is absurd, since it is sufficiently established that the ancients reclined not upon the ground but upon dining couches, and indeed at a table. But my statement that Satan gradually seized from this an occasion to introduce that dreadful crime is by no means an empty conjecture, since the proper and principal use of altars was sacrifice. When the Israelites formerly considered this, a dreadful civil war almost arose among them (Joshua 22). How, then, can Andreae truthfully have said that sacrifice produced altars and not altars sacrifice?

## The Theses of the Württemberg Doctors Concerning Baptism

[Part II | PDF page 43 | logical source scan 42 | printed page 41]

### Matters Placed Outside Controversy

I.

*Baptism consists of water and God’s Word.*

## Theodore Beza’s Response to the Theses of the Württemberg Doctors Concerning Baptism

### Antithesis 1 of Theodore Beza

We REQUIRE in this thesis the principal part of this sacrament which has been omitted—namely, the blood of CHRIST, whose sacramental sign is water. We also say that the essential rites of this sacred action, explained by the Apostle in Romans 6:4–6 and Colossians 2:12, ought by no means to have been omitted.

### Andreae’s Marginal Annotations

1. Since it seemed good at the outset to expound those things which were beyond all controversy and could not be denied, the first thesis was also stated generally: baptism consists of water and God’s Word. Yet the word WORD includes what the adversaries miss here—namely, that cleansing from sins does not occur without Christ’s blood.

2. Water does not signify Christ’s blood but is the organ through which, in Christ’s blood and by the Holy Spirit’s power, we are cleansed from sins, regenerated, and renewed.

3. The essential rite is also included in God’s Word: “to baptize.” We are therefore reproved without cause as though we had omitted it.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Marginal Observations

Water and Christ’s blood are properly the essential parts of baptism, as sign and thing signified.

To 1.

The singer is off-key, as they say, from the threshold. How important a right declaration of the essential parts from which the sacraments consist is in the controversy over the sacraments is shown even by this: one cannot err more ruinously in this subject than by confusing the sign, or what belongs to the sign, with what belongs to the thing signified, or conversely. Yet in how many ways is what is stated here concerning baptism incorrectly expressed? However one understands “the Word”—whether as the verbal explanation of the Lord’s institution which customarily precedes the action so that all may understand what is being done, by whose authority, and what promise faith regards; or as the principal formula of baptism itself divinely prescribed—it will be a sign, signifying God’s ordinance and will, just as the expressions by which we speak were devised so that the mind’s—

[Part II | PDF page 44 | logical source scan 43 | printed page 42]

—conceptions might be disclosed. Water likewise necessarily possesses only the character of a sign and does so in the most proper sense, since it is material and visible. In Andreae’s division, then, there is not even the slightest mention of that primary essential part of the sacraments, namely the thing signified, for whose sake a thing otherwise natural is transferred to an entirely different use. As Augustine says, “In sacraments”—that is, in signs—“what they signify, not what they are, is considered.” And with what outcries would Andreae assail us if we said that the essential parts of the Lord’s Supper were the Word, bread, and wine, passing over the Lord’s body and blood, although these men themselves, whenever they please and certainly incorrectly, define the Lord’s Supper by those things alone?

Next, why are other signs of the greatest importance omitted from this division? I mean the outward rites, such as immersion or sprinkling. These rites are by no means empty, as the Apostle describes them part by part in Romans 6, from which it is certain that those apostolic phrases also arose: putting off the old man and putting on Christ; baptism being the washing of regeneration; sins being washed away by baptism; and being born again of water. I therefore asked with the most just reasons why he omitted both the other essential part of baptism—namely Christ’s blood—and the sacramental rites. Andreae answers that these are included under the designation “Word” and manifestly acts the sophist, for even water is commanded by God’s Word.

Augustine indeed rightly says, “The word comes to the element and it becomes a sacrament,” but his statement concerns something other than the present matter. This is plain from the fact that there, as very frequently elsewhere in this writer, the word “sacrament” is restricted to the sacred signs alone. Augustine thus explains not the essential or component parts of which baptism consists, but by what principle—namely God’s ordinance declared through the Word—common things such as water and outward sprinkling in baptism, and bread and wine with the peculiar rites of the Lord’s Supper, are employed for signifying and sealing sacred things. It is by no means surprising that Andreae wished to answer nothing suitably here, since, as we shall soon hear, he refuses to acknowledge that the Lord’s blood is signified by water, expressly denies the division of baptism into inward and outward, and finally contends that the very person of the Holy Spirit is part of baptism on page 441. I could therefore rightly have broken off the disputation here, and perhaps ought to have done so, since everyone acknowledges that one must not dispute against a person who denies first principles.

For my part, confident that everyone will agree with me, as I hope, I do not hesitate to affirm that baptism, like the Eucharist and indeed all sacraments generally, consists of an earthly and a heavenly thing. In baptism these are water and Christ’s blood, to which correspond by analogy the cleansing of bodily filth by earthly water, which is outward, and the cleansing of sins by Christ’s blood together with renewal, which is heavenly and inward.

Water—

[Part II | PDF page 45 | logical source scan 44 | printed page 43]

—in baptism is the sign of Christ’s blood poured out for us, and sprinkling with that water is the sign of the application of his blood both for the remission of sins and for spiritual regeneration.

To 2.

Hear, Christian reader, something much more absurd still: Andreae denies that Christ’s blood is signified by baptismal water. One of two things is necessary: water either signifies something or nothing—that is, it either has the character of a sign or does not. If it signifies nothing, then it is not even a sign. On that supposition, what visible and material thing will finally be posited in baptism to which the definition applies that a sacrament is a visible sign of invisible grace, and that in it one thing is seen and another understood? Let Andreae therefore openly deny that baptism is a sacrament. But if baptismal water is the sign of something by which we are cleansed from sins, what other thing can that be than the blood through whose apprehension by faith alone every sin is washed away? Unless we maintain this, how, according to the Apostle, will outward immersion and emergence represent by a visible sign the inward mortification of the old man and restoration of the new?

But let us hear what Andreae wishes water to be if it is not a sign in baptism, since it signifies nothing. “It is,” he says, “an organ and instrument through which, in Christ’s blood and by the Holy Spirit’s power, we are absolved from sins, regenerated, and renewed.” Good God, where does the lust for quarrelling finally carry people? Do we deny, I ask, that baptismal water is an organ or instrument when we call it a visible sign divinely ordained, by which the true blood poured out for us is represented to our minds—not as an empty spectacle but so that, through its apprehension by faith and spiritual application, both the remission of sins and the renewal of God’s image are really established within us? If Andreae agrees with us, why does he quarrel with us instead of frankly acknowledging that his thesis was defective? Why does he prefer the equivocal designation “organ”? Otherwise as many sacraments must be established in God’s Church as there are instrumental causes of our salvation.

But something else lies hidden in this equivocation, which we shall discuss in its proper place. Come, let us first set Andreae against his colleague Herbrand and then against himself. On page 583 of his *Theological Compendium* Herbrand says, “The ancients sometimes use the word sacrament to signify together the sign and the thing signified, and sometimes ascribe it to the signification,” and on page 584, “The element is a sign of God’s grace.” Andreae himself says on page 172 of these Acts, “No one has ever denied that sacraments are signs.” Let Andreae therefore except himself. On page 445 of this very conference he says, “In baptism adoption is not only signified and represented but also exhibited.” Who among us speaks otherwise concerning sacramental signification? If—

[Part II | PDF page 46 | logical source scan 45 | printed page 44]

—adoption is signified in baptism, it certainly ought not to have been denied that water signifies the blood through which we are reconciled and adopted. But it is well that, finally returning to himself on page 456, he says: “As regards signification or representation, if the parts of baptism are compared with each other, in that manner we can permit it to be said that water is the sign of blood, and its sprinkling or immersion the signification or representation of spiritual mortification and regeneration. Yet this water and its bare sprinkling is not baptism but only a part of baptism.” It is well, then, that we possess a defendant who confesses and refutes himself. But to what purpose and against whom is it said that water is not baptism? Does he think us so foolish that we judge a part of some whole to be the very whole of which it is a part? And when we say that water is the sign of blood, do we not declare the whole itself from both parts as one integral thing composed of sign and thing signified?

Baptism is indeed one when considered with respect to its underlying subject, but twofold according to its parts: one outward and properly so called, the other inward and called so by transference.

Andreae certainly cannot deny without absurdity that the word “baptism” properly signifies outward, bodily immersion in water. Because of the sacramental conjunction, the word is afterward figuratively transferred to testify to us the spiritual power of Christ’s death applied to removing the filth of our soul, just as outward washing of the body cannot be applied to the soul’s cleansing from sin except metaphorically. For to these two outward signs, considered distinctly though always supplied conjointly as regards God—namely water and outward sprinkling—two corresponding things signified answer correlatively: Christ’s blood, or Christ himself suffering for us, and its inward application through faith. From these two corresponding effects likewise follow: the outward washing of bodily filth and inward cleansing from those stains of the soul.

Accordingly, when during the disputation, as recorded on page 438, I heard Andreae walking as though on lofty buskins and using this magnificent preface—“I wish to answer not in my words but in those of the Apostle Paul, who writes to the Ephesians: one Lord, one faith, one baptism”—and then concluding, “It is manifest from Paul’s words that there is only one baptism and not two, inward and outward,” which he was not ashamed afterward to repeat everywhere in express words, I was greatly astonished that anyone could imagine that what is divided into two is cut into as many complete wholes. Do we divide one human being into two human beings, I ask, when we say that one whole human being is composed of two things, body and soul? Or do we judge that there are numerically several souls when we consider an inward and out—

[Part II | PDF page 47 | logical source scan 46 | printed page 45]

—ward human being, as Paul and Peter speak, in one and the same subject? This division of body and soul corresponds exactly to these essential parts of baptism and indeed of all the sacraments, since, as Augustine rightly says, in them one thing is seen and another understood, and not what they are but what they signify is considered. Is not what they are and what is seen visible and outward? And is not what they signify and what is understood intelligible and inward? Accordingly, we rightly and orthodoxly say that the signs, since they are bodily, are offered to bodies, but that the thing signified—Christ himself with his benefits—is offered to mind and faith. Augustine himself also testifies that Christ is food not for the teeth but for the mind, and consequently a washing to be received by the mind, not the body, although he spiritually both washes and feeds the whole person for spiritual life.

But because Andreae perhaps will not acquiesce even in these things, come, let us compare him again with his own people, though sounder on this subject. Thus Herbrand, on page 640 of the same book I cited, answering explicitly that most inept and plainly ridiculous argument, says: “Although there is one baptism in Ephesians 4, it is nevertheless understood in three ways. First is the OUTWARD baptism of water. Second is Christ’s INWARD baptism, by which hearts are washed by him through the Holy Spirit from the stains of sins, and people are given the Holy Spirit. This alone is properly Christ’s, and it is joined with the OUTWARD. The third is the miraculous outpouring upon the apostles on the day of Pentecost.”

Let us also hear Brenz. In his explanation of his *Syntagma*, published in 1527, he says: “A human being is twofold, inward and outward, spiritual and carnal”—that is, bodily—“and therefore in the sacrament through the Word his own good is offered to the inward and spiritual person, namely the spiritual; to the carnal person his own, namely the outward.” Likewise: “Baptism has two things, water and the Word. In baptism the body sensibly receives water by which it is washed; faith receives the Word by which the inward person is washed.”

But why did Brenz not join blood as the thing signified with water as the sign, rather than the Word? “Because,” Andreae says on page 445, “the Lord did not say of water, ‘This is my blood,’ as he did of the wine in the Lord’s Supper.” Yet Christ also did not say of water, “This is the Word,” or, “This is the Spirit.” But what if I demonstrate clearly enough from God’s Word that Christ’s blood answers baptismal water as the sacramentally signified thing which is spiritually received by faith? To which of those twin sacraments of the new covenant does that legal sprinkling of the blood of old-covenant victims, so fully explained in Hebrews 9:20, correspond? Its formula was, “This is the blood about which the Lord gave a command to you.” Certainly it corresponds to baptism, for that rite cannot be referred analogically—

[Part II | PDF page 48 | logical source scan 47 | printed page 46]

—to the sacrament of the Lord’s Supper, because sprinkling is different from giving something to eat or drink. The latter could not occur under the law, since it was lawful neither to eat flesh with its blood nor to consume the blood itself, which also indicates how much clearer the new covenant’s sacraments are than the old covenant’s. Did that legal blood of the victim lack not the new antitype, which is water, but the reality, which is the Lord’s own blood?

Moreover, when John in 1 John 5:8 places those three witnesses on earth, explicitly joins blood to water, and distinguishes both—without separating them—from the Spirit, the very effect of the sacraments, does he not clearly enough declare baptismal water to be the sacrament of Christ’s blood, by which we are both purified and renewed? Augustine therefore wrote not without reason that the sacraments of Christ’s Church flowed, as it were, from his pierced side pouring forth blood with water, since from him they receive all their designation and power as sacraments.

Finally, the exception that it is not said of baptismal water, as of the wine in the Lord’s Supper, that it is Christ’s blood is mere evasion and a sophistic escape. Among true theologians this axiom is perpetual: things necessarily inferred from the written Word are regarded as expressly written. Since analogy must necessarily be maintained in the sacraments, as Augustine testifies, water itself cannot properly signify inward sprinkling, inward washing itself, or its effect, regeneration. Rather, outward washing distinctly answers inward washing, and bodily cleansing, the effect of outward washing, answers inward cleansing of the soul. Something inward signified must therefore necessarily answer water. What else can it be than that blood which alone takes away the world’s sins?

Thus in the Lord’s Supper Christ’s body answers the sacred bread and his blood the sacred wine. Likewise, bodily reception of bread and wine represents spiritual reception of flesh and blood to the faithful mind and seals it there. Finally, the mutual communion of believers both with Christ and with one another, which follows that reception as its effect, presents as though visibly to be seen the inward communion with Christ the head and among the members of his mystical body, and therefore their spiritual conjunction. Accordingly, when baptism of water is called the washing of regeneration, although the name of blood is not expressed, just as neither is the inward washing, both are regarded as expressed: blood answering water on the opposite side, and the inward washing answering the outward. Thus the inward effect, regeneration, is truly attributed—though by sacramental signification—to both the water and the outward washing, although properly it is the work of God alone.

I therefore rightly said that the essential parts and matter of baptism are water as the earthly thing and Christ’s blood as the heavenly thing. I also joined the outward action upon a person, which is outward baptism—

[Part II | PDF page 49 | logical source scan 48 | printed page 47]

—with the Spirit’s inward action, which is inward baptism, though Andreae wrongly denies this. It does not follow that there are two complete baptisms, but two parts of one whole. I say something further which cannot be denied except stubbornly: as regards God himself speaking, these parts are always so joined that nevertheless the thing signified is sometimes received without that sign, and the sign without the thing signified, as I shall teach in its proper place.

Andreae also manifestly twists the Apostle’s statement in Ephesians 4:5 about one baptism to abolish the distinction between inward and outward baptism, as though deliberately resisting the words of both John the Baptist and the Apostle Peter. John the Baptist expressly places and enumerates both two baptizers—himself and Christ—and a twofold baptism, the one of water, that is earthly and outward, and the other of the Spirit, that is spiritual and heavenly. He does so distinctly, though not separately as two complete wholes. Peter in 1 Peter 3:21 just as expressly distinguishes, though he does not separate, baptism by which bodily stains are washed away from the baptism of a good conscience’s pledge before God through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

Paul, however, exhorts the godly—whom all rightly baptized people are not necessarily, but are probably presumed to be—to cultivate mutual brotherhood, seizing the occasion likewise from one baptism. He by no means considers the distinction between sign and thing signified or between baptism’s essential parts, but one and the same token of association toward the same end: not numerically one, for there are numerically as many baptisms as there are baptized persons, but the same in definition and in all its causes and parts. Thus elsewhere he says that through one Spirit we are baptized into one body, namely the mystical body whose head is Christ.

When I objected this to Andreae, explaining it, I dare affirm, more fully and clearly than his copyists recorded, he said on page 440, “The matter itself demonstrates that I cited these things suitably.” To prove this suitable citation, he says: “The question is not what Paul had in view or to what end he wrote these things. It is enough that he said, THERE IS ONE BAPTISM.” I ask, what is to be done with a man who, to demonstrate that he was not inept, dares to testify to his ineptitudes not only orally but also in writing? What is it to cite a passage ineptly except, when the meaning of someone’s statement is disputed, to say that no consideration should be given to the purpose for which it was spoken or written? Then let Eutyches be heard citing, “The Word became flesh”; Sabellius producing, “God is one”; Arius shouting, “The Father is greater than I”; and what not?

We by no means cut baptism into two complete wholes when we distinguish its parts, even if by the usage of Scripture we attribute the same name to each part as to the whole itself, though with a different denotation. We have already explained this, and from it Andreae’s slander on page 447 is plain, where he says that we tear the Spirit—

[Part II | PDF page 50 | logical source scan 49 | printed page 48]

—away and separate him in baptism. If he speaks truly, let John and Peter be guilty of the same crime; or rather, let Andreae himself be regarded as one for whom distinction and separation are one and the same.

### What a Type and Antitype Are

What sort of answer is this? When I objected to him Peter’s passage, in which he makes sacred baptism twofold—one outward, by which bodily stains are washed away, the other inward, which is the pledge of a good conscience through Christ’s resurrection—Andreae replies on page 449 that “this passage of Peter opposes me diametrically.” Come then, why? “Because,” he says, “Peter there compares baptismal water as the antitype of the waters of the flood.” And what then? Let baptism indeed be an antitype of the flood. This has nothing to do with the matter. I do not touch this part of the verse in 1 Peter 3:21. I say that Peter afterward establishes as twofold the baptism by which we are saved and which for this reason is called by Peter the antitype of the waters of the flood, in which Noah with his family—that is, the Church—was saved. No one can deny this without being refuted by the context itself.

For who could understand by the baptism which washes bodily stains the waters of the flood, unless he contended that Noah and his family were submerged in the floodwaters? On the contrary, the antithesis rests in this: Noah and his family were not submerged in those waters as the other people were but instead were saved, just as baptismal water overwhelms the old man so that the new may emerge.

Yet lest Andreae seem to say nothing, let us hear his response: “Type,” he says, “indicates a foreshadowing of the thing, but antitype the thing signified by the type.” On page 450 he says: “In the sacraments of the New Testament there are not types—that is, figures and shadows which signify absent things—but antitypes, that is, the realities themselves signified and represented by shadows and figures.” From where did he finally learn this? The word “type,” certainly, by a metaphor drawn from image-makers, denotes a form or figure representing some other thing by a certain resemblance either to the eyes, the mind, or both. In this sense the Apostle in Hebrews also uses the names “parable” and “example.” Although those terms extend more widely than what we call sacraments—that is, signs which are adapted to sacred use and have the promise of salvation joined to them—nevertheless both old and new sacraments are sometimes called types by the ancient Greek and Latin fathers, as they are also called figures, forms, seals, signets, likenesses, exemplars, images, remembrances, and memorials.

And lest Andreae complain that I do not rightly call the sacraments of the new covenant types, let him hear Herbrand himself writing on page 586, after previously using “signs” and “marks” as synonyms: “Types are those which admonish us concerning many duties of life. Baptism concerning the mortification of the flesh; the Lord’s Supper concerning brotherly charity.” Why should they not also have been types of the gospel—

[Part II | PDF page 51 | logical source scan 50 | printed page 49]

—promises? For the word “type” declares something more expressly than the designation “sign,” “mark,” or “testimony.” Nor do I deny that under the old covenant not all types were signs of the sort we call sacraments, having the promise of saving grace attached. Yet I affirm that the name “types” and “antitypes” suits those very sacraments, both old and new, because of their common manner of signifying, although it is not identical in every case nor equal among similar things.

Properly speaking, as the very derivation of the name shows, “antitypes” are two types compared with each other; improperly speaking, they are types set over against the reality of which they are types and figures. In the former, proper kind of signification, baptism is called by Peter an antitype of the flood, and conversely the flood may by precisely the same reasoning be called an antitype of baptism. The former is so because the floodwater by which the ark containing Noah and his children was preserved was a kind of image and likeness representing baptism; the latter because baptism in turn was represented by the flood—on either side a type opposed to a type. Herbrand himself, as I just said, does not hesitate, following the ancients, to attribute this designation both to baptism and to the Lord’s Supper. Thus the legal purifications can also appropriately be said to have been antitypes of our baptism, and the legal Passover meal antitypes of the Lord’s Supper.

The Apostle supplies an example of the improper signification, in which a type is compared not with another type but with the very reality signified by the type, in Hebrews 9:24, calling the handmade sanctuary an antitype of the true things, namely heaven. The ancient Greek theologians Clement, Basil, Macarius, and Eutychius likewise call that bread and wine antitypes of the Lord’s body and blood. In this sense we would also rightly call the manna an antitype of that true heavenly bread who is Christ himself, and the Passover lamb an antitype of that true Lamb. But we could not, except most ineptly, say that heaven is an antitype of the handmade sanctuary, the Lord’s flesh and blood antitypes of that bread and wine, the heavenly bread an antitype of manna, or the Lamb an antitype of the legal lamb, since neither heaven, that flesh and blood, that heavenly bread, nor that Lamb who takes away the world’s sins has the character of a type. Andreae’s chimera is therefore empty when he says that “antitype” denotes not another type but the thing signified by the type.

As for the comparison between old and new antitypes, I grant that the former are called shadows and ours images, but not in the sense Andreae thinks, and this has no bearing on the matter. If by “images” you understand the new sacraments and by “shadows” the old, they will mutually be antitypes which effectively represented the reality—that is, Jesus Christ and his benefits—the former more obscurely and as though in a rough sketch, the latter more clearly and expressly. Otherwise, as Augustine says, they are equal in reality: in both cases—

[Part II | PDF page 52 | logical source scan 51 | printed page 50]

—the same Christ with all his benefits is sacramentally, though not equally, represented, as Chrysostom declares through an elegant likeness in his particular homily on the Apostle’s passage in 1 Corinthians 10. But if by “the images” you understand Christ himself and the things he accomplished for our sake—for the Apostle uses the plural—to which the shadows are opposed, has Andreae thereby proved his interpretation?

### Circumcision Also Was Twofold According to Its Parts

I must not pass over at this point another matter from which it may be understood where stubbornness finally carries disputants. In order to maintain baptism’s distinction into inward and outward, I twice objected to Andreae that the Apostle twice expressly makes the same distinction in circumcision—the true antitype of baptism—namely, in Romans 2:28–29 and Colossians 2:11: circumcision of the flesh, of the letter, made by hand; and circumcision of the heart, of the Spirit, and not made by hand; in short, inward and outward circumcision. Hence the Apostle also establishes a twofold Jew: one openly, that is, merely outwardly; the other in secret, that is, inwardly. What does Andreae answer to this? In the former place, page 450, he openly dared to answer sophistically: “There are not two circumcisions, but only one which God commended to Abraham.” To answer appropriately, however, instead of “two circumcisions” he ought to have said “twofold circumcision.” I then answered this briefly—for what else was I to do with one who openly contradicted the Apostle? But his copyists passed over my whole response, or at least refused to publish it. When, on another occasion offered, I had put the same argument to him again and explained it more fully, his copyists again omitted what I said from Colossians 2 about circumcision not made by hand, with the same fidelity with which they omitted many other things. Then Andreae himself, on page 475, perhaps forgetting my earlier answer, denied that “Paul makes a twofold circumcision, but rebukes the Jews’ abuse of circumcision, since, having rejected Christ, they boasted that because of circumcision they were in grace and were God’s people.” This answer is not only false but also ridiculous. That it is ridiculous is clear from the fact that there can be no place for rebuking this abuse unless this very twofold circumcision, which he denies, is posited. Yet he posits it while denying it: one which Paul expressly establishes openly, in the flesh, in an outward sign humanly administered, that is, outward circumcision, to which the Jews bound their salvation, as the Papists and Andreae bind theirs to outward baptism; and another which the Apostle says is of the heart and Spirit and is performed by no human hands. Who, preserving the truth, can deny that inward circumcision is signified by these words?

[Part II | PDF page 53 | logical source scan 52 | printed page 51]

I therefore say that with this answer Andreae, plainly contradicting Paul, sufficiently refutes himself, since he is compelled to posit something inward and spiritual—though he does not dare call it circumcision of the heart—in opposition to the fleshly and outward. But what, moreover, will he answer Moses, through whom God at two distinct times and places commanded that twofold circumcision: the outward circumcision of the flesh in Exodus 12:48 and Leviticus 12:3, and the inward circumcision of the heart in Deuteronomy 10:16? Anyone who attentively considers the course of Paul’s disputation will readily judge that Andreae offers a false interpretation of this passage, one wholly unsuited to it. For although it is true that, as long as circumcision remained in force, true Jews could not be circumcised inwardly except in Christ and through Christ’s Spirit, Paul nevertheless does not yet discuss either Christ or the righteousness of faith before Romans 3:21. Rather, beginning at Romans 1:18, he commenced a disputation in which he refutes both Gentiles and Jews who pursue the righteousness of the law or of works, taking his argument from the very nature both of the law and of Moses, namely, because all are transgressors of the law. The law regards none as righteous except those alone who live according to its prescription. Hence at last he rightly concludes that so far are either the law of nature from justifying Gentiles or the moral or ceremonial law from justifying Jews before God that, on the contrary, it consigns both to the Lawgiver’s curse. Since this is so, he teaches that righteousness and life must necessarily be sought from Christ alone, revealed through the gospel and apprehended by faith, as he afterward proves by very many invincible arguments, and especially by the example of Abraham, the father of believers. When Paul in chapter 4 presents circumcision as a seal of the righteousness of faith in Abraham and in all Abraham’s true children, he no longer presents it as in chapter 2, as a bond obligating the Jews to perfect performance of the law and consequently tending to their ruin, but as a pledge and token of the old gospel covenant, saving for circumcised believers. This is the other end of the whole ceremonial law, entirely different from and indeed opposed to the former, just as the seal of the righteousness of faith contradicts the handwriting of death; yet both are said of circumcision, the former in Romans 4:11 and the latter in Colossians 2:14.

### The Holy Spirit Is Not Rightly Called a Part of Baptism, If He Is Consequently Taken for His Effects in the Baptized

But come, let us proceed elsewhere. Whether distrustful of his own distribution of baptism’s substantial parts or, in his usual way, disagreeing with himself, Andreae nevertheless remains constant in this error: he does not allow baptism to be distinguished into outward and inward—which, as we have shown, is the same as if he denied baptism to be a sacrament. In the conference he pressed another distribution in these words on page 439: “The sub—”

[Part II | PDF page 54 | logical source scan 53 | printed page 52]

“—stance of baptism consists of water and Spirit.” And on page 440: “These are the substantial things of baptism.” And on page 441: “The Holy Spirit, as the principal and substantial part of baptism—if it is permissible to speak so—is comprehended and included in the Word.” Lest anyone think that the word “PART” is softened by “AS” and by that parenthesis inserted, let the reader observe that the matter is otherwise and that those words were added only to soften “included.” Andreae again speaks thus on page 442: “In baptism we say that the Holy Spirit is the reality of the sacrament.” And on page 443: “There is only one baptism, consisting of an inward and outward reality, namely water and Spirit.” Likewise on page 450: “The substantial parts of the Lord’s Supper—bread and body, wine and blood—are expressly joined in the word of Christ’s testament; so also water and Spirit in baptism.”

Let us now investigate this assertion, than which I affirm that scarcely anything more absurd can be said. I do not deny that the Holy Spirit has parts to play in baptism, since from him rather, as its proper and supreme efficient cause, proceeds all the efficacy both of the simple preached Word and of the sacraments. I deny that the Holy Spirit is, most absurdly, said to be a part of baptism itself. For who, except the Manichaeans or other madmen of that kind, ever incorporated God as a part of any created thing? Indeed, how rarely does it occur—if it ever does—that one and the same thing in different respects sustains both the character of a part of the effected thing and of its efficient cause? So much is this so that this proposition is maintained in the schools: the soul formally vivifies the body, but God effectively does so.

This one reason by itself suffices to overturn Andreae’s doctrine. Even if I granted it to him, however, he would still be convicted by his own words of wrongly denying the distinction of baptism into inward and outward, which even common sense announces, since Andreae himself, as we just said, testifies on page 443 that there is one outward reality and another inward reality in one and the same baptism. From this it necessarily follows that its own action must be assigned to each reality: the outward action to water and the inward to the Spirit. These two actions, numerically and by nature different, cannot be signified by the single designation “baptism” with one and the same definition. It follows—as the very derivation of the name also declares—that baptism is properly said of water, whether sprinkled or into which someone is immersed, while it is figuratively accommodated to the Spirit and the inward action. Likewise in the Lord’s Supper the words “eating” and “drinking” are necessarily transferred only figuratively to the faithful mind, since properly they are bodily actions.

The same must also be determined concerning the objects of these two actions, or the subjects in which they are exercised: bodily stains answer baptism with water, while the soul’s stains—also metaphorically so called, which are sins—answer the inward baptism of the Spirit. Finally, the same judgment applies to the effects of each of these different actions—

[Part II | PDF page 55 | logical source scan 54 | printed page 53]

—namely, the washing of bodily stains through outward baptism with water, and the remission of sins and renewal of the person through the inward baptism.

It is also worth considering how beautifully Andreae agrees with himself here too. On page 439, and rightly, he warns that “the effect of baptism, which is regeneration, must not be confused with the substance of baptism, which consists of water and Spirit.” Yet this is precisely the fault he commits on the same page. Because Christ, speaking of regeneration in the words, “Unless one is born again of water and Spirit,” joined water with Spirit and Spirit with water, Andreae concludes that baptism is so thoroughly one that one baptism must not be constituted as outward and another as inward. This is certainly no less inept than if he affirmed that everything contributing to a common result is performed by one numerically single action. That was the foundation of the Monothelite heresy, streams drawn from the pools of the Eutychians.

But Christ did not say, “Unless one is baptized of water and Spirit.” Even had he used that phrase, however, one simple action still would not be understood, but necessarily two actions, just as there are two baptizers: a person washing bodily stains with water—which, whether Andreae wishes it or not, is certainly outward baptism—and Christ himself, by the power of his Spirit inwardly washing the person for the remission of sins and the renewal of the baptized. Regeneration is also the common end of both washings: of the inward washing as the thing signified, and of the outward as the sign. These are not our inventions; John the Baptist himself in Matthew 3:11 and Peter in 1 Peter 3:21 taught us so to speak.

I also say this: Andreae on page 444 and elsewhere wrongly applies those words of Christ to his assertion that water and Spirit are baptism’s substantial parts. Even if we grant him that Christ there restricts the name “water” peculiarly to baptism—a point on which not all learned and orthodox interpreters agree—the matter itself shows that Christ did not there intend to enumerate baptism’s substantial parts. Rather, he showed the two principal causes of its effect, or common result, namely regeneration: the outward instrumental cause, which takes place through human ministry and has the promise of inward washing joined to it; and the inward cause, which is the principal cause of that effect. From this again follows that necessary distribution of baptism into inward and outward.

But let Andreae tell us, I ask, what he understands by the designation “Spirit” when he says that baptism consists of water and Spirit. Surely the very person of the Holy Spirit, who is the consubstantial and coeternal power of the Father and the Son, proceeding from both; in this sense John 1:13 says that God’s children are born of God. Here I agree with him. But I do not agree with what he says on the same page when, wishing to explain why Christ in those words, “Unless one is born again of water and Spirit,” mentioned neither the Father, the Son, nor even the Son’s blood, but only—

[Part II | PDF page 56 | logical source scan 55 | printed page 54]

—“the Holy Spirit: there was no need,” he says, “for the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are one most simple Spirit.” This is true, but wholly inapposite. For there the designation “Spirit” is taken not essentially but personally, as the ancients rightly and frequently cite this passage against the Macedonians.

If Andreae also refers to the very hypostasis of the Holy Spirit those words of John in which John is said to baptize with water but Christ with the Spirit, and similar expressions—for example, when the saints are said to receive the Spirit and to be filled with the Holy Spirit—he again errs badly. Inward baptism must indeed be attributed to the three very persons of the sacred Trinity, though in different respects. But it is one thing to be baptized by or through the Spirit, another to be baptized with the Spirit. In the former the person of the Spirit is denoted; in the latter, his energy operating within us, through which, by creating faith in us, he applies Christ to us for the remission of sins and the renewal of the whole person, or seals Christ once applied to us.

Accordingly, as Herbrand, Andreae’s colleague in the church at Tübingen, truly writes on page 583, “by the reality of the sacrament sometimes the substance is understood”—namely the thing signified, as he previously expressed it, which one may call the kernel of the sacraments, as the signs are the nutshell—“but sometimes the fruit which is perceived from the sacrament itself.” In neither way could Andreae truly say that the Holy Spirit himself is the reality of the sacrament. For the fruit of the sacrament is regeneration, which certainly is not the Holy Spirit himself but his effect, as are his other gifts. The visible material of water, however, was employed so that it might make us more certain of the invisible and spiritual washing from sins. It must therefore designate something else analogous to it, whose power in cleansing the conscience is what water’s power is in washing bodily stains. That is not the Holy Spirit immediately, but Christ’s shed blood, as is also clear from Christ’s express words: “He will take of mine,” he says, “and announce it to you,” John 16:14. For remission of sins does not occur without blood, Hebrews 9:22, so that God is not only the one who justifies, but is himself just in that manner of justifying, Romans 3:16. Likewise, the same Holy Spirit in the Lord’s Supper does not join us to Christ except by making us participants in the Lord’s body and blood, which therefore are also the realities of those sacred signs of the Lord’s Supper, namely its bread and wine.

### Why Andreae So Greatly Shrinks from the Word “Sign” and the Verb “Signifies”

If anyone asks why Andreae shrinks so greatly from the terms “sign” and “thing signified,” and refuses to confess that Christ’s own blood is the reality to which the sign of water answers—putting in its place now the Word, now the Holy Spirit himself—I shall reveal this further mystery akin to consubstantiation. It appears, therefore, that to Andreae—

[Part II | PDF page 57 | logical source scan 56 | printed page 55]

—the words “sign” and “signifying” are too weak. Hence on page 447 he repeatedly presses the word “IS” three times, IS, IS, IS, in the sense of real subsistence, explaining Paul’s statement that we are saved through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit in Titus 3:5 and the other statement in Ephesians 5:26 that Christ cleansed the Church with the washing of water through the Word. From this he even concludes that no figure, metaphor, or signification has a place here. If this conclusion were true, one of two utterly absurd and false consequences would follow: either there is some sacrament in which there is no sign and consequently no thing signified—that is, some defined thing to which its own definition does not apply—or baptism must be erased from the number of sacraments.

Who does not know that the verb “IS” is not always taken as asserting real subsistence, since it has a place in propositions from every category? Nothing therefore could have been said less suitably than this opposition between “IS” and “SIGNIFIES.” Sacramental signification should instead have been distinguished from common and simple signification, which we do diligently when we say that a certain and true promise of the thing signified—namely Christ himself with his benefits, as truly to be presented in reality and received by a faithful mind—is annexed to sacramental signs as surely as those signs themselves strike the bodily senses.

But come, let us constrain Andreae still more tightly. If the verb “IS” is taken so emphatically as asserting real subsistence, what is that subsisting reality? Is it the Holy Spirit himself, as Andreae just wished when he said that the Holy Spirit himself was the principal part of baptism’s substance, whose words we cited above from pages 439, 440, 441, 442, 443, and 450? It would therefore follow from Andreae’s doctrine that, if that water is not transubstantiated into the Holy Spirit himself, it is at least really consubstantiated with the Holy Spirit in the manner of real subsistence. And why not, since on page 441 he wishes the Spirit to be comprehended and included in the word of baptism, however he attempts to soften those words?

But if he says that regeneration is the reality of the sacrament, it certainly is not some essential subsisting reality. Consequently the verb “IS” will not be taken properly and as asserting real subsistence; and, by changing the kind of question as sophists are accustomed to do, Andreae will be refuted by himself, since he rightly warned on page 439 that “the substance of baptism—which he says is water and Spirit—must not be confused with baptism’s effect, which is regeneration.” But why say more? It cannot be denied that all the ancient fathers used terms far weaker still in explaining the nature of the sacraments, as we warned above and our theologians have shown with countless myriads of testimonies. Andreae himself everywhere acknowledges signification, representation, visible things, and invisible things. But he denies that merely what the sacrament’s word promises is signified. We deny that too, concerning which we shall speak more fully presently. Yet this denial concerns not the question of—

[Part II | PDF page 58 | logical source scan 57 | printed page 56]

—the thing signified but the use and effect of sacramental signification, or the fulfillment of the spiritual promise. While sophists confuse these two questions, they do not explain the controversy but entangle it. Andreae also denies that these things should be torn apart—as if the distinction of parts were the dissolution of the whole, so that whoever distinguishes the head from the body might seem to have cut off the head, and whoever separately considers the essence, faculties, and actions of body and soul might seem to have killed the person. Such is these disputants’ acumen, as I think you perceive, whoever you are, Christian reader.

To bring this inept dispute about “sign” and “signifying” to an end once for all, I shall produce two passages from Gregory of Nyssa. In one, from his treatise against the Jews, he discusses the water and blood which flowed from the Lord’s side and distinguishes water and blood as the twofold matter from the Spirit as the cause from whom the efficacy of both outward and inward baptism proceeds. “The whole of our salvation,” he says, “is mystically perfected and completed through water and the Spirit, since we believe that through divine baptism and through participation in and use of the holy blood we are consecrated and clothed with divinity.” The other comes from his discourse on holy baptism, where he says: “Baptism is expiation for the sinner and the cause of regeneration; by regeneration understand what is perceived in thought but not seen by the eyes.” And shortly afterward: “Water does not confer this benefit—for then it would be more sublime than all creation—but God’s ordinance and the Spirit who comes mystically for our deliverance. Water serves to DISPLAY purification. Since we are accustomed to render a body defiled with stains clean by washing it with water, we therefore also employ water in the mystical action, by the sensible thing SIGNIFYING incorporeal splendor.”

### Why Andreae Refuses to Say That Christ’s Blood Is the Reality Signified by Water and Outward Baptism, and What the Constitutive General Form and Distinguishing Particular Form of the Sacraments Are

It remains for us to reveal the mystery of the other question: why Andreae refuses to establish Christ’s blood—that is, Christ himself, dead, buried, and raised from the dead for us—as the reality signified by water. One might rightly wonder at this, since on page 456 Andreae concedes what he so often denies elsewhere, namely: “if the parts of baptism are compared with each other, it may be said that water is the sign of blood, and its sprinkling is the signification or representation of spiritual mortification and regeneration.” Thus, against his will, he was compelled in the disputation to unravel his own position and expressly acknowledge that blood is baptism’s other part, and indeed the part signified, because not even in a chimerical imagination can a sign be posited without the thing signified—

[Part II | PDF page 59 | logical source scan 58 | printed page 57]

—The matter stands thus.

Every correct definition consists of a constitutive form, which is the genus common to all species under it, and a distinguishing form, which distinguishes the thing defined from other things of the same genus. When, therefore, in the controversy concerning the Lord’s Supper we inquire into both of its forms, we say that the constitutive form of all sacraments consists in this: by divine ordinance they consist of signs and things signified, joined by one and the same relation. Of these two, the one which has the character of a sign is given to the body to be perceived by a bodily manner and instrument; the other, namely what is signified to the mind and therefore is invisible and intelligible, is presented to the mind to be taken by the faithful mind through the one instrument of faith specified in the covenant’s formula. We establish the distinguishing form, by which one sacrament is distinguished from another, partly in the diversity of the signs and partly in the diverse analogy and peculiar end of each sacrament, concurrent with the universal end of all these sacraments, which is the sealing of Christ himself and his benefits.

Thus, for example, we distinguish the sacraments of the old covenant from each other both by their essential signs and by their rites and peculiar ends, though otherwise they possessed that common constitutive form. Thus with Augustine we affirm that those sacraments differed from ours in signs and were distinguished by their own ends, but that they too were divinely constituted of signs and things signified, that the sacramental signification of all was the same, and finally that the reality itself was common to all those sacraments without exception—namely Christ himself and his benefits. They were entirely equal to ours in this respect, though within this equality they differed by degree, the more obscure from the clearer.

Thus finally we affirm that the Lord’s Supper and baptism agree entirely with each other in the definition of a sacrament, by which a sacrament is distinguished from the simply preached Word—namely, that it consists of signs joined by one and the same sacramental relation and directed toward the common end of enjoying Christ—and indeed in the identity of the thing signified, as in their constitutive form. But they differ in the signs themselves: both the essential signs, such as water in baptism and bread and wine in the sacred Lord’s Supper, and the ritual signs, such as sprinkling or immersion in baptism, and in the Lord’s Supper the breaking and eating of bread and the pouring and drinking of wine. They also differ in the analogy and peculiar end of those signs: in baptism Christ’s blood is presented under the image of a washing by which, cleansed from all sins, we become a new creation; but in the Supper the same Christ is presented to us as the spiritual food of everlasting life, to be apprehended spiritually by faith and increasingly enjoyed by us, who are to be nourished by that perennial life-giving sap.

But as regards the sacraments of the old covenant, our antagonists establish the distinguishing—

[Part II | PDF page 60 | logical source scan 59 | printed page 58]

—form by which they are distinguished from ours even in the thing signified itself. They suppose that those sacraments possessed and presented to those who approached not Christ himself according to the flesh—since that flesh did not yet exist in the nature of things—but only according to his divinity and the saving efficacy of the one who would at last be born. Thus both Christ’s person is torn apart and the nourishment of the food is separated in reality from the food itself, although Christ was never a half-Christ either as the Church’s mediator before God or as the object presented to believers’ faith. The Apostle testifies, both in 1 Corinthians 10 and Hebrews 11, that the fathers’ spiritual food and drink and ours were the same, namely Christ himself, and that their faith and ours were and are the same, as Augustine explains very fully against Faustus. For in the designation of the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world, and indeed of Christ in whom we were elected before the world’s foundations were laid, not simply the Son of God only insofar as he is God must be understood, but the God-man, both before God—to whom all things are present—and before the faith of the godly, which is the true and firm substance of things hoped for.

Not content with this error, some strike the same rock when establishing the distinguishing form of baptism and the Lord’s Supper. They place it not only in the things we have stated, which are most evident, but also in the thing signified and in the sacramental conjunction of that signified reality with the signs. For as regards the thing signified, in baptism as in the ancient sacraments they again do not establish Christ’s own blood—that is, Christ himself, raised from the dead for satisfaction for our sins and for our regeneration into new life—but only his merits and efficacy. Thus Andreae writes on page 445: “Through water the Holy Spirit by the power of Christ’s blood works what Christ merited for us by his blood,” that is, Christ’s merit, as the same Andreae speaks more openly on page 446. As though anyone could share Christ’s merits and the spiritual efficacy poured into us from him without becoming a member of Christ himself, Ephesians 4:16; as though, by the same Apostle’s testimony, we are not baptized by the same Spirit into one body with Christ himself, 1 Corinthians 12:13, and thus put on Christ himself, Galatians 3:27, just as in the outward rite of baptism we appear to be clothed and covered with water.

In the Lord’s Supper, however, they at last establish the very essential body and real blood. In baptism Andreae only with difficulty admits any signification, while in the Lord’s Supper he contends that the Lord’s body and blood are present in reality, under, or with bread and wine, in their own very essence. If they are asked on what foundation so many doctrines diametrically opposed to the principles of the Christian religion are confirmed, some flee to the literal wording of the institution of the Lord’s Supper. Yet by their own explanation they utterly overthrow it, since—

[Part II | PDF page 61 | logical source scan 60 | printed page 59]

—it is entirely different for bread to be the body than for the body to be in, under, or with bread, if the words are understood according to the literal wording in each case. Others retreat to the hypostatic union, though that alone overturns everything they build upon their most shameless invention. They have dared to offer the Church, in order to establish their ubiquitous omnipresence, the very blasphemies of Nestorius in dividing the person, Eutyches in a real communication of essential properties between the natures themselves, and the Monothelites in positing one false theandric operation, and finally to defend what they have offered with the greatest stubbornness.

Let this suffice concerning these things, if I add this one point, which I also wish you, whoever you are, Christian reader, carefully to observe: with what conscience do those who expressly and manifestly separate Christ himself from his merits and benefits both in the ancient sacraments and in our baptism dare accuse us of separating the things signified from the signs when we distinguish them? We say that the whole Christ with his benefits is always truly presented together with the visible signs to all who approach, even the most unworthy, to be received by faith, because God is always true. Yet the whole sacrament is not received by unbelievers, through their own fault: while they unworthily perceive the visible signs with the bodily instrument, by their unbelief and profane contempt they reject the spiritual reality to their certain destruction unless they repent.

### Beza’s Response to Andreae’s Marginal Annotations

#### The Same Must Be Maintained, So Far as Applicable, Concerning the Supper and Baptism

Andreae says: “Beza teaches concerning baptism as concerning the Lord’s Supper, namely, that regeneration, sealing, and remission of sins are OFFERED but not conferred upon all baptized persons.” I answer that we teach precisely the same concerning baptism as concerning the Lord’s Supper in respect of those things common to both according to the definition of a sacrament. Because Andreae does not do this, he errs no less than if, after abolishing the definition of the common genus, he contended that species could be subordinated to that genus. Whether I rightly or wrongly accuse him here of this most serious error, let judgment be made from the two matters which he places before us for dispute here.

#### Andreae Falsely Says That Baptism’s Grace Is Necessarily Conferred upon All the Baptized

Andreae contends that baptism’s effect—which here he pleased, and quite ignorantly as befits a man entirely unskilled in logic, to establish as threefold—is not only presented in baptism but also conferred; that—

[Part II | PDF page 63 | logical source scan 62 | printed page 61]

—is, to speak more plainly, it is not only given to be taken, but is also received by those taking it, and that not only by some but universally by all. Yet not even Andreae would dare say this of the sacred Lord’s Supper, although he seems to attribute almost everything to that mystery alone. For the effect of the Lord’s Supper corresponding opposite the effect of baptism is not Christ’s body or blood itself—which Andreae wishes to be received by everyone who approaches—but the fruit of their reception: the sealing of our spiritual coalescence with Christ himself and of the benefits following from it, just as baptism’s effect is the sealing of our regeneration unto remission of sins and our renewal.

But who, Andreae, would say that all who orally consume the Lord’s Supper are incorporated into Christ or become members of Christ? Yet you are not ashamed to affirm, on the contrary, that Christ himself is consumed by the worthy unto salvation—which we concede—but by the unworthy unto judgment. This is a very great error, as we refuted in its place, since the ruin of those who approach unworthily proceeds not from Christ himself unworthily received, but, on the contrary, from Christ rejected through unbelief and from profanation of the sacred symbols. Andreae therefore plays the sophist by exploiting the ambiguity of “the reality of the sacrament”: that is, he compares the Lord’s Supper’s other substantial part with baptism’s effect, although he himself rightly teaches on page 439 that this must never be done, if only he wishes to seem to teach the same concerning baptism and the Supper. Otherwise, he must confess that he attributes far more to baptism than to the Lord’s Supper.

But what shall become of the simple Word of the Lord? If we believe Andreae, far less must also be attributed to it than to the sacraments. Yet the covenant’s Word is, as it were, the soul of those very sacraments, and although the outward form differs, nothing other is proposed to be believed and received in simple preaching than in the mysteries. Thus someone can even be saved without the sacraments when he could not obtain them without contempt; but every adult who has not been taught to apprehend Christ by faith, either through extraordinary inspiration or the ordinary preaching of the Word, remains outside Christ and will undoubtedly perish.

If these strongest reasons do not sufficiently move Andreae, let him hear Augustine—otherwise too severe toward infants dying without baptism—in Homily 27 rebuking his hearers. They listened drowsily to God’s preached Word but carefully and almost superstitiously watched lest anything from Christ’s body—that is, sacred bread, necessarily so understood by Augustine metonymically, whether Andreae likes it or not—delivered into their hands fall to the ground. Augustine says: “What seems greater to you, God’s Word or Christ’s body? If you wish to answer truthfully, you must surely say that God’s Word is no less than Christ’s body.” And why seek more witnesses?

[Part II | PDF page 64 | logical source scan 63 | printed page 62]

Andreae himself, with most absurd arguments on page 500, attempts from this to establish baptism by a woman in the case of necessity which his party invents: because it is permissible even for women to announce the Word to the dying and, as he speaks, absolve the hearers. “Which,” he asks, “is greater: the Word and preaching of the Word, or the sacrament of baptism and its administration?” Yet so far is that Word from actually conferring eternal life, which it offers to all hearers by one and the same preaching, that on the contrary to unbelievers, as most are, it is the aroma of death unto death. This occurs through no defect or fault either of the Word itself or of the sacraments, but through the fault of those who hear unworthily and use the sacred signs profanely. Let Andreae therefore teach us about this excellence of baptism above God’s Word and the Lord’s Supper, or correct these errors also, along with far too many others.

Now, as for those three things—regeneration, sealing, and remission of sins—I cannot conceive how they can be mutually opposed as the method of division requires. For if regeneration is taken generally as reception among God’s children, it will also comprehend remission of sins; but if it is understood more narrowly as our renewal, then a division of baptism’s benefits into two members would be suitable. Sealing, however, is something different from the things sealed and consequently must be enumerated outside them. Moreover, things are properly said to be CONFERRED which someone did not previously possess, but things are sealed which are already possessed. Therefore regeneration, remission of sins, and their sealing cannot together be regarded either as species of the same genus or as parts of some whole. Nor do I press the proper signification of these terms pointlessly or with an empty quibble, as will appear from what follows.

But let us hear by what arguments in the conference Andreae proves that universality—that is, that regeneration is necessarily CONFERRED upon all rightly baptized persons—not only probably but necessarily. First, on page 480 he cites Paul’s statement from Galatians 3:27: “All of you who were baptized into Christ have put on Christ.” Since there is no doubt that Paul’s words properly concern those very people to whom he wrote—that is, adults who had for the most part been bewitched by false apostles—everyone sees that they cannot be understood of every individual baptized adult without every appearance of reason. Even if the purity of the Galatian churches had been so great as the Apostle complains it was not, and so great as no church has ever attained, what spirit taught Andreae that the same must be maintained concerning all churches of those times? It is also clear from what the Apostle adds there that he does not thus designate individual persons but abolishes distinctions of nations, conditions, and ages. Yet I infer that Andreae holds hypocrites and unbelievers, though baptized, not to be regen—

[Part II | PDF page 65 | logical source scan 64 | printed page 63]

—erated because he so precisely requires personal faith in the baptized that he does not except even infants, which we shall discuss in its place.

Andreae himself now supplies us a clear answer to another argument of his, expressed on the same page and frequently pressed elsewhere against us: namely, that unless we judge that everyone rightly baptized is necessarily regenerated, we separate the Spirit from water in outward baptism. Yet if this were true, it would be necessary, in order to avoid this separation, to hold either that unbelieving adults are never rightly baptized with outward baptism or that they too are regenerated. Andreae cannot maintain either without the greatest absurdity.

As regards this separation, since God, whose truth always remains constant, presents grace with the promise of grace both in the simple Word and in the sacraments to all who hear the Word and likewise to all who approach to receive the sacraments, but presents the Word and sacred symbols to the bodily senses while presenting grace itself to the mind to be taken only through the instrument of faith—as is clear from the covenant’s formula—it is no wonder that those who bring only bodily organs and lack faith’s instrument receive the sacred symbols unto ruin because of their profanation of them and contempt of God, while rejecting the reality both of the simply preached Word and of the sacraments, that is, Christ himself together with his benefits and grace. Since Andreae is compelled to acknowledge this entirely in the simple Word and to some extent in the sacred Lord’s Supper, namely with respect to the Supper’s fruit, from what knowledge or conscience have come those tragic cries against us that those who deny all and every baptized person necessarily to be regenerated at that very moment bring into God’s Church a false, impious, blasphemous, unheard-of, and intolerable error?

Even the scholastics, however much they defend the doctrine of *opus operatum*, are so far from necessarily and always joining the sacrament’s reality with the sacred symbols by a bond with respect to their use that they confess that some receive the reality and the sacrament, some the sacrament and not the reality, and some finally the reality and not the sacrament. Augustine therefore says in *On Baptism*, book 5, chapter 25: “People sometimes put on Christ as far as reception of the sacrament”—that is, of the signs—“and sometimes as far as sanctification of life. The former may be common to good and evil, but the latter is proper to the good and godly.” And on Psalm 77: “The sacraments were common to all the Jews, but grace, which is the power of the sacraments, was not common. So now baptism is common to all baptized persons, but baptism’s power is not common.” I do not doubt that Andreae will object here that this question concerning adult baptism seems less necessary in our times, since, with very few exceptions, only infants are baptized today—

[Part II | PDF page 66 | logical source scan 65 | printed page 64]

—and that consequently it is principally their baptism that must be examined. We shall at last undertake that when we have answered the rest of Andreae’s arguments pertaining to the preceding question.

### An Extensive Treatment of How Truly to Raise the Spirits of Those Who Despair, Doubting Their Election and Salvation Either Because They Never Heard God Calling or Because They Abandoned Him After Hearing Him

“Therefore,” he says and repeatedly insists on pages 471, 472, 473, 480, 481, 482, and elsewhere as well, “unless regeneration always coheres with baptism and in every baptized person, no place of consolation is left for raising the consciences of those who, because they then feel no motions of the Holy Spirit in themselves, have nowhere to flee except to the Word and sacraments, and especially to baptism. But this remedy would be of no importance unless this were necessarily opposed to those thoughts inspired by the Devil: that God is greater than our heart, who in baptism not only offered us adoption but actually conferred it, just as he said, ‘He who believes and is baptized will be saved,’ and Paul, ‘As many of you as were baptized have put on Christ.’ Armed with this consolation of circumcision, David attacks Goliath. Otherwise, it follows that baptism is an idle ceremony and that the whole Trinity is accused of falsehood. Satan must therefore rather be driven away with these words: ‘Depart from me, Satan; you have no part in me, because I have been baptized in the name of the holy Trinity and have truly been adopted as a son of God.’”

Are these, then, the things by which Andreae, repeating them so often and so verbosely, has utterly overthrown me? Since this argument is very entangled, however, I unravel it as follows. First, as regards the passages of Scripture cited, “God is greater than our heart” would increase despair, not bring consolation. For John does not mention this in 1 John 3:20 in order to aid the despairing by setting before them the greatness of God’s mercy, but to crush the proud with the immensity of the divine majesty. Next, what comfort would these words bring to a man who doubts his election and feels no testimonies of faith in himself—the kind of man at issue here: “He who believes and is baptized will be saved”? On the contrary, he would reason thus: “But I have not believed; therefore, although baptized, I shall not be saved.” For Augustine rightly says in tractate 6 on John, speaking of Simon Magus: “What did baptism profit him? Therefore do not boast of baptism as though salvation from it alone were sufficient for you.” I show a little later how openly Andreae distorts that passage of Paul from Galatians 3. Nor are the arguments from absurdity with which Andreae tries to buttress his argument any stronger. Is God not truthful because his truth is neglected or even mocked by its despisers? Is baptism’s ceremony therefore empty in itself because some—

[Part II | PDF page 67 | logical source scan 66 | printed page 65]

—reject the grace promised in it, while others, if Andreae is to be believed, cast off the grace they received? Is the gospel not the power of God unto salvation because it is the aroma of death unto death to unbelievers? Is the Lord’s Supper not the pledge of the Lord’s covenant because so many abuse the sacred signs—or even, as Andreae contends, Christ’s body itself once eaten? And, to take an argument from things most familiar as well, is light separated from the sun because it shines neither upon the blind, nor upon sleepers, nor upon those who voluntarily close their eyes so that they cannot enjoy its light?

But the most inept thing of all, and what Andreae places beneath this argument as its foundation, is this: that flight to baptism in such temptations is vain unless we maintain with him that all baptized persons are necessarily adopted in baptism. First, if this inference is valid in baptism, it would likewise be futile to refer a tempted person to the testimony of the Word and the Lord’s Supper unless we say that all to whom God’s Word is preached and all who approach the Lord’s Table are also necessarily regenerated and adopted. Andreae cannot affirm either without shameless falsehood. But even setting this aside and conceding what Andreae demands concerning baptism, would not someone tempted at Satan’s instigation repel this remarkable comforter by his own argument? “Grant, Andreae, what you say: I was baptized and then adopted. Yet since you say that this grace is not firm but that I could fall away from it—as indeed I feel that I have fallen away—do you not yourself snatch from me with one hand what you seem to grant me with the other? Why, then, do you suggest these things to me, which not only fail to raise me up but, on the contrary, set my detestable ingratitude more and more before my eyes?” Behold how firm a consolation Andreae’s doctrine supplies to consciences laboring under a most dangerous temptation.

If someone asks, therefore, how that temptation is to be met, I shall say what rests upon the surest foundation, what I have very often proved by experience in many people, and what I have determined to explain fully for the Christian reader’s sake. First, contrary to what Andreae most falsely charges against us, we teach that the eternal *prothesis* (πρόθεσιν), as Paul calls it, which is the foundation of the elect’s salvation, must never be sought by us in the abyss of God himself, but in its revelation—that is, in calling through the Word and sacraments. I speak of adults, such as those whom we are considering how to console here must necessarily be. And because that outward calling is not peculiar to the elect alone (“many are called, but few are chosen”), but only the calling that is effectual—that is, through which the understanding is not only illuminated with a saving knowledge of the true God, but there is also created in the will, though not perfectly yet truly, a hatred of evil, from which arise flight from sin and love of the good, or zeal to will and act rightly—therefore in that most dangerous temptation—

[Part II | PDF page 68 | logical source scan 67 | printed page 66]

—we take the true nepenthe, as it were, from this medicine chest and apply it to the afflicted conscience.

If, then, I am dealing with someone who either has never been called by the ministry of the gospel, or who, although called, has nevertheless seemed to himself or others never to have heard the one calling, and who thence concludes that he is not among those upon whom the Lord has determined to have mercy, I suggest to him that Satan is a sophist who teaches him to draw that conclusion no less falsely than if someone standing in the middle of the night affirmed that, because the sun had not yet risen, it would never rise. And when I had brought this before Andreae on page 482, he published it in a most shamelessly corrupted form in these words: “The tempted person must be told that the sun has risen even if it has not yet risen”—as though I teach men to lie, whether this shameless corruption of my statement proceeded from Andreae’s copyists or from Andreae himself. And when Andreae objected to this that the consolation would be useless because that miserable man could also doubt whether the sun would rise for him afterward, I added briefly what his copyists here omitted entirely and what for that reason I shall now repeat more fully.

Therefore, after driving that miserable man away from that false and satanic conclusion, I am accustomed immediately to suggest to him that, although outward calling by itself does not suffice to calm the conscience completely, it nevertheless has the greatest presumptive force and weight against Satan. Those who are never called either inwardly or outwardly—for we are speaking here of ordinary calling—necessarily perish; but whoever is called already has his foot upon the first threshold of salvation, whence, unless the hindrance lies with him, a sure entrance lies open into the forecourt and at last into the very palace of salvation. I confirm this exhortation in many ways. “Why,” I say, “should you doubt the will of him who sent me to call you? You have no reason, unless perhaps it is the multitude of your sins. Set against this the immensity of the divine mercy which God offers you through me. God is leading you into the way of the elect; why would you refuse to follow it? If you do not yet feel yourself moved inwardly, ask that you may be moved. Know that this desire is a pledge of his fatherly benevolence toward you. He can neither fail nor will to fail what he alone has stirred up in you.” Finally, I add to these exhortations the passage about those called at the eleventh hour, the calling of the Gentiles after so many centuries, the thief hanging upon the cross, and other things of this kind—remedies which I do not remember ever using in vain.

But if I am dealing with those who once heard God calling but have fallen into some grave sin, or have remained outside the Church, or after long despising public and private admonitions have become a stumbling block to the Church, or—as I have more than once observed happen even to certain of the best and most devout—are so wholly occupied in constantly reproving themselves because they do not satisfy themselves that for a time they forget divine clemency, not to mention those who through the fault of a weaker constitution are sometimes most dangerously—

[Part II | PDF page 69 | logical source scan 68 | printed page 67]

—exercised by very grave temptations of this kind unless they find a suitable physician, I have been accustomed, I say, to deal with them as follows.

First I require them to pour into my bosom whatever especially torments them so greatly; and once I have learned from them what and how great it is, I take particular care not to cast down completely those already more than sufficiently terrified by setting the Law’s severity before them. Yet I do not entirely draw them away from condemnation of their sins and meditation upon divine judgment, but temper the language of consolation according to my ability so as by no means to conceal that God is nevertheless deservedly angry with them. Once they have thus been prepared, I finally ask whether they have always been affected in this way.

“No,” they usually answer. “There was a time when, with a most peaceful conscience, I called joyfully upon God, blessed and full of faith and hope. Miserable man that I am, I have now poured away that grace, and nothing now torments me more than the memory of that former time.”

“But,” I say, “which thought presses you more heavily: apprehension of divine judgment, or displeasure that you have offended so kind a Father?”

“Both,” they say, “and especially the latter.”

“Then,” I say, “sin displeases you also insofar as it is sin—that is, because it is shameful and God, as supremely good, is offended by it?”

“It is so,” they say, “and I am ashamed to present myself, so wicked, before one so good.”

“But no one,” I say, “is grieved by an injury to one whom he hates, but rather rejoices in it.”

“That is so,” they say, “and far be it from me to hate God himself, even though he should pursue me with hatred. If only it might rather be possible for me to be reconciled to him!”

Then I say: “Take courage, my brother; the matter is safe. For who can love God, especially when he strikes; who can lament the benevolence which he thinks he has lost; who can desire to return into favor with him, except one whom God, however angry, has not ceased to love? Unless perhaps you have not learned that knowledge of our salvation in Christ arises not from flesh and blood, but from God himself, who first deigns to grant us knowledge of himself, and from Christ himself revealing the Father to us; and that God is not loved first by us, but loved in return through the kindness of him who first loved us when we were his enemies. You therefore have reason, my brother, to be displeased with yourself for many past things, but not to despair. Finally, you have within you and in your own possession sure testimonies that the very reconciliation with him which you desire will follow, provided that you do not cease to entreat with constant prayers him who laid in you the foundation of true repentance—namely, displeasure with sin and desire for reconciliation. Even the sheep which wandered outside the flock for a time is still a sheep; and you yourself are that sheep which, leaving those ninety-nine, the faithful keeper of every sheep entrusted to him by the Father does not so much announce through my ministry that he is seeking as that he has found after seeking it, although it was not seeking him. ‘Knock,’ he says, ‘and it will be opened to you.’ Have you forgotten those promises so often pressed upon the repentant, indeed—

[Part II | PDF page 70 | logical source scan 69 | printed page 68]

—confirmed by so many examples even of those who could have seemed utterly beyond hope?”

“But,” he says again, “I now have no motions of that Comforter, no sense of faith or hope, but experience all things contrary to them.”

“No,” I say, “you deceive yourself, as I admonished you shortly before. For no one other than that Comforter teaches a person to hate sin not only because of punishment, but because it is shameful and for God’s sake, although he does not yet reveal himself entirely. For you have offended him in so many ways that for a little while he seems to have abandoned you almost completely. But the very thing of which I now remind you for the second time proves that he has not entirely departed from you, but has only withdrawn into some innermost corner of your mind, whence, won over again by your prayers, he will reveal himself. But even if we grant you the very thing you say, this will have been a lethargy of faith, not its extinction. You lived in your mother’s womb and were yourself unaware of your life. A drunken person certainly loses not reason, but the use of reason—and sometimes even of his body—for a time. In winter you would call trees dead which truly become green again. Today the sun will set, but tomorrow it will rise again. And how often in duels has the man who was thrown down nevertheless emerged the victor? Know that the same happens to everyone in that spiritual struggle between flesh and Spirit, partly through our frailty, partly through our sloth, and partly even through our fault.”

To this he replies—for temptations of this kind are most obstinate in some—“If only I could establish that these things pertain to me. For my present condition compels me to doubt whether I am among those upon whom God has determined to have mercy.”

### The Elect Never Entirely Lose the Holy Spirit or Wholly Fall Away from the Faith by Which They Are Justified and Truly Sanctified

From this arises that most weighty question: whether the Holy Spirit and that true faith can be entirely lost. For I do not deny that one can lose the sense and exercise of faith for a time, and in this way I interpret the meaning of learned and godly men who have used those expressions. But I deny that true faith itself is lost; and consequently I affirm that every time Andreae’s copyists omitted that particle ENTIRELY from my miserably abridged responses in that conference, they became liable to the charge of falsehood. Andreae, then, affirms that this can occur and contends that it actually does occur in the sins which he calls mortal. We must first dispute concerning this distinction of sins.

### What Must Be Maintained from God’s Word Concerning the Distinction of Sins into Venial and Mortal

Let us therefore see, I ask, what an excellent distinction he establishes on page 465 between the sins which he calls mortal and venial. “Venial sins,” he says, “are—

[Part II | PDF page 71 | logical source scan 70 | printed page 69]

—the stains of human infirmity which are not imputed to believers and the elect, according to the saying, ‘The righteous man falls seven times in a day and rises seven times,’ and through which the Holy Spirit is not lost. Mortal sins, however, are crimes against God’s law, according to Paul’s saying, ‘If you live according to the flesh, you will die.’” These are his words. Although I acknowledge, as I often answered him, that there is a great distinction between faults and crimes, I nevertheless demonstrate that nearly every statement here is both falsely and improperly expressed.

For what, I ask, is that stain of infirmity except the source and origin of all sins—in those, at any rate, who have been regenerated? For only they are at issue here. In them, reigning corruption has been changed into an infirmity which struggles painfully and not without a great contest against the remnants of that corruption. Nor is this observation of no weight; rather, it is of the greatest importance, so that the struggle between reason and the passions as described by the philosophers—which the scholastics for the most part retained, changing only the names—may be distinguished from that conflict of flesh and Spirit described in Galatians 5. In this latter conflict, even the philosophers’ “right reason” (ὀρθὸς λόγος), though rendering man “without excuse” (ἀναπολόγητον) in not a few matters, must nevertheless be subdued by the Spirit as a chief enemy, as becomes clear when the question arises concerning no few sins whether they are sins or otherwise, and how light or grave they are.

But what sort of statement is this: “Venial sins are not imputed to believers and the elect”? For if this refers to God’s pardoning those sins, it will follow either that the mortal sins opposed to them are not pardoned—in which case, if that is true, who will be saved?—or that venial sins are falsely and ineptly distinguished from mortal by this difference. But if Andreae refers it to the very nature of venial sins, as though some sins do not deserve eternal death, let him go openly over to the sophists’ camp and, if he wishes, take upon himself the burden of those sins for which Christ need not have died. Yet I would not attribute this to him, since on that very page—perhaps reminded by my response—he acknowledges the contrary.

But again, by restricting Paul’s statement, “If you live according to the flesh, you will die,” to only those offenses which he calls mortal sins in opposition to venial sins, how will he not in turn be judged to reckon each according to its nature—the former, namely, as worthy of pardon and only the latter as worthy of eternal death—or to believe that venial sins are pardoned but mortal sins are not likewise pardoned? For my part, I place beyond controversy that in those words Paul, without any distinction of sins, included all in whom sin reigns.

On the immediately following page, that is, 466, attempting to explain this mystery more fully, he says: “Infirmities must be distinguished from manifest crimes.” I concede it. But since both the former and the latter deserve eternal death—as appears even from the sacrifices under the Law for even the lightest sins, and from Christ’s prayer for those who through ignorance had sworn that he should be crucified—and since both are pardoned to the repentant, what more inept thing could have been adduced to establish that distinction between—

[Part II | PDF page 72 | logical source scan 71 | printed page 70]

—venial and mortal sins?

What, then, are those manifest crimes? “Those,” Andreae says, “which Paul calls works of the flesh, which are fornication, uncleanness, and so forth; those who commit them will not obtain God’s kingdom.” But again, how do these things pertain to that distinction of sins? Does not the Apostle in that catalogue include among these sins strifes, jealousies, and animosities, which very often steal upon even the unwary? Indeed, what crime will a man in whom anger rules not commit? And whence come all even the lightest sins except from the flesh, which reigns completely in those who are not regenerated but is not yet subdued in the regenerate? And since Paul, neither in this passage nor anywhere else, opposes to those works of the flesh any infirmities which Andreae calls venial sins, but instead the works of the Spirit, what is it to twist the Lord’s Word to the sense of one’s own mind if what he does is not precisely that?

But let us also hear certain still worse things. “There is another kind of sins,” he says, “which even the elect cannot put off until they die, but against which they fight until the last breath of life. Thus they sin not deliberately but against their will, as when someone more prone to anger than another becomes angry against his own will. Concerning these sins Paul says, ‘If I do what I do not will, it is no longer I who perform it, but the sin dwelling in me’; and, ‘There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus’; and, ‘Blessed are those whose sins are covered.’”

These, then, are Andreae’s venial sins, marked by three differences: namely, that they cannot be put off even by the elect before death; that the elect fight against them; and finally, that they are committed against the will. If these marks are true, and if Andreae has observed the laws of division, which provide that the members of a division do not coincide with each other, since they are opposites, it will follow that mortal sins are defined as those which can be put off even by the elect in this life, against which the elect no longer fight, and which, when committed, are committed willingly. Let us therefore speak distinctly of these three points.

As regards the first, I say that it is most false and utterly untheological (ἀθεολόγητον) to suppose that the sin born with us—that is, the origin of all sins—can be put off by the elect. For no one should be said to have put off sin who, although by the power of the Holy Spirit—in whose place the philosophers establish their own ill-defined “right reason” (ὀρθὸν λόγον)—does not produce those fruits which the Apostle calls works of the flesh, nevertheless still has the root implanted in him, which will soon sprout again unless it is gradually destroyed by constant prayers and every other means employed. Let the greatest of all sins serve us as an example: doubt concerning God and his truth and promises, even in those who have embraced them with true faith. Hence that statement of David: “In my hasty flight I said, Every man is a liar.” Witnesses are Moses himself and Aaron, who fell into an by no means empty appearance of distrust and were consequently barred from entry into Canaan. A witness is that perpetual petition of Christians, pertaining without exception to every kind of temptation: “Lead us not into temptation.”

[Part II | PDF page 73 | logical source scan 72 | printed page 71]

A witness is Paul himself, crying aloud that sin universally, and the body of death, still dwell in him. What must be maintained concerning other, most manifest sins as well is shown by that grievous example of David, who, though most thoroughly regenerated, was at the same time an adulterer and a most treacherous murderer. For whence came these fruits in such great men except from sin not yet put off, nor to be put off before death? The same must be maintained even concerning the lightest sins of the regenerate, which themselves sprout from that same root. Andreae is not very far from the madness of the Anabaptists, who dream that they have arrived at the point of being sinless (ἀναμάρτητοι); he certainly lays the first foundation of that error when he thinks that sin can be put off in this life in the works of the flesh, which he wrongly restricts to what he calls mortal sins. But just as sin is not put off entirely in the regenerate in this life, so the fruits of righteousness—although they do not always exert themselves with equal vigor—show that it is not retained whole, but gradually dies. Vain, therefore, is that distinction between venial and mortal sins. Neither the one nor the other casts out the Holy Spirit, but both interrupt his efficacy.

Andreae places the second difference in this: that in venial sins the elect fight, but not likewise in mortal sins. I say that this is no less false than the preceding point. For the Apostle’s statement that the flesh in the elect fights against the Spirit is necessarily reciprocal. And although the passions are sometimes so headlong that neither reason, much less the Holy Spirit, may seem to have been called into counsel, nevertheless it can be gathered only by conjecture—and by no means a necessary one—that in these excesses the elect are carried headlong so completely by the flesh that they feel no motion of a regenerated conscience, even if for a time they do not give it a hearing. For this axiom is most certain: sin does not reign in the regenerate, but as much has been taken away from its tyranny as grace has wrested from it in the mind and affections of a person—grace which will necessarily be victorious in the elect.

Sarah, therefore, sinned through impatience when she gave her maidservant to her husband, and Abraham likewise when persuaded by her, as though the promise concerning that blessed seed would otherwise come to nothing. Nevertheless, the faith which had apprehended that promise fought within them and strengthened them; for they would not otherwise have attempted that useless remedy unless they had hoped that what they believed God had promised would thus come to pass. Nor is Rebekah’s example dissimilar. She had heard and believed that the elder would serve the younger, and yet through impatience substituted Jacob for Esau by evil arts, a deed for which both she and Jacob afterward paid great and lengthy penalties. Finally, it would be wicked to number Moses and Aaron among unbelievers, and to count David, although slumbering in his sin, among those in whom the spirit of adultery and murder reigns peacefully. This distinction of Andreae’s between venial and mortal sins, therefore, is no truer than the other—

[Part II | PDF page 74 | logical source scan 73 | printed page 72]

—preceding one. And since the regenerate necessarily fight, though not always with equal strength, both in light and in grave sins, the Spirit is entirely extinguished in neither.

The third of these differences consists in this: that those who err venially do what they do not will—that is, sin unwillingly—whereas those who sin mortally do what they will. For example, Andreae says, “When I become angry, then I do what I do not will; but when someone fornicates, he does what he wills.” As though someone could not knowingly and deliberately, while refusing to hear even his protesting conscience, also become most violently enraged and—unless he either returns voluntarily to himself or is restrained from elsewhere—commit even the greatest crime; and likewise as though someone drunk either in mind or body could not fornicate inadvertently. Examples of both exist even among the most holy: of the former in David when he was preparing to destroy Nabal with his whole household, but was restrained by Abigail’s entreaties and gave God thanks on that account (1 Samuel 25); of the latter in incestuous Lot (Genesis 19), whom Peter nevertheless calls righteous in 2 Peter 2:7. And although he was certainly not righteous in drunkenness and incest, he nevertheless was righteous even while incestuous, as he doubtless showed by repenting.

Next, in this point too not only is the greatest absurdity apparent, but nearly incredible ignorance is detected. For who besides these men ever became so insane as to say that someone sins unwillingly or under compulsion? Even the philosophers, setting forth the example of those who can seem to suffer loss unwillingly, nevertheless rightly refer actions of this kind not to “the compulsory” (τὸ βίαιον), however much it intervenes, but to “the voluntary” (τὸ ἑκούσιον), because they proceed from the agent’s consent, though it has been extorted. It is therefore certain that the elect, however thoroughly regenerated, sin only willingly, whether in lighter or even the lightest sins—yet not willingly in the same way as those who are not regenerated and in whom sin reigns. For these latter, inclined with their whole will, are carried away even against reason, whence the saying: “I see and approve the better things; I follow the worse.” But although a regenerate person is one and the same man, endowed with numerically one and the same will, nevertheless, because two contrary principles are situated in that one man—namely, the flesh, insofar as he is not regenerated, and the Spirit, insofar as he has been set free from slavery to sin by the Spirit of the Son—one and the same man is said both to will and not to will to sin.

Paul manifestly indicates this, establishing in himself, as in one subject, both the man who does not will and the man who wills. Yet he sets the latter, the man who wills to sin, as though outside himself like a certain guest, both to teach that this corruption in man is not something created but adventitious and because he was certain that it would at last be expelled entirely. By his example he teaches that we too ought to be certain, since in chapter 6 he had broken forth into this declaration: “Sin will not have dominion over you, for you are not under the Law but under grace.” I confess, therefore, that in that passage Paul presents to us in himself—

[Part II | PDF page 75 | logical source scan 74 | printed page 73]

—the example of a strong athlete, in whom the Spirit so fights as to rise above the flesh, though not without a wound. For he still fights who does not do the good that he wills, yet in this contest he overcomes who unwillingly does evil. But far be it from us therefore to say that the Spirit is defeated in those who are under discussion here—that is, in those who for a time do the evil which they will more than they do not will, though they do not will it wholly—just as, conversely, the flesh is not entirely defeated in those who so will the good that they nevertheless do not will it wholly. For both belong to those still wrestling in that contest in which the Spirit, though by God’s grace it will necessarily be victorious at last, nevertheless wrestles with varying fortune. Hence the lament of the Apostle, although he by no means doubted the victory: “Who will deliver me from this body of death?”

Since these things are so, let Germany judge from them what kind of physicians of conscience these men are, both in recognizing the causes of diseases and in recognizing and applying their remedies as they ought. Thus far, then, these things have been said concerning this distinction of sins which arose from the scholastics. If anyone asks what I think of it once for all, I say—with the good leave of those otherwise most learned theologians by whom these designations have been retained, however much softened—that this distinction seems to me rather to be eliminated entirely from the Church, both because it is improper however it is explained and because it is most dangerous. For the usage of the Latin language shows that these words cannot properly be referred anywhere except to the very nature of sins. But no sins are venial by their own nature, not even the lightest in comparison with others, as is also manifestly apparent from Matthew 5:21. For the wages of sin, without any exception, is death. But if this is referred to God’s judgment, all sins washed away by Christ’s blood in the elect are venial—that is, in those who sin in such a manner that through the same beneficence they are also always granted the gift of repentance before death, because God’s purpose cannot be changed or fail—with the single exception of the sin which is therefore called the sin unto death, because it is never remitted and consequently does not befall the elect.

Refutation of the arguments by which it is maintained that David, through adultery and murder, entirely lost the Holy Spirit and fell wholly away from faith.

“But,” says Andreae on p. 467, “unless Nathan had reproved David and David had repented, he would never have been saved. Therefore he had fallen away from the Holy Spirit and faith.” I concede the antecedent, but deny the consequent. For David sinned, yet not as a whole, but insofar as he was unregenerate: the man unwilling to sin was indeed lulled to sleep within him for a time, but was not wholly abolished. The prophet did not restore this man to David as though he had been lost; rather, by God’s grace—which never forsakes his own—he raised him up after he had for a time been laid low beneath the wound inflicted by the man willing to sin.

[Part II | PDF page 76 | logical source scan 75 | printed page 74]

For, as we have just said, the same man at one and the same time both sins and does not sin, being unwilling in such a way that he wills, and willing in such a way that he does not will. This has a place only in the regenerate; all others, when they sin, will nothing but to sin, since, as the Apostle testifies, they are not even able of themselves to think the things that are of God. And to the regenerate pertains John’s statement, “He who is born of God does not sin,” although in the same epistle he says, “Little children, do not sin; but if anyone sins, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ.” The same is true of this: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves.” He is surely speaking of sins of every kind, even the gravest, as is clear from the most grievous examples of even the holiest men.

And yet it is true that David would have perished unless he had repented. But insofar as he was elect and regenerate, the grace of repentance could not be denied him, nor could he, by God’s grace, fail to repent. For just as God, because he can neither change nor be deceived, necessarily brings his elect, with respect to his ordination, to the glorification prepared for them before the ages, so throughout the whole course of their life he never permits the intermediate causes—namely, calling and justification—to wither away entirely, although they are not always vigorous with equal strength. Rather, as it has seemed good to him, he does not cease to measure out and foster them until he perfects his work in them in the life to come.

And lest I seem rashly to have affirmed that David was not then newly endowed with the Spirit of regeneration after having entirely lost it, but that the Spirit which lay sleeping within him was divinely awakened through Nathan’s ministry, I indeed acknowledge that in those most sorrowful cries David employs the language of creating and renewing, as though that divine work had to be begun in him entirely anew. Nevertheless, that the case was otherwise, and that the enormity of his crimes wrung these expressions from him, may be established at least from this: he could not even have made this request unless the remnants of that very Spirit for whom he was asking had dictated these unutterable groanings to him. He also indicates this most plainly when he immediately asks that this very Spirit for whom he was asking, and whose remnants the Lord had awakened within him, not be taken away from him. Hence Psalm 27:8: “My heart said to you, Seek my face”—that is, the voice of God resounding in the hearts of the regenerate, especially after grievous falls, in the souls of the regenerate no differently than an echo from hollow places, so that confidence in invocation arises from the mutual harmony of those who answer him by faith. For it is one thing to grieve the Holy Spirit, as happens at times even to the holiest, but another to drive him out entirely. And although he has been badly received, nevertheless after reproofs he raises up the elect, confirming them by the remembrance of that former happy condition, as is shown to us in the parable of the prodigal, Luke 15:17, and as David teaches us by his own example in these words of Psalm 123:5: “I have remembered the days of old, and I have meditated on all your work.”

[Part II | PDF page 77 | logical source scan 76 | printed page 75]

Simon Magus never possessed that true faith which is proper to the elect.

Andreae also sought on p. 462 to persuade us by the example of Simon Magus that some can fall away from faith. For, he says, Luke openly declares that Simon believed Philip as he preached the gospel, that after being baptized he continued with Philip, and that when he saw the signs which Philip performed he was astonished. As though even the demons did not believe, and as though there were not a kind of temporary faith (πρόσκαιρος), another that is entirely feigned, and another that is dead. But here we are dealing with that faith which is proper to the elect, which does not merely taste but truly eats the flesh of the Son of God and truly drinks his blood, as is clear from the true efficacy and effects of his Spirit. Speaking of these things, John says in John 6:64, “Jesus knew from the beginning who they were who believed in him, and who would betray him,” thereby excepting Judas Iscariot from the number of the other eleven disciples who truly believed. Yet Judas had seemed to adhere to Peter’s confession in Matthew 16, and possessed the faith of miracles in Luke 9; and unless he had believed Christ more deeply than Simon believed Philip, he would not have followed him for so long.

This example therefore accomplishes nothing against me, since I am speaking of the Spirit bearing witness to our spirit that we are children of God, or of those grafted into Christ truly and not merely in the bark—that is, only so far as outward profession and the sacraments extend—and therefore to be cut off because they do not bear fruit in him. And to return to the example of Simon Magus himself, I wish to admonish Osiander here. Because of that most ardent zeal of his, he could not contain himself in that colloquy, but broke the silence and burst out in these words: “If it is always permissible in this way to evade the testimonies of Holy Scripture by glosses—‘He believed,’ that is, ‘He pretended to believe’—what certainty shall we finally retain in the whole of Holy Scripture?” I admonish him, I say—whom I did not then deem worthy of an answer—that if he does not trust this gloss of mine, he should at least trust Augustine and his own colleague, certainly the more learned of the two in this matter.

For Augustine treats this matter as follows in Tractate 6 on John: “Even if he did not believe, later events will show with what sort of faith he believed. With what sort, I say, not how long he believed. He may have baptism, but he did not cleave to the dove’s inward parts; he was in the Church, not as a dove, but as a raven. What profit did baptism bring him? Do not therefore boast of baptism as though salvation from it alone were sufficient for you.” Behold how everyone who is baptized is regenerated! And how could he have lost that Holy Spirit and faith when he never possessed either? Herbrand, however, says on p. 620 of his *Compendium of Theology*: “Simon Magus was truly baptized, but he neither received remission of sins nor was regenerated, because he lacked faith. For the sacraments to profit, the faith of the recipient must be added.” Do you hear this, Osiander? Do you hear it, Andreae? And when you returned home, did that colleague of yours—

[Part II | PDF page 78 | logical source scan 77 | printed page 76]

—either already receive a beating from you, or will he receive one hereafter? Or will you not even permit that man—certainly far more learned than either of you in very many matters, pitiable not only because of extreme old age but also because of blindness—to die quietly and peacefully?

Saul was never truly circumcised in heart.

The example of Saul, pressed by Andreae over four pages—namely 474, 475, 476, and 477—carries the same weight. He also tries to show that Saul was truly adopted as a son of God by circumcision, this adoption not merely being offered to him but truly and actually conferred upon him, from which he afterward fell away. He employs the following arguments.

First, because there was one circumcision. I concede this when circumcision is taken for the whole sacrament, but not when only one part is meant. For there is one circumcision of the letter and another of the heart. As we showed in its proper place, both are always joined as regards God who offers them, but not as regards all who receive them; for those whom the prophets and Stephen therefore call uncircumcised in heart must clearly be excepted. That conclusion, therefore, is ridiculous because it arises from a ridiculous antecedent.

Second, Andreae says, “What a profanation of God’s name it is to say that by circumcision God sealed the grace of adoption in Saul as an infant, although he was never adopted—as though, for example, some prince promising his favor and a most ample fief were to seal a parchment on which nothing had been written.” But how gross and foolish, I ask, is this sophistry? For who among us ever dreamed, much less said, that by the sacraments grace comprehended in the sacramental promise is sealed in someone upon whom that grace was not conferred either at the same time or beforehand—that is, that something which does not exist is sealed? And yet Andreae dared to fasten this upon us on pp. 476, 477, and 487. And under what pretext, I ask? “Because,” he says, “you say that baptism is a sealing of God’s grace.” But where, finally? “In your response,” he says, “to my fourth thesis.” What shamelessness is this? Paul says that circumcision was the seal of the righteousness of faith—but in Abraham and in those who followed Abraham’s faith. So too we said that baptism is the sealing of remission and regeneration, but not in everyone and not always. Thus the gospel is God’s power unto salvation—always, absolutely considered and in itself; but relatively, or with respect to its hearers, it is an aroma of life unto life only to believers, whereas to the rest it is an aroma of death unto death, and this through their fault, not God’s or the gospel’s. So too the bread which we break is a communion of the Lord’s body. Is it so also to those who are not Christ’s members? Yet this is Andreae’s logic: whoever does not assent to it accuses the sacred triad of falsehood and introduces into God’s Church a false, impious, unheard-of dogma.

[Part II | PDF page 79 | logical source scan 78 | printed page 77]

Third, Andreae says, Saul possessed manifest signs of the Holy Spirit dwelling in him. But what were they? Obedience toward his father. Yet this too, and indeed much more remarkable obedience, has shone forth in many who are altogether alien to God’s covenant. For Satan cannot abolish natural affections (Στοργὰς physicas) in everyone. Remarkable humility toward all; singular patience and moderation; prudence worthy of a king. Yes—to procure a kingdom for himself by these arts and preserve it once procured, not from any genuine zeal for piety or sincere love toward the people, as his whole life testified. But let us grant that these virtues in Saul were genuine; nevertheless, they cannot be attributed either to adoption or to that regeneration which is proper to the saints and children of God, since one everywhere reads in histories that far more illustrious and splendid gifts of God of this kind were conferred upon many otherwise profane men. Add also the spirit of prophecy: what does this contribute to the matter? For Balaam too was a prophet, and indeed remarkably endowed with God’s Spirit. And among those to whom the Lord will answer, “I do not know you,” are numbered not only those who prophesied but also those who cast out demons in the Lord’s name, while by their impure life testifying themselves to be Satan’s slaves. From these things, therefore, the Spirit of adoption and regeneration is falsely and ineptly inferred.

Fourth, Andreae says, the Spirit of the Lord—that is, the Holy Spirit—is said to have withdrawn from Saul. Therefore he previously possessed him whom he afterward lost. He possessed, to be sure, that gift of the Spirit which he had, but not that sanctifying Spirit, that solid and enduring foundation of God which testifies to the spirit of the children of God, whose seal is this: “The Lord knows who are his,” and, “Let everyone named after Christ depart from unrighteousness.”

Fifth, Andreae, the champion of Saul’s adoption, says that Samuel does not tell Saul, “You have been rejected by God because God never loved you and you were destined by him for eternal destruction”; but rather, “Because you rejected the word, God has therefore rejected you from being king.” But when, finally, will this lust for slander cease? As though anyone perished whom God was unwilling to save when that person wished to be saved; and as though, rather, no one truly wished to be saved except the one to whom it was given that he should so will, and to whom the same grace was given that he might be saved. As though, because none perish except those whom God destined from eternity for just destruction, this eternal decree were therefore the cause of their destruction, and not rather their own sins—of which God is not even the cause, but only their corruption and, in adults, their perverse will. For although God no more admits of being deceived or changed in one direction than in the other, and consequently it is just as necessary that those destined for salvation be saved as that those devoted to destruction perish, nevertheless the cause of the destruction of the latter does not flow from God as does the cause of the salvation of the former. For God changes, moves, and governs the former, whom he has effectually called by his Spirit, until he glorifies them in Christ, the beginning, middle, and end of the election, calling, and glorification of the elect. But abandoning the latter in their native corruption, he hands them over, enslaved to their own lusts and to Satan, exercising his various judgments upon them, while he himself is the author of no evil—

[Part II | PDF page 80 | logical source scan 79 | printed page 78]

—conceived or perpetrated by them, is debtor to no one, and never sentences them to eternal destruction unless they have deserved eternal death. It does not follow, however, that whatever must happen comes to pass from necessary causes, as Augustine shows by treating this very matter rightly and learnedly in book 5 of *The City of God*. To what purpose, then, are those slanders? Even if we were to grant—which God forbid—that from that hidden decree and efficacy of God flow the causes of destruction in those who perish, just as the causes of salvation flow in those who are saved, how inept and ridiculous would this conclusion nevertheless be: because Saul perished on account of rejecting God’s Word, he therefore does not appear to have perished by that hidden decree of God? For subordinate causes do not remove a higher cause, but are rather ordered by it; and what prevents that which was hidden at first from being said afterward to have been disclosed by the outcome itself? But if Andreae is ignorant of these matters, as of very many others which ought to have been most familiar to him—or pretends to be ignorant and does not permit others to know them—is Beza therefore a heretic? So much, then, concerning Saul.

In what sense the Galatians are said to have fallen away from faith.

Moreover, in defense of his dogma Andreae also brings forward that statement of the Apostle in Galatians 5: “You who are justified in the Law have fallen away from grace.” But why not also those statements from 1 Timothy 2:19, “Some have made shipwreck of faith”; from chapter 4:1 of the same epistle, “Some will depart from the faith”; and from 2 Timothy 2:18, “They overthrow the faith of some”? This too is by an inept inference. For even if I were to concede that the subject here is that very faith proper to the elect, which once given is therefore never entirely taken away, is there no difference between a shipwrecked man and one who has drowned? Does whoever has fallen from sound health through even the gravest illness possess no seed of life? And is there no difference between a dying man and a dead one? What follows shows that Paul thought no differently about those Galatians. And from the number of those whose faith Philetus and Hymenaeus had overthrown, the same Apostle immediately excepts the gold and silver vessels of the Lord’s house—that is, the elect, who sometimes fall for a time in such a way that they nevertheless never fall away entirely, because their foundation stands firm.

In what sense Paul is said to have labored lest he should become reprobate.

“But,” Andreae says, “the same Apostle beats his body, lest, after preaching to others, he himself should become reprobate” (1 Corinthians 9:27). Therefore even the most elect can become reprobates and consequently fall away entirely. On the contrary: just as, insofar as he was a man not yet perfectly regenerated, he could fall away and therefore resorted to the remedy, so insofar as he was elect and regenerate, he could never fall away, because he embraced the remedy of perseverance. For just as with the immutable decree of election—

[Part II | PDF page 81 | logical source scan 80 | printed page 79]

—there necessarily coheres the enduring gift of perseverance, though not always with the same measure of constancy, so God, never permitting his own to be tempted beyond what they can bear, always supplies at the fitting time those intermediate causes upon which perseverance depends. Thus also must the saying, “Let him who stands take heed lest he fall,” be understood: the elect never fall away entirely precisely because it is given to them to take heed that they do not fall away entirely, even after grave lapses.

These, then, are Andreae’s invincible arguments. It remains for me to clear away the signal slander with which he and his secretaries assailed me. On p. 463, after he had asked me whether David, while committing adultery, lost faith and the Holy Spirit, his secretaries wrote that I answered, “By no means did he lose them, but retained them.” And again on p. 464 he recorded that I answered that David retained faith and the Holy Spirit in the commission of adultery. Hence Andreae himself, bursting forth on p. 465 into that most ardent zeal of his, says: “I would not accept a thousand florins—indeed, not even the whole world”—an utterance suited not so much to a faithful teacher as, in Nazianzen’s phrase, to a Christ-monger and Christ-peddler (χριστεμπόρῳ & χριστοκαπήλῳ)—“to set such manifestly impious things before my hearers: namely, that those who commit sins against conscience—fornicators and adulterers, if they are elect—retain faith and the Holy Spirit in the very act of sinning.” As though any sin were dictated by the Holy Spirit and faith, much less as though David had committed those horrendous crimes with the Holy Spirit conscious and consenting! Far be it that even Satan himself should dare say this, much less that it should ever have occurred to me to think any such thing, by God’s grace neither impious nor profane though I am a most miserable sinner, or that some little word should have slipped from me from which it could deservedly be inferred.

I did not therefore say—God is my witness—that David retained faith and the Holy Spirit while committing adultery or in the commission of adultery. Rather, I said that although he was an adulterer and even a murderer—which were works of the flesh, not of the Spirit—nevertheless, because he was elect, something of the Holy Spirit and faith was preserved in him; after it had slumbered for some time, the Lord at last awakened it again. These things, I say, although perhaps I did not then answer in so many words, I nevertheless answered in this sense.

The comparison drawn from the insane and the drunk is appropriate.

It remains finally to add what Andreae regarded as a certain crowning point. Andreae thought that he most suitably evaded, in these words, the comparison drawn from lethargic, insane, and drunken persons, who lose the use of the mind but not the mind itself. On p. 4 he says: “The case is far different with God’s grace, faith, and the Holy Spirit”—reader, understand this of grace, faith, and the efficacy of the Holy Spirit proper to the elect alone—“which are not substantial parts of a human being, but gifts of God. Just as they are given by God, so they can also be lost; and Holy Scripture testifies that they are in fact lost—

[Part II | PDF page 82 | logical source scan 81 | printed page 80 | clean unit boundary; stops before fresh necessary-arguments heading]

—when the psalmist prays, ‘Do not cast me away from your face, and do not take your Holy Spirit from me,’ and, ‘Restore to me the joy of your salvation.’” These are his words. I then declined to answer them so as to spare him shame; but I answer now, since he was not ashamed to publish such inept things. To begin with the last points: what could have been cited less appropriately for this dogma than statements by which it is most manifestly destroyed? In the midst of temptation, the psalmist prays that he not be cast away from God’s face. Therefore, although an adulterer and murderer, he had nevertheless not been cast away entirely. For who taught him to pray except that Comforter who had not yet entirely departed from him, and faith, the remnants of which he had retained by God’s grace? He prays that the Holy Spirit not be taken from him. Therefore he had not been entirely stripped of him. But the joy of salvation which he had lost is different from salvation itself, from which he had not entirely fallen away.

Next, how firm is this inference? God gave those things; therefore he can take them away. For God indeed, if his power is considered absolutely, can as surely destroy whatever he made as he was able to make it. Yet if one considers the exercise and effect of that power, since he gave those things under the law that he would never take them away, he certainly cannot take them away—not from a defect of power, but because he cannot lie; and this inability is the mark of supreme power. And how ridiculous is this conclusion too? God can take them away; therefore he actually does. For the antecedent is utterly false, as I have just said, and the consequent ridiculous, unless he proves that nothing was made or given by God which he wills to be everlasting. But Christ speaks very differently about Mary and about this very matter with which we are now dealing: “Mary,” he says, “has chosen the best part, which will not be taken from her”; and elsewhere, speaking of all the elect in the person of Peter, “I have prayed for you,” he says, “that your faith may not fail.”

Finally, as regards the foundation of this argument, although by nature the gifts of God, which are created qualities, differ from the soul, which is a substantial part of a human being and indeed his form, nevertheless in the matter now before us they can and even ought to be compared with one another most fittingly. For just as the soul is the form of the natural man (ψυχικοῦ), so the Holy Spirit, through faith—insofar as faith is a quality effected by him and that through which Christ with his benefits is apprehended and imparted to the believer—informs the spiritual man. Hence that distinction between the natural and the spiritual (ψυχικοῦ & πνευματικοῦ) which is familiar to Paul. What prevents, therefore, the use of the soul’s faculties from being interrupted for a time while the soul itself nevertheless remains, and likewise the spiritual motions and activities of the Holy Spirit and faith from ceasing while neither the power (δυνάμει) of the Spirit itself nor faith is removed? So far are the Holy Spirit and faith in the elect—to whom they are proper and peculiar—from being something less stable in the spiritual man than the soul and its faculties are in the natural man, that, on the contrary, the natural man (ψυχικὸς) is dissolved by death, while through this very death the spiritual man (πνευματικὸς) draws nearer to his goal and everlasting consummation.

Necessary arguments from which it is gathered that the elect never entirely lose the Holy Spirit, nor fall wholly away from true faith.

Just as we have shown that those arguments by which Andreae sought to establish that the elect can sometimes fall entirely from God’s grace have no weight, so we must confirm the contrary doctrine by the most certain arguments and explain the use of this truth. First, therefore, I lay down this point: the faith with which we are dealing is that certain mark by which those who will be saved are distinguished from those who will be condemned. Whoever denies this contradicts the whole of Holy Scripture, so that theologians should not dispute with him. But if anyone can fall away from this faith, then, besides the fact that no one will be certain of his salvation and will therefore call upon God not from faith but from uncertain conjecture—and will obtain nothing, James 1:6—this uncertainty will arise either from God or from a human being wavering between firm persuasion and doubt. It cannot arise from God, whose decrees cannot be uncertain and wavering, since he is himself immutable. But if this stability depends on a human being, its support is overturned and the buttress of salvation will be placed in the human being himself, than whom nothing is more changeable if left to himself.

Nor can it proceed from both without this consequence: if the outcome of the divine decree concerning our salvation depends upon a condition which lies within human power—say, whether he wills to believe and persevere in faith—then human salvation will be said to lie in the person’s own hand and power, and not wholly in God’s grace; and the will of God, upon which nevertheless everything that exists depends, will be suspended from the will of man. The Apostle would then cry out in vain that neither election nor salvation belongs to him who wills or runs, but to God who shows mercy and works in us, both “to believe” and “to will and to do”—and this wholly, not in part, as Augustine rightly acknowledged in the *Retractations*. Bernard too most aptly insists that in ourselves we are nothing except what deserves destruction, but in God’s grace and through Christ’s mercy we truly are something.

Since all these points are most firmly established, it follows both that the decree concerning the preservation of the elect in Christ is immovable and that the intermediate causes through which God executes this purpose of his are immovable. The first is effectual calling, which does not come from us in any way, but from God, who, as Luke says, opens the heart and pierces the ears of those predestined to life. Its effect is that unique and most noble instrument—namely, full assurance (πληροφορία) and faith—by which we apprehend Christ, so that we may be justified in him when he is freely imputed and sanctified when he works in us. Next comes the gift of perseverance, though often interrupted through our fault, which lays hold of the crown. All these intermediate causes, I say, I affirm to be from God’s grace alone and to be as certain in the elect in him as his immutability is firm and certain—

[Part II | PDF page 84 | logical source scan 83 | printed page 82]

—And lest I seem to rest only upon reasons rather than upon the express words of Scripture, does not that indissoluble chain of the causes of our salvation described by Paul in Romans 8:30 and following manifestly and necessarily demonstrate this stability of the salvation of the elect? And if anything in them is uncertain and something from which the elect can fall away entirely, whence come those statements of the same Apostle immediately added in the same passage: “Who will bring charges against God’s elect?”; “Who will separate us from Christ’s love?”; and, “I am persuaded that no created thing can separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord”? And how do we have peace, having been justified by faith, unless a certain gift of perseverance coheres with faith? And how does hope not put us to shame because God’s love—that is, the love with which we are loved—has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us, if we can fall away entirely from him? And how will that conclusion of the same Apostle stand, “If children, then heirs,” if we can be disinherited, and if that most certain testimony by which the Spirit assures our spirit that we are God’s children is not firm?

And was David’s exclamation, “In you, Lord, I have hoped; let me not be confounded forever,” poured forth rashly by him? And when Christ speaks of the efficacy of the spirit of error in Matthew 24:24, was this condition rashly added—that, if it were possible, they might deceive even the elect? Was Mary, Martha’s sister, the only person from whom that good part which she had chosen could not be taken? And did Christ pray only for Peter that his faith should not fail? On the contrary, he prayed for all his sheep and was heard concerning them. Speaking of them he says, “My sheep hear my voice”; “I know them, and they follow me”; and, “I give them eternal life, and they will not perish forever, nor will anyone snatch them from my hand or from the Father’s hand,” John 10:27; and John 6:37, “Whatever the Father gives me will come to me, and the one who comes to me I will by no means cast out.”

How, moreover, is the Church founded upon an immovable rock, and how are those who trust in Jehovah like Mount Zion, which remains unmoved forever, if even the least citizen of the Church—that is, one truly elect and in whom election has been made sure through faith—can fall away from that rock? And although that agreement of God made with Abraham cannot fail, as the Apostle testifies, despite such great and long-continuing, untamed apostasy and even the dreadful cutting off of the Israelite nation, but hope is still laid up for Israel, can such a thing happen in some individual elect believer? And how will this statement of 1 John 2 be true: “They went out from us because they were not of us; if they had been of us, they would certainly have remained with us,” if one who was once truly made a member of Christ can fall away entirely from Christ? And how will that firm foundation stand—“God knows who are his”—if anyone falls entirely from that foundation, 2 Timothy 2:19? Indeed, not even those who, by the Church’s just judgment on earth—which is also ratified in heaven—are handed over to Satan for a time—

[Part II | PDF page 85 | logical source scan 84 | printed page 83]

—are necessarily regarded as excluded once and for all. They are handed over not that they may perish, but that, with the pride of their flesh thus subdued, their spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord, which will dawn for them at its appointed time through the gift of repentance.

The use of this doctrine in raising up stricken consciences.

Nothing, therefore, is more certain than this doctrine, nor is there another remedy in those most grievous temptations in which the very remembrance of God not only fails to console us but even reproves us. Repelling Satan alone by it when he reproaches us with either our negligence or our perfidy, we say with David, “Why are you cast down, my soul, and why do you roar within me? Hope in God, for I shall yet praise him.” “How can that be?” Satan and the troubled conscience will say. “For you have fallen from grace, which makes you a hundred times more guilty, and all that Spirit the Comforter once kindled within you has now, as you yourself perceive, entirely vanished.”

“No indeed,” the person placed in this temptation will answer. “By God’s singular mercy, I once apprehended with true and living faith the promise of my eternal and immovable election and salvation, offered to me when I remembered former days in the Word and sealed in me both in Baptism and in the Lord’s Supper, a promise resting upon God’s truth alone and his immovable purpose. Although I am a most miserable sinner, I neither lost it nor could lose it. Therefore, in this present disturbance, however grave, I look to his mercy and the promises of repentance, and even now detest my sins from the heart—which is one of the chief motions of the Holy Spirit—and return to him whom I offended. Nor will I cease to cry, ‘Have mercy on me, O God, according to your great mercy,’ until I am heard. For that firm foundation of God stands, bearing this seal: ‘The Lord knows who are his,’ and, ‘Let him who assumes Christ’s name depart from unrighteousness.’” To say everything briefly, God’s eternal purpose concerning the calling of the elect, their justification through the free remission in Christ’s blood of sins however grave, and their final glorification is impregnable even to the gates of hell, and God never repents of it.

Andreae’s corrupted assertion against the true exposition of Paul’s passage concerning God’s irrevocable gifts (ἀμεταμελήτοις).

Judge now, Christian reader, which doctrine supplies the firmer consolation in falls of this kind, and with what shame Andreae dared so often to insist that we refer sinners not to the Word, not to the sacraments, but to God’s hidden decree. What more? Not content with those things already refuted by us, he attempts to wrest from us even that sweetest saying of Paul pronounced concerning this very matter: “Those gifts and that calling of God are of such a kind—”

[Part II | PDF page 86 | logical source scan 85 | printed page 84]

—“that he cannot repent of them”; that is, they are irrevocable, just as the very priesthood of our eternal mediator for his elect in himself, according to the order of Melchizedek, is irrevocable (ἀμεταμέλητον). For the Father’s decree concerning the sending of his Son for the elect and concerning the election, calling, justification, and eventual glorification of those for whom he was to be sent cohere by a necessary bond. Otherwise the foundation of the elect’s faith and hope would limp—or rather collapse. But let us hear Andreae, certainly not the builder of God’s house, if this view prevailed, but its manifest destroyer. “This is not,” he says, “the meaning of Paul’s words which is falsely and perversely fastened upon them in this passage.” Christian reader, you hear thunder, but thunder which by God’s beneficence ends in smoky and sooty exhalations.

What, then, is this falsehood and perversity? “That God does not take away bestowed gifts from those upon whom he bestowed them.” As though, because the Apostle wrote about a definite kind of gifts—and we extend his statement no further—Andreae himself could, without falsehood and perversity, instead fasten upon us the claim that we expound it either universally or indefinitely. Far be it from us to become so insane as to dream that every gift bestowed upon human beings is perpetual, whether what we call physical or hyperphysical, bestowed upon either soul or body. Even that good gift of God which is indeed called “faith,” but equivocally (ὁμωνύμως), vanishes—as when one calls human seed or a forming embryo “a human being,” or even, as James speaks, equivocally calls a human corpse “a human being.”

What, then, are those certain gifts of which the Apostle speaks in that place? Namely, those by which God adopted Israel as peculiar to himself, all other peoples being entirely excluded from the covenant of salvation, as the same Apostle testifies in Acts 14:16 and Ephesians 2:12. Paul testifies that these benefits are so firm that not even the most obstinate unbelief of the Jews—by which that nation is even now cut off, while we, formerly profane and godless (ἀθέοις), have been substituted for a time in their place—can ever make that covenant with this nation void, as the event itself will at last disclose when the number of the elect from the nations has been completed. If Andreae denies that this is so—that the Apostle there instituted a disputation concerning this very matter—then let him also deny that the sun is risen at midday. When we apply this to individuals from every nation and in every age who are admitted by true faith to the covenant of eternal life, what are we doing except drawing what necessarily follows from that saying of Paul? For the election, calling, and salvation of the whole body of the Church is certainly no different from that of each of its true members, since there is one and the same God of all who are to be saved, one faith, hope, and salvation; nor is a definition of a species true if it does not agree with even one true individual comprehended in it. What, therefore, does this most slanderous accusation indicate except the very things of which Andreae accuses me—falsehood and perversity—in that kind of sophistry called a transition to another class (μετάβασις εἰς ἄλλο γένος)? What, then, according to Andreae’s opinion, does Paul mean in that passage? “He proclaims,” Andreae says, “God’s—”

[Part II | PDF page 87 | logical source scan 86 | printed page 85]

—“truth, that he cannot lie and cannot deny himself. This truth pertains to the pious and impious, to whom God offers his grace equally. Therefore, whether people do not admit it or, after admitting it, cast it away again, God himself nevertheless remains unrepentant in himself; and, as the prophet says, he stretches out his hands all day long to all sinners, and through his ambassadors entreats even us who have shamefully fallen to return into favor with God.” These are his words. Good God! In them how much not only falsehood but ignorance appears, and indeed what an obstinate zeal for defending manifest falsehood, concealed under the pretext of a true axiom! For it is most true that God cannot lie or deny himself and thus is always like himself. So far are we from denying this that, on the contrary, having established this very principle, we assert the immovable stability of election and true faith, however often its exercise is interrupted.

But besides the fact that Andreae, as we shall presently say, uses “grace” equivocally (ὁμωνύμως) in this argument, what is his statement, “or cast away again the grace once admitted,” except begging the question, as they say in the schools? And who has conceded to him that saving grace is divinely offered to all—that is, every individual—and equally? That God stretches out his hands to all, that is, every individual sinner? And that through his ambassadors God entreats every individual, even those who have fallen gravely, to return into favor with him? We shall treat these points one by one presently. Finally, how shamelessly did he transfer to God himself what the Apostle says about God’s very calling—which is nothing other than the disclosure of that divine decree! And what ignorance of the Greek language is it to call God himself ἀμεταμέλητον, that is, one who, as the prophet says, never repents of his own judgment? For since this Greek word can only be taken passively, it will not mean that God never repents of himself, but that no one ever repents of God. What statement could be more inept and less suited to this passage? But let us return again to the matter itself.

The grace from which the elect cannot fall entirely. And not even every individual is outwardly called to salvation, much less are the inward gifts conferred upon every individual.

In the question whether the elect can wholly, even for a time, lose the saving grace of God itself once conferred upon them, we must not stop at those benefits which are offered to believers in the Word and sacraments, since these very things are what consciences doubt in those most grievous temptations. Such are those two general and principal heads: the remission of sins through Christ’s righteousness freely imputed to believers, and the effectual creation of the new man, begun both in the intellect and in the will. Rather, we must gradually ascend to that first grace—not directly, but beginning from its effects—

[Part II | PDF page 88 | logical source scan 87 | printed page 86]

—which David signifies by the piercing of the ear in Psalm 40; Christ often by ears for hearing and, in John 6, by the term “drawing”; Luke in Acts 16:14 by opening the heart; and in Acts 26:18 by opening the eyes. With what shame, then, does Andreae dare say that this first grace—by which a person is disposed through the inward operation of the Holy Spirit both to understand the voice of the gospel and to believe and apply to himself the gospel which he believes—is divinely offered to all, indeed equally to every individual? For not even the outward call is offered to all peoples, much less to every individual. Since the world was created and corrupted, how many nations—to say nothing of individuals—have existed, exist, and will exist, in whose ears not even the name of Christ or the gospel ever sounded or will sound! “God is known in Judea,” David says; and, “He has not dealt thus with any nation.” And how often is the covenant peculiar to this nation proclaimed by Moses and the prophets? And in Luke Paul says in Acts 14:16, “In past ages God permitted the nations to walk in their own ways”; and in Ephesians 2:12, “You were strangers to the promises, having no hope, and godless (ἄθεοι) in the world.” How false, then, how absurd, and how ridiculous is what Andreae dares to assert so shamelessly!

What more? Not even with the outward calling is an inward calling necessarily and always divinely joined—not only as regards its efficacy, but not even as regards God himself employing the outward voice through the ministry of his servants. I trust that in the disputation on predestination I shall prove this to persons who are not contentious by the clearest testimonies of God’s Word, firm reasons, and experience, which is the teacher even of fools. I say only this now: just as it is most true, as Andreae says, that saving grace is offered even to sinners who have fallen most grievously—for who would be saved if the matter were not so?—so the daily experience of all ages teaches that the implication he adds is most false: namely, that the grace of repentance to be received by faith is offered to all, that is, every sinner and every person who has fallen grievously, much less that it is effectually conferred upon every individual.

Nor can God rightly be blamed on this account, whether this grace is not offered at all—that is, neither outwardly nor inwardly—or whether it is offered outwardly but not inwardly. For he is debtor to no one, and all human beings without exception deserve not merely never to hear God’s voice but also, when it pleases God thus to exercise his just judgments, to hear outwardly that through which, by despising the grace they hear with their ears alone and hardening themselves, they may more and more fill up their measure. Hence this also follows: God does not mock when he offers his grace outwardly but not inwardly to those whom he is neither bound nor willing to heal. For he then exercises his just judgments through his Word no less seriously than when through that same Word he exercises his grace—

[Part II | PDF page 89 | logical source scan 88 | printed page 87]

—in those whom he deigns to save. Consequently, it is not astonishing that God does not even outwardly call all—that is, every individual—human beings, nor indeed every nation or family, and that he does not inwardly call all whom he calls outwardly. What is astonishing, rather, is his admirable and ineffable goodness, in that he deigns to show mercy to some by effectually calling them outwardly and inwardly. Andreae cites a passage from Isaiah which manifestly pertains only to Israel; to twist it not only to all nations but even to every individual human being is the height of shamelessness. And as for what Paul says about the ministry of the gospel appointed also for the nations after the dividing wall had been overthrown, no one devoid of common sense will suppose that it must be taken universally, but rather indefinitely.

Even boys with a moderate education in logic know the difference between a universal and an indefinite proposition: namely, a universal proposition is false if even one thing is excepted from its universality. For example, if even one mere human being (ψιλὸς) can exist who is not born a child of wrath, then the proposition “All human beings are born children of God” will be false. But for the truth of an indefinite proposition—as when one says, “The kingdom of heaven belongs also to infants”—it is enough that even a single one or some be found of whom it is truly said. For an affirmative or negative indefinite proposition is equivalent to a particular proposition.

It is not enough for the stricken consciences of elect persons who have gone astray to be ordered to hope well concerning their return.

It is worthwhile to resolve another difficulty here. The matter seems to come to the same thing whether we say—as is the position I defend—that in the elect who have already been endowed with true faith, the Holy Spirit, faith, and the fruits of faith are never entirely shaken out, but are merely lulled to sleep for a time as regards sensation and efficacy; or whether, with others—among whom Andreae is nevertheless not numbered, since he holds that even those elected to salvation can perish—it is taught that faith itself and the Holy Spirit are indeed entirely shaken out by sins committed against conscience, but are afterward restored to sinners through the gift of repentance. Yet there is a very great difference between these two positions unless this is added: that their repentance is as certain with God as election is certain and immutable. I do not, however, find this expressly stated by the authors and defenders of that opinion.

Moreover, even if it is expressly stated, I would have everyone consider how much more suitable in such temptations is this consolation: to teach that the Holy Spirit never entirely departed, nor could depart, from one who once possessed the sense of true faith; and consequently, with the full assurance (πληροφορίᾳ) of faith never entirely extinguished, that he must seek pardon from God which will by no means be denied him, according to the Apostle’s statement not only—

[Part II | PDF page 90 | logical source scan 89 | printed page 88]

—that God’s effectual calling is wholly irrevocable (ἀμεταμέλητον), Romans 11:29, but also that the salvation which sorrow according to God effects in sinners is wholly irrevocable, 2 Corinthians 7:10. Nor does the Apostle say in Romans 8:35—although this too is true—merely that nothing can prevent sinners from being reconciled to God through repentance, except for that one truly horrendous sin which, since it is never remitted, never befalls the elect. Rather, he says that “nothing can separate us from the love with which God loved his own, since he will never cast them out.” Let the Christian reader consider, I say, how much more certain from God’s Word, upon which alone we must rely, and how much more efficacious for reviving a mind stricken by the enormity of its sins, is this consolation than if someone tells the miserable person: “You did in fact once fall away from all faith and from the Holy Spirit, but hope is now offered you for an entirely new adoption and reconciliation.”

For this cannot come into his mind without his at the same time concluding either that God, who entirely deserted one whom he had once elected, must have been changeable, or that the outcome of election depends upon the human being’s own will and consequently rests once again upon a most uncertain and plainly wavering foundation. Finally, if God’s work in sins of this kind—which I do not know whether even one elect person can entirely escape—is not interrupted but wholly broken off, what else will the life of a Christian, however holy, be than a perpetual alternation between abolition and repetition of this covenant? And here we must take great care lest, if we maintain that someone has fallen away entirely, we open an entrance for the necessity of Anabaptism. For one who has once been cast outside needs a new entrance. Nor can the seal of baptism be firmer than the thing of which it is the seal.

Nor will the Anabaptists be satisfied by the comparison Chemnitz uses in his *Examination* of Canon 10 of the Council of Trent on baptism: “Just as someone can fall out of a ship while the ship remains intact, so many rashly leap from the ark of baptism into the sea of destruction and perish, but the ship itself remains intact. And if by God’s grace the one who fell out can return to the ship, he will be carried to the harbor of salvation not by some other new plank, but by the solid ship itself from which he fell.” The Anabaptist would answer that the matter here does not concern baptism considered in itself—which is always baptism with the promise of grace attached—but baptism in relation to the person baptized. Nor does it concern the Church itself, which is always the Church and which in this comparison would more rightly be signified by the ark than baptism would, but rather a return into that ark. For if someone entered that ark through baptism and then fell entirely out of it through sin, how is he restored by this very thing from which he fell, without some other new plank, to the place from which he fell? It is just as if you were to say that someone cast out of a house is restored to that house by the same introduction from which he fell away. This is altogether incoherent (ἀσύστατον), since one who has been entirely cast out requires another, new introduction. From this certainly arose that papal—

[Part II | PDF page 91 | logical source scan 90 | printed page 89 | clean argument boundary; stops before fresh universal-baptism heading]

—doctrine concerning the remission of sins committed after baptism not through baptism but through a new sacrament of repentance, restoration to one’s former state, and, as it were, swimming into harbor by a second plank. We rightly reject this doctrine, although sins are remitted only to those who repent. But if someone has for a time been deprived not of citizenship itself, but only of a citizen’s right, and is not so cast out of the house that he does not keep at least part of one foot on its threshold, he will need neither a new gift of citizenship nor an entirely new introduction into that house, but true reconciliation with the prince of the city and the father of the household.

Peoples and families can fall entirely away from faith, but not the elect from those peoples or families.

The passage which Andreae brings from Jeremiah in defense of his doctrine, concerning a wife repudiated for adultery and taken back again by her former husband even after another marriage, wonderfully commends God’s infinite clemency, but contributes nothing to the proposed question. For we are asking about individual persons. There, as often elsewhere, the subject is the nation itself. Without any change in the stability of the election of individuals, once the number of the elect whom he had determined to choose from it has been completed, God can repudiate that nation as unworthy of his covenant, either never to restore it or to restore it only after some period which he has determined. We see that this happened formerly to the ten tribes, and now to the whole Jewish nation—indeed, even to the largest part of the world—while the faith and salvation of the individual elect nevertheless remained firm. Let this suffice, then, concerning this most weighty question—that is, the steadfast foundation (ἑδραίῳ), as the Apostle speaks, of election and faith, placed upon the solid rock.

It is false that ALL who are rightly baptized obtain the grace of baptism.

Now at last I come to the question which Andreae has pursued with the greatest contention. In it I would have the Christian reader observe that the matter here is neither paedobaptism, which both sides affirm, nor whether baptism has annexed to it the promise of saving grace in Christ, always, so far as God is concerned, joined with the outward sign and rite. Rather, the controversy between Andreae and me is this: he loudly affirms that faith and the grace of baptism are necessarily conferred upon every rightly baptized infant without any exception. I, however, assert that this cannot be concluded from any testimonies of Holy Scripture or any reasons deduced from theological principles, and indeed contend that the contrary must be established by sure arguments. First, then,

[Part II | PDF page 92 | logical source scan 91 | printed page 90]

come, let us examine his arguments.

“First,” he says on p. 479, “it is horrendous to say what you assert: that many thousands of baptized infants are never regenerated but perish eternally.” I do not know whether I spoke in precisely those words, nor am I bound rashly to trust those secretaries without discrimination (ἀδιακρίτως). But grant that I did indeed speak so. The matter itself nevertheless cries out that my purpose was not to count either the many or the few for whom, through their own fault, God’s Word is an aroma of death unto death, and to whose destruction the profaned sacraments contribute. I meant only that it is so far from being true that, as Andreae teaches, everyone baptized receives the grace of baptism, that on the contrary very many who were baptized either in infancy or as adults—as is evident from their wicked lives and impenitence—perish outside Christ, never having been regenerated. Andreae, seeking to cover this with another error, teaches that all such persons fall from the faith and regeneration with which they had been endowed in baptism. This is not only a begging of the question, as they say in the schools, but is also refuted by those very firm arguments by which I trust I demonstrated shortly before that true faith—which alone truly and actually apprehends Christ both in the Word and in the sacraments—is God’s gift peculiar to his elect and therefore irrevocable (ἀμεταμέλητον), because of that indissoluble chain of the causes of the elect’s salvation which the Apostle sets forth in Romans 8:29–30. Otherwise the elect’s most firm full assurance (πληροφορία), which is established there, would be unstable. It is indeed horrendous that so many perish both outside the Church and in the very bosom of the Church; but is it therefore false? The entire blame for this most sorrowful outcome rests with human beings, not with God, who is debtor to none. Experience in every age teaches that we should admire rather his kindness in choosing and preserving some, even a few—as he himself foretells by calling them his little flock—than his just severity (ἀποτομίαν), as the Apostle speaks, in destroying the rest. Some of these hold down in unrighteousness the truth divinely engraved upon every mind; some scorn as ridiculous the gospel offered to them; some persecute against conscience what they once tasted; and some, finally, hear it to their own stumbling.

The same man says on p. 480: “You separate the Spirit from the water in the external baptism which you invent, when you teach that judgment concerning the grace of adoption and the regeneration of infants must at last be made from the motions of the Holy Spirit which they feel if they grow up and from whether they display signs of regeneration.” Both charges are slanderous, and the latter is also false. Is the Spirit of life separated from God’s Word because not every good seed falls into good soil? Is that bread which we break therefore not in itself a communion (κοινωνία) of the Lord’s body because those who receive it unworthily do not become members of Christ? Is light, finally, separated from the sun because it does not shine for the blind? We have said in its place how both the whole Word and entire sacraments are offered to all who approach, yet neither the whole of the Word nor the whole of the sacraments is received by all—

[Part II | PDF page 93 | logical source scan 92 | printed page 91]

The sacrament always remains entire in itself, though it is not received entire by the unworthy, as Augustine teaches most rightly in a few words against the Donatists: “When the integrity of the sacrament is in question, it does not matter what a person believes or what kind of faith the recipient of the sacrament possesses. But it matters greatly for the way of salvation, though not for the integrity of the sacrament. For someone can possess the entire sacrament and a perverse faith.” Therefore, when baptism is considered in itself, as it is and always remains, the things God joined are not separated, although the sign alone is received by the unworthy—and indeed received unto death, not as a sign. Its abuse does not change the thing itself. As for that certain judgment about infants or adults, far be it from us to claim what Andreae says we claim. That judgment, as we shall soon say, belongs to God alone, unless he has specially revealed it concerning certain persons. And when Christ admonishes us to judge the tree by its fruits, the inference is only likely and probable, not one of absolutely necessary conclusion. For trees may appear good from their apparent fruits, though the outcome later shows them to be rotten, as is clear in Saul, Judas the traitor, and innumerable other hypocrites. Good trees, too, have their winters, in which not even a green leaf appears for a time, much less blossom or fruit; and they do not lack their own diseases, as is evident from the falls of David and other men of the greatest holiness. Moreover, how could we defer all infants until adolescence and those inward motions of the Holy Spirit, when so many die in infancy itself? These charges are therefore too foolish to deserve any refutation.

The same man declaims on p. 485: “What certainty of salvation or consolation can there be in baptism when people are taught that no infants are renewed in baptism?” The word RENEWED has been slanderously seized from my antithesis to the seventh dogma condemned by Andreae, as though I meant it concerning those infants who die in infancy; how and to what extent renewal may be attributed to them I shall explain in its place. But concerning the others, namely those who grow up, I said and still say that renewal must not be restricted to the moment of baptism conferred either in infancy or adolescence, but begins only at the time when through hearing they apprehend Christ by actual true faith. Such faith can neither be attributed to infants, as it seems to me, nor is it necessarily joined to the time when baptism is administered; I shall soon demonstrate both points, I hope, with suitable reasons. Yet because we cannot assert as certain and without an attached condition God’s secret judgments concerning each and every other person, it does not therefore follow that no ground of joy and consolation remains for the pious parents of baptized infants or for baptized—

[Part II | PDF page 94 | logical source scan 93 | printed page 92]

—adults themselves. It is one thing so to doubt a matter that you are completely uncertain whether it is true or false; it is another to apply to someone, with the addition of an unknown condition, what is true in itself and absolutely—such as the salvation of the elect—when you cannot know him so far as to know certainly whether he is elect or whether he believes, and yet may presume without rashness that he is elect or even that he believes. Thus we command parents of infants—as far as it is permissible, leaving God his secret judgments, to judge concerning their children, even if perhaps they died before baptism and much more if they were baptized—to presume that their children have been received into God’s favor, both as born in the Church and as baptized. We add the exhortation that they so instruct them by holy education that, if they grow up, they may understand the mystery of their baptism and ratify it more and more by true faith. Adults who also confess their faith with their mouths we regard as truly faithful insofar as charity commands, although at their own peril if perhaps they are hypocrites; and we exhort them to labor to become increasingly certain of their salvation in Christ—inwardly by the testimony of the Holy Spirit, outwardly by the external calling divinely attested to them in both Word and sacraments—and to confirm it in themselves by every holy good work. If Andreae wishes to proceed further, let him see how he will actually perform what he dares to promise so certainly to himself and others. It seems altogether incoherent (ἀσύστατον) to me to deny that faith itself is irrevocable (ἀμεταμέλητον), yet to insist that things be certain and indubitable which, even if I grant them to Andreae, nevertheless have, by his own testimony, no certain outcome and must have at least this condition added to them—and that uncertainly: “If they persevere in faith.”

But, says the same man on p. 480, we must not opine, but know certainly and without doubt, and in confidence of the baptism received believe that we were regenerated in baptism. This is not expressed with complete accuracy either, since in adults approaching baptism the faith which certainly apprehends Christ and all his benefits ought to precede baptism as its sign and seal (σημεῖον & σφραγῖδα), just as, conversely, baptism precedes faith in infants, as we shall say in its place. Yet I concede this as most truly said: only that is true faith by which individuals apply to themselves the promise of salvation in Christ with the greatest possible certainty, both in the simple Word and in the sacraments, “doubting nothing” (μηδὲν διακρινόμενοι), as Paul and James teach. For this is the gift which throughout the entire course of our life we ought especially to preserve and establish within us through good works. What, then, does that censor of ours wish to infer from this? “You do wrong, therefore,” he says, “because you leave pious parents in perpetual doubt whether their children have been adopted as God’s sons through the baptism they received.” No—Andreae is a very poor logician, connecting such unlike things so ineptly. For, says the Apostle in 1 Corinthians 2:11, who among human beings knows the things of a person,

[Part II | PDF page 95 | logical source scan 94 | printed page 93]

except the spirit of the person which is in him? And who knows the things of God except the Spirit of God? Thus individual believers can indeed possess full assurance (πληροφορίαν) of their salvation from the Holy Spirit bearing witness to their spirit, as the same Apostle says elsewhere, namely by crying “Abba, Father” in their hearts; and, as John says, “By this we know that he dwells in us: from the Spirit whom he gave us.” I also acknowledge both that trees can be known to some degree from their fruits and that charity commands us not to despair even of brethren who sin, but rather to pray for their repentance—much less to refuse to acknowledge as truly Christian brethren those whose outward life is upright and honorable. But when Andreae infers from this that we can and even ought to determine certainly and indubitably, whether privately or before others, the faith and regeneration of other people—indeed, as he says, to pronounce judgment—let him enjoy his own rashness.

God never mocks human beings, even if the grace of the gospel is indiscriminately offered both in the simple Word and in the sacraments, though he has not determined to confer it upon all.

On p. 488 the same man, wishing to conclude from an absurdity that the grace of baptism is not only offered but also conferred upon ALL—that is, every individual—including reprobates, that is, those justly to be reprobated in their time, says: “For why, or how, is it offered to reprobates when God does not will it to be received by them?” This is the objection from which ignorant people conclude that we posit a God who mocks human beings unless we acknowledge that he determined to save, or at least desires to have saved, everyone to whom the salvation of the gospel is offered either in the simple Word or in the sacraments—to omit other no less false objections arising from this which we shall discuss in the treatise on predestination. Now, however, to answer this, I say first of all that calling extends more widely than the decree of election, as experience itself teaches and Christ testifies in express words when he says that many are called but few elected. It follows from this that two kinds of calling must be established: one which obtains its effect, and another which is ineffectual. Second, I presuppose that effectual calling rests upon the decree of election, as is perfectly clear from very many passages of Scripture, especially that notable passage of Paul in Romans 8:30 where he demonstrates the whole sequence of the causes of our salvation. Third, this efficacy—that the promise of the gospel is received by some, namely the elect—proceeds not from nature, not from any foreseeing of faith or works, but from the mere good pleasure (εὐδοκίᾳ) of the merciful God peculiar to the elect; I suppose Andreae needs no Scripture testimonies adduced to establish this. Fourth, the gospel promise itself, explained by the preaching of the Word and ratified by the addition of the sacraments, must be distinguished—

[Part II | PDF page 96 | logical source scan 95 | printed page 94]

—from that grace by which hearers’ hearts are prepared to believe and to receive that promise by faith, or are opened, as Luke speaks in Acts 16:14, according to the decree of eternal predestination, Acts 13:48. Once these points are established, it necessarily follows that those greatly err who say it is God’s purpose—although God’s will and his purpose cannot fight each other—to save all those to whom he wills the gospel to be preached and the sacraments administered; and much more do those err who, with Andreae, wish it to be believed as a most certain fact that the grace of baptism is not only offered but actually conferred upon every rightly baptized person. The fear of those who dream that, unless this is conceded to them, many false things unworthy of God are attributed to him is perverse. For first, some say, otherwise God will appear to delight maliciously in evil (ἐπίχαιρέκακος), in the destruction of the greatest part of humanity. As though judgment were not as fitting to God as mercy. His judgment consists in this: he condemns to death none but those who have justly merited death because of sin. To whom is he debtor for mercy? Indeed, others say, then he himself will have been the author of the destruction of those whom he destroys. Tell me why? Because, they say, they perish by God’s decree. I deny it. For God destroys in their time those whom he decreed from eternity to destroy in such a way that the outcome of this decree does not proceed from God but from the sins and fruits of sin of which all whom he condemns are guilty. Thus, although they perish according to God’s decree—that is, as God had determined—the efficient cause why they merit destruction is not God or his decree but the guilt of those who perish. It takes its beginning not from creation, not even in Adam in whom all sinned, but from corruption; and God was neither the author nor adviser of that corruption, but the human will spontaneously assenting to Satan and defecting from God. God could indeed have prevented this defection, but did not will to, as he is debtor to none, and did so justly, because he is God, willing and unwilling nothing unjustly. Adam, however, could and ought to have prevented it, since lust and the slavery of sin had not yet entered the world, and he was not ignorant of the divine command or of the threat annexed to that command. Nor does the infallible outcome of the divine counsel oppose these things, because divine ordination does not remove the nature of contingent causes, as will be explained in its place. Finally they object again: Why, then, does God offer what he does not will to confer? And what is this but mocking human beings, professing one thing with his mouth while having another in his mind? I answer that God discloses his counsels to human beings through human ministry only insofar as it pleases him and as he knows expedient. Hence it happens that through people sent by him, whose office is to remain within the limits of their calling, he often wills things to be announced as future which nevertheless he does not will to occur—

[Part II | PDF page 97 | logical source scan 96 | printed page 95]

—and this not by malicious deception or any blameworthy pretense, which falls upon no good man, but because it is expedient, whether we consider his purpose of saving by mercy or of destroying justly, as is clear from almost innumerable testimonies. Thus he left in every human being, indeed in every adult, the truth—that is, the traces of the divine image which philosophers call right reason (ὀρθὸν λόγον)—as a perpetual herald speaking within, even to the utterly deaf. They are not saved by its voice, although it shows the way of salvation if they could fulfill that truth or live according to its precept; but, as the Apostle admonishes, they are thereby rendered without excuse (ἀναπολόγητοι). The elect, to be sure, seek and find salvation elsewhere, that is, in the blessed seed, through God’s peculiar grace, while the rest are condemned all the more justly. With the same counsel our Lord God renewed through Moses’ ministry on Mount Sinai that same law which had become nearly obsolete—not so that anyone might be justified by it or so that it might circumcise hearts, but killing his elect through the law in order to lead them to the life to be displayed in its time by the gospel. As for the rest, God did not himself render them stubborn—for God is not the author of stubbornness—but showed them more and more convicted and entirely without excuse (ἀναπολογήτους). So too God had a twofold purpose in giving the ceremonial law. With respect to the elect, it was so that in it, as in a mirror, they might contemplate both what they themselves deserved, insofar as it was their bond of death, as the Apostle speaks in Colossians 2:14, and the grace to be brought to them through him who was to pay for their sins upon the altar of the cross. But for the rest, whom the one who is debtor to none left by just divine judgment, it was as a second rock against which they might strike. So even today, through the ministers of the divine Word, who are commanded indiscriminately to sow the good seed, plant, and water, while the hope of the increase is left with him who alone gives it according to his will, the gospel is preached and the sacraments administered. Therefore this condition is always added to the flock—a condition which God alone fulfills and only in the elect—IF YOU REPENT, IF YOU BELIEVE; and to individual sheep as the case arises, especially in the administration of sacraments, which are given to individual persons, IF YOU REPENT, IF YOU BELIEVE. These are the general points. If, however, specific examples are required: Moses was sent to Pharaoh not only when the Lord foretold that he would not obey, but also so that he would be hardened by God’s just judgment—that is, seizing, as the ungodly and obstinate customarily do, the occasion supplied by God’s command to release his people, Pharaoh freely hardened himself against the warning of conscience and opened the way to that glorious judgment of God, wholly without excuse (ἀναπολόγητος). Isaiah likewise is commanded to blind the people to whom he was sent, as though he had been sent for this purpose—at least as concerns by far the greatest part of the people, as the outcome finally showed—not because God or the ministry of his servants—

[Part II | PDF page 98 | logical source scan 97 | printed page 96]

—was the cause of blindness, which proceeds from the untamed rebellion of the stubborn alone, but because they deserve to be blinded more and more through themselves; their evil mind and evil disposition become God’s chief instrument in destroying them. Nor is that passage of Isaiah repeated six times in the New Testament against the cutting off of the Jews without reason. Conversely, the Lord, solicitous to preserve his elect, willed Jonah to preach to the Ninevites what appeared to be their certain destruction within forty days. He employed this pronouncement as though it were certainly fixed, although it was merely a threat; had the Lord not announced it categorically, the Ninevites would have scorned it. Nor was the Lord’s intention different when through Isaiah he announced to Hezekiah, “Set your house in order, for you will die.” For who could believe without blasphemy that the Lord changes a counsel once adopted? Therefore that customary scholastic distinction between the will of the sign and the will of good pleasure, as they call them, is not empty; nor is the distinction between the necessity of the consequent and the necessity of the consequence, though Luther—otherwise judging the matter itself correctly—wrongly condemned it. Nor does God ever mock human beings: he always sincerely calls, justifies, and at last brings his elect to glorification; but he either grants the rest no gospel call at all, or merely sets the gospel before them so that, whether they altogether disdain to hear it, refuse what they understand despite themselves, or even vomit out what they tasted, he renders them all the more without excuse (ἀναπολογήτους).

In what sense infants are said to believe, and what the effects of that faith are in infants who die before adult age.

The chief argument which Andreae and some with him misuse rests upon this most specious double foundation. First, that no one can please God without faith, since it is the sole instrument for apprehending Christ both in the simple Word and in the sacraments. Second, that Scripture says the righteous person lives not by another’s faith but by his own. With this foundation laid, since Christ says the kingdom of heaven belongs to infants, and since it is agreed between us that infants are no less to be baptized than the infants of the Jews were formerly to be circumcised—while baptism without their faith would nevertheless be vain and empty—it necessarily follows, they say, that all infants, since the kingdom of heaven belongs to them and it is impossible to please God without faith, are endowed with faith either before baptism, for which no Word of God establishes anything for us, or rather in baptism itself, and are consequently regenerated, since true faith necessarily apprehends Christ. They say this is also manifestly clear from Christ’s express statement in Matthew 18:6 and Mark 9:41 that little children believe. If we object that faith comes from hearing, of which infants are not capable—

[Part II | PDF page 99 | logical source scan 98 | printed page 97]

—they reply that, if this refers to outward hearing, it pertains only to adults, while nothing prevents God from conferring faith also upon infants by inward inspiration—and indeed almost more easily than upon adults, in whom the opposition of fleshly intelligence and depraved reason is active. They say that God both can and wills to do this is clear partly from the aforementioned testimonies that the kingdom of heaven belongs to infants, and partly from the example of John the Baptist, who, according to the angel Gabriel, was filled with the Holy Spirit from his mother’s womb and leapt in her womb at the Virgin Mary’s greeting. Thus I think I have briefly and orderly comprised the things Andreae pressed concerning this matter in our colloquy no less confusedly than verbosely. To them I answer one by one as follows.

First, I say that, even if everything laid down to support this argument is freely conceded to them, they nevertheless do wrong to affirm concerning each baptized infant without exception what Christ spoke indefinitely concerning infants. This is no less inept than when all the Jews formerly boasted in the name of Abraham’s seed, which Paul teaches must be restricted to the elect alone in Romans 4 and 9 and Galatians 3. Certainly, just as the new covenant succeeded the old with respect to the same grace and faith, so baptism is the seal of the same righteousness from faith, just as circumcision formerly was. Thus it is no less false to conclude that every baptized infant is regenerated than formerly to conclude that every infant circumcised outwardly in the flesh was also circumcised inwardly by the Spirit.

Second, an argument taken from God’s power does not even require refutation unless his will and ordinance have been disclosed to us by his Word. John the Baptist’s example is rightly cited against the Anabaptists, not so that we may say that what was given to him by a peculiar benefit is attributed to any and every infant, much less every individual one, but so that it may be clear that God exercises his marvelous hidden power in those infants in whom it pleased him to do so, as conceived and born within his covenant. For apart from the fact that this occurrence is singular and no general conclusion can be drawn from it, as Andreae himself finally acknowledges on p. 494, if it were to be made an example, the sanctification of infants would have to begin not with baptism but with gestation in the womb itself. Finally, no one explained more rightly or elegantly how far and in what respect John the Baptist’s example holds than Augustine in Letter 57: “I neither despise what happened in John nor from it establish a rule for what must be held concerning infants. Indeed, I proclaim it wonderful in him precisely because I do not find it in others.”

The passage from Matthew and Mark which Andreae repeatedly presses is manifestly twisted to assert infant faith. For it concerns neither childhood as an age nor childhood faith, but rebukes the disciples’ foolish ambition by setting before them a child’s example, because that age is still full of simplicity and free from envy, ambition, pride, and other—

[Part II | PDF page 100 | logical source scan 99 | printed page 98]

—vices of this kind. Thus when Christ calls those who believe in him “little ones” (τῶν μικρῶν), he manifestly regards childhood not absolutely (ἁπλῶς) but in a certain respect (κατὰ τί), meaning the very people whom the same Christ in Matthew 11:25 calls “infants” (νηπίους), a term proper to a humbler and infantile age—not with respect to age, but to sincere and holy simplicity, in opposition to the wise and intelligent (σοφοῖς & συνετοῖς) of this age. Of these the prophet says in Psalm 131, “My soul within me was like one newly weaned from milk,” and Paul says, “I became all things to all people.” Otherwise, if infants, and indeed all infants, were at the very moment of baptism endowed with faith—as Andreae contends, with that justifying faith—there would be strong reason why it had been desirable for very many to remain infants not only in malice (κακίᾳ) but also in understanding (ταῖς φρεσίν), contrary to the Apostle’s teaching, “to be infants” (νηπιάζειν). If it pleases them to number among these little ones who believe that very child whom Christ is said to have summoned and placed among the disciples as an example of childlike simplicity, and others not unlike him, since Christ spoke in the singular—“this child” (τὸ παιδίον τοῦτο), that is, like this child, and “such a child” (τοιοῦτο παιδίον)—I do not object. But I add that he was already somewhat grown, not merely three or four years old as Andreae too confidently guesses, but already among Christ’s hearers and, according to his measure, understanding and attending to his teaching: namely, in the middle stage of childhood between infancy and adolescence, such as the “children” (παιδία) whom John specially addresses in 1 John 2:14 and 18. This is also clear from the verb “to cause to stumble” (σκανδαλίζειν), which does not apply to infancy or earliest childhood. Although Mark says that Christ took this child in his arms, it was certainly not to carry him as an infant (βρέφος), but, after summoning him, to embrace him kindly and paternally and then set him beside himself, surrounded by his disciples. Thus this point does not help Andreae even in the least.

With these wholly non-artistic (ἀτέχνοις) arguments refuted, that truly solid and firm foundation remains: faith is everywhere joined to the efficacy of the promise of eternal life in the formula of the covenant of eternal life, and the statement that no one can please God without faith is universal. Since this neither can nor ought to be denied, while Christ expressly says that the kingdom of heaven belongs to infants and the Apostle declares them holy, it seems rightly and necessarily to follow that true faith must be attributed to infants. Even the most learned ancient theologians do not share the same view on this matter. For this question belongs to that class of questions in which, because we all know many things only in part, it is permissible to differ in opinions while preserving the foundation of orthodox faith, provided we agree upon the true doctrine of original corruption and guilt, and that justification, sanctification, and eternal life must be sought in Christ alone and apprehended through faith. All therefore agree that not even infants can obtain Christ and his benefits without the intervention of faith—

—

But it is rightly asked what that faith of infants is, since by reason of their age they are incapable of perceiving any matter by understanding, much less of any persuasion. Hence some have wished to locate this faith in the Church, others in those presenting the infants, and others in the parents. These views occur frequently in Augustine, who indeed numbers infants among believers and the faithful, but in Letter 23 denies that they assent in their minds to the thing itself, and in Letter 57 that they know divine things; he nevertheless says the Holy Spirit dwells in them, since another’s faith presents the little child for baptism, where water outwardly displays the sacrament of grace while the Spirit inwardly works the benefit of grace. All these things are admittedly spoken obscurely. That obscurity produced the scholastics’ various perplexing distinctions. To unravel them, the most learned theologians of our age maintain that faith itself is not actually infused into the infant’s understanding, but that a certain beginning of it, as it were its seed and root, is produced, as is the beginning of regeneration in the will, by a certain hidden and inscrutable power of the Holy Spirit. But Luther went so far as expressly to affirm and teach as something to be believed that infants are, in a hidden and inscrutable manner, endowed with actual true faith and regenerated in intellect and will in and through baptism, obtained by the prayers of the Church, those presenting them, and their parents. He rests on this double foundation: baptized infants please God, while each one needs his own faith, according to the statement, “The righteous shall live by his faith.” Andreae, however, not content even with this indefinite dogma, contends “that faith is given and infused into every baptized infant without any exception, and this at the very moment they are baptized,” and—what is almost unbelievable—“not in an extraordinary but an ordinary manner, because,” he says, “it is not extraordinary that God works faith in baptized infants.” So he speaks in express words on pp. 491 and 492. If this last statement of his were true, there would have to be two ordinary modes of conferring faith: one through inward hearing alone in infants, unless perhaps he wishes the bodily ears of infants also to be opened; another through outward and inward hearing in adults. In whom, then, shall we posit extraordinary inward inspiration? Namely, in adults suddenly changed by the Holy Spirit, as happened in the prophets and in Paul. I leave this opinion of Andreae to the Church’s judgment, along with his other novel and unheard-of dogmas. But who am I to set myself up as arbiter among such great men? Nevertheless, I shall say what seems to me concerning this matter, prepared in this as in other things to acquiesce in the judgment—not, certainly, of Andreae or those like him, but of learned theologians who love the truth. I think that some knowledge of the gospel, much less that Christian full assurance (πληροφορίαν), cannot be posited in infants’ minds before through age they become capable of hearing it, understanding it through the Holy Spirit, and—

—receiving it by true faith, on the basis of any testimony of Scripture, any firm reason, or indeed even any very probable conjecture—unless God has sometimes been pleased extraordinarily to exercise this marvelous power of his in some individual. I think the same concerning the renewal of the will or affections, insofar as this life is governed rightly or wrongly by them. Nor do I think anyone among either the Fathers or scholastics before Luther can be found who asserted that dogma. Indeed, Augustine says in Letter 57: “If we attempt to show by words that little children who do not yet know human things know divine things, I fear that we may seem to do violence to our own senses when we labor to persuade by speech where the evident truth surpasses every power and function of speech.” And concerning conversion of the heart the same author says in *On Baptism*, book 4, chapter 24: “In baptized infants the sacrament of regeneration precedes; and if they maintain Christian piety, conversion in the heart follows, whose mystery preceded in the body.” Experience itself supplies us with another firm argument for overthrowing this dogma. For if the Holy Spirit effected something of this kind in the souls or wills of infants, could it happen that among so many myriads not even one who emerged from that earliest infancy and acquired firmer organs of understanding and affection would give testimony of that faith and regeneration? Yet what infant can you give me who, when he grows up, will be a Christian unless taught by hearing the Word things which he did not formerly know and which neither flesh nor blood teaches? If it must be so certainly established that they believe in their understanding and are renewed in will, why do we not also admit the recently baptized to the Lord’s Supper, suspecting them of not even evil thoughts? Is it because, as seems inferable from another of Andreae’s dogmas, all those endowed with true faith and regenerated in infancy lose the Holy Spirit as they grow and must be recalled through catechism to the elements of faith? Thus that dogma can be established by no right reason whatever. Moreover, that interpretation of Augustine’s statement concerning the faith of the Church and those presenting the infants—namely, that faith is given to infants through the prayers of the Church and those presenting them—is a begging of the question. Nor can it be gathered from any passage of Augustine that this entered his mind, since he so expressly denies that little children can believe with the heart any more than confess with the mouth. Rather, he considered the Church as mother of all the faithful, and those presenting the infants—whether the parents themselves or others sustaining the parents’ place and person—as members of the Church. He therefore thought that the faith of these persons, who constituted one mystical body with the infants themselves, sufficed also to graft those infants into Christ; we shall discuss this shortly. But I shall soon attempt to explain that beginning which, as it were, has the character of a root and seed, insofar as it seems to me that it must necessarily be established.
