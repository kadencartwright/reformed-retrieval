XXV. On several of the following pages—namely, pages 67, 68, 69, and
70—Wolzogen disputes about Reason. We have heard certain things about it
above; we shall see whether he brings forward sounder things here. *[We do not
wish here*, he says, *to assert what they call the instrumental use of Reason,
which we presuppose; the chief difficulty is by no means involved in that. We
are seeking a guide for ministering reason.]* Answer: Correct. This is what the
Socinians seek; this is what *Paradox*, your fictitious adversary, seeks. They
all presuppose the instrumental use of Reason. But they wish reason to be the
*guide*, or, as your elder calls it, the *principle*. The Reformed, however, do
not say this or speak in this way. They acknowledge that Reason is an
*instrument*; they do not wish it to be the *Guide*, as is clear from those
who have taken up the pen against the *Socinians*, especially from
*Hoornbeeck*, the most celebrated champion of truth. Let us see whether you
agree more with the *Socinians* and *Paradox* than with the orthodox. *[But
because*, so you say, *we have said that nothing is contained in the
Scriptures which is not true, while many truths are of such a kind that they
do not escape the light of reason, it consequently happens that reason is
often both guide and minister.]* Answer: Now your *Socinian* disposition
betrays itself. The question in interpreting the Scriptures concerns a guide,
or the norm according to which interpretation is to be undertaken. But you
grant this prerogative to Reason together with the *Socinians* and *Paradox*;
and you give as your reason that, namely, nothing is contained in the
Scriptures which is not true, and that very many of these truths do not escape
the light of reason. From this you conclude that Reason is not merely an
instrument or Minister but also the Norm or guide. You will certainly refute
your adversary splendidly in this way! But you explain yourself: *[And this*,
you say, *in two ways. First, it should call all things—for I make absolutely
no exception, whatever kind they may be—to examination and see whether
anything among them,*

[Page 119 | ONB canvas 173 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000173]

*which we think to be contained in the Scriptures, is contrary to manifest
reason. If we should discover anything of this kind, one may pronounce
affirmatively that this is not the meaning of the Scriptures.]* Answer:
Previously you were saying that Reason is the *guide* with respect to those
truths which do not escape its light, and thus you seemed to *Socinianize* only
in part. But now you fly over entirely into their camp, for you wish it to be
the *guide* with respect to all things, so that it calls all things to
examination—for you make absolutely no exception—with a view, namely, to
eliciting the true meaning of Scripture, since this is the guide in question.
And if Reason discovers anything contrary to itself, you affirm that it must
be rejected and wish to pronounce affirmatively that this is not the meaning
of the Scriptures. What else do the *Socinians* seek? Is it not by relying on
this foundation that they reject the clearest meanings of the sacred writings
and attach others to them, because they say that such meanings are openly and
manifestly contrary to their reason? There is no need for me to refute these
things; enough has already been said by others. I have discharged my office if
I show that *Wolzogen* gives his hands to the adversaries and shamefully
deserts the orthodox cause. Let the reader judge whether this has been done
here, and let him consider whether that *Velthuysen* brings forward sounder
things in *On the Use of Reason*, pages 56, 88–92, 132–33, 142–44, 153, and
following. *Adriaan Heereboord* philosophizes much more soundly in
*Philosophical Exercises*, volume 2, disputation 10. I shall not be reluctant
to set down some of his words here, so that *Wolzogen*, who wishes to be
regarded as a theologian, may be all the more ashamed. [*The abuse of
Philosophy in Theology is the worst*, he says in thesis 2, when its principles,
axioms, definitions, canons, and precepts are employed to demonstrate the
mysteries of faith and to establish faith itself concerning them. What? Is
faith not rather destroyed by them? For none among the Gentiles were more
hostile to faith in Christ and belief in the mysteries of our salvation than
the Philosophers. And in every age it has been found that uneducated men,
ignorant of other things and *sim-*

[Page 120 | ONB canvas 174 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000174]

*-ple*, content with the bare Word of God, were far more fitted to believe the
mysteries of faith than the most subtle philosophers. Here that saying of the
Apostle applies: *The natural man does not receive the things which are of the
Spirit of God*, and *Reason must be led captive under obedience to faith*.]
Thesis 3 has this: [*Another most grievous abuse of Philosophy in Theology is*
that, concerning other dogmas and articles of faith and concerning theological
questions and controversies (apart from the mysteries of faith and
salvation), Philosophy assumes mastery for itself when ministry belongs to
it. Philosophy ought not to dominate Theology like a mistress but to serve
like a handmaid. Theology is like Sarah, Philosophy like Hagar. Theology is
the queen of all faculties and disciplines; Philosophy is her attendant.]
Thesis 4 speaks as follows: [*The worst abuse of Philosophy in Theology is*
when articles of faith are either denied or twisted into an alien meaning
because they do not agree with the definitions or axioms of philosophical
things. The *Socinians* are guilty of this crime: they make the principles of
reason and of Philosophy the norm and measure of faith and of things to be
believed; and they reject the most holy Trinity, the Incarnation, the
resurrection of the numerically identical flesh, the other mysteries of
salvation, and the dogmas of faith because they do not agree with
philosophical definitions and principles. It is as if Philosophy were the
ten-foot rod, rule, and level by which faith must be tested. Thus, because
*Aristotle defined the universal* as *that which is naturally fitted to be
predicated of many, and the singular as that which is predicated of only one*,
they deny that God is by his nature predicated of several persons, because he
is a singular being, and in this way they abolish the Trinity. They likewise
overturn it from the retained definitions of *real and rational agreement*,
from *Aristotle*, who says that *the latter is that by which things that are as
if several agree in the same third thing, and the former that by which things
actually several do so*. But if there is a Trinity, the Antitrinitarians say,
since the three persons are in fact, in reality,

[Page 121 | ONB canvas 175 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000175]

and actually several, they will agree in the divine essence by an agreement
of reason, which the Trinitarians do not admit. Or, they say, if the three
persons in the Trinity agree in the divine essence by a real agreement, and
not through the mind, as the Trinitarians maintain, they will not be actually
several but as if several, which again the Trinitarians do not admit. And so
in other articles of faith, these men prefer to preserve safe and sound the
truth *of philosophical definitions from Aristotle* and to deny the truth of
the articles of faith, rather than reject those definitions and admit and
believe the dogmas of faith. But I would rather cast off and renounce the whole
of *Aristotle*, with all his definitions and those of all the Scholastics and
with their philosophical principles, than detract even a whit or a hair from
any article, mystery, or dogma of faith to be believed. Thus either
philosophical principles must be reconciled with the dogmas and matters of
faith, or, if they cannot be, they must be rejected rather than even the least
thing be taken away from faith and revealed truth.] He formulates thesis 5 in
this way: [*The abuse of Philosophy in Theology is the worst* when foundations
are laid there which are hindrances here, and when those opinions and
positions are asserted and defended in Philosophy which are the seeds,
occasions, and causes of errors in Theology.] And a little afterward: [From
where, I ask, have so many heresies, sects, dissensions, and monstrous opinions
in theological matters been taken up by ancient and more recent writers and
most stubbornly defended? Is it not from foundations badly laid in Philosophy,
so that this kind of *Philosopher* has not inaptly been called by Tertullian
the *Patriarchs of Heretics*? Is it not from the storehouse and arsenal of the
Scholastics' vain, barbarous, unsound, and false Philosophy that the flatterers
of the *Popes*, the *Jesuits*, and the remaining crowd of *Monks* bring out the
weapons with which they fight against Theology, the Reformed Religion, and us,
since they permit neither the *Pope's* nor

[Page 122 | ONB canvas 176 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000176]

*Aristotle's* authority to be torn from them? Is it not from the quiver of the
same corrupt *Philosophy* that the *God-fighting defenders and champions of
ill-fated Socinus* hurl and brandish their missiles against us? Is it not from
here that those who have now fought the Sacramentarian war for so many years
equip their forces, contending for *Helen of ubiquity* as for altars and
hearths? What? Do not even those very men who have departed from the bosom of
their mother, the *Reformed* Church, seek *seasoning for their five-article
reheated cabbage from the cases of this Philosophy*? This is most familiar to
everyone from their *middle knowledge, the indifference of free will,
conditional decrees*, and other jewels of that kind dug from this dunghill.]
These and many more are his words. But we must return to *Wolzogen*.

He adds more: *[In the second place, it serves in proving things, though only
those which either possess a settled truth in nature or a consequence flowing
from grace. For the former things are proper to reason; the latter, although
they have their source in revelation, must nevertheless likewise credit to
reason whatever flows from it. Therefore here too it is permissible to use
reason to determine the ambiguous meaning of the scriptures.]* Answer: Here
you show in the second place how Reason is a guide in interpreting the
Scriptures. You say it is a guide in all those things which either possess a
settled truth in nature—and you say that these are proper to reason—or flow
from grace; and you say that whatever flows from it must likewise be credited
to reason. That is, Reason is the guide in all these matters, and according to
it we must interpret all the passages of Scripture which treat them or contain
the sources of these consequences. And in all these things it is permissible
to use reason to determine the ambiguous meaning of the Scriptures—not as an
*instrument*, for this use is always presupposed and is not in dispute, but as
a *guide, norm, rule*, or *ten-foot rod*, because you were saying that the
chief difficulty was involved in this, and you were seeking a *guide*

[Page 123 | ONB canvas 177 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000177]

for ministering reason. What? Are you not now a deserter, *Wolzogen*? But
perhaps you will correct these things in what follows. Let us see. *[Just as
the use is twofold*, you say, *so also is the abuse of reason twofold. First,
it must not be determined that everything which rises above the powers of
reason is therefore immediately brought against its clear and distinct
judgments.]* Answer: No one will deny this abuse of reason—neither the
*Socinians* nor your *Paradox*. The *Socinians* grant that many things in the
Scriptures rise above the powers of reason, as will be clear from their words
to be cited later. But they refuse to allow that anything is contained in the
Scriptures which is contrary to the clear and distinct judgment of their
Reason, and therefore they reject the Trinity and very many other things. It
is true that their judgment in this is perverse and that they judge things to
be contrary to reason which are not at all contrary to it. Nevertheless they
follow your method, *Wolzogen*: they call those mysteries to the examination
of reason; and because they think that such dogmas are contrary to their
reason, they therefore reject the Scriptures or interpret them more
conveniently. The Reformed judge very differently. *Hoornbeeck* puts it very
well in *Refutation of Socinianism*, book 1, chapter 6, page 123: [It is not
absurd that all the heads of religion are consonant with reason, nor do we
object to others that they are. Understand this not in the sense that sacred
things should be dragged to reason and reconciled with reason, but that reason
should submit and conform itself to Scripture.] See what follows, which I omit
for brevity's sake. *Wolzogen* continues: *[Second, one must not think that all
things belong to the jurisdiction of reason. For there are two kinds of things
which are not subject to reason's dominion.]* Answer: I wish he would explain
to me what that jurisdiction of reason is and what that dominion is. I
certainly do not grasp these things. *Wolzogen* is speaking here about the
normative use of Reason in interpreting the Scriptures, and above he was
saying that it calls all things of every kind to examination. I wish he would
indicate what difference there is between these things—calling to examination,
and

[Page 124 | ONB canvas 178 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000178]

exercising jurisdiction or possessing dominion. He was saying that Reason
judges whether anything which we think to be contained in the Scriptures is
contrary to manifest reason and that, if it discovers anything of this kind,
it can pronounce affirmatively that this is not the meaning of the Scriptures.
Is this not to exercise a dominion and jurisdiction such as the nature of the
matter under dispute can bear? To strike something from the register of
revealed truths is most certainly to exercise supreme jurisdiction. But what
are these things exempt from reason's dominion? *[All those things*, he says,
*which do not possess any necessity in the matter but either depend upon the
mere will of God or contain the deed of those who could act in one way or
another. Here reason has nothing to adjudicate, except that it ought and is
able to take repeated care lest it suppose something to be related as true
which conflicts with reason by an evident falsehood.]* Answer: If Reason has
this to adjudicate, it has enough—indeed, more than enough—in this business.
For the question does not concern the thing itself and its nature but whether
it is revealed and whether it is contained in the sacred writings. And with a
view to this, judgment must be delivered by reason. Reason can and must also
call these things to examination and judge whether anything of this kind
which is thought to be revealed conflicts with reason or does not conflict
with it. In *Censure of the Censure*, page 95, he answers that he *[affirms that
reason does only this here: it examines all the circumstances well, so that
one does not overthrow another. If that happens in a history, we assuredly*,
he says, *repudiate it.]* Answer: He deceives or is deceived in speaking this
way. For, 1. he openly says that Reason can and ought here to take care lest it
suppose something to be related as true which conflicts by an evident
falsehood—not with the history itself but with *reason*. Then, if in these
things it had nothing else to adjudicate, he said most falsely above that
Reason can call all things to examination, and so forth. Dr. *de Labadie* was
saying that on this account reason must not be corrected by Scripture, but
Scripture by reason. Wolzogen replies at the same place,

[Page 125 | ONB canvas 179 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000179]

page 96, that *properly speaking, neither takes place*. Why so? *[For when*, he
says, *it is certainly established that right reason teaches something, it
cannot and ought not to be corrected, because truth itself would be corrected;
nor will Scripture contain anything contrary to that reason.]* Answer: You
would have spoken more accurately if you had said, *when right reason teaches
something*. But because no sure rule is given by which it may be judged
whether right reason teaches it, it is safer to correct even the reason which
we think is right by the Scriptures than to say that the Scriptures contain
nothing contrary to that reason. The *Socinians* share this error with you,
and on its account they reject the very foundations of religion. For they
think it sufficiently certain to themselves that the things which they hold
are taught by right reason, and that Scripture cannot contain anything
contrary to it. He further replies: *[When it is also certainly established
that Scripture teaches something, it can never be corrected except for the
worse. But if reason is doubtful, if it is ignorant, if it is so imperfect
that it cannot grasp something, Scripture will instruct it and command it to
believe also those things which it otherwise cannot believe. Likewise, if Scripture
seems in its words to bring forward something contrary to manifest reason,
Scripture itself will not now be corrected by reason, but that which seems to
belong to Scripture will be; those doubtful and obscure things will be
explained by reason.]* Answer: It is not yet established whether Scripture
teaches it, for our question concerns the norm for eliciting the true meaning
of Scripture. But before the meaning is elicited, it is not yet established
what Scripture teaches. 2. Can it not also be corrected for the worse when it
is not certainly established that Scripture teaches something? Can Scripture
teach something when it is not established that it teaches it? And when it
does teach something, can reason corrupt it? *Not right reason*, he says. Very
clever indeed! In the compound sense, right reason cannot corrupt it when,
namely, it does not corrupt it. 3. In these matters which are now being
*discuss-*

[Page 126 | ONB canvas 180 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000180]

*-ed*, is reason not always doubtful, ignorant, and imperfect, so that it
cannot grasp them before it is instructed by Scripture? If so, it must
therefore always believe the things which it cannot attain. 4. The *Socinians*
also profess that they do not correct Scripture by Reason, but only what seems
to be said by Scripture, because they assert that what Scripture seems to
bring forward in its words is contrary to manifest reason. 5. What are those
doubtful and obscure things which will be explained by reason? I had thought
that they were all to be explained by Scripture. But what are those other
things which are not subject to reason's dominion? *[Then all those things
too*, he says, *reject the judgment of reason which, although they possess the
highest truth—not merely historical but also natural, in accordance with the
constitution of their own nature, as all those things which are contained
among the causes of our salvation must be considered to possess—are
nevertheless removed from reason's investigation. Here reason performs
nothing other than showing the probability of a thing which it does not
understand, or indeed confirming it once understood, but only when it has
already given assent to it because of God's authority.]* Answer: All these
things are very far outside the course. For the question is not what reason
can do in judging revealed mysteries, so far as the nature of the truths
themselves is concerned. The *Socinians* readily acknowledge that many things
are contained in the Scriptures which surpass reason's comprehension. And if
you grant that what reason judges to be not merely improbable but contrary to
itself must be rejected, they have what they want. Hear what your *Velthuysen*
says about the position of *Paradox* himself in *On the Use of Reason*, page
197: *[The author does not deny*, he says, *that very many things are revealed
in the sacred Bible which no one could have known by reason and the principles
implanted in nature. But he contends that nothing is revealed in the words of
Scripture whose meaning is not investigated by Philosophy or right reason as
a principle, and that the interpretation of a word ought to be tested by that
norm.]* Indeed, only just now you yourself

[Page 127 | ONB canvas 181 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000181]

were saying that all things, with no exception, must be called to the
examination of reason. *Wolzogen* concludes in this way what he had to say here
about Reason: *[We employ reason, confined within these limits, in the
interpretation of the Scriptures. It is not that the nature of Interpretation,
insofar as it is Interpretation, admits any principal use of Reason. Rather,
because in contrast with all other Writings the distinctive constitution of
the divine Writings is that they contain nothing but truth, reason can judge
that truth and thus often determine whether a particular meaning agrees with
the words or does not agree.]* Answer: 1. The *Socinians* and your friend
*Paradox* wish to confine reason within the same limits. Show, therefore, the
narrower limits within which you confine it. 2. If the nature of
Interpretation, insofar as it is interpretation, does not admit any principal
use of reason, why do you ascribe such a use to it in interpreting the
Scriptures, when you said above that one must proceed in interpreting them in
the same way as with other human writings? 3. The reason added does not appear
sufficiently valid. For just as reason judges the true, it can also judge the
false, and the records of all men contain either truth or falsehood; and those
which contain truth can be better grasped by reason than divine mysteries or
supernatural truths can. Therefore it ought to have a principal use in every
interpretation. 4. However this may be, we see it have a principal use in the
interpretation of Scripture, contrary to the doctrine of the Reformed. 5.
Above he said that many of the truths of Scripture do not escape the light of
reason; here, however, he speaks absolutely and without any restriction and
says that the Scriptures contain nothing but truth and that reason judges it.
Thus our *Wolzogen* always advances toward the worse. 6. If it determines in
this way whether a particular meaning agrees with the words, why do you say
only *often* and not *always*, so that there might be the most perfect
agreement between you and *Paradox*? But if it judges all truths, why can it
not also always determine whether a meaning agrees with the words or does not?
Or if

[Page 128 | ONB canvas 182 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000182]

you deny that it judges all truths, show the reason why Reason cannot
determine whether a meaning agrees with words which express a truth that
surpasses reason's comprehension—but in such a way that you do not fall into
an error far worse than the one of which your elder judges *Paradox* guilty,
as we have just heard. But enough about these things in this place; let us pass
on to what follows.
