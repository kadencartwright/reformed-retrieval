CHAPTER X

Principal and Solemn Time and Its Necessary Determination for the Worship of God

Although it may be clear from what has been said above what we understand by
*principal and solemn time*, and how necessary it is to determine it, it will
not be useless to set down a few things briefly here about this time and its
determination. For if we offer a clear explanation of this time and at the
same time show how necessary it is for this time to be precisely determined,
we will prepare the way for clarifying our principal question.

Our adversaries often speak of *sufficient time* and of time *fixed, regulated,
and limited* for solemn worship; and beyond this time they will have nothing
else in the Fourth Commandment to be moral. They say that this time is
determined by the Law of Nature, or that it is *moral by nature*. But they
sometimes change their opinion and speak very differently—indeed, they seem
to contradict these things—and so they are not sufficiently consistent with
themselves. For they say that *public or solemn worship* (they acknowledge no
other worship to be solemn) is the moral element of the Fourth Commandment,
that time is included and commanded only consequently, and that it is of the
same nature as place. Yet nowhere do they say that a *fixed, limited, and
regulated place* is something moral by nature, nor can they speak of a place
sufficient for religion in the way they speak of sufficient time.

When they speak about frequency, some of them think that our solemn time ought
to be no less frequent than the Jews' solemn time had been. Yet even these are
not consistent with themselves, for they argue at one point for lesser
frequency and at another for greater frequency. Meanwhile they say nothing
about sufficient duration, but go off into various opinions. Some limit the
duration to the hours before noon, some to only two hours, and some say
nothing at all about this continuation, although it is the chief matter under
question. Most concede nothing for private exercises; and those who seem to
concede time even for them do so rarely and rather timidly, afraid that by
conceding this they will yield the case.

It is therefore necessary that we explain the nature and properties of this
solemn time and show how necessary its determination is in relation to the
worship of God.

We said above that solemn time differs from common time in this: common time
necessarily accompanies the prescribed worship and needs no special
determination or command, since it is prescribed together with the worship it
accompanies. Solemn time, however, is particularly prescribed and has the
duties of religion necessarily accompanying it; that is, it requires that the
duties of religious worship be performed in it. We also said that both
*duration*, or *how long*, and *frequency*, or *how often*, concur in this
principal and solemn time. To explain this time more fully, the following
things must now be said.

First. The duration of this solemn time ought to be so ample that within it

[1674 logical printed p. 276 | IA leaf 294 | PDF p. 294]

sufficient time is granted for celebrating public worship, domestic worship,
and solitary worship. Nor is this sufficient time to be restricted to public
worship, as though no other worship should be called solemn, as the
adversaries would have it, or as though that time were a mere circumstance and
mere adjunct of worship.

The adversaries, who limit all solemn time to public worship, do not deny that
public or ecclesiastical worship ought to be performed during solemn time. But
I cannot confidently assert whether they refer to this public worship the
worship which in a time of persecution cannot be performed safely enough in
public places or churches, but sometimes in caves, woods, mountains, and so
forth. Yet whether they will or not, it is plain that worship performed in
places of that sort ought also to be called solemn and distinguished from
domestic and solitary worship, since it is performed in a solemn manner by
several distinct families.

Moreover, this is clear from all those passages of Scripture which describe
the duty of pastors to us and show that they are bound to administer the Word
and Sacraments, to pray for the people, and to proclaim the gospel in season
and out of season, and so forth. It is also clear from the passages which
establish that it is the people's duty to give ear to the gospel proclaimed,
to cultivate ecclesiastical communion, and solemnly to adore and celebrate God
in public worship. Hence it has hitherto been received and customary among all
Christians to celebrate public assemblies among themselves, so far as
possible, either openly or secretly from persecutors; and they have thought it
better, in order to hold these assemblies, to expose their life and the things
they held dear to many dangers than to discontinue them.

As for domestic worship, it too ought to be performed during solemn time. 1.
Very many families are so entangled in worldly business that they would rarely
worship God unless some solemn time were appointed in which domestic worship
could be performed; and unless this were also required during solemn time, it
would be omitted altogether in some families. Solemn time must therefore be
long enough for this worship also to be performed within it, so that the
refractory cannot defend themselves by any excuse. 2. Families also need to be
prepared for celebrating public worship, lest they take God's name in vain and
be deprived of the fruit of public worship. Their minds will be best disposed
to attend to divine things by domestic worship, and they will be better
prepared to appear devoutly and holily before God. 3. When public worship is
finished, domestic worship is necessary so that the impression made in public
worship may take deeper root; so that the things proclaimed in public may be
absorbed better and fixed in the memory; so that, with the divine blessing
obtained, they may possess the abundant fruit of their labors; and so that
individuals may repeat what they have heard and the fruit may be shared by
all, and so forth. 4. I say nothing of the catechizing which every father of a
household ought to institute, that he may instruct the members of his family
and teach them the things necessary to know for salvation.

Finally, solitary worship is also required, as is solemn time for performing
this worship. For 1. many people, especially servants, are not otherwise
granted sufficient time; nor, because of other most urgent business by which
they seek a livelihood for themselves and their own, are they permitted to
worship God solemnly as often as is proper. Hence Calvin says on Genesis 2,
“To make up what continual meditation has lost, every seventh day

[1674 logical printed p. 277 | IA leaf 295 | PDF p. 295]

was specially chosen.” 2. Solitary preparation is also required both for
domestic and for public worship. 3. In this way they obtain more abundant
fruit from the other duties; and the things stated generally are best applied
and impressed more deeply by private meditations and prayers. 4. Everyone also
needs suitable time for reading the Scriptures and other practical books, for
examining his conscience, for laying before God the difficulties that oppress
him, and for praising God for benefits received, and so forth. Since, because
of other business, a person cannot labor sufficiently at this every day,
solemn time must also be made longer for the sake of this worship. 5. This
solitary worship is a true and good—if not the best—mark of a true Christian,
and is therefore supremely necessary. 6. Public worship is sometimes
impossible and can be impeded in many ways. Solemn time therefore ought to
look first to this worship, since it can be performed less readily than any
other.

Thus all these kinds of solemn worship ought to concur here for spending
solemn time properly. It is consequently clear that this duration of solemn
time ought to extend to at least one whole day. For in a shorter span it is
scarcely possible to perform all the duties required in solitary, domestic,
and public worship, provided they are exercised with the reverence, gravity,
and solemnity which are fitting.

It is clear from what has been said how necessary it is that a determination
of such solemn time be made. It is also plain from this that, without this
determination, religion would be finished among everyone. For:

1. All religion would quickly perish unless some suitable and frequently
recurring time were determined for public worship (if, that is, we are
speaking of God's ordinary dispensation). For this public worship is a means
appointed by God for preserving and advancing religion. Experience teaches
how averse very many are to this religious work, and how many obstacles and
other distractions are always presented. Thus, if there were no definite and
determined time in which God was to be worshiped publicly, this part of
worship would be wholly neglected and religion would at length vanish.

2. Religion would also perish unless some suitable time were determined for
celebrating both public and domestic worship. For if domestic worship were
set aside and rejected, all zeal and the fruit of public worship would quickly
perish. It is sufficiently well known how little many fathers of families care
what the members of their households do in matters of religion. Yet once a
determination is made which includes this domestic worship as well, some will
be constrained by the spur of conscience to perform this duty. And once this
determination is made, profane servants who would otherwise refuse to attend
domestic sacred exercises can more readily be brought into order.

3. Religion would also perish unless some time were determined that was so
suitable and long that within it God could be worshiped not only publicly and
domestically, but also by individuals in solitude. For unless such a decisive
determination were given, there could be no provision against interruptions
which superiors, inferiors, and equals can cause; nor could the impediments
which arise on every side be removed. Neither could that aversion of mind from
which very many suffer be taken away in any other manner.

Second, this solemn time requires that the full proportion of continuation and
frequency together be no more ample than God's glory and

[1674 logical printed p. 278 | IA leaf 296 | PDF p. 296]

human salvation necessarily require. It must be possible to demonstrate that
by a smaller proportion God cannot ordinarily be sufficiently honored and
worshiped, nor our eternal salvation advanced. Thus this proportion must be
most certainly necessary to religion, and one which we can ordinarily spend
without obstructing our worldly business, once the reservation mentioned
several times above is allowed. Even those who are somewhat averse to religion
cannot deny that it is necessary, nor affirm that it is utterly hostile to our
earthly business. The adversaries will not deny this. For Primerose himself,
Part 2, chapter 7, section 4, says that “it would be more equitable if we spent
more days in divine worship than in our own business—indeed, if we spent them
all.” And shortly afterward he says that “it is not more equitable or better
proportioned, after six days have been given to our labors, to consecrate the
seventh to God than the sixth, fifth, or fourth; indeed it is less so. For the
more days we dedicate to God, the more we do what is just, equitable, and well
ordered.” Although I cannot endorse his calculation in these words, he
nevertheless abundantly confirms what I now intend.

That a determination of time of this sort is necessary is clear from this:
the mouths of those ill-disposed toward religion would thereby be stopped, and
they would have nothing they could modestly object to the contrary. By this
means the determinate time will also be better observed by those who would
otherwise complain, protest, and call it a most grievous burden; and who, even
if they did not altogether neglect public worship, would wholly neglect
private worship as a burden “hard to bear” (*δυσβάστακτον*) and unsuited to the
gospel state.

Let us apply this to our proposition in another way. No one will say that a
span of sixteen or eighteen hours (the span of one day after subtracting the
time appointed for sleep and rest), or even of twenty-four hours, is too large
to dedicate to God and our souls by comparison with the span of six days,
which contain 144 hours. Nor will anyone deny that this minimum portion is
necessary for everyone to appoint to this use, or say that it is in any way
injurious to our earthly business. Indeed, I scarcely believe that anyone will
say a smaller total portion can be sufficient both for celebrating the worship
of God and for advancing our salvation. And if the adversaries concede this,
as seems equitable and should be denied by no one, they will yield and will no
longer dispute against the Sabbath.

Third, the distribution of the total portion between duration and frequency
must be made so wisely and equitably that it may certainly be established that
our worldly business will not be intolerably burdened by an excessively
frequent recurrence, even though the duration is minimal. That duration must
nevertheless be so great, as was seen above, that in it God can be worshiped
publicly, in families, and by individuals in solitude.

Fourth, the distributor and determiner must possess such authority and wisdom
that the unwilling are compelled to observe this distribution as necessary,
while the willing and well-disposed have the means to stop their adversaries'
mouths and defend themselves and their practice against objectors.

When solemn time is of this kind, no one can regard it as a burden, nor can
anyone say on that account that such a time was never appointed by God under
the gospel. And when such solemn time is determined by one who possesses both
wisdom and

[1674 logical printed p. 279 | IA leaf 297 | PDF p. 297]

authority, every doubt and all deliberation will now be removed; every dispute
about it will end, and every consultation about changing it will cease.

Fifth, it is required that the total portion and its distribution be
determined so that this time is certainly *sufficient* in three respects.

1. With respect to God and his acceptance: that is, the total portion and its
distribution between duration and frequency must please God, so that he does
not require a greater portion or a different distribution from that expressed
by the determination, but graciously accepts so great a portion repeated so
often. For unless the determination is of this kind, it will be entirely vain
and useless. To what other end is a determination made, unless that some
service pleasing and acceptable to God may be performed? Whatever we are and
whatever we possess—time and all things—belong to God. Whatever we determine
is therefore in vain unless what we determine is pleasing to God.

2. With respect to our souls: it must be sufficient to advance our eternal
salvation. For such is God's loving-kindness (*φιλανθρωπία*) that he regards no
service as sufficient unless it is also sufficient, in its own kind, to
advance the spiritual good of our souls—that is, to acquire and confirm the
knowledge of God and to arouse and preserve good affections, and so forth. A
fair portion and proportional distribution are required for these and similar
things, so that on each occasion a suitable and proportionate span is granted
for confirming them. This must be so even with respect to those who are
ignorant and not sufficiently capable of instruction, and those so immersed
in the cares of this world that they cannot readily be stirred to pursue
spiritual things.

3. With respect to our consciences: that they may be satisfied both about
God's acceptance and about the sufficiency of the time for advancing our
spiritual good. This does not mean that every scruple of everyone can be
satisfied—namely, the scruples of those who are weak or labor to raise
scruples—but that the determination should be such as can, with the best
reason, satisfy every scruple raised from either side. Such a foundation must
be laid that from it we can know, not conjecturally but certainly, that such a
portion of time, distributed in this way—so long a continuation of time
recurring so often—is pleasing and acceptable to God, useful to us, and even
sufficient in its own kind to advance our spiritual good.

Since God's Word is the one solid foundation on which our consciences rest,
nothing but God's Word can present us with certain and infallible rules by
which we may judge this sufficiency. For this matter, which contributes so
greatly to advancing God's glory and our salvation, is of great importance;
and God's Word, which can make us perfect for every good work, can undoubtedly
also instruct us perfectly in discerning this sufficient time. We must
therefore rest in the determination revealed to us in Scripture, for the
conscience has nothing it can object against it.

Sixth, the determination required here is not *exclusive* but *conclusive*.
The time is therefore *principal and sufficient* because it is of this kind;
yet it is not the only time determined. For other times are also determined
for divine worship—

[1674 logical printed p. 280 | IA leaf 298 | PDF p. 298]

at least for domestic and solitary worship—namely morning and evening time on
each day, and extraordinary times both for humiliation and for thanksgiving.
Given occasion, everyone can determine other times for himself as well. This
solemn time is therefore sufficient not absolutely, but in its own kind as
solemn time. It is accordingly the *principal and solemn, necessary, and
ordinarily sufficient time* of worship. Thus it differs from every time which
is less ordinary, such as extraordinary time for prayer, fasting, and so
forth. It also differs from time which is less solemn, such as morning and
evening time determined by nature itself. Finally, it differs from time which
is less principal and sufficient, as are all the times mentioned above. It
therefore makes no difference by what name this time is designated: whether
it is called *necessary and principal time, ordinarily sufficient time,
sufficient time, the principal time of worship,* or, finally, *solemn time par
excellence* (*κατ’ ἐξοχήν*), provided the time already described and explained
is understood.

It must be noted, however, that although nature itself requires us to worship
God every morning and evening, both in solitude and in our families, we have
no determination of how long we must continue in these exercises on each
occasion. This is left free to individuals as occasion and necessity require.
The reason is that such a determination was unnecessary, because the solemn
continuation has been determined on the solemn day. Hence there is likewise no
determination of extraordinary times with respect either to this or that
person, or to this or that occasion. There is nevertheless a certain general
determination of the season or occasion—namely, when grave evils or the like
are imminent—and also of the continuation, namely, for an entire day.

Principal and solemn time therefore embraces both duration and frequency:
that is, *so much time together, and that so often*, or *so much time set apart
and dedicated to religion and divine worship so often*. To constitute this
time, the total portion must be suitably large, and the continuation suitably
long: namely, for celebrating the solemn worship of God, domestic worship, and
solitary worship, and for performing all the duties of these kinds of worship
with gravity and reverence. The recurrence of this duration must also be
suitably frequent. Thus no other proportion of time can equal it with respect
to equality, since every other is either notably shorter in duration or
notably less frequent. It is consequently not substantially useful for
advancing religion in general.

Furthermore, this solemn time is useful not merely to this or that person or
to this or that nation, but to absolutely everyone in every age and every
region of the earth.

Finally, it is determined not *exclusively*, as though it were the only time
that ought to be observed, but *conclusively*. It is not necessary that any
portion larger than the total, a longer duration, or a more frequent
repetition or recurrence be determined as the principal and solemn time of
worship which everyone must ordinarily observe. And although some other time
can be extraordinarily determined for this or that person or nation, here and
now, on account of this or that singular occasion—which differs greatly from
this principal and solemn time—nevertheless this

[1674 logical printed p. 281 | IA leaf 299 | PDF p. 299]

time already described and explained is sufficient to be principal and solemn
time.
