### Chapter IV

#### Third argument

Page 185.

For those for whom Christ properly died, he died in their place in such a way
that he transferred to himself the death deserved by them and saved them from
it. The very expression “dying for someone” sufficiently demonstrates this:
2 Samuel 18:33; Romans 5:7 and 9:3. But Christ did not thus transfer to
himself, and remove from them, the punishment or death owed by all and each:
John 3:36; Matthew 7:23. Therefore he did not properly die for all and each.

Reply of the Remonstrants: The expression “to die for someone” does not always
denote that transfer of death from one person to another. They show this by
various counterexamples, following in Socinus’s footsteps; he attempts to
attack the force of this expression through almost the same tunnels, lest it
be thought to contain satisfaction properly so called. This may be seen in
Hugo Grotius’s *Defense*, chapters 1 and 9. But in vain, for they themselves
report the answer given them from the conference: “The brethren, namely,
understand this of the kind of dying by which Christ died for us—he who, to be
sure, paid for us as surety.” “But this,” they say, “is not expressed here.”
Indeed, I say that it is expressed in the description

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 131 | printed p. 121]

which the argument proposes, for things are customarily expressed or
explained by definitions and descriptions no less than by their proper name.
“Neither David,” they add, “in 2 Samuel 18:33, nor Paul in Romans 5:7 and 9:3,
had regard to any suretyship.” David did have regard to it expressly, I say,
insofar as a wish can regard or conceive something which remains so undone
that it cannot now be made a fact, just as his words expressly teach: “Who
will grant me to die—me, I say—in your place?” Paul also had the same mind in
Romans 9:3.
Everyone sees that he put on precisely the same affection toward his brothers
and kinsmen which Judah had toward Benjamin in Genesis 44:32, when he had
become surety for him before his father and wished to remain in his place.
Such a transfer of death, as though by a surety bond, also seems
sufficiently intimated in Romans 5:7, in that account of undergoing death
“for a good man.” For the Apostle signifies that someone might perhaps be
found who, reckoning up within himself the benefits received from that man
and also considering how much advantage would follow if so beneficent a man
survived here longer, would not refuse to surrender himself in the man’s
stead to an immediately present death. Finally, they add that “for us” can
amount to “for our good,” as in 1 Corinthians 8:11 and so forth, or even “on
our account” or “on account of sins,” as in 1 Corinthians 15:3 and Romans
4:25. No one among our men—indeed, scarcely anyone among the heretics—has
ever denied that first meaning, “for our good.” But this is far too general a
veil, beneath which various deadly errors can easily be hidden. The other
meaning, if rightly explained—“for sins,” that is, bearing in our stead that
death which was owed for our sins—is the very thing placed in the argument by
our men. But if sins are regarded as abstracted from sinners, as the
Remonstrants do here, it is no wonder that the absurd conclusion is drawn
from it that Christ gave his very self to death in place of misdeeds. Socinus
was the author and inventor of this entire reply; on this account he is
rightly rebuked

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 132 | printed p. 122]

by Hugo Grotius in chapters 1 and 9. The reader should also observe how
carefully the Remonstrants flee from the designation “surety,” and how they
turn themselves into every shape lest they concede that Christ made
satisfaction for us as a surety, and that this is denoted by the expressions
“he gave himself for us” and “he died for us.” Yet he is expressly called by
the Holy Spirit “the surety of the new covenant” (Heb. 7:22), and is said “to
have borne our diseases, to have carried our sorrows, to have the chastisement
of our peace laid upon him, and to be the one upon whom the iniquity of us all
fell, so that he himself bears our iniquities and is offered as a sacrifice
for our guilt” (Isa. 53:5–7; 1 Pet. 2:24). All these things are denoted by that
contracted expression, “he died for us,” or “he was made sin for us,” as the
Apostle expressly interprets it in 2 Corinthians 5:21. This furnishes at least
a half-full proof that the doctrine of Christ’s satisfaction for us which our
theologians hand down from the Scriptures and the churches believe is not
approved by all the Remonstrants.

They observe in the second place that the expression “to die for someone”
often has regard more to the intention of the person who lays down his life
than to the outcome in saving the one for whom it is laid down. In this sense,
Christ died for all, since his intention was to save all. But they deceive the
unwary reader with ornamented words. For 1. they deceitfully insinuate that we
deny that the intention of Christ and of God concerning human salvation is
regarded in this expression, although the hinge of this controversy is placed
in this very point, as we saw before: whether bare procurement, which they
explain by the opening of a door, is the whole purpose of Christ as he dies,
as they maintain; or whether it also has joined to it, as we believe, the
certain intention of introducing some through that opened door into grace and
eternal salvation. 2. They also speak both contrary to the truth and contrary
to their own mind and custom

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 133 | printed p. 123]

when they say that in his death Christ intended the salvation of all, or to
save all. This is contrary to the truth because many at that time were in
such a state that they could not be saved or pass over to Abraham’s bosom
(Luke 16:26); therefore, at least, he did not intend to save them. Nor indeed
would anyone among the rest come to that place if Christ had intended their
salvation absolutely. For whatever he intended to accomplish by his death,
he obtained from the Father. But if he obtained it absolutely, it was also
truly granted, and consequently all would undoubtedly be saved if he intended
to save all. The same point can be proved from this: the testament was so
firmly established in Christ’s death that the dying man’s own intention and
last will could not be so annulled that it would not be the certain rule of
the ensuing effect, or of the distribution of the goods placed in the
testament. Nicolas Grevinchovius abundantly testifies that what they profess
here is also contrary to their own mind, when he relates that their position
is that “the application of reconciliation unto salvation is not properly
the end of the procurement, or the intention of Christ as he procures
reconciliation by his death. God could not have willed this application
except after the procurement was completed” (pages 8–9 and following).

Unless the preceding distinction is accepted, they deny that our position
can be reconciled with those passages of Scripture which say that Christ died
for all, or with our own confession concerning sufficiency, in respect of
which we confess that Christ died for all. The testimonies of Scripture have
been answered above. As for the expression “for all,” admitted by our men on
account of the sufficiency and the intention of God by which he willed that
it should be sufficient for all, it must be known that this expression is
improper. Nevertheless, it is not to be rejected, because it sometimes has a
foundation in the thing itself and has been accepted among theologians by
long use. Thus far the contest concerned the major premise.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 134 | printed p. 124]

In the minor premise they criticize nothing which has not already been
refuted more often. Nevertheless, a word must be added about Matthew 7:23,
lest they appear to have wrested this passage from us. Christ says that he
never knew the workers of iniquity—that is, they say, “as such”—as though
those workers of iniquity were such unjust judges of matters that they would
call into doubt, especially on that day, whether works of iniquity were
approved by Christ or not! The Savior says that he never approved them, not
even insofar as they prophesied through his name and performed many mighty
works. The Remonstrants add that it does not follow from this that Christ did
not die for them because he did not acknowledge them, since he had not only
to die for them but also to apply his death to them before he acknowledged
them as his own. It is so repugnant to reason for Christ to give himself to
death for those whom he in no way acknowledges as his own that it scarcely
needs refutation. Nevertheless, it is refuted by Christ’s sufficiently plain
words in John 10:14–15: “I know the sheep, and I lay down my life for them.”
There we see the knowledge of the sheep and the laying down of his life for
them joined in an indivisible bond. Indeed, this laying down of his life proves
that knowledge, just as an effect proves its cause. It is also refuted in the
type of redemption, the deliverance of the people from Egypt. I do not think
one should dispute with anyone who contends that God in no way acknowledged
that people as his own before he led them out of Egypt. God says of them,
“You are borne by me from the womb” (Isa. 46:4),
which is rightly applied to all the elect, just as is what he says in Jeremiah
1:5: “When I had not yet formed you in the womb, I knew you.”
