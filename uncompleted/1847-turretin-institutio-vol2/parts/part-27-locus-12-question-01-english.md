THE COVENANT OF GRACE

QUESTION I.

*What are the origin and meaning of the terms ברית, διαθήκης, foedus
(“covenant”), ἐπαγγελίας (“promise”), and evangelium (“gospel”), which are used
here?*

[Marginal heading: The necessity of treating the covenant.]

I. Having explained the nature and use of the law, which stands antecedently
to the covenant of grace, we must now treat the *covenant* itself. Since it is
of the greatest importance in theology—as it is, so to speak, the center and
bond of all religion, which consists in God’s communion with man and embraces
within its scope all God’s benefits toward man and man’s duties toward God—the
sum of the matter lies in rightly knowing and keeping it. Therefore, its
investigation requires particular ἀκρίβειαν, *exactness*, so that the truth may
be confirmed against all the errors by which Satan has endeavored in almost
every age to obscure and corrupt this saving doctrine.

[Marginal heading: The etymology of the word ברית.]

II. In order that the covenant’s nature may be known more easily, a few things
must first be said about the origin and meaning of the words by which it is
usually designated in Scripture and which theologians commonly use. From a
careful elucidation of them no small light can be shed upon the various
difficulties which arise here.

The Hebrews express *covenant* by the word ברית, whose etymology is uncertain.
Some derive it from the root ברה, either in the sense of *eating*, because
feasts were customarily held when covenants were made, or rather in the sense
of *choosing*, because the persons, matters, and conditions under which a
covenant is made are chosen. Hence the Jews are called *sons of the covenant*
(Acts 3:25) and the *people of God*, γένος ἐκλεκτόν, *a chosen race* (1 Pet.
2:9), for there is a mutual choice here: on God’s part, choosing human beings
as a people; and on the part of human beings, choosing God as their God, as is
expressed in Josh. 24:22, 25.

Others derive it from ברר, *he purified* and *declared*, because by a covenant
the sincere and pure faith and friendship of the contracting parties is
confirmed and declared. Others more suitably derive it from the root ברא,
which in the Piel means *he cut apart*, or from the root בתר, *he cut apart*,
by a metathesis of the letters ת and ר. Scripture uses this word when it mentions
the confirmation of covenants (Gen. 15:10; Jer. 34:18), because when a covenant
was made, animals were cut apart and the parties passed between the pieces of
the slaughtered victims, signifying that those who violated the covenant were
likewise to be cut apart, as is evident from Gen. 15:10, 17. Among the Arabs,
אקסם, which means *to divide into parts* and *to cut apart*, also means *to
swear*, because covenants were sanctioned with victims cut apart. Cyril of
Alexandria, in book 10 *Against Julian*, ascribes this custom to the Chaldeans.
Hence the Hebrews say כרת ברית, *to strike* and *to cut a covenant*. This is
not inaptly

suited to the evangelical covenant, which is founded upon the death of Christ,
as it is called in Gal. 3:17, διαθήκη κεκυρωμένη εἰς Χριστόν, *the covenant
ratified in Christ*: a covenant made in Christ wounded and struck, and thus in
that immaculate Lamb who was divided, in whom God was reconciling the world to
himself (2 Cor. 5:19), and through whom, as though passing between the parts,
he confirmed and ratified that covenant.

[Marginal heading: What διαθήκη means.]

III. Among the Greeks, the word for covenant is commonly expressed by
διαθήκην. This can indeed refer to every covenant and compact, as it is taken
in this sense more than once (Luke 1:72; Acts 3:25; 7:8; Rom. 9:4; Gal. 3:15;
4:24; Eph. 2:12; Heb. 8:6, 8, 9, and so forth). But particularly in conjunction
with a covenantal compact, it denotes a *testamentary disposition*. It is taken
in this sense in Matt. 26:28; 1 Cor. 11:25; and especially Heb. 9:15, 16, where
Paul says that “Christ was made the Mediator of the New Testament, so that,
death having intervened for the expiation of the sins committed under the
first testament, those who are called might receive the promise of the
inheritance.”

It was not without divine direction that the Septuagint, whom the apostle here
follows, rendered the word ברית by διαθήκην rather than συνθήκην, which denotes
a simple compact, because the covenant of grace has something compounded of a
*testament and a covenant*. Hence it is aptly called a *testament-covenant*, a
*testamentary covenant*, and a *covenantal testament*.

It is a *covenant*, because an agreement is made between parties in the manner
of a covenant, conditions are laid down on both sides—on God’s part and on the
part of human beings—and a mediator is given to reconcile the estranged
parties. But it is also a *testament*: 1. Because the subject is a covenant in
which an inheritance is promised, and this necessarily requires the prior
death of the testator. For that person is at last considered an heir to whom
goods come by the intervening will of the one who disposes them. Since,
therefore, a διαθήκη is an ἐπαγγελία, *promise*, of such a kind that an
inheritance is obtained by its force, it must be testamentary. Thus the
apostle adds: *Where there is a διαθήκη*—namely, one by which an inheritance is
promised, that is, a *testament*—*the death of the testator must intervene*.
2. Because this covenant was struck solely for our advantage, just as in a
testament it is not the advantage of the testator but that of the heirs which
is regarded. Nor does God covenant concerning mutual benefits, but concerning
his alone. 3. Because, although conditions are proposed on both sides, they are
put into effect by the power and faithfulness of only one party, namely God.
Therefore, it rests upon God’s grace alone, not upon any disposition or merit
of man, as will be proved more fully in its place.

[Marginal heading: Does the word διαθήκη denote only a testament in the New
Testament, or does it also imply a covenant?]

IV. From this it is easy to gather what must be determined in the question
raised here by some concerning the use of the word διαθήκης: From the usage of
the New Testament, and especially Paul, does it signify only a testament and
not also a συνθήκην, that is, a covenant and compact? Since διαθήκης is used
indiscriminately by secular and sacred authors both for a testament and for a
covenant, no solid reason can be given why only the sense of testament should
have a place in Paul to the exclusion of the sense of covenant. Henri Estienne
explains διατίθεμαι by *to make a testament* and *to make a compact*. Guillaume
Budé agrees with him, asserting that διαθήκην is used indiscriminately for a
testament and a covenant: Διατίθεμαι, ἀντὶ τοῦ συντίθεμαι, *I make a compact*.
Hesychius explains διαθήκην by συνωμοσίαν, *a sworn compact*, which is commonly
employed in making a covenant. The Septuagint translators explain the word
ברית by διαθήκην where no meaning other than a compact and covenant can obtain
(1 Sam. 11:1; Gen. 31:44).

V. If the New Testament passages in which the word διαθήκη occurs are
carefully weighed, they not only do not exclude the meaning of συνθήκης,
*compact*, but often necessarily require it. In Luke 1:72 and Acts 3:25,
mention is made of the διαθήκης made with the fathers in Abraham. It cannot be
denied that this had the nature of a covenant, as is evident from Gen. 17:10,
11. Hence the covenant sealed by that sacrament is called

the διαθήκη περιτομῆς, *covenant of circumcision* (Acts 7:8). In Rom. 9:4, the
Jews are said to possess the διαθῆκαι—not, surely, testaments, which were not
numerous, but covenants, which were renewed rather often. In Gal. 4:24, *these
are διαθῆκαι*, two covenants, namely the legal and the evangelical, as will be
proved later. And whenever Christ is called the Mediator of the διαθήκης in
Hebrews (chapters 8 and 9), and his blood the blood of the διαθήκης, although
the idea of a testament is connoted, a covenantal σχέσις, *relation*, cannot
and ought not to be excluded. For properly speaking, testaments do not have
sureties; rather, a mediator is appointed between opposed parties to reconcile
them and unite them with one another through a covenant.

VI. It must indeed be admitted that the testamentary idea is especially
pressed here by the apostle in Heb. 9:15, so that the necessity of Christ’s
death and satisfaction as testator may be designated more clearly, and so that
the efficacy of grace—to which everything in the covenant of grace must be
wholly credited—may be asserted more strongly. Yet this cannot be understood
as excluding the covenantal relation, which requires faith and obedience on
man’s part. Since this is clear in itself and is not denied by the brethren,
the controversy which is raised over it appears futile and vanishes of itself
if both applications of σχέσις, *relation*, the testamentary and the
covenantal, are regarded here: the former as chief and primary, and the latter
as secondary and serving the former.

[Marginal heading: The derivation of foedus.]

VII. Among the Latins, *foedus*, *covenant*, has been given various
derivations. Some, like Festus, maintain that it is derived *a feriendo*, from
striking, because when a compact was made a victim was struck and slain, and
such a death was invoked upon the one who first violated the covenant. Livy
sets out the manner of this in book 1 when he recounts the formula of the
covenant made between the king of the Roman people and the dictator of the
Albans. And Virgil says in *Aeneid* 8:

*And with a slain sow they joined their covenant.*

Others more suitably think it is derived *a fide*, from faith, as Isidore
maintains in book 8 of the *Origins*, because faith was pledged in a covenant.
Hence Ennius, as quoted by Varro, said *fidus* for *foedus*. Indeed, a covenant
is made for no other reason than that faith may stand firm between the parties.

[Marginal heading: Its various senses.]

VIII. God’s covenant does not have only one meaning in Scripture. *Properly*,
it denotes a compact and agreement made between God and man which consists
partly in the stipulation of a duty, or of the thing to be performed, and
partly in the promise of a reward. It is taken in this sense in Gen. 17:2,
where God is said to will to establish his covenant with Abraham.

*Improperly*, it is sometimes taken for a simple promise, as in Gen. 9:9, where
the promise not to destroy the world by a flood is called a covenant.
Sometimes it is taken metonymically for the *symbols* and sacraments of the
covenant, as circumcision is called the covenant in Genesis 17, and in Luke
22:20 *the eucharistic cup is said to be the New Testament in Christ’s blood*,
that is, its sign and seal. Sometimes it is taken for the *tablets of the
covenant*, which were kept in the ark (1 Kings 8:21); sometimes for the
*covenanted parties themselves*, in which sense the people of God are called
the *holy covenant* (Dan. 11:28, 30); and sometimes for the Messiah, the
foundation of that covenant in whom it was made with us, as in Isa. 49:8, *I
gave you as a covenant of the people*, and so forth. But passing over the
improper meanings, we regard the proper one here when we discuss the covenant.

IX. This covenant is called the *covenant of grace*: *causally*, because it
arises from grace strictly so called, such as has place toward a sinner (Luke
1:54, 55, 72, 74; Eph. 1:6; Titus 3:4); *materially*, because everything in
this covenant, even the conditions themselves, is gratuitous (Jer. 31; Ezek.
36); and *finally*, because through it God willed to manifest the riches of
his grace (Eph. 2:7). It is also called *evangelical*, because it is the object
of the doctrine of the gospel

[Printed p. 154 | Internet Archive PDF 182 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

(Rom. 1:16, 17); and *new*, not only because it succeeded the old covenant of
works and because it was renewed in Christ under the New Testament in a far
more glorious manner, but also because it is eternal, immutable, and never to
be abrogated (Heb. 8; Jer. 31; 32).

[Marginal heading: Why the covenant is called the Promise.]

X. Besides these terms, the words ἐπαγγελίας, *promise*, and εὐαγγελίου,
*gospel*, also occur frequently to designate the covenant of grace. First, it
is called ἐπαγγελία, or *promise*, and indeed κατ’ ἐξοχήν, *preeminently*, not
only because it is God’s, not man’s, but also because it is the most excellent
of all, being the only saving promise. Thus it is distinguished from the
promise given to Abraham concerning his bodily offspring in Isaac (Rom. 9:9;
Gal. 4:23), from the inheritance of the land of Canaan—which is therefore
called the *land of promise* (Heb. 11:9)—and from all the other earthly
promises given to the Israelite people. Indeed, it is also distinguished from
the promise added to the law concerning life to be given to the one who
obeyed, which was rendered void through man’s fault once the law had been
violated.

It particularly denotes the evangelical promise concerning the Messiah and
the spiritual and eternal blessings to be bestowed through him. Thus Rom.
4:13, 14 says, “The promise of inheriting the world was not given through the
law,” and so forth; and Gal. 3:16, 17, 18, “If the inheritance is from the law,
it is therefore not from the promise,” namely, the evangelical promise. Hence
the faithful to whom those blessings promised to them in Christ belong are
called *children of the promise* (Rom. 9:8). The Holy Spirit, who is given to
us from that promise and seals it in our hearts, is called the *Spirit of
promise* (Eph. 1:13), and even the *promise of the Father* (Acts 1:14).
Scripture speaks not only of the Promise in the singular but of the Promises
in the plural (Gal. 3:16, 21), either to denote its excellence or because,
though made once, it was repeated often to the fathers.

XI. It was not without reason that the Holy Spirit willed to designate the
covenant of grace by the name *promise*, because the covenant in its entirety
rests upon the divine promise. In this it differs wonderfully not only from
all human covenants, which consist in a mutual obligation and stipulation of
the parties, but also from the covenant of works. Although that covenant also
had its own promise from God’s side to those who obeyed, and thus rested upon
God’s goodness, it nevertheless required obedience from man’s side in order
to be put into effect. But here God willed the whole covenant to depend upon
his promise, not only with respect to the reward promised by God, but also
with respect to the duty required from us, so that God here carries out not
only what belonged to his part, but also what belonged to ours. And if a
covenant is given solely for the happiness of one party, it is kept and
fulfilled by the faithfulness of only one party. Hence not only God’s benefits
but also man’s duties fall under the promise; not only the end, but also the
means and conditions which lead us to it, as will be taught in its place.

[Marginal heading: Why it is called the Gospel.]

XII. The Greek Εὐαγγέλιον, corresponding to the Hebrew בשורה טובה, means any
*good and joyful message*. Yet in Scripture it is applied κατ’ ἐξοχήν,
*preeminently*, to the joyful message concerning Christ’s advent into the
world, the covenant of grace made in him, and the salvation obtained through
him—a message than which none more welcome can be given.

It is not used in only one sense. Sometimes, broadly and generally, it denotes
the *gracious covenant* contracted in Christ. In this sense, when Paul speaks
of the ancient people in Rom. 10:16, he says that *not all obeyed the gospel*,
and in 1 Pet. 4:6 it is said that *the dead were evangelized*. Sometimes it is
taken strictly and specifically, either for the promise and doctrine
concerning Christ to come—in which sense Paul says in Gal. 3:8 that the gospel
was preached beforehand to Abraham when the promises were given to him—or,
more properly, for the doctrine concerning Christ manifested and the
fulfillment of all the promises. In this sense, it is used throughout the New
Testament for the doctrine of Christ’s mission, or the complete gospel, and is
opposed to τῇ ἐπαγγελίᾳ, *the promise*, which denotes simply the doctrine of
the promise of Christ in the Old Testament. And it is called the gospel

[Printed p. 155 | Internet Archive PDF 183 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*promised* in Rom. 1:1, 2. Yet this distinction is not perpetual. For just as
the doctrine of the Old Testament is called the gospel no less than the
doctrine of the New Testament, as has already been said, so the doctrine of
the New Testament is called ἐπαγγελία, or *promise*, no less than the former
(Gal. 3:14; 2 Tim. 1:1).

XIII. There are many other highly emphatic synonymous terms by which this
covenant is customarily expressed. It is called the *Oath* (Luke 1:73),
because God interposed his oath in this covenant to denote its immutability,
which had not been done in the former covenant (Heb. 6:17; 7:21); the
*sure mercies promised to David* (Isa. 55:3), because it rests upon God’s
pure mercy and grace and not upon any human merit; the *law of faith* (Rom.
3:27), because in it faith is prescribed to us as the condition of the
covenant; and the *life-giving Spirit* and *ministry of righteousness* (2 Cor.
3:6, 9), because in it everlasting righteousness in Christ is promised to us
for justification, and Christ’s life-giving Spirit for sanctification.
