QUESTION V.

Whether the Pope is the head of the Church and Christ’s vicar appointed by
Christ.

All the Papists affirm it; we, on the contrary, prove by these arguments that
he is not the head of the Church.

Argument 1. If Peter was not the head of the Church, much less is the Pope.
But the former was proved in Question 2. Therefore. Refer here the arguments
presented in that place.

Argument 2. No invisible body that has an invisible head in heaven can have a
visible head on earth, because the head and the body ought to be of the same
nature. But the Catholic Church, insofar as it is such, is an invisible body,
because what is believed is invisible, Hebrews 11, and even the Papists
themselves concede this, Bellarmine, book 3, *On the Church Militant*, chapter
15. But the Catholic Church is believed. Therefore.

Argument 3. The Catholic Church of which they wish the Pope to be head is
Christ’s one bride, body, kingdom, and so forth. To this body belong both the
triumphant Church and

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 77 | printed col. 155 | bsb10175568]

the militant, both the Church of the Old Testament and that of the New,
Ephesians 2:14, 16; Galatians 3:28. But the Pope is not the head and bridegroom
of the Church Triumphant and of the Old Testament. Therefore, he is not the
head of the Catholic Church.

Argument 4. If the Pope is the head of the Catholic Church, then all the elect
are his members, grafted into him through faith, and from him through faith
they draw spiritual life, motion, sense, and so forth, because these are the
benefits that pass from a head into its members. But the consequent is absurd.
Therefore, so is the antecedent. If, however, they say that this is true of the
principal head, not of a ministerial head and vicar, the following argument is
made to the contrary: every vicar acts for someone absent, but Christ is always
present with the Church, Matthew 28:10. Likewise, a vicar performs the office
of someone absent. Christ’s vicar, therefore, ought by virtue of his office to
perform the things Christ would perform by virtue of his office if he were
present.

Argument 5. The Pope cannot arrogate to himself more right in the Church than
the apostles. But the apostles profess that they are servants of the Church,
2 Corinthians 4:5; Colossians 1:25. Indeed, the Pope in his bulls calls himself
the servant of servants. Therefore, he is not the head.

Argument 6. If Christ’s kingdom is not of this world, while the kingdom the
Pope usurps is of this world, then at least insofar as he usurps earthly
empires he is not Christ’s vicar. For such as the kingdom of a king is, such is
that of his viceroy. The antecedent is evident from John 18:36. The Pope,
however, says that the right of distributing and conferring kingdoms and
empires belongs to him insofar as he is Christ’s vicar and Peter’s successor.
Therefore.

Argument 7. If the authority of head of the Church and vicar of Christ has not
been bestowed upon the Pope by divine right, then he ought not to be
acknowledged as head of the Church. But the former is true, because no one can
produce the divine records for it, since neither did the prophets foretell
that this would be so, nor do the apostles testify that this authority was
given to the Pope. Bellarmine therefore says in book 2, *On the Roman Pontiff*,
that the basis of papal succession is not Christ’s institution, and afterward
that it is not by

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 77 | printed col. 156 | bsb10175568]

divine right that the Roman Pontiff, as such, succeeds Peter in the government
of the whole Church.

Argument 8. If the Pope is not even the bishop of a particular church, then
much less is he universal bishop and head of the whole Church. But the former
is true, because he does not perform the office of a bishop, for he does not
teach, and so forth. Therefore.

Argument 9. If Popes were heads of the Church, then the Catholic Church could
at some time have a heretical head. But the latter is absurd. The proposition
is proved because history testifies that many Popes subscribed to heresies,
Liberius to Arianism, Honorius to the Monothelites, and so forth. Therefore.

Argument 10. If the Pope is the head of the Church, then when there were
several Popes at one time the Church simultaneously had several heads, as when
Liberius and Felix were Popes at the same time. See Nicephorus, book 9, chapter
34, and Sozomen, book 4 of the *Ecclesiastical Histories*, chapter 15, and so
forth. The latter is absurd. Therefore.

Argument 11. If the Pope is the head of the Church, then what will be the head
of the Pope himself on earth? For he wishes to be numbered among the Church’s
members, and no one can be his own head, nor is another head appointed on
earth. Christ alone, therefore, will be his head. But if Christ alone is his
head, how does it happen that Christ cannot also suffice as head for the other
faithful?

Argument 12. By the most ancient fathers the Pope is called only brother,
colleague, fellow bishop, and so forth. Cyprian calls Cornelius, Lucius, and
Stephen Popes in this way, and so forth; see also Epistles 41, 58, and 67.
Therefore.

Argument 13. The bishop of Rome was one of the number of patriarchs. Although
the Roman is called first in order, nevertheless all were equal in power, as
is evident from Nicaea, canon 6; Constantinople, canon 3; Chalcedon, canon 28;
and Trullo, canon 36. Sozomen, book 1, chapter 6, also calls these four
patriarchates primatial sees,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 78 | printed col. 157 | bsb10175568]

and in the councils cited the bishop of Rome is designated no differently
from the others. Indeed, by Chalcedon the bishop of Constantinople is made
equal in power to the Roman.

Argument 14. Primacy is denied to the bishop of Rome by the Third Council of
Carthage, chapter 26: “The bishop of the first see,” it says, “is not to be
called chief of the priests, or high priest, or anything of that kind, but
only bishop of the first see; and not even the Roman pontiff is to be called
universal.” This canon is also cited in the canon law, distinction 99.

Argument 15. If Popes were subject to emperors, then they were not heads of
the Catholic Church, of which the emperors were members. But the former is
true, because an emperor delegated a Pope, as Emperor Constantine delegated
Pope Miltiades in the case of Donatus.

2. An appeal was made from a Pope to an emperor, as from Miltiades to
Constantine.

3. Popes no less than other bishops were summoned with authority by emperors
to councils. See Leo, Epistle 13.

4. When Popes dealt with emperors concerning ecclesiastical matters, they
made the most humble supplications to them, as Leo does in Epistles 24 and 26.

5. Emperors confirmed or invalidated a Pope by their own authority, as Otto
deposed John I XII and ordered Clement II to be ordained. See the rest in
Platina and others.

Argument 16. If it was expressly forbidden to appeal to the Pope, then he is
not the head of the Church. But the former is true, for Nicaea, canon 5,
decreed that a bishop’s judgments were to be examined in the provincial
council of bishops. So too Antioch, canons 6 and 15, and the Council of Milevis,
canon 22: “If,” it says, “bishops think an appeal should be made, let them
appeal only to African councils or to the primates of their

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 78 | printed col. 158 | bsb10175568]

provinces. Whoever thinks an appeal should be made overseas is to be received by
no one within African communion.” When Pope Zosimus, however, contended for
his primacy against the African bishops, he brought forward corrupt canons of
the Council of Nicaea in which it was stated that the Council of Nicaea had
granted the right of appeals to the Roman Pontiff. The African bishops found
this to be false when they consulted authentic copies sent to the Africans
from Constantinople and from Alexandria by Cyril.

Argument 17. An appeal could be made from the judgment of the bishop of Rome,
as Donatus, when condemned by Pope Miltiades, appealed from him. Afterward he
was judged by the bishop of Arles, from whom he also appealed to the emperor,
as is evident from Augustine’s Epistle 162, *To the Donatists*. There Augustine
also teaches that others were delegated by the emperor together with Miltiades
and judged together with him.

Argument 18. When resistance was made to the bishops of Rome, they referred
the judgment to a general synod. Therefore. The consequence is proved: if the
Pope could make a supreme determination himself, he would not have referred
the matter to others. The antecedent is proved: when Emperor Constantius
resisted Pope Liberius in the case of Athanasius, Liberius did not boast of the
authority of the universal head but requested that a council be summoned, as
Theodoret, *History*, book 2, chapter 16, reports. Pope Innocent did the same in
the case of Chrysostom, as Sozomen, book 8, chapter 26, reports, and Pope Leo
in the case of Anatolius, Epistle 33 to Emperor Theodosius Augustus.

Argument 19. In former times one could dissent from the bishop of Rome on
matters of faith while preserving the unity of the Church. Thus Irenaeus,
bishop of Lyons, sharply rebuked Pope Victor because he wished to
excommunicate the Asian churches over a disagreement about Easter, as Eusebius
testifies in book

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 79 | printed col. 159 | bsb10175568]

5 of his *History*, chapter 24.

Argument 20. Despite the bishop of Rome’s opposition, other bishops performed
their office and were deposed. Thus, according to Cyprian, Epistle 68,
Basilides was deposed by the bishops of Spain and restored by Pope Stephen,
though Cyprian teaches that this restoration was not to be accepted, and so
forth.

Argument 21. Councils framed canons despite the opposition of the Roman
bishop, as the African council did despite the opposition of Popes Zosimus,
Boniface, and Celestine. Therefore.

Argument 22. Pope Pius II, who was also called Aeneas Sylvius, says in Epistle
301 that each man lived for himself and that little regard was had for the
Roman Church. If the Roman bishop had been appointed head, the primitive
Church would not have been unaware of it or neglected him.

Argument 23. The ancient Roman bishops refused to have the primacy conferred
upon them. Thus Gregory the Great, book 4, Epistle 36: “None of my
predecessors,” he says, “consented to use this most profane title”—namely,
universal bishop. And to Eulogius, patriarch of Alexandria, he says: “Behold,
in the preface of the epistle you directed to me, who forbade it, you used the
word of a proud appellation, calling me universal Pope. I ask that you do this
no more, because what is furnished to another beyond what reason requires is
subtracted from you.” And in book 9, Epistle 30, to Emperor Maurice, he says:
“I therefore say with confidence that whoever calls himself universal priest,
or desires to be so called, is in his self-exaltation a forerunner of
Antichrist.”

Argument 24. “Whoever,” says Chrysostom in Gratian, canon *Many Priests*,
“desires primacy on earth will not be counted among Christ’s servants.”
Likewise Bernard, book 2, *On Consideration*: “By what right,” he says, “does
the Pope claim these things for himself? Certainly not by apostolic right, for
Peter could not give what he did not possess. But he gave to his

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 79 | printed col. 160 | bsb10175568]

successors what he did possess: solicitude for the churches.”

Argument 25. A heretical Pope can be deposed by the Church, according to
Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 30. Therefore, at least in some case he is subject
to the Church.

Argument 26. The Greek churches never acknowledged the Pope’s primacy,
according to Eusebius, book 5, chapters 22 and 23, and the *Tripartite History*,
book 9.

Argument 27. If the Roman bishop possessed no jurisdictional power beyond the
other bishops, then he is not acknowledged as head of the Church. But the
former is true. Therefore. The assumption is proved because the Church’s
jurisdiction has four parts: 1. The ordination of ministers.

2. The convocation of councils.

3. The adjudication of appeals.

4. Censures. But in none of these did the Roman bishop possess power beyond
the others. Therefore. The assumption is proved. He possessed no such power
in ordination, because all the ancient synods direct bishops to be ordained by
their own metropolitans, and testify that the bishop of Rome was never called
upon except within his own patriarchate. Indeed, not even in Italy were
metropolitans admitted by the Pope. Thus Constantius of Milan was admitted
without the Pope’s authority, because, according to the Council of Nicaea, he
initially had care only of the suburban churches. Nor did he possess it in the
convocation of councils, because, according to Bellarmine, book 1, *On
Councils*, chapter 11, provincial synods ought to be convened by metropolitans,
but general synods by the emperor alone. Thus Constantine summoned the Synod
of Nicaea, according to Eusebius, book 3, chapter 6, and Theodosius summoned
the second universal council, namely the First Council of Constantinople,
according to Sozomen, book 7, chapter 7. Nor did he possess it in the
adjudication of appeals, as was proved above. Finally, he possessed no such
power in censures, because Popes were no less subject to the censures of
others than others were to theirs. Thus Irenaeus sharply rebuked Pope

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 80 | printed col. 161 | bsb10175568]

Victor. Cyprian inveighs against Stephen with great harshness of words in his
epistle to Pompeius. Honorius was judged and condemned by the Sixth Synod, and
so forth.

Argument 29. The legates of the Roman Pontiff who were present at councils did
not preside and did not sit in first place. Thus at the Synod of Nicaea Vitus
and Vincentius, who were present in the name of Pope Julius, sat only in fourth
place. At the First Council of Ephesus, over which Cyril of Alexandria
presided, the legates of the Roman bishop sit. At the Second Council of
Ephesus, over which Dioscorus of Alexandria presided, the legates of Pope Leo
were present. Although they contended for the first see, it was not granted to
them. At the Fifth Council of Constantinople, the patriarch Menas presided
with the Roman legates present. Hence disputes about primacy later arose
between Rome and Constantinople.

Argument 30. Popes took care to be preferred to other churches by edicts of
emperors, as in distinctions 96 and 97, canons *Constantinus* and *Primo*. The
Pope was set over them by Emperor Constantine so that he might be called head
of the Church; afterward power over the Greeks was granted by Emperor Phocas
and power over the Gauls by Pepin.

Argument 31. The Council of Chalcedon, canons 9 and 17, decreed as follows: “If
a cleric has a case against a cleric, it is to be judged by the bishop; if
against a bishop, by the archbishop; if against an archbishop, by the primate
of the diocese or by the bishop of Constantinople.” Here the final judgment is
assigned to the bishop of Constantinople. According to John of Torquemada on
the canon *If a Cleric*, by *primate of the diocese* is meant a bishop of
higher rank than an archbishop and lower rank than a patriarch, not the Pope,
as Bellarmine would have it, book 2, chapter 22.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 80 | printed col. 162 | bsb10175568]

Argument 32. The Sixth Universal Synod prescribed a law to the Roman Church:
in canon 13, that it permit priests, deacons, and subdeacons to have wives;
and in canon 56, that they not fast on the Sabbath in Lent. What Bellarmine
brings forward against this council in chapter 27 of book 2 is futile.
