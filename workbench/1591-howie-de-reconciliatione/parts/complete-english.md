## On the Reconciliation of Man with God, or, On the Redemption of the Human Race

A theological treatise.

By Robert Howie, a Scot.

To it have been added two disputations by the same author: one on the communion
of the faithful with Christ, the other on the justification of man before God.

Basel, by Sebastian Henricpetri.

[Frame 00007; printed p. 3]

### To the distinguished servant of Christ, Dr. Johann Jakob Grynaeus, faithful bishop and teacher of the church and academy of Basel, his instructor

Robert Howie, a Scot, sends greetings.

Most outstanding of men, you will receive this treatise of mine, which is
rightly dedicated to you on account of your merit. My studies furnished the
occasion for writing it; your merit furnished the occasion for its dedication.
For when, after I had entered upon the study of theology, I judged that it would
contribute greatly to my progress if I gathered into one what the most
distinguished theologians of earlier generations and of our own age had written
on the same article of religion, this labor was born—a labor which I have also
pursued in other parts of Christian doctrine. Since I was writing these things
only for my own use, brevity was my aim. Thus, if I had either observed
something myself or noticed something said piously and learnedly by others, I
merely touched upon it in a brief theorem or aphorism. I wished to begin with
this article of doctrine concerning man's redemption (although in explaining
the others I have applied equal, and sometimes even greater, diligence,
especially in those matters concerning which

[Frame 00008; printed p. 4]

there is now controversy), because of both its dignity and its usefulness. In
this treatise I have chiefly followed Dr. Caspar Olevianus, of pious memory, a
supreme theologian and my teacher: to others I owe much; to Olevianus, very
much. But to you, most illustrious man, I wished to offer these first fruits of
mine as witnesses of my love and esteem for you. During these three years I
have had you as my most learned and excellent teacher and patron; and to that
cultivation of mind which I received from your most learned lectures and
disputations have also been added your many other great kindnesses toward me.
I therefore wished this to stand as a testimony both to my gratitude toward you
and to a mind mindful of benefits received. I ask that you receive this slight,
yet sincere, expression of my esteem for you in the spirit in which it has
proceeded from me. Since I trust that, in accordance with your singular
kindness and the love with which you embrace me, you will do so, I shall not
entreat you with any more words, lest I seem to doubt your goodwill toward me.
May the Lord Jesus bless your life and your holy labors. Basel, 20 January
1591.

### To John Johnston, a most learned man, his beloved countryman and brother

Robert Howie sends greetings.

Many men endowed with outstanding ability and singular knowledge of Holy
Scripture have, both in earlier centuries and in our own, fruitfully presented
to the Christian church a sacred theology composed methodically from the
canonical books of both Testaments. They judged that it would contribute very
greatly to the benefit of those whom they had undertaken to teach if they
gathered into a single summary the things proposed in various passages of Holy
Scripture concerning the same article of the Christian religion, and set out
for inspection at a single glance a complete explanation of the subject—one
delivered in different places, certainly, but everywhere in Scripture in
precisely the same sense. With this purpose Clement of Alexandria, a teacher in
that celebrated Alexandrian school, published his books of *Stromata*. With the
same purpose Origen, who taught in that school, produced four books *On First
Principles*. There is also a learned exposition of the Creed under Cyprian's
name. Gregory Nazianzen left four books *On Theology*, though they are chiefly
occupied with questions about the Trinity. Augustine, who seems far to have
surpassed those earlier men in knowledge of Holy Scripture, left us his
*Enchiridion to Laurentius* and many other books, in which he explained very
solidly many—and indeed especially difficult—articles of the Christian
religion. Several centuries after him came Damascenus, who produced four books
*On the Orthodox Faith*, which Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples translated. Several
centuries after Damascenus, Peter Lombard, bishop of Paris, following
Damascenus's example, sought in four books

[Frame 00010; printed p. 6]

which he compiled from sayings of the ancients gathered here and there (whence
he also received the title “Master of the Sentences”) to embrace theology.
Lombard certainly seemed to the scholastics of the succeeding age to have
excelled so greatly in this kind of writing that he alone was judged worthy for
students of Holy Scripture to follow. Yet he obtained this standing beyond his
merit, since, endowed with no discernment, he embraced adulterated and spurious
writings as genuine works of the Fathers; and when he ought to have set forth
Christian doctrine, he proposed things wholly alien to and at odds with Holy
Scripture. In our age—than which there has been none more learned since the
times of the apostles—many have composed very successfully summaries of the
articles of Christian doctrine gathered from the books of Holy Scripture. I
shall say what I think, though without wishing to deprive others of liberty to
judge: no one seems to me to have explained the foundations of our religion
either more solidly or with a better method than John Calvin. To speak of
method, he seems to me to have judged most correctly that, since the Apostles'
Creed is an epitome of the whole Christian religion and is composed according
to a plainly divine method, theology cannot be treated more conveniently or
correctly than if everything is brought into conformity with the method of the
Apostles' Creed. For this reason he himself judged that method to be the one he
should follow in his *Institutes*. When I had entered upon theological study,
stirred by the solidity of its doctrine and the excellence of its method, I so
embraced this *Institution*, composed with singular industry by an excellent
craftsman, that I judged I should follow its guidance in my studies. Yet I
followed it in such a way that I also read others diligently and accurately
noted what they had written in conformity with the rule of heavenly truth.
When I observed that some things were explained more fully by some writers and
that certain things were noted by one which another had entirely passed over,
I began, for my private

[Frame 00011; printed p. 7]

use, to collect what I saw had been piously and prudently noted by them and to
refer it to the same article of doctrine. I joined those materials together in
such a way that I judged an exact method should be observed everywhere. I did
not think it right to depart rashly from the arrangement of the Apostles'
Creed. I followed the arrangement which I had observed Calvin to have executed
more accurately than others, so that I rarely employed a concealment of the
method. A specimen of my private study is now appearing. It is slight, indeed,
and not to be compared with the industry of great craftsmen in this field; yet
I hope it is not useless, since I have labored especially to adduce those things
which seemed to promote true piety and the firm consolation of souls. I have
divided the doctrine of the reconciliation of man with God into two parts. In
the first I treat Christ's merit and efficacy—that is, his priesthood and
kingship—which I call the substance of the covenant. In the second I treat
God's Word and the sacraments, which are the testimonies and seals of the
covenant. In this treatise I deal only with Christ's merit. If I learn that my
study will meet with your approval, I shall add in due course the doctrine of
Christ's efficacy or kingdom and the doctrine of the testimonies of the
covenant, since these subjects extend very widely. I had indeed resolved to
begin farther back, to set forth theology itself in order from its beginning,
and to prefix to this commentary all the matters which, in the order of method,
precede the doctrine of man's reconciliation with God. But I decided to tear
this small portion of my theological commonplace-book from the whole body and
send it to you, so that if I perceived that this labor of mine did not
displease you—whose judgment I shall regard as that of many—I might approach
the remaining subjects with greater confidence and eagerness. Since I had to
treat the person of Christ in this work, you will see the doctrine of the
ubiquity of Christ's body noted and refuted with great restraint and without
attacking anyone.

[Frame 00012; printed p. 8]

Nor did the matter seem to demand my own labor, for just as this monstrous
dogma was born in Germany, so it has been refuted by none more learnedly or
solidly than by the Germans themselves. Books on this controversy have
certainly come from that academy in which you lived in former years, written
with such learning that posterity will give splendid testimony to German
erudition. I have noted that the cause which moved them to devise ubiquity was
their desire to establish the real presence of Christ's body in the bread of
the holy Supper. For since the institution of the Lord's Supper was plainly
contrary to their opinion—and Christ had not said, “Here is my body,” but had
called the bread his body; nor had he said, “These are my body,” pointing,
namely, to the bread and the body in the bread—they wished to use a certain
shortcut to establish that presence of Christ's body in the bread. They
therefore devised a monstrous ubiquity, as though it ought not to seem strange
that Christ's body is in the bread since it is everywhere in all the creatures
of the world. They afterward fortified ubiquity with their preliminary
syllogisms—namely, the real communication of properties, as they call it, and
a fictitious majesty by which they overthrow the reality of Christ's human
nature, although they shamelessly commend this majesty by calling it the
session at the right hand of the Father. It is said most truly that, once one
absurdity is granted, infinitely many follow. What else moved them to force
this ubiquity upon the churches except that, overwhelmed by the force of truth,
they saw that the bodily presence of Christ in the bread could not be
established from the words of the Supper? Why do they not instead acknowledge
with us the familiar manner of speaking customarily observed in the
sacraments? What is more, even they cannot establish their presence in the
bread without a trope—from which they wish to appear to recoil most violently.
For when they say that what is signified by the word “bread” as the subject in

[Frame 00013; printed p. 9]

that proposition is the body in the bread—although falsely—do they not
acknowledge a metonymy? Others have called it synecdoche, rather ineptly, since
the body is neither the whole nor a part of the bread. There is no doubt that
God so punished their blindness that, because they refused to approach sound
words, he permitted them finally to fall into Eutychianism. Since I was about
to write to you at Geneva during these days, I wished to send you this treatise
of mine together with my letter, commended to you, as I hope, both by me and by
Dr. Grynaeus, whom I have had as teacher and patron during these years. In
keeping with your singular learning and the very close bond between us, please
take care to admonish me freely and boldly if there are any matters about which
you judge I ought to be warned. If you do so, I shall reckon that I have
received this as an extraordinary fruit of our friendship. Farewell. Basel, 20
January 1591.

## On the Reconciliation of Man with God

### Chapter I

That reconciliation with God was necessary for man fallen into sin, and that
its author could have been none other than God himself.

#### First aphorism

1. Since through the fall man has forfeited his right arising from creation,
and consequently the hope of eternal life which God had promised, man must
either remain perpetually subject to eternal death or establish a new covenant
with God in place of this violated covenant, if he wishes to recover that lost
right to life.

2. Yet man could not accomplish this, since he did not even dare approach God
(for he fled from him, as the example of Adam, who hid himself at God's voice,
teaches), much less negotiate his own reconciliation with God. It was therefore
necessary for our salvation that God descend to us, since we could not ascend
to him. Hence it was necessary that this covenant be gracious, so that God
would not deal with us according to our previous merits. [Marginal reference:
Genesis 3:8.]

3. But since God is just, and his justice once offended cannot tolerate impious
sinners or cultivate friendship with those who would offend his majesty more
and more every day—and even if he had entirely forgotten his justice, men
would still not have approached the God whom they hated, nor ever have allowed
themselves to be persuaded that God loved them—there was needed [Marginal
references: Deuteronomy 4:24; Hebrews 12:29.]

[Frame 00015; printed p. 11]

a Mediator who, with respect both to God and to us, would remove all these
seeds of dissension and reconcile the estranged parties.

4. The Mediator's office with respect to God was therefore to intercede with
God for us; to plead for the removal of guilt; to offer himself to make
satisfaction; actually to satisfy by paying the penalties due for our sins; to
pray to God and obtain from him the acceptance of this satisfaction as a price
sufficiently worthy, on account of which he would forgive our sins and regard
us as sons; and, finally, to pledge that we would cease to sin any further.

5. With respect to men his office was this: to disclose God's decree concerning
our salvation (for unless this occurred we would be ignorant of God's will,
and there is no desire for what is unknown); to impute that satisfaction to us;
to give the Holy Spirit so that we might recognize and embrace so great a good
(for no reconciliation can exist or be entered between estranged parties
unless each party consents); through the same Holy Spirit to bring it about
that we at last cease to sin; to give us perseverance in this obedience begun;
and finally to raise and glorify us.

6. Since these things were necessarily required for man's salvation—things so
far beyond human accomplishment that they could never even have entered men's
minds—God himself discovered the means of reconciling himself with us and
establishing a new covenant. For he sent us his only-begotten Son to be our
Redeemer, and he willed through the Holy Spirit to renew us to the original
image. Thus this reconciliation with God was made by God and through God.

7. First Peter 1:2 teaches how the hypostases in the divine essence concur in
accomplishing one and the same work of our salvation while each nevertheless
retains his own peculiar action. The Father elects us—that is, appoints us to
salvation in the Son. The Son redeems those given to him and sprinkled with his
blood. The Holy Spirit sanctifies those brought to the knowledge of the Son.

[Frame 00016; printed p. 12]

### Chapter II

That the supreme justice, mercy, wisdom, and truth of God are seen in the
reconciliation of God and man.

#### First aphorism

1. First, the supreme justice of God shines forth in our redemption, because he
does not receive sinners into favor unless satisfaction is made for sins. For
God said in Exodus 23:7, “I will not justify the wicked,” and therefore he
punished in his Son the sins of all men who are to be saved. [Marginal
reference: Isaiah 53.]

2. It appears next in the fact that he does not acknowledge men as sons unless
they are clothed with the perfect obedience and righteousness of Jesus Christ.

3. From these points it is evident—and will become still clearer later—how
great an evil sin is. No remedy other than the death and intercession of the
Son of God could be employed to expiate it, for neither angels nor men could
make that expiation. Indeed, it could not be expiated even by the eternal death
of men, since they would never emerge from death. Hence Bernard said that our
wounds were deep, since they could be healed only by the wounds of the Son of
God. [Marginal references: Isaiah 66:24; Matthew 25:41.]

4. Second, God's mercy shines forth because the Father himself sent his
only-begotten Son to die for his enemies. He did not allow himself to be moved
to exercise just wrath against us either by the evil works we committed before
regeneration or by those we would commit after regeneration. On the contrary,
from his sole and infinite grace, moved by the unworthiness and misery of his
creatures, he willed to have mercy upon them. [Marginal references: Romans
5:6–8; 8:32; 1 John 4:9; Ephesians 2:4–6.]

5. These circumstances greatly commend God's mercy: first, that we were
ungodly and his enemies. What godly man in the world would be willing to die
for a desperate scoundrel? Second, that he gave his Son. Third, that he gave
his only-begotten Son. Fourth, that he had mercy upon us rather than upon the
angels, although they were more excellent creatures. [Marginal reference:
Romans 5:8.]

[Frame 00017; printed p. 13]

6. God's mercy also shines forth in this: he willed to restore us to a state
far better than the one in which the first man stood. The first man could fall
from his state, as the fall proved, but we will not be able to fall from ours.
As we shall hear in its proper place, the obedience of the Son of God—which is
far more excellent than the obedience of a mere man—serves this end.

7. So merciful is God, and so manifestly does his mercy shine forth in our
redemption, that even if all men had remained in their original integrity and
only one of those whom he predestined to salvation had fallen, the Son of God
would nevertheless have descended from heaven, left those ninety-nine sheep,
sought the one which had strayed, and carried it home on his shoulders (Luke
15:4).

8. Third, God's wisdom shines forth because in his infinite wisdom God so
tempered his justice and mercy in the work of our redemption that he remains
at once infinitely just and infinitely merciful: supremely just, because he
punishes our sins with eternal death and remits none; supremely merciful,
because he does not punish them in us, but in our surety whom he himself gave
us, and so forgives us everything. This is that wondrous wisdom of Christians
which plainly surpasses all knowledge. Human minds are not sufficient for an
exact knowledge of these things. Angels delight to gaze into this wisdom.
Indeed, so great is it that both we and all the angels will have something to
learn and weigh in it for eternity. [Marginal reference: 1 Peter 1:12.]

9. Finally, God's truth also shines forth here. He was found truthful both in
the threat, “Dying you shall die,” and in the fact that, after the fall, out of
infinite mercy he promised us a Mediator. For in the fullness of time he sent
this Mediator into the world, who both underwent and swallowed up for us the
death which God had threatened. [Marginal references: Genesis 2:17; 3:15.]

10. These were the reasons why God did not will to employ absolute omnipotence
in vindicating the bride for his Son, namely, in redeeming the church.

[Frame 00018; printed p. 14]

He could indeed have freed us from Satan's power without any difficulty, and
even reduced Satan himself to nothing just as he had created him from nothing.
But in that case a rupture would have been produced within God's own form. For
just as he is omnipotent, so he is just and truthful in punishing sins; he
therefore willed his omnipotence to concur with his other virtues. Indeed, his
omnipotence united with justice shone forth most especially when he willed,
through what appears most powerless in him (for God never restrained his
omnipotence more than when he died in the flesh, and indeed in flesh belonging
to himself), to free us from death and from the Devil, who held the dominion of
death. [Marginal references: 1 Corinthians 1; Hebrews 2:9.]

11. Moreover, if he had employed absolute omnipotence, how would the Son's love
for mankind and the Father's supreme love have appeared? Likewise, how would
that infinite wisdom of God in our redemption have shone forth—a stumbling
block to Jews and foolishness to Greeks, yet wiser than men themselves?
[Marginal references: Titus 3:4; 1 Corinthians 1:23–24.]

12. Finally, if he had employed absolute omnipotence, how would God, who said,
“Dying you shall die,” have been found truthful? How would the Scriptures have
been fulfilled, as Christ himself says? [Marginal reference: Matthew 26:54.]

### Chapter III

That man's reconciliation with God is called in Scripture a gracious covenant
and God's testament.

#### First aphorism

1. This whole work of our reconciliation with God is called our redemption,
and also a new covenant or gracious covenant—that is, a contract, obligation,
or pact. It is likewise called a marriage and a testament, and finally the
gospel, because its proclamation is most welcome and full of joy. [Marginal
references: Jeremiah 31:31; Hebrews 10:16.]

2. This reconciliation between God and men was made in the form of a covenant
so that men might be more certain and secure concerning God's favorable will
toward them. For just as [Marginal references: Ezekiel 16; Hosea 2:1, 2, 20.]

[Frame 00019; printed p. 15]

among men divided by deadly hatred, hatred and enmity are not laid aside until
they have promised in a covenant sanctioned by oath that they will cultivate
peace and put away enmity and hatred, so after God willed to bind himself to us
in the form of a covenant, hatred was removed and security and tranquility were
left to consciences. [Marginal references: Hebrews 7:17, 15.]

3. The gracious covenant is God's promised and sworn gift of himself—of
himself, I say, as our God who will never be angry with us and who recreates us
in his image through and on account of Christ the Mediator. [Marginal
references: Isaiah 54; Jeremiah 31; Hebrews 6:13.]

4. It is called a gracious covenant with respect to us, who, being unworthy and
enemies, could not merit his grace, but not with respect to Christ, for our
salvation cost Christ a sufficiently great price. The name also distinguishes
it from the covenant which God made with Adam in creation, later disclosed in
the Law, by which Adam was bound to render perfect obedience to God from his
own powers.

5. In this covenant God gives himself to us as God, so that God himself, who is
the supreme good, may be ours and the portion of our inheritance. Hence we are
heirs of all things and all things are ours, because all things belong to God,
who is ours, and all things that are in him are ours. Lest we think anything
will be lacking to us in him, he calls himself *Shaddai*, that is,
all-sufficient, so that his omnipotence serves us and keeps watch over our
salvation, his justice frees us from the oppression of the ungodly, and his
mercy perpetually preserves us and our children. Indeed, he lowers himself so
far as to affirm that he will have enemies and friends in common with us.
[Marginal reference: Genesis 12:3.]

6. He confirmed in actual fact that he gives himself to us as God in Christ
when he joined his Son to our flesh, so that the Son began then to be ours and
the proper possession of our flesh.

7. God indeed covenants with those joined in covenant and stipulates from them
that they walk before him—that is, faith—and that they be upright in
life—that is, repentance. Hence theologians commonly establish two parts of
the covenant of grace:

[Frame 00020; printed p. 16]

the promise of grace and the reciprocal pledge of a good conscience, the former
on God's part and the latter on ours. But because God liberally confers upon us
the very thing which he stipulates from us, and through the prophet Jeremiah
says that he himself will inscribe his laws upon our hearts, we must therefore
come down to the promise of grace alone, since this reciprocal pledge is itself
a part of that grace.

8. Indeed, if God wished to exact these things from our own powers, everything
would be void. For even if God willed to bind himself to give us eternal life
on condition that only once throughout our entire life we thought something
which could please him, we cannot think anything good, much less put it into
action. Since God knew this well, just as he willed the promise of grace to be
gracious, so also he willed the reciprocal pledge of obedience to be gracious,
because he himself decreed to bestow upon us what he stipulates from us. It is
equally difficult for us, from ourselves, to believe the gospel and begin new
obedience as it is perfectly to fulfill the Law. [Marginal reference: 2
Corinthians 3:5.]

9. Hence those conditions whose fulfillment God requires from us are called
evangelical, not legal, because they are not demanded from our own powers. For
God commands and, in commanding, brings about in the elect the very thing he
commands. Thus Augustine says in *On the Spirit and the Letter*, chapter 13:
“Through the law of works God says, ‘Do what I command’; through the law of
faith we say to God, ‘Give what you command.’” And in *Confessions*, book 10,
chapter 29: “Give what you command, and command what you will.”

10. Therefore this covenant is gracious in every respect. For God gives
himself to us freely, and whatever we render in return comes from him.

11. We say that these things take place through and on account of Christ the
Mediator, because he is the covenant's surety and arbiter, through whom the
estranged parties came together, and by whose obedience satisfaction was made
to God's justice. Moreover, because this covenant was established by Christ's
death, it is called a testament, for through his death we obtain [Marginal
references: Hebrews 12:24; 9:8, 16–17.]

[Frame 00021; printed p. 17]

these rights and privileges: that we are sons of God. Hence throughout the
Epistle to the Hebrews the apostle uses the word *diathēkē* instead of
*synthēkē*. It will meanwhile be the duty of a diligent interpreter to
distinguish these terms appropriately when the subject requires it.

12. This covenant therefore differs from other covenants in this: the others
are broken apart and terminated by death; this one never is, but is actually
confirmed by death. It is therefore rightly called a testament.

13. Properly speaking, then, the testator is the Son of God. The heirs are all
believers. The inheritance is all the goods acquired for us through Christ's
death. The tablets of the testament are the sacred books, or Holy Scripture.
The seals are the sacraments, and in the New Testament specifically Baptism and
the Supper.

14. Because we are also called heirs of God the Father, so that he too is a
testator, some have said that this is an additional reason why this covenant is
called a testament and the heavenly goods our inheritance: we have no less a
right to those goods than children to whom their parents' goods have fallen by
death possess a right to those goods. [Marginal references: Romans 8:17;
Galatians 4:7.]

15. The ends of the gracious covenant are two. The first and highest is the
glory of God's mercy. The second is our adoption as sons, so that our souls may
possess tranquility and peace, and so that in this life we may serve God with
joyful and eager devotion and with true humility of mind. This is what the
angels sang: “Glory to God in the highest, and peace on earth.”

### Chapter IV

That God's gracious covenant is eternal and one.

#### First aphorism

1. The perpetuity of this covenant includes a threefold eternity: that of God
who promises; that of the covenanted men, indeed of every single individual;
and third, that of the grace and benefits which are offered and confirmed by
the force of this covenant.

[Frame 00022; printed p. 18]

This eternity is manifest from the following testimonies of Scripture. Isaiah
54:9 and following; Psalm 89:29 and following. There the Lord also meets the
objection that we might perhaps think our sins would make God's promise void.
He answers, “By no means. But if his children forsake my Law, then I will visit
their transgression with the rod and their iniquity with stripes; yet I will
not remove my mercy from him, nor prove false to my truth.”

Likewise Jeremiah 31:31–32 and chapter 33. There God says that he is unwilling
to turn back or offer as excuses that he was deceived and that we failed to
observe or fulfill the conditions of the covenant. You will observe there that
in Isaiah and Jeremiah God illustrates the immutability of his covenant by
comparison with similar things, namely, things immutable in nature. Indeed, he
does so in such a way that he finally descends to a comparison of unequal
things, as follows: “Just as the sun and moon will shine perpetually, so my
covenant too will remain perpetually in force. The mountains may indeed be
moved, and heaven and earth will pass away, but my covenant will neither totter
nor be moved.” David declares the firmness of the covenant by the same
comparison in the cited passage of Psalm 89.

Psalm 110; Hebrews 7. The firmness of this covenant is shown by the oath added
to it. God speaking must certainly be believed, but who will refuse to believe
him when he swears? God willed thus to provide for our weakness so that our
consciences might rest in him more securely. For because of the former fall by
which we offended God, our weakness and unbelief were so great that it was
difficult to persuade us of God's favorable will toward us.

In Galatians 3 Paul confirms this same eternity of the covenant with an
argument from the lesser. If a man's testament remains firm and is not
abrogated, how will God's testament be abrogated? In this argument he also
reproves the Galatians for not understanding it, saying, “I speak after the
manner of men.” That is, “I shall use an example taken from the ordinary and
common practice of men, so that you may be ashamed to attribute less to God's
covenants than to men's.”

Genesis 17:7 says that he will also be the God of our offspring. This shows
that he will be God to us and our posterity not in one age only but in all
succeeding ages—an argument by which Christ convicted the Sadducees of error.

In Romans 5:9–10 and Hebrews 7 the apostle uses this argument: if he freely
loved his enemies, much more will he perpetually love those who have now been
made his friends. If he loved us then,

[Frame 00023; printed p. 19]

when he had not yet overcome death, and therefore willed to save us through
death, much more will he, now living, cherish and love us perpetually, since he
ever lives in order to intercede for us.

2. God took from himself, who is immutable, the cause of the love with which he
pursues us, and not from us or our powers or merits. From this, I say, it follows
that this covenant is eternal and immutable. [Marginal reference: Hosea 14:5.]

3. Because of this covenant's immutability, God did not will Circumcision and
Baptism, the sacraments of our admission into God's covenant, to be repeated
and performed again.

4. And although the seals of the covenant instituted formerly have been
changed, the covenant itself has not. Indeed, those very seals, such as
Circumcision and Passover, were not so much abolished as exchanged for other,
more significant seals. They therefore detract nothing from the firmness of
the covenant.

5. This covenant is called one because it is the one and only means by which
men are freed from Satan's tyranny and the slavery of sin, and because no one
has ever been saved in any other way than through this mercy of God which is
seen in this covenant and gracious contract. Therefore the covenant made with
Adam is repeated and confirmed with Abraham; likewise with David and with the
faithful in Jeremiah during the Babylonian captivity. New and different
covenants concerning the salvation of the human race are not sanctioned and
erected. [Marginal references: Hebrews 4:2; 13:8.]

6. Although the promise was one if one looks at the reality itself, it was
nevertheless repeated by God many times. This is why in Ephesians 2:12 Paul
calls this gracious covenant *diathēkai* in the plural.

### Chapter V

That this covenant of God is distinguished, with respect to its differing
circumstances, into the Old and New Testaments, and concerning the agreement of
the two.

#### First aphorism

1. Although this gracious covenant is one and its surety and mediator is one,
it is nevertheless distinguished with respect to men

[Frame 00024; printed p. 20]

and its circumstances into Old and New. Hence two covenants are spoken of,
which are commonly called the Old and New Testaments. To understand this, we
must consider both their unity and their diversity. [Marginal reference:
Galatians 4:24.]

2. The apostle demonstrated beautifully that the covenant of the ancients and
ours is the same when he compared the church to a tree whose root is Christ.
Thus both they and we adhere as branches in the same covenant, but with this
difference: they are natural branches and we are grafted ones. Let us examine
this identity more closely.

3. There is unity and agreement in all the causes of the covenant. The
efficient cause of both the Old and New Testaments was God's mercy alone. Moses
testifies to this repeatedly, asserting God's mercy in opposition to the merits
of the Jews. [Marginal references: Deuteronomy 8:17–18; 9:5–6.]

4. It is certain that Christ the Mediator was the covenant's foundation. We
have already demonstrated that God cannot receive men into favor except
through a Mediator. Below, from his person and office, it will be demonstrated
that Christ is that Mediator and that he alone is. Hence the apostle said in
Hebrews 13:8 that Christ is the same today, yesterday, and forever. Elsewhere
he shows that nothing other was evangelized to the Jews than what is
evangelized to us, and that precisely the same thing is proclaimed to us as to
them. In Hebrews 9:15 he plainly declares that the sins committed under the old
covenant were removed by the blood of the new covenant's surety. All the
apostles confirm this same truth: both Gentiles and Jews are saved through the
one Christ. Moreover, all God's promises in Christ were “yes” and “amen,” for
it was solely on account of Christ that God bestowed benefits upon men.
[Marginal references: Hebrews 4:2; Acts 15:9; 2 Corinthians 1:20.]

5. Because in the first promise the Mediator was called the seed, it follows
that in the repetition of this covenant all the way to David's time the
Mediator is continually called the seed, as though the first promise were being
pointed out with a finger. After David's time he is more frequently called the
flower, tree, branch, king, and shoot of David. Indeed, by a metonymy of the
efficient cause the Messiah himself is commonly called David.

[Frame 00025; printed p. 21]

6. The prayers of the faithful under the Old Testament testify that they knew
well that they had been received into God's favor through and on account of
Christ the Mediator. In these prayers they ask God to give them this or that,
saying, “Remember, Lord, the covenant which you made with Abraham, Isaac, and
Jacob. Act, Lord, for the sake of your servant David.” This is the same as if
they had said, “Give us this for the sake of the Messiah or Christ.” The point
is far more manifest in Daniel's prayer. [Marginal reference: Daniel 9:17.]

7. Augustine therefore rightly says against Adimantus, the disciple of Mani:
“Before the Lord's advent, in accordance with the wondrous and most orderly
distribution of the times, that people who received the Old Testament were
held under certain shadows and figures of realities. Yet in it there is so
great a proclamation and foretelling of the New Testament that no commands or
promises, however difficult and divine, are found in evangelical and apostolic
teaching which are absent from those ancient writings as well.”

8. The apostle testifies that the Israelites not only had with us the same
substance of the covenant but also the same seals of the covenant—Baptism and
the Supper. Some diversity certainly appears in the material of the signs, but
that does not enter into consideration here. In a wedding ring used to contract
an obligation, it is not considered whether it is gold or silver, but rather
its end and the promise set before it, which it is employed to confirm,
whatever its material may be. The same principle is considered in the
sacraments. [Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians 10:2–4.]

9. Paul shows in Hebrews 11 that its form was one and the same: namely, faith,
through which we cleave to God. For on the other side God stipulates faith from
us, without which it is impossible to cleave to God. Thus Christ says,
“Abraham rejoiced to see my day, and he saw it”—namely, by faith. [Marginal
references: Hebrews 11:6; John 8:56.]

10. It must now be confirmed with several arguments that the end of the Old
Testament was the same as that of the New—namely, with respect to men, adoption
and blessed immortality, not earthly pleasures or de-

[Frame 00026; printed p. 22]

lights. This must be shown against certain madmen who think that God had in view
nothing other than the land of Canaan which he promised them under the Old
Testament. We shall show that God promised them eternal life as adopted sons
and that the ancients expected this very life from God's hand. [Marginal
reference: 1 Peter 1:23.]

First, all those whom God so dignifies with participation in his Word that they
embrace it, he also makes alive. For the end of the Word is that through
knowledge of it he should unite himself to them, and life consists in that
union. But God dignified the Fathers with participation in the Word so that
they embraced it. He therefore also made them alive.

Next, the testimony or formula of the covenant's words proves that the reality
testified to the Fathers was eternal life. The formula of the covenant is this:
“I shall be God to you, and you shall be a people to me.” The prophets testify
that the reality certified by these words is solid blessedness, life, and
eternal salvation. Thus David says, “Blessed is the people whose God is
Jehovah,” and again, “Blessed is the nation which he chose for himself as an
inheritance.” Habakkuk says, “You are our God; we shall not die.” Moreover,
this formula promises the special presence of God. Therefore it promises life.

In the formula of the covenant he not only testified that he was their God but
also promised that he would always be so. Genesis 15; Hebrews 13:5: “I will not
leave you nor forsake you.” Therefore, since the reality promised was eternal,
he willed that the Fathers' hope should also extend into eternity.

The covenant also says, “I shall be God to you and to your seed after you”
(Genesis 17:7). God therefore regarded the future, and indeed eternal life. The
preliminary syllogism underlying the consequence is twofold. First, if God was
going to declare his benevolence toward the dead Fathers by bestowing benefits
upon their posterity, much less would his favor toward the parents themselves
be lacking. For the case of God and men is not the same: men cannot confer
benefits upon their friends after death and therefore benefit their children,
but the godly always live to God. Second, after their death God himself
testifies that he is the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Once the adjunct is
posited—namely, that he is the God of Abraham—the subject, namely Abraham,
Isaac, and Jacob, must also be posited. It follows, therefore, that the
Patriarchs' happi-

[Frame 00027; printed p. 23]

ness was not terminated by the span of this life, since after their death God
wills to be called their God. With this illustrious argument Christ convicted
the Sadducees of error.

If the life which God promised the Patriarchs was not blessed, it will follow
that God did not keep his promises and that they were wretched—indeed, far
more wretched than any others. First, he promised the land of Canaan to
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, yet they possessed nothing from it except a burial
place. Indeed, their heirs did not possess it until four hundred years after
the first promise was made. Second, how full of miseries was the very life which
the Patriarchs lived in the land of Canaan! So much so that they were frequently
in danger of wasting away from famine in that promised land.

Finally, in their songs the blessed Virgin and Zacharias say that the salvation
revealed in Christ is the fulfillment of the promises God had formerly made to
Abraham and the Patriarchs. It follows, therefore, that with respect to God
promising to the ancients, the end of the gracious covenant was blessed and
eternal life. [Marginal references: Luke 1:55, 72–73; Acts 13:32.]

It must next be demonstrated that the ancients expected from God's hand another
life beyond this one, namely blessed immortality—a point Paul discusses more
fully in Hebrews 11.

In 1 Peter 1:11 the prophets are said diligently to have inquired about the
salvation which is now disclosed through the gospel. They therefore expected
it and knew that they, together with us, would participate in the same mercy of
God.

The Patriarchs acknowledged that they were sojourners and pilgrims on earth.
They therefore expected another home and dwelling.

As a pledge of eternal life, the Fathers wished to be buried in the land of
Canaan, reasoning from the testimony to the reality attested. For the land of
Canaan was to them a pledge of God's promises. By this act they therefore
confirmed that the happiness they expected belonged not to this life but to
another.

From the intended effects it is proved that the aim of the holy Fathers under
the Old Testament was the blessedness of eternal life. This can be seen in the
birthright sought by Jacob, whose aim was either earthly happiness or eternal
happiness. It was not earthly, as the accompanying dangers opposed to earthly
happiness teach. It was therefore eternal, as his words uttered among his final
breaths testify: “I will await your salvation, O Lord.” What salvation
[Marginal reference: Genesis 49:18.]

[Frame 00028; printed p. 24]

would he have expected when he understood that he was breathing out his soul,
unless he had seen in death the beginning of life?

What is more, even the false prophet Balaam knew this when he said, “May my soul
die the death of the upright, and may my end be like his.” [Marginal reference:
Numbers 23:10.]

The same point is proved by David's testimony in Psalm 102 and is concluded in
this syllogism: if the destruction of heaven and earth is not a sufficiently
effective cause to break off the salvation of the godly, it follows that their
salvation is joined to God's eternity. The former is true, as is evident from
verses 26–29 of the psalm. Therefore the latter is also true. This hope would
not be firm unless it rested upon a promise as its object or correlative. That
promise appears in Isaiah 51:6: “The heavens will dissolve like smoke, the
earth will grow old like a garment, and its inhabitants will perish in like
manner; but my salvation will be forever, and my righteousness will not be
broken.” Here perpetuity is attributed to righteousness and salvation not as
they are in God, but as they are received by men. There are innumerable other
testimonies of David.

The same point is evident from Job's testimony: amid his utmost calamities and
miseries he consoled himself with the hope of eternal life. [Marginal
reference: Job 19:25, 33.]

As we approach nearer to Christ—that is, to the full manifestation of God's
promise—everything is seen more clearly, as in the prophets. Here it must be
observed that by outlining God's will in temporal benefits as with certain
strokes, the prophets painted such an image of it as would carry minds beyond
the earth and stir them to meditation upon the future life. Thus temporal
things in the Old Testament commonly look to eternal realities; earthly things
to heavenly realities; and all things must be sought in Christ. Thus in Luke
the angel attributes to Christ's kingdom what is said about Solomon's eternal
kingdom. Thus Peter in Acts transfers to Christ Moses' statement that God would
raise up a prophet for them, although it can also be understood of prophets who
would never be lacking for the people to consult. Thus although the promise
made to Abraham concerning the seed is truly spoken of Isaac, Paul nevertheless
applies it to Christ. So too there are innumerable other promises whose
fulfillment, since it does not correspond on earth, must be sought in eternal
life. This led the Chaldean paraphrast and the Rabbis very often to accommodate
God's promises to the Messiah's kingdom. [Marginal references: Luke 1:32;
Deuteronomy 18:15; Acts 3:22; 7:37; Galatians 3:16.]

The testimonies of Christ and the apostles, which are innumerable, will settle
the whole matter.

[Frame 00029; printed p. 25]

“Many,” says Christ, “will come from east and west and recline with Abraham,
Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven.” Experience confirmed this very
testimony, for many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep rose at Christ's
resurrection. Likewise, in Acts 3 Peter affirms that the Jews of his time are
heirs of evangelical grace because they were sons of the prophets, included in
the covenant which God made with their fathers. Thus far, therefore, it has
been demonstrated that the gracious covenant had the same end for the ancients
and for us. [Marginal references: Matthew 8:11; 27:52–53; Acts 2:39.]

### Chapter VI

On the diversity of the Old and New Covenant or Testament.

We must next discuss the covenant's diversity: namely, how, although it is one
covenant or testament, it comes to be called now Old and now New.

1. Aphorism. The entire diversity of the covenant consists in its circumstances
or manner of administration. This detracts nothing from the unity set forth
above, as will be manifest from a review of its causes.

2. The first unity lay in the source or efficient cause: the inheritance of
eternal life comes to us from God's free mercy, through no merits of theirs or
of ours. The diversity lies in the fact that the manner of leading people to
that source was very different. God led the ancients to that source, or to the
knowledge of that free mercy, much more rigorously by introducing alongside it
the Law or legal covenant, which he pressed upon them. He leads us to the same
fountain of mercy, but without the legal covenant. [Marginal references:
Galatians 3:19; Romans 5:20; John 1:17.]

3. Hence, because of the diversity of this administration, the covenant is
called Old and New, or the Old and New Testament, earlier and later—a division
of the subject according to its circumstances.

4. Thus the Old Testament is nothing other than that solemn manner of
administering the gracious covenant formerly comprehended in the moral Law,
ceremonies, and sacrifices. [Marginal reference: Hebrews 9:1.]

5. This was the manner of administration: through the tutelage [Marginal
reference: 2 Corinthians 3:14.]

[Frame 00030; printed p. 26]

or ministry of the Law they were led as though by the hand into participation
in the gracious covenant, like effects directed toward their end.

6. Hence Christ is called the end of the Law, and Moses is said to have been a
servant for testifying to the things which were to be spoken afterward.
[Marginal references: John 5:46; Romans 10:4; Hebrews 3:5.]

7. Paul expressed the manner of administration clearly and distinctly through
several metaphors when, on account of the end we have described, he compared
the Law to a garrison soldier. For the moral and ceremonial Law fortified them
as with a garrison, lest they follow their own direction and stray outside the
road leading to Christ, who is the end of the Law, or to the knowledge of God's
free mercy. He also compares it to a tutor: the Law was a tutor leading to
Christ. He further compares it to a guardian and steward, because God kept his
church, still immature and less learned, under this guardian until at Christ's
advent—when greater knowledge would come and the body itself be exhibited
after the shadows were abolished—it left its guardianship. [Marginal
references: Galatians 3:23–24.]

8. The Law kept the Jews in their duty by leading them to Christ the Mediator,
and at the same time distinguished them from all others to whom the promise of
the gracious covenant did not extend. Hence the same apostle calls it the
dividing wall. [Marginal reference: Ephesians 2:14.]

9. It is therefore worthy of consideration that God gave the moral and
ceremonial Law for no other end than that the Jews, from acknowledgment of
their sin, might be led to seek salvation in that gracious promise made to them
concerning the blessed seed. [Marginal reference: Galatians 3:19.]

10. When they did not rise to this end, but clung to those very means appointed
to that end—and especially to ceremonies as a work performed—as though they
received life and righteousness through them, God sent to them prophets who
rebuked them most severely and restored them to the way. Thus, seeing these
things twisted by them to an end other than that for which he had instituted
them, God says that he had commanded their fathers nothing about sacrifices.
[Marginal references: Isaiah 66:3; Jeremiah 7:21.]

[Frame 00031; printed p. 27]

11. The moral Law is nothing other than the covenant of creation. In that
covenant God stipulates perfect obedience from us, and indeed from our own
powers, since he gave us sufficiently great powers in the first creation; and
he promises eternal life to those who render that obedience. Because God had
not forfeited his right through man's fall, he repeated this covenant of
creation through Moses, but for the end that follows.

12. In administering his gracious covenant, God willed to use the works of the
Law so that his grace might become better known. For it cannot be known what
adoption and eternal life are, or how glorious the goods conferred upon men in
this gracious covenant are, unless men first understand that they are guilty
of eternal death and alienated from the life of God. But where there is no Law,
sins are not reckoned or recognized for what they are, namely, the cause of
death. [Marginal references: Romans 3:19–20.]

13. Therefore the apostle denies that this was the principal doctrine, but
says that the Law entered alongside, or was added afterward, not to overturn
the gracious covenant but to lead us to it. This tutelage was indeed both quite
harsh and obscure. In contrast, that full manifestation of the gracious
covenant which we call the gospel is both pleasant and clear. [Marginal
references: Romans 5:20; Galatians 3:21.]

14. The gracious covenant which the Jews had with God therefore cannot be
called legal in any sense except by a metonymy of its circumstance, because in
the administration of the gracious covenant God employed the works of the
legal covenant. The Law was not given to keep the Jews within itself, but to
send them on to Christ.

A difficulty: But why is the legal covenant itself called a *diathēkē*, a
testament? Is every pact called a testament by synecdoche? Or is it because the
end for which the Law was given was to lead men to that testament of God, or
that gracious covenant? The latter is more correct.

15. The Law was therefore given to the Jews so that they would not be under the
Law, but would cleave entirely to the promise concerning the blessed seed which
God made to their ancestors.

[Frame 00032; printed p. 28]

16. This is what Augustine said in chapter 19 of *On the Spirit and the
Letter*: “The Law was given that grace might be sought. Grace was given that
the Law might be fulfilled. For the Law was not unfulfilled through a defect
of its own, but through the defect of the wisdom of the flesh. This defect had
to be disclosed through the Law and healed through grace.”

17. The examples of the faithful under the Old Testament testify that this
tutelage was very useful to them. For they fled entirely to Christ as their
Savior.

18. Because under the Old Testament there was not the abundance of the Holy
Spirit which exists under the New, that legal covenant was always pressed more
vehemently, and indeed upon everyone, than it is pressed under the New upon
those who have embraced Christ by faith.

19. Therefore, when the Law attained its end among the ancients by sending
them away from itself to the gospel, it was no longer an obligation to those
who now believed in the Messiah, binding them to render obedience from their
own powers or making them liable to eternal death if they did not render it.
Rather, among those who, believing in Christ, ought to meditate upon the
sanctification of life, it was a prudent instructor in the ordering of life.

20. Just as it pleased God, through the promulgation of the moral Law, to lead
the Israelite people to knowledge of the gracious covenant formerly initiated
with their fathers, so even today he wills the Law to be set before and pressed
by his ministers upon all who either are entirely ignorant of Christ's grace or
do not esteem it as they ought—a Law which they are bound to fulfill from
their own powers.

21. The Law therefore holds obligated all in whom it has not attained its end,
that is, whom it has not yet led to Christ, just as if they had never fallen,
requiring them from their own powers—which God gave them sufficiently great in
the first creation—to render perfect obedience. If they do not render it, the
Law pronounces them subject to the curse, that is, eternal death. If they say
that they cannot fulfill the Law, they say nothing to the purpose, for God did
not lose his right over us because of our fall.

[Frame 00033; printed p. 29]

Since they acknowledge this, why do they not instead flee to Christ, who
fulfilled the Law for us?

22. Among the Jews, for those whom the Law led to place their trust in the
Messiah, the Law was abrogated as an obligation. Much more, therefore, after
the full manifestation of the gospel shone forth following Christ's advent in
the flesh and ascension into heaven, and after the outpouring of the Holy
Spirit upon believers, that harsh tutelage ceased and was abolished.

23. Among the holy Fathers whom the preaching of the Law led to Christ, the
Law continued to have its uses even after they began to cleave to Christ. So
too, although we have embraced Christ, it still greatly benefits us.

24. We therefore cannot adequately proclaim that infinite wisdom of God, who
employed the promulgation of the covenant of creation toward us after we had
already fallen and become transgressors of that covenant, so that it might
serve as a tutor leading us by the hand to the gracious covenant.

25. The ceremonial Law, like a kind of mystical theology, led them to Christ,
and all the ceremonies were occasions for instruction. The apostle calls them
the elements of the world because through them, as through rudiments, God
instructed the ancient world. Their end is explained extensively by the
apostle in the Epistle to the Hebrews. In our doctrine concerning the
testimonies of the gracious covenant, we too shall in due course speak fully
about the use of the moral and ceremonial Law. [Marginal reference: Galatians
4:3.]

26. Because the Old Testament, or the gracious covenant sanctioned with the
ancients, was administered by the moral and ceremonial Law, it follows that
the Old Testament is sometimes understood under the name of the moral Law and
sometimes, indeed very frequently, under that of the ceremonial Law. [Marginal
reference: 2 Corinthians 3:14.]

27. We call the full manifestation of God's grace which shone upon the world
after Christ's advent to earth the New Testament. It is accomplished without
the harsh exaction of the moral Law and the administration of ceremonies.

[Frame 00034; printed p. 30]

28. Looking to this, Christ said that the Law and the Prophets lasted until
John and that from then on the kingdom of God began to be proclaimed by the
gospel, because it had now pleased God to lead us to knowledge of himself by
means different from those by which he formerly led the Jews. For the temple's
veil was torn after Christ's death. [Marginal references: Matthew 11:13; Luke
16:16.]

29. Indeed, the Jews themselves understood that that ancient manner of
administering the covenant would not last forever, but would be abolished at
the Messiah's advent, as is evident from their question in John 1:25. This is
not surprising, since they could easily have learned it from Jeremiah 31 and
David, Psalm 110; Hebrews 7.

30. Because the means which God employed in administering his covenant were
not merely different but even contrary, a comparison and opposition of the Old
and New Testaments drawn from the Law and gospel is commonly made. Second
Corinthians 3. The Old Testament is called deadly because it can do nothing but
envelop the human race in the curse. The New, by contrast, is called an
instrument of life because it restores to favor with God those freed from the
curse. Again, the former is a ministry of condemnation because it prosecutes
everyone as guilty of unrighteousness; the latter is a ministry of
righteousness because it reveals the mercy of God through which we are
justified. This opposition depends upon men's contrary states, namely,
integrity and misery.

31. Meanwhile the doctrine of the Law has not been entirely abolished under
the New Testament. Although it has lost its former use, it still has another
and singular use: under the New Testament it is for Christians the rule and
standard of life according to which they ought to order their actions.
[Marginal reference: Matthew 5:17.]

32. The second unity was in the covenant's foundation, or, so to speak, its
matter; to this we join a third unity in form. The diversity here is twofold.
The first is in the quality of revelation, which is now much clearer, whether
one regards the substance itself or the seals. At many times and in many ways
God formerly spoke to the Fathers through the prophets, but now he has spoken
to us through his Son. Moreover, a past event is much clearer than a future
one. To the Fathers [Marginal reference: Hebrews 1:1.]

[Frame 00035; printed p. 31]

the future death and resurrection of Christ were obscurely presented through
external ceremonies, such as sacrifices and other things of that kind, which
the apostle calls shadows of things to come. To us, by contrast, all things
have become known as already accomplished and completed. Many kings desired to
see what you see and did not see it. [Marginal reference: Matthew 13:17.]

33. Hence the Old Testament is called an image of absent realities and the
gospel the body itself and presentation of present realities; the former is
called fragile—for shadows ought to yield to the body, and therefore the
ceremonies were dissolved at Christ's advent through their own inherent
weakness—and the latter perpetual.

34. In the last days God addressed us through his Son and most fully disclosed
to us his counsel concerning our salvation, so that after this revelation made
by Christ none more illustrious or complete ought to be expected. Thus the Old
and New Testaments are distinguished by duration of time, since on account of
its most lucid manner of administering the covenant the New Testament will
last until the end of the age.

35. Because of this clarity of revelation produced after Christ's advent in
the flesh, the apostle compares the Fathers who lived under the Law to children
learning the first elements and rudiments of the arts. This teaches us that, if
we are compared with them, we can be called learned. For scarcely a child can
now be found in the church who does not know more about the redemption of the
human race than many prophets then knew. Hence, although John the Baptist is
called greater than the prophets, one who is least in the kingdom of heaven is
called greater than he: that is, the least herald of the gospel who proclaims
Christ crucified and raised, and so forth. [Marginal references: Galatians
3:24–25; Matthew 11:11; Luke 7:28.]

36. Although Christ was set before the ancients much more obscurely than he is
before us, if you compare among themselves the promises and disclosures God
made to them, setting the earlier alongside the later, you will see a new light
shining upon them more and more. The one promised to the first parents in the
first promise is later said to be born from Abraham. The seed was not to be
called through Abraham's son Ishmael, but in Isaac. The same promise

[Frame 00036; printed p. 32]

is restricted to Jacob's family and within it to the tribe of Judah; finally,
he is promised as one to be born from David's family. Among the prophets all
things are proposed still more clearly and brightly. The time when he would
come in the flesh, where and from whom he would be born, how long he would live,
what he would do during his life, and how he would finally die are all
accurately indicated. This is no wonder, since when the sun is soon to rise, a
greater light which heralds the rising sun commonly shines once the darkness
of night has been driven away. [Marginal references: Daniel 9:23–24, 26; Micah
5:1; Isaiah 7:14; 53.]

37. We have discussed the distinction located in quality. The other is in
quantity: according to the predictions of the prophets, there is now a greater
abundance of the Spirit under the New Testament than there formerly was under
the Old. “I shall pour out”—that is, with the greatest abundance—“from my
Spirit upon all flesh.” Hence Paul called the Old Testament literal, because
the Law was promulgated without the Spirit's efficacy, but the New spiritual,
because God spiritually engraves it upon men's hearts and exerts a greater
efficacy of his Holy Spirit in the preaching of the gospel than in that of the
Law. For the gospel is an effective instrument by which he leads men into
communion with himself. [Marginal references: Joel 2:28; Isaiah 44:3; Acts
2:17; 2 Corinthians 3.]

38. Furthermore, this difference between Letter and Spirit must not be taken
as though the Lord had given his Law to the Jews with no fruit and none of them
had been converted to him. These things are spoken by comparison, to commend
the abundance of grace with which the same Lawgiver, as though assuming a new
character, honored the preaching of the gospel.

39. This quantity or abundance of the Spirit shines forth especially in the
fact that God now has a church everywhere on earth, whereas before he had one
only within the borders of Judea and had formerly opened a meager school in one
corner of the world alone. [Marginal references: Isaiah 54:1; Zechariah 8:20;
Matthew 28:19.]

40. The Jews themselves understood from Holy Scripture that this would
eventually occur under the New Testament and that God had not entirely rejected
the Gentiles from salvation and eternal life. They therefore labored to lead
the Gentiles to acknowledgment of God. [Marginal references: Ephesians 2;
3:6.]

[Frame 00037; printed p. 33]

Thus Christ tells the Pharisees that they traverse sea and dry land to make one
proselyte. He does not condemn their effort simply, but rather the fact that
they made the proselytes far worse than they themselves were, which was not to
lead them to God but away from him. The Jews certainly would not have labored
to lead some of the Gentiles to the legal covenant unless they had known that
God also willed to receive the Gentiles into favor, or that God willed also to
be the God of the Gentiles. [Marginal reference: Matthew 23:15.]

41. Just as under the New Testament knowledge of Christ is greater, so the
efficacy of the Holy Spirit, which mortifies our flesh so that we obey God's
commands more earnestly, is also far greater. For this reason the Lord tolerated
not a few blemishes of the Israelites which would now be by no means tolerable
in us. It is just as a most prudent father will pardon many youthful sins in
his sons as long as they are children which he could by no means tolerate once
they have grown. Thus the Lord affirms that the certificate of divorce, which
was nothing other than permission to dismiss a wife for even the slightest
cause, was permitted to the Israelites because of the hardness of their
hearts—that is, because of both their immaturity and their weakness. Meanwhile
he shows that afterward it is not lawful except on account of fornication.
[Marginal reference: Matthew 19:8–9.]

42. You will indeed find certain men under the Old Testament, such as the
Patriarchs and prophets, who seem to have been adorned by God with greater
gifts than anyone under the New. Here, however, we judge not individuals but
the whole population or entire body.

43. The fourth unity was in the end, namely, the promised inheritance of
eternal life. The diversity lay in the manner of leading to that inheritance.
The ancients were led by the type of the land of Canaan, which was to them a
testimony and pledge of eternal life. They therefore did not cling to it but
rose beyond it, as David says: “The Lord is the portion of my inheritance.”
“You are my hope, my portion in the land of the living.” Hence deprivation of
this land, as a [Marginal references: Psalms 16:5; 142:6.]

[Frame 00038; printed p. 34]

symbol of eternal life, is treated as a curse. We, by contrast, under the New
Testament are led directly to meditation upon eternal life, since the heavenly
inheritance has already been taken possession of in our name in Christ. That
inferior manner of exercise is omitted because of the greater light shining
upon us in Christ, which ought not be obscured by the shadow of the land of
Canaan—although it is also true that even the promises of the present life are
not lacking to us. [Marginal references: Hebrews 7; Ephesians 2; 1 Timothy 4.]

44. From this comparison of the two Testaments it is evident that our
covenant—that is, this administration of the gracious covenant—is in many
ways more excellent and worthy than the ancient covenant, since in it Christ
himself has addressed us, has disclosed himself more clearly to us, has heaped
more abundant grace upon us, and wills this administration of the covenant, in
whose perfection nothing can be lacking, to last until the end of the age.

45. Since no distinction is seen in the covenant's essence but only in its
external circumstances, many have thought that this covenant is called new as
though renewed, since, if one regards the reality itself, nothing else is
promised here than what was promised to the first parents in Paradise. But, as
we said above, it is called new on account of the new administration, because
God formerly administered his covenant among the Jews in a manner different
from that among the Christian church.

46. Thus far we have spoken about the diversity of the Old and New Testaments.
Here it must be observed that this does not prove mutability in God, with whom
there is no change, but rather infinite wisdom. In different ages he prudently
knows how to use different remedies, just as a physician uses one method in
treating a child and another in treating a robust man, yet cannot on that
account be called unlike himself.

47. By a metonymy of the efficient cause, Paul called this ordering or
dispensation of the covenant God's manifold wisdom, because in his wisdom God
calls the Gentiles in a way different from that in which he formerly called
the circumcised. He instructed the circumcised [Marginal references: Ephesians
3:8, 10.]

[Frame 00039; printed p. 35]

concerning Christ to come through various ceremonies and shadows; he instructs
the Gentiles concerning Christ exhibited through clear testimony, employing
very few ceremonies, and those most significant—namely, Baptism and the holy
Supper.

### Chapter VII

That the gracious covenant has two parts, merit and efficacy, and that both are
to be sought in Christ, who is the covenant's surety.

#### First aphorism

1. Knowledge of the gracious covenant consists in two things: the very
substance of the covenant and its testimonies. We call God's Word and the
sacraments the testimonies of the covenant. Augustine's distinction is not far
removed from this one—although he speaks more generally than we do—by which,
in his books *On Christian Doctrine*, he divided theology into thing and sign.

2. The parts of the covenant's substance are two: merit and efficacy. By a
metonymy of effect these are commonly called reconciliation with God, and new
creation through knowledge of God; likewise justification, or the forgetting
of our sins, or peace; and sanctification. The former shows how salvation is
acquired, and the latter how it is dispensed. [Marginal references: Jeremiah
31; Hebrews 8:9, 11.]

3. These two parts were necessarily required for us to be restored to that
original dignity from which we had fallen. For in the fall man committed a
twofold evil. First, through disobedience he offended God; then he so corrupted
himself that he could do nothing other than sin. Two remedies had to be opposed
to these two evils. First, satisfaction had to be made for the sins of all who
are to be saved, and made sufficiently, so that it would be a satisfaction
worthy to merit eternal life: hence merit. Second, it had to be brought about,
and brought about powerfully, that men cease from sinning and no longer offend
God by their sins: hence efficacy.

4. Both must be sought in the Mediator, to whom we must flee as to a new Adam
and stock of the human race, so that just as sin and death flowed from that old
and first Adam into us all, righteousness and life may flow from this new Adam
into all believers.

[Frame 00040; printed p. 36]

5. Therefore Christ our Mediator is said to have been made by God our wisdom,
justification, sanctification, and redemption. I refer justification and
redemption to merit, and wisdom and sanctification to efficacy. [Marginal
reference: 1 Corinthians 1:30.]

### Chapter VIII

On the Mediator's person: that the Mediator is true God, true man, and both in
one person; and why these things were required.

#### First aphorism

1. We have said that both parts of the covenant must be sought in the Mediator.
In order to arrive at these parts of the covenant, we shall consider two things
in him: his person and his office.

2. Three things must be considered in the Mediator's person: first, that he is
true God; second, true man; and third, both in one person.

3. On account of both parts of the covenant the Mediator had to be God, and
first on account of merit. Since by sinning Adam sought equality with God, what
humiliation could have been opposed to so proud an exaltation unless he who was
in the form of God emptied himself, expiated the contrary sin by a contrary
remedy, and became obedient even to the death of the cross? Moreover, who could
have sustained the infinite penalties due for men's sins? Could anyone but one
who is infinite? There is no proportion between the infinite and the finite.
He therefore had to be God, since none is infinite except God. Hence arises the
dignity of the merit: God himself emptied himself and became obedient unto
death. Had this not been so, the blood shed would not have been God's blood and
therefore neither a sufficient nor adequately excellent ransom. [Marginal
reference: Philippians 2:6–8.]

4. Efficacy likewise required the Mediator to be God, because there is no
Savior besides God. Add that none but God could have revealed to us God's
decree concerning our salvation, [Marginal references: Isaiah 43:11; 44:5.]

[Frame 00041; printed p. 37]

since any other would himself have been ignorant of it. Thus John says that
the Son is in the Father's bosom, taking a metaphor from men, who are said to
admit to their bosom those with whom they share secrets. Who could have
swallowed up death but life? Who could have destroyed sin but righteousness
itself? Who could have routed the powers of the world and air but a power
superior to the world and air? Who, in view of our depravity, could have pledged
for us that we would finally cease to sin, except one who possessed the power
to give the Holy Spirit? But with whom do life, righteousness, dominion over
heaven and earth, and the power to give the Holy Spirit reside except God
alone? [Marginal references: John 1:18; Matthew 11:27.]

5. Since the nature of the covenant required such a mediator, God provided for
us in this respect. To disclose his incomprehensible goodness, he willed his
grace not merely to equal our sin but far to surpass it, so that if Adam's
rebellion terrifies us, the righteousness of Jesus Christ should much more
confirm us, and that we should hope for a better life in Jesus Christ than in
Adam, in proportion as Christ is more excellent than Adam.

6. Most learnedly, from this point and by considering the Mediator's office,
the ancient theologians asserted Christ's deity against the Arians and other
heretics. For as we have shown above, it was altogether necessary for our
Mediator to be God.

7. Our Mediator had to be neither the Father nor the Holy Spirit, but the Son
of God himself. We had to be adopted by God as sons, so that that adoption
might come to us. Therefore the Son of God willed to be our Mediator, so that
we might become heirs of God and his fellow heirs. This too may be added: we
had to be redeemed through the one through whom we were created; Christ
possessed this right of ownership over us. The scholastics too have noted this
reason: “It would have been unfitting,” they say, “for there to be two sons,
one in deity and another in humanity,” which would have occurred if either God
the Father or the Holy Spirit had become incarnate. Therefore [Marginal
reference: Colossians 1:16.]

[Frame 00042; printed p. 38]

the Son alone had to become incarnate, lest the relation of the persons be
removed or disturbed.

8. Hence in the Creed the Mediator is called God's only-begotten Son, to
distinguish him from God's adopted sons. For although the others are called
sons, they are so called only by the grace of adoption, not by reality of
nature; and although others are called lords, they are so called by granted,
not innate, power. Cyprian, *Exposition of the Creed*.

9. On account of the same parts of the covenant he had to be true man, yet
undefiled. Man had defected from and offended God; therefore man was required
to expiate that offense, since God does not punish in one subject the things
committed by another, just as he did not punish men for the angels' fall. He
therefore had to become man so that he could die, because without the shedding
of blood there is no remission of sin; and so that, tempted in all things after
the manner of men, he could be a faithful and sympathetic high priest. He also
had to become man so that he could address men and be understood by them. If he
were not man, men could not even hear or endure him speaking, much less
understand him—I mean, if the Mediator were God only. Man was also bound to
fulfill God's Law, for he was created for this end. This man also had to be
pure and immaculate, since otherwise he would himself have been subject to the
curse and in need of a mediator. Finally, since we have said that the Mediator
had to be the Son of God, it was not fitting for the Logos to assume a polluted
nature into the unity of his person. [Marginal references: Hebrews 2:14;
Romans 8:4; Isaiah 53:9.]

10. Efficacy also required our Mediator to be man, because we could not cleave
to a Mediator who was God alone except through this intermediate instrument,
that is, through a common nature or flesh. Hence arises the right of kinship. I
do not mean that the enlivening and raising of our bodies from the grave are
effects of Christ's flesh eaten orally by us and dwelling bodily in us. I say,
rather, that Christ's human nature (for, as will be seen below,

[Frame 00043; printed p. 39]

it was necessary for the Mediator to be God and man in one person) serves not
only his merit but also his efficacy, and is the foundation of that communion
which we have with Christ and of the consequent benefits which flow from it.
As Cyril said elegantly: “Unless Christ had been born carnally, you would not
have been born spiritually.”

11. Therefore no angel could have been our mediator. Since the angels did not
sin, the very nature which was to be delivered had to be assumed; nor are the
angels infinite. Neither could a mere man have been mediator, because if such a
man had attempted our redemption, he would have made matters worse, since he
himself would have offended God every day; nor could a mere man have sustained
or overcome that infinite mass of God's wrath.

12. It would have benefited us little for the Mediator to have been either God
or man unless our nature and the Logos had so coalesced into one person that
God began to be Emmanuel with us, and so received what was ours as to transfer
to us what was his, making by grace ours what was his by nature. Otherwise, had
they not coalesced, there would have been neither a sufficiently close
proximity nor a sufficiently firm affinity from which we could hope that God
was ours and wished to dwell with us.

13. Add that God alone could not die, nor could man alone overcome death. It
therefore had to be brought about that the same one should die and conquer
death. [Marginal reference: Vigilius Martyr, book 5.]

14. Third, no one can be an arbiter between estranged parties unless he enjoys
favor with each party. Thus, if the Mediator had been God alone, it could have
occurred to us that he would be not so much for us as against us, and also that
one who interceded for us appeared too highly exalted.

15. Augustine therefore speaks excellently in the book *On Questions*:
“Divinity without humanity is not mediatrix; humanity without divinity

[Frame 00044; printed p. 40]

is not mediatrix; but between divinity alone and humanity alone, human divinity
and divine humanity is mediatrix.” Likewise, discussing the goal of faith in
book 11, chapter 2 of *The City of God*, he teaches that we must know both where
we are going and by what way, and immediately afterward concludes that the most
fortified way against all errors is the same one who is God and man. For God is
where we tend, man the way by which we go, and both are found only in Christ.
This is that ladder which reconciles and joins heaven to earth, earth to heaven,
divine things to human, and human things to divine.

16. First conclusion. There is therefore only one Mediator, because none is God
and man except Christ Jesus. This is against the papists.

17. Second conclusion. Stancaro erred by attributing the office of mediator to
the humanity alone; Osiander erred by attributing it to the deity alone.

### Chapter IX

On the union of the two natures in the Mediator's person: what it is and what
it is called; what natures and natural properties are; and how the natures and
their properties coalesced in the Mediator's person.

#### First aphorism

1. This union of the Logos and our nature, which is called the incarnation of
the Son of God, is that by which the Son of God, emptying himself, assumed a
human nature—not, however, some man or individual subsisting in himself, for
this nature was without its own personality and never existed before the
incarnation of the Word. He assumed it, namely by creating it for himself, in
the womb of the Virgin Mary, and united it to himself in the unity of his
person so that the redemption of the human race could be accomplished. Or thus:

The union of the natures in Christ is the joining or uniting of God's Word with
flesh, by which the Logos in the fullness of time assumed flesh and so made it
his own that, with the properties of each nature preserved, they constitute
one particular being.

[Frame 00045; printed p. 41]

Hence Mary is called *Theotokos*, that is, God-bearer, for according to the
humanity she is the mother of the person who subsists in the divine and human
natures. [Marginal reference: Luke 1:43.]

2. This union of the two natures in Christ, which the Fathers called the
economy, is designated by various names in Scripture. It is called emptying in
Philippians 2—emptying is attributed to the divine nature, and humiliation to
the whole person composed of both natures. Scripture also signifies it by the
words assuming, Hebrews 2:16; dwelling, John 1:14 and Colossians 2:9; sending,
Galatians 4:4; entering into the world, Hebrews 10:5; coming in the flesh, 1
John 4:2; appearing, 1 John 3:8; descending, John 6; offering, Hebrews 9; and
fitting, Hebrews 10:5. The scholastics explain it less suitably by the word
*adherence*, when they say that through the incarnation the human nature began
to adhere to the person of the Word. The Fathers commonly call this union of
the natures a *koinōnia*, a communion of natures, and a communion of
properties. By this they signify nothing other than that the divine and human
natures are joined in the unity of the person. They therefore rebuke Paul of
Samosata and Nestorius because each said that the human and divine natures were
*akoinōnētoi*, or incommunicable.

3. From this one may see that the Fathers used the word communion, or
*koinōnia*, intransitively.

4. Because the Ubiquitarians of our age did not observe this, they fell
wretchedly, using the word communion transitively. They explain it by saying
that in the hypostatic union the Logos communicated to his flesh—that is,
poured into it—ubiquity, omniscience, and omnipotence. They contend especially
for these three because they seem sufficient to establish the presence of
Christ's body in the eucharistic bread.

5. It is remarkable that they did not observe that this communion is mutual.
By this reasoning they could also have poured the properties of the human
nature into the divine. But

[Frame 00046; printed p. 42]

ubiquity alone was their concern, and so they passed over the things which did
not seem so conducive to establishing it.

6. Below we shall say what the scholastics understood by the expression
“communication of properties.”

7. We have not hesitated to call deity and humanity in the Mediator's person,
or in this hypostatic union, two natures, since the apostle called them two
forms. Vigilius Martyr, book 2. [Marginal reference: Philippians 2:6–7.]

8. In the New Testament the divine nature in the Mediator's hypostasis is
frequently understood under the name spirit: 1 Peter 3:18; Romans 1:4; 1
Timothy 3:16; John 6:63.

9. By the name *idiōmata*, or properties, we understand the things which belong
to these natures in the fourth mode, as logicians speak.

10. The properties of the divine nature are, with respect to quantity,
eternity and ubiquity; with respect to quality, omniscience, perfect justice,
omnipotence, absolute goodness, and finally all infinite things, for nothing in
God is other than infinite.

11. The properties of the human nature are all finite things and those which
separate a corporeal from an incorporeal nature.

12. Because natures act through their properties, the actions or *energeiai* of
the individual natures are also referred to their natural properties. If one
regards the divine nature, these actions are creating, preserving, and so
forth; if the human, suffering, being crucified, hungering, thirsting, and so
forth.

13. Those created gifts in Christ which the scholastics called gifts of
habitual grace are not properties of the divine nature, because they are
created. Therefore the Ubiquitarians argue most ineptly from parity when they
say: Christ walked upon the waters according to the flesh; therefore according
to the flesh he is everywhere, and consequently in the bread of the holy
Supper, or at least with the bread. These are false parallels, since created
gifts in the human nature, precisely because they are created, are not
properties of the divine nature. Indeed, by this reasoning you can conclude
that Peter's body is in the eucharistic bread, because he too walked upon the
waters. [Marginal reference: Matthew 14:29.]

[Frame 00047; printed p. 43]

They therefore err gravely and abuse the word *idiōmata* when they call these
gifts of habitual grace conferred upon Christ according to the flesh by the
name of properties.

14. There are properties not only of the natures considered in themselves, but
also of the entire person insofar as he consists of both natures. Such are the
things said about Christ with respect to his office. For just as the office
belongs to both natures, so the things said about Christ with respect to the
office are properties of the whole person, not merely of one nature, and
therefore must not be transferred to the humanity or deity alone. Although
these matters are better understood from the administration of the office,
which is discussed below, we shall touch lightly upon them here.

15. The properties of the person are commonly reduced to three orders.

16. The first embraces the designations or names of the office, such as Jesus,
Christ, Priest, Mediator, Advocate, and so forth.

17. The second embraces the effects of the office, which the ancients call
*apotelesmata* or *energēmata*: that is, works accomplished through the common
operation and concurrence of the natures, such as redemption, propitiation,
satisfaction, intercession, mediation, and so forth.

18. It must be observed carefully that although we ascribe the same
*apotelesma* to each nature, for which reason we call it a property peculiar to
the person, it does not therefore follow that each nature acts in the same
manner in producing this *apotelesma*. Each nature acts here according to what
belongs to it and contributes what is its own to producing the common
*apotelesma*. Thus Leo rightly said that each nature performs, in communion
with the other, what belongs to it. Although Gregory of Nyssa acknowledged one
*energēma*, he nevertheless distinguished the *energeiai* carefully: “The
suffering,” he says, “belongs to Christ's body, but the fact that through it we
are saved is God's operation.” Consequently, to attribute the same *energeia*
to the Word and flesh, as the Ubiquitarians do, is one thing; to attribute the
same *energēma* and *apotelesma*, as we do with orthodox antiquity, is another.
The former is a manner of acting which is necessarily different

[Frame 00048; printed p. 44]

because there are two natures; the latter is the same effect produced by
agents which are different and dissimilar. Hence Damascenus carefully
distinguishes between *energeia* and *energēma*. He teaches that in Christ two
*energeiai* must be acknowledged—the divine energy of the divine nature and
will and the human energy of the human nature and will—and likewise two
*energētika*, that is, two distinctly active natures; but there is one and the
same *energēma* of the whole person, just as there is also one *energōn*, that
is, one person acting according to each nature. Therefore, just as Scripture
attributes the effects of the office to the whole person with respect to each
nature while still observing the natures' properties and the distinctions of
their actions, so let us learn soberly to distinguish these very things.

18. The third order embraces the states of the office. Humiliation and
exaltation are properties of the whole person. Here too the distinction and
action of each nature must be observed and distinguished carefully. Thus,
although Scripture says that Christ became obedient to the Father even to the
death of the cross, it nevertheless says that he suffered in the flesh. The
suffering and crucifixion did not extend into the deity, which was incapable
of suffering. Yet the deity itself meanwhile performed its own part in this
state of humiliation by resting so that the redemption of the human race could
be accomplished.

19. Who does not see how very ignorantly the Ubiquitarians transfer the
properties of the whole person, which belong to Christ according to each nature
while the distinction of properties and *energeiai* is preserved, to asserting
a pouring of the divine nature's properties into the human?

20. It is therefore worthy of observation that the Ubiquitarians were deceived
because they thought the properties of the person were properties of the
natures. Hence they argue from parity: If Christ's flesh cleanses us from all
sin, why can it not equally be said to be everywhere? For cleansing from sin
belongs to deity just as much as being everywhere. The reasoning is not
parallel. Since purification from sins, or redemption, is a property of the
whole person with respect to each nature, it can be called a property of the
person and be said of the natures separately, because each contributes what
belongs to it toward this *apotelesma*. But ubiquity,

[Frame 00049; printed p. 45]

since it is a property of the divine nature alone, can no more be said of the
human nature than this property of the human nature—that it is finite—can be
said of the divine.

21. They speak still more ineptly when they call those gifts of habitual grace,
about which we said a little above and will say more as we proceed, and their
bestowal in the human nature, properties of the divine nature and a
communication of properties. First, they err because these gifts, whatever
they may be—and they certainly are now very great in Christ according to the
flesh—are not properties of deity, since they are created gifts, whereas
God's properties are uncreated and infinite. Second, the examples they adduce
are such as are seen in angels and holy men too. An angel entered the prison
although the doors were shut, and Peter walked upon the waters. I do not think
that any Ubiquitarian will say that through the communication of properties
these men were Christ. Third, they will never prove that ubiquity, omniscience,
and omnipotence are numbered among these created gifts.

22. Therefore they not only speak ineptly and ignorantly but also overturn the
state of the controversy. The question is not whether Christ possesses the
greatest gifts according to the flesh. Indeed, now exalted, even with respect
to his flesh he is adorned with gifts above all angels. Nor is the question
whether the properties or *apotelesmata* of the whole person belong also to the
natures. The question is whether the properties of the divine nature are
poured into the flesh or those of the flesh into the divine nature—something
which certainly no one can affirm without implying a contradiction. Thus when
they are ordered to prove that ubiquity, a property of the divine nature, has
been communicated to the flesh, they descend in vain to created gifts, such as
his walking upon the waters, because these are not properties of the divine
nature; or to the office's *apotelesmata*, such as that the flesh merited
eternal life for us, because the office's *apotelesmata* are properties neither
of the divine nor the human nature, but of the whole person.

23. They accomplish nothing by adducing the Fathers to show that the
properties

[Frame 00050; printed p. 46]

of deity were also made proper to the human nature, because the Fathers say
that whatever was given to Christ in time was given according to the human
nature. For this is what the Fathers mean: things said to have been given to
Christ in time must be understood of the Logos manifested in the flesh. Why
should they be transferred to the flesh rather than to the incarnate Logos? It
is far more correct to say that the flesh was given to the Logos than that the
Logos was given to the flesh, because the flesh is not a person. And one speaks
most correctly with Scripture by understanding the person of the God-man when
it says that a child was given to us. Moreover, you will never show that
ubiquity was given to Christ's flesh in time, that is, that it was poured into
the human nature. If they now bring forward properties of the person, that is,
the office's *apotelesmata* and states, they labor in vain, since the latter are
properties of neither nature and the former are common to both natures.

24. This union of the natures with their properties in one person did not take
place according to the order of nature, because it neither could nor ought to
have done so. It could not, because merit and efficacy depended upon both
natures, or upon this whole person.

25. It ought not, because the mass to be assumed in the Virgin's womb had to
be immune from sin and therefore could not be conceived according to the
natural conception of Adam's heirs, since in that case it would have been
subject to sin. The Creed therefore says that the Holy Spirit effected this
union, not because he purified as unclean the mass which the Son of God
assumed, but because by exempting it from the common order of nature he
prevented sin from making inroads into it. The Holy Spirit knows perfectly how
to distinguish man's nature made by God from the sin added by the Devil.
[Marginal reference: Ephesians 2:1–2.]

26. Much less, when we say that the Holy Spirit effected this union, should it
be thought that this mass itself was from the substance of the Holy Spirit. For
the little word “from” in the Creed denotes not the material but the efficient
cause, as it often does elsewhere in Scripture: Romans 11:36; John 8:44.

27. When we say that the Logos united the human nature to himself, under the
name human nature we understand soul and body; we do not mean

[Frame 00051; printed p. 47]

that the Logos or deity stood in place of a soul for the body. For Christ
himself says, “My soul is sorrowful unto death.” Add that in that case Christ
would have to be considered the Redeemer only of our bodies and not also of our
souls, which is absurd and intolerable. [Marginal reference: Matthew 26:38.]

28. Therefore, just as there are two natures in the same person of Christ, so
there are two minds or intellects, two wills, operations, wisdoms, powers, and
so forth: one divine and uncreated, the other human and created. This is against
the Monothelites.

29. Likewise, when we speak of human nature, we understand it to be real, not
phantasmal flesh or some specter of flesh, against Marcion; nor stellar, aerial,
or heavenly, against Mani. For these things conflict with God's promises, in
which he says that the Messiah will arise from David's seed and loins. This
would be false if he originated from the air, heaven, or stars. Moreover, he
was made like us in all things except sin. [Marginal references: 2 Samuel 7;
Psalm 132:11.]

30. Theodoret says excellently in Dialogue 2: “If the body which he assumed is
different, what does this have to do with mine, which needs salvation? If he
brought flesh down from heaven, what does it have to do with my flesh, which
was taken from the earth?”

31. We have said that this human nature was not something subsisting by itself.
For he assumed it, deprived of its own personality, into the unity of his own
person. It neither ever subsisted outside the Logos nor ever will subsist
outside him.

32. We have said, in accordance with the prophets' predictions, that this
incarnation and conception took place in the womb of a virgin, and specifically
Mary, and such a conception was required. “Regard nothing as impossible there
where the power of the Most High was present.” Cyprian, *Exposition of the
Creed*.

33. By noting that this incarnation occurred in the fullness of time, we mean
that it occurred when the time established by God had arrived, so that it ought
to have happened neither later nor earlier. [Marginal references: Galatians
4:4; Ephesians 1:10.]

34. Some offer these reasons why God deferred the sending of his Son in the
flesh until the last ages: first, so that in

[Frame 00052; printed p. 48]

the meantime we might learn to recognize our misery and evils; second, so that
when these were known, an ardent desire for the revelation of our salvation
might be kindled within us; third, so that God might thus make manifest his
patience toward unbelievers. We do not approve of curiously examining the
reasons for God's counsels which he willed to be hidden from us.

35. We have said that the end of this wondrous conception is the work of human
redemption. The Son willed to become the Son of Man so that he might make us
sons of God with himself. He willed to descend to earth so that he might lay
open for us an ascent to heaven. He assumed our mortality so that he might
confer his immortality upon us. He received our poverty into himself so that
he might transfer his riches to us. He received into himself the mass of
unrighteousness by which we were oppressed so that he might clothe us with his
righteousness.

36. Leo therefore says excellently to Flavian: “That the Creator of all things
willed to be one of the mortals was a condescension of compassion, not a
failure of power.”

37. We have said that the two natures, namely, the Logos and the human nature,
were united in one hypostasis so that our Mediator might be true God and man,
and this without the rending or confusion of the natures or of their
*idiōmata*, or properties. Although the natures remain distinct, they are not
torn apart; and although united, they must not be thought confused or equalized.
Rather, in this union, just as each remains unconfused, so each must be thought
to preserve its properties. For he is like us in the flesh, and that in all
things except sin. Sin, however, is not a proper or essential circumstance of
human nature, but an adventitious evil. As Augustine said, “From the Virgin the
Lord derived only the origin of his race, not also of guilt.” [Marginal
references: Hebrews 4:15; Isaiah 53:9.]

38. The Council of Chalcedon expressed this manner of the union of the two
natures together with their properties by these words: it occurred
*asyngchytōs, atreptōs, adiairetos, achōristōs*—without confusion, without
change, without division, without separation.

39. Damascenus, book 3 of *On the Orthodox Faith*, chapter 17: “After the union
both the natures themselves remained unconfused and their properties
unimpaired.”

[Frame 00053; printed p. 49]

40. All the Fathers who wrote against Eutyches and Nestorius consistently
affirm that the natures remained distinct and unconfused, whether one regards
their essence, their properties, or their operations.

41. In the Creed Athanasius illustrated this manner of union by the following
analogy: “Christ is one, not by confusion of substance, but by unity of person.
For as the rational soul and flesh are one man, so God and man”—he meant to
say, the human nature—“are one Christ.” Basil illustrated it by the analogy of
glowing iron; Origen by ointment and its fragrance. In these matters, however,
we ought not be curious or examine the analogy in all its parts, but remain
within the point of comparison. For this wondrous union cannot be sufficiently
expressed or explained by any natural thing, as Cyprian rightly said: “Since
Christ is God's only Son, how can what is unique admit any comparison?” The
ancients wished to teach only this: even by an example from earthly things it
can be shown possible for two natures to be united in Christ without confusion.

### Chapter X

That this union is a mixture or equalization neither of the natures nor of the
properties of these natures.

#### First aphorism

1. This union is not a confusion or mixture of the natures, such that by
assuming the human nature the Logos was changed into it; or, conversely, that
the human nature assumed by the Logos into the unity of his person was changed
into the Logos or deified. For by this reasoning the Mediator would be neither
God nor man, but something intermediate between the two. Rather, the Word was
made flesh: that is, he assumed flesh, but was not changed into flesh. Thus
Athanasius, cited by Maxentius, explains: “The Word became flesh; flesh did not
become the Word.”

[Frame 00054; printed p. 50]

The Word is said to have become flesh because the flesh became the Word's and
not some man's; that is, God became man.

2. Christ himself demonstrates the open and manifest distinction of the
natures when he says, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I shall raise it
up.” Christ would not have called his body a temple unless the deity dwelt
distinctly in it, and unless the nature which was destroyed was one, and the
nature which raised the destroyed one another. [Marginal reference: John
2:19.]

3. Nor did this union produce any equalization or coextension of the properties
of these natures, such that Christ's humanity is now eternal as the deity is;
or the deity created or circumscribed by place as the humanity is; or the human
nature extends as far as the divine, so that it is everywhere. Such a union
ought by no means to have been made. First, the Creator wills perpetually to be
distinct from his creatures. Second, such an equalization of properties would
overturn the work of human redemption from its very foundations. Vigilius
Martyr says learnedly in book 3: “How does the truth of a name remain if the
property of the nature is absent?” Could the Devil not easily object that
deified flesh cannot be called human? We therefore assert that in this union
each nature preserved what was proper to it: namely, that Christ's flesh
remained finite, born in time, visible, and capable of suffering, and finally
that the properties of deity in no way passed into it. The same must be
believed about the properties of the human nature. How could these be called
properties if such a pouring and communion were imagined?

4. Just as this confusion of properties ought not to have taken place, so
Scripture testifies that it did not take place, but that each nature retained
its own properties, and no equalization of properties followed from the union.
For both before and after the glorification, Scripture attributes to Christ the
properties of the human nature. Thus in Romans 1 Paul splendidly distinguished
the effects of each nature. Thus the Evangelists testify that Christ hungered,
thirsted, became weary, and so forth. In the very glorification—that is, in
the resurrection, for the resurrection is the first degree of
glorification—Christ testifies that he is visible [Marginal references:
Matthew 4; John 4:6; 19:28.]

[Frame 00055; printed p. 51]

and tangible. He affirms that he retained and will retain these same properties
not only in the resurrection but also after his ascension into heaven: “They
will see him whom they pierced.” Therefore the Ubiquitarians' distinctions
concerning Christ's human nature—that it is considered in itself or according
to glorification and majesty, so that with these distinctions maintained it
can be called everywhere and not everywhere—vanish entirely, since not even
the glorified nature is everywhere. [Marginal references: John 20:20; Luke
24:39.]

5. Hence those splendid sayings of the ancients. Damascenus, book 3, chapter 4:
“When speaking of the divinity, therefore, we do not predicate of it the things
which belong to the humanity, for we do not say that the deity is passible or
created. Nor do we predicate of the flesh, that is, the humanity, things proper
to deity; for we do not say that the flesh, that is, the humanity, is uncreated.
But concerning the hypostasis, whether we name it from both natures or from one
of them, we add to it the proper things of each nature.” Jerome, in the
*Explanation of the Creed to Damasus*: “Likewise we execrate the blasphemy of
those who attempt to assert with a novel meaning that, from the time the flesh
was assumed, all things which belonged to deity migrated into the man”—he
meant to say, the humanity—“and in turn all things which belonged to humanity
were poured into God, so that by this confusion each substance seems emptied,
a thing which no heresy ever dared to say.”

6. Meanwhile we do not deny that, although no equalization of the natures'
properties occurred, the flesh of the Word himself was nevertheless suffused
with the greatest and most illustrious gifts of the Holy Spirit. We add, “as
much as the constitution of a body could bear.” Even to the glorified body the
Spirit indeed gave immortality, but did not remove its nature. [Marginal
reference: Augustine, *City of God*, book 22, chapter 30.]

7. Therefore, as concerns the dignity and majesty of Christ's human nature, we
assert it more magnificently with Scripture than the Ubiquitarians, who wholly
overturn it by their absurd dogma, so far are they from establishing it. We
place it in these three things, which will be understood better as we proceed.
First, it was assumed into the unity of the person and so is the flesh of the
Son of God. Second, in the union itself excellent

[Frame 00056; printed p. 52]

gifts were conferred upon Christ's human nature; in his Baptism they were
increased; in the resurrection, ascension into heaven, and session at the
Father's right hand they became greatest. Yet the distinction between God and
creature always remains.

8. It does not therefore follow, as certain people foolishly think, that if no
equalization of properties occurred, there is no difference between Peter and
Christ, since God is also present to Peter and Paul and dwells in them. But does
God not dwell personally in Peter and Paul? Christ the man is properly God and
God's Son, not by communicated but by natural deity. Who ever called Peter and
Paul God or God's only-begotten Son because God's Spirit dwelt in them, as
Christ incarnate and our brother is so called?

## Chapter XI

### The personal union according to the Ubiquitarians, or the Eutychians of our
own time, who locate it in a confusion of properties; and its refutation

The Ubiquitarians of our time, who fastened ubiquity upon Christ's body in
order to assert Christ's bodily presence in the Holy Supper, thought that the
doctrine of the hypostatic union provided them some support for asserting
ubiquity. They maintained that Christ's flesh is everywhere because the Logos,
who is everywhere, had conferred this property upon his flesh on account of
the hypostatic union. And lest anyone should think it strange that ubiquity had
been conferred upon Christ's flesh, which is a creature, they tried to meet the
absurdity with an argument from parity. They said it was no wonder, since by
the force of the hypostatic union omnipotence and omniscience had also been
communicated to Christ's flesh, and these are no less properties of God than
ubiquity. They could indeed have gone still farther with this foolish argument
and begging of the question, but shame restrained them, and they thought these
three enough to establish the local presence of Christ's body in the bread or
at the place of the bread. They had first made the doctrine of the personal
union the foundation of ubiquity; but when they noticed this foundation
tottering, they placed beneath the failing structure the article concerning
Christ's session at the Father's right hand.

[Frame 00057; printed p. 53]

From this they subsequently acquired two foundations for this error. Therefore,
since we are treating the doctrine of the personal union, we shall briefly set
forth and refute the Ubiquitarians' position, which overturns the orthodox
doctrine we have proposed thus far.

### First aphorism

1. They define the union of the two natures in Christ in this way: the deity,
in the person of the Son, poured its properties into the human nature and so
assumed it that it does and performs all things through this assumed man. They
call this a real or essential communication of properties.

2. What they sometimes add to this definition—“the property of each nature
being preserved”—they seem to say in jest, since what they posit before it
utterly excludes this addition.

3. They do not dare confidently to assert that the human nature communicated
anything to the divine nature, and therefore they place that entire
communication on the side of the divine nature. They do this because they are
concerned only to assert ubiquity. But below we shall see that the things
belonging to the humanity—if this can contribute anything to that real
communication—are equally communicated, that is, attributed by predication,
to the person named from the divine nature, just as the things belonging to
the divinity are said of the same person now named, or receiving his title,
from the human nature. Thus the Lord of glory is said to have been crucified,
God to have redeemed the Church with his own blood, and God to have suffered
in the flesh. Since the Ubiquitarians could not explain these statements, they
invented the claim that they are said of deified flesh, which was God by a real
communication of properties, or by “personal deity”—a thoroughly obscure and
absurd phrase.

4. If they understood their “real communication of properties” in this way,
that the divine nature was communicated to the human nature, so that

[Frame 00058; printed p. 54]

the former assumed the latter into the unity of the person and the latter in
turn possesses the former as the hypostasis in which it subsists, we would
agree with them. But since they mean a transfusion of properties, they ought
more properly to have called it a confusion of properties.

5. This is the personal union according to the Ubiquitarians. From it they
have constructed not only omnipresence but also omnipotence and omniscience,
and finally a certain deification of Christ's body. This position conflicts
with the truth of the two natures in Christ's person, with the articles of
faith, and with the whole of Holy Scripture.

They grossly err in locating the specific difference of the union of the two
natures in Christ in this: that the humanity is endowed with the same
properties as God the Word; or, as they put it in another way, that the
difference between God's indwelling in the man Christ and in Peter or other
saints is that he pours measured gifts into the latter but immeasurable gifts
into Christ, rather than locating it in the bond of the natures in one
hypostasis. I ask, what difference do they make between Christ and Peter before
Christ's glorification, since they admit that before his glorification Christ
did not exercise those communicated properties?

Nor, if they defined that union of the two natures as a union into one
hypostasis, would it follow that the deity communicated its properties to the
humanity, or poured them into it, any more than it follows that the soul pours
its properties into the body, as if because of the union of soul and body the
body also reasoned and performed the soul's other functions.

Christ assumed human nature so as to become like us in all things except sin.
Therefore he did not give ubiquity, omnipotence, and omniscience to his flesh.

Whatever is omnipresent, or everywhere, is infinite, uncreated, eternal, and
incomprehensible; that is, it is God. This is the supporting syllogism: in
Jeremiah 23 God attributes ubiquity to himself as his proper attribute: “Do I
not fill heaven and earth?” But Christ's body, even by their own testimony, is
not infinite, uncreated, eternal, or incomprehensible—that is, in a word, God.
Therefore Christ's body is not omnipresent.

By this very argument, from ubiquity as a proper attribute, the godly teachers
of the Church asserted the divinity of the Holy Spirit,

[Frame 00059; printed p. 55]

as Didymus does in *On the Holy Spirit*, book 1, chapter 1: “If the Holy Spirit
himself were one of the creatures, he would at least have a circumscribed
substance, just like all things that have been made. For although invisible
creatures are not circumscribed by place, they are nevertheless bounded by the
property of their substance. But since the Holy Spirit is in many, he does not
have a circumscribed substance.”

If Christ's body is everywhere because of the hypostatic union, how could it
be carried in the Virgin's womb? The personal union was effected in the
Virgin's womb. Unless perhaps they wish to invent the claim that Christ's
flesh emptied and humbled itself before he was born—which is utterly
monstrous.

If Christ's body is everywhere because of the hypostatic union, and a pouring
out of properties occurred, how can Luke's statement that Christ advanced in
wisdom, and so forth, stand? [Marginal reference: Luke 2.]

If ubiquity, omniscience, and omnipotence are properties of the divine nature,
how could they be communicated to the human nature? For then they would be
common, not proper. It is an outright contradiction in terms.

Either all, some, or none of the deity's properties were communicated to the
humanity. Not all, because they themselves dispute about only some among many.
Not only some, because Paul says that the fullness of deity dwells in Christ.
If they understand this statement as a real outpouring—as indeed they do—they
cannot restrict all fullness to a few or to many properties. Therefore none
were communicated.

If Christ's body is infinite—as it must be if it is everywhere—his soul will
also be infinite, for they will be ashamed to attribute less to the soul than
to the body. And so there will be two infinite things; indeed three: the
Logos, the flesh, and the soul.

If the communication of properties is real, it follows that the human nature
also gives to the divine nature, which they deny. The reason is that the human
nature is no less really united to the deity than the deity is to the human
nature; indeed, equivalent phrases concerning each are found.

It follows that the doctrine concerning the gifts conferred upon the human
nature is useless. What need was there to confer certain finite gifts upon one
who possessed infinite gifts by force of the personal union?

If omnipresence, omnipotence, and omniscience were communicated to the human
nature, surely eternity and infinity were also, because the properties of the
divine nature

[Frame 00060; printed p. 56]

cannot be separated. But they do not dare assert that these were communicated.
Therefore ubiquity was not communicated to it either. The Ubiquitarians are
very tightly constrained by this argument, and so they labor with the greatest
anxiety to free themselves. It is useful to observe these men's ignorance and
deceptions: they assert that only those properties which they wish, and which
they think serve their cause, were communicated to the human nature. They do
not want eternity and infinity to be transfused into the human nature, just as
if it lay in their own hand to give to Christ and take from him whatever they
wished at their own discretion.

They have not yet agreed among themselves when Christ began to be everywhere,
or whether the cause of ubiquity is this personal union or glorification. Some
say both, but foolishly, since at least one ought to be sufficient. The
personal union is not its cause, because this was effected in Mary's womb; yet
he was not then in the wombs of other women. Therefore ubiquity does not arise
from the personal union.

Nor was he made everywhere by his session at God's right hand. For if
exaltation to God's right hand is the cause why Christ's flesh is everywhere,
omniscient, and omnipotent, then it was not endowed with these things before
Christ was glorified. But Christ was not yet glorified in the personal union,
because he had not yet died: this is the order, these are the stages, of
Christ's office. [Marginal reference: 1 Peter 1:11.] Christ himself asks the
Father that he may be glorified, and John testifies that Christ had not yet
been glorified. [Marginal references: John 17:5; John 7:39.] If they judge the
personal union sufficient to construct ubiquity, why do they flee to the
article concerning the session at God's right hand? Therefore, before they
thrust ubiquity upon us, let them tell us what they think is the cause of
ubiquity and consequently of the presence of Christ's body in the bread, for
the sake of which they invented ubiquity. If exaltation to the Father's right
hand is the cause of the ubiquity of Christ's body, then it was not everywhere
before the session, and so they cite the personal union in vain. Moreover, on
this reasoning it would not have been in the Supper, because in the first
Supper he was not present by the manner of a session at God's right hand,
since he had not yet ascended to the Father.

We therefore have good reason to rebuke the Ubiquitarians severely for
confusing the articles of the Creed and explaining the personal union by the
article concerning the session at God's right hand, taking them as though they
were the same. For in the personal union Christ had not yet sat at God's right
hand, since he had not yet died or ascended into heaven. But in order to prop
up the collapsing ubiquity, which they saw was not firmly enough established
by the personal union,

[Frame 00061; printed p. 57]

they wished to derive it from the session at God's right hand.

There are also these harmful consequences: by this false doctrine concerning
the personal union they establish Marcion's dogma concerning Christ's
invisible and ubiquitous body. Indeed, their entire doctrine concerning the
real communication, or confusion, of properties is plainly Eutychian. And
this single phrase alone, “the majesty of the man Christ,” when they mean to
speak of the humanity, proves them Nestorians.

This doctrine is likewise full of contradictions. The same body will be
everywhere and yet finite; it will be a body and will not be a body; it will be
God and yet a creature.

By this reasoning they also deprive the orthodox of the arguments which they
used against the Arians. The first of these, or certainly one of the foremost,
was this, according to Cyril disputing against Arius in *Thesaurus*, book 12,
chapter 13: he to whom God's actions and properties are attributed and really
belong must necessarily be God.

This position now manifestly conflicts with the articles of faith. Do we not
confess one God in the Creed? Therefore, if Christ's human nature is
omnipotent, omniscient, and everywhere, there will no longer be one God but
two: one created and born in time, the other uncreated and eternal. So too with
all the remaining articles. How could one who was already everywhere in all
things according to the flesh be carried in the Virgin Mary's womb? How could
he suffer, when what is everywhere cannot even be comprehended by place? How
could he die, since death is the separation of soul and body? If the body was
then everywhere, it could not be separated from the soul. How could he be
buried, since he had already been in the tomb beforehand? How could one who
never fell rise again? How, I ask, could one who is everywhere ascend into
heaven? Will one who was already everywhere in his body change place? How
could one who did not depart from earth sit at God's right hand in heaven, and
who first had to suffer before entering into glory? [Marginal reference: Luke
24:26.] How will one who never departed from here return for judgment?

It manifestly conflicts with Scripture itself. Christ himself says, “You will
not always have me with you,” and says that he leaves the world and is no
longer in the world. [Marginal references: Matthew 26; John 16.] The angel
likewise testifies, saying, “Christ is not here; he has risen”; it is remarkable
that they do not call this angel a Nestorian. [Marginal reference: Matthew
28.] Likewise, the words for going away and returning in the Gospel imply a
change of place and therefore utterly overthrow ubiquity. For example, “he
entered through closed doors.” Therefore he was not there before he entered.

[Frame 00062; printed p. 58]

When these objections were raised against their definition of the hypostatic
union as an outpouring of properties, they finally took refuge in saying that
the hypostatic union consists in this: the Word does nothing except with and
through the humanity, just as, they say, the soul does nothing except with and
through the body. But this too is false in many ways. Each nature performs its
own work in this hypostasis, though the resultant work belongs to the whole
person. Thus Christ raised his flesh from the tomb, not through the flesh but
by his divine power; so he saw the Pharisees' thoughts, not with the eyes of
the flesh but by the power of the Spirit; and so too he saw Nathanael while
absent. [Marginal references: Matthew 9:4; Mark 2:8; John 2:25.] Indeed, even
if the Logos performed all his works through the flesh, this would contribute
nothing to constituting the hypostatic union, for the hypostatic union does not
consist in this. The Father performs all things through his Son Jesus Christ,
yet they are not thereby hypostatically united; and each performs through the
Holy Spirit, yet there are three hypostases. So too a scribe uses a pen
whenever he wishes to write, yet he is not thereby hypostatically united to
the pen.

But because their purpose is to defend the real presence of Christ's body in
the bread by whatever means, and because this cannot be established more
conveniently than by the assertion of ubiquity, while they neither can nor
will abandon the ubiquity on which that dogma rests, they strive to escape by
various distinctions from the preceding difficulties that press upon them.
First, therefore, they answer that ubiquity, omnipotence, and omniscience are
indeed properties of the divine nature, but nevertheless may be said of the
human nature personally, though not essentially. How foolishly this is said!
Can anything be said personally of the human nature when it is not a person?
Do they mean that what is said of Christ's person is to be said of the human
nature? If they do, it will follow that the human nature is not a creature,
because it is truly said of the person that the ages were made through him.

Second, they make the exception that the human nature has these from another,
while the divine has them from itself. I answer: it is exceedingly foolish to
say that these properties are common in such a way that they nevertheless
remain proper to the divine nature. It is just as if someone were to deny that
riches formerly proper to one man had really become common after they were
shared with many, because those with whom they had become common did not have
them from themselves but from elsewhere. Who does not see that this exception
contains a contradiction? Moreover, this different manner of existing and
possessing no more removes

[Frame 00063; printed p. 59]

the equality of the natures and their properties than the Son's generation
from the Father and the Holy Spirit's procession from both can be said to make
the Son or Holy Spirit—who do not have their persons from themselves—inferior
to the Father.

Third, they make the exception that the human nature in Christ must be
considered in two ways: “in the Logos” and “outside the Logos,” which they are
accustomed to call “in itself” and *kath’ hauto*. Consequently, they say, it is
not absurd for it to be both everywhere and not everywhere. I answer: this
distinction is foolish and false. Christ's human nature never subsisted, nor
will it subsist, outside the Logos, for it is without a personality of its own.
Consequently, whenever it is considered, it is considered as a true human
nature and as the same nature in the Logos. This distinction would indeed
accomplish something if they could show that it had existed outside the
personal union, or that the personal union had ever been dissolved. They
therefore distort the state of the controversy. The question is not about a
human nature subsisting outside the Logos, since it cannot so subsist, but
about the human nature insofar as it is united to the Logos: whether it, when
considered in that union, is omnipresent, omniscient, and omnipotent.

The same judgment must be made about all the other distinctions they bring
forward to show that it is not absurd for the human nature, considered in
different ways, to be and not to be omnipresent, omnipotent, and omniscient.
These distinctions err first in wholly destroying the very subjects between
which they seem to distinguish. They want to make the body omnipresent, but if
it is so it will not be a body. They likewise introduce a contradiction,
namely, that the body is visible and invisible, everywhere and not everywhere.
Finally, they wish to establish the contrary of what is contained in the
articles of the Creed and in Holy Scripture.

In defense of their error they object that if no equalization of the
properties of the two natures is granted, a sundering of the natures will
follow. On this reasoning, they say, Christ's humanity will have to be judged
united not to the whole deity but only to some part of it; we will have not the
whole Christ but half of him with us; and the Logos will be somewhere that the
flesh is not. I answer that this argument is altogether foolish. It springs
from the false imagining that the Logos, when existing outside the flesh, does
not remain united to the flesh. This is utterly false and impossible, since
the same one who is outside the flesh is simultaneously whole in his flesh,
and therefore is as united to the flesh where he exists outside it as where he
exists within it. There are not two Logoi, one of whom is in the flesh

[Frame 00064; printed p. 60]

and the other outside it; rather, there is one Logos in the humanity and
outside the humanity. To prove them manifestly guilty of error, we demonstrate
as follows that an equalization of the natures does not follow from their
union, and that the separation and sundering of the natures does not follow
where their properties are preserved. In death Christ was personally united
to his flesh—for the personal union was not dissolved even in death—and the
same Christ according to his deity was everywhere. But shall we say that the
body which then lay in the tomb was also everywhere? By no means. Yet the
union was not dissolved, although the body was not diffused everywhere as the
Logos was. Likewise, when he was in Mary's womb he was not in the wombs of
other women, although his deity was everywhere. The union was not meanwhile
dissolved, as the Ubiquitarians think, nor was our nature united to a part of
the Logos—a thoroughly foolish manner of speaking. Likewise, the Logos hanging
on the cross at Jerusalem was not hanging at Rome, although his deity was not
only at Rome but everywhere. Hence it is evident that the personal union does
not entail the omnipresence of each nature, nor are the natures immediately
sundered although they are said to be distinguished by their properties.

If they object that when two things are inseparably united, wherever one is
the other must also be, I answer that the proposition is not true without
qualification. The sun's light is inseparably joined to the sun's body, yet it
does not follow that the sun's body is wherever its light is. Likewise, the
soul and head are inseparably united, yet the head is not wherever the soul
is. Again, God is inseparably joined to Peter, yet it does not follow that
Peter is wherever God is.

If they make the exception that they are speaking of two things inseparably
united in one supposit, so that wherever one is the other must also be, the
Scholastics answer that this is true of things mutually united in such a way
that both are finite and neither exceeds the other. But in Christ, says Thomas
in *Against the Gentiles*, the person exceeds the assumed nature, because that
person is everywhere while the assumed nature is not. Bonaventure, who
philosophized most soberly among the Scholastics, answers this very objection
in the same way, in book 3 of the *Sentences*, distinction 22, question 2. “To
the objection,” he says, “that whatever things are inseparably united extend
themselves equally to every place, it must be said that this holds true when
two unifiable things are so united that

[Frame 00065; printed p. 61]

one does not exceed the other. But if one has a greater extent than the other,
it does not hold true; and so it is in the present case, for the divine nature
is immeasurable, but the human nature is limited.” And this is indeed no
marvel, since it is not even the case with our souls and bodies, which are not
so unlike in nature within a hypostasis as deity and humanity are in Christ's
person. But if the question concerns the manner of speaking, the Scholastics
answer that this proposition is true by the communication of properties:
“Christ the man is everywhere.” So Lombard, distinction 22, book 3: “The whole
Christ,” that is, the whole person subsisting in each nature, “is everywhere,
but not the whole of Christ,” that is, not according to each nature.

They also bring forward this objection, in which they take great pleasure, to
establish the transfusion of properties and by the same reasoning the
omnipresence of Christ's body. Vivification, they say, which is a proper effect
of deity, is attributed to Christ's flesh; cleansing from sins and redemption
are attributed to Christ's blood; and Isaiah ascribes healing to Christ's
stripes. Why, therefore, is omnipresence not attributed to his flesh by equal
reason? I answer: it does not follow, because the cases are not alike.
Vivification is a theandric work, perfected by the activity of each nature,
and so it can in some fashion be attributed both to the natures and to the
person without qualification. But omnipresence is not a resultant work of the
Mediator; it is a property of deity itself. And as we also said above, although
a theandric work is attributed to each nature, it is not ordinarily attributed
to each in the same way. Thus vivification is attributed to the flesh as a
meritorious cause, not as its proper effect immediately effected in creatures
by its own spirit. In this latter sense it is a property of deity, just as
omnipotence is. In this sense Christ also said, “the flesh profits nothing.”

To explain the matter a little more fully, so that it may be clear to everyone
that the vivification ascribed to Christ's flesh does not entail a transfusion
of properties, we shall add a few things.

In John 6 Christ says that he is the life-giving bread. He then adds that this
bread is his flesh, which he is going to give for the life of the world. This
is just as if he had said that his flesh is life-giving, and thus what was
previously predicated of the whole person is now predicated of the one nature.
The question, then, is how Christ's flesh is life-giving.

The Ubiquitarians answer: because in the personal union the Logos poured into
his

[Frame 00066; printed p. 62]

flesh that power of giving life, so that by a real communication of properties
the flesh is life-giving in itself. Thus, when received orally within us in the
Lord's Supper, it gives us life—just as pepper and cinnamon received within
our bodies warm the stomach—and finally, in the resurrection of the dead, it
raises our bodies to eternal life.

We answer: because vivification is a predicate of the Mediator, just as it is
truly said of the person, so in its own manner it may also be said of the
natures—not because of the same activity, but because of the resultant work.
The activity of the flesh is this: by suffering it merited eternal life for us;
it is called life-giving because of its merit.

If it is asked again how flesh can merit life for a human being, the Council of
Ephesus answers: “Conclude, therefore, that it is the flesh of the Word,
because it is called life-giving. For if it did not belong to that person, the
flesh could not be called life-giving.”

That Christ's flesh is not life-giving in the sense the Ubiquitarians
understand—that is, endowed with that activity of giving life which is proper
to the Logos, and exercising this same activity in us after we receive it with
the bodily mouth—is clear from the following:

1. On this reasoning it could no more have died than the deity itself. But it
died and lay dead in the tomb. Therefore it was not endowed with that power of
giving life which the Ubiquitarians invent.

2. Christ is not said to have raised his body through that body itself; rather,
he is said to have been “put to death in the flesh, made alive by the Spirit.”
[Marginal reference: 1 Peter 3:18.]

3. Christ's flesh received within us is not said to be what will raise us on
the last day, but Christ's Spirit, the same Spirit who dwells in him. [Marginal
reference: Romans 8.]

4. The fathers of the Old Testament could not have been saved, because they
could not eat this bread of life, which at that time—in the Ubiquitarians'
sense—did not exist. Yet according to Christ's testimony, whoever does not eat
of this bread cannot be saved. For Christ says not only that eating this bread
gives life, but also that no one can live without this eating. In chapter 7 of
*On the Holy Eucharist*, Bellarmine the Jesuit says that this argument belongs
to Luther and Martyr. He cannot solve it, but calls it a common difficulty.
[Marginal reference: John 6:53.] Nor is it surprising if people who invent
absurd dogmas are so pressed by the force of truth and driven into those
absurdities that they can in no way disentangle and free themselves from them.

[Frame 00067; printed p. 63]

The testimonies of Scripture which they ignorantly bring forward to assert the
omnipresence, omnipotence, and omniscience of Christ's body will easily be
resolved and explained from a knowledge of the forms of expression discussed
below.

## Chapter XII

### That this union is a conjunction of two natures distinguished by their own
properties, while one hypostasis is nevertheless constituted from both

### First aphorism

1. If these natures remain distinguished by their own properties, it does not
immediately follow that there are two persons, as Nestorius maintained. For I
have said that each nature comes together in one person; they do not make two
persons, one called the Son of God and the other the son of man. Rather, it is
the same person who is customarily called now the Son of God and now the son
of man.

2. We therefore rightly rebuke as Nestorian these Ubiquitarian formulas of
speech: “the majesty of the man Christ,” when they wish to speak of the majesty
of the human nature, and “the Son of God assumed the son of man.”

3. Nevertheless, we do not deny that many of the ancients, though absurdly,
called the human nature “the man.” They had not yet been taught by heretics to
distinguish concrete terms from abstract ones. But it should have been
observed that “man” signifies the person or concrete term, while “human nature”
or “humanity” signifies the abstract term or the second nature.

Thus far we have spoken about the natures in Christ, their hypostatic union,
and how it was effected. It now follows that we should speak about the phrases
or formulas of speech which Scripture customarily uses when speaking about
this person. This contributes very greatly to explaining the nature of this
hypostatic union.

[Frame 00068; printed p. 64]

## Chapter XIII

### The various phrases and titles which Scripture uses when speaking about
the Mediator, and the three rules that must be observed in them

### First aphorism

1. This person—namely, God's Son, the Logos incarnate—receives names from the
natures of which he consists and from his office.

2. From both natures, namely the Logos's and ours, he is called Emmanuel, and
in Greek *theanthrōpos*, God-man. [Marginal references: Isaiah 7:14; Matthew
1:23.]

3. From the divine nature which assumed the human he is called God's
only-begotten Son, the Logos, or the Word.

4. From the human nature assumed and sustained by the Logos he is called the
son of man, son of David, son of Mary, seed of the woman, and shoot of David;
and by synecdoche a child, a man, and a male.

5. “The things proper to a nature become common to the person,” as Theodoret
says. Therefore the ancients customarily called these titles of the person,
derived from the natures, the “communication of properties,” about which more
below.

6. From his office this person is named Messiah or Christ, propitiation or
propitiatory sacrifice, ransom, Jesus, Lord, prophet, high priest, mediator,
advocate, king, shepherd, servant of the Father, judge, lamb, and so forth.

7. Because all these words and titles so far adduced signify not only one
nature but both—that is, the whole person—the Scholastics call them concrete.
Hence they say that concrete names stand for the person, not for the natures.
They call the names of the natures abstract, such as deity, divine nature,
spirit; humanity, human nature, flesh, and so forth, whether these are
understood in the union or outside the union.

8. In order to defend the presence of Christ's body in the bread, the
Ubiquitarians corrupted the whole doctrine of Christ's person for us.

[Frame 00069; printed p. 65]

Contrary to truth itself and to the Church's proper usage of speech, they
distorted these designations “abstract” and “concrete” so that they might
deceive the less learned. They call the natures themselves by the names
concrete and abstract. They say that “abstract” denotes a separated nature
considered outside the union, while “concrete” denotes a nature joined or
united to the other nature. Thus, for them, “to be said in the abstract” means
to be said of a nature by itself and separated. Hence they deny that it is
right to say that the human nature received or possesses anything in the
abstract, or that Christ's flesh gives life in the abstract—that is, separated
from the deity. Conversely, they want it to be right to say that the human
nature received omnipresence, omnipotence, and omniscience “in the concrete,”
that is, as united with the deity, and consequently that it is said in the
concrete to be omnipresent.

I ask, what verbal monstrosities are these, since “humanity” is the name of a
nature and an abstract term even insofar as it is understood to be united to
the deity? For the human nature in Christ can never be considered torn away
from the divinity, since the union has never been dissolved. The question is
therefore whether it is omnipresent insofar as it is united to the deity. Who,
apart from these men, ever called the human nature united to the deity a
concrete term, since “concrete” is a name by which the person is designated?
And what sort of monstrous expression is this: “omnipresence is said of the
human nature, not in the abstract but in the concrete”? Is “concrete” anything
other than a designation of the person, or “abstract” anything other than a
designation of the natures? Therefore, to say that omnipresence is said of the
human nature in the concrete, when “human nature” is not concrete but
abstract, is to speak things altogether incoherent and contradictory.

9. This union of two such different natures in one person brings it about that
contraries are predicated of Christ in Scripture without any contradiction,
because they are not predicated in the same respect. Some are predicated in
respect of the divine nature and others in respect of the human. Vigilius the
Martyr proves that this is not absurd, since it is also truly said of us that
we are mortal and immortal—namely, not in the same respect. Thus Christ is
called:

[Frame 00070; printed p. 66]

“Man,” 1 Timothy 2:5: “There is one mediator between God and men, the man
Christ Jesus.”

“Son of man,” Luke 19:10: “The Son of man came to seek and save what was lost.”

“Son of David,” Matthew 22:42.

“Jehovah,” Jeremiah 23:6: “This is his name by which they will call him:
Jehovah our righteousness.”

“Son of God,” Matthew 17:5: “This is that beloved Son of mine in whom I am well
pleased; listen to him.”

“David's Lord,” Psalm 110:1: “The Lord said to my Lord, sit at my right hand,”
and so forth; Matthew 22:43.

“Lord of all,” Acts 10:36: “The word which he declared to the children of
Israel, announcing peace through Jesus Christ, who is Lord of all.”

“Image of God and exact representation of the Father's person,” Colossians
1:15: “Who is the image of the invisible God”; and infinitely many other such
statements throughout Scripture.

“Servant of God and servant of all,” Isaiah 53:11; Zechariah 3:8; Matthew 20:28.

“Form of a servant,” Philippians 2:7.

10. Second, this personal union brings it about that something proper to only
one nature is very often predicated of the whole—that is, of the person—and
the expression is synecdochic. Thus, “the Son of God suffered,” namely, in the
flesh. This occurs whenever properties of the divine or human nature, or the
effects of those properties, are predicated of the person, or of a concrete
name signifying the person.

11. But what is proper to one nature is never predicated of the other nature,
or of an abstract term, for this would yield a false statement. The Holy
Spirit therefore never uses such a formula of speech. Hence Vigilius the
Martyr rightly says in book 5: “It is impious and sacrilegious to refer the
things proper to Christ's flesh to the property of the Word's nature, and to
ascribe the things proper to the Word to the property of the flesh's nature.”
And in the same way

[Frame 00071; printed p. 67]

Theodoret, against Nestorius, dialogues 1, 2, and 3: “When we speak about the
natures, the things fitting each must be attributed to it, and one must know
which things are proper to the deity and which to the humanity. But when we
speak about the person, the things proper to the natures must be made common,
and the things of each nature must be attributed to Christ the Savior; the one
and same must be called God and man, Son of God and son of man,” and so forth.
It must be understood that the union makes the names common.

12. In this class the most striking predication occurs when something proper
to one nature is predicated of the whole—that is, the person—named from the
other nature, as when it is said, “God redeemed the Church with his own blood.”
[Marginal reference: Acts 20:28.] “God” is the name of the person, and indeed
one taken from the other nature, namely the deity, as we said above. “To redeem
the Church with blood” belongs to the nature which has blood, namely the human
nature. Hence this predication appears more unusual than those in which either
a name of the person taken from a nature is placed as subject and a property
of that same nature as predicate, or a name of office is placed as subject and
properties of the natures as predicate. But since it is frequent in Scripture
and was treated well enough by the ancients and the Scholastics, we shall
explain it with examples and the words of the ancients.

13. According to this manner of predication, all things which belong to the
human nature are said of the person named from the divine nature. Thus the Son
of God, or the Logos, is visible, tangible, and corporeal, is in the world, and
was born from Mary. [Marginal reference: 1 John 1.] The Lord of glory was
crucified. [Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians 2:8.] God redeemed the Church
with his own blood. [Marginal reference: Acts 20:28.]

Where, then, is the Ubiquitarians' real communication of properties which they
invent on the side of the divine nature, when here things proper to the human
nature are predicated of the person who received his title from the divine
nature?

14. All divine things are predicated of the person named from the human
nature. Thus the shoot of David is Jehovah; the one whom Thomas saw and
touched is Lord and God. [Marginal reference: John 20:28.] Likewise, the son

[Frame 00072; printed p. 68]

of man is eternal, existed before Abraham, is uncreated and infinite, and is
with us until the consummation of the age. Thus the Son of man is said to have
descended from heaven and to be in heaven while he was on earth. [Marginal
references: John 8:58; Matthew 28:20; John 3:13.]

Where, I ask, is the Ubiquitarians' transfusion of properties? They dispute
about the transfusion of only three—omnipresence, omniscience, and
omnipotence—when all the properties of deity, such as eternity itself and the
rest, are attributed to the man Christ.

15. Because at first sight this predication seemed to attribute the things
proper to the divine nature to the humanity and conversely—for the predication
is mutual—some of the ancients and many more recent writers called it the
“communication of properties.” They nevertheless maintained that the
properties are not communicated in reality—that is, transfused, as the
Ubiquitarians understand it. But they did not speak with sufficient care. For
if God redeemed the Church with his own blood, or if the Son of man, while
existing on earth, was also in heaven, it does not follow that blood was
communicated to the deity or ubiquity to the humanity, since these things are
not there said of the natures but of the person. There is no such communication
between the natures—the word “communion” must here be taken intransitively—since
the properties of the natures remain distinct, as was said above. Rather, it
is a phrase customary to Scripture. Some today therefore do call it a
“communication of properties,” but a verbal one: not because it is fictitious
and invented, but because it pertains chiefly to explaining the phrase.
Nazianzen called it “the bestowal of names”; Theodoret, “the commonality” or
“communion of names.”

16. Others, such as Damascene, Cassian, and many recent writers, say that it is
a figurative expression, namely a synecdoche, and a double one: both because a
subject named from one nature is placed for the whole person, and because a
predicate proper to one nature is attributed to the whole person. This is
entirely correct.

17. Thomas Aquinas and some others, considering the whole proposition,

[Frame 00073; printed p. 69]

say that it is literal, because either a property or an accident is predicated
of the hypostasis as an individual, since there is only one hypostasis in
Christ. For, as Thomas says, because the hypostasis or person of each nature is
the same, the same hypostasis also stands under the name of each nature, and
the things proper to the natures are predicated of that hypostasis as their
supposit.

18. Philip Melanchthon calls it neither literal nor figurative but unusual.
Yet this manner of speech is customary both in the Scriptures and in ordinary
discourse, and is figurative or tropological, namely synecdochic, since by it
something which properly and distinctly pertains to a part of the whole is
declared of the whole.

19. Scripture itself explains the rationale of these predications, showing
that these things are said about the person according to one of the natures.
The particle *kata*, “according to,” must therefore always be understood, so
that the true and proper subject of the attribute may be understood. Thus it
says that Christ suffered, but “in the flesh,” or according to the flesh; and
likewise that he is the Son of David, but according to the flesh. [Marginal
references: 1 Peter 4:1; Romans 1:3.] Ambrose makes this same point in *On the
Faith*, book 2, chapter 4: “Let us preserve,” he says, “the distinction between
divinity and flesh. The one Son of God speaks in each, because each nature is
in the same person; and although the same one speaks, he does not always speak
in one manner. Observe in him now God's glory, now the man's sufferings. As God
he speaks the things that are divine, because he is the Word; as man he says
the things that are human, because he spoke in my substance.” Observing this
same thing, Zwingli, following Damascene, called this figurative expression an
“alteration.” More recent writers, explaining this synecdoche in the same way,
say: “Christ,” or “the man Christ”—for each title denotes the person, not only
one nature—“is everywhere according to the other,” that is, according to the
other nature, to which this property belongs. But if they were to say,
“Christ's body is everywhere, but according to the other,” it would be false
and would be a confusion of properties. Therefore no such manner of speech is
found in Scripture.

[Frame 00074; printed p. 70]

20. The union of the natures in one hypostasis is the cause of these
predications, as Ambrose also rightly observed in the book cited above. “Hence,”
he says, “when we read that the Lord of majesty was crucified, we should not
think that he was crucified in his majesty. Rather, because the same one is God
and man—God through divinity, man through the assumption of flesh—Jesus Christ
is said to have been crucified as the Lord of majesty. For, sharing in both
natures, that is, the human and divine, he underwent suffering in the nature
of man, so that the one who suffered is called without separation both the
Lord of majesty and the Son of man, as it is written: ‘He who descended from
heaven,’” and so forth. Nor is this remarkable, since because of their union
the same holds in earthly things, namely in ourselves. Indeed, the force of
this personal union is so great that even after its dissolution there is still
room for similar formulas. Thus you may say, “Abraham was buried near Hebron,”
although this is true only in respect of his body. Theodoret rightly says,
“The things proper to a nature become common to the person,” and again, “The
communion of names does not produce a confusion of natures.”

21. It is therefore no wonder that the Ubiquitarians, because they did not
observe these things, fell miserably when they posited a certain real
communication or transfusion of properties in order to construct ubiquity.
This is their *prōton pseudos*, their first falsehood and the source of their
error: they thought that whatever is said of Christ's person is truly and
really declared of his human nature. This is plainly false, since it is said of
the person that he existed before Abraham and that he founded the ages, but
these things can in no way be said of the human nature. [Marginal references:
John 8:58; Hebrews 1:2.]

22. In these formulas of speech, therefore, it must be carefully observed that
no necessary argument may be drawn affirmatively from the person or concrete
term to either of the natures in the abstract. Thus: “Jesus Christ died;
therefore his deity died.” Likewise: “The Lord is with us until

[Frame 00075; printed p. 71]

the consummation of the age; therefore his body is with us.” Each inference is
false. Philip Melanchthon calls this error the fallacy of a changed
predication. From consideration of this matter also come Augustine's correct
statements in Letter 57, when he says that the whole Christ is everywhere with
the qualification “as God.” “But it does not follow,” he says, “that what is in
God is everywhere as God.” Likewise Damascene, book 3, chapters 4 and 18: “The
whole Christ is God, but not the whole of Christ is God; for ‘the whole of’
represents the nature, while ‘the whole’ represents the hypostasis.”

23. Third, the names of the office, the resultant works of the office, and the
states of the person are said without qualification neither of the divine nor
of the human nature. They are said of both, or of the whole person according
to both natures, because he is Mediator according to both natures. Thus Christ
is said to be priest, prophet, king, mediator, advocate, and redeemer, and all
power in heaven and on earth is said to have been given to him, and so forth.
Hence these expressions are literal.

24. Because these things are not properties of the natures but belong to the
whole person, Scripture very rarely predicates a resultant work of the office
separately of a nature, but almost always of the person. For it always calls us
to consider the person, because all these things depend upon the hypostatic
union of the natures.

25. Perhaps this moved Damascene to think that the states of the person should
not be predicated simply of the natures but of the person, because he saw that
Scripture calls us to consider the person. He therefore thought it more
correct to say with the Nicene Creed that the one who descended from heaven,
consubstantial with the Father—that is, the God-man—was crucified for us, than
to say that God's flesh suffered.

26. He rightly calls us to consider the person, and it is true that the whole
person was humbled and exalted. But lest in this state

[Frame 00076; printed p. 72]

we confuse the things proper to each, Scripture says that the Lord redeemed
the Church with his own blood and was crucified, but then adds that Christ
suffered in the flesh, lest we think these things also pertain to the deity as
though it had blood and were crucified. [Marginal references: Colossians 1:22;
1 Peter 4:1.]

Thus far concerning the person of the Mediator, who is described in these
words of the Creed: “And in Jesus Christ, his only-begotten Son, who was
conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary.” The first two words,
however, “Jesus” and “Christ,” as also “Lord,” signify his office as well. This
follows next.

## Chapter XIV

### The Mediator's office, its two parts, Priesthood and Kingdom, and its two
stages, humiliation and exaltation

### First aphorism

1. The Mediator's office consists in saving his people, from which the angel
called him Jesus; or, as Cyprian says in his exposition of the Creed, in
bringing into the kingdom of heaven a people led out of the darkness of
ignorance and summoned out of the errors of the world. [Marginal references:
Matthew 1:21; Acts 4:12; 1 John 4:14.]

2. In the same sense he is also called Lord, and indeed by a threefold right:
first, because he is our God who created us; second, because he redeemed us;
third, because he is the firstborn among many brothers and so has dominion and
right over the rest. [Marginal references: Isaiah 25:8–9; Colossians 1:16, 18.]

3. For this end it was appointed that he should be Priest, Prophet, and King.
From these he is properly called Christ, that is, anointed, or appointed to
these offices. And because he is the deliverer both of Gentiles and of Jews,
he is called by the Hebrew and Greek name Jesus Christ. [Marginal reference:
Isaiah 61:1.]

[Frame 00077; printed p. 73]

4. In this anointing—which indeed was not performed with any material oil—two
things must be considered. First, Christ the Mediator is called anointed
because he was ordained by the Father to these offices, and because the
Father's command came together with the promise that the Father's will for
accomplishing our salvation would prosper in his hand. Second, Christ was
suffused with the supreme gifts of the Holy Spirit, for he was anointed with
the Spirit above his companions so that we all might draw from his fullness.
It was not enough to be ordained to this office unless the supreme conferral
of gifts also accompanied it. This anointing was solemnly performed in
Christ's baptism. [Marginal reference: Hebrews 1:9.]

5. We shall reduce these three offices to two, namely Priesthood and Kingdom,
including Prophecy under the latter. These two correspond to those two parts
of the gracious covenant: Priesthood to Merit, and Kingdom to Efficacy.

6. Christ's office is likewise distinguished, according to the manner of its
administration, into Humiliation and Exaltation.

7. Almost all the things done during the time of humiliation are referred to
Christ's Merit or Priesthood. The things done during the time of exaltation
look partly to Christ's Kingdom and partly to his Priesthood, but chiefly to
his Kingdom.

8. Humiliation chiefly concerns the priestly office, because Christ exercised
his royal office sparingly in the state of humiliation, revealing his glory
only as much as the conditions of that time and of humbly performing the
priestly function allowed. For here the royal office had to yield to the
priestly office, because the priestly office—its greatest part, I mean, as
regards the offering—had to be completed on this earth; that is, satisfaction
altogether had to be made. The royal office ought not at all to impede this
satisfaction, but would have impeded it if it had been administered fully and
perfectly, as it is now. For if they had known the Lord of glory, they would
never have crucified him. And this was one

[Frame 00078; printed p. 74]

of the reasons why he forbade the miracles he performed to be publicized:
namely, lest the execution of the priestly office should have been impeded in
this way if he had become known before the proper time. But another reason was
added for his forbidding demons to preach the Gospel: the Gospel announced by
them might deservedly have been suspected of falsehood.

9. Nevertheless, certain sparks of the royal function appeared and shone even
in the humiliation itself, as when he glorified himself on the mountain,
forgave sins, cast his enemies to the ground by his voice alone, and so forth.
[Marginal references: Matthew 17:2; John 18:6.]

10. Exaltation chiefly concerns Christ's royal function. In it, freed from all
miseries, he no longer administers his office in weakness as before, but with
the greatest splendor and glory. No longer subject to death, but triumphing
over death and his enemies, he pours the Holy Spirit into the hearts of the
elect.

11. He also administers the priestly office in exaltation, since, now exalted,
he intercedes for us with the Father. But we shall now speak separately about
each part.

## Chapter XV

### Christ's priestly function, and its two parts, Satisfaction and
Intercession

### First aphorism

1. Christ's humiliation is that by which the Logos, who was in the form of
God, became obedient to the Father in all things in the assumed flesh and,
while serving as the victim for the sins of all the elect and sustaining the
Father's wrath due to our sins, demonstrated obedience to the Father even to
the death of the cross. This obedience is called Christ's Righteousness.
[Marginal reference: Isaiah 53.]

[Frame 00079; printed p. 75]

2. Christ's obedience has two parts: fulfillment of the Law and payment of the
penalty for our sins.

3. Fulfillment of the Law is the first part of Christ's obedience, by which he
rendered perfect obedience to God's Law throughout his whole life. Some call
it Christ's active obedience and the obedience of Christ's holy life.

4. Payment of the penalty for our sins is the second part of Christ's
obedience, when he sustained God's wrath for us, that is, temporal and eternal
penalties. Some call it Christ's passive righteousness, or the obedience of
his passion and death. Therefore the Priesthood shines forth in Christ's
humiliation.

5. A priest is a person ordained by God, or constituted by God's command, for
this end: that by a holy victim and his intercession he may reconcile God to
human beings. [Marginal references: Hebrews 8:3–6; Hebrews 5:1.]

6. A just curse blocked the approach, and God in his office as judge was
offended with us. Therefore, for the priest to reconcile God's favor to us, a
propitiatory sacrifice had to intervene to appease his wrath. Consequently, it
was required that he offer a victim for the people's sins and intercede after
the victim had been offered.

7. But since there could be no victim excellent enough to satisfy God's
infinite justice, which had already been violated, God's own Son became both
priest and victim when he offered himself upon the altar of the cross. Hence
Christ is called *asham*, that is, “sin,” because by metonymy of the subject he
is the victim for sins. [Marginal references: Hebrews 9:14; Hebrews 3:7;
Daniel 9; Isaiah 53:10.]

8. The Priesthood has two parts: Satisfaction through the victim offered, and
Intercession after the offering. This is not because Christ did not also
intercede for us with the Father on earth, but because after ascending into
heaven he solemnly undertook the function of intercession.

9. From these parts of his priesthood Christ is called Redeemer, namely from
satisfaction, and Mediator or Advocate from intercession,

[Frame 00080; printed p. 76]

although we are accustomed to signify each function by the title Mediator.

10. The two parts are most closely connected. Hence John, after saying, “We
have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ,” immediately adds, “And he is
the propitiation for our sins.” And Paul says, “There is one mediator between
God and men, the man Jesus Christ, who gave himself as a ransom for all.”

11. He fully discharged the function of Redeemer on earth during the time of
his humiliation, and the function of Mediator in heaven during the time of his
exaltation—although he also interceded and prayed for his own while on earth,
as we have said. We shall treat this under Christ's exaltation.

## Chapter XVI

### Christ's Satisfaction, and first Christ's suffering under Pontius Pilate,
his crucifixion, and death

### First aphorism

1. For the Son of God to discharge the parts of the Redeemer and offer himself
as a propitiatory victim is this: to transfer to himself all the sins, without
exception, of all those—both dead and living—for whom he offers himself to the
heavenly Father in the death of the cross; and thus to present himself as the
guilty party and debtor for them all, so that all the Law's curses might be
poured out upon him, all the waves and tempests of God's wrath strike his
heart, and he finally become a curse and be abandoned by God for a time in the
greatest disgrace, until satisfaction has been made in full for the sins of
those for whom he offers himself. [Marginal references: Isaiah 53:4–6; Hebrews
5:7.]

2. Thus by the Father's decree Christ was made a victim for our sins. God
caused all our iniquities to meet upon him, and so he paid those penalties due
to us, not only temporal but eternal, of body, I say, and soul. Hence the
Apostle says that Christ will be seen without sin at his second advent. He was
not, of course, a sinner in himself at his first advent, but he was so in

[Frame 00081; printed p. 77]

us: for he was surrounded by all our sins and consequently was subject to
miseries, death, and all things that arise from sin. All these have now been
removed through his death. [Marginal references: Isaiah 53; Hebrews 9:28.]

3. These penalties began immediately with his entrance into this world. He
suffered afflictions and the persecutions Herod stirred up immediately after
his birth, and throughout his whole life poverty, misery, disgrace, insults,
and so forth.

4. But because they shone out most brightly in death itself, they are omitted
in the Creed, and it passes immediately from the birth of God's Son to his
passion and the pains of death. All these are contained together in these
words, “suffered under Pontius Pilate,” and then individually, “was crucified,
died,” and so forth.

5. But perhaps someone may be frightened by this sort of doctrine, since we
now treat the death of the one whom a little before we called coeternal with
God the Father and begotten from his substance, and whom we taught to be one
with the Father in kingdom, eternity, and majesty. But, faithful hearer, I do
not wish you to be afraid. A little later you will see again as immortal the
one whom you now hear is dead. For death is assumed by the one who will strip
death of its spoils. Cyprian, in his exposition of the Creed.

6. Three stages must be observed in this humiliation of God's Son: first, that
he was crucified and died; second, that he was buried; third, that he descended
into hell. Three stages of exaltation are opposed to these: first,
resurrection; second, ascension into heaven; third, session at the Father's
right hand in heaven.

7. Here we must consider not so much the external pains of the body as the
internal pains of the soul, which truly were the pains of hell. For Christ felt
God's immeasurable wrath in soul and body, which showed itself more openly as
the time of death approached.

8. The pains of the soul—for he had to deliver not only our bodies but chiefly
our souls—were those which Christ felt after administering the Supper, and
which rose through their own successive degrees.

[Frame 00082; printed p. 78]

First, feeling God's wrath kindled against our sins and these torments of soul,
Christ cries, “Remove this cup from me,” but adds, “Not as I will.” [Marginal
reference: Matthew 26:37–38.] Then they so increase that he sweats clots of
blood. Finally, as though overwhelmed by them and abandoned by God, he cries
out, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” [Marginal references: Psalm
22; Matthew 27.] For, as Irenaeus says in book 3, chapter 20, against the
Valentinians, he rested so that he could be tempted, dishonored, crucified, and
die.

9. If an angel, or all the angels, had had to endure these pains, they would
have been reduced to nothing and utterly crushed by God's wrath.

10. The bodily pains are those which he suffered from the soldiers after he
was captured, and then in death after he was crucified.

11. After Christ had struggled in the garden with God's wrath, which was
kindled against our sins, he was captured—namely, so that he might die, because
God had said, “Dying you shall die,” and because without the shedding of blood
there is no remission of sins. He was captured in a garden because, as the
ancients who pursue allegories hold, the first sin was committed in a garden.
He was deserted by his disciples in accordance with the prophecies. [Marginal
references: Zechariah 13:7; Matthew 26.]

12. He is brought to trial—for it was not fitting that he should be killed in
a riot—so that the case might be examined. We had to be brought before God's
eternal judgment, so that God might condemn us for our sins. Now, however, we
shall never appear before God's tribunal because of our sins, but only to hear
that decree of God by which he will adjudge to us the taking possession of the
eternal inheritance.

13. This judgment before which Christ was brought was twofold: ecclesiastical
and political.

14. This ecclesiastical judgment was a general council or convoked synod, over
which Caiaphas the high priest presided. Its purpose was that the stone should
be rejected by the builders upon which we would be built.

15. For this purpose they used false testimony concerning the Temple. But God
confounded the liars so that they might understand that Christ died not for
himself but for us.

16. Christ

[Frame 00083; printed p. 79]

remains silent at their false testimonies, because he was brought to trial not
to defend himself but to suffer, and this by divine appointment.

17. Nevertheless, when put under oath by the high priest, he answers and
confesses both his essence and his priestly and royal office. For when Caiaphas
says, “I adjure you by the living God to tell us whether you are the Christ,
that Son of God,” he answers, “You have said it.” Mark adds, “I am,” namely,
that Son of God. [Marginal reference: Mark 14:62.]

18. From this confession he is immediately condemned as though he had
blasphemed, that is, taken honor away from God the Father and claimed it for
himself. Christ is therefore condemned because he confessed himself to be the
Son of God, or equal to God, since Adam had sought equality with God.

19. Disgraces follow condemnation: they spit in his face, strike him with
their fists, and so forth. These disgraces ought to have been the fruits of
our condemnation, which Christ was willing to suffer so that he might free us
from every disgrace.

20. The political trial took place under Pontius Pilate. The Roman governor's
name is expressed as evidence that, when Christ suffered, the royal scepter had
already been taken from the Jews, as had been foretold, and consequently that
no other Messiah was to be expected. Second, it is stated so that we may be
reminded that we have escaped not only punishment but also condemnation in the
divine judgment, because the innocent Christ was condemned for us by an
earthly judge.

21. In this political trial you will observe the adversaries' accusation and
then Christ's defense.

22. The accusation has these parts: first, he is accused of sedition; second,
of forbidding tribute to be paid to Caesar; third, of seeking the kingdom.
[Marginal reference: Luke 23:2.] The hinge of the case turns on this last part,
for they chiefly accuse him of treason—the offense of which we were
guilty—because he had called himself king. The remaining parts of the
accusation were a contrived pretext for proving this crime of seeking the
kingdom. This is evident, first, because their conclusion in the ecclesiastical
council was, “What need do we have of witnesses, since he has said that he is
the Son of God and will reign at the right hand of the power

[Frame 00084; printed p. 80]

of God?” Second, it is also evident that this was the chief accusation because
Pilate questions him only about this and does not touch the rest as too empty.
Third, it is evident from the title on the cross written by Pilate.

23. It must therefore be carefully observed that the whole action instituted
against the Son of God was an action for treason, both in the ecclesiastical
court and the political court: in the former against divine majesty, in the
latter against human majesty, that is, Caesar's. Thus Christ transferred to
himself the crime of treason belonging to us all and expiated it by obeying.

24. Christ's answer and defense: Christ answers from the equivocation of the
word and teaches what sort of kingdom his is.

25. After hearing the scribes' accusation and Christ's defense, the judge
pronounces him innocent—for Christ was righteous in himself—and therefore
plans to free him. To do this more conveniently, he offers the Jews a choice
between the greatest brigand and Christ, for he thought they would ask that
Christ be released to them rather than the greatest brigand. In addition he
scourges Christ, so that by this means he might perhaps appease their minds;
but it accomplishes nothing. For those who wish to scourge Christ to please
men are also compelled a little later to kill and crucify him. Therefore a
little later he condemns the one whom he previously pronounced innocent and,
after releasing the brigand Barabbas, hands Christ over to be crucified. For
Christ was the most guilty and unrighteous in us, because he presented himself
before the Father as our surety, so that he might be judged altogether worthy
of death.

26. In this accusation and legal proceeding the Jews' cry must be carefully
observed, for by it they, raving and mad, confirm that Jesus is the Messiah.
They cry that they have no king but Caesar. [Marginal reference: John 19:15.]
Therefore the scepter has departed from Judah and the Messiah has come.

27. Condemned by the judge Pontius Pilate, Christ is handed over to the
governor's cohort to be punished.

28. The guards mock him with purple, bending the knee, a reed scepter,

[Frame 00085; printed p. 81]

a crown of thorns, and spitting in his face. For human beings had deserved
such things because they had sought divine majesty.

29. We must weigh all these things, not so that we lament the fate of one who
now reigns gloriously because of them—which alone foolish preachers in former
centuries strove to accomplish—but so that we may be persuaded of the Lord's
immeasurable love toward us. That love compelled the Lord of all things and
the eternal Son of God himself to endure so many and such unworthy things for
the sake of our salvation. This should both rouse us to integrity of life and
produce hatred of sin in us, when we see that our sins were so great that, to
expiate them, the obedience of God's Son joined with extreme disgrace and the
bitterest torments had to intervene. As for those foolish people, they ought
to mourn more for themselves and their own than for Christ.

30. He is led outside Jerusalem bearing the cross, the mark of our curse. The
reason is noted in Hebrews 13. Just as the bodies of those animals upon which
the sins of the Israelite people had been placed were led outside the camp and
were therefore accursed, so that the priests ate nothing from them, so Christ
bears our sins and the curse due to them, together with the cross as the
symbol of the curse, outside Jerusalem as an accursed victim unworthy of human
society.

31. The city of Jerusalem was appointed for Christ's death, for there Abraham
had once wished to offer his son Isaac, who was a type of Christ. The place of
Calvary was appointed so that the standard of salvation might be raised where
the ground of the condemned had formerly been.

32. He is crucified. Death by the cross was cursed by God's own mouth.
[Marginal references: Deuteronomy 21:23; Galatians 3:13.] God knew well
beforehand by what death Christ would die, since he himself had assigned this
very death to his Son. God's Son therefore wished to undergo this very curse
so that he might free us from every curse. Isaac was likewise placed upon a
pile of wood,

[Frame 00086; printed p. 82]

and in the wilderness Moses raised up a bronze serpent placed upon a pole. As
the ancients say, just as sin occurred on wood, satisfaction was made on wood.

33. Because Christ offered himself on the cross, it served him as the place of
an altar. Indeed, the apostles everywhere explain Christ's death by alluding
to the lifting up of a victim on the altar. For the victim was lifted upon the
altar and slaughtered there, and Christ was lifted from the earth, suspended,
and slain upon the cross. Consequently, when they speak about Christ's
crucifixion they use the same formula of speech as for the offering of victims.
[Marginal references: Hebrews 9; 1 Peter 2:24.]

34. He is crucified between two brigands and prays for sinners, so that the
Scriptures might be fulfilled: “He was numbered among evildoers, and prayed
for sinners.” [Marginal reference: Isaiah 53.] Hence Augustine said that the
cross was made Christ's pulpit from which he preached. The fruit is expressed
in John 3: “Just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so the Son
of man must also be lifted up, that everyone who believes in him should not
die but have eternal life.” He was lifted upon the cross so that we might be
raised into heaven.

35. While he hangs upon the cross, one of the brigands acknowledges him as the
Mediator and Redeemer of the world. [Marginal reference: Luke 23:40.] This is
wonderful, as Augustine says. This man begins to cling to Christ by faith when
the disciples were deserting him in unbelief. He flees to Christ when the
others were fleeing from him. He gives Christ what he had, his tongue and
heart, when others were stripping Christ. He defends Christ's innocence when
others judged him worthy of death. He asks for the kingdom of heaven from the
one whom he sees placed in the greatest miseries. Finally, he asks to be
remembered by the one whom he sees about to die at any moment.

36. The time of the crucifixion is noted: it took place at the feast of
Passover. Christ wished it to take place at that very time so that he might
show himself truly to be that Passover lamb.

[Frame 00087; printed p. 83]

37. After the crucifixion follows, “He died.” The crucifixion would have
profited nothing unless the soul had been torn from the body. Death is the
crowning point of satisfaction for the human race, for in death Christ said,
“It is finished.” Thus for human sins God's Son underwent temporal death and,
by tasting eternal death, swallowed it up. For he was infinite and therefore
could not be swallowed up by it. [Marginal reference: Acts 2:24.]

38. Hence comes that highest principle of Christian doctrine: without the
shedding of blood, or a bloody death, no remission of sins occurs, or the
memory of no sin is laid aside. Christ therefore died to ratify the Testament
and remove the fear of death. [Marginal reference: Hebrews 9.]

## Chapter XVII

### Christ's burial

### First aphorism

1. The second stage of Christ's humiliation is his Burial. Burial is a
continuation of death and consequently also of the covenant's confirmation.
For he wished to be buried so that it might be certain he was truly dead. The
type of Jonah shows how this serves to confirm the covenant. Just as the
tempest of the sea was calmed and tranquility restored when Jonah was thrown
into the sea, so, when Christ was cast out from the number of the living and
laid in the tomb, all the tempests of God's wrath stirred up against our sins
were calmed, as that serene face of God our Savior began to shine. And just as
the same submerged Jonah was the instrument of the sailors' conversion to God,
so we read that after his death and resurrection Christ converted many who had
been the instruments of his death.

2. This burial and rest in the tomb continued until the third day, yet in such
a way that Christ's body remained free from corruption.

3. The cause of corruption arises from sin, but there was no sin

[Frame 00088; printed p. 84]

in Christ's flesh or bones. Indeed, besides having no sins of his own, Christ
no longer had the sins of others which he had willed to be imputed to himself,
for he had abolished them through the preceding death.

## Chapter XVIII

### Christ's descent into hell

### First aphorism

1. The third and lowest stage of Christ's humiliation is his descent into
hell. Although, according to Cyprian's testimony, this article is not contained
in some Creeds, it was nevertheless received by the orthodox Fathers.

2. The basis upon which those who omitted this article from Creeds of faith
relied seems to have been taken from the Apostle's words in 1 Corinthians 15:
“For I delivered to you what I had received, namely, that Christ died for our
sins, was buried, and was raised on the third day.”

3. It is indeed remarkable that the Nicene Fathers, so many synods that
followed, and even the Roman Church itself should have wished rashly to expunge
this article if it had been written in so many words in that common Creed.

4. However the matter stands, in the whole of Christ's passion and the
penalties which he sustained for our sins, those pains and anguishes of soul
which we discussed above are especially prominent. Certainly this article, by
which they are signified, deserves to occupy its place among the rest because
of the supreme consolation which God's children receive from it.

5. Before we state what is signified by the composite expression “Christ's
descent into hell,” we shall see what is understood by the words “hell” and
“descent” taken individually.

6. “Hell” is used in three ways in Scripture. First, it signifies—and this
literally—the grave. [Marginal references: Psalm 16; Genesis 42:38.] Second,
the place of the condemned, as in the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, and
indeed by a transferred sense. Thus the place

[Frame 00089; printed p. 85]

of the condemned is signified because, since a foul odor, horror, and every
impurity are customarily found in graves, the word “hell” is transferred to
signify the place where condemned unbelievers are punished among evils of
every kind. Third, it signifies the pains of hell, that is, terrors and
torments of soul and conscience such as the condemned feel in hell. This
meaning too is transferred, for it is a metonymy of the subject. [Marginal
references: Psalm 116:3; 1 Samuel 2:6.]

7. The word “descent” literally signifies motion from a higher place to a
lower. This meaning cannot be accommodated to this article, since such a
change of place from higher to lower is fitting neither to the deity properly
speaking, which is everywhere; nor to the body, which lay dead in the tomb;
nor to the soul, which was commended to the Father and immediately after its
separation from the body was in Paradise. It must therefore be understood in a
transferred sense as a stage of Christ's humiliation.

8. Hence, because of the different meanings of these words, this article is
explained in different ways. Some think that “Christ's descent into hell”
signifies that Christ descended in his soul itself to the devils in hell so
that he might undergo the pains of hell there and destroy the powers of hell.
They think—but foolishly and falsely—that Peter signifies this. [Marginal
reference: 1 Peter 3:19.] Yet in death Christ said, “It is finished,” and
Hebrews 2 says that through death he took dominion away from the devil.
Therefore nothing remained for him to suffer. Add that he commended his spirit
to the Father and said to the brigand, “Today you will be with me in Paradise.”
Hell is certainly not there.

9. Others think that “hell” here means the grave, so that “to descend into
hell” will be the same as to be buried. It is true, as we noted above, that
this word is sometimes used this way in the Scriptures. But it would be
altogether absurd in such a summary to repeat the same thing twice and explain
a plain matter through a more obscure one.

10. Others say that the descent into hell signifies those supreme pains of
soul which Christ sustained, about which

[Frame 00090; printed p. 86]

we spoke above. Thus Samuel's mother, speaking about the greatest distresses
and sorrow, says that God makes one descend to hell and rise again from hell.
They say these pains are placed after the bodily pains because the latter
strike the senses first, while the pains of the soul—which they think are
signified by these words—do not. It can be objected, however, that although
they ought to be placed after death and crucifixion, they ought not to be
placed after burial, in which there was no pain.

11. Others think that what is understood here is not so much those pains of
soul as that greatest disgrace—which itself also accompanies the condemned in
hell—by which God's Son was overwhelmed when he lay in the tomb until the
third day as if prostrated and conquered by death and the devil, while the
devil and the Pharisees, as it were, exulted over him as if he no longer
existed. These latter two opinions are orthodox and true, and both can be
joined in the explanation of this article.

Thus far concerning that price of redemption, which was paid not to Satan but
to God and by which satisfaction was made to his justice. This price of
redemption is followed by the overthrow of Satan's kingdom, to which we had
previously been subject by God's just judgment.

## Chapter XIX

### That Christ's Satisfaction has two fruits: a person's justification
before God, and eternal life

### First aphorism

1. The fruit of so great a humiliation and of this victim once offered on the
cross is this: that this great obedience of God's Son in his passion,
crucifixion, death, burial, and descent into hell, together with the obedience
which he rendered throughout his whole life, is itself the satisfaction for
all our sins, or our righteousness before God, and the cause of our adoption
and heavenly inheritance. [Marginal reference: Hebrews 5:9.]

2. This entire benefit is generally called *apolytrōsis*, redemption,

[Frame 00091; printed p. 87]

by which the Son of God, made a victim on the cross, destroyed sin and death
and brought us righteousness and life. [Marginal references: Romans 3:24;
Ephesians 2:18.]

3. Therefore we are not only freed from sin and death, but righteousness and
life are also conferred upon us.

4. This benefit is the fruit of the obedience of the whole person, not of only
one of the natures. For each nature had to concur in the production of this
resultant work, and this was the obedience of God Jehovah himself, Emmanuel.
Hence it is rightly said that God's Son is the Redeemer, not that the human
flesh is the redeemer, and rightly that God's Son suffered, although in the
flesh. For he sustained his flesh so that it would not succumb to those
penalties, and likewise so that it would not see corruption.

5. Indeed, the worthiness of the merit depends on the fact that the one who
suffered was God, or the incarnate Logos. Hence the apostles, when speaking
about the good things we obtained through Christ's death, carefully mention
the deity, as: God redeemed the Church with his own blood. [Marginal
references: Acts 20:28; Isaiah 1:20.]

6. And indeed this resultant work belongs to the person, but as the victim
once offered upon the cross. Hence the Temple veil was torn as soon as Christ
expired. This signified that it was now permitted for us to look and enter
into heaven, and that we obtain this by no other means than this alone. Indeed,
so far are we from needing other means for this purpose that we no longer need
a repeated offering of God's Son, for he was offered once to take away the
sins of many. [Marginal reference: John 1:29.]

7. These things are not to be understood as if we do not likewise obtain these
benefits from the exaltation and intercession of God's Son. Rather, they are
said because by his obedience and death he acquired and merited these things,
while by his intercession he preserves for us the things he acquired.

8. We say that all believers have received this redemption through Christ's
obedience, because just as Adam's disobedience did not remain contained in him
but was diffused to his offspring and descendants, so Christ's righteousness
also flows to those who believe in him, so that it is imputed to them for
righteousness,

[Frame 00092; printed p. 88]

and, having been constituted righteous in him, they become heirs of eternal
life. Just as Adam—and we in him—guilty of an offense against divine majesty
and stripped of those ornaments which he received at creation, dreaded the
sight of God and fled from him, so, conversely, we who have been constituted
righteous in Christ, fully persuaded with all believers that in Jehovah, the
shoot of David, righteousness reigns over sin and death, flee to God and dare
to present ourselves fearlessly before his sight as that of our beloved Father.

**Objection.** Adam subjected everyone to condemnation; Christ frees only some
and exempts them from condemnation. Therefore Christ's satisfaction is weaker
than Adam's fall.

**Answer.** The inference is denied, because the power, efficacy, and excellence
of Christ's satisfaction do not consist in the multitude of people applying
Christ's merit to themselves but in the greatness of the benefit itself.
Second, the satisfaction is not the cause why few are saved through Christ;
the cause lies in human beings, who do not apply Christ's merit to themselves
as universally as they applied Adam's sin. Third, God also wishes to show
himself not only merciful but just.

9. Redemption has two parts: liberation from sin and liberation from death.
By these we understand not only the expulsion of evils but also the restoration
of all the things we lost through the fall; and indeed they are conferred in
many ways more richly and abundantly than they were in the first creation.

10. These parts are established because our misery likewise has as many parts,
namely sin and death, that is, the evil of guilt and the evil of punishment.

11. Scripture distinguishes this redemption in the same sense into “the
redemption of liberation” and “the redemption of possession.” The former
signifies liberation from sin and restoration of righteousness; the latter,
liberation from death and restoration of eternal life. [Marginal reference:
Ephesians 1:14.]

12. This distinction indeed concerns not so much the parts themselves
individually

[Frame 00093; printed p. 89]

as the state of each: namely, the beginning with its growth in this life and
its completion in the other.

13. For although we have already obtained each of these fruits in Christ, so
that in his own mind God fully justifies us in this life and has certainly
decreed to confer eternal life upon us at his appointed time, we nevertheless
do not fully experience either the first or second fruit in this life.
Consequently, Scripture says that we are saved in hope, not yet in reality,
and that we are already God's children but what we will be has not yet
appeared. [Marginal references: Romans 8:24; 1 John 3:2.] Theologians likewise
say that we are freed in this life by inception, and in the future life by
consummation. It is just as an infant or orphan, when his father has died, is
indeed heir to all his father's goods. Yet he does not fully enjoy them; in
fact, he receives nothing from them except what is altogether necessary for
his food and clothing, and will receive them fully when he has reached mature
age. So we, as long as we live in the adolescence of this life, indeed
experience that our heavenly Father does not fail us, but we do not see him
confer the full enjoyment of heavenly goods in this life.

14. This easily resolves Romans 7:23, where it is said that we are still held
captive by sin although we are regenerated. Understand it in a qualified
sense. For the benefit of Christ our Liberator, which is Christian liberty,
does not perish altogether. Yet while life is lived here its use and progress
are often halted, as if impediments had been thrown in the way.

15. The Apostles' Creed refers these benefits obtained for us by Christ's
death to three heads, which can nevertheless be reduced to those two preceding
members of redemption. In the Creed we believe the remission of sins in
Christ, against the Law's accusations and every misery which sin carries with
it. We believe the resurrection of the flesh, against the penalties and wages
of sin, that is, most bitter death. We believe eternal life, against eternal
condemnation. Two conclusions follow.

[Frame 00094; printed p. 90]

**First conclusion.** Since, then, we have obtained such and so great benefits
in Christ, it is established that we have a most perfect Savior in Christ. The
reason is the perfection of his merit. The perfection of the merit depends on
these things: first, because this person who suffered is God, for God redeemed
the Church with his own blood; second, from the severity of the punishment he
sustained, for he suffered such torments that the condemned cannot sustain
such great and sufficient afflictions and torments throughout eternity, and
therefore cannot satisfy God's wrath and justice.

**Objection.** The punishment due to sin, and therefore sufficient for it, must
be eternal. But Christ does not seem to have sustained an eternal punishment,
because he did not suffer eternally.

**Answer.** A punishment sufficient for sin must be eternal or must be
equivalent to an eternal punishment. But the punishment Christ sustained is
equivalent to an eternal one for the reasons already stated.

**Second conclusion.** That distinction between guilt and punishment among the
papists is execrable and wicked. By it they think the punishment is retained
after the guilt is remitted, thus tearing apart the fruits and benefits of
Christ's death, as if a liberated person could receive the one but not the
other. From this wicked distinction arose that execrable doctrine concerning
meritorious satisfactions before God, indulgences, purgatory, and prayers for
the dead.

16. Because the benefits we obtain in Christ are so excellent, and so great
that no human voice can explain them sufficiently, Scripture is accustomed to
compare Christ together with his benefits to natural things which are either
best and dearest or altogether necessary for the preservation of that for
which they are appointed. Hence he is called light, pearl, treasure,
inheritance, bread of life, water of life, foundation, and our life. [Marginal
reference: Colossians 3:4.]

[Frame 00095; printed p. 91]

## Chapter XX

### The first fruit of Christ's Satisfaction, which is called liberation from
sin, or a person's Justification before God

### First aphorism

1. To establish what is properly understood by this first fruit and in what a
person's justification before God consists, this distinction must be set down
first, since “liberation from sin” is not used in only one way.

2. Liberation from sin is customarily given two parts. The first is that by
which all sins are removed as regards guilt, while Christ's Righteousness is
imputed in their place. The second is that by which sins not only are removed
as regards guilt but also gradually die in this life through the regeneration
of life and lose their reign as newness of the Spirit succeeds them. Augustine
said of the former that sins are taken away not so that they do not exist but
so that they are not imputed. Of the latter it is said that sins lose their
reign on earth and their root in heaven, for regeneration will be perfect in
the other life.

3. We rightly referred this latter part, which is nothing other than the
sanctification of life, to the second fruit of Christ's satisfaction, as will
be stated in the following chapter. The first belongs properly to this place,
and a person's justification consists in it.

4. Liberation from sin according to that first meaning, therefore, is that by
which a person is constituted perfectly righteous in God's sight because of
Christ's merit. Hence through Christ we are said to be reconciled to God, made
near to God, and made God's friends.

5. The righteousness from which a person is called righteous and which is
understood by the designation “liberation from sin” is generally called
Christ's obedience by Paul. For God reckons this to be ours just as if every
believer had borne God's wrath in his own body and soul and had undergone and
sustained the same things which Christ sustained for their sake and in their
name. [Marginal reference: Romans 5:19.]

[Frame 00096; printed p. 92]

6. Because this obedience shone out most greatly in Christ's death, our
righteousness is therefore everywhere ascribed to the obedience of Christ's
death, from which we are said to be justified by his death. [Marginal
reference: Romans 5:10.]

7. And because the shedding of blood shone out most brightly in his death, we
are therefore said to be reconciled and made near to God through his blood.
[Marginal references: Romans 3:25; Romans 5:9; Ephesians 2:13.]

8. From this satisfaction for our sins Christ is called victim, sacrifice,
satisfaction, propitiation, redemption, reconciliation, our peace, ransom, and
counter-ransom. Hence what Christ acquired for us by his humiliation is called
*apolytrōsis*, redemption.

9. And because God accepts for ours this obedience of Christ, which we have
noted is called in the Scriptures the obedience of Christ's death and blood,
it is called imputed righteousness.

10. God's act of justifying, or accepting this righteousness as ours, is
called Remission of sins and Imputation of Righteousness.

11. It is difficult to say which of these actions precedes the other. God
cannot impute righteousness to anyone whose sins he has not remitted, nor remit
the sins of one to whom he has not imputed Christ's Righteousness.

12. But this must be carefully considered: in Christ we are constituted not
only without sin but also supremely pleasing to God and adorned with the most
perfect righteousness.

13. Imputation is that benefit of the heavenly Father by which he deigns to
reckon Christ's righteousness as ours. That is, he reckons as ours and credits
to our account his Son's obedience, by which, perfectly fulfilling the Law,
the Son sustained that penalty announced in the Law which God could by his own
right demand from us. [Marginal reference: Romans 4:3.]

14. Remission of sins is God's act by which, imputing no sin to the elect and
believers, he frees them from all the penalties of sin and freely gives them
eternal life because of Jesus Christ's merit.

15. It is of the greatest importance for us to know this—that sins are, I say,
perfectly remitted. Because of the horrible

[Frame 00097; printed p. 93]

falls and enormous sins into which we lapse even after embracing Christ, and
because throughout our whole life we carry around the flesh, the fertile
mother of all sins, we often hesitate. We think within ourselves whether God
can regard as righteous those who daily and continually provoke that righteous
God to wrath, or whether he can remit such great and horrible sins. Scripture
therefore illustrates this part magnificently and with splendid metaphors so
that such thoughts cannot rightly arise in us. Thus God is said to have cast
our sins behind his back, erased them like a cloud, sunk them into the depth of
the sea, kept them covered, and bleached sins red as scarlet whiter than snow.
[Marginal references: Isaiah 38:17; Isaiah 44:22; Micah 7:19; Psalm 32:1;
Isaiah 1:18.]

**Objection.** But how is our justification called remission of sins when a
payment intervened and Christ sustained that penalty announced by the Law, so
that God cannot appear to have acted leniently with us or yielded anything of
his right?

**Answer.** Because although satisfaction was made, the payment nevertheless
did not proceed from us but from God himself, to whom we owed eternal penalties.

16. This satisfaction concerns all sins already committed or to be committed
in the future, because the blood of Jesus Christ cleanses us from every sin.
[Marginal reference: Colossians 2:13.] Satisfaction was therefore made both
for actual and original sin. It is not the case, as certain people
blasphemously assert, that we must satisfy for actual sins by our own
satisfactions. On this reasoning Christ's victim would not have been
sufficiently efficacious or sufficient. Moreover, his death would have
profited us nothing. For if God remitted only certain sins because of Christ's
obedience, we would have to perish just as if he pardoned no sins, since one
sin rendered the whole human race liable to destruction. On the contrary,
Christ's victim is sufficient to abolish all sins, as even the type itself
testifies in Leviticus 16. When that universal expiatory sacrifice is described,

[Frame 00098; printed p. 94]

all sins are said to be cast upon the goat. And in Matthew 18 it is said, “I
forgave you the whole debt.” God's mercy toward the human race is full and
perfect.

17. Romans 3:25, however, says, “through the remission of preceding sins,” as
if only those sins had been removed by this victim. It says this for these
reasons. First, before that shedding of blood no sins had been paid for in
reality, for the blood of bulls and goats could not take them away. Second,
the Gospel does not announce the remission of sins that have not yet been
committed, because what does not yet exist cannot be remitted. Third, this
would conflict with repentance. Meanwhile, it was demonstrated above that
Christ's propitiation also extends to sins committed after baptism.

18. From this it follows that, since satisfaction was made for sin or guilt,
the liability or punishment has been abolished. For where there is no sin,
there is no obligation to punishment. We shall therefore never have to pay
penalties for sins for which the fullest satisfaction has already been made to
God's justice. The afflictions still laid upon us, about which in their proper
place, are certain exercises and chastisements, not penalties for sins.

19. Because this satisfaction, or Christ's merit, is always efficacious and
precious, it caused John the Baptist to say, “Behold the Lamb of God who takes
away the sin of the world.” He did not say, “who took away” or “will take away,”
but in the present tense, “who takes away.”

20. This same righteousness which God imputes to us and for which he remits
sins is called the Righteousness of faith in the Scriptures, because it is
apprehended by us through faith and becomes ours. Hence we are also said to be
justified by faith. [Marginal references: Romans 10:6; Hebrews 11:7.]

21. It is therefore worth observing that, in the divine mind and in reality,
the imputation of Christ's obedience, or our justification, precedes the
sending of the Holy Spirit and therefore faith and repentance, or all
sanctification. For Christ died while we were still enemies, and God does not
give the Holy Spirit to one to whom he still imputes sins and whom he regards
as unrighteous. But in execution and in us, the gift of the Holy Spirit and
repentance precede justification.

[Frame 00099; printed p. 95]

For we then truly perceive and conclude that we are justified when the Holy
Spirit has begun to dwell in us.

22. From this one may also see why Christ's satisfaction, although sufficient
for the sins of all human beings—indeed, more than sufficient, for grace
abounded above the offense, and it is the righteousness of God's Son, which is
greater than the obedience of a mere man, indeed of angels—nevertheless does
not serve everyone. The cause lies not in any insufficiency of the satisfaction
but in people's lack of the instrument by which this righteousness must be
applied to them, namely faith. This instrument is found only in the elect, to
whom God promised free salvation and therefore also that he would give the
faith by which it can be apprehended.

## Chapter XXI

### The second fruit of Christ's Satisfaction, namely Eternal Life, whose
firstfruits in this life are the gifts of the Holy Spirit poured into us

### First aphorism

1. The other fruit which we obtain from the humiliation of God's Son, and
which flows from the first, is the inheritance of eternal life. [Marginal
reference: Hebrews 10:19.]

2. It is called liberation from death and contains two things. First,
liberation from calamities and all the miseries of this life insofar as they
proceed from God when angry and avenging our sins. [Marginal reference: Romans
6:23.] Second, liberation from the sense of God's wrath, despair, and eternal
death. [Marginal reference: 1 Thessalonians 1:10.] From these understand the
restoration of the contrary goods, namely, a living and most delightful sense
of God's love toward us kindled in our souls, and that heavenly inheritance.

3. The Apostle calls it “the redemption of possession.” It is likewise called
adoption, because through it we are adopted into the number of God's other
children. [Marginal reference: Ephesians 1:14.] It is also called the
redemption of the body, because we shall first enjoy it perfectly in the
resurrection of bodies. [Marginal reference: Romans 8:23.] It is likewise
called liberty, because when we begin to enjoy it our liberty will at last be
perfect and absolute, not only in right

[Frame 00100; printed p. 96]

but also in enjoyment. For as long as we live in this world, because we
neither fully receive nor enter upon that inheritance, what we have obtained
in Christ does not yet appear.

4. Nevertheless, even in this life we obtain certain firstfruits of that
eternal felicity when the Spirit of God's children, whom Christ merited for us
by his death, is conferred upon us and creates faith and repentance in us.

5. Everywhere the Apostle calls these gifts of the Holy Spirit the firstfruits
of eternal life, or of that future inheritance, as in Romans 8:23 and Hebrews
6:5. He could not have expressed it more elegantly than when he called the
Holy Spirit, who has been given to us, the seal and down payment of our
inheritance. [Marginal reference: Ephesians 1:14.] For just as a down payment
is not only the testimony of an obligation to pay the whole sum at its proper
time but also a small part of that sum which is rendered at its proper time,
so these firstfruits of the Holy Spirit, which the elect receive through the
Gospel, are both the divine testimony by which God obligates himself to
communicate to his believers at the proper time the fullness of his Spirit, or
the full inheritance of heavenly felicity, and certain firstfruits of that
same heavenly inheritance.

6. Therefore, when the question is to whom eternal life is given, a certain
learned theologian thought it more correct to answer that eternal life is given
to the elect than to say it is given to the converted. For conversion and
faith are the beginning of eternal life; to say eternal life is given to the
converted would be the same as saying life is given to the living.

7. Experience declares the taste of these firstfruits to be most delightful,
when believers perceive in their souls that they have a favorable and kindly
God, so that no pleasure can be compared with this joy of conscience. How
precious and pleasing do we think the full enjoyment of that inheritance will
be, when God will be all in all?

8. Because, as we said above, this second fruit has its foundation

[Frame 00101; printed p. 97]

in the first, namely in Christ's merit or righteousness obtained for us by
Christ's death, hence comes the phrase in Galatians 5:24, “they crucified the
flesh,” and so forth. The verb “crucify” indicates the efficient cause of
regeneration, namely the Holy Spirit obtained for us through the death of the
cross by Christ the priest. Hence too, from communion with Christ's death and
burial, sin is said to die and be buried in us. First, this is because through
his death sin lost its sting so that it can no longer destroy us. Second, as
was said, it is because by his death Christ merited for us the Holy Spirit, who
mortifies and buries sin in us, so that it loses its reign in us and gradually
dies. Efficacy therefore has its foundation in Christ's merit. We receive the
Holy Spirit because Christ merited him for us by his death.

9. No one, therefore, should be troubled that we here ascribe human
regeneration to Christ's death, while Peter ascribes it to his resurrection.
[Marginal reference: 1 Peter 1:3.] For this gift, like the others, is attributed
to Christ's death as regards its merit: by dying he merited regeneration for
us. But it is attributed to the resurrection as regards application: after
the resurrection he sent his Spirit into our hearts, through whom, as the
things he merited for us by his death are applied to us, we begin to enjoy
them in reality.

10. Although sanctification is a fruit of Christ's righteousness, just as
eternal life is, Scripture is nevertheless also accustomed to make this
sanctification a cause of eternal life, as if this eternal life were its
reward—but by grace. This should not be thought to be literally so. Rather,
Scripture is accustomed to call what precedes in the order of dispensation the
cause of the thing that follows. Elsewhere, when it wishes to speak literally
about the causes of eternal life, it says that God's grace, love, charity, and
Christ's obedience, death, and blood are the cause of eternal life, and so
forth. [Marginal reference: Romans 6:23.]

11. From all these things it is now sufficiently established that, just as we
proved above from the Person that Christ alone is Redeemer and

[Frame 00102; printed p. 98]

Mediator of the human race, so also from the Office Christ is that sole
Redeemer and victim for the sins of the human race, and there is no other
besides him. Neither gold, silver, Masses, nor anything of that kind can free
people from the coming wrath. [Marginal reference: 1 Peter 1:18.] Therefore,
when the devil in the papacy persuaded people that their souls could be
snatched from the torments of hell, or—as they call it—Purgatory if they
arranged for Masses to be sung for themselves or left bequests to this or that
monastery, and when he invented the claim that people's souls wander and can
be given rest in no other way than by bequeathing goods and revenues to this
or that monastery, he was, I say, effectually deceiving people and driving
them mad.

12. The sacrilegious papist opinion concerning the Mass, satisfactions, the
merits of works, works of supererogation, holy water, the work worked,
indulgences, and so forth conflicts with this sound doctrine concerning our
redemption through Christ's merit.

## Chapter XXII

### Christian Liberty

### First aphorism

1. Just as in Christ we obtained this, that we are no longer subject to the
slavery of the devil, sin, and death but freed and justified from them all, so
we were also freed from the obligation of the Law by which we were bound to
all these things. For while the Law showed what we owed God and exposed our
inability to pay, it held us bound to God for punishment. But that obligation
has now been removed. From Christ's satisfaction therefore follows the
abrogation both of the Moral Law—as far as the obligation just discussed is
concerned—and of the Ceremonial Law. This is customarily called Christian
Liberty. [Marginal references: Galatians 4:5; Romans 6:14; Ephesians 2:15;
Colossians 2:14.]

2. As will appear below, all the rights and privileges of a Christian person
are truly signified by the name Christian liberty. These are the two fruits
discussed above:

[Frame 00103; printed p. 99]

that, freed from sin, a person is constituted righteous in Christ; and that,
freed from death, he is constituted an heir of eternal life and therefore a
true lord and possessor also of these earthly things which are appendages of
that life. Thus it may seem that we are going to say nothing new here which was
not already recalled above among the fruits. Nevertheless, because it seemed
that we ought by no means to omit a warning about the abrogation of the Law,
we shall speak briefly about the whole, with this proviso: the warning about
the Law's abrogation insofar as it was an obligation should be judged proper
to this place.

3. Christian liberty in general is described by Bullinger as follows:
“Liberty is nothing other than the condition of a free person—I mean, the
fruit which a free person receives from liberation and now enjoys—namely,
that, freed from the dominion of the devil, from sin, from the Law's curse, and
from death, and made God's child, he is an heir of eternal life. Likewise, that
he has received the Spirit of liberty through whom he makes himself wholly a
servant of God, whom he serves throughout his whole life. Finally, that,
liberated from the Mosaic Law and all human laws, he now depends upon the
Gospel alone, having the free use of external things such as food, drink,
clothing, and other indifferent things.”

4. Christ is the Liberator, as was said above. “If the Son sets you at liberty,
you will truly be free.” Those liberated are servants whom he set at liberty
into the freedom of God's children.

5. Christian Liberty ought to have as many parts as there are fruits of
Christ's humiliation. For the whole of what Christ merited for us by his death
is nothing other than Christian liberty, and liberation from the Law includes
both fruits. To say that a person is liberated from the obligation of sin and
death is to say that the same person is constituted righteous and an heir of
eternal life. But because liberation from the Law as an obligation is treated
here, we shall follow the distinction of others who say that Christian liberty
concerns things commanded by law or things indifferent.

6. Things commanded by law are those commanded by the Moral and Ceremonial
Law. We shall first speak about the abrogation of the Moral Law.

7. So that

[Frame 00104; printed p. 100]

the abrogation of the Moral Law may be rightly understood, this distinction
must be observed, lest, deceiving ourselves, we think that what is perpetual in
the Law has been removed. The moral Law has a twofold use. First, it has its
own use if you consider those who are not yet in Christ. Second, it has a
particular use toward those who have already been grafted into Christ. Its use
toward those not yet led to Christ is twofold. First, it imposes upon them the
necessity of obeying the Law, and indeed by their own powers according to the
covenant of creation. Second, it curses them if they do not obey. But toward
those who are already in Christ, about whom we are speaking here, it has this
use: it serves them as the rule for ordering life and discharges the office of
a learned teacher and admonisher by showing them the way they ought to pursue
in the sanctification of life.

8. Just as those who are in Christ cease to be alien from Christ or to live
outside Christ, so that use of the Law which prevailed during the time of
their alienation from Christ is abolished and ceases after they have been led
to Christ. Therefore the Law is abrogated as regards necessity, or that harsh
exaction, and the curse—or, what is the same, insofar as it is an obligation.
But it is by no means antiquated insofar as it is the rule for living rightly.

9. It is rightly abrogated as regards that harsh exaction and curse, since the
strict righteousness which it demanded was fulfilled through Christ for all
the elect. It would therefore have to be considered altogether unjust if it
demanded that the same debt be paid to it twice. Hence Christ is called the
end of the Law, and the fulfillment of the Law is found in Christ, because
whoever believes in Christ has what the Law requires, namely perfect obedience
to the Law.

10. The consciences of believers are therefore liberated from the Law's
justification, that is, from the necessity of perfectly performing the Law in
order to obtain righteousness, and consequently from anxiety or fear of the
curse. They have been liberated from the Law's rigor, that is, that severe
exaction by which the Law, demanding perfect obedience,

[Frame 00105; printed p. 101]

condemns every imperfection. The following things result from this:

First, the confidence by which believers are certainly assured that their
acts of obedience, however imperfect, please God.

Second, eagerness in rendering acts of obedience to God, a fruit which arises
immediately from the first. Both of these are altogether necessary for the
right worship of God.

11. When we say that Christian liberty is the abrogation of the Law, and
likewise the destruction of the devil, death, and so forth, it must not be
thought that these are completely removed, since the Law remains forever, as
do the devil and hell. But they have been abrogated in such a way that,
although they remain, they do not harm those liberated by Christ. Just as
Peter, when liberated from prison in Acts 12, goes where he wishes while the
prison, guards, and chains remain, so too the devil, hell, and the other things
remain and have not been abolished, as unbelievers will one day experience.

12. Still less must it be thought that “liberty” here signifies an equal
inclination toward good and evil, for on this reasoning not even God himself
would be free. Rather, it is the sort of liberty by which, emancipated from
that harsh obligation of the Law, we are constituted free for God, so that,
looking to the Law as the wisest guide for ordering life and conforming our
life's actions to it, we may strive to please God. This use of the Law is
perpetual as long as we live in this life. And it is in this respect that we
said the Law has not been antiquated.

13. Those, therefore, who think there is liberty to act wickedly are not yet
free but slaves, for whoever sins is a slave of sin. “For you were called to
liberty, brothers; only do not seize liberty as an opportunity given to the
flesh, but serve one another from charity.” [Marginal reference: Galatians
5:13.] Those who throw themselves into every crime with a vain confidence in
Christian liberty therefore err most gravely. For not only remission of sins
but also their mortification flows from Christian liberty. Thus Baptism no

[Frame 00106; printed p. 102]

less admonishes and assures us about the mortification of sin in us than about
the remission of all our sins. It is foolish, indeed perverse, to tear apart
the fruits of Christ's obedience. For just as he removed evils, so he conferred
goods and substituted them in the evils' place.

14. Indeed, it can truly be said that there is no person beneath heaven freer
than a Christian person, and no one beneath heaven more a servant than a
Christian person. He is most free, because he has been claimed by God for the
highest liberty. But he is a servant because he must serve his enemies and all
who need his work, and indeed in all the lowliest offices, and must become all
things to all people after the example of Christ and the apostles. [Marginal
references: Romans 6:18–19; 1 Peter 2:18.] What else is this holy servitude, by
which believers' actions are conformed to the Law's rule or God's will, than
the highest liberty?

15. Since, therefore, this liberty is such that, after the Law's harshest
exaction has been removed, the actions of our life must be conformed to God's
Law so that we may thereby be shown to be truly free, it is evident that civil
servitude, which in no way conflicts with God's Law and holiness of life, is
not removed by the force of Christian liberty. For although you may be a
servant or slave, you can nevertheless be God's freedman; and although you may
be held captive by enemies, you can nevertheless be free for God, indeed God's
beloved child in Christ. Paul therefore carefully commands servants to obey
their masters, lest they think Christian liberty is immunity from civil
servitude. The latter does not conflict with it, since you can also be the
brother—provided he is a Christian—of the same master whose servant you are.
[Marginal references: Ephesians 6:5; Colossians 3:22; Philemon 16.] Thus much
concerning the moral Law insofar as it is and is not abrogated.

16. Since the Ceremonial Law was only a type of Christ's satisfaction, it
follows that when the body is exhibited the shadow ceases, and that
foreshadowings of a future thing have no place when the thing is present,
because they would thereby indicate it had not yet been exhibited. Hence they
cannot be employed without treachery and sacrilege. Consequently, all
ceremonial laws have been completely abrogated as regards use and practice so
that they may never afterward be employed, for they were all

[Frame 00107; printed p. 103]

nailed to the cross with Christ. As regards their effect, we see that they
have not been abrogated, since they were fulfilled in Christ; nor as regards
knowledge, for considering them still has its use when they are compared with
the body that has been exhibited.

16. So that they would never be employed after Christ had been exhibited and
thereby obscure the grace offered in Christ as if the Messiah had not yet been
exhibited, God willed the Temple to be destroyed from its deepest foundations
and the city, priesthood, and sacrifices to be utterly abolished. This
happened at the time of Jerusalem's overthrow.

17. They did not cease immediately after Christ's death but were employed for
some years after the Messiah had been exhibited, even by the apostles
themselves. This was done for the weak, lest a sudden change and abolition of
ceremonies which had been employed for so long and seemed likely to last
forever should offend their minds. As the Fathers say, the Synagogue had to be
buried with honor.

18. From the foregoing it is now established that Christ redeemed us from the
Law in two ways. First, from the Law's curse, when he became a curse for us.
Second, from the ceremonies of the Law which had been given to believers in
the Old Testament, liberating believers insofar as he himself performed and
fulfilled what the ceremonies foreshadowed. John 1:17 pertains to this: “The
Law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ.” By
the word “grace” he teaches that we were redeemed by Christ from the Law's
curse; by the name “Truth,” that we were redeemed from the ceremonies as
symbols and figures of the things Christ performed in reality.

19. Therefore, if we were liberated from the yoke of the Law which God himself
laid upon us and to which he wished us to be subject, what shall we say? Have
we been liberated from human traditions by which superstitious people wished
to burden our consciences as though they were God's commands? Certainly.
Indeed, we are said only improperly to be liberated from them, because we were
never subject to them by God's Law. On the contrary, those who enacted such
traditions are cursed by God, as are those who

[Frame 00108; printed p. 104]

obey them. We ought not to be servants of human beings; we were redeemed with
a price.

20. Indifferent things, also called intermediate things, are those which are
neither forbidden nor commanded by God's Law. From their own nature they are
neither good nor evil, and they make people neither dear nor hateful to God.
Hence God's kingdom is said not to consist in them. [Marginal reference:
Romans 14:17.]

21. Of this kind are servitude, liberty, country, poverty, wealth, marriage,
celibacy, food, clothing, and so forth. Just as these things do not make people
dear to God, neither do they make them hateful. There is no distinction
between Greek and Jew before God; neither marriage reconciles God to you nor
celibacy, nor does food commend you to God, and similarly with things of that
kind. Because these are human beings' external accidents, Paul said God is not
a respecter of persons, that is, he does not care about these external worldly
forms.

22. The use of these intermediate and indifferent things is therefore granted
so that we may freely use food, drink, clothing, and other such things without
any conscientious sense that we are acting less rightly, for God's glory and
our neighbor's salvation. Previously we could not use them in this way even
though they were intermediate things, because they were the goods of God's
children, among whose number we were not yet. God's glory and our neighbor's
salvation and edification must therefore be their end.

23. From considering those ends it follows that the use of indifferent things
is sometimes rightly restricted by the Magistrate, whom we are bound to obey
for conscience's sake. Yet our conscience is not thereby bound; the external
work is bound so that the conscience remains free. But because it is God who
commanded us to obey the Magistrate, and we must seek God's glory, it is
rightly said that the Magistrate must be obeyed for conscience's sake, since
God has bound the conscience by such a Law.

24. Judge that what we have said about the Magistrate's authority is also
permitted to the Church. This occurs when it institutes certain ceremonies for
the sake of good order and ecclesiastical decorum. Because these stand out
especially here, they are customarily called “indifferent things” by synecdoche.

25. The end

[Frame 00109; printed p. 105]

is overthrown by each if the things instituted are either plainly impious or
superstitious.

26. They are plainly impious if they directly conflict with God's Law, as if
images to God or the saints, and so forth, are ordered to be erected in
churches. In reality these things ought not to be called indifferent; they are
sins, although someone may foolishly call them indifferent.

27. They are superstitious when they are joined with an opinion of merit,
worship, perfection, or necessity. But if this opinion is absent from these
things—there is no doubt that plainly impious things must be entirely
omitted—they should no longer be considered superstitious and therefore can
either be observed or omitted by the force of Christian liberty.

28. They can indeed be observed for the sake of harmony and avoiding offense.
They can also be omitted outside a case of scandal, without the appearance of
denied truth, and without omitting a necessary confession.

29. Hence according to the circumstances they are sometimes lawful and
sometimes unlawful.

30. But if some conflict and opposition appears to be present in the
circumstances themselves, then matters of less importance should yield to
those of greater importance, and the fewer to the many. Two conclusions follow:

1. Whoever makes necessary things from these intermediate and indifferent
things, wishing them to be observed without regard to these ends, is an
idolater.

2. The one who establishes these things as intermediate in such a way that he
wishes to use them at any time, in any manner, without exception, sins no less.

31. One can learn from the rule of conscience or faith when one ought to use
or not use them. If he has used them from knowledge and a good conscience,
these things will be clean for him; if from ignorance and unbelief, unclean.
For conscience has such power that, if it is good, it makes good a work which
by its own nature is indifferent, and conversely makes it evil if the
conscience is evil.

32. Since Christians ought to keep their Lord's commands,

[Frame 00110; printed p. 106]

that conscience is properly called right and good which is ruled by God's
word. It follows that every believer must examine and weigh both what he does
and what he omits according to that revealed word, and must determine whether
it is just or unjust.

33. Consequently, it cannot happen that any work evil by its own nature should
become good through our conscience. Whatever conscience you have when you
commit perjury and fornication, you have sinned most gravely.

34. But because indifferent things are neither forbidden nor commanded by
God's word, they must be referred to that general equity, namely, insofar as
they serve God's glory and our neighbor's salvation. If something is done
contrary to this—as when someone sins in excess of clothing, commits idolatry
in these intermediate things, or oversteps God's boundaries by our good
intention—then one must know that these things are done neither lawfully nor
from a good conscience.

35. Just as philosophers establish that one always goes astray whenever one
does not follow right reason in acting, so in Theology sin is defined as a
departure from the faith of a sound conscience. Whatever is done with a
doubting conscience is sin, because it is done not from faith but from
ignorance. [Marginal reference: Romans 14:23.]

36. Our liberty in these intermediate things is therefore this: that we use
God's gifts without any scruple of conscience or disturbance of mind for the
purpose for which they were given to us by God, namely, for his glory, so that
we may thereby acknowledge his generosity toward us and our souls may have
peace with him; and so that our neighbor's salvation may be advanced by their
use.

## Chapter XXIII

### The abuse of Christian liberty, which is called Scandal

### First aphorism

1. Scandal arises from neglect of these ends. Although scandal is customarily
considered chiefly in indifferent things, it nevertheless has a place in all
things, both

[Frame 00111; printed p. 107]

those commanded by law and those indifferent. In that respect it will also be
considered here by us. For scandal is the abuse of all Christian liberty.

2. Scandal is a word or deed by which the course of the Gospel is hindered.
The enlargement and propagation of the Gospel ought to be the goal of our
whole life and the Cynosure toward which we look.

3. A word, such as shameful conversations: “Bad conversations corrupt good
morals”; likewise wandering, empty, and curious disputes about sacred things.

4. A deed, as if someone does things which are not expedient to do, such as
running to excess in drunkenness, lusts, and luxury.

5. Scandal is called *mokesh* in Hebrew, that is, a snare, a metaphor taken
from hunters or bird-catchers who entrap wild animals or birds in this way.

6. The Greek word “scandal” comes from *skazō*, which means “I limp.” Hence a
scandal is something against which a person strikes and slips or totters so
that he limps. Thus “scandal” or “stumbling block” literally means whatever is
thrown in the way of travelers so that, striking against it, they fall or at
least encounter an occasion for falling. But because the conduct of human
life is called a way, they metaphorically call “scandal” or “stumbling block”
whatever is accustomed to obstruct those walking in God's precepts or the way
of salvation, or to furnish an occasion for sinning. For sins are like stones
against which we strike or fall. Hence 1 Corinthians 10: “Let him who thinks
he stands see that he does not fall.”

7. Scandal is either given or taken.

8. A scandal given is one whose blame proceeds from the author of the thing
itself.

9. In things commanded by law, it occurs when the obligation of the Law is
urged by false Prophets, when the ceremonies and rites of the Law are
introduced again, or when someone lives wickedly, harms others by his evil
example, and gives God's enemies occasion to slander the Gospel. [Marginal
reference: Romans 2:24.]

10. Just as the fruits of good works are excellent, so the fruits

[Frame 00112; printed p. 108]

of evil works are the worst and altogether contrary to them.

11. Christ says of those who commit scandals of this kind that it would be
better for a millstone to be hung around their neck and for them to be cast
into the depth of the sea than for them to commit such things. [Marginal
reference: Matthew 18:6.]

12. It is committed in intermediate things when, for example, we use them
inopportunely to our neighbors' offense. Hence it follows that, if a weak
person is not present, one does not sin even if the Magistrate has forbidden
the use of indifferent things.

13. A person is recognized as weak if he has not been fully instructed about
the liberty, belonging both to him and to us and obtained through Christ, to
use these intermediate things. While we abstain, it will be our duty meanwhile
to instruct those for whose sake we do so concerning our liberty obtained
through Christ.

14. Something must be yielded to the weak for as long as they permit
themselves to be taught.

15. We must also see to it that, while we yield something to the weak, we do
not harm by our example another multitude, namely the godly, as Peter did and
was therefore rebuked by Paul. Otherwise some law and necessity may be imposed
upon them because of our example and their liberty thereby prejudiced.
[Marginal reference: Galatians 2:11.] This usually occurs when the strong, who
are harmed by us, are joined to the weak, or when stubborn people seize our
example in order to impose a law upon the godly and strong. Paul carefully
considered this matter. Although he once circumcised Timothy, he nevertheless
refused to circumcise Titus. [Marginal reference: Galatians 2:3.]

16. Those who, by abusing their liberty, give scandals to the weak and are a
stumbling block to them are said in 1 Corinthians 8 to sin against Christ.
Chrysostom explains this as follows. First, because Christ testifies that
whatever has been done to his own has been done to him: “Whoever receives you
receives me”; “what you did to one of the least, you did to me.” Second,
because the weak belong to Christ's body, since they are his members. Thus when
they are struck or harmed, Christ is harmed. Third, because by their evil
example these people overturn that edifice which Christ constructed by his
blood. “For we are God's building.” [Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians 3:9.]

17. Nor

[Frame 00113; printed p. 109]

may people say that they are permitted to do what they wish with their own
things and to squander and destroy them at their discretion. By no means.
Scandals must be carefully avoided, and we must take care that they do not
arise from us. Indeed, the things dearest to us must first be cut off and cast
away rather than made to serve scandal, Mark 9:43, which Christ teaches there
with a comparison taken from surgeons. Just as in bodily diseases not even the
most excellent members are spared when destruction threatens the whole body
from them, so, in order to remove scandals, no desire, honor, or the like must
be indulged from which the contagion of some crime can arise and bring the
destruction of souls either to ourselves or to others.

18. A scandal taken occurs when someone who is fully and abundantly instructed
about our liberty nevertheless stubbornly resists our liberty; or when he in
no way permits himself to be instructed; or even when he is offended by
something prescribed by the Moral Law and just and equitable in itself. Thus
the unlearned who have zeal without knowledge are offended by the preaching of
the Gospel because it appears to be a new doctrine, because it has no
followers, or because it produces divisions of minds. Likewise, the Pharisees
are offended by the same preaching because so much thereby departs from their
profit, honor, and dignity. Since this scandal is most often observed in such
Pharisees, it is customarily called Pharisaical. Against all such people our
liberty must be asserted and employed in the deed itself.

Thus far concerning Christ's humiliation and its fruits, where Merit was
treated. Exaltation follows, in which the Royal office and Efficacy shine most
brightly.

## Chapter XXIV

### Christ's Exaltation, in which the second part of the priestly function,
namely Intercession, and the Royal office are treated

[Frame 00114; printed p. 110]

### First aphorism

1. Christ's exaltation is the state belonging to his whole person according to
each nature, in which, after rendering satisfaction for the sins of the human
race, by the Father's will he fully and perfectly shows himself King and Lord.

2. This condition began after he paid the ransom for the sins of the human
race on this earth. For according to the divine counsel he first had to offer
the sacrifice on this earth—that is, suffer and die—before entering into
glory. He could not have reigned unless he had first satisfied for our sins.

3. Christ's exaltation or glorification according to the divine nature is
nothing other than the full manifestation and disclosure of the infinite
deity, which does not inhere in the human nature subjectively but dwells in it
bodily—that is, hypostatically and personally—and yet until then was, as it
were, hidden in it.

4. Although the divine nature is subject to neither passions nor changes, as
if it could be deprived of the glory which it had from eternity and afterward
adorned with it again, this is nevertheless truly said of the Logos because he
humbled and emptied himself in the assumed nature. Just as the sun, when a
cloud is placed before it, is said to be obscured—not in itself, but
relatively, namely because of the cloud—and to become bright again when the
cloud is removed and dispersed, so the Logos is said to be emptied in the
human nature because of it and exalted again in the same nature.

5. According to the human nature, however, exaltation is the effecting and, as
it were, creation by the personally indwelling deity of gifts above all
creatures in the human nature as their subject.

6. In kind it does not differ from the glorification of the blessed angels and
human beings, but in quantity, that is, in the greatness and number of the
gifts.

7. This glorification according to the human nature has two parts: the laying
aside of infirmities and the conferral of new gifts.

8. The laying aside of infirmities is that Christ is no longer subject to
sufferings and does not need food, drink, sleep, or things of that kind.

9. The conferral

[Frame 00115; printed p. 111]

of new gifts is that by which Christ's human nature, with its essential
properties preserved, is adorned in itself with new qualities, through the
power of the indwelling deity, to the highest degree of perfection, far above
all creatures.

10. Christ's humanity was indeed adorned with the most illustrious gifts at
the very moment of conception, and with still greater and more numerous gifts
at Baptism; but exaltation brought consummation.

11. Because these gifts were created, effected, and conferred in the human
nature above and beyond nature—that is, the common order of nature—some call
them hyperphysical and paraphysical. The Ubiquitarians invented the claim that
they are antiphysical, although the former adorn nature while the latter
overthrow and remove it. And because they exist and inhere habitually in the
human nature as their subject, the Scholastics call them habitual grace.

12. These gifts belong either to the soul or to the body. Those of the soul
include wisdom, joy, and so forth. Those of the body include incorruptibility,
subtlety, agility, and radiance. These will also be common with Christ to
blessed human beings after the resurrection, although not in the same measure
and degree.

13. We demonstrated above that a transfusion of properties does not follow
from this glorification, because these are not properties of deity but created
gifts in the human nature.

14. Just as no transfusion of the deity's properties into the humanity
followed through the exaltation, so the human nature did not lose its own
properties—which would plainly happen if divine properties were transfused
into the flesh through exaltation. Rather, even now Christ's body, according
to the ordinary economy, is and remains visible, tangible, organic, finite,
and circumscribed, just as Christ himself taught after his resurrection. For
these are marks of a creature, which cannot be erased unless you do not wish
it to be a creature. Christ indeed gave glory to his body, but did not remove
its nature.

[Frame 00116; printed p. 112]

## Chapter XXV

### Christ's Resurrection

### First aphorism

1. Christ's exaltation, like his humiliation, has its own advances,
progressions, and stages. There are three; the third concerns the Priestly and
Royal function, but chiefly the Royal, as we shall say below.

2. The first stage of exaltation is in the Resurrection. The Resurrection is
that by which, after the third day during which he lay in the tomb, Christ rose
again as victor and lord of death by the proper power of his deity.

3. Christ proved his resurrection to his own with every kind of argument,
namely by witnesses—angels, women, and men—to whom he showed himself alive in
that very same body, marked even with the scars of the wounds, adding the
testimonies of the Prophets. [Marginal references: Matthew 28:5 and following;
Luke 24; John 20; 1 Corinthians 15.] To all these he added forty days of
association, lest either a shorter span be insufficient or a longer intervening
delay persuade them that he had risen to the same sort of life he lived before.

4. The time during which he lay in the tomb did not have to correspond in
every respect to the type of Jonah. Rather, one must understand that, as if
Christ were hastening to victory, Scripture by synecdoche gave the name of the
whole matter to its beginning and end. For Christ was placed in the tomb at
the end of the day preceding the Sabbath; he lay in the tomb throughout the
whole Sabbath according to the Jewish custom, which reckons a natural day from
evening to evening; and he rose at the beginning of the following day, which
was the first of the week.

5. Christ rose because he had fully expiated sin through his death. He
sustained temporal penalties and, by tasting them, swallowed up eternal ones;
for this reason he rose as victor and lord of death by the proper power of his
deity.

6. Because

[Frame 00117; printed p. 113]

the external works of the deity are common, Christ is said both to have raised
himself and to have been raised by the Father. [Marginal references: Ephesians
1:20; Acts 13:30; Acts 2:24; Romans 6:4; Galatians 1:1.]

7. Christ's Resurrection is public testimony that the sins of all the elect
have been abolished and consequently eternal death destroyed. For if even one
sin had remained unexpiated, Christ would have remained in death, because when
the cause is posited its effect must be posited. But if Christ had remained in
death and the tomb, human beings would have had to despair altogether of
salvation. For Christ had said beforehand, “Where I am, I will that my servants
also be there.” But Christ remained in death; therefore we too would have to
remain. [Marginal reference: John 17:24.]

8. Further, the raising of the Son from the dead is the actual—if I may say
so—absolution from sins of all those who through faith in Christ are raised
from eternal death. For just as the Father, by handing Christ over to death,
condemned our sins in Christ in reality, so by raising him from death he in
that very act absolved Christ from our sins laid upon him, and us in Christ.

9. From consideration of these things come Paul's words: “If Christ has not
been raised, you are still in your sins.” [Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians
15.] Therefore, by the contrary sense, since Christ has been raised we are no
longer in our sins.

10. Hence it also follows that, since by Christ's glorious resurrection it was
fully and abundantly testified that satisfaction was made by his preceding
death for the sins of all the elect, so that no doubt can any longer arise
among people that God's wrath and justice have been satisfied, we are, I say,
sometimes said to be justified through Christ's glorious resurrection. This is
not because any addition was made to the price for our sins, since satisfaction
was fully made in death, but because the Resurrection testifies with the
greatest certainty that Christ abundantly satisfied God's wrath and completely
conquered death.

11. For this very reason Peter says that God raised Christ from the dead

[Frame 00118; printed p. 114]

and gave him glory so that our faith and hope might be in God. [Marginal
reference: 1 Peter 1:21.] For because a person cannot cling confidently to
God—since he is a righteous Judge—unless the memory of sin is completely
erased, and because this perfect erasure of sins is conspicuous only in the
risen Christ, Peter rightly affirms that, since Christ has risen from the dead,
our faith and hope rest safely upon God.

12. Finally, for this very reason the Resurrection is always joined to
Christ's death in the Scriptures, namely so that our faith may not waver.

13. Indeed, this too must be observed. When God raised his Son, our surety,
from the dead, thereby declaring that sin had now been entirely expiated, he
also declared that he wishes to regard us perpetually in that purity in which
God's Son rose as the firstborn from the dead. Since Christ cannot fall from
this purity, neither shall we ever fall from that salvation which he obtained
for us by his death.

14. Thus much concerning the Resurrection insofar as it serves Merit. As for
Efficacy, the matter stands as follows. Just as in raising the incarnate Logos
from the dead the Father exercised the strength of his power so that, having
been justified, he might rise to a life free from all mortality, so we are
said to be regenerated into a living hope through Christ's resurrection from
the dead, because in giving us faith and repentance he exercises in making us
alive the same power which he demonstrated in raising his Son. [Marginal
references: Ephesians 2:5; Romans 6:5–6; 1 Peter 1:3.] And just as there will
never be an end to that life into which God's Son was raised, so there will
never be a limit or end to our spiritual life.

15. Further, by this very power of the Resurrection by which God's Son rose to
a new life, he both raises us from the eternal death of souls and will also
raise our bodies from the tombs on the last day, so that we may be conformed
to him not only in soul but also in body. [Marginal references: John 5; 1
Corinthians 15:12–13; 1 Thessalonians 4:14.] For since Christ is our head and
has risen, we who are his members will necessarily follow. Just as, when we see
a person's head projecting from the water, we do not doubt that the remaining
members will emerge

[Frame 00119; printed p. 115]

and immediately follow, so we must think concerning Christ and ourselves.

## Chapter XXVI

### Christ's Ascension into heaven

### First aphorism

1. The second stage of Christ's exaltation is in his Ascension into heaven.
We assert that this was not a disappearance or merely a change of state—for he
changed his state in the Resurrection—but a change of place, and consequently
a true ascension into heaven, so that in his body he is no longer on earth,
either visibly or invisibly, but only in heaven, and indeed until the day of
judgment. [Marginal references: Acts 1; Matthew 26:11.]

2. This is testified both by the history of the Ascension and by all Scripture,
which says that Christ departed from the disciples and was lifted upward into
heaven, went away, left this world, was taken up from them into the sanctuary
not made with hands, into heaven itself, is there continually and without
intermission, and must be received by heaven. [Marginal references: Colossians
3:1; Luke 24:51.] In this manner Nazianzen also interpreted it in his second
oration *On the Son*: “and to be received by heaven until the times of
restoration”; and Oecumenius the Greek scholiast calls heaven “the receptacle
of the one sent,” that is, of Christ.

3. Scripture mentions only two advents of Christ: the advent in the flesh and
the advent for judgment. It makes no mention of an intermediate advent. Indeed,
Christ himself foretold that antichrists would say, “Behold, Christ is here;
behold, he is there.” [Marginal references: Isaiah 53; Zechariah 9; Galatians
4:4.] But when he comes from heaven, he will appear like lightning, and this
on the last day. Looking to that advent, Paul commands that as often as we eat
this bread and drink the cup we proclaim the Lord's death until he comes.
[Marginal reference: Matthew 16.] His bodily presence on earth is therefore
sought in vain, whether it is called visible or invisible. For one who is
invisibly present is certainly present, the contrary of which we have shown
concerning Christ's body. Add that this dream concerning the invisible presence
of the body cannot be proved from any passage of Scripture.

[Frame 00120; printed p. 116]

All the Fathers confirm the same thing. Thus Augustine in tractate 50 on John:
“When Christ said, ‘You will not always have me with you,’ he was speaking
about the presence of the body. For according to his majesty, according to
providence, and according to ineffable and invisible grace, what he said is
fulfilled: ‘Behold, I am with you until the consummation of the age.’ But
according to the flesh which the Word assumed, according to that which was
born of the Virgin, according to that which was seized by the Jews, nailed to
the wood, taken down from the cross, wrapped in linen cloths, laid in the tomb,
and manifested in the Resurrection, ‘you will not always have me with you.’
Why? Because according to his bodily presence he associated with his disciples
for forty days and, as they escorted him by seeing but not following, he
ascended. He is not here, for there he sits at the Father's right hand; and yet
he is here, because the presence of his majesty has not departed. Otherwise,
according to the presence of his majesty we always have Christ, but according
to the presence of the flesh it was rightly said, ‘You will not always have
me.’ For the Church had him according to the presence of the flesh for a few
days; now it holds him by faith and does not see him with the eyes.”

Cyril: “Although Christ is absent in body, appearing for us before the Father
and sitting at his right hand, he nevertheless dwells in the saints through
the Spirit and does not permit those to be orphans to whom, ascending into
heaven, he sent the Spirit. For the one who ascended into heaven could not
associate in the flesh with the apostles.”

Bernard, on the Song of Songs: “As if Christ, when he has now ascended into
heaven, either wishes or can be touched. He certainly can be touched, but by
affection, not the hand; by desire, not the eye; by faith, not the senses.”

Vigilius the Martyr, book 4 against Eutyches: “When the flesh was on earth, it
was certainly not in heaven; and now, because it is in heaven, it is certainly
not on earth. To such a degree is it not here that we expect him to come from
heaven according to it, while according to the Word we say that he is with us
on earth.”

4. Consequently, the madness of the Ubiquitarians can be tolerated still less.

[Frame 00121; printed p. 117]

To assert Christ's bodily presence in the Supper, they perverted this article
and devised new tropes so that they would not be compelled to concede a trope
in the words of the Supper. For them “heaven” here is merely a glorious or
heavenly state—for they confuse heaven and heavenly glory—not a place. But if
heaven were the same as God's glory and thus the words were synonymous, would
it not follow that these interpretations were true? “On the last day Christ
will descend from heaven to judge the living and the dead,” that is, he will
descend from the glory of God the Father. This is false and impious, for then
he is said rather to be coming with glory. Likewise, “Christ ascended above
all heavens,” that is, he ascended above all God's glory.

5. For them “to ascend” is the same as to disappear or become invisible, not
to change place. Thus they nevertheless invent the claim that Christ is present
on earth in his body, a dogma whose falsehood we refuted above. They say, “Just
as Christ, when he descended from heaven and assumed human flesh, did not
change place, so the Ascension should also be thought to signify not a change
of place but merely a change of state.” But they err, for these things are not
equal. The Logos descended without a body and is therefore said to have
descended by anthropopathic language; but he ascended with a body and
therefore necessarily changed place. How, then, is the one who ascended said
to have descended? Because, namely, Christ is one and the same in different
natures. Athanasius likewise answers, as found in Vigilius: “How does he say,
‘I go to the Father,’ with whom he always was and from whom he never departed?
For going and coming belong to one who is circumscribed by certain boundaries
of places, and who leaves the place in which he was in order to come to another
where he was not. Unless, certainly, he spoke concerning that man whom he
assumed.”

6. The causes of the Ascension, or of this motion, were: first, the indwelling
deity itself; second, the agility of the body itself, a gift which followed the
glorious Resurrection.

7. The fruits of the Ascension are these. First, it is the solemn testimony
that eternal righteousness has been brought in, when God manifested in the
flesh ascends. For no man subsisting by himself could have ascended by his own
power. Since, therefore, by his Ascension as a proper effect

[Frame 00122; printed p. 118]

of deity he demonstrates himself to be the eternal incarnate Logos, ascending
with the pledge of his deepest humiliation to the place from which he
descended, he testifies that Adam's crime against majesty, together with its
effects, has been erased in every respect. Not only has it been erased, but he
led sin, death, and hell in triumph, since by rising he triumphed over all his
and our enemies. Consequently, those believing in his name are truly liberated
from them and justified from every crime. [Marginal references: Psalm 68;
Romans 10; Ephesians 4:8.]

8. Second, the Ascension is not only testimony of our justification and
therefore that the heavenly inheritance has been brought in, but also the
taking possession of that inheritance in the name of all his brothers. Thus
we do not merely expect salvation in bare hope and expectation but already
possess it in reality in our head and our flesh. For there our brother, by his
continued possession of eternal life, preserves it for us in his own person,
certain and safe against the attacks of all enemies. [Marginal references:
Hebrews 7:20; John 14:2–3; Ephesians 2:6.]

9. Third, Christ sends us from there the firstfruits or down payment of that
life or inheritance which he took possession of in our name, namely the Holy
Spirit. The Spirit seals God's promises upon our hearts, or makes us certain
of future life; indeed, he makes us fit and suitable to possess it. [Marginal
references: Luke 24:49; John 14:16; Acts 2; 2 Corinthians 1:22.] For flesh and
blood—that is, corrupt, unregenerate nature—cannot possess God's kingdom.
[Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians 15:50.]

10. Because of this benefit which followed Christ's Ascension, Christ said
with great solemnity, “I tell you the truth, it is expedient for you that I go
away. For if I do not go away, that Comforter will not come to you.” [Marginal
reference: John 16:7.] You see, therefore, that although he ascended bodily
into heaven and according to the body is only there, we are not thereby
deserted by him. Indeed, because of the Ascension he is most present to us by
his Spirit. Most people, and especially in the papacy, think Christ's bodily
presence would be very expedient for us. But Christ steadfastly

[Frame 00123; printed p. 119]

asserts here that it is expedient for us that he go away. Concerning these last
two fruits there is an excellent passage in Tertullian's book *On the
Resurrection of the Flesh*: “This one, called the mediator between God and
human beings, from the deposit of each party committed to him, also preserves
in himself the deposit of flesh as a down payment of the whole sum. For just
as he left with us the down payment of the Spirit, so he received from us the
down payment of flesh and carried it into heaven as a pledge of the whole sum
which will one day be gathered there. Be secure, flesh and blood: you have
taken possession both of heaven and of God's kingdom in Christ. Or, if they
deny you in Christ, let those who deny heaven to you also deny Christ in
heaven.”

## Chapter XXVII

### Christ's Session at the Father's right hand

### First aphorism

1. The third and highest stage of Christ's exaltation is that after the
Ascension he sat at the Father's right hand in heaven.

2. The session at the Father's right hand is distinguished from the Ascension
and therefore neither can nor should be confused with it. Mark 16 distinguishes
them: “After the Lord had spoken, he was received up into heaven and sat at
God's right hand.” The Ascension into heaven, if you consider the change of
place, was indeed common to the incarnate Logos and the angels. But to which
of the angels did he ever say, “Sit at my right hand,” as if the angels had
ascended after making expiation for sins by themselves, and were consequently
victors over the devil and death? [Marginal references: Hebrews 1:3, 10.]

3. Because this is the highest stage of Christ's exaltation, it is
metaphorically called the session at the Father's right hand. The comparison
is taken from earthly Princes, who place at their right hand those whom they
hold in the highest honor and through whom they administer all things. Thus
the Father is said to have exalted the Son to his right hand and given him the
name above every name. [Marginal references: Psalm 45; 1 Kings 2:19.]

4. That the expression is metaphorical is evident from this: if you consider
the literal meaning of the words, you will find it can rightly be said neither
of the divine nor of the human

[Frame 00124; printed p. 120]

nature. Session in its proper meaning is in no way fitting to deity. And
although it can in its proper meaning be accommodated to the other, bodily
nature, insofar as it is opposed to the body's standing and motion, it must
nevertheless not be thought that that glorious body in heaven perpetually
moves, stands, or sits, although it is truly circumscribed and local. All these
things are crude and curious and must not be accommodated so specifically to
the state of this life.

5. What is added concerning God's right hand must still more be taken by
anthropopathic language, since incorporeal God has neither a right nor left
side but is altogether immeasurable and infinite.

6. As we said, therefore, this expression is taken from the custom of kings
and princes, who are accustomed to display to their subjects in the most
honorable place—namely, placed at their right side—those whom they wish to be
equal in honor with themselves and through whom they exercise their dominion.
By this formula of speech, I say, it is signified that Christ received all
power in heaven and on earth, as he himself, his own best interpreter,
explains. [Marginal reference: Matthew 28:18.]

7. As regards right, this session at the Father's right hand belonged to the
humanity from the very first moment of the hypostatic union, since it was to
be adorned with this glory at its proper time. Nevertheless, to accomplish
our reconciliation with God, Christ had to be humbled in this humanity even
to the death of the cross. This glorification of our head is therefore the
crown of that obedience which he rendered to the Father for us even to the
death of the cross, and follows not only his passion, death, burial, and
descent into hell, but also the Resurrection and Ascension themselves and is
later in time. It is altogether foolish and false to call the personal union
the session at the Father's right hand, to assert that the session occurred
then, or to use the personal union to explain it. For in this way

[Frame 00125; printed p. 121]

all the articles of the Creed of our faith are confused.

8. And although insofar as Christ is God and coeternal with his Father he must
not be thought not to have received this glory after the resurrection of his
flesh, but to have had it common with the Father from eternity, from whom he
received sonship itself from eternity, nevertheless, because Christ is here
considered as incarnate and therefore Priest and King of the human race, he is
truly said to have been humbled and exalted, because these are states and
properties of the person.

9. This session at the Father's right hand is therefore the exaltation of the
whole person, which we also indicated generally above concerning the whole
exaltation, not only of one nature, such as the human. Certain people foolishly
assert the latter when they write “On the Exaltation of the man Christ,”
understanding by “man” only the human nature in Christ. But just as the whole
person was humbled, so he was exalted in the Resurrection, Ascension into
heaven, and session at the Father's right hand.

10. This session of the Mediator at God's right hand is said to be in heaven
in respect of the human nature—for as regards the divine he sits at the
Father's right hand everywhere—and therefore contains the highest dignity or
eminence of place to which the Mediator was raised. Hence Paul says that he
was made higher than the heavens. That place is likewise called inaccessible
light and the heavenly sanctuary. [Marginal references: Colossians 3:1; 1
Peter 3:22; Ephesians 1:20; Hebrews 7:26; 1 Timothy 6:16.]

11. The Ubiquitarians call this session at God's right hand “the mode of
majesty,” by which they think Christ's flesh was made omnipresent, omnipotent,
and omniscient. This is to deprive Christ of a body. Thus they made this
fictitious majesty a veil for all their errors, just as if someone hid snakes
and vipers with a silken garment. To refute this error, the testimonies of
Scripture which we often adduced above are sufficient, along with other
arguments drawn from the truth of the human nature and the articles of the
Creed, and the consensus of the orthodox Fathers.

Theodoret, Dialogue 2: “The body of the Lord which rose is indeed foreign to
corruption

[Frame 00126; printed p. 122]

and destruction, impassible and immortal, glorified with divine glory, deemed
worthy of session at the Father's right hand, and adored by the heavenly
powers. Nevertheless, it is a body and has the same form, shape, and
circumscription which it had before.”

Augustine, Letter 57 to Dardanus: “Do not doubt that the man Christ is now
there whence he will come according to the Christian confession. He rose from
the dead, ascended to heaven, sits at the Father's right hand, and will come
from nowhere other than there in the same form and substance of flesh, to
which he gave immortality but did not take away its nature. According to this
form and substance of flesh, Christ must not be thought diffused everywhere.
For we must take care not to construct the man's divinity in such a way that
we remove the truth of his body.” In the same place: “For remove spaces of
places from bodies and they will be nowhere; and because nowhere, they will
therefore not exist. Hence believe that Christ is everywhere according to that
which he is as God, but in heaven according to that which he is as man, in the
manner of a true body.”

12. This exaltation to the Father's right hand concerns both the Priestly and
Royal function, and indeed the Royal stands out here especially. Consequently,
this session at the Father's right hand is customarily described in Scripture
from the Kingdom more often than from intercession, and theologians
synecdochically define this session at the Father's right hand as nothing
other than sharing in the supreme dominion. [Marginal references: Ephesians
1:21–22; Acts 2:35; 1 Peter 3:22.]

13. It concerns the Priestly function in this way. After the incarnate Logos
had discharged the parts of his priesthood on earth, he had to enter heaven
with that offered victim, into the Holy of Holies itself, and there appear
without any interruption for us, so that the power of the victim offered once
might always prevail. Then, by his intercession there for us, he had to obtain
that promised Holy Spirit—whose sending he had already merited by his
obedience—who would give us faith, by which we are joined to that offered
victim and it becomes ours, and repentance, and would confirm us in them until
the end of life.

14. From this

[Frame 00127; printed p. 123]

two things are deduced which demonstrate Christ's immeasurable love toward us.
The first is that he was unwilling ever to lay aside the administration of our
salvation or to leave us to ourselves, so that we might do for ourselves what
remained and acquire salvation for ourselves by acting and meriting, or at
least preserve the salvation already obtained. Rather, he himself wished to
preserve what he acquired. If he did not do this, we would fall again into our
former misery.

15. The second is that Christ, though exalted to the highest honor, is
nevertheless not ashamed of us, so as to cease calling us brothers, lay aside
our flesh, or cast away the work of administering our salvation. Indeed, he
wishes both to retain the wounds which he suffered in our flesh and to remain
Priest forever.

16. Indeed, from this part of the priestly function the Apostle confirms us in
God's love. If, he says, Christ loved us when that part of the office had to be
undertaken which was both more severe and more burdensome, and was then
present with us, how much more now, when he need no longer die but always lives
to intercede for us?

17. This work of the Priesthood, which he now administers exalted and raised
into heaven, is called Intercession, from which Christ is called our Mediator
and Advocate. Just as the sufficiency of the Merit belonged to the first part
of the Priesthood, so in this latter part the application and preservation of
the same Merit are considered.

18. The intercession of the Logos, therefore, is the presentation of the
incarnate Word himself before the Father in the heavenly sanctuary, so that
the efficacy of the sacrifice offered once on earth with entreaty might
prevail perpetually. At the same time it is the perpetual will to save, which
in this Mediator agrees with the will of the Father, who accepts the victim
offered once for eternal reconciliation and approves this zeal to save in the
Mediator.

19. Hence we have been provided for most securely against all sins

[Frame 00128; printed p. 124]

which we commit or will commit, since we have an Advocate with the Father,
Jesus Christ the righteous.

20. The Apostle understood this intercession, and consequently Christ's
presence in heaven after the victim was offered, to be so necessary that he
said Christ would not even be a Priest if he were still on earth with the
offered victim.

21. The sacrilegious papist opinion concerning the intercession of angels and
dead saints, as if they were our Advocates and Mediators, conflicts with this
sound doctrine.

There follows the treatment of Christ's Kingdom, which shines most brightly in
this exaltation and which he solemnly inaugurated after the Ascension into
heaven.

Glory to God.

## Theological theses on the Communion of Believers with Christ

### I

*[Marginal heading: That union with Christ is necessary for a person.]*

The union of believers with Christ is so necessary for salvation that either
the Father might seem to have sent the Son into the world in vain or the Son
to have died for us in vain, unless we embraced that gift of the Father and
were so united to Christ that we could be partakers of his goods. John 3:3, 5,
8; also 6:53 and 17:3; Romans 8:9.

### II

*[Marginal heading: That it cannot be effected by human beings but by God.]*

But how can God's enemies—people dead in sins, hating God, and unable to know
the things of God's Spirit, Colossians 1:21; Titus 3:3; 1 Corinthians 2:14—believe
either that the Father was willing to give his only-begotten Son to death for
his enemies, or that the Son was willing to die for people who exercise
hostility against him? This cannot happen unless God himself exercises his
infinite power here, changes human hearts, and fully persuades them that he
ardently loves the human race; and unless the same God through the Holy Spirit
creates in human beings that instrument by which they can embrace that gift of
the Father, that is, Christ with his merit and efficacy. Jeremiah 31:33;
Romans 8:9.

### III

*[Marginal heading: It is therefore effected by the Holy Spirit through
faith.]*

The instrument or bond created in us by the Holy Spirit is faith, through
whose benefit this communion with Christ is perfected. Hosea 2:22; John 6;
Romans 8:14–16; Ephesians 3:17; 1 John 3:24.

### IV

*[Marginal heading: What it is.]*

Communion with Christ is the closest spiritual union with Christ through
faith, by which we are partakers both of Christ and all his benefits unto
eternal life. John 6:40; 1 John 5:1; Romans 8:11.

[Frame 00131; printed p. 127]

### V

*[Marginal heading: Two things to be considered in it.]*

The consideration of this communion is completed in two parts: first, that we
know *to koinon*, what that common good of all believers is; second, that we
understand the right which every believer has in it.

### VI

*[Marginal heading: Two things likewise to be considered in the first part.]*

The common good is Christ with his merit and efficacy. He is that treasure,
bread of life, fountain of living water, and measuring cord of the inheritance
of every elect person. Ephesians 3:8; John 6:51; John 4:10, 14.

### VII

*[Marginal heading: Anticipation.]*

It is indeed true that our misery was also made common to us with Christ.
Hebrews 3:17 and 4:15; Psalm 22; Isaiah 53. But he assumed it so that, after
abolishing our evils, he might confer his goods upon us. Not only that, but
this too is true: all of us as an acquired people are possessed by God himself
and are the sheepfold, vineyard, and inheritance of the Father, Son, and Holy
Spirit. John 10; Mark 12:1; Isaiah 5; Jeremiah 2:21; Exodus 15:16; Deuteronomy
4:19; Psalm 78. But our purpose, since we wish to provide for the need of the
elect, properly concerns showing what it is in God that is communicated to
human beings and how they become partakers of it.

### VIII

*[Marginal heading: In the believers' common treasure, Christ himself must be
considered first.]*

We say that not only Christ's merit and efficacy—that is, his benefits—but
Christ himself is that common good. For both the tablets of the covenant offer
him to us, Romans 8:32; Psalm 2:12; Isaiah 9:5; 1 John 4:10, and the sacraments,
the seals of this communion, seal the same one to us, 1 Corinthians 10:16. And,
as Vigilius the Martyr rightly said, neither can this treasure be possessed by
anyone without the field, nor this field without the treasure.

### IX

*[Marginal heading: Christ must be considered as Emmanuel and God-man; the
reason why this must be done.]*

But because God's Son cannot be ours who have fallen into

[Frame 00132; printed p. 128]

sin unless he is also man, we rightly consider Christ here as the God-man.
This is what Cyril said in the *Apologetic*: “Unless Christ had been born
fleshly, you would not have been born spiritually.” The Fathers also look to
this when they say we are joined to Christ bodily and by a carnal coupling.
For they allude to the Apostle's words, “He did not assume angels, but the seed
of Abraham.” Thus the Fathers do not look to the proximate cause of communion,
which we indicate below, or to the instrument by which it is effected, but to
the foundation upon which it rests, or, as others prefer, the indispensable
cause. For in order to be Christ's members it is not enough that we are human
beings, or have a nature common with Christ; we must also be Christians.

### X

*[Marginal heading: Christ must be considered as victim.]*

Just as Christ's benefits cannot be ours unless Christ is Emmanuel, so Christ
cannot be the bread of life for us unless he is crucified.

### XI

*[Marginal heading: Believers so possess Christ that by the same right they
possess the Father and Holy Spirit.]*

When we say that Christ is that common treasure of believers, it follows that
the Father too is that common good of believers; and since we are Christ's
brothers, that we likewise have a common Father. Psalm 103; Malachi 1:3;
Matthew 6:9; John 20:17 and 14:21. Indeed, we also have the Holy Spirit,
because the Holy Spirit is Christ's Spirit. John 16; Romans 8.

### XII

*[Marginal heading: The second part of the common good, that is, Christ's
benefits.]*

Christ's Merit embraces two things: first, Christ's obedience, which is
imputed to a person for righteousness; second, eternal life. Romans 5:12, 19.

Efficacy is the giving of the Holy Spirit by which Christ brings it about that
we experience in ourselves the things which he acquired for us by his merit,
namely remission of sins through justification by faith and the restoration
of God's image in newness of life, which you may call the firstfruits of
eternal life. Ephesians 1:14. From this communion which we have with Christ and
his benefits, which we embrace under the names Merit and Efficacy, it follows
that he

[Frame 00133; printed p. 129]

presents us to God the Father as priests, prophets, and kings.

### XIII

*[Marginal heading: Why such benefits were required.]*

These were required because God, who is holy, can have no society or communion
with sinners. Deuteronomy 4:24 and 9:3; Hebrews 12:29. Therefore, for some
society and communion to stand between us, he must acknowledge us in the Son
not as sinners but as righteous. Furthermore, it conflicts with God's nature
to acknowledge as righteous those who bear the devil's image and do not cease
sinning. Therefore again, for communion to stand between us, he must through
the Holy Spirit restore his own image in us and bring it about that, advancing
more and more in the sanctification of life, we finally cease sinning entirely.

### XIV

*[Marginal heading: From consideration of Christ's benefits, two parts of
communion are established.]*

Hence from consideration of this common good—when, I say, Christ's benefits
are regarded—two parts of the believers' communion with Christ are
customarily established: Justification and Sanctification.

### XV

*[Marginal heading: Christ's benefits are never torn apart; those whom Christ
clothes for righteousness he also clothes for sanctification.]*

These two are so connected that when Scripture mentions one, it is accustomed
almost always to remember the other as well, lest anyone think they can be torn
apart, as if justification could exist without sanctification or sanctification
without justification. Indeed, from the sanctification of life as a striking
argument, Scripture is accustomed to infer communion with Christ and to
describe those who have communion with Christ. 1 John 1:7 and 2:4, 6; 1 John
3:14 and 4:6; Romans 8:1. It concludes our communion with Christ sometimes
from the duties of the first table and sometimes from those of the second.

### XVI

*[Marginal heading: Just as the heavenly inheritance belongs only to
believers, so the tablets of the covenant and the Sacraments belong only to
them.]*

Just as Merit and Efficacy are Christ's benefits made common to all believers
through his death, so the testimonies and seals of those benefits—or rather of
Christ with his benefits—are common to all the godly,

[Frame 00134; printed p. 130]

namely the Word and Sacraments. For since the inheritance has passed through
Christ's death to believers and every individual believer, so that all may
enjoy that whole inheritance by an equal right, the tablets of the covenant
or Testament and the seals which we call Sacraments—in which is contained
that right in the inheritance which believers and each individual believer
obtained through Christ's death—are also appointed for them.

### XVII

*[Marginal heading: Unbelievers are sacrilegious whenever they employ the
Sacraments, since these were instituted only for God's believers, who are
heirs.]*

And because, just as the inheritance is appointed for believers alone, so the
tablets and testimonies of this inheritance were instituted for them alone, it
follows that the Sacraments of God's covenant cannot be employed by
unbelievers—whom God does not acknowledge as heirs—except with sacrilege.
Unbelievers certainly prove by the deed itself that the Sacraments were not
instituted for them by God, since they recognize that they cannot be admitted
to receive them unless they pretend to be God's children, for whom alone they
see that the Sacraments were appointed by God's command.

### XVIII

*[Marginal heading: Refutation of an error.]*

They therefore err gravely who think that God instituted the Sacraments both
for believers and unbelievers—I mean those who persevere in unbelief—whom he
never acknowledged and never will acknowledge as his heirs. If they force
themselves into receiving the Sacraments, they are no longer Sacraments but
beggarly elements of the world for unbelievers.

### XIX

*[Marginal heading: Inequality of spiritual gifts does not infringe the
communion of believers.]*

Although a certain diversity is visible in Christ's Efficacy, since he is
accustomed sometimes to confer greater gifts, both spiritual and temporal, on
some than on others, as may be seen by comparing the Old and New Testaments,
this nevertheless detracts nothing from communion, as though the same things
were not common to believers. Rather, this diversity establishes that
communion. For God gives unequal gifts in such a way that he meanwhile wishes
everyone to refer his own gifts to the edification of the body and its common
salvation, so that they ought to be regarded not so much as his own as the
property of each person.

[Frame 00135; printed p. 131]

### XX

*[Marginal heading: The believer's right, which is the second general part of
this doctrine; that this right is twofold.]*

The right of believers in this common good is twofold. First, just as God sent
the Son into the world for the salvation of the elect, so he gives and offers
the same Son to each of them through the preaching of the Gospel so that this
treasure may be theirs. 1 John 4:9; Ephesians 3:8; John 1:16. Second, every
believer through faith raised in the heart by the Holy Spirit embraces Christ
with his benefits, that singular gift of the heavenly Father. John 1:12;
Galatians 4:4–6. The bond of this conjunction is Christ's Spirit, through whom
we are bound to Christ.

### XXI

Because this faith, through which—if you consider us—we obtain a right in
Christ and his benefits, is an effect of the Holy Spirit, it is often signified
in a transferred sense by the word “Spirit” in the Scriptures.

### XXII

*[Marginal heading: The second member of this right.]*

This communion is effected through faith by application of God's promises to
ourselves.

### XXIII

*[Marginal heading: What application is.]*

This application, by which we are made partakers of Christ and all his
benefits, is nothing other than the work of the Holy Spirit, since faith, from
which it arises, is from the Holy Spirit. By the Spirit's power the enlightened
intellect does not await a particular word from heaven, but by reasoning from
general promises directed to general objects descends to particular objects,
accommodating the general promises to them.

### XXIV

*[Marginal heading: The manner in which it is effected, illustrated by an
example.]*

This application or accommodation consists in what they call a practical
syllogism. That is, the heart perceives in practice that it is the sort of
object to which the promise is directed, and by reasoning from the promise and
the nature of the truthful and omnipotent God, it concludes that the promise
will be fulfilled. Example: the promise is, “Blessed are those who hunger and
thirst for righteousness, because they will be satisfied,” Matthew 5:6.
Application: the promise of present blessedness and future satisfaction
belongs to every object which experiences in itself hunger

[Frame 00136; printed p. 132]

and thirst for righteousness. By God's grace I experience that hunger and
thirst in myself. Therefore the promise of present blessedness and future
satisfaction also belongs to me. And since God who promised is truthful and
powerful, it cannot happen that I will not enjoy them, and so forth.

### XXV

*[Marginal heading: If human beings are considered, this communion is effected
only through faith, and has always been so effected.]*

No believers have ever been made partakers of Christ and his benefits by any
other means than this application, which is effected through faith, by which,
embracing Christ the Mediator, they looked to him alone. Exodus 19:6; Hebrews
4:2; John 8:56; Galatians 4:29. Therefore, since all have been saved in the
same way, it follows that believers of each Testament have an equal condition,
whether you consider what is communicated or the right which they have in that
common good.

### XXVI

*[Marginal heading: Commendations of faith in respect of this communion.]*

Hence, because this communion with Christ is perfected by faith alone, come
those commendations of faith in Scripture, John 8:24; Romans 14:23; Hebrews
11:6; John 6:47. The reason is that all who lack faith, since they have no
communion with Christ, are strangers to God and his covenant and therefore
already condemned. John 3:18.

### XXVII

*[Marginal heading: That this communion is spiritual; its reason.]*

When it is said to be effected through faith, it is evident why it is called
spiritual communion, since this whole apprehension belongs to the soul and
faith and is effected in human souls through the Holy Spirit's operation.

### XXVIII

*[Marginal heading: The absurd opinion of certain people is refuted.]*

It is therefore called spiritual not in respect of what is communicated,
since it is certain that Christ is communicated to us not only in Spirit but
also in humanity, indeed wholly, however great he is. Much less is it called
spiritual because we receive Christ's body with the mouth invisibly,
non-locally, and hyperphysically, as crude men have absurdly invented contrary
to the analogy of faith and the truth of the human nature in Christ.

### XXIX

[Frame 00137; printed p. 133]

*[Marginal heading: That it is the closest conjunction.]*

Likewise, since it is effected through faith by the Holy Spirit, it is evident
that it is not imaginary but the closest, truest, and, if I may say so, most
real union, since what is effected by God's Spirit is effected most truly and
really.

### XXX

*[Marginal heading: Confirmed by comparison with lesser unions.]*

Indeed, this union of us with Christ is so firm and close that, except for the
union of the persons in the divine essence and the union of the two natures in
Christ, no union is closer or firmer than this.

### XXXI

*[Marginal heading: Since no equally close earthly union is given, it cannot
be expressed by the conjunction of earthly things but may be shadowed in a
certain way.]*

So that this supreme conjunction may be shadowed for us, Scripture is
accustomed to explain it by various comparisons taken from earthly things
which have the closest union. Thus it uses the comparison of the union of food
and drink with our body, John 6:53: “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of
man,” and so forth; that is, unless you are joined to my body as closely as
food and drink are joined to yours, you will not have life. It uses marriage,
that is, the conjunction of bridegroom and bride, husband and wife: Jeremiah
3:1; Hosea 2:16, 18, 21; Isaiah 45; Ezekiel 16:8. It uses head and members:
Ephesians 1:10. The conjunction of father and children: Hebrews 2:13. Brothers
with one another: Hebrews 2:11–12; Matthew 12:49; Romans 8:29. Coheirs: Romans
8:17. The head of a household and the household, whose ruler and Head is God.
Friends: Luke 12:4; John 11:11 and 15:12. Indwelling, and indeed reciprocal:
Ephesians 3:22; John 14:23 and 15:4–6. Hence we are called God's Temples, 2
Corinthians 6:16. A garment and the thing clothed: Romans 13:14. A building and
its parts: Colossians 2:7; and within a building, again, the comparison of the
foundation, 1 Corinthians 3:11, and the cornerstone, Ephesians 2:20. It uses
the comparison of grafting seen in trees, Colossians 2:7, and likewise of
gluing, 1 Corinthians 6. Hence the Church, that is, the assembly of believers,
is the body whose head is Christ, the bride whose bridegroom is Christ, the
house whose foundation is Christ, and so forth.

[Frame 00138; printed p. 134]

### XXXII

*[Marginal heading: That these comparisons can be referred to two orders.]*

These comparisons are partly only metaphorical and partly also metonymical,
because they look to the Sacraments by which this communion is sealed.

### Three corollaries

1. Christ alone is that bread of life, because in him alone, and indeed
abundantly, people find that with which they may satisfy and quench the hunger
and thirst of souls which, deprived of God's grace outside Christ, languish and
waste away, and by which they may be nourished unto eternal life.

2. Unbelievers have no communion with Christ. First, because he was neither
given to them, nor they to Christ, nor did Christ pray for them, John 17:9.
Second, because they lack the faith by which this communion is perfected.
Third, because they never experience the fruit of this communion, John
6:50–51.

3. This communion is not physical: it is not effected by a mixing of
substances, oral eating of Christ's flesh, or any conjunction of his flesh with
ours; nor is it such a communion that, in order to constitute it, a local,
real—they are accustomed to explain it in various ways—presence of Christ's
body in or with the bread of the Holy Supper is in any way required.

First, because it has not been promised; indeed, it conflicts with the truth
of Christ's body, the analogy of faith, and all Holy Scripture.

Second, it ought not to occur. For if it is forbidden to mix together the
persons in the Trinity, where there is the highest identity of essence, or the
natures in Christ, where there is identity of subsistence, much less will it
be permissible to mix them with us.

Third, it would not profit, John 6:63. The flesh, when slain, profits greatly;
when eaten, however—namely, by the bodily mouth—no matter how you might say
this happens in order to conceal the error, it profits nothing.

Fourth, because Christ's body assumed within our bodies would on this
reasoning not escape corruption, since our bodies are corrupted.

Fifth, the bodily absence of Christ from earth, and therefore also from the
bread of the Holy Supper, is so far from hindering the union of believers with
Christ, as papists and Ubiquitarians think, that on the contrary Christ says
his absence according to the flesh is the cause of this union: “Unless I go
away,” he says, “that Paraclete will not come to you; but if I go away, I will
send him to you.”

Sixth, it would follow that all the Fathers under the Old Testament perished,
because since Christ had not yet

[Frame 00139; printed p. 135]

assumed a body, how could they have eaten it orally? Yet Christ says not only
that eating the bread of life gives life but also that no one can live without
eating the bread of life. Therefore, just as there is no reason for us to drag
the sun's body down from heaven when it shines abundantly for us and benefits
us by its heat, illuminating the earth with its rays, so let us not drag
Christ's body down from its heavenly throne, but be content with the enjoyment
of its power, grace, and benefits.

Although Christ's body is as far not only from the bread of the Holy Supper—to
which certain unlearned people either fasten or confine it—but also from this
earth as heaven is distant from earth, which we profess in the Creed of faith,
Christ is nevertheless as present to every believer and as closely joined to
each as the head is to any member of the body. Indeed, head and limbs are not
bound together through the nerves descending from the head as closely as we
are bound to Christ through the common Spirit, as was abundantly demonstrated
by us above.

And why do we marvel that the absence of Christ's body from earth does not
hinder our communion with him, when not even in human things does an interval
of places dissolve communion? For a wife separated from her husband by a
distance of places is still bone from her husband's bones and flesh from his
flesh, nor is she released from the law of her husband because of the interval
of places.

### XXXIII

*[Marginal heading: That the conjunction of believers with one another rests
on the communion which they have with Christ.]*

This union of the godly with Christ is the cause of the conjunction of
believers with one another, John 15:12 and 1 John 4:11. For since they are
members of the same body whose head is Christ, all enjoy it by an equal right.
Hence they are called coheirs, members of the same body, sharers of the
promise, fellow citizens with the saints, citizens together, members of the
household, brothers, and all one. Ephesians 2:19; Matthew 23:8; Romans 14:15;
Galatians 3:28.

### XXXIV

Just as righteousness and eternal life belong to all believers by an equal
right, so it is also equitable that those spiritual gifts which are the
firstfruits of eternal life, and which God sometimes confers more liberally on
some than others, should be directed by those on whom they were conferred to
the salvation and preservation of the whole body.

[Frame 00140; printed p. 136]

### XXXV

Indeed, insofar as it can be done and God's glory and the Church's edification
allow, the use of these gifts ought also to overflow even to those who are
outside. This is both because some who belong to God's sheep are often hidden
among them, and because we must imitate the heavenly Father, who makes his sun
rise over the good and evil.

### XXXVI

Therefore, since believers have communion first with Christ and then with one
another, theologians distinguish the Communion of Saints into the communion
which believers have with Christ and that which they have among themselves.
It was our purpose in these theses to demonstrate what that first communion
is. Since the latter is an effect of the first, when the first has been
understood the latter will itself be easily understood.

### XXXVII

*[Marginal heading: By what means the Holy Spirit effects that conjunction of
Christ and believers.]*

The means which the Holy Spirit uses both to effect through faith this
communion of believers with Christ and with one another, and also to seal the
same communion, are the Word and Sacraments. Hence in the Supper the sacrament
of bread is called the communion of Christ's body, 1 Corinthians 10:16, because
it seals the communion which we have with Christ through faith.

### XXXVIII

*[Marginal heading: That this conjunction is indeed perfect, yet meanwhile has
its stages of growth, and will have its last in the other life.]*

This union of believers with Christ, according to the order or dispensation of
divine wisdom, has its own growths and, as it were, stages by which it is led
to completion. Although faith by God's free gift possesses the whole Christ
in common with all believers as a common fountain, it nevertheless enjoys him
in this life according to the given measure of faith, which draws from that
fountain only as much as the Lord knows is expedient, although it meanwhile
possesses him whole. Ephesians 2:21 and 4:15–16.

### XXXIX

*[Marginal heading: What those stages are.]*

The stages of communion change nothing in the substance

[Frame 00141; printed p. 137]

of the covenant or in what is communicated, but only establish a difference
in quantity and quality. In quantity, as in the abundance of the Spirit given
to believers. In quality, as in clarity of revelation on God's part and of
knowledge on ours. Acts 2:17; Joel 2:28; Isaiah 44:3; John 1:17; Hebrews 10:1.

## Theological Aphorisms on the Justification of a Person before God

### First Aphorism

1. *[Marginal heading: What justification is.]* Justification is an act of God
by which he pronounces a person righteous because of the perfect obedience
rendered to him and makes that person a participant in eternal life. Romans
8:33; 3:26; 4:1, 4, 5; Matthew 19:16.

2. *[Marginal heading: Since the obedience on account of which a person is
justified is twofold, justification also is twofold, namely legal and
evangelical.]* This obedience either inheres in the person himself as its
subject, who is accounted righteous by it; or it inheres in another subject,
but is imputed to the person whom God justifies. The former is called ours,
the latter Christ’s; the former legal, the latter evangelical. Galatians 2:16;
Romans 10:3; Ephesians 2:8.

3. It is called legal, not so much because the exact form of this obedience is
prescribed in the Law, as because the Law demands this obedience from us—and
indeed from our own powers, powers which God gave sufficiently great in the
first creation—and declares that, unless we render it, we are already liable
to the curse. Romans 10:5; Deuteronomy 6:5; Matthew 22:37.

4. *[Marginal heading: What legal obedience is; what justification from the
Law is.]* Legal obedience, therefore, is the fulfillment of the whole Law
rendered by the person who is pronounced righteous by it, or the conformity
with the Law of the nature and every action of the person who is justified by
it. Galatians 3:10, 12; Romans 2:13; Romans 4:4.

5. Consequently, to say that a person is justified through the righteousness
of the Law, from the Law, from works of the Law, from good works, or from his
own righteousness is to say the same thing; all these expressions return to
the same point, as is plainly evident from Philippians 3:9 and Romans 10:3.

6. The reason that works of the Law are so often mentioned

[Frame 00144; printed p. 140]

is that only those works which are commanded in the Law have promises of life,
and only those please God. Thus acts of self-chosen worship and observances of
human commandments fall to the ground: no one is justified by them, nor indeed
can anyone be.

7. Although it is certainly true that, when we have done everything commanded
in the Law, eternal life ought to be adjudged to us—even though we are
unprofitable servants and have done only what we were obliged to do, Luke
17:10—nevertheless it pleased the most gracious God voluntarily to bind
himself as well to give eternal life to those who fulfill the Law.

8. Hence, if eternal life were to come to a person through fulfillment of the
Law, it would be called merit and debt, as the Apostle says in Romans 4:4.

9. *[Marginal heading: No person renders the obedience which the Law demands,
and therefore no one is justified from the Law.]* Since justification from the
Law requires perfect observance of God’s commandments, it is evident that no
person who has ever lived or will live, Christ excepted, can be accounted
righteous from the Law, Galatians 2:6. The unregenerate person sins
continually, while the regenerate person sins daily, so that every day he
says, “Forgive us our debts.” Now the Law is not from faith: that is, it does
not promise life to those who believe but to those who do: “The person who
does these things will live in them.” Life, therefore, cannot be hoped for
from the Law. Add that, once you have transgressed the Law, the Law has no
promise of forgiving your sins. But if you should say that you will at least
make up by a subsequent good work what was lacking, I ask you: since you
already owe the very work which you are going to do, how will you pay the
debt? Moreover, no good work of yours whatever is equivalent to the sin
committed.

10. From consideration of this fact the faithful cry out in Psalm 143:2, “Do
not enter into judgment with your servant”; and in Job 9: “How will a person
justify himself with God? If he wished to contend with him, he could not
answer him one thing out of a thousand.”

11. *[Marginal heading: In what sense justification is denied to works of the
Law.]* Therefore, when we say that a person is not justified from the Law, or
from good works, or from his own righteousness, we do not say that good

[Frame 00145; printed p. 141]

works could not do this—for if Adam had continued upright, he would have been
righteous from his own righteousness—but we say that in people who have now
fallen, and all have fallen, good works are found neither so great nor so many
in number; that is, perfect fulfillment of the Law is not found. Consequently,
no person is justified from the Law.

12. *[Marginal heading: Legal justification, which can have no place among
fallen people, having been rejected, evangelical justification, or
justification by faith, must be discussed.]* There remains the alien obedience
which we have called evangelical and which is imputed to a person as
righteousness. Thus, once the other obedience has been removed, all who are
justified can necessarily be accounted righteous from no source other than
this righteousness, which occurs by imputation. The orthodox are therefore
entirely right to say that a person is justified by faith alone, by which they
understand this alien obedience. For Paul says, in Galatians 2:6, Ephesians
2:9, Romans 3:24, and 2 Timothy 3:5, “only through faith,” and likewise “not
from the Law,” “not through the Law,” “not from works which we have done,”
“without works,” “freely,” and “by his grace,” continually opposing these as
immediate contraries. The papists are mad in their reasoning. Who does not see that,
when one immediate contrary is posited, the other is excluded, and therefore
that what is present in the subject is alone because the other has been
excluded, and that the two cannot be in the same subject since they conflict
as one contrary with one contrary?

13. *[Marginal heading: What evangelical obedience is; what evangelical
justification is.]* Evangelical righteousness is indeed fulfillment of the
Law, since there is no other righteousness in God’s sight, Galatians 4:4–5 and
Romans 8:3–4. The Law is established through faith, and God sent his Son in
the flesh so that the right—or righteousness—of the Law might be fulfilled in
us. But this righteousness is not in the person who is called righteous by
it; it is in another, and is imputed to the person whom God justifies just as
if that person had himself rendered it. It is called evangelical because it
is disclosed through the preaching of the Gospel, Romans 1:17.

14. *[Marginal heading: This obedience is Christ’s righteousness.]* This is
Christ’s obedience, which is imputed to us when the heavenly Father deigns to
regard it as ours. Clothed in it, we come into God’s sight and obtain the
right to eternal life in just the way that Jacob long ago, clothed in the
garments of the firstborn Esau, approached his father, was regarded as Esau,
and obtained the birthright.

15. Therefore

[Frame 00146; printed p. 142]

*[Marginal heading: It is Christ’s in such a way that it is ours.]* it can be
called both alien and not alien. It is alien insofar as it is outside us and
resides in another subject, namely Christ; but it is not alien insofar as
that very subject, namely Christ, is ours and has moreover spiritually become
one with us through faith, so that we are accounted his members. Ephesians
5:30.

16. Nor should that obedience of Christ be regarded as any less certainly ours
because it is only imputed to us and is not within us. For just as not all the
things we possess are of such a nature that they are within us, so we must
think about this righteousness. Meanwhile, however, the Gospel and the
Sacraments testify that this obedience is imputed to us who truly believe and
is ours no less than if it had been exhibited by us ourselves, and than if it
adhered to our bodies and souls. Indeed, it is safer and more certain for us
in the merit and intercession of the Mediator than if it were within us,
because on that foundation it cannot be shaken; in us it could be shaken in
many ways, as other spiritual gifts can be.

17. Consequently, you must not think that this imputation of alien
righteousness is an empty thing or exists only in imagination. For just as
our sins were truly and actually laid upon Christ, who knew no sin, in God’s
judgment, and he therefore had to undergo the most bitter death, so also his
obedience is truly and most certainly imputed to us for life in God’s
judgment, just as our disobedience was imputed to him for death.

**Difficulty.** But you will say: Will God not declare himself unjust in this
way, if he regards someone else’s righteousness as ours?

**Solution.** That would indeed be true if it had not been rendered for our
sake. But since Christ rendered for us this obedience to which he was not
obligated, and since God accepts this condition—and no wonder, since he
himself devised this means of reconciling himself to us—it by no means
follows.

28. *[Marginal heading: How legal and evangelical justification differ.]*
Legal and evangelical righteousness therefore differ, not in matter or form,
but in efficient cause and end. They do not differ in matter, because

[Frame 00147; printed p. 143]

each is obedience rendered to God. They do not differ in form, because God’s
Law is the rule of each. For God acknowledges no righteousness other than that
which agrees with this Law.

19. They differ in efficient cause and end. Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:12. In
efficient cause, because legal righteousness is taken for the perfect
obedience rendered by the very person who is accounted righteous by it.
Evangelical righteousness is the perfect fulfillment of the Law rendered not
by the person who is accounted righteous by it, but by Christ; nevertheless,
this obedience of Christ is imputed to the person as righteousness. They also
differ in end: for the end of evangelical righteousness is the glory of God’s
mercy, while that of legal righteousness is rather the glory of human beings,
because a person would have something about which to glory before God. Romans
3:27.

20. *[Marginal heading: Evangelical obedience is called the righteousness of
God.]* This alien or imputative righteousness is called the righteousness of
God partly from its efficient cause, because it is freely given by God through
and for the sake of the Son; and partly from its object, because it alone can
bear the rigor of divine judgment and stand before God’s tribunal. Romans
1:17; Romans 10; Romans 3:22. Daniel 9:23 calls it eternal righteousness: namely,
because it was decreed from eternity in God’s eternal counsel; because the
death of Christ is perpetually efficacious; and because those who have
forgiveness of sins will certainly have eternal life. *[Marginal citation:
Augustine, On the Spirit and the Letter, chapter 9: The term “righteousness of
God” signifies “not that by which God is righteous, but that with which he
clothes a person when he justifies the ungodly.”]*

21. *[Marginal heading: It is called grace.]* In the same sense it is also
called grace, by metonymy of the efficient cause, and a gift, Romans 5:15,
because it comes to us from God’s grace and is imputed to us. In this sense we
also are said to be justified freely, Romans 3:24. But the scholastics’ claim
that we are justified by grace—that is, by infused grace, by which designation
the sanctification of life is understood—is madness, and is openly refuted by
the Apostle when he says, “If it is from works, it is no longer from grace;
otherwise grace will not be grace.” Since this disputation has been undertaken
by us against that opinion, we have refuted it below with the most solid
arguments brought forward from Sacred Scripture.

22. Hence eternal life, which is the effect of righteousness, is now called an
inheritance and a gift, just as with respect to legal righteousness it was
called merit and debt.

**Difficulty.** But how are we said to be justified freely, and how

[Frame 00148; printed p. 144]

is eternal life called a gift, if we are justified because of Christ’s merit?

**Solution.** Human merits, not Christ’s, are excluded here. For our salvation
cost Christ dearly. Therefore you can rightly say that we are justified by
merits, because we are justified by Christ’s merits, and by works, because we
are justified by Christ’s works.

23. *[Marginal heading: It is likewise called the righteousness of faith.]*
The same righteousness, with respect to us who apprehend it through faith and
apply it to ourselves, is called the righteousness of faith. Hence we are also
said to be justified by faith. Romans 4:13.

24. *[Marginal heading: This designation shows the application of this
righteousness on the human side, against those who think that God also
accounts righteous those in whom the Holy Spirit has as yet accomplished
nothing.]* Therefore, to say that we are justified by faith is the same as if
you said that our righteousness before God is the obedience of the Son of God
which is apprehended by faith. Hence “through faith in Christ” is customarily
added, Galatians 2:16, 20, and in countless places, so that the object of faith
may be denoted. That distinguished servant of Christ, Dr Bucer, put it
splendidly: “When the righteous person is said to live by faith, the meaning
is this: he lives by faith because faith receives, but does not bring, the
cause of God’s life in a righteous person, namely righteousness.” But come,
let us illustrate the matter by a comparison. Just as the hand which receives
a donated treasure does not enrich, but the treasure does, and the mouth
which receives food does not nourish, but the food itself does, so neither the
work nor action of faith justifies us, but Christ himself whom we apprehend by
faith. Through faith we understand that we are justified because of Christ,
and through faith we embrace that same Christ. Hence faith is both the eye by
which we see Christ and the hand by which we apprehend him when offered.

25. Because the obedience of Christ is understood by the righteousness of
faith, the righteousness of faith is always opposed in the Scriptures to the
righteousness of the Law, and faith to works of the Law. Considered in
themselves, unbelief is contrary to faith, while idleness, or the interruption
of good works, is contrary to works of the Law. But with respect to
justification, faith, which relies on Christ’s merit, and works, which rely on
human merit, are contraries. Thus the one who works is opposed to the one who
believes. Considered in themselves, the one who does not believe is opposed to
the one who believes, and the one who does not work to the one who works. But
if you have regard to justification, the believer is opposed to the worker,

[Frame 00149; printed p. 145]

both with respect to the righteousness upon which each relies and with
respect to the end of working. For the believer does not wish to be found
having his own righteousness which is from the Law, which he regards as dung,
nor does he wish to be accounted righteous from it. Rather, he relies solely
upon Christ’s righteousness, by whose imputation he is fully persuaded that
God accounts him righteous. The worker, by contrast, acts in order to be
justified by his own merit and to establish only his own righteousness.

26. *[Marginal heading: In what sense we are said to be justified by faith.]*
By faith we understand not faith itself as a work or some new quality in
us—for we refuted this above, and we are so far from being justified by faith
as a work that we must instead confess the weakness of our faith—but Christ’s
obedience. Therefore, by a metonymy of the container, the thing contained is
understood. What others have said, that we are justified by faith
correlatively, comes down to the same thing. The truth of this assertion is
plain from this fact: in Paul the statement “We are righteous by faith” is
opposed to the proposition “We are justified by works” as its contradictory,
Romans 3:27–28. From the nature of contradiction, therefore, it is clear that
no one is justified by faith insofar as faith is a work, whether you call it
our work or God’s work in us.

27. All whom God justifies he finds ungodly, just as all whom a physician
heals he first finds sick. But the Lord does not leave ungodly all whom he
finds ungodly and justifies. He gives them the Holy Spirit, who begins a new
obedience in them.

28. *[Marginal heading: Justification by faith overturns justification by
works.]* Although the one who has embraced Christ for righteousness also
embraces that same Christ for sanctification, as was just said, Deuteronomy
24:12, so that he also begins to be righteous in himself, Luke 1:6, and to
produce good fruits—which are also called righteousness, but begun
righteousness, not that perfect imputative righteousness which is a person’s
righteousness before God; and which are called merits by the scholastics and
some of the ancients, but improperly and badly, the occasion being taken from
the old version of Hebrews 13:16—nevertheless, these works of regenerate
people are so far from being that righteousness on account of which God
pronounces them righteous and participants in eternal life

*[Marginal note: The brightness of the sun cannot be separated from its heat;
shall we therefore say that the earth is warmed by the light and illuminated
by the heat?]*

[Frame 00150; printed p. 146]

that they are not even any part of it. First, because they are effects of
justification, not causes; but here the question concerns causes. Second,
because they are always polluted by some stain of ours and are few, whereas
righteousness from works requires obedience in all things, and perfect
obedience at that.

29. Hence, when the orthodox are asked why people are justified, or on account
of what, they answer: on account of Christ’s obedience. But when they are asked
who, or what sort of people, are justified, they answer: people who pursue
holiness of life, showing the tree from its fruits, the cause from its effects,
and justification from sanctification. See Psalm 15.

30. *[Marginal heading: In what sense someone can be said to be justified from
works.]* Therefore, if you wish to speak as follows—“A person is justified by
works,” that is, is proved from the sanctification of life to be that person
who is justified by Christ’s obedience, since sanctification follows it as an
effect and testifies about it—you will speak correctly. For James also speaks
this way in chapter 2:21. But our papists reject this. They make good works not
effects but causes of justification, although the tree must necessarily be
good before it bears good fruit, so that in affirming this they seem to be
bereft even of common sense.

31. It is therefore an outrageous slander of the papists that, when we exclude
the merits of good works from justification, they slander us as condemning
good works, or at least as deterring people from the pursuit of good works.
Yet the Apostle, and we with him, teach both: that no one is justified by his
works, and nevertheless that everyone ought to devote himself to the pursuit
of good works. The reason is that the effect neither can nor ought to be torn
away from the cause; those whom Christ has justified he has also sanctified;
and the faithful ought to make their calling sure, 2 Peter 1:10.

32. *[Marginal heading: The state of the controversy between the orthodox and
the papists concerning a person’s justification before God.]* The state of
the controversy between the orthodox and the enemies of God’s grace is
therefore this: whether good works, which we have said above are effects of
justification, justify a person, or are the material cause of justification,
either entirely and alone, as the Pharisees once thought

[Frame 00151; printed p. 147]

and the Jews think today, or in part, as our papists hold; or whether rather
Christ’s obedience alone, works being excluded as associated causes, is that
which is imputed to a person as righteousness, which is the position we
defend. Thus, between us and the papists—to omit others—there is a
contradiction. They say that we are justified by our own righteousness; we,
on the contrary, assert that we are justified by an alien righteousness, that
is, Christ’s, and by that alone. They say by good works, or works of the Law;
we say not by the good works which we have done, but by Christ’s works, or the
righteousness which is through faith.

*[Marginal heading: Good works are therefore excluded from justification, not
absolutely—namely, insofar as they are reckoned to be something, not insofar
as they are its effects—but insofar as they are reckoned to be causes of
justification.]*

33. It must be observed here that the question concerns the works of a
regenerate person. For it would be foolish to ask about the works of an
unregenerate person, since the unregenerate person, being outside Christ, can
never act well.

34. Great, therefore, is the ignorance of the papists, who distinguish good
works here into those of free will, or of an unregenerate person, and those of
a regenerate person, since the former member has never existed in the nature
of things and never will exist.

35. When our papists saw that Scripture plainly teaches that we are justified
only through faith, and therefore could not deny that Christ’s righteousness,
which is called the righteousness of faith, is the material cause of our
justification, they introduced a certain mixture. They said that we are
justified both by faith and by works, as if these two joined together as
associated causes to produce this result. We have said that the state of the
controversy consists in this matter.

36. We will refute this invention of the papists in two ways. First, we will
show that the righteousness of the Law and that of the Gospel cannot join as
associated causes of justification and eternal life. Second, we will show
that the righteousness of works is simply and universally excluded from the
causes of justification, so that those who make something which does not
exist at all concur with something which stands entirely alone plainly waste
their effort. Because this latter part, which by the law of method ought to
have been the first part of the proof undertaken, is longer, we will place it
second.

[Frame 00152; printed p. 148]

*[Marginal heading: Refutation of the papists’ opinion concerning the
justification of a person before God.]*

That we are not justified partly by our own works and partly by Christ’s
obedience is evident from the following. 1. Since these conflict as one
contrary with another, it is impossible for them to concur. 2. Since it has
already been shown simply that no one is justified by works, it is pointless
to ask whether one is justified by faith and works together. Indeed, this
papist invention would make it permissible to affirm against the Apostle as
follows: if the Apostle said that we are justified by faith without works
because we are also justified by faith, it will be permissible to say with
the papists, “We are justified by works without faith,” because we are
justified not only by faith but also by works. What could be said that was
more absurd, more blasphemous, or more contrary to the mind of the Holy
Spirit? 3. It would follow that God is neither perfectly merciful nor
perfectly just. 4. Christ profits such people nothing, Galatians 5:2, 4: let
them either seek their whole salvation in Christ or lose the whole of it. 5.
This invention was the cause of the Jews’ ruin, Romans 10:3. 6. This error was
condemned in the first Synod held after Christ’s Ascension, at which the
Apostles were present and over which they presided, and which was therefore
called Apostolic. 7. Finally, the Apostle, showing the cause of this error in
Galatians 5:8, says, “This persuasion has not proceeded from him who calls
you,” but from the Devil.

*[Marginal note: The question here is not whether good works have a place with
God as things most pleasing to him, and whether God appoints a temporal and an
eternal reward for them. The question is whether they justify a person, or
whether a person is justified on account of them, which we deny.]*

But come, let us hear the Apostle Paul—whom these people, if he were alive,
would drive onto a cross, since they persecute with extreme cruelty those who
profess the same doctrine—first destroying the righteousness of the Law with
five arguments and then constructing the righteousness of faith with seven.

### First Demonstration, from Contraries, Concluded in a Disjunctive Syllogism

A person is justified either from his own works—that is, from the Law—or by
Christ’s obedience—that is, by faith. Romans 1 and 3:20.

He is not justified from works. Therefore he is justified by faith.

The proposition, since it consists in a disjunction of immediate contraries,
is clear and wholly necessary. For the necessity of the disjunction depends
upon the necessary opposition of its parts.

The assumption is proved in two ways. 1. Neither Gentiles nor Jews can be
justified from works. The former are convicted by the law of nature of impiety
toward God and injustice toward their neighbor, Romans 1:18 and following;
the latter are likewise convicted of sin by the written Law of Moses, with
the Prophets, interpreters of that Law, agreeing, Romans 2:17 and following
and 3:10 and following—indeed, to such a degree that through them God’s holy

[Frame 00153; printed p. 149]

name is spoken evil of even among the Gentiles. But righteousness from the
Law requires perfect fulfillment of the Law in every work, not even one
excepted: “Cursed is the one who has not continued in all things.” 2. It is
proved from the effect of the Law, because through the Law we know sin; we do
not wash it away. The Law works wrath. Romans 3:20; 4:15. Therefore the
Apostle concludes, “We conclude, therefore,” he says, “that a person is
justified by faith apart from works.” Romans 3:28.

**Difficulty.** What, then, is the Law? Was it therefore given in vain, if we
are not justified by it? Do we therefore make the Law void through faith?
Romans 3:31.

**Solution.** I deny the consequence. It is a fallacy of a false end, for the
Law was not given for that end. It “was added because of transgressions,”
namely, so that they might be shown, Galatians 3:19, and so that it might thus
lead us to Christ, namely, by directing us to seek Christ’s righteousness. The
Apostle answers in this way when he says, “We establish the Law.” Romans 3:3.

The Apostle turns the argument back *by force*: “If the Law could give life
and was given for that reason, righteousness would truly be from the Law. But
it is not. Show it, therefore, if it is.” Galatians 3:21.

The papists’ first exception to the prosyllogisms which the Apostle brought
forward for the assumption: “It is indeed true,” they say, “that justification
is taken away from works which are done only according to the letter and by
the exertion of free will outside Christ’s grace, as are the works of people
not yet regenerated, but not from the works which are done by the
regenerate.”

*[Marginal note: That is, they say that merit of congruity, not merit of
condignity, is excluded.]*

**Response.** That justification is taken away from the works of the
regenerate is evident from this: Paul takes justification away from the works
of the Jews, whom he opposed to the Gentiles; he takes it away from the works
of Abraham, David, and himself, Romans 4 and Philippians 3:8, and the papists
will not deny that these were regenerate. Add that, since we are dealing here
with good works, do we not exclude the works of the regenerate, since they
alone act well, while the unregenerate never act well? Thus they accuse the
Holy Spirit both of contradiction and of ignorance, as though he labored to
exclude things which exist nowhere.

**Exception 2.** “It is true,” they say, “of the ceremonial Law, but not of
the moral Law: the former does not justify, but the latter does.”

**Response.** That the Apostle is speaking here about the moral Law is plain
from this: he teaches that Jews and Gentiles are transgressors by bringing
forward their sins which conflict with the Decalogue. Likewise, he says that
he would not have known covetousness to be sin unless the Law had said, “You
shall not covet.” Which Law says this? Is it not the Decalogue? Moreover,
since it is said, “Cursed is the one who has not continued in all things that
are written in the book of the Law,” will transgressors of the moral Law not
also be understood under the word “all”? *[Marginal citation: Augustine uses
this very argument in chapter 8 of On the Spirit and the Letter.]*

[Frame 00154; printed p. 150]

**Exception 3.** The third sophism is based on a distinction of time. “It is
indeed true,” they say, “that when we begin to believe we are justified
freely; but afterward righteousness must be acquired for us by our own
merits.”

**Response.** This sophism overturns the earlier ones. Next, that it is
manifestly false is evident from this: David, who had already spent a long
time in God’s school, says, “Blessed is the person to whom the Lord has not
imputed sin.” John confirms the same thing in 1 John 2, writing about sins
committed after faith was received: he shows that we are justified from them
in no other way than by their forgiveness through Christ. Refer here also to
the example of the father of believers.

### Demonstration 2, from an Absurd Effect Posited

If people were taught to be justified from the Law, God’s grace would be
abolished.

The consequent is intolerable. Therefore the antecedent from which it is
inferred is absurd.

The connection is proved because God would have sent his Son into the world,
or delivered him to death, in vain if salvation could have come to people in
another way than this, namely through the Law. For “if righteousness is
through the Law, Christ died in vain,” Galatians 2:21; likewise Romans 4:14.

By a similar line of reasoning it is said: if people can be saved by reception
of the Lord’s Supper, why did Christ not ascend into heaven immediately after
celebrating the Supper? This is against the papists’ *work wrought*.

Nor does it make any difference that the papists say that, when they ascribe
justification to works, they do not in this way take anything away from
Christ or detract from his glory, because they affirm that these good works in
them are from Christ, or that they ascribe justification to works of grace.
For they act just like that Pharisee in Luke 18:3 who wished to establish his
own righteousness. For although in giving thanks to God he confesses that he
is not so upright and better than that publican from himself, but has this by
divine gift, nevertheless, because he shortly afterward boasts of his works,
that is, seeks in himself the thing on account of which he wishes to please
God—just as the papists do today—he departed from God’s presence
unjustified.

### Demonstration 3, from the Removal of the True Effect

If righteousness were from the Law, the inheritance, or eternal life, would
be from the Law.

But it is not from the Law, as the very word *inheritance* shows; and because
it is from grace, is a gift, and is from promise, it is not merit. Galatians
3:18; Ephesians 2:8.

[Frame 00155; printed p. 151]

### Demonstration 4, by Exposition of the Contrary Effect

Through the Law we know sin, or are convicted of sin. The Law works wrath and
a curse, not liberation. Romans 4:15; Galatians 3:10; 2 Corinthians 3:7, 9.

Therefore we are not justified through the Law.

The reason for the consequence is this: because one who is justified must
first be shown to be righteous, whereas being convicted of sin conflicts with
that from the opposite direction.

*[Marginal note: The same argument can be drawn from the end. For the Law was
given to lead us to Christ, not to keep us within itself. It leads us when it
points out our disease, warning us that it cannot be cured unless we flee to
Christ the Physician.]*

### Demonstration 5, from the Adjunct of Time

The Law was given after the free promise concerning justification and eternal
life. Galatians 3:15–17.

Therefore neither righteousness nor life is from the Law.

**Difficulty.** But the Law which was given afterward could abolish the
earlier free promise.

**Solution.** It could in no way do so, and this is shown by an argument from
the lesser: if a person’s testament and promise is firm, God’s is much more
so, Galatians 3:15. But the former is true. Therefore the latter is also.

### Demonstration 6: The Righteousness of Faith Is Established by the Testimony of the Law and the Prophets

The righteousness which has testimony from the Law and the Prophets is that
righteousness by which a person is justified. Romans 3:21.

But only the righteousness of faith has this testimony from the Law and the
Prophets. Therefore it alone is that righteousness.

The assumption is proved by an induction of the following testimonies, to
which no contrary testimonies can be given, since the Holy Spirit does not
contradict himself.

Genesis 3:15: “The seed of the woman will crush the Serpent’s head.”

Genesis 22:18: “All nations will be blessed in your seed.”

In Galatians 4:21 the Apostle brings forward what is written in Genesis 21
about Abraham’s sons, Ishmael, whom Hagar bore, and Isaac, whom Sarah bore,
showing by the allegorical testimony of the Law that the righteousness of
faith is established.

Christ was the end of the Law, Romans 10:4, and therefore, in the promulgation
of the Law, Moses continually recalls to the Jews that free promise made to
their fathers. Galatians 3:1, 23–24.

Circumcision was a seal of the righteousness of faith. Romans 4:11.

*[Marginal note: Our righteousness through faith, although it does not come
to us through observance of the Law, nevertheless has testimony from the
Law.]*

[Frame 00156; printed p. 152]

The Apostle shows expressly in the Epistle to the Hebrews that all the
sacrifices and legal ceremonies prefigured Christ and the righteousness which
is through faith. Stephen the protomartyr confirms the same thing in Acts 7.

Moses did not lead the people into the promised land, but Joshua did. In the
same way, neither does observance of the Law—which is weakened by us—lead us
into heavenly life, but that true Jesus, God’s Messiah, does. The Apostle in
Hebrews 4.

That it is intolerable to God for the glory of salvation and eternal life to
be ascribed to our works is evident even from the type. Just as it was
intolerable to God in the type for the people of Israel to ascribe to
themselves and their merits the glory of their entrance into the land of
Canaan, so neither now—and indeed much less—will he be able to bear our
ascribing eternal life to the works which we have done. Deuteronomy 8 and 9;
Ephesians 2; 1 Corinthians 1.

Isaiah 53:11: “My righteous servant will justify many by the knowledge of
himself.”

Jeremiah 23:6: “He will be called Jehovah our righteousness.”

The same prophet in chapter 31: “I will make a new covenant with the house of
Israel.”

Habakkuk 2:6: “The righteous person will live by his faith.” This is cited by
the Apostle when he says, “That no one is justified by the Law before God is
evident, because the righteous person will live from faith,” with Habakkuk as
witness. Galatians 3:11; Romans 1:17.

Psalm 32:1: “Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven”; and, by
contrast, in Psalm 143 he says, “No flesh will be justified in your sight,”
understand, from obedience to the Law. This is cited by the Apostle in Romans
4:6–7 and Galatians 2:16.

Hence comes that saying of Christ in John 5:39: “Search the Scriptures; they
are those which bear testimony about me.” Christ himself showed this very
thing to the two disciples going to Emmaus by an induction from the
Scriptures, Luke 24.

Hence Peter says in Acts 10:43 that all the Prophets bear testimony about
Christ, that through him we receive forgiveness of sins. Stephen also affirms
the same thing in Acts 7.

And lest anyone should think that Scripture says both things—that salvation
is through the Law and through Christ—Peter, wishing in Acts 4:12 to meet this
delusion of the papists, says that salvation is in no one else, and that no
other name has been given under heaven among people through which they must be
saved. Paul confirmed the same thing when rebuking Peter at Antioch, Galatians
2; and the whole Apostolic Synod did the same, Acts 15.

Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us be
confirmed in the truth of the doctrine,

[Frame 00157; printed p. 153]

namely, that the righteousness of faith is imputed to us for salvation.

### Demonstration 7, from a Comparison of Equals

As Abraham was justified, so also are all those whom God justifies. Romans
4:1–2; Galatians 3:6.

But Abraham was justified through faith, not through the Law, says Paul, by
the testimony of Scripture, Genesis 12:3.

Therefore all are justified in no other way.

The force of the connection depends upon the comparison of equals. The
Apostle himself expressly demonstrated the equality by bringing forward the
final cause, saying that this was written not for Abraham’s sake alone, but
also for ours. The equality is twofold. First, it is in God, who, since he is
always like himself, follows one and the same principle in the justification
of all people. For he does not have two different principles, but follows the
same principle and manner in justifying a person both in circumcision and in
uncircumcision, so that this may be called identity rather than equality. In
those who are justified there is equality: 1. because the condition of the
father of believers and that of his children is the same; 2. because, since
Abraham’s works do not enter into the reckoning of justification, who does not
see that those who are to be justified are equals, and that those already
justified are likewise equals because the same righteousness of Christ has
been imputed to them?

Thus we argue against the papists:

If anyone could have been justified from works, certainly Abraham, the father
of believers, could have been.

But Paul says that he was not justified from works. For if works must be
alleged, you will allege the sacrifice of his son and the things he did in
circumcision.

But he was already justified at that time, and indeed in uncircumcision.

Therefore Abraham does not have from works anything about which to glory
before God. Romans 4:10.

### Demonstration 8, from a Comparison of Similar Things

Just as through Adam sin entered into all people and death through sin,
because all sinned in Adam, so through Christ righteousness entered into all
believers and life through righteousness, because all believers made
satisfaction for sins in Christ.

[Frame 00158; printed p. 154]

This argument is not only from similar things but also from equal things. For
the principle is altogether the same. Why would we become liable to death by
sinning in the old Adam any more than, by satisfying God’s righteousness in
the new Adam, we would become participants in eternal life?

### Demonstration 9, from Effects

From justification through faith arise reconciliation with God, security of
conscience, and hope of the glory of God, that is, of eternal life. Romans
5:1; Titus 3:7.

None of these arise from the Law, because the cause—namely, righteousness
through the Law—is absent. Therefore the righteousness of faith alone is that
true righteousness.

### Demonstration 10, from the Efficient Cause, and Indeed the First Cause

The Apostle proceeds by anticipation to the doctrine of Predestination in
Romans 9. For it could have been objected: “If people were justified through
the obedience of Christ, whom you say is from the Jews according to the flesh
and was given to the world as Savior, the Jews above all would embrace him and
would be justified by his righteousness.” He shows that the connection is
false. For it is not merely contingent; it is also a fallacy of treating what
is not a cause as a cause. Therefore he brings forward a most powerful
argument for the righteousness of faith, in which he also answers their
objection abundantly.

Those whom God chose before the foundation of the world for salvation in
Christ, or that they might be righteous in Christ, are, I say, those who are
justified through faith.

But not all Jews are elect.

Therefore not all are justified. Indeed, since those who are justified were
chosen by God before the foundation of the world, it follows that they are
not justified by their works, since the cause of their salvation precedes not
only their works themselves but also the birth of those who work. Hence
Scripture customarily says that we are saved by grace, and so forth. This
argument is so clear that it alone ought deservedly to stop the mouths of all
the papists.

### Demonstration 11, Likewise from the Efficient Cause

Salvation is obtained in the same manner in which it was promised.

But it was promised not to the one who fulfills the Law but to the one who
believes.

Therefore it is obtained by faith alone. Romans 4:13; Galatians 3:8, 18.

The assumption is proved because, if the contrary were established, this
absurdity would follow:

[Frame 00159; printed p. 155]

namely, if salvation were promised under the condition of fulfilling the Law,
faith would be void and the promise empty, because no one fulfills the Law.
Romans 4:14. Therefore, that from which absurdities follow cannot but be
absurd, and that from which truths follow cannot but be true.

### Demonstration 12, from the Final Cause

The end of a person’s justification before God is that God alone should be
celebrated by it, that the glory of his mercy in the person’s salvation should
be made known, and that a person should have nothing in his merits or powers
about which he could glory before God.

This glory cannot stand unless a person is justified by faith alone. It is
utterly overthrown by the righteousness of the Law. Romans 4:6; Ephesians 2:9;
Romans 3:27; 4:2.

Therefore, in this life a person is pronounced righteous through the
preaching of the Gospel, as though from God’s own mouth, in no other way than
by faith. And in that most renowned assembly of mortals on the last day, he
will be accounted an heir of eternal life in no other way than by the
declaration of God’s free grace as the first cause of salvation. Matthew
25:34.

In order to meet the sophistry of the papists, who strive with all their
effort to overthrow this foundation of the Christian faith even by violently
twisting the testimonies of Sacred Scripture, the following points must be
observed so that the true sense of the passages of Scripture may be
understood:

1. In certain sayings which the papists bring forward against us to establish
justification from works, it must be observed that an argument is not being
brought forward from the cause by virtue of which a person is a participant
in eternal life. Rather, it is shown by an argument from effects or adjuncts
what person is a participant in eternal life. Thus, when John says in his
First Epistle, “The one who does not love remains in death,” he does not mean
to show that our love is the cause of life. Rather, it is an argument drawn
from an effect. It is just as if you should say, “One who does not breathe is
not a human being,” although breathing is not the cause for which someone is
a human being. Thus, in Luke 7:47 the sinful woman is said to have had her
many sins forgiven because she loved much. That the woman’s sins were forgiven
is demonstrated by this argument: that she loves much. The argument is from
an effect, as can be evident to anyone not entirely blind who considers
Christ’s conversation with the Pharisee. For, analytically, as the logicians
say, forgiveness of sins is inferred from its effects.

[Frame 00160; printed p. 156]

Nor does it follow from the fact that the word *hoti*, that is, “because,” is
used, that the argument is therefore drawn from a cause. For this is
ridiculous, because that particle is only a marker of consequence, not of the
topic from which the argument is drawn. Thus you say, “The sun has risen,
because it is day”; “He lives, because he walks”; “There is a great fire in
the furnace, because the heated room is very warm.” In all these examples you
see that the cause is inferred from the effect and that the particle
“because” is the marker of the argument.

2. Sometimes good works are even brought forward as a cause, but then nothing
follows, because the reward is from grace and is not given because of the
merit of the good work, but is given to the person because of Christ. Yet
because the person is sanctified, it is said to be given to him as one who
works, although he obtained a right to it before he worked. This can be seen
in the example of Abraham, who received the promise from God before Isaac was
born, and after he was willing to offer his son received the same promise as
though it were a reward of the work. And in Matthew 20, in the parable of the
servants hired into the vineyard, it is shown most plainly that, although the
reward is given to the one who works, it is nevertheless not given because of
the merit of the work.

3. Indeed, even where Scripture ascribes eternal life to works as a reward,
it calls it an inheritance, Matthew 25 and Colossians 3:20, as though by
prescribed words taking care that we ascribe our blessedness not to works but
to God’s adoption. Thus in this disputation that saying which the rabbis
customarily use about the Sacred Writings is true: “In every place where you
find an objection for the heretic, you also find the remedy beside it.”

They accomplish nothing by their cavils, for the truth cannot be moved from
its place by them. “What is not alone does not justify alone,” they say; “faith is
not alone; therefore it does not justify alone.” Although many things could be
answered to this inept sophism, we will use this answer: the proposition is
false. It is just as if you should say, “One who is not alone in the church
does not speak alone,” although the pastor, while he is not alone there,
nevertheless speaks alone while the others are silent. This cavil indeed
seems clever because the parts seem to be contradictorily opposed, but there
is no contradiction. For it is one thing to be alone, another thing to justify
alone. These two are not opposed; rather, to be alone and not to be alone are
the two things opposed.

“If evil works,” they say, “condemn, then good works will justify.” This is an
argument from a contrary sense and is referred to the topic of equals.

[Frame 00161; printed p. 157]

**Response.** This would indeed be true if they could bring forward someone
who had perfectly fulfilled the Law, for transgression of the Law and
fulfillment of the Law conflict. But since they can bring forward no one who
has fulfilled the Law, and, on the contrary, all are transgressors of the
Law, they accomplish nothing by this argument. I do not deny that God’s holy
servants live here according to God’s Law and produce good works. But I deny
that their good and evil works are opposed in equal degrees, since by their
evil works they transgress God’s Law altogether, while by their good works
they do not observe even the smallest commandment in the Law. The enemies of
God’s grace also customarily bring forward many other objections, which, if
the Lord wills, we will answer in the course of discussion.
