CHAPTER XII

Some True Marks of Moral Precepts Are Proposed and Explained

Having examined those marks and characteristics of Moral precepts which the
adversaries offer us, let us see whether we can propose better indications of
Moral precepts. We do not intend to indicate, much less explain, everything
which is indeed of this kind, for everyone agrees about some of the indications
of moral precepts. No one denies that those things are moral which natural
light, even now in its corrupted state, will readily teach to be most equitable
and necessary to observe, as also those things which are natural to God himself
and whose nonexistence or different existence would imply a contradiction, and
similar things. It will suffice for our purpose to propose some indications or
κριτήρια, “criteria,” by which it will be plainly established that some of the
precepts which the adversaries call Positive and refuse to admit as Moral are
also Moral.

Therefore let this be the first mark: *Those precepts are moral which were
given concerning matters that are, in themselves and not merely because they
are commanded, suitable, congruous, and agreeable to rational nature as such,
or to natural principles—even though this agreement and suitability is not at
first sight apparent to our natural light, now so greatly corrupted, or obvious
to everyone without some external light.* For these precepts concern morals and
are not peculiar to one Nation or people, but are common to all. This rule is
clear from what was said above and is confirmed from the passages cited from
*Thomas*, part 1 of the second part, question 99, article 2, and question 100,
article 2. Perhaps the adversaries will not admit this rule because I added that
this agreement may not be perceived without some external light, for they
maintain that the entire Moral law is perceived by innate light. But who does
not know that our innate light is now exceedingly corrupted and obscured, so
that many things now escape it which were once most familiar? Those remnants of
the image of God with which we were once endowed, and which still survive, are
quite insufficient for discerning and understanding the things which

[1674 logical printed p. 144 | IA leaf 162 | PDF p. 162]

Adam, while not yet corrupt, readily perceived. Dr. *Burmann* did not ignore
this when, as we saw above, he called that moral “which flowed from the image of
God either created together with the human being before the fall or restored
by grace after the fall.” And it is most certain, as explained above, that the
Law of God is far different from that light and that the Law of God in no way
depends upon that light. Therefore, in whatever way we perceive the law—whether
by innate light, restored light, internal light, or external light—the law
always remains the same, having the same nature and force. The matter about
which the law is enacted also remains the same, neither differentiated nor
altered in any way by the manner of perceiving it or by different or varying
means. The law enacted by God concerning covetousness was good, just, and
equitable, agreeable in itself to rational nature, and moral, even though
*Paul* did not perceive it by innate light, Romans 7.

Second: *That Law which is proposed to us in the Scriptures, and which is
neither abrogated by the Legislator nor of its own nature becomes obsolete or
passes away, is moral.* No Orthodox person will deny that the Scriptures are
our sacred pandects, containing all God’s will, or all the laws concerning both
our faith and our conduct which have been proposed to us. And no sane person
will deny that divine laws once given always bind unless they have either been
abolished or expired. For only the one who established and fixes laws has the
power to rescind them again. Divine laws are abolished by God either when he
expressly declares that particular laws have been abolished individually, or
when he indicates that laws of a certain kind now cease—that is, when in general
terms he indicates that laws of such a kind have been abolished. Such are the
expressions by which he testifies that the Jewish ceremonial law has now been
abrogated, when, namely, he calls them “weak and beggarly elements,” Galatians
4:9; “a shadow of things to come,” Colossians 2:17; “elements of the world,”
Colossians 2:20; “carnal rites imposed until the appointed time of reformation,”
Hebrews 9:10; and “a yoke which not even the fathers were able to bear,” Acts
15:10. From this last expression we infer that certain Judicial laws of the
Jews have been abolished. There are also other laws which, although they have
not been formally abolished, either in kind or individually, nevertheless do
not now bind because they have expired of their own nature and have lost every
force of binding. Such are those enacted for a peculiar people or on account of
some particular reason or circumstance which now has no place. In this way some
other Judicial laws, whose particular reason now ceases, have become obsolete,
as have other laws mentioned in the Gospel, as we will show immediately.

Although this rule appears most equitable and is really conceded by everyone,
since no one is accustomed to say that a law once in force now binds no longer
without also attempting to prove it, either by showing that the obligation
ceases when its particular reason ceases, or by demonstrating that it has been
abolished either individually or in kind, it will nevertheless be worthwhile
to resolve some little objections.

First, it can be objected that the law about not eating things offered to idols
appears to have been established by the Apostles and others at the Jerusalem
Synod in Acts 15, and yet it is not regarded as a moral precept. *Response:* That
law was originally ceremonial, enacted for the end that the Jews should be
instructed to reject idols; see Exodus 34:15 and Numbers 25:1–3. Yet that Synod
judged it best that, for as long as the ceremonies were not

[1674 logical printed p. 145 | IA leaf 163 | PDF p. 163]

deadly, though they were indeed dead, and the Synagogue had not yet been buried,
as they say, the people should for a time abstain from those things offered to
idols—namely, for as long as the scandal to Jews, the Weak, and Gentiles
continued. For this deed would have been a scandal to them all: to the former,
because by this means they might perhaps have been confirmed in their idolatry,
as though that idolatrous mania were approved by Christians eating things
sacrificed together with the idols themselves; to the second, as people not yet
sufficiently instructed concerning their Christian liberty, who might therefore
be induced by the others’ example to practice the same thing against their own
conscience; and finally to the last, because in this way they would have become
more alienated from Gentiles converted to Christ and would have regarded them
as idolaters or as people who favored idols. Therefore this ceremonial law was
not established perpetually. Rather, at the time when it was in itself an
indifferent matter, its observance was proposed for as long as this threefold
scandal endured. But now this law is completely removed: first, because when
the scandal ceases, the precept itself, which was to be continued only by reason
of the scandal, ceases; next, because the very subject matter of this command
ceases among us, although the precepts about avoiding scandal still morally
bind us generally in a similar case; and finally, because it is expressly
abrogated by *Paul* in 1 Corinthians 10:25, 27: “Eat whatever is sold in the
market, making no distinction for conscience’s sake. If any unbeliever invites
you and you wish to go, eat whatever is set before you, asking no questions for
conscience’s sake.”

*Heidegger* rightly says in *On the Dietary Law*, page 25: *[3. Moreover, we
rightly contend that this abstinence from eating things offered to idols was
necessary, not in itself, but on account of necessary causes which the Apostles
undoubtedly considered here in the council. When these causes cease, Christian
liberty is consistent with either abstaining or not abstaining from eating them.
No one will disclose the Synod’s intention to us more successfully than Paul
himself, who together with the other Apostles also framed the decree. The
Corinthians asked him whether it was lawful to eat things offered to idols or
not. He proceeded to resolve this question in three entire chapters—the eighth,
ninth, and tenth of the first Epistle to the Corinthians. And throughout he
acts in such a way as to assert the right to eat things offered to idols, while
teaching that the practice must be restrained for many reasons. This is evident
from the text for many reasons which are not to be despised.]* He enumerates and
explains seven of these reasons in the same place, pages 254–256, which the
reader can consult.

It is further objected from the same Synod’s decree that there was a precept
about abstinence from things strangled and from blood. The same response
suffices. For those precepts were ceremonial, Leviticus 17:11–13, 23, and
3:16–17; Genesis 9. *Heidegger* demonstrates this more fully in the place cited
above, chapter 4. That these laws have now been abolished is evident from
Matthew 15:11, 17; Romans 14:14–15; 1 Corinthians 8:4, 7, 10; 1 Timothy 4:4;
Colossians 2:20–22; and Titus 1:14–15. “If,” *Heidegger* says on page 271, “in
those passages which testify that nothing can defile a person, that all things
are clean to the clean, that all things are lawful for believers but not all
things edify, and that no one is to be hindered by these decrees—‘Do not taste,
do not touch,’ and so forth—this liberty from the prohibition of blood is not
also declared, then it would not have been committed to Christ and the Apostle
to add this exception at some point and to teach, even by a slight indication,
that the concession was not general and indefinite but that this law concerning
things offered to idols and blood had to be excepted. Who would believe that the
apostle, who everywhere most prudently instructed the faithful and distinguished
the vile from the precious and unlawful things from lawful things, would by so
obscure an expression furnish
an occasion for incrimination? Once an evident reason has been given, why, when
the occasion presented itself so often,

[1674 logical printed p. 146 | IA leaf 164 | PDF p. 164]

would he too readily have introduced that exception to the universal rule? The
Synod’s decree concerning this matter was framed in very few words
and supported by neither many nor very clear reasons. Indeed, it is not fitting
to strengthen decrees with appendages of reasons. For a law does not debate but
commands. It would have been more proper elsewhere, where it could conveniently
have been done, to treat this matter more fully, to illuminate the Synod’s
intention, and to demonstrate plainly that this precept binds in every
circumstance of time and persons. So far is this from having been done anywhere
by the Apostles that, on the contrary, Paul manifestly clothes these decrees in
the meaning which we represent in our response.”

1. Further, blood was prohibited for a typological reason, for it was a type of
the reconciliation to be obtained by Christ’s blood, Leviticus 17:11. Because
blood is in some manner the life of animals, Genesis 9:4, by means of this
prohibition the Lord taught plainly enough that the thing in which the life of
animals consists ought to be devoted to him, so that Christ’s substitution in
the sinner’s place might be represented and foreshadowed. It makes no difference
that no word about sacrifices occurs here. For, as *Heidegger* rightly notes in
the place cited above, page 66, “It was not necessary for him to mention
sacrifices, because their νομοθεσία, ‘legislation,’ preceded the flood. It was
therefore quite sufficient to establish by an explicit edict that which had
never been established before and which, as a new and more explicit type, was
added to the law of sacrifices.” God therefore did not wish the sacrifices to
be profaned, or human beings to claim for themselves what God wished to be
reserved to himself for the representation of Christ’s sacrifice. Therefore
Leviticus 17:11 says, “Because the life of the flesh is in the blood, I have
therefore given it to you upon the altar to make expiation.” For וau is an
inferential conjunction of the consequent from the antecedent when כי precedes.

*Heidegger* demonstrates briefly as follows that this precept was ceremonial,
in the place cited above, page 70: “For it concerned a matter indifferent by its
own nature, in the observance of which, once God’s positive command is set aside,
no part of holiness consists. It did not bind either always or everyone. For
before the flood this law was not observed with an understanding of holiness
and religious worship. They lacked God’s command and promise, without which
ἐθελοθρησκεία, ‘self-imposed worship,’ makes a work vicious. But there were at
one time periods in which that entire obligation was erased, and it was
expressly declared that every creature of God is good, that nothing is to be
rejected, that nothing which enters a person’s mouth can defile him, and that
all things are now clean. Indeed, since the antitype had already been present
and expiation had been made in Christ’s blood, the type could not fail to become
obsolete. And at the very time when some were bound to keep this precept, others
were released and free from it. I understand the period in which the Gentiles
were rejected from the external visible church. I think this occurred not in
Abraham’s time but only four centuries after the covenantal promise made to
Abraham, Genesis 15:15–16—namely, the time when the Gentiles’ measure of sins
was full, their rejection was mature, and in particular the posterity of Ham,
the Canaanites, were devoted to destruction through wars first waged under
Joshua’s leadership against the Canaanites and afterward against them as the
accursed seed of the serpent, and so forth.”

In these words we also see reasons which sufficiently confirm the abrogation of
this law. For after the antitype’s coming, the types vanish, so that it is no
less lawful now to eat blood than fat, which was also prohibited formerly,
Leviticus 3:16–17; 17:23, and so forth. This is also evident from the fact that
this abstinence was first instituted as an instruction in a spiritual matter,
as may be seen in Genesis 9:4–5. The meaning of these words here appears to be,
as some maintain: “I therefore will that you abstain from the blood of animals,
so that by this means you may learn to

[1674 logical printed p. 147 | IA leaf 165 | PDF p. 165]

abhor the shedding of human blood, because I value human life so greatly that I
will not spare even beasts which have shed a person’s blood. Therefore I do not
wish you to eat beasts’ blood, lest, gradually degenerating into savagery, you
also begin to thirst for human blood.” “Very recently,” *Heidegger* says in the
place cited above, page 67, “insolent people had mingled themselves with human
communities: some had already acted violently before, while others had sprung
from the ill-fated union of the Sethites and Cainites. Fierce and terrifying in
the strength of their bodies, they had advanced to human slaughter under a
Fury’s torch. Lest thereafter, when the human race was restored, their mind
should grow hot with similar savagery and they should learn to regard human
blood as cheap, God guards against this with a severe law and
acts with great earnestness. First he forbids them even to taste the blood of
brute beasts, lest, as it passes into a person’s disposition, they should be
imbued with the cruelty to which the world had lately offered funeral sacrifice
by its own ruin and destruction.”

Nor are we moved by the arguments of those who maintain that this law is still
in force because *Paul* did not properly abrogate the law enacted at the Synod,
but rightly interpreted it by showing that, outside the case of scandal which
prevailed at that time, the practice of this liberty was free to everyone—or
that all Christians were free from ceremonial laws—although in that critical
case, when many who were ignorant of Christian liberty were liable to be
offended by such a practice, it was necessary for the sake of those weak persons
and on account of the law of love to abstain for a time. Nor is it of great
importance that permission to eat blood is not expressly given. This was not
necessary, since the general abolition of all ceremonies sufficiently proves
the abolition of this or that ceremony in particular; and when permission is
given to eat every food, permission is also given to eat this food in particular
outside a case of scandal. In such a case the Apostle piously resolved in 1
Corinthians 8:13 “not to eat meat forever, lest he scandalize his brother.”

What *Curcellaeus* says is indeed true: “We must embrace all God’s commands as
being of perpetual duration unless God declares the contrary to us elsewhere.”
But this does nothing for the present matter, for we have already demonstrated
that God has declared the contrary with respect to this precept. And from what
has been said it is plainly evident that God did not wish this precept’s
obligation to be of perpetual duration.

They should not say that no example can be produced of a decree made so solemnly
which would endure for only a short time. For if we consider properly what the
Synod decreed, its decree was not to endure for some short time but binds us
even now. For the Synod had decreed that people should abstain from those things
in a case of scandal and for as long as the scandal endured, and consequently
even to this very day if a scandal were posited—which is now indeed impossible,
the Gospel having been sufficiently promulgated. This is far different from
ordaining that they should abstain simply. It is in fact to say that this
abstinence is not simply necessary or commanded in itself but is indifferent,
which supposes that the law formerly enacted concerning this abstinence has now
been abrogated.

Nor is the contrary proved by the fact that this very thing “seemed good to the
Holy Spirit.” For not everything the Holy Spirit commands is perpetual;
otherwise all the ceremonies would be perpetual, as would the positive divine
laws which also took their origin from the Holy Spirit. Moreover, as was said,
the decree concerned abstinence in a case of scandal and thus was to last as
long as the scandal endured. Even in this case the things concerning which a
suitable decree had been made were called “necessary” in no other actual sense.

[1674 logical printed p. 148 | IA leaf 166 | PDF p. 166]

It is further objected that the washing of feet prescribed in John 13 has not
been abrogated and yet does not now bind. *Response:* There is no institution
there requiring that rite to be perpetually observed or even repeated. By that
sign, which Christ did not prescribe for renewed use, he merely taught them
humility and mutual service. Christ himself does not employ this sign in the
manner of an institution binding the Disciples, but by this example alone
teaches how they ought to conduct themselves toward one another. Hence by that
washing, which signifies nothing absolutely in the manner of an institution
but naturally denotes humility as an effect denotes its cause, after the act
had been performed he finally instructs them concerning the end for which it
was done—namely, to propose to them an example of humility and teach that they
ought kindly and courteously to submit themselves to duties outwardly sordid
for the good of one another. Hence we nowhere read that this rite was repeated
by the Apostles or the primitive Church, because it was proposed merely as an
example. “As for the external washing of feet,” *Beza* says on the passage,
“Christ by no means intended to institute such a sacred rite in the Church.
Rather, by this manner of speaking, following the custom of washing feet which
had been customary in those times and regions from remote antiquity,
particularly for receiving travelers in the evening, as appears from very many
passages of Scripture, he commended the mutual union of all true Disciples of
Christ among themselves for performing whatever is required for mutual
edification—not only by word but also by his own example. Hence Paul’s words, ‘I
made myself the servant of all, that I might gain more,’” 1 Corinthians 9:19.
Hence that examination for choosing the widow who was to be a Deaconess:
whether she had washed the saints’ feet, 1 Timothy 5:10; and the reproach made
to the Pharisee, Luke 7:44. “Therefore this is a general precept comprehending
the mutual love of all Christians among themselves, to be demonstrated by every
kind of service, and especially appropriate to those whom the Lord has set over
others in teaching and as an example of all true virtues, among which
ταπεινοφροσύνη, ‘humility,’ so greatly commended to them, certainly excels,”
Matthew 20:27–28. “Here,” *Gualter* says on the passage, “one must not insist on
the words or the letter, as Kings, Pontiffs, Abbots, and their like do in an
excessively superstitious manner, converting Christ’s example into a theatrical
game. Rather, by this expression he commands humility and an industry prepared
for any duties by which we consult our neighbor’s advantage and welfare. The
end of this precept is love, which cannot exist where people do not willingly
submit themselves to serve anyone. He mentions the washing of feet partly to
teach by the lowest and most contemptible kind of service that nothing should
seem so vile or sordid to us that we would not willingly undertake it if our
neighbor needs it, and partly to commend to us the end of all duties toward
which they ought to be directed.” See what follows. Consult also *Calvin* on the
passage; *Piscator*, *Aretius*, and *Rollock* on the passage; and the most
illustrious *Voetius*, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 1, book 2, treatise 1,
chapter 7, pages 446–447.

It can also be objected that the command concerning the “holy kiss” apparently
does not now bind, although we do not read that it was abrogated. We have said
enough elsewhere concerning this kiss. See the book against *Velthusius*, last
assertion, page 703.

Finally, James 5:14 is objected: “Is anyone among you sick? Let him summon the
elders of the Church, and let them pray for him, anointing him with oil in the
Lord’s name.” *Response:* We indeed see a practice customary at that time, but
we read no command for the perpetual employment of this rite. Those endowed
with the gift of healing used this external Symbol when they miraculously cured
diseases, as may be seen in Mark 6:13. But once this gift has ceased, why should
that rite

[1674 logical printed p. 149 | IA leaf 167 | PDF p. 167]

employed in connection with this extraordinary gift not cease? See
*Beza* on the passage. “The use of anointing,” *Calvin* says on the passage,
“must be restricted to the effect of the Holy Spirit—but just as the reality of
this sign endured only for a time, I say that the symbol was likewise temporal.
And this is entirely plain: nothing is more absurd than to call something a
sacrament which is empty and does not truly offer us the thing signified.
Everyone is compelled to confess that the gift of healing was temporal, and the
matter itself demonstrates it. Therefore its sign ought not to have been
perpetual. Hence it follows that those who today place anointing among the
sacraments, unless they simultaneously restore the effect which God removed
from the world fourteen hundred years ago, are not true imitators but apes of
the Apostles. Thus our dispute is not whether anointing was at some time a
sacrament but whether it was given to us so that it should still be in use among
us today. We deny the latter because it is evident that the thing signified
ceased long ago.” The words of *Aretius* on the passage must also be noted. “The
sick,” he says, “are to be treated so that the beginning is indeed made with
prayer, while the other means of attaining health are by no means despised.
Therefore let the beginning be made with prayers, indicating this to have been
the ancient custom among the godly: that the beginning of every action at a sick
person’s bedside should be made with prayers—if, of course, it is expedient for
the sick person, if his future health will be for the glory of the divine name
and the neighbor’s usefulness, that God should deign to restore him. If not,
may he give the sick person a constant confession of faith in the struggle of
death. Then, after the prayers are completed, let them anoint him with oil—that
is, let them not neglect health-giving medicines. Thus let some skilled
Physician, or someone else who has knowledge of the disease, be present with
the assembly of Elders. The use of Oil has a place in almost every disease,
especially in Eastern regions and toward the south, where there is a great
abundance of oil. Therefore he commands anointing with oil as an immediately
available remedy, easy to obtain. For this reason the Lord Christ had added this
Symbol to the gift of healing, so that the Apostles should anoint the sick in
his name and they should be healed, which was performed by them in Mark 6. Hence
the practice became established so that later people used anointing more freely
than other remedies, and for a long time this was usefully effective. But when
the gift of healing ceased in the Church, the Symbol also began to be employed
in vain as a Sacrament. For what are Symbols without the things themselves?
Today, therefore, let the same order be maintained: let the Elders gather with
the sick person, let them pray at the beginning, and then let them apply some
healthful remedy. And let this be done in the Lord’s name. In this way, I think,
this precept of the Apostle James will be satisfied.” See also *Pareus* on the
passage. He indeed maintains that the oil was not a medicinal alexipharmic or
natural ἀντίδοτον, “antidote,” to disease, but an external Symbol freely added
to miraculous spiritual healing, just as we read that Christ and the Apostles,
when curing diseases, sometimes employed spittle or clay, sometimes touch or
the laying on of hands, sometimes handkerchiefs, and sometimes a shadow. He says
that this oil and rite of miraculous healing endured in the Church as long as
the gifts of miracles also flourished in the Church. He further says that
*Chrysostom*, homily 4 on *Matthew*, and *Augustine*, *On True Religion*, chapter
25; *To Honoratus, On Miracles*, chapter 6; and *City of God*, book 22, chapter
8, testify that miracles had ceased before their times. Therefore, he says, the
Symbol of miraculous healing among the sick also ceased at that time. See also
all our writers disputing against the Papists concerning this imaginary
Sacrament.

Enough concerning this mark. A *Third* follows: *A Law prescribing what is
conformed to the imitable attributes and works of God is moral.* For it is
always equitable and just that human beings should imitate God insofar as he
proposes himself for imitation. Hence by his own example God urges us to
exercise mercy toward the wretched, to forgive enemies, to bear evils patiently,
and similar things. The human being was created in God’s image and thus was
created to imitate God insofar as it is lawful. I concede that we cannot

[1674 logical printed p. 150 | IA leaf 168 | PDF p. 168]

imitate, and indeed ought not to imitate, all God’s attributes or all his works.
For we can neither imitate his omnipotence nor ought we to imitate it or produce
the effects of omnipotence. But I say this: when and insofar as those things are
proposed to us for imitation, the things thus commanded are moral. I also
concede that sometimes the attribute itself and its effect are proposed so that
we may imitate him according to our capacity and measure; at other times neither
the attribute nor its effect, but rather the manner observed in producing that
effect, is proposed for imitation. For example, in that miraculous destruction
of Sodom we can imitate neither God’s omnipotence nor that effect; yet we can and
ought to imitate the justice and wisdom which shone forth in that act. Hence it
follows that it is moral for every judge, before passing sentence and executing
it, first to investigate and properly examine everything, as we read was done
here by God himself. Nevertheless, that infinite disproportion which exists
between the infinite God and finite creatures must always be observed and
admitted here.

This is the Fourth: *That Law which was engraved upon Adam’s heart in the state
of integrity is moral.* For that law was common to the whole human race, since
*Adam* was a public person. That law was also equally suited and agreeable to
all human beings. Yet through the remnants of primitive perfection and integrity
which still remain in us, we cannot adequately perceive what was originally
engraved upon human nature unless we are aided by the Scriptures. Therefore,
whatever we can understand through the light of Scripture to have been written
upon the human mind, we call moral. I believe the adversaries themselves will
not deny this.

Let this be the Fifth rule: *That law which in itself and with respect to its
substance is useful to everyone in every age is moral, unless it has certainly
and plainly been abrogated by God.* By a useful law we understand one which
prescribes things that certainly and by their own nature tend to promote God’s
glory and the spiritual good of souls, as also to promote the common good of
societies and the personal good of private persons, whether it concerns the body
or one’s external condition. We also understand by it a law which determines
things which must indeed be determined, but which the now blind and corrupted
wisdom of human beings cannot determine with sufficient success and suitability;
or, if it does determine them, cannot so satisfy consciences that the human mind
rests in that determination without any doubt, scruple, or controversy, nor can
it make a willing person secure in putting it into practice. When I say that
this law is useful *in itself and with respect to its substance*, I sufficiently
indicate that it does not derive all this usefulness from mere legislation.
Accordingly, I exclude from this number every ceremonial law which, although by
divine ordinance it was useful for as long as it remained in force for the
typological ends for which it was appointed, was nevertheless not useful for
those ends when considered in itself or substantially, but purely on account of
God’s good pleasure which constituted it. I also sufficiently indicate that a
distinction must be made between a law’s substance and some circumstance
attached to it from its first origin. Finally, when I add, “unless it has
certainly and plainly been abrogated,” it is evident that God’s prerogative of
rescinding his laws as he wills is always reserved. But unless that abrogation
is plain and certain, we do not say that such a substantially useful law should
be rashly rejected.

[1674 logical printed p. 151 | IA leaf 169 | PDF p. 169]

By this rule it is evident that every law which is truly a burden, whose
observance would bring inconvenience and is a certain prejudice to a rational
human being, must be erased from the list of moral laws. But no law which tends
to promote God’s glory and human salvation can be called inconvenient or
prejudicial to a person, even though by observing it we expose ourselves to some
temporal inconvenience. For God’s glory and our eternal salvation are to be
preferred far above our earthly good, even the greatest. Therefore the greatest
external loss, even if most certain, cannot remove the substantial goodness and
usefulness of a law by which our spiritual good and God’s glory can be promoted.
Similarly, just as the public good of human society must be preferred far above
our private good, a substantially useful and therefore moral law can oppose our
private advantage in some way, provided it promotes the public benefit of human
society. For such a law may be inconvenient and harmful to someone, but only
accidentally, not in itself; otherwise it would be no less harmful to the whole
society than to any individual.

The equity of this rule is evident from the fact that, if the force of a command
were removed and it were regarded as nonbinding on account of some inconvenience
or loss arising from obedience rendered to it, then in a short time even the
most moral precepts—that is, natural and supremely necessary precepts—would be
rendered empty. Who but a profane person would say that divine precepts are to
be violated on account of persecution or any danger? *Daniel* and others had
been instructed very differently: notwithstanding the most bitter persecutions
and most imminent dangers, they considered it better to obey God and faithfully
observe his precepts than to set them aside and consult their own safety.

Moreover, what wise and upright legislator would wish his salutary and useful
laws to be changed as long as no loss appears which necessarily follows from
rendering obedience to them? How, therefore, will we say that God did not wish
those laws to bind perpetually which are no less substantially useful to us
than they were formerly to God’s people, unless it is openly evident that such
precepts have been abolished? We indeed know, because this is clearly indicated
to us and demonstrated in the New Testament, that those laws have been abrogated
which were useful by reason of divine institution but not substantially or in
themselves, such as the laws concerning circumcision, sacrifices, and not a few
similar things. But we do not read this concerning other laws useful in
themselves or substantially; and unless it appears at least equally clearly and
plainly that these too have been abrogated, all reason demands that we regard
them not as abrogated but as binding perpetually.

Finally, it must be noted that, even when it seemed good to God to change and
abrogate the particular and individual object of a precept, he did not wish to
abrogate the substance of that precept, or the precept itself with respect to
its substantial elements. Rather, when the particular object and other
circumstances had changed, he willed that the law, with respect to its substance,
as always useful and equally necessary, should bind perpetually. This is evident
in those precepts which concern ministers set over the flock for instructing
the people and leading them in truth. For although under the New Testament this
order or office is now not assigned to the firstborn, fathers of households, or
any family in particular, as it was to the Levites and Aaron’s descendants, the
same precepts remain firm with respect to their substance and bind the ministers
now set over the people no less than they formerly

[1674 logical printed p. 152 | IA leaf 170 | PDF p. 170]

bound the Levites, Priests, and others. The same is evident in precepts
concerning the Sacraments, which are seals of the covenant. With respect to
their general features, which are equally useful under every divine
dispensation, they bind no less now than formerly, although the old Sacraments
have been abolished and the individual object thus changed by the substitution
of new ones.

This is the Sixth rule: *All the precepts contained in the Decalogue are moral;*
or, All those ten precepts are moral. Some adversaries do not deny but freely
concede that nine of these ten are moral; they except only the fourth and do not
wish it to be regarded as moral. Others concede that all are moral but interpret
the fourth so mystically, spiritually, and generally that they concede nothing
which pertains to this controversy concerning the Sabbath. Indeed, they might
just as well concede that every ceremonial law is moral as concede that the
precept concerning the Sabbath is moral if it is now to be called moral only
with respect to general equity and not with respect to its literal and
grammatical meaning. Thus they actually assert that this precept has been
abrogated and is not moral. In any case, when they concede that even this
precept is moral, they will not deny that this rule is firm.

Since later, God willing, we will treat the Decalogue more fully and address all
the controversies concerning it between us and the anti-Sabbatarians, it will
suffice to confirm the proposed rule briefly.

First, presenting the sum of the entire Decalogue, our Lord reduces all the
precepts to two heads in Matthew 22:37–40, saying, “You shall love the Lord your
God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your thought. This is
the first and great precept. The second is similar to it: you shall love your
neighbor as yourself. On these two precepts hang the whole law and the
Prophets.” No sane person will deny that these two heads are moral. Therefore,
if the sources and principles are moral, why not the streams flowing immediately
from them?

Next, this Decalogical law was honored by peculiar privileges from God and was
therefore preferred above all other laws. For in the greatest assembly of the
people—a multitude of six hundred thousand men and still more women and
children, besides the mixed multitude of foreigners, Exodus 12:38 and Numbers
11:4, prepared for several days to hear this law, Exodus 19:10–11—God himself
descended before the eyes of the whole people upon Mount *Sinai* in fire, verse
18, with sounds issuing, lightning flashes and dense clouds appearing over the
mountain, and a trumpet producing an exceedingly violent sound, so that all the
people in the camp trembled, verse 16; while the whole mountain itself smoked
because *Jehovah* had descended upon it in the fire, emitting its smoke like
the smoke of a furnace, and the entire mountain shook greatly, verse 18. He
proclaimed this law and no other, Deuteronomy 5:22, from the midst of the fire,
cloud, and darkness with a great voice. Thus, when the people heard that voice
from the midst of that darkness and the mountain burning with fire, they said,
“Why should we die? For this great fire would consume us,” Deuteronomy 5:22–23,
25 and Exodus 19:19. When they had approached the mountain and the burning fire,
the whirlwind and darkness and storm, the sound of the trumpet and the voice of
words, they asked with an excuse that the message should no longer be spoken to
them, Hebrews 12:18–19. Indeed, the sight which appeared was so terrifying that
*Moses* himself said, “I am terrified and trembling,” Hebrews 12:21. Then God
himself twice engraved upon stone tablets with his own finger this law
promulgated in this manner, Deuteronomy 5:22 and 9:10;

[1674 logical printed p. 153 | IA leaf 171 | PDF p. 171]

Exodus 31:18 and 32:19, compared with 34:1, 4, 28; Deuteronomy 10:1–2, 4. He
caused these tablets to be placed in the ark made from the choicest cedar wood,
Deuteronomy 10:1, 5. When all these things have been properly considered, it is
manifest that these ten words—for so they are called in Exodus 34:28—differ
greatly from all other laws and possess above them this prerogative: that they
bind perpetually. For to what end would God give and promulgate, with such
solemnity, majesty, glory, splendor, terror, and shaking of creatures, laws
which were to endure for only a time? We know what is usually objected here, but
the whole matter will later be examined in its proper place, book 4.

Third, Christ himself confirmed and ratified this law as one that would endure
perpetually, Matthew 5:17–20, as we will demonstrate more fully later in its
proper place; and what is objected by the adversaries will be called to
examination in book 4.

Fourth, the same thing is also proved from James 2:10–11: “For whoever has kept
the whole law but stumbled in one point has become guilty of all. For he who
said, ‘Do not commit adultery,’ also said, ‘Do not kill,’” and so forth. From
the examples given it is sufficiently evident that he speaks of the Decalogue.
And he asserts that the one who had proclaimed one command of the Decalogue had
also proclaimed the other, or all of them, in the same solemn manner indicated
above. Thus he intends that all the precepts of the Decalogue bind equally and
perpetually. No one, however, will say that this also holds for ceremonial
commands, though they too were given by God but in a different manner—namely,
that one who stumbled in one, for example by eating swine’s flesh, became guilty
of them all.

Fifth, this can be shown by many testimonies of Theologians. “The Decalogue,”
*Polanus* says in *Syntagma of Theology*, book 6, chapter 10, “is the brief sum
of the moral law, containing ten precepts,” and so forth. “The moral law,” by
which the Decalogue is understood, “is so called,” *Scharpius* says in his
*Course of Theology*, on God’s law, “because it is the rule of life and conduct,
not only external but especially internal conduct and affections. And it is a
constant and perpetual rule of justice, framed concerning the duties of
individuals both toward God and toward their neighbors. The moral law is not
different in substance from the law of nature but is a repetition and fuller
explanation of the law of nature.” *Wendelin*, in *System of Christian Theology*,
book 2, chapter 4, thesis 5, makes the Decalogue and the moral law one and the
same, forcefully saying: “God commands virtues and prohibits sins in his moral
law, that is, the Decalogue, which he delivered to Moses written on two tablets
and commanded to be promulgated to all the Israelite people.” *Alting*,
*Commonplaces*, part 1, page 102, confirms the same κατ’ ἰδίαν, “individually,”
saying: “The moral law is proposed in the Decalogue, whose precepts God himself
spoke and engraved with his finger on stone tablets,” Exodus 20:1 and following;
31:18; 34:1, 4, 28, “so that a few principles of various conclusions might stand
forth, adapted to the weakness of human capacity. This very thing is indicated
synecdochically by the designation ‘ten words,’” Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13
and 10:4. “The Decalogue is commonly called the immovable rule of divine
justice, namely because it is lawful for no person to change this rule or depart
from it. Nowhere,” *Martinius* says in his seventh commonplace on the law,
thesis 7, “do we perceive those principles—indeed, the whole natural right—more
constantly and clearly than in the Decalogue or moral law so called in a special
sense. This is well defined as: A perpetual rule of living, shadowing forth the
eternal wisdom and justice of God, first implanted in human beings at creation
and solemnly repeated on Mount Sinai by the ministry of angels, comprehended in
two tablets and in all the precepts pertaining to them, and so forth.” “The sum,”
*Tilenus* says in his *Syntagma*, part 1, disputation 32, thesis 28, “or epitome
of the moral law is the Decalogue, whose precepts God himself

[1674 logical printed p. 154 | IA leaf 172 | PDF p. 172]

spoke ἐμφώνως, ‘aloud,’ and engraved with his finger on stone tablets.” It
would be easy to accumulate more testimonies of Theologians.

Sixth, the Church of Christ agrees, calling this law the *Decalogue* because it
contains ten precepts according to God’s own declaration in Exodus 34:28 and
Deuteronomy 4:13 and 10:4. It includes them all καθ’ ἕν, “one by one,” in
its Catechisms so that children may learn them thoroughly—except that the Roman
Church removes the second because it too openly contradicts its idolatry. The
pastors likewise interpret them all in their catechetical exercises. Indeed, in
some Reformed Churches this entire law is read aloud every Lord’s Day and is
inscribed upon tablets affixed in the most prominent place in the church, so
that everyone may know that they regard all the precepts of that law as equally
moral and perpetually binding. Otherwise it would be charged against them that,
by thus teaching this entire law to children, explaining it, reading it aloud,
exposing it to everyone to read, and as it were engraving it on columns for the
perpetual memory of the matter, they would be a great scandal to younger and
less instructed people. For these people, perceiving no distinction, would
treat them all with equal honor and judge them worthy of equal reverence and
observance. For if any one of these commands were purely ceremonial, why would
they teach, explain, inculcate, prescribe it for the young to learn, and propose
that it always be read and retained in memory any more than the law concerning
blood and things strangled, circumcision, Passover, and similar things? Would it
not be better, in the Papists’ manner, to erase such a command entirely from
this list, lest the less instructed, who are sufficiently prone to superstition,
be further corrupted in this way? Indeed, do not all those Churches in this way
publicly teach falsehoods and defend an inadmissible error if they propose as a
command of God something which has already been abrogated and which for these
whole 1,600 years and more ought not to have been regarded as God’s binding law?
What else is this than to take God’s name in vain, proposing as God’s command
something which is not his command? We will say more about this matter in the
book already mentioned, book 4.

This is the Seventh rule: *That precept is moral which was formerly given and
is now confirmed under the Gospel, or which is declared to be of such a kind
that it still binds and will bind to the end.* In the preceding chapter I indeed
demonstrated that a command once prescribed to the Church can be called moral
even though it is not specifically confirmed and ratified under the New
Testament. The reason is plain: a law once given and not abrogated forms part of
our rule according to which we ought to order our conduct, and it still binds
on account of the Legislator’s authority. Just as the natural law would have
bound the Jews even if it had not been repeated on Mount *Sinai*, so a law once
given by God binds the Church under the New Testament, even if it has not been
renewed or repeated under the New Testament. Much more, therefore, must that
law be said still to bind which was both given formerly and is repeated clearly
enough under the New Testament, or declared to be of such a kind that it binds
perpetually. In this same chapter I also demonstrated that a precept is moral
which was once given, is never plainly abrogated, and does not of its own nature
pass away or become obsolete. Much more, therefore, must that precept be called
moral which not only is not abrogated and does not of its own accord become
obsolete by reason of its very nature, but on the contrary is declared to be of
such a kind that it binds perpetually.

I scarcely believe that the adversaries will contradict this rule, since it is
sufficiently evident that such a precept was not given to some peculiar people
so as to bind them alone, nor was it given for only a certain time so that,
when that time had ended, it would finally lose all

[1674 logical printed p. 155 | IA leaf 173 | PDF p. 173]

force of binding. Rather, it is a precept common to all, necessary and useful
at every time, and therefore moral.

It is not to be believed that God wished to continue any law in force and bind
even the Church under the New Testament to observe it unless it were useful and
in itself suited to the state of the Church under the New Testament, rather than
adapted only to any single state of the Church and constituted to govern it in
that state or by reason of that economy. Who will deny such a law to be moral,
since by this continuation or confirmation God has declared plainly enough that
it prescribes nothing except what is agreeable and useful to the Church at every
time?

This is the Eighth indication of a moral precept: *A precept which was not given
as a type or merely as an external instruction in a moral duty, but whose
primary, proper, internal, and particular reason, upon which it specifically
rests and by which it is particularly urged, is moral, may be regarded as
moral.*

I know well enough that many ceremonial laws were given to the Israelite people
which were instructions in some moral duty. In this respect the precepts were
symbolic and adapted to that ecclesiastical economy. Such were the ones which
commanded “that they be clean in their clothing, that they wash the body, wipe
away sweat and filth, bury excrement,” and similar things, which as symbolic
precepts taught them, as is thought, to purify their hearts and works and keep
themselves free from corrupt conduct. Such also was the precept concerning
*unleavened* bread, which the Israelites were bound to eat at Passover, and
which, according to Paul, taught something moral when he speaks as follows in 1
Corinthians 5:5–6: “Purge out the old leaven, that you may be a new lump, as you
are unleavened. For our Passover, Christ, has been sacrificed for us. Therefore
let us keep the feast, not with the old leaven of malice and wickedness, but
with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.” Such was the law in
Deuteronomy 25, “You shall not muzzle the mouth of the ox which treads out the
grain,” which indicated something moral, as the Apostle demonstrates in 1
Corinthians 9:9–10 and 1 Timothy 5:17–18. Although these precepts or instructions
do not now bind, the moral duty symbolically indicated through them binds us
too, as the passages just cited from *Paul’s* Epistles to the *Corinthians* and
to *Timothy* make manifest.

There were also not a few other things of this kind, although the duty which
they symbolically taught is not so certain. Such was the precept in Leviticus
19:19, “Do not cover yourself with a garment variously woven from two kinds of
material.” It is uncertain whether by this symbol he wished to signify that
sincerity in worship pleased him, or whether this was prescribed because the
idolatrous priests of that time were accustomed to go about dressed in this
way, or whether both explanations should be given. Similarly, concerning the
distinction between clean and unclean foods, *Origen*, book 4 against *Celsus*,
maintains that all those things were pronounced unclean which the Egyptians and
other nations considered to possess a power of divination, while all those
outside that class were clean. A certain *Barnabas*, in the Epistle praised by
*Clement of Alexandria*, book 5, laboriously attempts to demonstrate the moral
duties which symbolic precepts of this kind taught. *Heidegger* transcribes his
words as follows in *On the Dietary Law*, pages 83–84:

“What Moses said—‘Do not eat the pig, eagle, sharp-winged bird, deer, or fish
which lacks scales’—comprehends three teachings in a secret meaning. He himself
expressly shows in Deuteronomy where this tends: ‘And my righteous ordinances
will be attested to my people.’ Therefore the proper precept is not to avoid
eating this or that thing; rather, Moses said these things in the Spirit.

[1674 logical printed p. 156 | IA leaf 174 | PDF p. 174]

Concerning the pig, this is what he intended: do not cleave to those people who
are like pigs. For when they are overflowing with luxury, they forget their
Lord; but when affairs go against them, they acknowledge the Lord. For when a
pig has eaten, it pays no attention to its master. When it is hungry, it cries
out, and when it has again received something to eat, it becomes silent. ‘Do not
eat,’ he says, ‘the eagle, the sharp-winged bird, the kite, or the raven.’ By
this he says: do not cleave to people of this kind who do not know how to seek
their living by labor and sweat but unjustly seize the property of others by
their deeds and lay traps while assuming the appearance of people walking in
good faith. So too those hateful animals, while sitting idle, watch how they may
feed upon the flesh of others, harmful by their evil disposition. ‘Do not eat
the moray eel either,’ he says, ‘nor the octopus, nor the cuttlefish.’ By this
he says: do not by attaching yourself become like such people, who are
perpetually ungodly and are kept for death. For among fish only those which are
condemned swim on the bottom and do not float at the surface like the others,
but live on the lowest ground. ‘Nor shall you eat the hare,’ he says. Why? That
is, do not become a corrupter of boys or like such people. For the hare grows
more prolific in its abdominal outlets each year: as many years as it has, so
many openings it has. ‘Nor,’ he says, ‘eat the hyena’; that is, do not commit
adultery or become like adulterers. How does this follow? This animal changes
its nature yearly and is now male, now female. He also rightly hated the weasel.
Namely, he warns this: do not become like those whom we hear committing infamous
acts with the mouth, nor join yourself to women who accommodate themselves in
that manner. For that animal conceives through the mouth.”

But we except these and similar things from this rule, partly because the moral
reasons of at least some of these laws are uncertain and merely devised by human
beings; partly because, beyond this reason, whether manifest or hidden, they had
a typological reason attached; and finally because all these things were
instituted as external instructions and were thus particularly adapted to that
economy. They all rested on this principal reason: that the Church at that time
was in infancy under Tutors and Governors, under the elements of the world, and
reduced to servitude.

Hence it is evident that the precept concerning *abstinence from blood* is not
to be referred to moral laws, even though a moral reason is attached to the
prohibition in Genesis 9:4–5—namely, that they should not desire to shed human
blood. For this was not the principal reason, nor is this reason clearly enough
established from the text, although it seems probable to some, as noted above.
Rather, the principal reason was another which was typological—namely, that it
typologically prefigured or foreshadowed Christ’s blood, through which alone
reconciliation was made, Leviticus 17:11. Therefore, since this reason was not
the primary, proper, internal, and special reason of this precept, it is evident
that this law does not belong to our rule.

Further, I do not deny that God sometimes urges ceremonial laws by moral
reasons, as may be seen in Leviticus 11:44. But neither these nor similar
instances undermine the indication given, because that reason is only a general
one derived from the Legislator or his authority, which has the same place in
all precepts, and is not special and proper. But we speak of those laws whose
proper, internal, and particular reason upon which they rest is moral.

That every such law should be regarded as moral is evident from the fact that
the law’s character and nature can readily be perceived from this internal and
proper reason. And if that reason is moral, the law itself which rests upon this
reason must also be moral. Next, this

[1674 logical printed p. 157 | IA leaf 175 | PDF p. 175]

moral reason binds us equally with others, for it is neither peculiar nor
proper to any nation or dispensation but is common to all. Therefore the law
must also be common, since it rests on this common reason as its foundation.
Hence Theologians commonly say that Judicial laws whose reason is common and
moral also bind us. Hence it is also commonly said that no law binds contrary
to its own reason, for the reason of a law is the soul of the law. And the
reason of right, or the Legislator’s intention, *Cicero* says in *For Aulus
Caecina*, is the best interpreter of the law.

Nevertheless, it must be noted that we do not judge it sufficient for that
proper and peculiar reason to be merely devised by human beings. For it does
not appear sufficiently safe to pronounce such a command moral. It is much
safer if that reason is plainly proposed by God himself and exhibited as the
foundation of that precept, or if it is manifest in itself.

This can be the Ninth indication: *That Law is moral which, through creation,
the order of creation, the mutual relation of created things to one another,
their harmony, and other things which God established for imitation, is
instrumentally exhibited and proposed so that human beings may be admonished
concerning their duty both toward God and toward one another and bound to
perform it—even though in our corrupted state an explicit and distinct
knowledge or notion of this law does not now appear.*

For this to be better understood, it should be known, *first*, that God does
nothing without the highest and best reason. Since no sane person can deny that
he could have created everything in another manner or by another method than he
actually did, we must confess that God created everything for some weighty
accompanying reason, just as we read in the sacred text. It is certain that he
was driven by no necessity to observe this method rather than another. This
method was therefore observed for the sake of human beings, so that they might
be instructed concerning their duty.

*Second*, it should be observed that God sometimes admonishes and instructs his
people concerning their duty by the things which he does not do, as appears in
Deuteronomy 4:15–16: “Therefore take great care for yourselves, for you saw no
likeness on the day when Jehovah spoke to you in Horeb from the midst of that
fire, lest you corrupt yourselves and make for yourselves a carved image, the
likeness of any representation, the form of male or female,” and so forth.
Therefore, by showing the people no likeness on the day he spoke to them, he
instructed them not to form representations or carved images.

*Third*, from this it is evident that God much more speaks to rational creatures
through his works and indicates what they ought to believe and do. *Paul* also
teaches this in Romans 1:19–21: “Because what can be known concerning God is
manifest in them, for God has manifested it to them. For ever since the creation
of the world, his invisible things are clearly perceived, being comprehended by
the mind from the things which he made—namely, both his eternal power and
divinity—so that they are without excuse. Because when they knew God, they did
not glorify him as God or give thanks,” and so forth. Therefore, from the things
God had made the Gentiles could know many things concerning God, including his
invisible qualities, eternity and power, as also his goodness and similar
things, which would move them to obedience and thanksgiving and cause them to
abhor utterly that brutish idolatry to which they gave themselves when they
changed the glory of the incorruptible God into a formed image of corruptible
human beings, birds, four-footed animals, and reptiles.

*Fourth*, God not only teaches and instructs rational creatures through these
works of creation,

[1674 logical printed p. 158 | IA leaf 176 | PDF p. 176]

but also through these things and the matters which come to be considered about
their creation, as through so many signs of his will, he establishes the duty
owed, or prescribes what they must do or avoid. The Apostle teaches us this in
1 Timothy 2:12–13: “For I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise
authority over a man, but command that she remain silent. For Adam was formed
first, then Eve.” From the very order of creation the Apostle here infers the
moral superiority and preeminence of the man and the due subjection of the
wife. This duty, therefore, is moral and rests upon this natural foundation in
creation, even though very many now know nothing at all concerning this
foundation, being ignorant of how the man or woman was created. The same is
evident in the institution of the first marriage between one man and one woman,
which was founded upon the creation of one man and one woman. From this
foundation polygamy, intercourse between males, adultery, and fornication are
unlawful, and so forth. By these acts this firm rule of creation is violated,
to which Christ appeals in condemning rash divorces. We touched upon this matter
above, especially in chapter 6, pages 82–84, and will touch upon it several
times later.

The Tenth indication of the moral law: *That Law is moral which commands
something having a firm principle in right reason, or in the dictate of right
reason, even though that principle is more remote, from which the command can
be drawn or inferred, provided that principle is properly cultivated and
examined.* No one will affirm that only the first principles obvious to everyone
are moral. Rather, everyone affirms that conclusions properly deduced and
necessarily flowing from them are also moral, although they are not equally
easy for everyone to perceive. Nor is it any objection that with respect to
certain more remote conclusions, divine illumination or divine aid through the
light of the Word is necessary. For the conclusion can have a firm connection
with principles of this kind, even though because of our natural blindness this
connection escapes the eyes of our mind. Indeed, even if some free and
particular determination is necessarily required here so that the practical
conclusion can be more readily inferred, this determination by no means weakens
the foundation. While the natural and moral foundation remains, the conclusion
deduced from it, though by means of a particular determination, is moral; and
the duty itself which rests upon that moral foundation is moral, especially if
that determination is made known to nature and the law itself, so particularized,
if I may speak this way, is proposed to be read in the very nature and order of
created things.

This can be the Eleventh indication of the Moral Law: *That Law which binds
perpetually is Moral.* The Adversaries themselves will not call this mark of the
moral law into question. For they deny that the precept of the weekly Sabbath
is moral because it does not bind perpetually, or they deny that the fourth
precept concerning the weekly Sabbath binds us now because, namely, it is not
moral. Further, all reason urges us to regard as moral that precept which binds
the Church in every state and under every economy. For such a precept has no
relation to some particular state or economy of the Church, which must
necessarily be said concerning ceremonial precepts. Thus those precepts which
bind perpetually to the end of the world were not given merely for some period
of time. Therefore there is no need for us to say more about this matter,
since, as stated above, the adversaries themselves do not deny that perpetuity
of obligation is a necessary consequence of the morality of a precept.

[1674 logical printed p. 159 | IA leaf 177 | PDF p. 177 | ends before CAP. XIII]

Let this be the Twelfth mark of the moral law: *That Law which binds all human
beings is Moral.* The adversaries will not deny this mark either. For because
they think that the Gentiles were not bound by the precept concerning the
Sabbath, they conclude that this precept was not Moral but was given particularly
to the Jews. Therefore a precept which was not given particularly to the Jews
must be moral, because it was given to form the conduct of everyone. Thus it
differs from purely political laws which were given to the Jews alone as such a
Commonwealth.

Later, in book 3, chapter 23, we will make manifest how these indications of the
moral law serve our purpose, and especially how from them it can be demonstrated
that the precept concerning the weekly Sabbath is moral.
