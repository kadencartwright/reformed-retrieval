QUESTION XX.

THE NINTH COMMANDMENT.

Can a lie be justified and rendered lawful under any pretext? Negative,
against the Socinians.

I. Just as one’s neighbor ought not to be injured by *deed*, neither ought he
to be injured by *word*. The ninth commandment, *against bearing false
testimony against one’s neighbor*, forbids this injury. Testimony against
one’s neighbor is not forbidden without qualification, since judicial
proceedings often demand it and zeal for justice binds us to it. Only *false
testimony* is forbidden, by which the life, honor, or

[Printed p. 114 | Internet Archive PDF 142 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

goods of one’s neighbor are injured in any way, together with whatever is
contrary to the truthfulness and sincerity that ought to prevail among human
beings. This is customarily expressed by the term *lie*.

[Marginal heading: What a lie is.]

II. Augustine, in *On Lying to Consentius*, chapter 4, defines a lie as “the
voluntary assertion of a falsehood for the purpose of deceiving.” Others
define it more precisely as “a testimony by which someone declares one thing
while feeling another in his heart.” Although the intention to deceive
constitutes a more complete species of lie, it is not always required in a
lie, provided that the will to assert something false is present. Thomas
Aquinas explains this well (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, question 110, article
1): “The character of a lie is derived from formal falsity, namely, from the
fact that someone has the will to assert something false.” He adds shortly
afterward: “That someone intends to establish a false belief in another’s
opinion by deceiving him does not belong to the species of a lie, but to a
certain perfection of it.”

Thus two things are always required for a lie.

1. The assertion must be false and contrary to the truth of the matter.

2. The will to assert something false must be present, so that the speech is
contrary to the thought. Hence some derive *mentiri*, *to lie*, as though it
meant *contra mentem ire*, *to go against the mind*. It is possible for
someone to say something false without lying, because through ignorance he
believes it to be true and his speech does not disagree with his mind.

[Marginal heading: The threefold lie.]

III. The *lie* forbidden by the ninth commandment is commonly distinguished
according to its object as follows: a *harmful lie*, which is uttered with the
intention of harming and tends toward the ruin of one’s neighbor; a *jocular
lie*, which is spoken in jest for the sake of amusement; and a *helpful lie*,
which tends toward the advantage of one’s neighbor and is uttered for the
sake of helping him.

[Marginal heading: Whether any lie is lawful.]

Everyone agrees that the first is a grave sin. A dispute arises with the
Socinians, however, about the latter two and especially the last. They contend
that these are either slight sins or no sins at all. Some of the more lax
casuists join them and free these lies from the character of sin. Although we
recognize a distinction among these lies, so that the harmful lie is more
serious and the other two less serious, we nevertheless judge them to be true
sins condemned by the law of God.

[Marginal heading: 1. Scripture condemns every lie.]

IV. First, a lie is universally condemned in Scripture without any exception
as a sin abominable to God (Ps. 5:6; Prov. 6:17, 19; 12:22; Eph. 4:25; Col.
3:9). It proceeds from the instigation of the devil, the father of the lie
(John 8:44), and it injures the majesty of God. God is the author of truth and
such a lover of it that he cannot lie, has never by any dispensation granted
permission to lie, has expressly forbidden it, and has decreed that it be
severely punished (Prov. 19:5, 9; 21:28).

[Marginal heading: 2. Because it is evil in itself.]

V. Second, what is evil in itself within its own kind can in no way become
good and lawful. Every lie is evil in itself and falls upon improper matter.
It is a disorder both in the liar himself, because contrary to the order of
nature his speech, which is the interpreter of his mind, plainly disagrees
with his mind, and toward his neighbor, because by an obligation of natural
and divine law each person owes his neighbor the observance of truthfulness.

Hence Thomas Aquinas says (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, question 110, article 3),
“Words are naturally signs of intellectual conceptions. It is therefore
unnatural and improper for someone to signify by speech what he does not have
in his mind.” Similarly, Durandus says (book 3, distinction 38, question 1),
“Words were instituted not so that human beings might deceive one another by
them, but so that they might make their thoughts known to others. It is
therefore an improper act when someone uses words to signify what he does not
hold in his mind.” Hence the Philosopher concludes in *Ethics* 4.7 that lying
is evil in itself and to be avoided. No lie, therefore, can be so

[Printed p. 115 | Internet Archive PDF 143 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

helpful that it is not unhelpful to the liar, since it possesses an intrinsic
formal ἀταξίαν, *disorder*, and a deformity alien to nature and the law of
God. What is evil in itself and intrinsically cannot be rendered good by
consideration of even the best end. Good things must be pursued well, and a
good end must be sought by good means; no external circumstances can change a
thing evil in itself.

VI. Third, if only a harmful lie were a sin, its ἀνομία, *lawlessness*, would
be derived only from its end. This is false, because in the deformity and
ἀταξίᾳ, *disorder*, of the lie itself—namely, in the separation of the mind
and tongue, which ought to be the interpreter of the mind—consists its
ἀνομία, *lawlessness*.

Fourth, if a lie could be justified by its end, namely by the intention of
helping one’s neighbor, then thefts intended to help could likewise be
justified whenever someone wished to benefit his neighbor through theft. No
one would say this, because the apostle’s rule must hold here: “Evils must not
be done so that goods may come” (Rom. 3:8).

VII. Fifth, Augustine repeatedly insists upon this. He says in *Enchiridion*,
chapter 18: “Every lie is a sin, although one who lies with the will to help
does not sin as much as one who lies with the will to harm.” He repeats this
in chapter 22. Likewise he says in *Against Lying*, chapter 15: “To say that
certain lies are just is to say that certain sins are just and, through this,
that certain things which are unjust are just.” He treats this more fully in
the two books *On Lying* and *Against Lying*.

Gregory says in *Moralia* 18.4: “Every lie is iniquity, because whatever
disagrees with truth departs from equity. Nor should anyone’s life be defended
through the deceit of lying, lest one who strives to preserve life for
another’s flesh harm his own soul.”

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VIII. Although God blessed the Egyptian midwives, who lied to Pharaoh (Exod.
1), he did not thereby reward their lie, but their piety and mercy toward the
infants, because they preferred to expose themselves to the king’s
indignation rather than obey his cruel command. As Augustine says in *On
Lying*, chapter 15: “Their deceit was not rewarded, but their benevolence; the
kindness of their mind, not the iniquity of their lie.”

Two acts must be distinguished here. One was their refusal to obey the king’s
tyrannical edict, which proceeded from fear of God and is commended. The
other was the lie by which they excused themselves and spoke falsely. It
proceeded more from faintheartedness and servile fear of the king than from
fear of God, and it was sinful. God therefore, in his kindness, wished to
reward the good act while forgiving the sinful act, in order to show how
pleasing their piety and mercy were to him, although they were not without
their blemishes.

It can also be said with some that these midwives did not lie, because what
they said could have been true: that the Hebrew women were lively and
vigorous and gave birth before the midwives came to them.

IX. Whatever we read that holy persons did must not immediately be taken for
imitation as though it were lawful. Although they were holy, it is certain
that they did not live without sin. Hence neither Jacob’s lie nor the lie of
Rahab the prostitute can render a lie lawful. Nor, if God blessed Jacob when
he lied (Gen. 27:20), does it follow that his lie was approved. The blessing
followed the faith he exercised toward the Word of God, which declared that
the elder would serve the younger (Gen. 25:23); it was not given because of
the fault and blemish adhering to his faith.

Nor does the apostle in Hebrews 11:31 attribute Rahab’s lie to faith, but the
beneficent and honorable act by which she received and preserved the spies,
although she used a vicious means to preserve them. The benefit she obtained
from God, therefore, was not the reward of her lie, but of her love toward
God’s people and her fidelity toward the men she had received as guests.

Thus in similar deeds one must always distinguish *the virtue of the action
from the fault and blemish*

[Printed p. 116 | Internet Archive PDF 144 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*adhering to it, and from the manner in which it is done*; one must also
distinguish human infirmity from the goodness of God. Human infirmity always
mingles something vicious even into the best actions, while God’s goodness
graciously recompenses the good by forgiving the vice. Thus it pleased God
that the son of Moses was circumcised, although he did not approve the
impulsive manner of acting which Zipporah displayed in it (Exod. 4:25).
Likewise it pleased God that the midwives abstained from infanticide, that
Rahab looked to the safety of her guests, that Michal looked to the safety of
her husband David (1 Sam. 19:17), and that Jonathan looked to the life of his
friend (1 Sam. 20:6, 28), although the manner of acting in these good works
was not free from fault before God because of the lie.

X. It is no more lawful to bear false witness in favor of one’s neighbor than
against him, just as it is no more lawful to pervert justice in favor of a
widow or orphan than against them (Lev. 19:15). Just as one ought ἀληθεύειν
ἐν ἀγάπῃ, *to speak truth in love*, so we are always bound ἀγαπᾷν ἐν
ἀληθείᾳ, *to love in truth*, even as far as the altars. Indeed, although no
more plausible pretext for a lie can be offered than the glory of God and the
defense of divine truth, God nevertheless rejects this and does not wish it
to be employed in defense of his cause. “Must a lie be uttered by you in
favor of the mighty God? Must deceit be declared in his cause?” (Job 13:7).
God, who is truth itself, ought not to be defended by a lie, nor should
weapons be borrowed for him from the devil.

XI. Although people are sometimes confronted by such difficulties that,
placed “between the sacred knife and the stone”—that is, between a rock and a
hard place—they prefer to sin against truth rather than against charity, in
that case they may be excused from so great a degree of fault, but they cannot
immediately be excused from all of it as though they were free from every
blame.

These words of Augustine in *Enchiridion to Laurentius*, chapter 22, bear on
this point: “A lie must not be thought to be no sin merely because we can
sometimes benefit someone by lying. For we can also benefit someone by
stealing,” and so forth. Therefore, if a lie has benefited some people, this
must not be attributed to the nature of the lie, but to the goodness and
providence of God, who frequently uses the sins of human beings for some good.

XII. Elisha did not lie to the Syrian soldiers (2 Kings 6:19), because he
uttered nothing that was not true with respect to the outcome. When they
asked for the road by which they could find Elisha, he replied that it was
not that road, since he was going to Samaria, nor that city, from which he had
already departed. He promised, however, to lead them to the man they sought.
He fulfilled this in fact by leading them to Samaria, where they recognized
the prophet.

XIII. When Micaiah advises the king to go to war (1 Kings 22:15), he speaks
not so much affirmatively and prophetically as ironically and μιμητικῶς,
*imitatively*. He speaks not from his own judgment but from that of the king
and the false prophets, not in order to deceive them but to teach them that
they were deceived. For afterward, when the king adjured him to declare the
truth in the name of Jehovah (vv. 16–17), he presented something far
different. The king himself sufficiently perceived this either from his
gesture or from his manner of speaking.

Nor can any lie be attributed to Jeremiah in Jeremiah 38:27 for the sake of
the king. What he reported to the princes was entirely true: that the king
had been asked by him not to thrust him back into his former prison in the
house of Jonathan. Jeremiah had in fact requested this from the king, as is
clear from Jeremiah 37:15, 20 and 38:15–16, so that he would not be delivered
into the hands of his enemies. It is one thing to keep silent about an
unnecessary part of the truth, and another to relate a falsehood as truth;
the former is lawful, the latter is not.

XIV. *Simulation* is threefold: 1. that of *fraud and malice*; 2. that of
*caution and ingenuity*; and 3. that of *instruction and investigation*. The
first occurs when virtue is displayed outwardly while vice lies hidden
within. The second is practiced in military stratagems and is approved by
God, as in Joshua 8:5–7. The third is intended not for deceiving but for
investigating and teaching, and it often occurs in human society.

Such was the simulation of the angels, who presented

[Printed p. 117 | Internet Archive PDF 145 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

an intention to spend the night in the square in order to arouse Lot’s
eagerness to receive them
under his roof. Such also was the simulation in Christ (Luke 24:28), who
presented himself as wishing to proceed farther. He did this not to deceive
the disciples, but to test whether they were burning with the desire to
retain him. Moreover, this προσποίησις, *pretense*, is to be understood not
so much *formally* as *materially*, because Christ did what someone feigning
to proceed farther customarily does: he advances some steps beyond the
appointed place in order to draw out their entreaties and kindle their desire
for him more ardently, just as parents about to depart customarily elicit the
entreaties of their children, and friends those of their friends. It can also
be said that Christ had intended to proceed farther and would have done so if
he had not been detained by the disciples’ entreaties.

XV. It is assumed without basis that God suggested a falsehood to Samuel in
1 Samuel 16:2 when he commanded him to indicate to Saul that he had come to
Bethlehem for a sacrifice. Although Samuel would come for another end,
namely to anoint David, that did not prevent him from also going there for
the purpose of celebrating both the sacrifice and the feast. It is one thing
not to disclose every end of an action, and another to put forward a false
end. The Lord suggested the former to Samuel, not the latter; nor are other
ends denied when one end is stated.

XVI. When Paul denies in Acts 23:5 that he knew Ananias was the high priest,
his words can be understood perfectly well without any charge of lying.
Either, as some would have it, he spoke *ironically*, because from Ananias’s
furious manner of speaking Paul saw nothing in him worthy of the high priest,
and Ananias had not conducted himself toward Paul as such; or, as others more
plausibly hold, Paul spoke *seriously*, because Ananias was not truly the high
priest but had thrust himself into the office of the true high priest, either
Ishmael or Joseph. Such was the ἀναρχία, *anarchy*, of that time that there
were, as it were, high priests from day to day—or there were none. Or it may
be because Paul had been away from Jerusalem for a long time and the new high
priest was still unknown to him. Nor could he infer it from the seat, because
they were not in the temple, the high priest’s house, or the regular place of
the Sanhedrin, and perhaps they were seated tumultuously and confusedly.

XVII. Parables are wrongly brought forward in defense of lying, because a
parable is not presented to signify that what is said is so, but to signify
that what is shadowed forth by what is said is so. Hence it is presented not
for the sake of its material element, but for its formal element. As Jerome
aptly says on Ecclesiastes 12, parables “have one thing in the marrow and
promise another on the surface.”

A parable therefore differs from a lie in many ways.

1. With respect to *origin*, every lie comes either from an evil mind or from
a good mind turning aside to evil, while a parable comes from a good mind
persisting in good.

2. With respect to *form*, a lie has an intrinsic disagreement with the mind
of the speaker; a parable does not.

3. With respect to *end*, a lie is designed to deceive, while a parable is
designed to teach.

4. With respect to *effect*, a lie produces a false conception in the hearer’s
mind, while a parable produces a true one.

The same must be said of fables. They present not history but a type; nothing
is represented as a fact, nor is the thing proposed, although false and
fictitious, itself intended, but something wholly different and indeed true.

XVIII. We conclude with Augustine in *On Lying to Consentius*, chapter 21:
“Whoever thinks that there is some kind of lie which is not a sin will
shamefully deceive himself when he supposes himself to be an honorable
deceiver of others.” And in *Against Lying*, chapter 21: “Lies, therefore,
must either be avoided by acting rightly or confessed by repenting; they must
not, when they abound through living unhappily, be increased also by
teaching.”
