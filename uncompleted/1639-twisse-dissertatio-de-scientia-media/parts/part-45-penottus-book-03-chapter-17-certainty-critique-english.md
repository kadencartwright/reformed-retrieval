## Book I — Part 45: Against Penottus, Book III, Chapter XVII, the Certainty Claim

[Book I, printed p. 86; BSB canvas 00112]

What learned person ever maintained that God understands things in one way when they are present
and in another when thus far they were only future? I mean that no one ever said that, on the side
of the knower, God understands things differently before they are present and when they are present,
although he understands them differently on the side of the things known. Before they exist, he
understands that they are future; when they exist, he understands that they are present. Yet he does
so by one and precisely the same intellection, which is also called intuition or vision. For the
knowledge of vision comprehends future and past things just as much as present things. Nor does he
see things, whether future, present, or past, otherwise than in himself, according to Aquinas's most
explicit opinion: “God sees himself in himself because he sees himself through his essence. But he
sees things other than himself not in themselves but in himself, insofar as his essence contains the
likeness of things other than himself,” I, question 14, article 5, in the body. Add from article 11:
“or insofar as it is their active principle.”

And what sober Christian would dare say what Penottus professes here: that whoever attains only the
future being of a thing in its causes cannot have certain knowledge of that thing? From eternity God
knew that creation would occur, and knew it from its causes. Did God therefore not possess certain
knowledge of creation from eternity? Perhaps you will say that, according to your opinion, God knew
creation not only according to its future being but also according to its present being. These are
indeed your turns of phrase. But I ask whether, by this cognition which you attribute to God from
eternity, God knew creation to be present from eternity or merely future. If present from eternity,
then either according to real being or only according to intentional being. If only according to
intentional being, then either only in known being or in willed being, both of which displease you.
If according to real being, then either according to real being in time or according to real being
in eternity. If according to real being in time, you will dare say what not even the Dominicans
dare, although you rebuke them for saying excessively monstrous things in this argument. If
according to real being in eternity, this is the very Dominican opinion which you attack.

Therefore, whoever attains the present being of a contingent thing which it either will possess or
has possessed in time, when it will exist or did exist, has as certain a knowledge of the thing as
one who attains that same present being which it possesses in time as soon as it is outside its
causes. That this is true of the condition of a past thing is established beyond all controversy:
one who knows that a thing is past has as certain a knowledge of it as one who knows that it is
present. Certainly, one who knows a thing to be future does not know it to be present or past; one
who knows a thing to be past does not know that same thing to be present or future; and one who
knows a thing to be present does not know it to be future or past. But let us come to the fitting of
Penottus's reasoning.
