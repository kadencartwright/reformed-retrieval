XXXIV. The *Paradoxical Exercitator* says in § 3 of the same chapter: *[From
these definitions it becomes manifest that perspicuity as well as obscurity
has regard to the understanding of the hearer or reader, and thus that the
entire nature of each consists in a relation to something else.]* And for that
reason he censures the Theologians and charges them with a serious error,
because, when they dispute about the perspicuity and obscurity of Scripture,
they speak not as about relative things but as though about absolute things,
and explain them by a comparison with the sun and its light. What does
*Wolzogen* say to these things? He does indeed show that *Paradox* is not
consistent with himself in this, since in § 26 he says that the ambiguity of
discourse can be considered absolutely, and it is certain that ambiguity
forms a part of obscurity. It is nevertheless surprising what came into
*Wolzogen's* mind, that he should say the same thing as *Paradox* and at least
tacitly lash all the Reformed. For on page 100 he not only says that *Paradox's
censure is an unjustified criticism merely of a word.* But on page 101 he also
has the following: *[Although I judge this fault to be lighter and one of
wording alone—which, if it corresponded most accurately to the nature of
things, would exhibit to us no perspicuity called absolute, since all
perspicuity is without doubt relative.]* And since the account of perspicuity
is the same as that of obscurity, as he himself says near the beginning of the
same page, it follows of its own accord that all obscurity, no less than
perspicuity, is relative. But, to pass over other things in silence, does he
not himself seem to assert the contrary on pages 99 and 100, where he has the
following: *[So that no labor now remains for the interpreter except to direct
his mind keenly toward those particular things which are present in
individual passages and are more remote from common precepts. If these are
not too many, if they are all sufficiently close, and, finally, if each is
distinct in its own place, then no one truly doubts that the discourse is to
be called perspicuous. But if it happens that,

[Page 145 | ONB canvas 199 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000199]

although they are gathered together and there is no need to seek them far
away, and they are not entangled in a confused order, so that they can be
distinguished, they are nevertheless great in number, that first glance must
now be scattered. But if they are indeed few and not involved in any way, yet
are widely separated from one another, because each must be sought at the cost
of more time, they cannot be seen at the first cast of the eyes. It also often
happens that they are found intermingled, although both few in number and
contiguous in position; in that case the mind's acuity must be blunted. But if
they appear both numerous, scattered, and intricate—as experience bears
witness can happen—it is manifest that the discourse is rendered the most
obscure of all.]* Do these words not sufficiently indicate that not all the
obscurity and perspicuity of discourse are relative? Why, then, did he wish,
even by contradicting himself, to assert the same thing as *Paradox*, unless
it was to betray the cause? Does he not himself also say on page 102: *[Because
it nevertheless happens sometimes that the discourse is not at all impeded,
while our minds are darkened; but it often happens that every fault is in the
Discourse and scarcely any in the reader's understanding, it has consequently
come about that the clarity which proceeds from discourse is called absolute,
while that which is for us is regarded as such only with respect to us.]* Who
upon reading these words would not believe that *Wolzogen* had merely
forgotten himself when he had said that *all perspicuity is without doubt
relative?* But one who reads what follows will be compelled to pronounce
otherwise. For as diffusely as the *Exercitator* himself—indeed, at length—he
endeavors on pages 102, 103, 104, 105, and 106 to prove that all this obscurity
and perspicuity is relative. *[For there are*, he says, *in this whole matter
of perspicuity and obscurity three things which must by no means be mixed in
confused thoughts: the thing itself which we call perspicuous; the person who
perceives that thing by the senses or mind; and the presentation of that thing
to the mind of the person who beholds it.---- But what the thing itself,
presented as an object—which in this dispu-*

[Page 146 | ONB canvas 200 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000200]

*tation is discourse—requires in order that it can be perceived is not
difficult to explain. The true meaning, namely, which is judged to agree most
exactly with the author's mind, must be elicited. People, however, are
accustomed to explain their mind by those signs of words which have been
approved by tacit consent and received by usage, and which we then call a
language, which has certain laws and rules of its own.---- If anyone observes
all these laws of speaking or writing, it should be judged that nothing can be
lacking in his discourse for that perspicuity of which we speak. But the
discourse should not for that reason be called perspicuous any more than we
would call the blood flowing in veins red when it is presented to no eyes, or
snow cast into the farthest north cold when it has never struck anyone with
any chill. All these things consist in sensation, and we cannot even conceive
of any real sensation unless there is someone who senses. Thus we say that
all perspicuity consists in perception; I certainly do not understand it
unless I understand at the same time someone who perceives. Nevertheless, all
those things which are said by Theologians about the Scriptures—that they are
perspicuous in themselves in those things which are necessary for salvation—
must be referred to this. For they understand by it that the Scriptures are
so written that they observe the common usage of writing, and thus that
nothing which ought to contribute to producing perspicuity is lacking in
their discourse. Nor do I see here what can be criticized in the meaning. The
word seems less suitable, because they call that perspicuity which possesses
only the first perfection of those things which are required to produce
perspicuity. For in the second place we said that the mind of the knower must
be so constituted that it can know---- yet we do not have perspicuity. Indeed,
I will say that these things which I said about the thing to be known and
about the person who knows contribute nothing to the intimate nature of
perspicuity. They must necessarily be posited so that perspicuity may at last
shine forth, but they do not themselves make perspicuity. The individual or
separated things must be united.---- They are truly united when a discourse
disposed in the manner fitted to produce perspicuity is either perceived or
is understood by the reader to be perci-*

[Page 147 | ONB canvas 201 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000201]

*perceived. Only then should perspicuity be judged complete.]* The Reformed
Theologians have therefore thus far explained themselves badly when they have
said that Scripture is perspicuous in itself, although the unilluminated
person does not see that perspicuity; just as the sun is bright in itself and
remains so, whether a person covers his eyes or closes them, whether he sees
or is blind. And for that reason they are liable to censure no less from
*Wolzogen* and from his elder *Velthuysen*, who says the same thing on page 15
of his book *On the Use of Reason*, than from *Paradox*. Is this the faithful
patron and champion of the truth and of the Orthodox Doctrine asserted and
vindicated by the Reformed? By no means. Indeed, in the *Censure of the
Censure*, page 126, he does not blush to agree here with *Paradox*. For he
says: *[If the Exercitator offers anything truer, anything more precisely
distinguished, than the Orthodox Theologians, I prefer to follow him rather
than them. I therefore side with the Exercitator not only here, where there is
a difference merely of a word, but also in many other places where the matter
itself is in question, because he himself sides with the truth.]* He surely
speaks the truth. For, as will become evident and as we have already partly
made manifest, in many places where the matter itself is in question he
agrees with *Paradox* and abandons the Reformed.

But let *Wolzogen* be heard further. On page 102 he says that three things
must not be mixed in this matter of perspicuity: the thing itself which we
call perspicuous; the person who perceives that thing by the senses or mind;
and the presentation of that thing to the mind of the person who beholds it.
Here we have the whole foundation of *Wolzogen's* error. For the question is
raised only about the perspicuity of the object, but he further requires for
this a suitable disposition of the organ and perception itself. For he speaks
thus on page 103: *[The thing itself must be such that it can be known; the
mind or eye must be so disposed as to be capable of knowing; finally, the
relation requires that the mind, applied to those things which are to be known,
actually perceive.]* We indeed grant that it is required that the object can
be known, or that it is perceptible in itself. But

[Page 148 | ONB canvas 202 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000202]

the other things do not belong to the perspicuity of the object. For the sun
is bright in itself, although the person who looks at it is blind and never
actually perceives its splendor or light. *Wolzogen* himself admits, both on
pages 103–104 and in the *Censure of the Censure*, page 126, that Scripture is
light in itself, or that it possesses everything which ought to contribute to
producing perspicuity. Why, then, does he fault Our men for employing a less
suitable word when they say that it is perspicuous in itself? *[Because*, he
says on page 105, *they call that perspicuity which possesses only the first
perfection of those things which are required to produce perspicuity.]*
Response: On the contrary, it possesses every perfection which is required on
the part of the object for it to be called perspicuous, nor is any other thing
needed for it to be rendered perspicuous, although other things are required
for it actually to be perceived, namely, both in the subject and in the
medium, etc. But here the discussion concerns only the perspicuity of the
object. How far he errs, therefore, when on page 106 he says: *[Only then is a
discourse perspicuous when it is disposed in the manner fitted to produce
perspicuity, and either is perceived or is understood by the reader to be
perceived.]* For then the discourse is not merely fitted to be perceived, but
is actually perceived. And before it is actually perceived, it can be fitted
to be perceived, that is, perspicuous. The sun, as was said, is bright and
fitted to be seen, although it is not actually perceived by the blind. Nor
does he appear Orthodox when on page 105 he requires nothing else in the
subject than that he be acquainted with all those laws by which the entire
structure of discourse is held bound together. For the Spirit is not needed
here, and any *Pelagian, Arminian*, or *Socinian* would say all of this. But
more on this matter elsewhere.
