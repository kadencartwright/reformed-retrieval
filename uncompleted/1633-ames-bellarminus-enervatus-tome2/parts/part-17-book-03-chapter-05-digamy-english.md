ON DIGAMY.

Because of its affinity with the preceding question, another is annexed to it:
Whether it is forbidden by law that one who, after the death of his first wife,
married another should be admitted to holy orders. The whole controversy
concerns those words of Paul in 1 Timothy 3:2 and Titus 1:6: *a bishop and a
deacon must be the husband of one wife.* By these words we understand that
several wives at the same time are prohibited, not wives in succession; the
Pontificians understand wives in succession also.

Bellarmine's arguments.

23. Bellarmine. *First Timothy 5:9, “the wife of one husband,” cannot be
understood as though several husbands at the same time were prohibited,
because it has never been heard that one woman had several husbands at the
same time.*

Protestants. I. Christ sufficiently testifies that this has been heard of and
seen when he said in Matthew 19, *“should marry a divorced woman.”* Tertullian
testifies in his *Apology*, chapter 39: *All things are shared among us except
wives. In this one matter we dissolve fellowship, in which alone other people
practice fellowship: they not only make use of their friends' marriages, but
also most patiently supply their own wives to their friends, following, I
suppose, that discipline of their elders and wisest men, the Greek Socrates and
the Roman Cato, who shared their wives with their friends.* II. Although in
chapter 5, verse 9—where a word in the past tense is used—we were to grant that
succession is taken into account, which is nevertheless false, the reasoning
is nevertheless different

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n324 | printed p. 107]

in chapter 3, verse 2, where it is required in the present time, as Cajetan
observes, that if a man has a wife, he should have only one. In monogamy the
past is no more regarded than it is in *vigilance, temperance, and
hospitality.*

24. Bellarmine. *At the time when Paul wrote this, it was not customary either
among the Jews or among the Gentiles for one man to have two wives at the same
time; there was therefore no reason why it should be prohibited so seriously.*

Protestants. Polygamy in fact was customary even among those who condemned its
open profession. *At that time*, Cajetan says on this passage, *many had several
wives.* Justin Martyr, in his work against Trypho, also charges the Jews with
the profession of polygamy itself: *Our teachers, down to the present time,
permit each man to have four or five wives.*

Our arguments.

25. Protestants. A husband of one wife is opposed to a certain crime, for
*without crime* and *the husband of one wife* are joined as genus and species;
but to have several wives in succession has never been a crime.

Bellarmine. 1. *In this passage we can understand by the name of crime not only
sin, but also whatever is commonly censured by people or bears a bad appearance
as the sign of some vice, just as even successive bigamy is a sign of
incontinence.*

Protestants. I. The name of crime cannot be extended in that way in this
passage, because all the other things individually prohibited there are truly
and properly sins. II. To marry a second wife after the death of the first has,
in the eyes of upright people, no greater appearance of incontinence than to
pass the same span of time with one and the same wife.

Bellarmine. 2. *We can understand a grave sin by the name of crime; but then
there will be a progression in the words from blamelessness to endowments,
monogamy, sobriety, and so forth.*

Protestants. Monogamy is not an endowment, nor is it customarily numbered among
the χαρίσματα. Nor does any reason allow that in Titus 1:6 *not having several
wives* should be an endowment any more than *not having unruly children.*

26. Protestants. No reason can be given why one who married another wife after
the death of the first should on that account be excluded from holy order.

Bellarmine. 1. *One cause is moral, namely, a lesser suspicion of incontinence,
or more perfect continence.*

Protestants. I. A moral perfection is imagined which is not contained in the
whole moral law, not even as it was most perfectly explained by Christ. II. If
the reason were moral, then fornicators and adulterers would certainly be
removed from holy orders much more readily.

Bellarmine. 2. *Adulteries and concubinage are also prohibited, but no more
than digamy. Those things are not prohibited for one reason alone, and that one
not primary; but digamy is prohibited for two reasons. Digamy also by its own
nature is a sign of an incontinence which is more lasting and more firmly
entrenched.*

Protestants. I. Here the whorish disgrace of the Pontificians is acknowledged:
they exclude from their holy offices those whom they do not dare accuse of sin
more than they exclude adulterers and whoremongers. II. One cause founded upon
a flagitious sin ought to carry more weight than two, or even two hundred,
invented without sin and introduced without the word. III. It is plainly a
matter of absurd and reprobate perversity that what is affirmed here is this:
to marry another wife after the death of the first—a thing which is not a sin
of incontinence—is a sign of greater incontinence than the greatest sins of
incontinence, adultery and impure concubinage.

Bellarmine. 3. *The primary cause is mystical, v. 1., the perfect signification
of the marriage of Christ with the Church.*

Protestants. The primary cause of this prohibition, therefore, is a mere
fiction. For marriage does not signify the union of Christ with the Church by
some appointed and mystical representation of a sacrament; it only illustrates
that union by a voluntary accommodation of similar things. Nor does Scripture
anywhere require that the signification in the marriage of ministers be greater
than in that of others. Nor is it agreeable to any reason that a mystical and
perfect signification should be sought in a marriage perhaps contracted by
pagans, and that afterward it should be reckoned an endowment and perfection in
ministers of the word.
