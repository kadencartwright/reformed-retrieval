NINETEENTH LOCUS

ON THE SACRAMENTS

QUESTION ONE

ON THE TERM AND DEFINITION OF A SACRAMENT

What is a sacrament with respect to its name and with respect to the thing
itself?

[Marginalia: The necessity of treating the sacraments.]

I. Just as God willed to enter into a covenant with the church, which we have
treated thus far, in order to apply to her the salvation acquired through
Christ, so in his goodness he did not disdain to seal this covenant with the
sacraments as with seals, for the greater confirmation of faith. By these as
badges he would mark his people and distinguish them from the rest of the
world. We are therefore under a necessity to treat them, not only so that we
may know their nature and use more distinctly, but also so that we may more
easily unravel the exceedingly serious controversies that various adversaries
commonly raise concerning them. For we see with sorrow that the things which
God instituted to be bonds and symbols of union and concord among Christians
have, through human fault, become a seedbed of contentions and an “apple of
discord” that has torn Christians apart from one another in a disastrous
divorce.

II. Since this locus contains several heads, we will have to dispute, first,
about the sacraments in general; second, specifically about baptism and the
Lord’s Supper, which are the two sacraments of the New Testament instituted by
Christ; and third, about the five false and spurious sacraments added by the
papists. But first some things must be said about the term and definition of a
sacrament.

[Marginalia: The use of the term “sacrament,”]

III. We do not think the term *sacrament* should be disputed. Although it is
unknown to the books of the Old and New Testaments, since it is Latin, its use
has nevertheless become established in the church and has been received by
long usage. Thus, if anyone were led by some scruple to abstain from it, as the
Socinians do—who in this way attack not so much the term as the very nature of
the sacraments—we would judge him to be religious in an ill-timed and
misdirected manner. Hence the papists do Luther an injustice, and the
Lutherans do us an injustice, when they accuse us of abhorring this term,
since its ordinary use among us is evident. But the papists are no less
ridiculous when they wish to infer Scripture’s insufficiency from the fact
that this name does not occur in it. Who would be surprised that a Latin word
does not occur in Scripture, which was written in Hebrew and Greek?

[Marginalia: And its origin.]

IV. It is well known that *sacramentum* is derived from *sacrare*, that is, to
consecrate and initiate, just as *juramentum* is derived from *jurare*, to
swear, and *testamentum* from *testari*, to testify. Among the ancient authors
of the Latin language it signifies two things. (1) Money or a pledge deposited
by litigants with the priests in a sacred place. The party who lost his case
was fined this pledge as a penalty for unjust litigation,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 288 | IA leaf 322 | PDF p. 322]

as Varro observes in book 4 of *On the Latin Language*. (2) An oath, which was
formulated only after some sacred deity had been invoked. Hence in Cicero “to
contend by a *sacramentum*” means to affirm by an oath; likewise, “to be
questioned by a *sacramentum*,” “to be bound by a *sacramentum*,” and so
forth. In particular, it is used to denote the military oath by which soldiers
were bound to the commonwealth and magistrate, according to a fixed rite and
prescribed words, that they would vigorously do everything the commander had
ordered and would not desert the military standards. Hence the phrase “to
bind by a *sacramentum*” (Cicero, *On Duties*, book 1; Isidore, *Origins*, book
9, chapter 3). Hence in the ancient glosses a *sacramentum* is a “military
oath.” Tertullian, *To the Martyrs*, chapter 3, says: “We were called to the
army of God at the very time when we responded to the words of the
*sacramentum*.”

V. Transferred from military affairs to sacred uses, the word was employed by
ecclesiastical writers to signify any mystery, or sacred and uncommon
doctrine. Hence throughout the Fathers one finds the sacrament of the Trinity,
of the incarnation, and of the faith, and in general the whole Christian
religion comes under this name. The word is used in this sense in the Vulgate
where the word *mystery* occurs (1 Tim. 3:16; Eph. 1:9; 5:32): “The sacrament
of the seven stars” (Rev. 1:20), and “the sacrament of the beast” (Rev. 17:7).
More strictly, it is taken for a sacred sign or external symbol that presents
one thing to the sense and another to the mind. In this sense Augustine says
in letter 5 to Marcellinus: “Signs, when they pertain to divine things, are
called sacraments.” Finally, through various stages its meaning most recently
settled upon a sign and seal of the covenant in Christ instituted by God in
the church. Again, a sacrament in this sense is taken either simply for the
external signs or rites, or for the internal thing signified, or for the
combination of both the external and internal thing, the sign and what is
signified. We consider it here in this last sense.

[Marginalia: The corresponding term is *mystērion*.]

VI. Among the Greeks the word corresponding to this term is *mystērion*,
which was derived even by profane writers either from *myein*, meaning “to
initiate into sacred rites,” whence the superintendents of the sacred rites
were called *mystai*, “initiates”; or from “to close the mouth,” because, as
Eustathius says, “the initiates had to close their mouths and not disclose
things not to be revealed”; or from the Hebrew *satar*, meaning to conceal,
whence *mistor*, a secret. The sacred writers afterward transferred this word
from a superstitious use to a better one, to designate any heads of the
Christian religion that are hidden from flesh and blood. But in Scripture it
is never employed for a sacrament as that term is taken strictly for the signs
of the covenant of grace. Nevertheless, we do not otherwise reject the word,
because the sacraments truly are signs of a hidden thing or invisible grace.
They have a hidden signification and chiefly commend themselves by a concealed
mystery.

VII. Scripture more properly calls them “signs of the covenant” (Gen. 9:12–13;
17:11), “sign and seal of the righteousness of faith” (Rom. 4:11), and simply
“signs” (Exod. 12:13); also “copies” (Heb. 8:5; 9:23) and “antitypes” (1 Pet.
3:21). In the Old Testament the term *’oth* occurs (Gen. 17; Exod. 31; Ezek.
20). It denotes a sign and is applied to the ancient sacraments.

[Marginalia: Various definitions of a sacrament.]

VIII. Although a sacrament cannot be defined perfectly, because it is
something concrete and extraordinary composed of things that do not belong to
the same genus, it can nevertheless be suitably described. Many, following
Augustine, call it “a sign of a sacred thing,” for he says in letter 5 that
“signs, when they pertain to sacred things, are called sacraments.” The
scholastics define a sacrament as “a visible form of invisible grace.” But
neither definition pleases the papists of today. Bellarmine, in book 1 of *The
Sacraments in General*, chapter 2, says that these definitions are imperfect
if they are accepted as the words sound, but legitimate if accepted according
to the sense of the Roman church, because by

[1847 vol. III printed p. 289 | IA leaf 323 | PDF p. 323]

signs they understand sensible, instituted, and practical signs. He therefore
appears to criticize two things in particular in the definitions given. The
first is that sacraments are called a visible form too specifically, for he
contends that it is enough for the nature of a sacrament if the sign is
sensible, that is, perceived by some sense, even if not by sight. The other is
that sacraments are not called practical signs, that is, signs that effect
grace. He expressly includes both points in the definition of a sacrament that
he produces from the catechism of the Council of Trent in these words: “A
sacrament is a thing subjected to the senses that has the power not only to
signify grace but also to effect it.” Bellarmine cleverly proposes this in
order to favor the contrived sacraments of penance, matrimony, and the like,
which lack a visible sign, and then to lay the foundation for that false
hypothesis that the sacraments of the New Testament confer grace *ex opere
operato*, as they say. These matters will be treated below.

[Marginalia: The true definition is proposed.]

IX. In defining a sacrament, we follow Paul, who, speaking about circumcision,
says that it is “a sign and seal of the righteousness of faith” (Rom. 4:11).
This definition is generic and is rightly attributed to the species. Guided,
then, by this light, we say: “The sacraments are signs and seals of the grace
of God in Christ.” Or, a little more fully: “They are sacred, visible signs and
seals divinely instituted to signify and seal to our consciences the promises
of saving grace in Christ and, in turn, to attest our faith, piety, and
obedience toward God.” In this definition the nature of the sacraments is
clearly made known from their causes. Something must be said about these
didactically before we dispute about them elenctically.

[Marginalia: The matter of a sacrament is twofold: the sign,]

X. The matter of a sacrament is twofold: one external and sensible, the other
internal and intelligible. The former is called the sign and the latter what
is signified. The former is perceived by the bodily senses and chiefly by
sight; the latter by the mind equipped with a suitable instrument for it,
namely faith. The former is an element instituted by God to signify and seal
grace; the latter is the grace of God in Christ, or Christ with all his
benefits. By *sign* we understand whatever in the sacraments has the character
of signifying, which is of two kinds. First, the external symbols belong here.
Second, the ceremonial rites or actions belong here, both those of the
minister acting in God’s stead and those of the faithful receiving. Such
actions in baptism are the sprinkling of water or immersion, and in the Supper
the breaking, distribution, and reception of bread and wine, all of which have
their own signification. Nevertheless, the external elements themselves are
called signs in a distinctive sense, because they are the earthly things and
substance upon which such ceremonies and actions are performed.

[Marginalia: And the thing signified.]

XI. The other part of the sacramental matter is the thing signified. By this
designation is understood Christ himself with all the things that faith
applies to itself for salvation. Faith applies to itself Christ with all his
benefits flowing from his suffering and death, which Paul embraces when he
says, “He was made for us by the Father wisdom, righteousness, sanctification,
and redemption” (1 Cor. 1:30). Thus Christ crucified, who is as it were the
kernel of the sacraments, is signified together with all the saving benefits
that he acquired for us by his death. These signs were therefore instituted
to commemorate him and to confirm both his truth and his benefit: the truth
by signifying it, and the benefit by presenting and sealing it. Now, just as
Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever (Heb. 13:8), so he has always
declared and sealed the same grace to his church through the sacraments. This
is why, with respect to the internal matter, Paul attributes the sacraments of
the New Testament to the faithful of the Old (1 Cor. 10:1–3), and conversely
the sacraments of the Old to the faithful of the New (Col. 2:11; 1 Cor. 5:7).
Moreover, the thing signified differs from the sign in three respects. (1) In
nature and properties, for the sign

[1847 vol. III printed p. 290 | IA leaf 324 | PDF p. 324]

is an earthly and visible thing, while the thing of the sign is heavenly and
invisible. (2) In object, for the sign tends toward the body, but the thing
signified toward the soul. (3) In the mode of communication, for the mode of
the sign is bodily, but that of the thing signified is spiritual.

[Marginalia: The form.]

XII. The form of a sacrament is placed in the analogy or relation of the
external matter to the internal, and of the sign to the thing signified. By
this relation the promised thing is so represented to our souls that it is
also truly presented for communication. In this analogy consists the union of
the sign with what is signified. This union, therefore, is neither natural by
bodily contact, nor local by the absence of distance, nor even spiritual by a
spiritual activity through which the signs might immediately give life or be
given a power to regenerate or justify. Rather, it is relative and
sacramental, consisting chiefly in three things: signification, sealing, and
exhibition. Signification depends upon the likeness between the sign and what
is signified. Sealing depends upon God’s institution, by which these external
symbols have not only the character of a sign but also of a seal. For the
sacraments are added to the promises to produce greater assurance concerning
the promised thing, yet in such a way that our faith is supported by them,
not the Word of God, which is self-authenticating. Exhibition depends upon
God’s truth, because God does not play games by instituting naked and empty
signs. Just as in the spoken Word he actually performs what he promises, so in
the sacrament, which is a palpable and visible word, he actually exhibits what
the signs represent. Thus in the lawful use of the sacrament, true possession
and enjoyment of the thing signified are joined with the signs, as the French
Confession teaches in article 38. Nevertheless, we acknowledge no implanted
or inherent power in the signs by which they either act upon grace or upon any
quality of the soul, whether we call it a character or something similar. Just
as the sound of the spoken word, finally striking the ears and air, does not
shake hearts by any power implanted in it, so the external sign has no power
that affects the soul but only strikes the senses.

XIII. From this analogy and correspondence between the sign and what is
signified arises sacramental phraseology, or those statements most frequently
used in Scripture that theologians call sacramental predications. In these,
the names of signs and things signified are interchanged: the sign is
predicated of the thing signified, as Christ is called the Passover (1 Cor.
5:7), or conversely the thing signified is predicated of the sign, as
circumcision is called the covenant and the bread is called Christ’s body. The
foundation of these predications is the sacramental union, which joins the
sign and what is signified according to analogy and thus makes these
predications true on account of the truth of signification, sealing, and
exhibition in lawful use. In the same way, the hypostatic union of the natures
in the person of Christ makes true those predications in which the properties
of one nature are declared either of the whole person or even of the other
nature expressed by a concrete term. Yet this involves no confusion of the
natures, because they are united without confusion and without change.

[Marginalia: The efficient cause.]

XIV. God alone is the efficient cause of the sacraments. (1) He alone is the
author of the promise and covenant of grace. The one to whom it belongs to
promise and give grace also has the right to seal it. (2) God alone is the
author of the Word, and therefore also of the sacraments, which are the
visible word. (3) Since they are a part of divine worship, they can be
instituted by God alone. God makes the sacrament by the word of institution;
for when the word is added to the element, it becomes a sacrament, not by the
infusion of a new quality but by a change of use. The word that is called
sacramental has two parts, command and promise. The command explains the
dignity of the sacrament and the manner of its lawful administration and use.
The promise shows the thing signified, the clarity and truth of the
signification, and thus the whole efficacy of the sacrament. Therefore,
nothing more should be sought in the external sacrament than is contained in
the promise, lest it degenerate into an idol. The word of command is that by
which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 291 | IA leaf 325 | PDF p. 325]

God commands the sacraments to be rightly administered and received,
prescribing their form and commanding their lawful use. The word of promise
is, as it were, the power and soul of the sacrament, for in it grace is
proposed and offered, and faith in that grace is supported in us through the
lawful use of the sacrament. Hence the dignity and integrity of the sacrament
must be judged in a way that is diminished neither by the minister’s faults
nor by the participant’s unbelief, although an unbeliever receives only the
sign without the thing. The essence of the sacrament is from divine
institution. Therefore, if the sacrament is administered according to that
institution, it makes no difference to its essence whether the recipient
believes or not. The truth of the promises does not depend upon the faith of
those who believe. Otherwise unbelievers would weaken God’s truth, and God
would be made a liar by human unbelief, which is absurd. Thus, although faith
is necessary for receiving the thing signified, it is not necessary for
constituting the essence and integrity of the sacrament.

[Marginalia: The end.]

XV. The end of the sacraments is either proper or accidental. The proper end
is either principal and primary, or secondary and less principal. The
principal end, on God’s part, is the confirmation of the covenant of grace and
the sealing of our union with Christ promised in the covenant and of all his
benefits; on our part, it is the attestation of solemn gratitude toward God
and charity toward our neighbor. The less principal end is that the
sacraments may be badges of public profession and divine worship by which
those who belong to the visible church are distinguished from other
assemblies. Hence it is evident how great is God’s benevolence toward
humanity. Stooping in a manner to us who creep along the ground, he wishes to
carry away not only our souls but also our external senses into the taste and
wonder of his grace, insofar as he subjects it to the bodily senses—to hearing
in the spoken Word, and to touch and sight in the sacraments. Signs are
customarily employed in weightier matters. Trifling affairs are not confirmed
by signs, but matters of great moment are. Thus, when princes are inaugurated,
marriages entered into, gifts contracted, or other covenants made, signs are
customarily employed to confirm the things we wish to be most fully attested,
so that they may be known not only by reason but also by sense. The accidental
end is the just condemnation of the ungodly and hypocrites who abuse the
sacraments. This end, which is accidental through human fault, does not
overturn the proper end. Christ does not cease to be in himself the author and
giver of life with respect to the faithful, even though, accidentally because
of human unbelief, he is the aroma of death unto death and a stone of offense
and ruin with respect to hypocrites and unbelievers.

XVI. From these things it is easy to gather “what are the requisites of a
sacrament properly so called” that are required and concur to constitute it
intrinsically and extrinsically, either as essential parts or as necessary
conditions. There are four principal requisites. (1) An external and visible
element with a rite, or an earthly and bodily matter that has the character of
a sign. (2) A heavenly and spiritual thing contained in the promise of grace,
which has the character of the thing signified. In lawful use it coheres with
the sign, not substantially by composition, but sacramentally through a
mystical relation. (3) Immediate divine institution, in order that it may be a
sacrament. (4) Its established and ordinary use in the church. A sacrament
properly so called is constituted intrinsically and extrinsically from these
four things, and from them its truth is established and known. Intrinsically,
it is constituted by the element with the rite and by the word of the promise
of grace as essential parts. Extrinsically, it is constituted by divine
institution as its efficient and immediate cause and by its established and
ordinary use for attestation and obligation in the church as its proximate
end.

XVII. This is clearly proved by surveying the sacraments of the Old and New
Testaments,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 292 | IA leaf 326 | PDF p. 326]

for divine institution was common to them all, as is evident with respect to
circumcision (Gen. 17), Passover (Exod. 12), baptism (Matt. 28), and the
Supper (Matt. 26). An external sign with some accompanying rite was common to
them: in circumcision, the cutting off of the foreskin; in Passover, the lamb
slain and eaten; in baptism, washing with water; and in the Supper, broken
bread and poured-out wine. The word of the promise of grace was common to
them: concerning God’s covenant and the circumcision of the heart (Deut.
30:6), concerning Christ the slain Lamb (1 Cor. 5:7), concerning washing from
sins through the blood and Spirit of Christ (Acts 2:38; 1 Cor. 6:11), and
concerning Christ’s body broken and blood poured out for us. Finally, the use
of the sacraments of both testaments was established, ordinary, and common to
the church.
