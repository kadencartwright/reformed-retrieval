CHAPTER II. On the calling of ministers.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. *The second question concerns the institution of ministers of the
Church. Under the designation “appointment of bishops,” however, three things
are contained: election, which is the designation of a definite person to an
ecclesiastical office of oversight; ordination, which is the sacred ceremony
by which the future bishop is inaugurated and consecrated according to a fixed
rite; and calling, or mission, which bestows jurisdiction and actually makes
him a pastor and prelate.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine creates some confusion here at the outset when he
proposes the question under the designation *institution* but then explains it
under the designation *appointment*. For among the Pontificians institution
signifies *the granting of a church or benefice to a cleric nominated and
presented by a patron*; and it differs properly from *confirmation*, and also from
the *collation* of a benefice, Azor, part 2, book 6, chapter 22. Yet these are
contained under the appointment of a minister. II. Without any reason he
restricts, in his explanation, the question instituted concerning the
appointment of ministers to the appointment of *bishops*. III. He establishes
three parts of institution, or appointment, which do not have the nature of
parts. *Calling* is one and the same thing as institution or appointment, for
the entire derivation of ecclesiastical right to this or that person is called
a *calling*, both in the Scriptures and in common speech. *Election* also is
nothing other than the essential mode of ordinary calling, and thus this
definition of election by Azor, part 2, book 6, chapter 14, is praised as apt:
*Election is a calling, canonically made, of some person to a church destitute
of a pastor.* Ordination, however, is nothing other than the attestation and
completion of election. Hence also that saying of Cardinal Cusanus:
*Ordination is often the same thing as election*, *On Catholic Concord*, book
2, chapter 32. IV. Ordination is badly described as a *ceremony of anointing*,
unless both terms are taken in a very improper sense. V. After a legitimate
election and ordination, no calling or mission is generally to be awaited; nor
can it be understood what element of calling is lacking in a minister thus
elected and ordained.

2. Bellarmine. *The opinion of the Protestants is that election and calling
pertain by divine right to the universal Church, and ordination to the pastors
in the name of the whole Church. The Catholic Doctors, on the contrary, teach
with complete agreement that the right to ordain and call bishops cannot in
any way pertain to the people; but that the right of election was at one time,
and in some manner, in the possession of the people, though by the grant or
connivance of the pontiffs, not by divine law.*

Protestants. I. Either Bellarmine understands something novel by the right

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n280 | printed p. 63]

of calling, or what he affirms about the *complete agreement of Catholics* is
plainly false, both in taking that right away from the people and in granting
them the *right of election* only *by the grant and connivance* of the Pope.
For the Parisians testify against him as follows, *On Ecclesiastical and
Political Power*, Protestant section 2: *From the practice of the primitive
Church and the sacred canons it is most thoroughly established that the
collations of benefices, as they now call them, were made for fourteen hundred
years by common right, that is, by sacrosanct elections. The reason is that
every principality, with respect to coercive force, depends upon the consent of
human beings, as divine and natural law ordain.*

3. Bellarmine. *In order that we may demonstrate all these things distinctly
and in order, we shall dispute first about ordination, then about calling, and
lastly about election.*

Protestants. This is not to dispute distinctly and in order, but backwards and
confusedly. To place ordination before calling and election is to put the cart
before the horses. The very essence of calling consists in legitimate
election. Ordination depends upon election, just as the coronation of a prince,
or the inauguration of a magistrate, depends upon election, succession, or
some equivalent appointment. Although the rite of ordination, just like an
inauguration or coronation, is also sometimes bestowed upon others for the sake
of honor, its right nevertheless depends upon election and by its own right
always looks to the electors. *From election the one elected acquires a right
to the thing*, Azor, part 1, book 6, chapter 14. From the same source, therefore,
arises the complete character of the cause on account of which he ought to be
introduced into possession of the thing, which some call a *right in the
thing*. This in truth makes no alteration in the right, but only in the use and
effect of the right. But Bellarmine deliberately seems first to have proposed
these things in this order—*election, ordination, calling*—and now in another,
almost inverted order—*ordination, calling, election*—so that no account would
be taken of the true and genuine order of these acts. By the light of that
order alone the perversity and corruption of the Pontifical clergy is easily
exposed, as will appear immediately.

On Ordination.

4. Bellarmine. *The ordination both of bishops and of other ministers of the
Church pertains not to the people but to the bishops alone.*

Protestants. I. By divine right it cannot pertain to papal bishops, who are
superior in rank to presbyters, because they themselves exist by either human
right or human wrong. II. We grant that in a well-constituted Church the act of
ordaining pertains to divine bishops, that is, presbyters. III. Nevertheless,
with respect to the right, force, and power which it has in constituting a
minister of the Church, it always pertains to the whole Church, just as the
celebration of a marriage refers all the force or power it receives to the
legitimate consent of the spouses.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n281 | printed p. 64]

IV. When the Church's condition, ministry, and order are failing, collapsed,
or corrupted, even the act of ordination, insofar as it is necessary to the
constitution of a minister, can in such a case be legitimately exercised by
the people.

5. Bellarmine. *Moses ordained Aaron.*

Protestants. I. Nor has any one of us ever doubted that it is lawful for one
who possesses similar authority to ordain ministers, whether extraordinary or
ordinary. In constituting ordinary ministers of the Church of the New
Testament, however, the power of the Church now free is greater than was that
of the old Church, which had something servile in all things.

6. Bellarmine. *In the New Testament, wherever mention is made of the laying on
of hands, it is always attributed to the Apostles and the other bishops, but
never to the people.*

Protestants. I. In the history of the New Testament we have examples of the
constitution of the ministry and also of its preservation, but not of its
restoration or reformation after a general apostasy. Therefore, all the things
that pertain to the faithful people in such a case are not represented there
by definite examples. II. It is attributed to the presbyters, 1 Timothy 4:14.
For although some of the Fathers, from the usage of the times that followed,
seem to interpret that passage of bishops who transcend presbyters, they do so
without reason and contrary to the phraseology and usage of Scripture. III.
When it is also attributed to the Apostles and Evangelists, it is in some
manner attributed to their true successors, who were constituted by them—that
is, to presbyters, not to bishops who transcend them. IV. The Apostles
themselves did not constitute ordinary ministers without the consent of the
people as a concurrent cause, Acts 14:23.

7. Bellarmine. *The same thing is clear from very many decrees of councils and
testimonies of the Fathers.*

Protestants. Nothing else is clear from them except that, after some time, the
act and rite of ordination was attributed principally to a presbyter or bishop,
partly by custom and partly by human decrees.

8. Bellarmine. *In no age has it been heard that anyone but a bishop ordained a
bishop.*

Protestants. I. With respect to bishops who transcend presbyters, Jerome
testifies that this is false in those words cited by Bellarmine in chapter 4,
when he teaches that there was a time when such bishops did not yet exist, but
*churches were governed by the common counsel of presbyters.* For how could the
first bishops have been ordained by bishops? II. We say that true bishops
should ordinarily be ordained by true bishops, but in the name of the Church
to which they are ordained.

9. Bellarmine. *That pastors are ordained by pastors in the name of the whole
clergy and people can be understood in three ways.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n282 | printed p. 65]

*1. In the way that the tongue speaks and the eyes see in the name of the whole
body, and so forth. If this is the meaning of our adversaries, we need not
contend greatly over it. 2. That the true power of ordaining is in the people
itself. This adversarial meaning ought not to be approved if they wish to be
consistent with themselves, because in that case the duty of ordaining could
be assigned to those who are not pastors. 3. That the power of jurisdiction is
in the people, so that they can command the pastors to lay hands upon definite
persons designated by them. This matter will be disputed in the following
chapter.*

Protestants. I. If Bellarmine grants in the first sense that pastors are
ordained in the name of the Church, then he admits that the power of ordaining
is in some manner *originally* in the whole Church. For the power of sight is
originally in the whole animal, although formally and subjectively it is only
in the eye; and so also the exercise of ordination depends upon the whole
Church, just as the act of determinately seeing this or that depends not upon
the eye but upon the whole. II. The second meaning can be defended without any
difficulty. For although in a well-constituted Church the duty of ordaining
ought not to be entrusted to any but presbyters, in the absence of suitable
presbyters it can be entrusted to those who are not presbyters. III. In the
third meaning, *jurisdiction* is mentioned without cause. For according to the
Scholastics and Canonists the power of ordaining is not a power of
jurisdiction, but of simple office; and some act of the Church (such as a
legitimate election) can oblige presbyters to ordain without the command of
jurisdiction.

10. Bellarmine. *From what has been said, an insoluble argument (unless I am
mistaken) is gathered: those who have not been ordained by the laying on of
hands by true pastors are not true pastors. Luther, Zwingli, Calvin, and so
forth, were not ordained bishops by true pastors but were presbyters only.
Therefore, neither were those who succeeded these first men and were ordained
by them true pastors. But the Church cannot exist without pastors. It follows,
therefore, that the adversaries do not have a true Church.*

Protestants. I. The rite of laying on of hands is not absolutely necessary to
the being of a pastor, any more than coronation is necessary to the being of a
king, or the celebration of nuptials to the being of a marriage. Bellarmine,
together with Driedo, teaches in *On the Church*, book 3, chapter 10, *that
external baptism is not required for someone to be of the Church, and that
someone unbaptized sometimes enters the Church otherwise than by the ordinary
door.* Is the laying on of hands more necessary to the ministry? Can it happen
in no way that someone enters otherwise than through this door? II. The
Pontificians teach that because of baptism's necessity it is valid when
conferred by any Christian. Will they hold the laying on of hands to be more
necessary, yet make it invalid unless it is applied by those whom they regard
as bishops? III. Bellarmine teaches in the same place that *for determining who
holds the place of Christ, and that obedience is consequently*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n283 | printed p. 66]

*owed to him and in this sense he is a true pastor, and so forth, a legitimate
election (which also embraces ordination) is not required, but only that he is
regarded as such by the Church.* Therefore, although the first Reformers had
not been rightly ordained, nevertheless, since both they and their successors
were regarded as true pastors by the churches over which they presided, this
very fact suffices (if nothing else stands in the way) to preserve the Church
in its condition. But by a sophistical disproof Bellarmine wishes his bishops
to be acknowledged on account of the Church, while he wishes our churches to
be condemned on account of their bishops. IV. It is the common opinion of the
Pontifical Doctors that a simple presbyter can either administer the sacrament
of confirmation or confer upon someone one of the higher orders *when the Pope
commands* or grants it, Filincius the Jesuit, *On Cases of Conscience*, part 1,
treatise 9, chapter 5. Why not, therefore, without the Pope's grant *when
necessity commands*? All the Pontificians teach with complete agreement that a
bishop once anointed and consecrated, though he be a simoniac, a heretic,
excommunicated, and so forth, can nevertheless ordain validly, in the same
place. But is anyone so senseless as to think that there is more power to
institute ministers in one such scoundrel, covered with every crime and cut off
from the Church, than in all godly presbyters together with the faithful
churches of Christ? VI. If this is granted—that, according to the regular
order, no one can be a legitimate pastor unless ordained by a legitimate
pastor and bishop—nevertheless, when that order is absent and the order must
now first be restored, it cannot be observed so precisely; and thus something
extraordinary can then intervene without any fault.

Bellarmine 1. *A well-constituted Church cannot fall or fail.*

Protestants. Miserable experience has demonstrated more than sufficiently
that particular churches can fail, and that the outward face and
administration of nearly all the churches can be shamefully corrupted.

Bellarmine 2. *If Luther, Calvin, and so forth had possessed an extraordinary
calling, they would certainly have come with signs and miracles.*

Protestants. I. We call the calling of certain ordinary ministers
extraordinary only in the sense in which the Pontifical priests are said by
certain Jesuits themselves legitimately to do certain things *by extraordinary
right*, although in order to do these things they are not accustomed to come
with signs and miracles, Filincius, as above. II. A calling that is called
extraordinary because it surpasses the power communicated to ordinary means
does not always and necessarily require miracles for its confirmation, as is
clear in many Prophets—much less does one that is so called only because it

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n284 | printed p. 67]

departs in some respect from an order already corrupted, so that it may restore
that order, as can be seen in Ezra and is sufficiently clear from the nature of
the thing.

11. Protestants. Bellarmine was mistaken when he thought that trivial sophism
of his was an *insoluble* argument. The solution of the opposing argument,
however, will not be found so easily. The Pontifical priests are deprived of
what they require in their ordination: I. They are deprived of the principal
and necessary *efficient cause*, that is, the institution of Christ, because no
*order of feeding priests* has been instituted in the New Testament. II. They
are deprived of the *matter*, because nearly all of them are, according to the
Apostolic canons, completely incapable of ecclesiastical ministry. III. They
are deprived of the *form*, because receiving with fixed words *a stole and
chasuble, a chalice and paten, being anointed, and so forth*, has no more savor
of the Apostolic pattern than does some childish or theatrical game. IV. They
are deprived of the *end*, because those sacrilegious words of the bishop
playing a part—*Receive the power to offer sacrifice to God and to celebrate
Mass, both for the living and for the dead*—have nothing in common with the
office of a Christian pastor. V. They are deprived of the *object*, because
they are customarily ordained to no particular church, but indeterminately, or
as they say, *without title*, contrary to the decree of the Council of
Chalcedon. VI. They are deprived of the *necessary adjunct*, namely, a suitable
prior examination or investigation. VII. Finally, they are deprived of the
sole *foundation* of just ordination, that is, the legitimate and free election
of the Church. The Pontificians, therefore, have no rightly ordained pastors,
and thus (as Bellarmine concludes) they have no true Church.
