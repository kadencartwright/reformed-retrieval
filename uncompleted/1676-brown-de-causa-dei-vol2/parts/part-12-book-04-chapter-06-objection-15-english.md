# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 15.* Matthew 11:13: *“For all the prophets and the law itself
prophesied until John.”* From this they conclude that both all the prophecies
and the law itself are abrogated.

*Answer 1.* [Pareus says on the passage: *“From this, fanatics exclude the
prophets and the law from the new church. But Christ does not intend this.
Instead, he terminates the ministry of the prophets and the shadows of the
law in John's preaching, because whatever was prophesied about Christ in the
law, or Deuteronomy, and the prophets has its fulfillment in the ministry of
John and Christ. But concerning the substance of the law he had said, ‘I did
not come to dissolve the law, but to fulfill it.’ He therefore speaks, first,
of the shadows of the law and the prophets, which were then beginning to be
fulfilled and abrogated; and second, of the predictions to be fulfilled in
the New Testament, which then began to be fulfilled and terminated, because
no new prophecies or oracles concerning Christ were any longer to be
expected.”*] Gualter has similar things on the passage: [*“That saying,”* he
says, *“should not be understood as though the writings of Moses and the
prophets were to have no further use, or as though no mention of them should
be made in the church. For Christ himself contradicts this opinion, affirming
that even if heaven and earth pass away, not so much as one jot will fall
from the law. And he himself frequently uses testimonies from Moses and the
prophets, as the apostles too habitually did after him. He therefore does not
intend to abolish the books of the Old Testament, but rather shows their
proper use and says that they prophesied until John; that is, Moses and the
prophets had no other goal set before them than that kingdom of heaven whose*

[Printed p. 67 | IA PDF 75 | IA leaf 75 | IA decausadei02brow]

*first minister was John.”*] This conclusion therefore does not follow.

2. The words can be punctuated and construed in different ways, as Piscator
testifies. A full stop can be placed after ὁ νόμος (“the law”). They will then
have this meaning: the prophecies and the law do not extend themselves beyond
the time when John was raised up, but end precisely at that point. Or a comma
can be placed after τὸν Ἰωάννην (“John”), and the meaning will then be this:
all the prophets who existed before John, and the law, prophesied concerning
Christ's coming. John, however, pointed him out with his finger. Thus the
emphasis will be on the word *prophesied*, which expresses a tacit contrast.
From this it is clearly proved that no greater prophet than John had been
raised up, as Christ had said in verse 11. In the verb *to prophesy*, Calvin
says, lies the emphasis: the law and the prophets did not set God before
people's eyes but foreshadowed him in figures as one absent. But however we
understand the words, they do nothing for the opposing opinion. For if they
are understood in the *latter* way, as expositors commonly think—see Marlorat
on the passage—it is evident that nothing favoring the opinion of the
Antinomians can be deduced from them. And if they are understood in the
*former* way, we will attribute this exemption from the obligation of the law
to John rather than to Christ, if the words denote any such thing. Moreover,
Christ could then have had no right to say that the law remained in force
even in his own time, nor could he rightfully have vindicated the law from
the false glosses of the Pharisees, since it had already been annulled.

3. But the plain and obvious sense of the passage is that the contrast here
is not between the subject matter or doctrine, but only between the manners
of delivering it. Thus it indicates the distinction between the ministry of
the law and the prophets, or the Old Testament, and the ministry of John, or
the New Testament. For the law and the prophets merely *prophesied*
(προεφήτευσαν) concerning Christ's coming and kingdom; but John pointed out
the one present with his finger and proclaimed Christ's present kingdom in
the Gospel. This sense is confirmed by Luke, who expresses himself thus in
chapter 16, verse 16: *“The law and the prophets were until John; from that
time the kingdom of God is proclaimed in the Gospel.”*

4. Although Christ says that the law and the prophets prophesied concerning
the Christ who was to come, or foretold his coming, he nevertheless does not
say that the moral law lost all force. The law—namely, the ceremonial
law—and the prophets had this use: to illustrate and confirm the promises
concerning the Messiah, the sole redeemer of the world, and to foreshadow the
Messiah. Thus by this saying Christ stops the mouths of the Pharisees, who had
received his teaching with jeers in verse 14 and hated him on the ground that
he seemed to be an enemy of the law and the prophets. For he clearly
indicates that this use of the law vanishes because the very one whom it
prefigured has now come. [Gualter says on the passage: *“But this should not
be understood as though at his coming the law were wholly abolished together
with the prophets, or as though neither the law nor the prophets should have
any use for us today. (For the Lord himself will presently testify concerning
this.) Instead, by that saying we are admonished that Jesus Christ is the end
and goal of the law, and thus of the whole Old Testament. He himself explains
the sense of this saying when he testifies in John 5 that Moses wrote about
him, and when, after rising from the dead, he says to the disciples in Luke
24, ‘These are the words which I spoke to you while I was still with you:
that, namely, all the things written about me in the law of Moses, the
prophets, and the Psalms had to be fulfilled.’”*] But he is not speaking
specifically about the moral law, nor does he say or even imply that it has
now been abolished in such a way that it is no longer to be regarded as a
rule of conduct, as the following words clearly prove in verse 17: *“It is
easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one stroke of the law*

[Printed p. 68 | IA PDF 76 | IA leaf 76 | IA decausadei02brow]

*to fall.”* On these words Gualter comments very well: [*“But the Pharisees
could have understood this pronouncement of Christ in a false sense, as
though after this the authority of the law and the prophets ought to have no
force. This he denies most steadfastly, saying, ‘It is easier,’ and so forth.
What is said in Matthew in more words—‘Do not suppose,’ and so forth—helps
interpret this saying. By these words he acknowledges, or rather openly
declares, that he recognizes the inviolable authority of the law. Indeed,
nothing in this whole structure of the heavens and earth is so firm and
stable as the law of God. For because it contains God's will, which is always
consistent with itself, it can never be changed or abolished. And although
it contains certain things which had to be abrogated at the time of
reformation, this did not happen before those very things reached their goal
and were fulfilled in Christ. At this point the whole scheme of the law must
be considered. It is divided into the moral, ceremonial, and judicial law.
The moral law delivers precepts concerning conduct and all the actions of
life, from which faith in God and his true worship cannot be separated. Its
epitome is the Decalogue. The ceremonial law concerns the external worship
of God, which once consisted in fixed ceremonies and rites. All of these
were in part seals of the divine promises and in part admonished people of
their duty. They called the judicial law that part which pertains to the
court. As for the fulfillment and abrogation of the law or of its individual
parts, both are found in Christ alone. The ceremonial laws had their
fulfillment in him when, in his death and resurrection, nearly all the things
foreshadowed in them came to pass. Among the judicial laws, those which
pertained solely to the commonwealth of the Jews were abrogated in this way.
But those among them which agree with the law of nature and are also common
to others living according to it, together with the whole moral law, remain
in force and ought never to be abrogated or removed. The curse and
condemnation which once awaited all who did not perform everything contained
in the book of the law, however, have been abrogated. If all these things are
weighed a little more carefully, it will readily appear that the true use of
the law is so far from being abolished in Christ Jesus that it has its
fulfillment nowhere else. Add to this that in him the Spirit of adoption is
given to the regenerate. Strengthened by that Spirit, we do the things which
previously could not be accomplished by our own powers; and although we
stumble repeatedly each day, by the power of that same Spirit we finally
rise again to newness of life. Thus the authority of the law always remains
valid and sacrosanct, and nothing falls from it, since it leads us back to
Christ as a tutor, and in him perfect righteousness and redemption are
presented to us.”*] Thus he makes the whole matter perfectly clear. Indeed,
Christ confirms this still further by the example in verse 18, when he says,
*“Whoever dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery, and
whoever marries a woman dismissed by her husband commits adultery.”* For by
this instance he clearly indicates that the moral law is not abrogated by
him but rather confirmed.
