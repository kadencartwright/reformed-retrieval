Verse 14. The mouth of strange women is a deep pit; he with whom the Lord is indignant will fall into it.

In שוחה (*puteus*, pit) there is a metaphor transferred from wild beasts which, captured by hunters in dug pits, cannot get themselves out, to foolish young men who are ensnared by the blandishments of prostitutes. In פי (*os*, mouth) there is a metonymy of the effect for speech proceeding from the mouth. In זרות (*extranearum*, of strange women) there is a metaphor drawn from the law which provided that Israelites should not be joined in marriage to foreign women, as being strangers to the religion of God, and transferred to women who, though born of Israel, are nevertheless not joined in lawful marriages. They must be shunned no less than those foreign women.

The purpose is to deter people from the alluring speech of prostitutes.

There are many arts and snares of prostitutes by which unwary youth is deceived: first, adornment and clothing of the body arranged to entice them, Prov. 7.10; then the adulterer's eyes, Prov. 6.25. Hence Job made a covenant with his eyes, Job 31.1, and David humbly asks for the turning of his eyes away from the sight of vanity, Ps. 119. But of all allurements none is more powerful than speech, and none is more suited to deceive. Prov. 5.3 and 7.21.

Therefore, before all else we must beware of conversing with prostitutes. Yet not a few people are found who, trusting in their own ability and prudence, desire conversations with prostitutes for this reason alone: that they may

[1632 witness | PDF p. 500 | printed columns 984-985]

make trial of the arts of prostitutes and of their own strength. For they reason with themselves as follows: women are easily surpassed by men in judgment and prudence; therefore they do not doubt that they are well fortified against all those women's poisoned weapons. Eph. 6.12. But they err in the whole course. Nor do they consider, as the Apostle wisely admonished, that our struggle is not with flesh and blood, but with, etc. For the prostitute comes forth instructed not only by her own deceits but also by the deceits of Satan himself. Hence Joseph refused the company of his mistress, Gen. 39.10. But if dishonourable women are so dangerous to men, women must much more beware of lustful men, because that enemy of the human race, having obtained an instrument more suitable for deceiving, will vomit forth and spit out his much more potent and effective poison.

One who compares a prostitute to a ditch or pit must compare the person who gives ear to her blandishments to a beast which unwittingly falls into a hunter's pit. Thus at Prov. 7.22 he is likened to an ox hastening to the slaughterhouse, and at 2 Pet. 2.12 the ungodly are compared to beasts destined for capture and destruction. Therefore, unless we wish to strip off human form and put on the nature and image of beasts, we must diligently beware of that corruption.

The second argument by which he dissuades us from this evil is drawn from its subject: namely, that the person captured here by the deceits of prostitutes is one with whom God is indignant. People carefully guard against committing those things by which they would render themselves hateful to a king and princes. Above all, however, they take care that if they have fallen from a prince's favour, others should nevertheless know it less fully, lest, if it becomes known that the king is hostile to them, almost everyone should rush to oppress them. How much more must we strive that neither we ourselves should feel God alienated from us nor others perceive it? From the loss of health, riches, children, and so forth, people very keenly feel that God is angry with them. But they ought to perceive God's much more bitter and severe offence against them if at any time they fall into sins, and specifically into adultery. See Rom. 1.18.

Verse 15. Folly is bound to the heart of a child; the rod of correction will drive it far away from him.

In קשורה (*alligatur*, is bound) there is a metaphor by which it is indicated that sin adheres to children no less tenaciously than things which are bound to one another by bonds. In ירחיק (*procul arcebit*, will drive far away) there is also a metaphor transferred from physical things separated from one another by so great an interval that they cannot be joined again, to the perfect cure of sin.

The sum is that due and just correction does children much good.

By the word *folly* is signified, as almost everywhere in this book, sin, or the violation of the divine law, and not a narrow understanding of divine or even human things, which, as long as tender age lasts, cannot be driven out by any correction. From this it is evident how depraved our nature is, since sin adheres in this way even to children educated most holily and piously.

For when he says without qualification that folly is implanted in the minds of children, it is proof that we are all conceived and brought forth in sins, and consequently that those who allege childhood as a defence for wrongdoings committed by children are shamefully mistaken. For Scripture most plainly cries out against them, teaching that every thought of human beings, not to mention their words and deeds, is perverse and depraved from youth and even from infancy itself. Gen. 6.5; Job 11.12. Those who mocked the Prophet were little children, 2 Kings 2.23. Was their sin therefore slight and worthy of excuse? No; by this deed they drew upon themselves the Prophet's dire curse and God's vengeance. How, therefore,

deservedly are those people condemned who diminish the sins of the young because they are youthful, though these are among the worst sins of all ages. Ps. 25.7 and 119.

When he says that folly is bound to the heart of a child, he teaches that the condition of children is still more lamentable, because folly is not hung by slender threads, but is so fastened to their minds, as it were, by bonds and ropes that it cannot easily be torn away. But if bad education, depraved examples of parents, and the company of wanton children are added to these bonds of natural corruption, folly is bound still more firmly to their hearts by this threefold cord, most difficult to break. We must therefore beware that sin, already fixed too tenaciously in children by the very depravity of nature, is not further strengthened by other bonds.

Hence the Pelagians are refuted, who deny original sin and say that it is contracted by imitation alone.

In the latter hemistich the Holy Spirit establishes a remedy and cure for the disease which he had identified in the former: first, in correction, intimating that no child is so well-born as not to need correction; then in moderate and mature correction. He therefore wills that a rod be employed; the person who spares it hates his son. But he further adds what sort of rod it ought to be: namely, one joined with instruction and νουθεσίᾳ (*admonition*). For he appears to indicate this clearly when he calls it a rod of correction. It is beastlike to burden a child with blows and set before his eyes neither the baseness of his sin nor the way by which he may return to good conduct. Let it be a cause for shame that dogs should be trained better than children. We will undoubtedly do this if, when about to beat a dog which has killed a sheep or goat by biting it, we place it before the carcass and shout in a loud voice so that it may abstain thereafter, but when correcting children we retain admonitions and teachings drawn from the word of God.

Hence it is also evident which people Solomon wishes to be beaten with a rod: namely, those who are old enough to understand the instruction and teaching of their parents.

From these things it is readily evident that the condition of those children whom the foolish indulgence of their parents allows to rot in sins is most unhappy, and much more that of those who are confirmed in wrongdoing by rewards set before them. Let us remember the sons of Eli and David, of whom we have spoken several times already. Although they had been trained for godliness not only by the excellent teachings of their parents but also by the examples of their lives, they nevertheless perished because correction was lacking.

Yet it must not be thought that bare words or blows can do anything to break the fierceness of sin unless the Spirit of God is added, who renders correction fruitful and effective. But the Prophet teaches that God, out of his charity toward human beings, will cause his Spirit himself, with his strongest finger, when this slight arm of the parents is added, to drive the sins of children so far away from them that they will never return again. Hence this conclusion also blossoms forth: correction must not be employed without prayers.

Hence, finally, it can be gathered how fierce in disposition are those children who pursue their parents and teachers with hatred because they correct them, although for that very reason they ought to embrace them with a closer love.
