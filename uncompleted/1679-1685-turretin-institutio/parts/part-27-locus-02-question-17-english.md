QUESTION XVII

ON THE PERSPICUITY OF SCRIPTURE

Is Scripture so perspicuous in matters necessary for salvation that it can be
understood by a believer without the external aid of unwritten tradition
(ἀγράφου) or ecclesiastical authority? We affirm, against the Roman Catholics.

I. It was not enough for the Roman Catholics to charge Scripture with
imperfection in order to introduce the necessity of traditions. Rather, so
that they might with some pretext keep the people from reading it and hide the
lamp under a bushel—so that they might reign more easily in darkness—they
began to urge its obscurity, as though its meaning could be known with
certainty by no one without the judgment of the church.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. To establish the state of the question, observe: 1. The question does not
concern subjective perspicuity or obscurity, that is, the perspicuity or
obscurity of persons. For we do not deny that the Scriptures are obscure to
unbelievers and the unregenerate, to whom Paul says that his gospel is veiled,
2 Corinthians 4. We also acknowledge that the illuminating Spirit is
necessary for believers to understand them. Rather, the question concerns the
objective obscurity or perspicuity of Scripture: is it so obscure that a
believer cannot apprehend it for salvation without the authority and judgment
of the church? We deny this.

III. The question does not concern the obscurity of the things or mysteries
handed down in Scripture. For both sides acknowledge that many of the
mysteries proposed in Scripture are so sublime that they far surpass our
understanding and to this extent can be called obscure in themselves. Rather,
it concerns the obscurity of the manner in which those most profound things
are handed down. We maintain that by a wonderful condescension
(συγκαταβάσει) the Lord has so tempered Scripture that a believer whose mental
eyes are enlightened can, by reading attentively, grasp these mysteries
sufficiently for salvation.

IV. The question is not whether Holy Scripture is perspicuous in all its parts
so that it needs no interpreter and no exposition of difficulties. Bellarmine
falsely and slanderously imputes this to us in book 3 of *The Word of God*,
chapter 1, framing the state of the question thus: “Are the Scriptures in
themselves so very easy and plain that they need no interpretation?” For we
freely acknowledge that Scripture has its inner sanctuaries and depths
(βάθη), which we cannot attain and search out. God willed these to be in
Scripture both to arouse the zeal of believers and increase their diligence,
to subdue human pride, and to remove the weariness which might arise from
excessive ease. Rather, the question concerns only things necessary for
salvation, and indeed only according to that relation (σχέσιν) in which they
must necessarily be known and cannot be ignorantly neglected without guilt.
For example, the mystery of the Trinity is perspicuously handed down with
respect to the fact that it is (τὸ ὅτι), which is necessary, but not with
respect to how it is (τὸ πῶς), which we are not permitted to know and which is
not required for salvation. Thus it pleased God that, as in nature, so also in
Scripture, almost everywhere all necessary things should present themselves
and be found with little trouble, but that some less necessary things should
be hidden more deeply in certain places, from which they could be drawn out
only with great study. Thus, besides food and bread, Scripture has, as it
were, its delicacies, its gems, and gold buried deep and to be dug out by
unwearied study. And just as heaven is interspersed with stars of different
sizes, greater and lesser, so Scripture does not shine equally everywhere but
is distinguished by brighter and darker passages, like stars of greater or
lesser magnitude.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 130 | IA leaf 184 | PDF p. 184]

V. The question is not whether things necessary for salvation are everywhere
handed down perspicuously in Scripture. For we acknowledge that there are
various things difficult to understand (δυσνόητα), by which God willed to
exercise our zeal and intellectual acumen. Rather, it is whether they are
perspicuous at least somewhere, so that a believer, by applying diligent
meditation, can perceive their truth. For nothing is drawn out of more obscure
passages which is not found stated most plainly elsewhere, as Augustine says:
“Magnificently and beneficially the Holy Spirit so arranged the Scriptures
that by the plainer passages he would meet hunger, while by the more obscure
he would dispel weariness,” *On Christian Doctrine*, book 2, chapter 6. And in
*On the Words of the Lord*, sermon 2: “We are fed by the plain passages and
exercised by the obscure; there hunger is driven away, here weariness.”

VI. The question does not concern a perspicuity which excludes the means
necessary for interpretation, such as the inward light of the Spirit,
attention of mind, the voice and ministry of the church, sermons and
commentaries, prayers and vigils. For we acknowledge that such means are not
only useful but ordinarily necessary. We wish to proscribe only that obscurity
which keeps the people from reading Scripture as though it were harmful and
dangerous, and which compels them to resort to tradition where they ought to
have rested in Scripture alone.

VII. The question therefore comes down to this: Is Holy Scripture so
perspicuous in matters necessary for salvation—not with respect to the things
themselves which are handed down, but with respect to the manner of handing
them down; not subjectively, but objectively—that without the external aid of
tradition or the infallible judgment of the church, it can be read and
understood savingly by believers? The Roman Catholics deny this; we affirm it.

[Marginal heading: Scripture's perspicuity is proved from Psalm 19:8 and
119:105; 2 Peter 1:19.]

VIII. That Scripture is perspicuous is clear, 1. from Scripture itself, which
proclaims itself luminous. Psalm 19:8 says, “The testimony of Jehovah is sure,
enlightening the eyes”; Psalm 119:105, “Your words are a lamp to my foot”; 2
Peter 1:19, “A lamp shining in a dark place”; and Proverbs 6:23, “The law is a
lamp.” Bellarmine's first objection is of no force: he maintains that only the
precepts of the law, not the whole of Scripture, are in view. For throughout
Scripture the whole Word of God is signified by the name *law*, and its effects
of consoling and restoring teach that it must be understood in this way. The
Interlinear Gloss, Nicholas of Lyra, and Arias Montanus also support us here.
Peter certainly calls the whole Word of God a lamp. Nor is Bellarmine's other
objection any better. He maintains that even if the whole of Scripture is in
view, this is to be understood only in the sense that Scripture illuminates
once it has been understood. But Scripture is called luminous not only because
it illuminates once understood, but because it is luminous in itself and is
naturally fitted to illuminate those who make use of it with the eyes of
faith. Thus it is luminous both formally and efficiently, because it spreads
rays like the sun and presents itself to the eyes of those who behold it.
Furthermore, nothing more inept can be said. It is the same as if I were to say
that Scripture does not illuminate unless it illuminates, for by the very fact
that it is understood, it illuminates.

IX. Deuteronomy 30:11, where the Word is said not to be hidden or far removed
from us, refers not only to the ease of fulfilling the commandments, but also
to the ease of understanding them, without which they could never be
fulfilled. Nor does it concern only the precepts, but the Word of God in
general. Hence Paul applies this to the word of faith, Romans 10:6, which is
not to be fulfilled by works but believed by faith.

X. The gospel is said to be veiled only to unbelievers, 2 Corinthians 4:3, and
therefore to be perspicuous to believers, not only as preached but also as
written. For the apostles did not preach one thing and write another, and the
clarity of the gospel is here contrasted with the obscurity of the Old
Testament, which the Jews were occupied in reading and which Paul had treated
in chapter 3:14.

XI. Scripture's perspicuity is proved by its efficient cause, namely, God, the
Father

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 131 | IA leaf 185 | PDF p. 185]

of lights. He cannot be said either to have been unwilling or unable to speak
perspicuously without impugning his supreme goodness and wisdom. 2. It is
proved by its end, which is to be the canon and rule of faith and morals;
Scripture could not be this unless it were perspicuous. 3. It is proved by its
matter, namely, the law and the gospel, which are readily perceived by anyone.
4. It is proved by its form, because it serves us as a testament, a covenant
contract, and the edict of a prince, which ought to be perspicuous and not
obscure.

XII. The Fathers everywhere acknowledge its perspicuity, although they do not
deny that Scripture has its own depths (βάθη), which ought to exercise the
zeal of believers. Chrysostom, homily 3 *On Lazarus*, says that Scripture has
been so tempered “that even the unlearned can understand it, provided they
read attentively,” and that “all things there are clear and straightforward;
all necessary things are plain” (πάντα ibi esse σαφῆ καὶ εὐθέα, πάντα τὰ
ἀναγκαῖα δῆλα). Augustine, book 2 of *On Christian Doctrine*, chapters 2, 6,
and 9, says: “In those things which are plainly handed down in the Scriptures
are found all things which contain faith and the morals by which one is to
live.” Irenaeus, book 2, chapter 46, says that prophetic and evangelical
Scripture is in the open and without ambiguity. Gregory, in the preface to
*Job*, says: “Scripture has in public that by which it nourishes little ones,
just as it preserves in secret that by which it holds the minds of the lofty
in wonder. It is, as it were, a river both shallow and deep, in which both a
lamb may walk and an elephant may swim.”

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XIII. It is one thing to speak of human ignorance and blindness; it is another
to speak of the obscurity of Scripture. The former is often emphasized in
Scripture, but not the latter. Nor can the latter be legitimately inferred
from the former, any more than it can be inferred that the sun is obscure
because a blind person does not see it. Hence, if David and other believers
ask that their eyes be uncovered so that they may see the wonders of the law,
the obscurity of Scripture cannot thereby be inferred, but only human
ignorance. The question here is not whether people need the light of the Holy
Spirit to understand Scripture, which we freely grant, but whether the
Scriptures are obscure to a faithful and enlightened person. Furthermore, it
is one thing to speak of theoretical illumination and another of practical
illumination; it is one thing to speak of the first degree and another of its
increases. Strictly speaking, David asks not for the former but for the
latter.

XIV. When Christ is said to have opened the disciples' minds so that they
might understand the Scriptures, Luke 24:45, this indeed signifies that a
person cannot apprehend the Scriptures by himself without the aid of grace.
But it does not immediately prove the obscurity of Scripture, nor can the
darkness which was in the disciples' minds be imputed to Scripture.

XV. It is one thing for there to be matters difficult to understand
(δυσνόητα) in Scripture, which are difficult to grasp; it is another for there
to be unintelligible matters (ἀνόητα), which cannot be understood no matter
how much diligence is applied. Peter says the former, 2 Peter 3:15, not the
latter. It is one thing for there to be some things difficult to understand
(δυσνόητα τινὰ), which we grant; it is another for all things to be difficult
to understand (δυσνόητα πάντα), which we deny. It is one thing for the manner
of delivery in Paul's epistles to be difficult to understand (δυσνόητα), which
we deny; it is another for the things themselves which are delivered to be
difficult to understand, which Peter signifies. For the relative pronoun οἷς
cannot refer to the word “epistles” (ἐπιστολαῖς), as Gagnaeus and Lorinus
acknowledge, but to the things being treated. It is one thing for matters to
be difficult to understand (δυσνόητα) to the unlearned and unstable, who by
unbelief and malice twist them to their own destruction, which we acknowledge
with Peter; it is another for them to be difficult to understand (δυσνόητα)
to believers, who humbly occupy themselves in searching them with the help of
the Holy Spirit.

XVI. No inference from the obscurity of certain parts of Scripture, such as
the prophecies and ancient oracles, to the obscurity of the whole of Scripture
is valid. For either those prophecies do not concern matters necessary for
salvation, or, if something obscure is handed down in them, it is plainly
explained elsewhere. Thus the closed and sealed book mentioned in Daniel 12:4
and Revelation 5:1 indeed teaches that prophecies are obscure before their
fulfillment, but it does not prove that the whole of Scripture is so obscure
that it cannot be understood by believers in matters necessary for salvation.

XVII. Although our knowledge through Scripture is obscure in comparison with

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 132 | IA leaf 186 | PDF p. 186]

the knowledge of glory, when we will no longer know through a mirror and in a
riddle but will see God face to face, 1 Corinthians 13:14, it does not follow
that Scripture is absolutely and in itself obscure with respect to the state
of this life. For its clarity is such as suffices for us in this life, since
through it, with unveiled face, we behold the glory of the Lord in the mirror
of the gospel, 2 Corinthians 3:18. 2. Paul speaks of enigmatic knowledge which
is common to all wayfarers, not even excepting himself. “Now,” he says, “we see
through a mirror.” But who would say that Scripture was obscure to Paul? The
passage therefore denotes only the imperfection of our knowledge in this life
and the difference between the revelation of grace and that of glory, not the
obscurity of Scripture.

XVIII. Although the Scriptures must be searched, John 5:39, it does not follow
that they are everywhere obscure, even in matters necessary for salvation. 1.
We do not say that they are perspicuous to everyone, but only to a person who
applies his mind and diligently investigates. Searching is therefore needed,
because Scripture is perspicuous only to one who searches. For even the
easiest things become obscure to those who read cursorily and inattentively.
2. We do not deny that Scripture has its inner sanctuaries and the depths
(βάθη) of its mysteries, which can be drawn out only by the greatest study and
diligent investigation. But this does not prevent very many other things from
presenting themselves, including things necessary for salvation, which readily
meet the eyes of believers.

XIX. Although the apostles could not sufficiently understand the resurrection
and ascension of Christ, John 16:18, it does not follow that Scripture was
obscure. For knowledge suited to the state in which each person lives and to
the doctrine being revealed is sufficient for each. The full revelation of
these matters was to occur only after the resurrection.

XX. One kind of knowledge of Scripture is literal and theoretical, by which
the words are understood according to the letter and grammatically. Another
is spiritual and practical, by which they are apprehended with true faith.
Many things occur in the Scriptures which are theoretically perspicuous even
to the natural person, and it is evident that profane people can dispute
ingeniously about the highest articles of faith. Practical knowledge,
however, belongs properly to believers, 1 Corinthians 2:14, 15; 2 Corinthians
4:3.

XXI. Whatever causes for the obscurity of Scripture on account of its manner
of delivery are alleged by our opponents, they cannot prove that it is so
obscure in matters necessary for salvation that it cannot be the complete
rule of faith and morals, and that to overcome those difficulties it is
necessary to resort to some infallible authority of the church and its
pretended tribunal. For besides the fact that we are never sent there, those
difficulties are not such that they cannot be overcome by applying study. And
the things which occur in those passages are either not necessary for
salvation or, if they are handed down more obscurely in one passage or
another, are explained more clearly elsewhere.

XXII. It is one thing to speak of the absolute obscurity of Scripture with
respect to all ages and states of the church; it is another to speak of
comparative obscurity in relation to a particular age of the church. We
acknowledge that the Scripture of the Old Testament is obscure comparatively,
with respect to the New Testament and in relation to the state and age of the
Christian church. But this does not prevent it from having been perspicuous in
itself and sufficiently so in view of the state of the Old Testament church
to which it was proposed.
