QUESTION XXV.

ON THE CALLING OF THE FIRST REFORMERS.

Was the calling of the first Reformers lawful? We affirm against the Romanists.

I. Among all the questions which deal with the calling of pastors, none is
raised more frequently by the Romanists or debated with greater contention
than the one concerning the calling of our Reformers. Their aim is to prosecute
them as guilty of schism and to condemn the Reformation instituted by them as
unlawful and undertaken without a calling. By this digression they wish to
draw us away from the heart of the matter and, after placing these obstacles
in the way, to turn the case elsewhere, so that they may evade the examination
of doctrine and escape more safely.

[Marginalia: The question about the Reformers’ calling is put in the wrong
order.]

II. But this manner of proceeding is altogether backward. (1) The question
between us and the Romanists concerns doctrine, not discipline. Yet even any
private person is permitted to discuss doctrine, ask questions about it, and
respond. (2) The question of calling cannot be understood without the other
question, since the first parts of a calling are the examination of doctrine
and the testing of the truth which is to be preached. (3) It is pointless to
inquire into the genuineness of a calling if the truth of the doctrine is
established, because faith does not depend upon calling; on the contrary,
calling depends upon faith. Faith is not true because those who preach it have
been lawfully called; on the contrary, those who retain and set forth true
doctrine are lawfully called. (4) This was the Pharisees’ manner of proceeding
with Christ when they inquired into his calling. “By what authority,” they
said, “do you do these things, and who gave you this authority?” (Matt. 21:23).
Christ answered them by questioning them in turn about the origin of John’s
baptism and doctrine, thereby sufficiently indicating that the question of
doctrine ought to precede the question of calling. Thus, if the adversaries
press us too insistently about where our forefathers obtained the authority to
teach otherwise than was customary, we in turn can reply with an appropriate
counterquestion and ask whether

[1847 vol. III printed p. 200 | IA leaf 234 | PDF p. 234]

the Reformers’ doctrine was from heaven or from men. If the former, why do they
oppose it? If the latter, why do they not demonstrate this? And if they cannot
show any difference between the doctrine of Christ and that of the Reformers,
why do they doubt the lawfulness of their calling? (5) This question can be
pressed with far greater right against the Romanists, who can show no trace in
Scripture of the calling of pontiffs, cardinals, and priests.

III. Although the question of the calling of pastors has no slight usefulness,
both with respect to the pastors themselves for their consolation and with
respect to the people for preserving good order and distinguishing lawful
pastors from false and surreptitious ones, its necessity falls far below the
necessity of the question of doctrine. The former has only the necessity of
order, without which salvation can be obtained, while the latter has the
necessity of a means, without which we do not attain salvation, since no
salvation can be hoped for without faith, which includes knowledge of saving
doctrine. Hence, if it is asked which of two assemblies we ought to join, one
of which is supposed to possess an uninterrupted succession but without truth,
while the other possesses the truth of doctrine but without succession, no one
will hesitate to answer that we must join the latter. A calling without truth
cannot save, but truth can save even without a calling.

IV. The adversaries wrongly suppose here that mission is the title by which we
claimed for ourselves the right to change the church. We had no title other
than the truth of doctrine. If it is false, whatever we did is unjust; but if
it is true, what we did could be done justly and lawfully. Nor should it be
replied that there is no hope of salvation for us if our church is false, and
that our church is false if our mission is false. A mission can be false in
one sense in its essentials and with respect to the doctrines proposed; in
another sense, a mission is called false and unlawful in its accidental
features and with respect to rites and ceremonies which could not be observed
in a disturbed state, but which are essential neither to the ministry nor to
the church and are not necessary to the salvation of those who believe. The
former proves a church false, but the latter does not, because it can be found
in a true church. Our mission is of this latter kind: it is defective not with
respect to doctrine, but only with respect to the rites then received.

[Marginalia: The validity of the Reformers’ calling is established.]

V. In order to vindicate our calling against the grossly unjust prejudices
with which it is customarily burdened, we must observe that the validity or
invalidity of a ministry must be considered especially in three respects: (1)
with respect to the functions which are to be performed and the things taught
and prescribed in it, whether they are true or false, just or unjust; (2) with
respect to the assembly in which it is exercised, whether that assembly has
the right of calling or not; and (3) with respect to the persons who exercise
the ministry in that assembly. In the first respect, the calling and ministry
of Jews, Muslims, and other unbelievers of that kind is impious and
sacrilegious, because the things taught in it are impious and false and its
functions unlawful. In the second respect, the ministry of the Donatists and
Luciferians was defective even though it was good in itself, because nothing
evil was taught in it; it was exercised in schismatic assemblies which had no
right to separate from others. In the third respect, the ministry of a
simoniac and usurper, although good in itself, does not cease to be evil and
unlawful because of a defect in personal calling. It is now easy to show that
the calling of our Reformers was lawful under this threefold relation. First,
with respect to the things taught or prescribed, the ministry which the
calling imposes upon us consists entirely in proclaiming the doctrine of
Christ and the apostles. If the adversaries deny this, we must proceed by way
of discussion, that is, to the examination of doctrine, and abandon the way of
prescription, that is, the question of calling, which they so insistently

[1847 vol. III printed p. 201 | IA leaf 235 | PDF p. 235]

press. In this way it will be easy for us to demonstrate the genuineness of
our calling from the truth of the doctrine we teach. But the Romanists will
never be able to prove a calling, or the right which they possess, to teach
transubstantiation, purgatory, the sacrifice of the Mass, the worship of
images, the invocation of saints, and other things of that kind which are
utterly contrary to the Word of God, because no calling can be given to teach
errors and superstitions. Second, the assembly in which the ministry is
exercised possessed the right to institute such a ministry: first, because
this ministerial authority and the right of communicating it to pastors belong
to a true church, as was seen in the preceding question; and next, because a
church in which Christ’s true doctrine is retained and errors and false forms
of worship are condemned cannot fail to be true, and we maintain that ours is
such a church. Third, with respect to the persons, they were not self-called
men who thrust themselves into the ministry on their own initiative. They had
a calling from the church to which that right belongs. Even if they were not
always called according to the rites then received, they were not therefore
destitute of a lawful calling in its essentials.

VI. To demonstrate the genuineness of the Reformers’ calling more
particularly, they must be considered in two classes. The first consists of
those who were called and ordained in the Roman church. The second consists of
those called by assemblies of believers which had no pastors. As for the
former, a lawful calling cannot be denied them unless the Romanists wish to
admit that they themselves lack a lawful calling. Their calling is either
lawful or it is not. If it is lawful, they cannot fault it in our people; if it
is not lawful, they do wrong to charge our people with lacking it.

VII. Nor can it be objected that they forfeited their calling by attacking the
doctrine received in the Roman church. They were so far from being rightly
said to have forfeited their calling for that reason that, on the contrary,
they directed it to its true and lawful end, the end intended by Christ. I
acknowledge that those who ordained them did not intend them to oppose the
received doctrine. Yet they had to heed Christ’s command and the original
obligation and nature of their offices. Two ends which occur here must be
carefully distinguished: one is the primary end of God, the Author of the
calling, and of the office itself; the other is the secondary and less
principal end of the people who issue the call. It is certain that the
Reformers did not regard the end of men, which was to defend and propagate
papal doctrine, because it was unjust. They instead regarded the primary end
of the office itself and of God who calls, which was to teach the truth and win
souls for Christ. Nor could the oath sworn to the pontiff stand in the way,
because they were bound to God first. Thus, when they discovered that they
could not in good faith fulfill the oath sworn to the pontiff without
violating the oath sworn to God, they were obliged to break faith with the
pontiff rather than break faith with God. Soldiers in a camp pledge loyalty to
their king and their commander, but first to the king and then to the commander
for the king’s sake. If they recognize that the commander has defected from
the sovereign and is plotting treason, by that very oath they are bound not
only to desert the commander but also to oppose him to the best of their
ability, unless they wish to incur the crime of treason themselves.

VIII. Here again one kind of calling must be distinguished from another. One
is false in its institution and unlawful in every way, and is directed
primarily to propagating impiety and idolatry. Another is holy and just in its
institution, but has degenerated through corruption by human abuse and is
directed by men to propagating error. The former must be rejected absolutely,
because there is nothing good in it. The latter must be corrected and
purified, so that once the errors and corruptions introduced by men are
removed, Christ’s institution and its use alone may remain according to God’s
intention. Such is the calling of bishops and presbyters in the Roman church:
it was good with respect to God’s institution but became evil with respect to
human abuse. Hence the cutting away of errors and corruptions

[1847 vol. III printed p. 202 | IA leaf 236 | PDF p. 236]

introduced by men could not be the abolition of the calling, but its
correction and restoration. For it should always have tended to this end, that
the person called discharge his office religiously and holily according to
God’s institution. Although all others might turn that office to another end,
if he listened to God’s voice and his own conscience, he was nevertheless
bound to search out the truth, embrace it once known, teach it publicly, drive
away error, and procure the salvation of the flock, because the institution of
every ministry must always be traced back to its source and primary author.
God himself, whom we must obey rather than men, demands this. The church’s
first intention and aim, which ought to have been nothing other than preaching
the truth and rejecting error, requires it. The necessity of the case itself
and the nature of calling require it. Hence anyone canonically ordained ought
to use his calling to propagate the doctrine of the church in which he
received it if that doctrine accords with the truth; if not, he ought to use
his calling to attack it.

IX. Although the Reformers were excommunicated by the Roman church, they cannot
therefore be said to have forfeited the calling which they had received,
because the excommunication was unjust and could not take away their right.
The apostles similarly did not lose their calling because they were
excommunicated by the Jewish Sanhedrin, nor did the orthodox bishops who were
excommunicated by the Arians, especially since, on the Romanists’ own
hypothesis, holy orders imprint an indelible character. This very point is
decreed in Gratian, Cause 24, chapter 35. Cœlestine says, “If anyone has been
excommunicated or deprived of either episcopal or clerical dignity by Bishop
Nestorius or by others who follow him, from the time when they began to preach
such things, it is evident that he both has remained and does remain in our
communion. We do not judge him removed, because the judgment of one who had
already shown that he himself ought to be removed could not remove anyone.”
This is confirmed in chapter 36.

X. Although we hold that there was a true calling in the Roman church, we do
not therefore acknowledge that it was a true church, because these things do
not correspond to each other equally. Where there is a true church, there is
undoubtedly a true calling, but not conversely. The witness next prints, “Where
there is a true calling, there is a true church,” although the argument plainly
requires “there is not necessarily a true church,” because the profession of
Christianity suffices for a true calling, and such a profession can exist in a
false and heretical church. The truth of a church can no more be inferred from
its calling than from baptism, which, it is agreed, can be true even in a
heretical church. Thus mission can exist among those who are not a true church
but retain something of the church, because mission does not arise from the
church as its source and principle, but from God through human beings, even
evil ones. Thus it makes no difference to the efficacy of sowing and planting
whether it is done with clean or dirty hands, provided that the seed is good
and the soil fertile. Augustine says in book 3 *On Baptism*, chapter 10, “The
light of a lamp or the sun is not polluted even if it passes through filthy
places. It matters little whether water is carried through a stone channel or
a silver one.” And in chapter 11 he says, “The Word of God is preached
effectively even by the wicked.” A calling nevertheless remained in the
idolatrous Jewish church. The Arians and Nestorians were not a true church,
yet they called many bishops who were received by the orthodox as lawful
without any new calling and acknowledged as true pastors. See Socrates, book
2, chapter 12; Sozomen, book 3, chapters 4, 7, and 9; and Theodoret, book 2,
chapters 13 and 31. Nor should it be said with Perron that such men were
rehabilitated, because their restoration consisted in nothing other than
confessing the truth and acknowledging error.

XI. The second class of Reformers consists of those who undertook this work
although they were not called by the Roman church. Their calling is the one in
question. We must recall here what we warned before: a constituted church must
be distinguished from a church being constituted or reformed, and the ordinary
way from a case of supreme necessity. In a constituted church we hold that the
established order

[1847 vol. III printed p. 203 | IA leaf 237 | PDF p. 237]

must be retained, so that all things may be done decently in the church and
disorder and confusion avoided. But in a church being restored, an ordinary
calling must not always be awaited. In a case of supreme and inescapable
necessity, any private person can undertake the work of reformation.

XII. We assume that such a case existed, and we are prepared to demonstrate it
from the state of the Roman church, which was utterly depraved with respect to
faith, worship, and tyranny. This was so in the very people who ought to have
governed the church and opposed errors; having been turned into wolves, they
were ravaging the Lord’s flock and attempting to drag the church with
themselves into the same precipice of error. Who could have expected
reformation from them? Therefore, every believer had a sufficient calling to
undertake the work. Although they had no authority from the Roman church to
preach the gospel, nevertheless the circumstances of those most disturbed
times, the indispensable necessity incumbent upon everyone to promote his own
salvation, and the law of love which commands us to procure the salvation of
our neighbors gave them authority to preach the gospel purely, reject the
Romanists’ errors, recall people from them, gather together those recalled,
institute sacred assemblies, and elect successors for themselves by the power
granted to them for this purpose by the converted people. Thus it is lawful
for good citizens, even if private persons, to rise against a treasonous
magistrate and to close the gates when an enemy approaches. This is all the
more true because, as was proved before, the right of calling pastors properly
belongs to the church, in whose name it is exercised by pastors when they are
present. When they are absent, however, the church can use the same right in
another way. If it has lost its pastors, it has not immediately lost its right;
and if it cannot exercise that right through ministers, it can exercise it by
itself or through someone else to whom it has entrusted it. It is not only the
church’s right but also its duty to prevent the ministry from ceasing, since
Christ instituted it not for a certain time but in perpetuity until the end of
the age as a means of faith and salvation (Matt. 28:20; Eph. 4:11–12).

XIII. We infer from various things that God’s calling truly intervened in this
ministry of the Reformers. First, it is inferred from necessity. When God wills
to cast people into such a condition that they must perish miserably unless
they provide in this way for their own salvation and that of their neighbors,
the one who permits them to be reduced to that necessity is also considered to
give them the power to discharge this duty rather than permit truth to be
removed from the world and their own salvation and that of others to be
endangered. It is evident that the Reformers were placed in such a state of
inescapable necessity. They saw the Roman church laboring under innumerable
deadly corruptions, which they could not profess without an immediate danger
to salvation. No reformation could be expected from the rulers of the church,
from whom the errors flowed and who fought for retaining them as though for
hearth and home. So far were they from wishing to consider reformation that
they pursued with sword and flame those who ventured to seek it and dared to
oppose the rampant errors. Added to this was God’s own voice, which imposed
this necessity: both in the general command for every kind of person in every
time and place to follow and confess the truth and to refute falsehood, and in
the particular command to come out of Babylon (Rev. 18) and withdraw from
communion with those in error (2 Cor. 6:16–17).

XIV. Second, we infer it from the character of calling impressed upon them,
which consists not only in purity of doctrine and innocence of life, but also
in the eminent and extraordinary gifts with which God adorned his servants,
and in the hidden impulse and heroic movement by which he stirred them to
undertake so great a work. For if God bestows eminent gifts upon no one except
when he calls him to something great and arduous, it cannot be denied that God
imposed such a calling upon the first Reformers, since it is evident that they
were furnished with extraordinary gifts. They were not

[1847 vol. III printed p. 204 | IA leaf 238 | PDF p. 238]

entirely miraculous and supernatural gifts like those in the apostolic church,
for those belonged to the founding of the church. Yet they were singular and
extraordinary insofar as they rose far above the measure and standard of those
times, when a darkness of errors and vices deeper than Cimmerian darkness
occupied the church’s heaven and the minds of its rulers. Who does not marvel
at their profound learning, the exceptional keenness of their judgment, their
most ardent zeal, admirable faith, invincible constancy, intense love, singular
integrity of life and morals, and the countless other gifts by which they
shone above the rest and proved themselves vessels of election set apart by
God for this extraordinary work? These were indeed authentic seals of their
divine calling.

XV. Third, we infer it from the marvelous and truly astonishing success which
the Lord gave to their labors. By this they not only began so great and arduous a
work but also advanced and finally completed it, notwithstanding the countless
efforts, stratagems, violence, frauds, cruelty, and most savage persecutions of
their resisting adversaries. In generously enduring and overcoming all these,
they demonstrated invincible strength of soul. God’s marvelous providence
showed itself so conspicuously in beginning and advancing this work that no
one can fail to see it unless he wills to be blind. Therefore, since this most
difficult work was accomplished beyond everyone’s expectation and has endured
from that time amid so many obstacles and contradictions, it cannot be doubted
that it was truly divine, according to Gamaliel’s statement in Acts 5, and that
the men who devoted their labor to it with such zeal and effort were stirred
to it by a divine calling.

XVI. The calling of the Reformers can be called ordinary and extraordinary in
different respects. It was ordinary: (1) with respect to the office, because
the ministry was not new and extraordinary, as the apostleship was, but was
that same ordinary office instituted by Christ and the apostles which ought to
continue until the end of the age; (2) with respect to doctrine, because they
were not to set forth a new doctrine but the same one already preached by the
apostles, nor to erect a new church but to reform the corrupted one, correct
depraved worship, and recall it to the original institution of Christ and the
apostles; (3) with respect to the perpetual and indispensable right belonging
to believers to profess the truth and reject errors, and with respect to the
duty which, by Christ’s command, concerns both pastors and believers themselves
to follow Christ and withdraw from false teachers; and (4) with respect to its
matter and the ordinary functions of the ministry, namely, preaching the Word
and administering the sacraments. But the calling is rightly termed
extraordinary with respect to the manner and rites customarily observed,
because it was not performed in the ordinary manner used in the Roman church,
but beyond the received order and rites on account of a case of supreme
necessity. (5) It was also extraordinary with respect to its exercise. Although
reformation was an ordinary function of their office, for which they needed no
new right or new ministry, since every pastor is bound to reform his flock
whenever there is need, nevertheless this is not done every day. There was
therefore something extraordinary in this work insofar as the people needed
extraordinary and unusual help to cleanse doctrine and worship of the
corruptions clinging to them. Thus, since this calling can rightly be termed
both ordinary and extraordinary according to its various relations, it should
not seem strange that our writers speak in different ways according to those
different respects, some calling it ordinary and others extraordinary in one
respect and another, while the truth of the matter always remains the same. If
“ordinary” is properly understood as what agrees with the order originally and
divinely instituted, the Reformers’ calling is rightly called ordinary. But if
the term is taken equivocally as an expression

[1847 vol. III printed p. 205 | IA leaf 239 | PDF p. 239]

for what has been publicly received through ingrained custom, whatever that
custom may be, the calling can be termed extraordinary because it differed
greatly from the custom and practice which had become established in the Roman
church. But we deny that this custom is a lawful order, since it is mere
disorder rampant in that church under the appearance of order.

XVII. Not every extraordinary calling must be confirmed by miracles. Various
prophets and John the Baptist, who were called extraordinarily, performed no
miracles. Only a calling termed extraordinary by reason of a new doctrine or
new office must be so confirmed, as were the callings of Moses and the
apostles, because they were making the old worship obsolete and instituting a
new one. But when the same doctrine delivered before is retained and purified,
miracles are not needed, because the same miracles by which it was confirmed
before still serve to confirm it. Such was the calling of the Reformers, who
did not introduce a new doctrine but cleansed Christ’s doctrine of the errors
with which men had corrupted it. Hence Gregory says in homily 29 on the
Gospels: “Those signs were necessary at the church’s beginning. For the
multitude, so that it might grow toward faith, had to be nurtured by miracles.
When we plant shrubs, we likewise pour water upon them until we see that they
have taken hold in the ground; once they have fixed their roots, watering will
cease.” Thus miracles are required when worship is being instituted, but not
when it is only being restored; when the church is to be newly erected and
founded, but not when it is only being reformed and cleansed of its filth. Add
that the marvelous success which God gave to the Reformers’ labors had the
character of a miracle, by which God openly made known that such a calling,
which accomplished so great a work, could not have proceeded from anyone but
himself.

XVIII. It is one thing to institute a new ministry and another to institute
new ministers. God alone can do the former, because he alone is Lord of
religion with power to change it at will; but the church can do the latter in
any condition, because Christ gave it this right, which cannot be taken away
from it for any reason. It is one thing to erect a new ministry and another to
reform a ministry already instituted but corrupted. The church has no right
to do the former, but it has the right and indeed is bound by duty to carry out
the latter. Just as it is bound to preserve a pure ministry, it is also bound
to reform that ministry when it has been corrupted.

XIX. Usurpation of the ministry is one thing; use of a lawful right granted by
God is another. Usurpation of the ministry, which occurs without any right, is
always unjust and unlawful, but the use of a right cannot be unjust. The
Reformers cannot be called usurpers, because the church at all times has the
right to call pastors for its edification, even if all the rites received at
other times cannot be employed. Thus, if it happens that pastors already
appointed fail in their duty and badly abuse their ministry, the church, for
whose sake the ministry was instituted, always has the right to cleanse the
corrupted ministry. And if it cannot do this because of human obstinacy, it has
the right to abandon that ministry and elect other ministers who discharge
their office properly.

XX. The calling which the Reformers received from the Roman church was
antichristian with respect to the ministers who ordained them, but Christian
and lawful with respect to God as its author and its primary institution. It
must not be referred back to the pope, but to God, the Author of the calling.

XXI. The Reformers had nothing in common with the Novatians and Donatists, who
made a rash withdrawal from the church without necessity because of personal
acts. Our people, by contrast, undertook reformation for the weightiest reasons
concerning the corruption of doctrine itself.

XXII. While the ministry flourishes in the church, the church must indeed use
it in calling pastors, nor can it ordinarily institute pastors except through
the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 206 | IA leaf 240 | PDF p. 240]

ministry already constituted. But when the ministry is lacking or miserably
corrupted, the church can elect ministers for its own edification even without
the involvement of the ministry, both because it possesses this right from God
and because it is bound at every time and place to preserve the ministry for
the instruction of believers. Nor can it be said without the gravest absurdity
that, in a case of necessity when all pastors have failed, it is better for the
church to remain without pastors and lack external and public worship and the
exercise of religion while waiting for God to raise up others beyond the
ordinary order, than to call pastors without the involvement of other pastors.
For the necessity that the ministry give a calling is only a necessity of
order, which ought to be observed in an established state but is not absolutely
and without qualification necessary to salvation. The necessity of preaching
the gospel and of calling pastors to it, however, is a necessity of salvation,
which cannot be obtained without the Word and faith in it. Add that since the
end must be preferred to the means, the institution of pastors, which is the
end, must be considered more necessary than observing the received order that
it be done only through pastors, which is merely a means of attaining that end.
And since primary obligations ought to prevail over later ones, who doubts
that the law which imposes the necessity of the ministry upon the church binds
far more strictly than the law which wills that no one be ordained except
through the ministry of other pastors? The latter is a particular law which
holds only in an established order and while its use is possible, and it has
its exceptions. The former is a universal law which must hold at every time
and place without any exception. Hence, when it is impossible to observe both
laws, account must certainly be taken of the first, which is earlier and
universal and is the cause and foundation of the second.

XXIII. Although God has not said expressly that in extraordinary cases
pastoral power may be communicated in some way other than through the ordinary
ministry, it does not follow that this cannot be done. The ministry’s
institution once made in the church, which ought to continue until the end of
the age, gives the church sufficient right always to preserve it, reform it,
and erect it anew if it has been corrupted and extinguished, so that no new
command is needed for this. Thus the command which God gave the church and
believers to preserve the truth obliges the church to reform itself when it
discovers that it has fallen from the truth. The same command embraces both
things: the preservation of truth and the restoration of truth when corrupted.

XXIV. (1) The reordination of those who now come to us from the papacy does not
prove that their calling was nonexistent, but only that it was corrupted and
defective and therefore must be purified, so that what is impious and
antichristian may be separated from what is good and Christian. Nor, if such a
calling could suffice for a church being reformed because no other could be
obtained, must it suffice in a church already reformed. (2) This reordination
is not so much an absolutely new ordination as a reformation and purification
of the former one, so that what was good in it may be confirmed and what was
evil corrected. In this way the church may be assured for its own edification
of the purity of the doctrine and morals of the person called and of the
consent of other pastors. Add that when entering a new society upon which they
were to depend, it was equitable that they receive a mandate and mission from
it. (3) Since ordination is not a sacrament, there is no harm in repeating it;
indeed, it contributes considerably to the church’s edification. Nothing
prevents its repetition, nor does a second consecration detract from the
validity of the first. Rather, it confirms the first in those things which are
good. An example occurs in Acts 13:2–3, where hands are laid again upon Paul
and Barnabas, who had already been called.

XXV. Just as it would be absurd in civil society to ask what calling a person
has to live, conduct his affairs, and flee whatever

[1847 vol. III printed p. 207 | IA leaf 241 | PDF p. 241]

is harmful to health or safety, so it is absurd in religious society to ask
what right believers possess and with what calling they have been furnished
to profess the true faith, worship God purely, reject whatever is contrary to
the truth of faith and the purity of worship, and reject what can harm their
spiritual life and salvation. The obligation by which everyone is bound to
promote his own salvation is sufficient, and the very nature of the matter and
God’s command impose it upon us. I acknowledge that this cannot be done without
breaking the bond of communion by which he was joined in society with others,
but this applies only with respect to error, not with respect to truth. Nor
should it be thought that the church’s true unity is dissolved, because the
assembly from which the withdrawal is made is no longer to be regarded as the
church of Christ, but as an assembly of people in error who, by their deadly
doctrines and false forms of worship, were the first to rupture the church’s
true unity.

XXVI. Nor should it be said that private persons cannot withdraw from the
public ministry without violating the obedience which God himself more than
once commanded to be rendered to it. Although no one denies that pastors and
faithful ministers of God who watch over our souls ought to be held in great
esteem among us, and that they are to be obeyed according to Paul’s command
(Heb. 13:17), it is nevertheless certain that this obedience and dependence is
not absolute and unlimited, which belongs to God and Christ alone, but is
confined within certain limits: insofar as it conduces to God’s glory and
salvation, and insofar as it can coexist with the faithfulness and obedience
owed to Christ. For the public ministry is nothing other than an external
means of leading people to salvation through the profession of the true faith
and the practice of pure worship. External means are such that when they depart
from the purpose of the one using them and not only fail to lead us to the end
but remove us from it, love for the end ought to prevail over love for the
means, because means are desired only for the sake of the end. If it is
established that the public ministry not only fails to lead us to salvation
and show us the way to heaven but also drives us to certain ruin through deadly
errors, who doubts that we must withdraw from it in order to provide for our
salvation? Nor can the example of a civil magistrate, who is not to be deserted
even if he performs his office less properly, prove the contrary. Only a
temporal good, which brings no harm to salvation, is at issue there; but the
ministry concerns a spiritual good and the salvation of our soul, than which
nothing ought to be dearer to us. Nor should it therefore be said that this is
to resist God himself, who subjected us to pastors. In the ministry we must
distinguish especially what belongs to divine institution from what belongs to
human arrangement. The existence of a ministry in the church is a matter of
divine institution, but which particular people exercise that ministry, apart
from the apostles and evangelists, the church’s first teachers, is a matter of
human arrangement. The order of the ministry is inviolable because it is from
God, but the case of ministers is not the same. For just as they are called
through human beings, so their calling can often be corrupted by various
faults either of those who give it or of those who receive it. In that case it
is not only lawful but necessary to withdraw from false pastors who endanger
salvation. Nor is the scandal which can arise from such a separation, if any
does arise, to be equated with the loss of salvation and the injury done to
religion.

XXVII. This is confirmed even more strongly if the nature of the union which
believers have with Christ and with their pastors is considered. The union of
believers with Christ is immediate, but their union with pastors is only
mediate, because believers are not united to their pastors except in Christ
and for his sake, since he is the center and bond of our communion both with
believers and with pastors. Hence it follows that believers ought to be united
to their pastors only insofar as it is evident that the pastors are united to
Christ, and that they ought to separate from them whenever they see them
separated from Christ and wishing to call others away from him. The same

must be said of the dependence which believers ought to have upon both Christ
and their pastors. The former is immediate and absolute, but the latter only
mediate and conditional. Christ alone has a right over the conscience as the
supreme and unaccountable Prince. Pastors are ministers and interpreters of his
will. Therefore, the dependence and submission owed to them rests entirely
upon the dependence which they owe to Christ, which is its cause and rule. As
long as pastors show themselves true ministers of God, believers ought to
depend upon them for Christ’s sake. But if it happens that they conduct
themselves as lords rather than ministers and lead people away from Christ
rather than to him, and if our dependence upon them requires violating the
dependence and obedience owed to Christ, who will deny that we must most justly
and indeed indispensably withdraw from them so that our union with Christ may
remain safe and unimpaired?

XXVIII. Innovators who set forth a new and false doctrine are one thing;
Reformers whose aim is not to introduce a new doctrine but to reform the old
doctrine which had been corrupted and cleanse it of superimposed errors are
another. The former must not be heard, according to Paul’s command (Gal. 1:8).
But the latter, if we are assured that they are true Reformers, are not only
not to be rejected but are to be embraced and followed with zeal. To know
whether this is so, we must come to an examination of doctrine. From the
conformity of their doctrine with Christ’s doctrine, we maintain that our
first pastors were such Reformers and assert that they are most falsely
denounced as innovators.

XXIX. The passage of Tertullian in chapter 37 of *On the Prescription of
Heretics*—“Who are you? When and from where did you come? What are you who are
not mine doing on my property?” and later, “I am the heir of the apostles. As
they provided in their testament and as they bound by oath, I possess”—is
rightly opposed to innovators who introduce a new doctrine different from the
doctrine of the apostles. Concerning them he immediately adds, “Whence are
heretics strangers and enemies to the apostles, except from the difference of
doctrine which each person at his own discretion either introduced or received
against the apostles?” But the passage does nothing against our Reformers,
whose only proposed end was to lead us back to the truth of the gospel and the
purity of apostolic doctrine from which the Roman church had fallen.

XXX. The examples drawn from the practice of the apostles concerning the
calling and ordination of pastors do not oppose us, because they concern a
church already ordered, in which the apostles willed the order they had
instituted in the presbyteries to be observed. But we are speaking of a
corrupted church which had no pastors except corrupted ones infected with
manifold errors.

XXXI. If in some churches the Reformation was instituted by laypeople, it was
nevertheless the case that in that most deplorable state and in a situation of
inescapable necessity each person had a sufficient calling to resist the abuse,
as was proved before and confirmed by various examples. To these belongs also
the old canon in Clement of Rome, book 8 of the *Constitutions*, chapter 38,
which decrees: “Let one who teaches, though he be a layperson, nevertheless
teach if he is skilled in speech and upright in morals, because all will be
taught by God.” If anything could have been lacking here, it was supplied both
by the subsequent ordination and by the public authority of the magistrate and
the consent of the people.
