# CHAPTER XXII

## The fourth precept of the Decalogue is moral

The questions raised between us and our opponents concerning the fourth
precept of the Decalogue can be reduced to two principal heads. The first
concerns the nature of the precept itself, and the other concerns the thing
commanded by this precept. As to the first, there are three different views.
Some hold that this precept is moral. The opponents, on the contrary, hold
that it is purely ceremonial. Finally, others hold that this precept is partly
moral and partly ceremonial. This view is maintained not only by some of the
Anti-Sabbatarians but also by others who speak more orthodoxly about the
Sabbath, although, as we shall hear, they explain the matter in a somewhat
different manner.

We embrace and maintain the first view. To confirm it, we first set down the
following observations.

*First*, when we say that this precept is moral, the meaning is not that it is
equal in every respect to the other precepts of the Decalogue with regard to
its subject matter or the quality of the thing. Although it is equal with
regard to obligation, having been imposed by the same authority as the other
nine, its matter is nevertheless not of the same quality as that of the other
precepts of the first table, just as certain other commands of the second
table are not of the same quality either.

*Next*, we must distinguish between the substance of the command itself and
the appendages attached to the command. The scholastics, with Thomas (I-II,
question 99), say that the ceremonial law determined the moral law in relation
to God, while the judicial law determined that same moral law in relation to
human beings in their civil condition or society, and that each did so by
accommodating it to that state of the church. It is therefore evident that
laws which are unquestionably moral can have certain ceremonial or judicial
appendages attached to them. Nevertheless, the nature of the precept is not
to be judged according to these appendages. That is, we cannot on their
account say that the precept itself is ceremonial or judicial. When we speak
of this fourth precept, however, we mean the substance of the precept itself
and do not consider those appendages, whatever they may finally be. For the
nature of a precept must be judged from its own substance, not from appendages
of this kind. Otherwise the greatest confusion will arise in rightly
explaining and distinguishing divine laws.

*Third*, one must also distinguish between the literal sense of a precept and
a certain general equity deduced from it. For a certain general and moral
equity can also be deduced from at least some ceremonial and political
precepts, yet those ceremonial and judicial precepts must not therefore be
called moral. The opponents themselves admit that ceremonial and judicial
laws contain within themselves something belonging to *moral right*, or, as
Primerose says in part 2, chapter 6, section 21, that they *are ceremonial and
temporary in what they express, and moral with respect to their foundation
and end*. He says that Deuteronomy 25:4, Exodus 23:19, and Deuteronomy 22:

[Printed p. 217 | IA PDF 225 | IA leaf 225 | IA decausadei02brow]

6, 7, 10 are of this kind. The nature of the precept itself must not be judged
from this general equity. Notwithstanding that equity, a precept in itself
and according to its literal sense remains political or ceremonial. When we
speak of the nature of the fourth precept, we take it in its literal sense,
not according to that general equity or that general foundation.

*Fourth*, one must distinguish between the fourth precept of the Decalogue and
the Sabbath of the seventh day in order, for these questions differ greatly:
Is the Sabbath of the seventh day, or the last day of the week, moral? And is
the fourth precept moral? The former can be and has been abolished, another
Sabbath, or another Sabbath day, having now been substituted, as will be made
clear in its proper place. The fourth precept, however, is not thereby
abolished, because in its literal sense it does not primarily enjoin the
Sabbath of the seventh day, as will be explained later.

The question, therefore, is whether the fourth precept of the Decalogue is
moral in its literal sense. Our opponents deny it; we affirm it and prove it
by the following arguments.

*Argument 1.* The definition of the moral law given and explained in book 1,
chapter 10, applies to this precept. Therefore the thing defined applies as
well. See the beginning of book 3, chapter 29.

*Argument 2.* The thing commanded by this precept is moral. Therefore the
command itself is moral. The consequence cannot be denied. The antecedent is
abundantly proved above in book 3, chapters 23 and 24. We suppose—and the
opponents do not deny—that this fourth command concerns the weekly Sabbath. In
the same place we proved by twenty arguments that the command concerning the
weekly Sabbath is moral. There is no reason to repeat those arguments here.

*Argument 3.* This fourth precept shared in all those privileges from which we
proved above, in chapter 7 of this book, that the Decalogue is the moral law.
Therefore it too is moral.

*Argument 4.* Above, in chapter 8, we proved that the Decalogue is the moral
law, and the fourth precept is an integral part of that Decalogue. Otherwise
it would contain only nine precepts rather than ten, as we shall hear.
Therefore it too is moral. See all those arguments which can be applied here.

*Argument 5.* The opponents themselves, as we saw above, concede that the
Decalogue contains the moral law, or the law of nature. They must therefore
concede that this fourth precept is moral. For if any one of these ten precepts
is ceremonial, the Decalogue will not be a compendium of the moral law but
will contain within itself other laws which are not moral.

*Objection.* Although all moral precepts are contained in the Decalogue and
every precept of the Decalogue is moral, it does not follow that everything
within each of these precepts is moral.

*Response 1.* If the precept itself is moral, everything that belongs to the
substance of the command must be moral. Whatever is attached to the command
but does not belong to the substance of the command is of no concern to us,
for it does not affect or alter the nature of the command itself and, properly
speaking, cannot be said to be contained in that command. The fifth precept of
the Decalogue had an appendage which, as the opponents themselves think, was
not moral. What then? Must it therefore be said that this precept was not
moral? Something peculiar and belonging to the Jews with respect to the

[Printed p. 218 | IA PDF 226 | IA leaf 226 | IA decausadei02brow]

letter was attached to the Decalogue. Must the whole Decalogue therefore be
called ceremonial and not moral? 2. But what is that ceremonial thing attached
to the fourth precept? Is it what the words clearly express? If so, the very
substance of the command will be ceremonial, not moral. Then, I ask, what is
that thing to which this is attached?

*Argument 6.* Our theologians use the following argument against the Roman
Catholics to prove that the second precept of the Decalogue is moral: namely,
that it was promulgated from the fire before the Israelite people by God's own
mouth and inscribed on tablets of stone by God's finger, no less than the other
moral precepts of the law; and that it is part of the Decalogue, the whole of
which must be retained in the church so that nothing is taken from it. See
Wendelin, *Major System*, book 2, chapter 4, thesis 11, page 1487 in my copy.
See the same points in Polanus, *Syntagma*, book 6, chapter 10, column 1130 in
my copy. If, therefore, this is a valid argument for the *second* precept of
the Decalogue against the Roman Catholics, why will it not also be valid for
the *fourth* precept against the Anti-Sabbatarians?

*Argument 7.* If this fourth precept is not moral, what reason can be devised
for its having a place in the Decalogue? We can press the Roman Catholics who
deny the *second precept* with this argument. Why not also our opponents who
deny the *fourth*?

The opponents devise various reasons why God gave this precept a place in the
Decalogue even if it was not moral but ceremonial. We shall now briefly
examine them.

*Objection 1.* [This precept is placed in the Decalogue because of what is
moral in it.]

*Response 1.* What they call moral in this precept is either the very
substance of the command; the general equity deduced from it and following the
thing commanded; or something attached while the substance of the command
remains whole. If the first is said, we have what we seek: namely, that the
substance of this command is moral. If the second is said, there was no more
reason for this precept to be placed in the Decalogue than for the many other
ceremonial and judicial laws from which some general equity can easily be
deduced. If the third is said, I would have them explain to us what that moral
appendage is by reason of which it obtained a place in the Decalogue. 2. Were
there not other non-moral precepts that also had appendages of this kind
attached to them, although they did not obtain a place in the Decalogue?

*Objection 2.* The precept concerning the Sabbath is inserted in the
Decalogue because Sabbaths were signs not of certain particular benefits, as
other festivals were, but of a more general benefit: namely, the creation of
the world.

*Response 1.* I would like to know where the weekly Sabbath is called a sign
of creation. 2. The sign of a benefit common to everyone cannot be called a
ceremony properly so called. This precept, therefore, will not be ceremonial.
Indeed, 3. because the reason of this precept is universal and concerns
absolutely everyone, this precept itself will be moral.

*Objection 3.* God wished this command to be inserted in the Decalogue so that
he might thereby indicate the perpetual necessity of ceremonies in the
church, although the very ceremony enjoined by this precept was abolished in
the death of Christ.

*Response 1.* By those ceremonies are meant either *divine* ceremonies—that
is, instituted by God—or *human* ceremonies—that is, devised by human beings.
The latter can be of two kinds: either those which concern mere order and
decorum, or those which form a part of divine worship. If divine ceremonies

[Printed p. 219 | IA PDF 227 | IA leaf 227 | IA decausadei02brow]

are meant, why would this precept be placed in the Decalogue for this purpose,
when the second precept of the Decalogue had sufficiently indicated this? For
when the Lord indicated in the second precept of the Decalogue that he was to
be worshiped outwardly, he also made manifest the necessity of ceremonies. If
the latter kind of human ceremonies is meant here, this precept cannot
indicate the necessity of such things, because they are utterly unlawful and
openly conflict with the second precept of the Decalogue. For that precept
condemns all *will-worship* and commands that God be worshiped only with the
worship he himself instituted, as we have seen. Finally, if the former kind of
human ceremonies is meant, this cannot be said, because this fourth precept
does not command something that concerns mere order and decorum, but something
that formed a part of divine worship. Moreover, the second precept had already
indicated the necessity of ceremonies of this kind clearly enough. 2. By what
right is it assumed here that the thing commanded by this fourth precept was
something purely ceremonial? 3. Are political laws not also necessary? Why,
then, did God not also insert some purely political precept among the moral
precepts of the Decalogue in order to declare this necessity? 4. How could a
ceremony destined to pass away indicate the perpetuity of ceremonies, rather
than the cessation of all ceremonies?

*Objection 4.* The Sabbath procured the observance of the other moral laws
more effectively than all other ceremonies.

*Response 1.* It therefore differs greatly from ceremonies and has a greater
connection and affinity with moral laws than the other ceremonies have. 2.
Did not certain political laws procure the observance of moral laws more
effectively than others? Why is such a law not inserted in the Decalogue? 3.
Can it not rather be concluded from this that this precept is moral, since
this usefulness has no less place now than it had formerly and, in view of
this usefulness, the Sabbath is no less necessary now than it formerly was?

*Objection 5.* The reason this fourth precept appears in the Decalogue is that
it is not purely ceremonial but mixed, partly ceremonial and partly moral,
and is inserted into the Decalogue because of what is moral in it.

*Response 1.* Is the substance of this precept, or its literal sense, partly
moral and partly ceremonial? I would like this to be shown. 2. The precepts
concerning new moons and other festivals possessed a general equity and
indicated that some time must necessarily be appointed for celebrating the
worship of God. This is the only thing our opponents hold to be moral in the
fourth precept. On that account, the former precepts could no less have been
called partly moral and consequently inserted into the Decalogue than the
precept concerning the Sabbath. We shall see later, however, whether this
precept is mixed.

*Objection 6.* Dr. Burman attacks our argument as follows in his *Vindication*,
pages 51–52: [*But they think it moral because it is placed among the moral
precepts of the Decalogue, as if God were not allowed to place his precepts in
whatever order he pleased; or as if he had said that each of those ten words
was plainly one of ten moral words; or as if we denied that there was
something moral in the Sabbath. Yet nothing prevents the Decalogue from being
a compendium and epitome only of the moral law, since it is a compendium, a
kind of system, of the whole Mosaic law or entire covenant initiated by God
through Moses with the Israelites, to which the ceremonial and*

[Printed p. 220 | IA PDF 228 | IA leaf 228 | IA decausadei02brow]

*political laws pertain. Thus we read in Exodus 24 that the book in which
Moses had written many laws, both ceremonial and moral, is called the book of
the covenant in verse 7, and in verse 8: Behold the blood of the covenant which
God has made with you concerning these words, although those words were not
all moral. See also, I ask, Exodus 34:10–27. Since, therefore, God made a
covenant with the Israelites according to the tenor of both kinds of laws, it
was fair that at least one ceremonial law, which would be a compendium of the
rest, should be placed among so many moral laws expressed in the Decalogue on
the tablets of the covenant.*]

The distinguished Dr. Essenius abundantly refuted this in his *Vindication*,
pages 360 and 261, as follows: [*Response 1. Indeed, we maintain that the
fourth precept is moral, also for this reason: that it was placed among the
precepts of the Decalogue, which are moral. 2. God is answerable to no one and
is absolutely free to place his precepts in whatever order he pleases. Yet in
the meantime he teaches us to recognize as distinct those things which God
was pleased clearly and wisely to distinguish for our distinct instruction.
3. God indeed did not expressly say that those ten words were moral, just as
he did not expressly say that the first, second, third, fourth, and so forth
were moral. But he distinguished those ten from the other precepts clearly
enough and adorned them with certain singular prerogatives from which their
moral character is gathered. Thus God himself pronounced them before the
whole people from the midst of the fire and among thunderings. He himself
inscribed them twice on tablets of stone with his finger, and wished those
tablets to be placed in the ark of the covenant, whereas the other precepts
were promulgated through Moses. Moreover, the inscription on the tablets of
stone corresponds to the inscription on our hearts in 2 Corinthians 3:3, 7.
It follows that those same precepts once engraved on tablets of stone also
pertain to the New Testament and are now inscribed on our hearts by the Holy
Spirit. 4. The covenant God made through Moses with spiritual Israel truly
was the covenant of grace. Yet at the same time the covenant of works with its
curses was repeated and pressed upon them. See Leviticus 18:5; 26:14–39;
Deuteronomy 27:14–26; 28:15, and so forth, so that carnal and reprobate people
might be convicted all the more, while the elect might be humbled in
themselves and compelled to seek and embrace grace in Christ. 5. Thus the
moral law, which is the matter of the covenant of works, was then committed to
writing and handed down, but at the same time as a pattern of the obedience
which the regenerate and sanctified elect render to their God under the
covenant of grace, on account of its universality and perpetuity. The
ceremonial law was added, embracing shadows and types of Christ and his
benefits and thus pertaining to the gospel. Meanwhile, the observance of that
ceremonial worship had its many most burdensome difficulties; nevertheless,
on the supposition that it had been prescribed by God, it possessed the
necessity of a precept and a legal relation, as a kind of appendage to the
moral law for that people until the time of reformation. The political law was
added as well, God ordering the polity in that theocracy and arming it with
such statutes and sanctions. Yet it by no means follows that some merely
ceremonial precept had to be included on the tablets of the moral law itself,
although ceremonial and political precepts could also be referred to the
Decalogue.*]

Dr. Burman replies to these points in his *Apology*, pages 55–56: [*We say,
therefore, that this argument is null: there are ten precepts or words of the
law; therefore there are ten moral precepts. For the Decalogue was not given
as the law of works, and therefore it can in no way be affirmed that there are
ten precepts of the law of works, and so forth.*] *Response.* I answered these
and the following points in this

[Printed p. 221 | IA PDF 229 | IA leaf 229 | IA decausadei02brow]

section above in chapter 17. I will now say only this. It cannot be denied
that all the precepts of the law of works are moral. If the Decalogue contains
precepts of the law of works, therefore, it contains moral precepts. 2. Even
if the Decalogue had not been given as the law of works, it could nevertheless
have contained the precepts of the law of works and nothing else, because the
precepts of the law of works contain the perpetual rule of life and conduct
which also retains force under the covenant of grace, as we proved above in
chapter 5.

In the following paragraph on page 57 he responds as follows: [Since,
therefore, the Decalogue *as the Decalogue* is the formula or stipulation of
the covenant of grace which God made with the Israelites, a stipulation of the
confession that God was their sanctifier and would provide the cause of
sanctification and justification was rightly and deservedly inserted into
that covenant. This occurs in the fourth precept, which contains a sign by
which it was known that God would sanctify Israel by the blood and burial of
his Son, through which the true *Sabbath-rest*, prefigured by that typical one,
was to come.]

*Response 1.* When he says that the Decalogue *as the Decalogue* is the
formula or stipulation of the covenant of grace, he supposes that the
Decalogue considered otherwise was not the formula or stipulation of the
covenant of grace; otherwise there would be no need for this reduplication.
And what is that other consideration? These pronouncements plainly do not
satisfy. But what does *as the Decalogue* mean? Can it mean anything other
than as it comprehends ten words? The Decalogue cannot be considered otherwise
than as it comprehends ten words or ten precepts. Does he hold that the
Decalogue, as comprehending only nine precepts, can be the formula of the
covenant of works? But what comprehends only nine precepts cannot be called
the Decalogue. The Decalogue, therefore, cannot be considered otherwise than
as the Decalogue. 2. Above, in chapter 15, we showed that this statement,
[the Decalogue is the formula or stipulation of the covenant of grace], is
false. This entire response therefore rests on a false foundation. 3. We know
that God made the covenant of grace with the Israelites and that, by virtue
of that covenant, they were bound to acknowledge God as the one who provides
the causes of sanctification and justification. But it is not evident that
the stipulation of this confession appears more in the fourth precept than in
another. 4. Every sign by which it is known that God sanctifies us through his
Son is not for that reason a type. 5. Scripture nowhere says that the weekly
Sabbath, or what is enjoined in the fourth precept, was a type prefiguring that
true Sabbath-rest which was in the *flesh* and burial of Christ.

He continues in the same place: [Those, therefore, who do not think that this
sign was suitably placed among the moral precepts leave nothing in the
Decalogue which regards Christ, and leave the stipulation of the covenant of
grace without Christ and without Jehovah sanctifying Israel. They thereby
give the Jews a new occasion for failing to acknowledge true sanctification
in Christ. Why, then, is the command of faith in Christ expelled from the
Decalogue, which contains God's covenant with the Israelites? In this way we
gratify the Socinians and Jews. For if the sanctification whose sign the
Sabbath is, is not future but present sanctification, what is there in the
Decalogue that commands them to expect what is future?]

*Response.* Since the Decalogue is not the formula of the covenant of grace,
there is no absurdity in saying that it contains nothing which directly and
immediately regards Christ. 2. Yet we do not thereby leave the stipulation of
the covenant of grace without Christ or Jehovah

[Printed p. 222 | IA PDF 230 | IA leaf 230 | IA decausadei02brow]

sanctifying Israel, because that stipulation of the covenant of grace exists
elsewhere than in the Decalogue. 3. It is therefore false that we give the
Jews an occasion for failing to acknowledge true sanctification in Christ. 4.
We deny that the Decalogue contains God's covenant with Israel, unless
*contains* is taken broadly so that it can include everything that can be
referred to the Decalogue. 5. Is the command of faith in Christ contained in
the fourth precept of the Decalogue? He certainly seems to imply this, because
only the fourth precept, as he holds, contains the stipulation of the
confession that God would be their future sanctifier. And only that which
regards Christ can contain faith in Christ. But if the fourth precept commands
faith in Christ, it is undoubtedly a moral precept, for in chapter 1 of his
*Apology* he admits that faith in Christ and evangelical repentance are moral
virtues. Furthermore, if this is so, the command of faith will formally be a
precept of the law and not merely insofar as the law commands a person to
receive all of God's Word, as he says in his *Synopsis*, book 2, chapter 16,
section 5. There he further says that the command of faith is evangelical
insofar as it flows from that testamentary promise concerning the
preservation of the elect seed through faith and uniquely apprehends the
benefits offered in Christ. 6. He plainly gratifies the Socinians when he
asserts that the fourth precept has been abolished. We by no means gratify
them when we say that the Decalogue is not the formula of the covenant of
grace, because, notwithstanding this, we confess that God made the covenant
of grace with the Israelites. 7. We do not gratify the Jews even though we say
that nothing in the Decalogue properly commands the expectation of what is
future. For we assert that they were commanded to expect all the benefits
prefigured by the types in the gospel then administered, not in the Decalogue,
except insofar as the Decalogue requires us to give credence to God in all his
revelations. Yet it is the first precept of the Decalogue, not the fourth,
which requires this. 8. If the sanctification of which the Sabbath had been a
sign was only future and not present sanctification, it will follow that the
Israelites did not become participants in it except in a type and consequently
were not really sanctified, and therefore were not justified either. This,
however, is utterly false.

In the following paragraph he has this: [*When I had proved in the
Vindication that the Decalogue is a compendium of the divine covenant and that
it therefore does not follow that all its precepts are moral, my vindicator,
leaving my argument untouched, says only that God indeed did not expressly
say, and so forth.*]

*Response.* Even if it were conceded that the Decalogue was in some way a
compendium of all the laws prescribed to the Israelites—insofar, namely, as
the other ceremonial and political laws can be referred to it, which is the
only sense in which the Decalogue can be called a compendium of the divine
covenant—it could properly follow that all its precepts are moral and that
none of them is properly ceremonial, just as it does not follow from this
that any of them is properly a political command. 2. The argument which he
complains was left untouched was only that the book in which laws other than
moral laws had been written is called the book of the covenant in Exodus
24:7. But does he not see how this argument vanishes of its own accord, without
a blow? Political laws no less than ceremonial laws were written in the same
book so named. He himself will affirm that God made the covenant with the
Israelites not only according to the tenor of the moral and ceremonial laws
but also according to the tenor of the judicial or forensic laws, as Exodus
34:10–27, the passage he himself cites, proves. Does he not now see that it
would have been no less equitable for one political law which summarized the
rest to be placed among the moral laws than for one law which was a summary

[Printed p. 223 | IA PDF 231 | IA leaf 231 | IA decausadei02brow]

of the ceremonial law and a compendium of the rest to be placed in the
Decalogue? Since no equity can require the former, what equity can urge the
latter?

Let us now see what he replies to Dr. Essenius's response. [*But first*, he
says, *let him prove that all these things argue that any precept is moral.
What is the force of the consequence: a precept was pronounced by God's
voice, engraved on tablets, placed among moral precepts, and so forth;
therefore it is moral? Who taught him this? How does he prove it, that he
always says with such confidence that this precept is therefore moral? How
often ceremonial precepts are found among moral precepts! See Leviticus 19:3;
26; the whole of chapter 20; Isaiah 2:6–8; Ezekiel 22:10.*]

*Response 1.* We showed above in chapter 7 how it is proved from these things
that those precepts are moral. Is it not clear from these privileges that
those precepts were distinguished far from all the rest? Do these privileges
not connote perpetuity? Otherwise, why would God himself pronounce these and
not the others in such a manner, inscribe them on tablets of stone, and hand
them over to be preserved more diligently in the ark, if they were of the
same nature as the others which were to perish together with that polity?
When legislators wish laws they have enacted to be perpetual and not quickly
forgotten by their subjects, they customarily commit them to memory in a
peculiar manner. Thus the laws of Solon set up in Athens were called *axones*,
because they were inscribed on wooden axles. Among the Romans they were
engraved on bronze tablets, according to Ovid's words:

> —— nor were threatening words marked
> Upon fixed bronze.

What else do the Romans' *Twelve Tables* signify? But enough was said about
this above. 2. He has not yet given us a more probable reason why this one
non-moral precept was placed among the moral precepts. Nor do we argue for the
moral character of this precept merely from the fact that it was placed among
the moral precepts, but also from the fact that it enjoyed all the privileges
in which the other moral precepts participated. 3. The Reformed teach us to
speak in this way when they dispute against the Roman Catholics, proving by
an entirely similar argument that the second precept is moral, as we saw.
Indeed, he himself teaches us as much when he says that the Decalogue is the
moral law and contains precepts by which the person who does them shall live,
or precepts of the covenant of works, and that it is a compendium of the moral
law and of all holiness and virtue. And when he affirms that the Decalogue
contains nothing but the commands of faith and repentance, he cannot deny
this consequence, since he calls faith and repentance moral things. Dr.
Cocceius himself also teaches us this when he openly says that nothing is
contained in the tablets of the covenant and the book of the law except what
the law of nature commanded an upright human being, and that everything is
contained radically in the law of nature. I say nothing of the orthodox who
call the Decalogue the sum of the moral law and the external representation
of the internal law of nature, and so forth. 4. We do not deny that ceremonial
precepts are somewhere listed among moral ones. But this does not invalidate
our argument, which is drawn from the fact that in such a compendium of moral
laws, pronounced in such a manner and peculiarly adorned with many remarkable
privileges, a precept of another and transitory nature could not appear, since
no sound reason can be devised why one non-moral precept, and not just any
other, ought to be inserted among those moral precepts.

[Printed p. 224 | IA PDF 232 | IA leaf 232 | IA decausadei02brow]

[He further says on page 58: *Nor is this precept moral any more because it
is inserted among the nine moral precepts than the fourth petition in the
Lord's Prayer must be understood of supersubstantial and spiritual bread
because all the other petitions among which it is inserted concern spiritual
things. This is the argument of certain people, who could reason in the same
way: the Savior himself pronounced these words, commanded us to pray and
preserve them, and the Spirit of prayers inscribes them on our hearts;
therefore all these petitions contain spiritual things.*]

*Response 1.* If by this argument we intended to prove that all the precepts
of the Decalogue concern the immediate worship of God, are natural to God
himself, or are in every way of the same nature, this counterexample would
indeed refute it abundantly. But when by this argument we conclude only that
all those precepts perpetually bind us, this counterexample not only fails to
weaken our argument but rather confirms it. For it can very properly be
inferred from this that we are always bound to present the fourth petition to
God as well, because it is contained in that rule which Christ prescribed to
us as a constant norm. This petition must be judged differently from certain
particular and temporary prayers, such as the one Christ himself prescribed
in Matthew 24:20 and Mark 13:18, and the other which the apostle enjoined in 1
Corinthians 14:13. 2. Christ indeed pronounced these words, but he pronounced
others as well. When Jehovah pronounced the Decalogue, however, it is said
that he added nothing else but prescribed other things through Moses. The
argument of those who hold that this fourth petition is spiritual is therefore
not similar to ours. 3. An excellent reason can be given why that fourth
petition, even though it does not concern spiritual things, should be inserted
among the other spiritual petitions. Yet the opponents can give no reason why
the fourth precept, if it is not moral, should be inserted among moral
precepts. The reasoning is therefore dissimilar.

Finally, he concludes as follows: [*2. To answer from the foundation, we say
in one word that because these things are said of the Decalogue's
prerogatives, they agree with the words of the covenant. And it belongs to the
covenant to require a confession of the hope of redemption. For the economy
of that time, God was pleased that this confession should be made through a
sign. See how nothing for the moral character of the Sabbath is inferred from
all these things.*]

*Response.* Those prerogatives of the Decalogue belong to the Decalogue, by
Dr. Burman's own testimony. In his *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 4, section 10,
he speaks as follows: [*Only the law in question was pronounced publicly with
such terror, by a voice formed in the air (just as God had spoken in the body
of flesh in his own time), which the whole people, assembled at the mountain
as if for a public address, heard from afar, while Moses and Aaron stood
nearer and drew God's words more certainly and distinctly. The remaining laws
for administering both the commonwealth and sacred things were handed down to
Moses alone in that holy withdrawal with God, and through him, as God's
interpreter and the covenant's mediator, were conveyed to the people. Exodus
20:19; Deuteronomy 5:22–23.*] The remaining laws also pertained to that
covenant, but they did not participate in these prerogatives. 2. Although it
belongs to the covenant to require a confession of the hope of redemption, it
does not follow that this confession exists in the Decalogue, much less that
this confession was made through the Sabbath, or the fourth precept of the
Decalogue. Thus nothing against the moral character of the Sabbath is inferred
from all these things.

Primerose argues against this argument in part 2, chapter 6, section 2: [*By
equal*

[Printed p. 225 | IA PDF 233 | IA leaf 233 | IA decausadei02brow]

*reasoning*, he says, *it can be inferred that the command to erect the
tabernacle and observe that ceremonial worship was moral, because the pattern
of that tabernacle had been shown to Moses on the same mountain with the same
majesty.*]

*Response.* The pattern of the tabernacle was indeed shown to Moses on the
mountain, and he was commanded to make everything according to that pattern.
Whatever God commands, he commands with divine majesty. Yet it does not follow
from this that the Decalogue, and consequently the fourth precept of the
Decalogue, is not moral. For the precepts of the Decalogue were promulgated in
a peculiar manner and with other tokens of the divine majesty which indicated
the perpetuity of the commands; and, as was said, they were also distinguished
from all the other precepts in other ways. What reason, then, was there for
Jehovah to honor this fourth precept with the same prerogatives with which he
honored the undoubtedly moral precepts, and no others, if it was not likewise
moral?

He says: [*We are not now bound to observe that law because it was pronounced
on Mount Sinai and inscribed on the tablets given to Moses, for in this
respect it bound only the Jews.*]

*Response 1.* We proved the contrary above in chapter 2 of this book. Yet even
if we granted this, our argument would not be weakened. For it follows clearly
enough from this that God distinguished those ten precepts from all the
others, and that the fourth, by the same reasoning with regard to the
perpetuity of its obligation, was of the same kind as the other nine.

In the same place, section 3: [*Since this law is very often called God's
covenant in Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13, 23; 5:3; 9:9, 11, 15, and so
forth; and 1 Kings 8:21, and since this covenant comprehended not only moral
but also ceremonial things in Exodus 24:7–8; 34:10, 27; Leviticus 2:13;
26:2, 15; and Jeremiah 34:13, it is perfectly plain that all statutes, both
moral and ceremonial, are comprehended in this epitome. Just as the nine
precepts are a summary of the moral statutes, so the fourth is a summary of
all ceremonial statutes.*]

*Response 1.* Then, just as the other nine precepts, which are the epitome of
moral precepts, are in every way moral and nothing but moral, so this fourth
precept, which is imagined to be a summary of all ceremonial precepts, will
be in every way ceremonial and nothing but ceremonial. Yet he does not dare
to assert this. See section 25 of the same chapter. 2. Since this covenant also
comprehends political precepts, as is clear from Jeremiah 34:13, the passage
he cites, and Leviticus 26:15, where not only precepts and statutes but also
judgments are mentioned, is it not equally plain that this summary must also
comprehend forensic laws? For this reason a special precept which summarized
all the political laws ought to exist in this epitome, and thus there would
be at most only eight moral precepts in the Decalogue. Since this is neither
said nor able to be said, who does not see how weak this consequence is? 3.
But what reason is there for the *fourth*, and not another, to be established
as the compendium of the ceremonial precepts? [*Because*, he says, *the
Sabbath was sanctified by the practice of ceremonies.*] But, 1. the fourth
precept says nothing about such a practice of ceremonies. 2. The external
worship of God, which is commanded by a precept other than the fourth, did not
occur without the practice of ceremonies. 3. Certain duties commanded in the
second table of the Decalogue also had certain ceremonies attached to them,
such as marriage with a foreign woman. 4. Does he not himself admit in section
19 that ceremonies are indirectly referred to the second precept?

[Printed p. 226 | IA PDF 234 | IA leaf 234 | IA decausadei02brow]

He continues in section 8: [*It can be said with better reason that all the
precepts, moral as well as ceremonial, were adorned with those prerogatives,
since the Decalogue is an epitome of them all; and that for this reason God
gave them all greater authority and commanded Moses to explain all these
things afterward, which we know he did as the interpreter of the Decalogue.*]

*Response 1.* As we made clear in chapter 7, the sacred text plainly
distinguishes between the ten words and all the other laws with regard to the
manner of promulgation. Other opponents concede this, as we just heard from
Dr. Burman. 2. Although ceremonial and political precepts can in some manner
be referred to the Decalogue as appendages, it does not follow that all these
were honored with the same prerogatives as the moral law, or the Decalogue; or
that the law of the Decalogue was not distinct from them all and of a
different nature. Much less does it follow that any of those ten precepts was
not moral. 3. Would God not also have wished to give authority to the judicial
statutes? Why, then, did he not adorn them too with the same prerogatives as
all the rest and therefore insert into the Decalogue some precept that was an
epitome of all the remaining judicial statutes? 4. Moses indeed declared and
explained moral precepts no less than ceremonial ones, but he also prescribed
political precepts, none of which nevertheless has a summary in the Decalogue.

He further says: [*The connection of verse 27 with verse 28 in Exodus 34 must
be noted. When he had said that he had made a covenant with Israel according
to the prescription of these words—that is, the words contained in the
preceding verses, among which are ceremonial as well as moral statutes—he
immediately adds in verse 28, And he, namely Jehovah, not Moses, as is clear
from Deuteronomy 10:2, 4, inscribed on the tablets the words of this covenant,
those ten words. By covenant he means the same covenant of which he spoke in
verse 27. It follows that the Decalogue written by God's finger was an epitome
of all the statutes pertaining to that covenant, which he had entrusted to
Moses to write down and explain more fully in Exodus 34:27. This was done in
Exodus 24:4; Deuteronomy 28:58; 30:10; and 31:9.*]

*Response 1.* Does he think God made the covenant with the Israelites
according to the prescription of those words taken exclusively, or as
including all other things? If the former is said, then he must affirm that no
precepts other than those mentioned there pertained to that covenant and,
consequently, that no other ceremonial precepts are comprehended in the
fourth precept. If the latter is said, then the judicial precepts which Moses
also taught are included. Consequently, among those ten words which God
inscribed on the tablets with his own finger there is at least one which
summarily expresses all the judicial laws. But which one is it? Unless he can
say this, he will never prove from these statements that the fourth precept is
a compendium of all ceremonies. Nor will he deny that God also entrusted the
political laws to Moses to write down and that Moses committed them to
writing. Therefore he will either say that they did not pertain to the
covenant; that there is some properly judicial command in the Decalogue which
summarily comprehends all the remaining judicial laws; or—which alone must be
said—that his consequence is utterly null. Indeed, the text here plainly
enough distinguishes between the ceremonial laws

[Printed p. 227 | IA PDF 235 | IA leaf 235 | IA decausadei02brow]

and the ten words when Jehovah says to Moses in verse 27, [Write these words
for yourself], namely the ceremonies and other non-moral laws, while God
himself inscribed those ten words on the tablets. This is still clearer from
Deuteronomy 4:13–14: [He declared to you his covenant, which he commanded you
to observe, those ten words, and inscribed them on two tablets of stone. But
Jehovah commanded me at that time to teach you statutes and judgments.]

He replies in section 10 and says that no distinction between the covenant
and the statutes and judgments, which denote the same thing with regard to
substance, is implied here, but rather one between those who proposed the
precepts, namely God and Moses, in this sense: God prescribed everything in
epitome when he engraved the Decalogue in verse 13, while Moses taught
everything more explicitly and fully in verse 14, for he also taught moral
things. The statutes and judgments God commanded Moses to teach in verse 14
are the same things Moses says he taught in verses 1, 2, 5, 6, and 8, and they
can be called moral rather than ceremonial. Moses taught both the former and
the latter in Leviticus 19:27; 20:8; 21:3; and likewise Exodus 20 and
Deuteronomy 5.

*Response.* It is manifest that in Deuteronomy 4:13–14 Moses not only
distinguishes between God and himself, but also shows a distinction in the
matter itself: namely, between the ten words which in a special manner are
called the covenant and the other statutes and judgments, that is, the
ceremonial and judicial laws. 2. Who ever previously said that the moral,
ceremonial, and judicial precepts were the same with regard to substance? 3.
We know that Moses also explained many moral things and particularly applied
the general heads contained in the Decalogue. Nevertheless, in a special
manner he taught statutes and judgments, that is, prescribed ceremonial and
judicial precepts. 4. This difficulty always remains to be removed: namely,
that it would also follow from this that there must be some precept in the
Decalogue which is the head of all political precepts. For if God set forth
all laws summarily in those ten words which Moses afterward explained and
declared more fully; and if it necessarily follows from this that there is one
precept in the Decalogue which is the head and sum of all ceremonies, why does
it not follow with equal necessity that there is some precept in the
Decalogue which is the head and sum of all judicial laws, and consequently
purely political? But this has not yet been shown and cannot be shown. What,
therefore, can be concluded against us from this entire line of reasoning?
Absolutely nothing.

*Argument 8.* If the summary of the whole Decalogue is moral, the Decalogue
itself will be moral, every precept of the Decalogue will be moral, and
consequently the fourth precept of the Decalogue will be moral. But the former
is true, by the testimony of Christ himself when he gives us the summary of
the Decalogue in Matthew 22:37, 39 and Luke 10:27. A summary of precepts which
are partly moral and partly ceremonial or non-moral cannot be wholly moral,
but would be partly moral and partly ceremonial or non-moral. Yet the summary
of the Decalogue which Christ sets forth in the cited passages is entirely
moral.

Primerose raises two objections against this argument in part 2, chapter 6,
section 12. First, he says that this summary regards the Decalogue insofar as
it was moral, and that because the greatest part of it is moral, its summary
can properly be moral.

*Response.* But if this summary is the summary of the Decalogue, all the
precepts of the Decalogue must share the nature of its summary, for summaries
are summaries of the whole, not of a part. This is therefore mere begging of
the question.

[Printed p. 228 | IA PDF 236 | IA leaf 236 | IA decausadei02brow]

He next responds as follows: [*This summary regards not only the moral law but
also the ceremonial law—indeed, the entire law—in Deuteronomy 6:1–3 compared
with verses 4–5. Christ, namely, says that the greatest command, not only with
regard to dignity but also with regard to extension, is, You shall love, and
so forth. Finally, he concludes that the whole law and the prophets depend
upon these two precepts. It is therefore evident that whatever exists in the
law and the prophets pertains to this summary. Furthermore, the love of God
bound people to obedience to all things, as if to say: the most general and
principal command of all and the foundation of the obedience to be rendered
to all the others is to love God, and so forth.*]

*Response 1.* If this is the summary of the whole law, moral, ceremonial, and
political, the entire law will be moral in all its parts. Thus the distinction
between moral, ceremonial, and political laws will be removed, for a summary
always reflects the nature of the thing whose summary it is; otherwise it
will not be a summary but something distinct. 2. In Deuteronomy 6:4–5 Moses
indeed urges them to love Jehovah their God, who alone is Jehovah, with all
their heart and all their soul, after he had urged them to observe all his
precepts and statutes. But it is not evident that Moses makes this love of God
a summary of all the statutes, only of the first table, as Christ interprets
it in Mark 12:29. 3. We know that the love of God is the greatest of all
precepts with regard to extension, but it does not follow that it is properly
a summary of all. 4. It is true that whatever is found in the law and the
prophets depends upon this summary, because all things can be referred to it.
Yet when he distinctly names two precepts on which the others depend as
appendages, he openly distinguishes these two precepts, or heads of precepts,
from all the rest. Since these two heads are the sum of the two tables of the
law and are undoubtedly moral, all the precepts of those tables must also be
moral. 5. We concede that the love of God moves us to render obedience to all
his precepts and that from such obedience this love is known to be true and
not feigned. But it by no means follows that this love can or should be called
the summary of all precepts, whatever their nature may be. 6. It is true that
the love of God is the principal of all precepts, but it is not therefore
called the summary of them all. It is clear, however, that Christ here gives
us the compendium of the whole law of the Decalogue when he names the first
and great precept, to love God with all one's heart, and so forth, and the
second which is like it, to love one's neighbor as oneself. Moreover, the
apostle Paul in Romans 13:8–10 and Galatians 5:14, and James in James 2:8,
testify that this second is the compendium or summary of the whole second
table.

*Argument 9.* This fourth precept is an integral part of that body of divine
laws which is distinguished from all the others and whose violator in one
precept is guilty of all, according to James 2:8–10. Therefore it is of the
same nature as the others with regard to the perpetuity of its obligation.
These ten precepts constitute one body of laws, specially distinguished and
preferred to all others. They are therefore called the ten words, the royal
law in James 2:8, the whole law in Galatians 5:14, and the law in Romans
13:8, 10; 1 Timothy 1:5; and frequently elsewhere; the law inscribed on
tablets and ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator in Galatians
3:19; and the fiery law in Deuteronomy 33:2. [The apostle applies this
pronouncement of James, Walaeus says, to Christians to whom he writes, and its
truth cannot stand unless under the New Testament it also applies to
transgression of the fourth precept,

[Printed p. 229 | IA PDF 237 | IA leaf 237 | IA decausadei02brow]

for otherwise, if there is nothing moral in this precept, the person who did
not keep it would not be guilty of violating the law but would rather be a
right observer of that law and a right defender of Christian liberty.]

This argument does not satisfy Primerose, in the place cited above, section
13. [For in all human and divine laws, he says, it is customary for there to
be distinct laws in one and the same body of laws, just as there are various
distinct and dissimilar parts in one and the same physical body. We frequently
see ceremonial laws mixed with moral laws in Scripture, as may be seen in
Exodus 34:10–28. It is therefore not surprising if, in the first table in
which God wished to comprehend everything pertaining to his worship, after
three precepts commanding moral and perpetual worship, ceremonial worship is
commanded in the fourth.]

*Response.* We do not deny that laws of distinct species are comprehended in
one and the same general body of laws. But when certain laws are selected from
such a great body of various laws, fitted together into one body,
distinguished from all others in a special manner, and bound together by such
a close bond that whoever violates one is held to be a violator of them all,
they are undoubtedly of a different nature from the rest. And when it is
admitted that the remaining precepts of that most closely joined body, except
one, are moral, why can that one not also be regarded as moral? This is
especially so since it is not established from elsewhere that this precept is
merely ceremonial; no probable reason can be given why, if it were of another
nature, it was fitted so closely with the rest; it forms an integral and like
part of that body of laws whose summary is manifestly moral; and together with
the other parts of that body it shares in the special prerogatives which are
marks of moral character. Finally, it is part of that body of laws in which
violation of one precept even now, when ceremonial and judicial laws have
been abolished, makes one guilty of them all. 2. We do not deny that
ceremonial laws are sometimes mentioned in Scripture together with moral
laws. But this does not weaken our argument, which is not drawn from its bare
placement among other moral precepts, as has now been shown. 3. It is true
that the moral worship of God is commanded in the first table of the
Decalogue. As to ceremonial worship, it is evident that this should better be
referred to the second precept, which, by his own admission in sections 19 and
32, prohibits all voluntary worship and is the head which comprehends all
external worship. See what was said above in chapter 19. To say that the
fourth precept commands ceremonial worship is mere begging of the question.
Indeed, in section 15 he expressly says that in the first three precepts God
commanded the internal and external worship to be rendered daily by every
individual, but in the fourth commands public and solemn worship and a solemn
time for celebrating that public worship, with cessation from every work
which could hinder it. How these statements can be reconciled is beyond me.

*Argument 10.* If the fourth precept is not moral, there are not ten moral
precepts but only nine; the ten words are not ten moral precepts. Indeed,
there will not be ten words, but only nine. All Reformed churches, which
always treat ten commands in their catechisms, will readily recognize this
absurdity. Why are ten precepts read aloud in public assemblies? Why are ten,
not merely nine, engraved on tablets, affixed to the walls of basilicas and
other churches,

[Printed p. 230 | IA PDF 238 | IA leaf 238 | IA decausadei02brow]

and set before everyone to read? Why do we teach children that there are ten
precepts of God? Why do commentators and writers on theological commonplaces
explain ten precepts to us, and not merely nine? Why is the Decalogue called a
compendium of the moral law if there are only nine moral precepts?

The opponents stumble here and do not know how to stand with a fixed foot.
They see how badly it sounds in Protestant ears to say that there are only
nine moral precepts. They therefore sometimes verbally confess that there are
ten moral precepts and for this reason attribute some general moral sense to
the fourth precept. At other times, however, they pronounce roundly that the
fourth precept is ceremonial and consequently that there are only nine moral
precepts. Thus Primerose says in the place cited above, section 28, [*There is
not the least inconvenience in saying that only nine moral precepts which now
bind us are given in the Decalogue, and that the law called moral does not
pertain to us in all its precepts.*] What could be plainer? Yet he adds:
[*Nevertheless, in a certain sense all ten can be called moral, because the
fourth itself is moral with respect to its foundation and principal end,
although the thing expressed there is a particular and ceremonial
determination.*] But in truth, what is moral only with respect to a general
foundation and principal end can no more be called moral than many ceremonial
and political laws can.

After Dr. Burman had said on pages 51–52 of his *Vindication* that it was fair
for at least one ceremonial law which was a compendium of the rest to be
placed among so many moral laws expressed in the Decalogue on the tablets of
the covenant, he adds: [*Yet the Decalogue does not thereby become an
Ennealogy. Just as this objection is trivial, since the fourth precept is not
devoid of all moral character and nothing whatever is taken from morality
when absolutely all moral character is fully exhausted in the remaining
precepts, so God's law lacks nothing whatever for perfect morality.*]

The celebrated Essenius responded to this in his *Vindication*, page 361:
[*6. Among other things the opponent now says*, as if we denied that there
was something moral in the Sabbath. *And again*, since the fourth precept is
not devoid of all moral character. *But he also immediately adds*, when
absolutely all moral character is fully exhausted in the remaining precepts.
*Yet if absolutely all moral character is fully exhausted in the remaining
precepts, what moral character, I ask, is left to the fourth precept?* Before
this, in his Annex after section 4, he said quite plainly that Paul calls the
weekly Sabbath a shadow not with qualification but absolutely and *in every
respect*. It is evident: first, because he does not say *in a certain respect*,
that the Sabbaths are a *shadow*, but calls them so *absolutely*, which alone
would be sufficient. Otherwise the Colossians would have had a ready
exception, the one we now commonly hear, that they did not observe the weekly
Sabbath with regard to what was ceremonial in it, but what was moral. There is
(note well) no place for this exception here. For, to state the matter itself,
it is not possible (note well) for any precept to be both moral and ceremonial
according to different respects or parts of itself. For what is moral flows
from the image of God and is therefore perpetual and immutable and, as it
were, complete in itself, so that it cannot be the type of another thing. And
when was any moral precept ever called a shadow or turned into a shadow? The
term is proper to ceremonial things. [*The law having a shadow of good things
to come, but not the very image of the things*], says the apostle

[Printed p. 231 | IA PDF 239 | IA leaf 239 | IA decausadei02brow]

in Hebrews 10:1 while treating the ceremonial law. How could this be said of
moral laws? Thus the argument is as follows. What is called a shadow is
ceremonial and (note well) not moral. But the precept concerning the Sabbath
is here called a shadow. Therefore it is excluded from the moral precepts and
consequently is (note well) ceremonial only. Otherwise, if the response is,
[it is not moral *only insofar as it was such*, namely, a shadow], the same
will hold for the *ceremonial* law itself, which, insofar as it has a shadow,
will indeed not be moral but nevertheless will be moral in another respect.
This is absurd, since it is only a shadow. The same applies (note well) to the
Sabbath. *After certain other statements to the same effect, he concludes:*
Therefore the apostle truly teaches that the weekly Sabbath is wholly a
shadow. *He also has similar things near the end of the same work. Again, in
the Inquiry, page 22:* I had said that the apostle here calls the Sabbath a
shadow, not in a certain respect or with a limitation but absolutely and
simply. I said that this alone sufficed, since it cannot be maintained, and so
forth. Therefore these two contrary attributes cannot fall upon one and the
same thing. For nothing can simultaneously flow and not flow from God's image,
be a *decree* and not be a *decree*. *I do not inquire how the opponent can
reconcile these things with one another. 7. If the precept concerning the
weekly Sabbath is simply ceremonial, or merely ceremonial, and is excluded
from the moral precepts, it can indeed be said that some kind of Decalogue was
inscribed by God on tablets of stone, but not a moral Decalogue. Nor can it be
said that, after the ceremonies of the Old Testament were abrogated, it
remained as the rule of our life and that we thus still have a Decalogue which
binds us. Indeed, according to the truth of the matter, it must be said that
we have only an Ennealogy, or nine moral precepts, remaining.* Thus he writes.]
I see that Dr. Burman replied to none of this in his *Apology*.

From this it can be seen that Dr. Burman himself is uncertain what to say
here. He does not wish the Decalogue to be an Ennealogy, because he concedes
that the fourth precept is moral in some manner, or that there is something
moral in it. Yet he must say that the Decalogue is an Ennealogy, since he
leaves nothing peculiarly moral to the fourth precept, repeatedly says that
this precept is ceremonial, and also asserts that no precept can be at the
same time moral and ceremonial, either wholly or in part. For in *Vindication,
Inquiry*, pages 6–7, he says: [Who ever heard that a precept concerning a
certain time of worship, whatever it may be, was moral, since it is always
only an adjunct of moral worship, mutable and indifferent in itself, and
therefore not moral?] On page 8 in the same place he has this: [Under what
pretext, then, can the precept concerning the Sabbath be called moral, when it
contains only an adjunct of worship which depends on God's bare command and
does not flow from his nature or image?] Indeed, in his *Inquiry*, pages
16–17, he openly says: [The precept concerning the external weekly Sabbath is
not moral but ceremonial.] He also attempts to prove this, but we shall see
later how childishly he does so.

Dr. Heidanus responds to this argument in his *Disputation on the Sabbath and
the Lord's Day*, thesis 20: [But, if I may say this with their kind indulgence,
they assert far too confidently that the Decalogue is only an epitome and
compendium of the moral law, since it is a kind of compendium and system of
the whole Mosaic law, or of the entire covenant initiated by God through
Moses with the Israelite people. Its parts are also the ceremonial and
political law, which are nothing other than special appendages of the moral
law and streams drawn from that fountain of equity. Thus ceremonial laws are
certain external exercises of the first table which determine the worship of
God prescribed in the first table through external circumstances, while

[Printed p. 232 | IA PDF 240 | IA leaf 240 | IA decausadei02brow]

forensic laws are exercises of the second table which similarly limit through
external circumstances the exercise of justice toward human beings prescribed
by the second table. Thus the first table is the root and foundation of all
Mosaic ceremonies and rites, of which the whole divine worship of that time
consisted; and the latter table was likewise the root and foundation of the
Jewish polity concerning obedience to magistrates and of its judicial laws
concerning theft, homicide, perjury, and so forth.]

*Response.* I pass over a careful examination of these words, partly because
something was said about this matter above and partly because it would not
contribute much to our present purpose. I say only the following. 1. What else
is replied here than what some Roman Catholic could reply? When we object to
them that removing the second precept makes the Decalogue an Ennealogy, could
they not similarly answer us that the Decalogue was not an epitome and
compendium of the moral law but of the entire covenant initiated with the
Israelite people, and so forth? Yet I admit that they proceed more cautiously
to preserve the Decalogue when they divide the tenth precept into two, so that
they may preserve the number ten intact and unimpaired. I am surprised our
opponents do not do this, since no other possible means remains of preserving
the number ten when they wish the fourth precept to be abolished. 2. Let us
grant all these things. How is it thereby inferred to be false that, when the
fourth precept is cast down and erased from the number ten, there will no
longer be a Decalogue but an Ennealogy, and thus no longer ten words? Certainly,
if the fourth precept is relegated to the ceremonies, there will no longer be
a Decalogue but an Ennealogy, and thus our argument is conceded. 3. Perhaps he
wishes to prove from this that it is not absurd to say there are only nine
moral precepts. But then our argument stands, and all who assert there are ten
moral precepts must be condemned. 4. How will these statements prove that the
fourth precept is less moral than the remaining precepts of the first table?
If ceremonial laws are external exercises of the first table, it no more
follows that the fourth is ceremonial than that the second is. This is also
manifest from the following: forensic laws, he says, are exercises of the
second table, and so forth; yet it does not follow from this that any law in
the second table is not moral. Thus, although ceremonies are external
exercises of the first table, it does not follow that any precept in the first
table is not moral. 5. What, then, will Dr. Heidanus conclude from this? He
will not conclude that all the precepts of the first table are ceremonial. If
he says it necessarily follows from this that one of them must be ceremonial,
I deny the consequence. Even if I granted this consequence, it would not
necessarily follow that this precept had to be the fourth. Moreover, even if
we granted this consequence too, could it not be concluded by an equally firm
consequence that there is some political precept in the second table, since
the reasoning is the same in each case? Would it not then follow that there
are only eight moral precepts? Thus we would have neither a Decalogue nor an
Ennealogy but an Octalogy.

In the Dutch treatise titled *Considerations on the Lord's Day*, pages 58–59,
the same Dr. Heidanus argues against this argument by way of retortion. He
says: [This can with better right be objected against us, who mutilate the law
in three principal precepts when we assert that the second, third, and fourth
are of positive right and concern not the natural worship of God but
instituted worship.]

[Printed p. 233 | IA PDF 241 | IA leaf 241 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Response.* There is no need for us to show at length how frivolous this
evasion is, for it is sufficiently clear that many things which are positive
to God are natural and moral to us. He himself will not say that all the
precepts of the Decalogue are natural to God himself, so that he could not
have commanded otherwise or not commanded them. What, then, is the purpose of
this? 2. We say that the second precept, insofar as it concerns instituted
worship in conjunction with natural worship, and the fourth—not everyone says
the same of the third—are of right which is positive to God himself but
natural, and therefore moral, to us. And what concerns worship instituted by
God can be moral to us. What will he say about faith in Christ and evangelical
repentance? Are these natural to God himself? Nevertheless, Dr. Burman grants
that they are moral, as we saw some time ago.

The opponents say that by striking the fourth from the list of moral precepts
they do not mutilate morality, because the remaining nine contain all
morality, and thus nothing is taken from morality by this procedure, as we
heard from Dr. Burman.

*Response.* Nevertheless, the Decalogue is mutilated and an Ennealogy is
presented to us. 2. Can the Roman Catholics not answer us similarly that they
do not mutilate morality when they reject the second precept of the Decalogue,
because all morality is contained in the remaining nine? 3. Since this fourth
precept contains something different from the other nine, and God saw fit to
comprehend all morality summarily in those ten words, those who remove even
one of these words curtail morality to that extent.

They also hold us guilty of the same crime because we say that the last day
of the week, or the seventh in order, is not now to be observed and that,
consequently, the fourth precept which enjoined the observance of that day has
been abrogated.

*Response.* We do not say that the moral substance of the fourth precept is
the observance of the last day. Indeed, the contrary will be shown later in
its proper place. We affirm that the fourth precept enjoins the observance of
the seventh, not in order but in number, after six working days, and we
maintain that this has by no means been abrogated. This objection therefore
rests on a false hypothesis. Even if we granted that a determination of the
seventh day in order is made in the fourth precept, it would not follow that
we wholly remove this fourth precept, because we say that one out of seven
must always be observed. And a command which enjoins the observance of the
seventh in order also enjoins the observance of the seventh in number; indeed,
it enjoins the latter prior to and more principally than the former, because
the latter is more general and comprehends what is particular.

Francis White, bishop of Ely, says on page 158 of his treatise on the Sabbath
that [this fourth precept is read and expounded in the Christian church in
the same way as the other positive precepts of the old law, not to denote its
perpetual obligation but to signify the manifold benefits of God typified and
represented by it, and to move the people toward those spiritual and
evangelical duties which were prefigured by it.]

*Response.* This is openly to acknowledge that there are only nine moral
precepts. For if it is expounded only in the same manner as the other positive
and ceremonial laws, which now bind us only with respect to some general
equity, it will be no more moral than those ceremonial laws are. 2. If the
reason why the Roman Catholics did not need to expunge the second precept from
their catechisms is to be sought from this, they could retain it in the same
manner in which they retain the other ceremonial precepts. 3. If this is so,
why do they join this precept, rather than any other ceremonial precept, with
the other moral ones, read it to the people, and explain it in catechisms? Is
the reasoning the same for this precept and all the other ceremonies? Did not
other ceremonies typify God's benefits? Does the church not need an
explanation of them? The Christian church, then, must be blamed because until
now it has not inserted and explained in its catechisms those ceremonies
whose exposition is clearly no less necessary. But this is a mere evasion.

[Printed p. 234 | IA PDF 242 | IA leaf 242 | IA decausadei02brow]

Another tries to weaken our argument as follows: [We take the Decalogue, he
says, as the stipulation of the covenant of grace proposed to the Israelites,
or as a formula of conversion to God through faith in the promise of
sanctification to be rendered in the future. This formula also comprehends the
sign by which it was known that God was ready to sanctify Israel. But the
precept concerning the sign absolutely required faith in the thing signified,
and without faith in the thing signified that day was in no way sanctified by
cessation. Therefore, the person who retains faith in the thing signified and
acknowledges its truth, but no longer employs the sign as one who still
requires, awaits, and thirsts for the truth, truly keeps Sabbath. He does not
eliminate this word but observes it better and more diligently in a good
conscience quieted through Christ. For just as the person circumcised in
Christ with a circumcision not made by hands is circumcised and cannot be
called uncircumcised in flesh and heart, so the person who acknowledges the
rest given by Christ gives rest to the weary, does not impose a yoke, and so
forth. But from Sabbath to Sabbath and from new moon to new moon he comes to
worship the Lord in an incorruptible and likewise gentle and quiet spirit. He
keeps Sabbath to God.]

*Response 1.* It was shown above in chapter 15 that the Decalogue must not be
taken as the stipulation of the covenant of grace proposed to the Israelites.
2. What reason is there for this formula comprehending ten words to embrace
this one sign more than others also given at that time? 3. How is it shown
that the sanctification of which the observance of the Sabbath was a sign was
sanctification to be rendered only in the future? 4. It has not yet been
proved that the Sabbath was a typical sign or that faith in the thing
signified was required for its sanctification. 5. If we granted all these
things to the opponent, it is evident that our argument is not refuted,
because it clearly follows from his own explanation that a Decalogue does not
now exist, but an Ennealogy. For the fourth precept concerning the
sanctification of the weekly Sabbath is no more in force now than the precept
concerning the administration of circumcision. Is the precept concerning
circumcision not eliminated even though believers now participate in the
circumcision of the heart? Why is the fourth precept not similarly eliminated
when, according to his exposition, it is no more observed now than the precept
concerning circumcision or the celebration of Passover? No one says that the
ceremonial law has not been abrogated merely because an obligation now remains
to acknowledge and apprehend by faith the thing signified by those types and
ceremonies. Why, then, should it not similarly be said that the precept
concerning the Sabbath has been abrogated when only a similar obligation to
apprehend the thing signified by faith remains now? 6. The person circumcised
in Christ with a circumcision not made by hands does not feed on the flesh of
circumcision and rightly does not observe the precepts concerning circumcision
given to Abraham, but acknowledges it to have been abolished.

[Printed p. 235 | IA PDF 243 | IA leaf 243 | IA decausadei02brow]

So too, the person who acknowledges the rest given by Christ does not observe
the Sabbath commanded in the fourth precept, but can acknowledge it to have
been abolished. Therefore there are not ten moral precepts, and thus our
argument is confirmed.

*Argument 11.* That precept which was given formerly and now, under the
gospel, is not only not abrogated but also asserted and confirmed, is moral.
The fourth precept is of this kind. Therefore, and so forth. The connection of
the major premise needs no proof, since no one can deny it. The minor is
confirmed by the fact that this fourth precept is not excepted when Christ in
Matthew 5 vindicates and confirms this law of the Decalogue, or moral law. We
made it clear above in chapter 8 that Christ speaks there of the Decalogue.
No trace of an exception for the fourth precept appears here. Indeed, the Lord
does not except even the least command when he says in verse 19: [Whoever,
therefore, breaks one of these least precepts and so teaches others will be
called least in the kingdom of heaven.] Let our opponents who break the
fourth precept and openly teach others to do so diligently consider this
threat. Where did our Lord speak about the Sabbath in the manner in which we
heard the *apostle* speak about ceremonial worship? When he disputed about the
Sabbath with the Pharisees, he did not teach that it was or would be
abrogated, but explained the true sense of the command and thereby confirmed
it all the more.

The opponents object, 1. [It follows that the Christian church, which changed
the seventh day into the first day of the week, must be condemned.]

*Response.* This objection rests on a false hypothesis which will be refuted
later: namely, that the substance of the fourth precept requires the
observance of precisely the seventh day in order.

*Objection 2.* [By law here is meant the entire law of the Pentateuch and
everything foretold by the prophets. And although Christ abrogated the ancient
positive law with respect to observance, he nevertheless confirmed its
legitimate use in the Christian church and enjoined the performance of the
spiritual evangelical duties prefigured by those ceremonies. 1 Corinthians
5:8; Romans 2:29.] Thus the bishop of Ely on page 162.

*Response 1.* Then not one jot or one stroke has yet passed from the whole
ceremonial law. 2. Does the judicial law not also pertain to this law of the
Pentateuch? But what spiritual duties, I ask, did that law prefigure or
signify? 3. From the explanation and vindication of the law which follows, it
is evident that he does not speak of such a confirmation as concerns the
observance of the command; the scope of the passage also proves this
abundantly. 4. Where does he enjoin that spiritual duty which the Sabbath
prefigured? Or where is it indicated what that duty was? 5. When he speaks of
the feast of unleavened bread, he not only entirely removes that law with
regard to observance but teaches nothing about its right use being constantly
observed and retained. 6. That Christ speaks here of the law with respect to
observance is evident from verse 19, where *to break* one of the least
precepts is opposed to acting upon it: [But whoever does and teaches it will
be called great in the kingdom of heaven.] To do this is not to perform some
spiritual and prefigured duty but to do the very thing commanded. Otherwise,
to break the precepts will mean not only to teach that they are now abrogated
and no longer bind, but also to say that they have or can have no use at all
now. But do his opponents suspect any such thing even about Christ himself?
7. How

[Printed p. 236 | IA PDF 244 | IA leaf 244 | IA decausadei02brow]

many moral duties which the ceremonies foreshadowed are enjoined in the New
Testament! Although some allusion is made to the ceremonies that prefigured
them, it must not therefore be said that the right use of those ceremonies is
enjoined in this manner, or that Christ confirmed and established their
legitimate use.

*Objection 3.* The same writer further objects in the same place: [All the
commands mentioned in Matthew 5 were not promulgated by God himself on Mount
Sinai, such as those found in verses 39–42, 44, and so forth.]

*Response.* All these belong to a further explanation of the sixth precept and
of the whole second table which God himself pronounced on Mount Sinai. What is
added concerning a certificate of divorce in verses 31–32 tends toward a
fuller explanation of the seventh precept. Thus Christ intends nothing else
here than to vindicate the Decalogue from the corrupt and corrupting glosses
of the Pharisees.

*Objection 4.* [The word *to destroy* in Matthew 5:17 means to annihilate,
demolish, and cancel, as in Matthew 24:2; Ezra 5:12; Acts 5:38. Thus it is
evident that because Christ says that he did not come to annihilate or cancel
the law and the prophets, it does not follow that he therefore established
moral obedience and the observance of all the precepts. For after he had
fulfilled the whole law by his personal obedience and performed everything
foretold about himself, he freed the Christian church from outward obedience
to those legal precepts which were not simply moral. Nevertheless, he defended
the honor of the old law and inspired the apostles to teach the true meaning,
use, and end of that law. He also established the reading and interpretation
of the law and the prophets and by his providence preserved the entire Old
Testament intact and unimpaired.] The same author, page 63.

*Response 1.* When the obligation of a law is removed, the law is
annihilated, destroyed, and canceled, because the entire force of law consists
in obligation. When Christ says that he did not come to destroy or cancel the
law, therefore, it is manifest that he says this of the moral law, because he
came to dissolve and cancel the ceremonial law, for it has now been made
obsolete and abolished. 2. If he freed the church from outward obedience to those
precepts which were not simply moral, he thereby abolished and canceled those
laws. He does not mean these laws when he says that he did not come to
dissolve or cancel the law. Therefore he does not mean all those precepts
which perpetually bind, for on page 27 the same writer says that there are
certain laws which perpetually bind although they are not simply and formally
moral, such as the precept against polygamy and against marriage within
certain prohibited degrees. 3. Although the apostles taught the right use, end
or scope, and meaning of that ceremonial law, they did not teach that Christ
had not abolished it. On the contrary, when they expounded the true meaning
and scope of the ceremonies, they taught that they had been abolished by
Christ's advent. As to the reading, interpretation, and so forth of the law,
these contribute nothing to the matter. Notwithstanding them, the law can be
said to be simply abrogated and made obsolete.

*Objection 5.* [The opponents must first show that the fourth precept is
simply and formally moral before they say that it is part of the law which
Christ declares must always be observed in Matthew 5:19.] The same author,
page 64.

*Response.* By a simply and formally moral precept he means one that is
morally natural. But I deny that we must show the fourth precept to be of this
kind before we say that it has a perpetual obligation, for he himself concedes

[Printed p. 237 | IA PDF 245 | IA leaf 245 | IA decausadei02brow]

that certain positive precepts perpetually bind, as we have just seen. 2.
Could not someone who asserts that fornication or marriage within certain
prohibited degrees is lawful argue in this manner: first prove that those
commands are formally and simply moral before saying that they pertain to the
law which Christ wished to be perpetually observed? 3. It is enough if we show
that the Sabbath pertains to that moral law of which Christ speaks, and this
has already been done.

6. Primerose, part 2, chapter 9, objects in section 10: [*It is most true*, he
says, *that Christ did not come to annihilate and destroy the ceremonies,
either by teaching or by actions. To destroy is the same as to render void,
the word the apostle uses concerning the same matter in Romans 3:31, where the
ceremonial law is also meant, as is evident from verse 21.*]

*Response.* But by both teaching and actions he did destroy the law of
ceremonies, or the ceremonies with regard to observance. Whoever removes the
obligation of a law destroys the law, for the essence of law consists in its
obligation to obedience. 2. The word *to render void*, which the apostle uses
in the cited passage, abundantly confirms this, for it means to cancel, make
useless, or cause to cease. But a law is canceled, becomes useless, or ceases
when it no longer binds to obedience. 3. Does the apostle not demonstrate in
all his epistles that the law of ceremonies was canceled? What else, I ask,
do these passages mean: Galatians 2:18, [*For if I build again the things which
I destroyed*, and so forth], and Ephesians 2:15, [*having abolished the law of
commandments in decrees*]? Therefore the apostle does not speak of this law
in Romans 3:31, nor does verse 21 prove it. The ceremonial law sufficiently
testified to those endowed with God's Spirit that sinners are justified
through faith in the Messiah. Yet legalistic Jews took that law legalistically
and abused it. 4. What do these things do against the fourth precept? He adds
in the same place: [*Since the Sabbath is part of the ceremonial law, Christ
did not render it void but caused it to cease by fulfilling the truth which
it prefigured.*] *Response 1.* It begs the question to say that the Sabbath is
part of the ceremonial law. 2. What is that truth of the Sabbath which Christ
fulfilled? 3. If he caused the Sabbath to cease, he would not have said that
the person who broke this precept and so taught others would be called least
in the kingdom of heaven. But it has now been shown that Christ said this of
the law of the Decalogue, of which the fourth precept is a part. He replies in
section 12: [*Christ speaks in verse 19 of dissolving and annihilating those
precepts—this is denoted by the word* breaks—*but to abrogate the Sabbath,
which was a type and figure, by fulfilling the thing figured by that outward Sabbath, and to free people from
the necessity of observance is not to annihilate and dissolve the Sabbath.*]
*Response.* But when the apostle speaks of the ceremonial law which Christ
abrogated by fulfilling it, he says that it was annihilated, canceled, and
rendered void, as we saw in Ephesians 2:15; and Paul says that he destroyed
that law in Galatians 2:18. Certainly, to remove the obligation of a law is to
annihilate, cancel, and destroy the law. The Greek word *luō* means to free
from bonds, to loose from bonds but not to annihilate bonds. See Mark 11:4;
Luke 13:15; 19:31, 33; Acts 22:30; 27:40; 1 Corinthians 7:27; Mark 7:35;
Revelation 20:7. See also John 2:19 and the parallel passages. Sometimes it
also means to transgress a law, as in John 5:18 and 7:23.

Furthermore, he objects in the same place, section 13: [*Those who press this
argument must concede that Christ does not here enjoin the
perpetual observance of the Sabbath according to the full sense of the fourth
precept, because in this way he would enjoin the perpetual observance of the
seventh day, and that*

[Printed p. 238 | IA PDF 246 | IA leaf 246 | IA decausadei02brow]

*with legal worship, in memory of creation. This limitation must therefore be
added: this precept enjoins observance insofar as it can bind us according to
the tenor of the gospel, insofar as it commands that God be publicly worshiped
and that some festival therefore be appointed.*]

*Response 1.* Those who hold that the substance of this precept is the
observance of the seventh day of the week in order are bound to remove this
difficulty. It does not touch us, for we say that this precept commands the observance
of one day in the cycle of the week, not precisely the seventh day. 2. That
legal worship no more affects the nature and substance of the fourth precept
than it affects the second. 3. That limitation is empty, for, as will be
evident in its proper place, public worship is not what is principally
commanded in this fourth precept; it is properly commanded in the other
precepts. 4. Since the opponent holds that the observance of the seventh day
is precisely commanded and that this observance has now been abrogated, it is
evident that Christ rendered one entire precept of the Decalogue void, no less
than he rendered the ceremonial laws void.

8. *Objection.* [Throughout this whole discourse Christ makes no mention of
the Sabbath or the fourth precept. This precept is therefore rejected from
the number of laws confirmed.]

*Response.* The same would then have to be said of the first, second, fifth,
and ninth precepts. 2. Nor is there any lack of a reason why he did not mention
the Sabbath: namely, because the Pharisees did not err by deficiency in
explaining this precept, as they did in the others expounded and vindicated
here, but by excess, and with regard to this he deals with them elsewhere.

*Argument 12.* That precept which enjoins something moral is moral. But this
fourth precept enjoins something moral: namely, the holy observance of a
principal and solemn time determined by God himself, as will be evident later.
All solemn worship indeed requires a time in which it is rendered, but an
indefinite time is commanded where the worship itself is commanded. The case
is different with a solemn time set apart, dedicated, and determined both
with regard to duration, or *how long*, and with regard to frequency, or *how
often*. To determine this solemn time belongs neither to just any person nor
to just any human society, but to God alone, as we made clear in book 2. See
chapters 11, 12, and 14. If the observance of such a determined time is not a
moral duty, people can either observe no determined time at all or observe a
time as much longer or shorter as they please. Neither can be said. We must
therefore stand by God's determination, and the time determined by God must be
observed in holiness. The opponents themselves concede that it is morally
natural in the fourth precept that some sufficient time be dedicated to God
and his worship. Above we proved that it belongs to God alone to determine a
necessary and sufficient time for divine worship. Even the pagans acknowledge
this. Since this is moral, in which precept of the Decalogue is it required if
not in this fourth precept? Every moral thing is in the Decalogue, and whatever
is moral is referred to one of these precepts. But this moral thing—namely,
the observance of solemn time, or of sufficient and necessary time—can be
referred to no other precept of the Decalogue

[Printed p. 239 | IA PDF 247 | IA leaf 247 | IA decausadei02brow]

more suitably than to the fourth, which determines this principal and solemn
time and commands it to be observed.

*Argument 13.* The moral character of this precept can be proved from its
general scope, which the *Larger British Catechism*, question 115, describes
as follows: [*The fourth precept requires everyone to sanctify, or keep holy
to God, that defined time which he himself designated in his Word, and so
forth.*] In the same way, the general scope of the second precept is that God
be worshiped with the kind of religious worship which he himself prescribed,
or that the religious worship and ordinances which God instituted in his Word
be received, observed, and preserved intact and unimpaired. Although the
particular determination of that kind of worship with which he wishes to be
worshiped, whose observance is prescribed there, flows from God's sheer good
pleasure, it must not for that reason be said that this precept is not moral.
In the same way, the particular determination of that solemn time, although
it flows from God's bare good pleasure, ought neither to remove nor in any way
lessen the moral character of this precept. [*God therefore*, says
Zanchi on the fourth precept, page 854 in my copy, *does not permit his people
to choose and appoint for themselves the day and time on which they ought to
assemble to worship God, but wills that they themselves, by worshiping God,
sanctify to his worship the day and time blessed and sanctified by him from
the very creation of the world. Meanwhile it must be noted that in the command
itself contained in verse 8—for what follows pertains to the explanation of
the command—he did not say without reason, Remember to sanctify the seventh
day, but, Remember to sanctify the Sabbath day, that is, the day of rest. The
substance of the precept, therefore, insofar as it is moral and pertains to
all nations, is not that we sanctify precisely the seventh day on which God
also ceased from the works of creation, but a day dedicated and consecrated to
rest—consecrated, I say, by God himself, and so forth.*] A little later he
says: [*And this is the legitimate reason why in the substance of the command
he did not put the seventh day, but the day of rest.*] And afterward, on page
855: [*The substance of this precept, therefore, insofar as it also pertains
to us and was confirmed by Christ, is not that we sanctify the seventh day,
but that we sanctify the Sabbath day, that is, whatever day is appointed for
rest.*]

*Argument 14.* That precept given to the Israelites which was neither
judicial nor ceremonial was moral. The fourth precept of the Decalogue is of
this kind. Therefore, and so forth. The truth of the connection in the major
premise rests on a complete enumeration of the laws given to the Israelites.
The minor is also certain: 1. No one asserts that the fourth precept is
judicial. Then, we shall later make it clear that it is not ceremonial. We say
only this here: the Reformed condemn this opinion in the Socinians and
Anabaptists; why should it not also be condemned in others? Who will say that
the observance of one day in the weekly cycle is now something deadly? But
the observance of ceremonies now abolished is held to be deadly and utterly
unlawful.
