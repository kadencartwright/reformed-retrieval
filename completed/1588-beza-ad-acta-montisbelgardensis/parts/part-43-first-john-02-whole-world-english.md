## Part XLIII: First John 2—The Whole World

### The Württemberg Doctors

1 John 2: “He is the propitiation not only for our sins but for the sins of the whole world.”

### Theodore Beza’s Antithesis

When John says in 1 John 2 that propitiation was made not only for our sins but those of the whole world, he means only the whole body of the elect and believers. It is now gathered not from the apostle’s one nation, the Jews, but, with the dividing wall destroyed, pours itself into the other nations, as the apostle abundantly explains in Ephesians and as Abraham was promised that all nations would be blessed through his seed. John seems intentionally to recall Christ’s own words in his final most ardent prayer in John 17:20: “I pray not only for these, but also for those who will believe in me through their word.” John likewise said Christ is the Lamb of God who takes away the world’s sins. But did Christ take away the sins of the whole world as you interpret it of every individual? We certainly found intolerable your claim that Christ died for the condemned and that human beings are not condemned because of sins.

### Andreae’s Observations

1. John’s words are too open to admit this manifestly false interpretation. He does not compare Jews and believers but speaks universally of THE WHOLE WORLD.

2. Nothing is more certain than that by “world” John understood not the elect indefinitely but all human beings, as almost countless other Holy Scripture passages testify and confirm.

3. Christ sufficiently satisfied for every individual’s sins, so that no new propitiatory offering would be needed to restore and reconcile to God a thousand worlds, so to speak, for which one drop of God’s Son’s blood would suffice. But because the greatest part of humanity despises him, it benefits them nothing and they are more gravely condemned.

4. We say it is supreme impiety if anyone denies this. Indeed, they are chiefly condemned because they refused to believe Christ died for their sins, which is the highest and greatest sin.

5. These are not our words but Christ’s words and doctrine when he says, “This is judgment”—that is, the cause of judgment and condemnation—“that light came into the world and the world loved darkness more than light.” Although sins therefore condemn by their nature, they can no longer condemn if you truly believe in Christ. Human beings are therefore not condemned because they sinned in such a way that no help remains possible; otherwise all the saints would also be condemned, since all sinned. They are condemned because they reject the deliverer and refuse to believe in him.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Observations

[Marginal note: To 1, 2, 3, and 4.]

I answered two things concerning this passage. First, the benefit of propitiation necessarily pertains only to the elect and, because they are elect, believers. Second, John signifies that this benefit belongs not only to himself and those whom he numbers with himself but is common universally to the whole world, because with the dividing wall destroyed the Savior no longer appeared for one nation alone. The adversary answers absolutely nothing to the first, although it was primary. Concerning the second he only presses “WHOLE” on page 545 and adds that John wrote this epistle not only to Jews but all Christ’s disciples embracing Christ’s doctrine, whom he addresses universally. Yet the former answer neither proves his fiction concerning every individual of every na—

—tion nor opposes me, since the elect also have their own universality. Thus the apostle says in 1 Corinthians 15:22, “As all die in Adam, so all will be made alive in Christ.” Although the first universal term, simply taken, applies to all human beings, since no one is exempt from the appointment to die, the second must necessarily mean the elect alone to preserve the opposition between the two members. This is also clear because Paul, as appears from every individual argument, applies his treatment of resurrection only to that resurrection cohering with eternal life. Propitiation and vivification are most closely joined and inseparable. The former, like the latter, must therefore be referred only to the whole body of the elect.

Nor should Andreae be ashamed to learn the force of “WORLD” here by comparing God himself with Paul. Where the Lord says in Genesis 22:18 that all nations would be blessed in Abraham’s seed, Paul in Romans 4:13 says Abraham received the promise that he would be heir of the world—that is, would possess the world as heir. But the Lord himself interprets “all nations” as “many” in Genesis 17:4–5, restricting that whole body to the multitude of the elect from every nation. John discusses this one world in our present passage. Augustine, treating this matter and explaining these very promises, says: “Some things may be ‘all’ which are not many, as we call all the Gospels, though contained within the small number of four. Again, some things may be many yet not all, as we say many believe in Christ, though not all believe. But when it was said, ‘All nations will be blessed in your seed,’ and, ‘I made you father of many nations,’ it was shown that the same ‘all’ are ‘many’ and the same ‘many’ are ‘all.’ Similarly, when it was said, ‘Through one man’s justification to all human beings unto justification of life,’ and again, ‘Through one man’s obedience many will be constituted righteous,’ we must understand the same ‘many’ as ‘all,’ not with some excepted, but not because all human beings are justified in Christ; rather, because all who are justified can be justified only in Christ.”

Andreae’s other objection on page 546 says “John’s epistle was written to all disciples of Christ embracing the gospel’s doctrine, all whom he means by OUR, while by THE WHOLE WORLD he means all human beings without exclusion, which,” he says, “is beyond doubt among every truly godly and sincere doctor.” Even if I granted this, rightly understood so that “all human beings” means every elect person and consequently believers, it does not oppose me. But when applied according to Andreae’s fiction to every individual without exception, asserting it is as impious and blasphemous as saying anyone can be condemned whose sins were expiated by Christ’s death, for whom Christ satisfied, or that Christ satisfied for someone who—

Yet Andreae so stubbornly and shamelessly dared press this in explicit words on page 546 and following that he declares himself ready not only for public recantation but even the severest punishment if anyone demonstrates the contrary from any approved ancient or modern writer.
