# How a Faithful Judge or Counselor Should Act in a Benefice Dispute

*An undated corporate French consilium; a fresh translation from CO X/1 251–252.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 251]

The passage from Monsieur Bartonnier's letter having been communicated to the company of pastors

### [CO X/1, p. 252]

of this church, their desire would accord with what the said Monsieur Bartonnier says he will do hereafter: namely, that all believers, so far as it lies in them, avoid every handling of such matters insofar as it may convey approval of or consent to any part of the idolatries, or to any profanation whatsoever of the things which ought to be consecrated for the use of the true church and for maintaining its lawful order.

They therefore judge that a counselor who knows the truth of Jesus Christ ought to decline well in advance commissions like the one mentioned in the aforesaid letter and leave them to others. Nevertheless, when such a man finds himself involved, not having perceived at the outset the difficulties for conscience as clearly as experience now makes him feel them, their advice would be that, in order to extricate himself with the least evil, he earnestly protest that what he does is not an approval of such an abuse. Rather, acting only as executor, he does not enter any further into adjudicating the merits.

As for judges and courts before which such matters come to be disputed, that is another matter. A judge does not have the same freedom as a counselor to avoid touching them, since the judge is so appointed to hear ordinary disputes that, according to customary practice and the king's ordinances, lawsuits concerning benefices fall to some extent within his jurisdiction. The advice would therefore be that, when thus constrained to give judgment, he should declare that although neither claimant nor defendant is well founded in this matter, and although by divine right the thing belongs to neither one nor the other, nevertheless according to the laws of the Roman court approved by the king, this party must prevail; and in accordance with that, he awards it to him.

Nevertheless, when a judge can find a way to send such litigants elsewhere, that will be best for his conscience. For example, if he has a papist deputy—whether an ordinary deputy or the first counselor of the court—let him willingly leave the cases to that man, showing for his own part that he takes no pleasure in stirring such filth. Finally, because the letter mentions certain districts without specifying which ones they are, it has not been possible to answer it otherwise than in general terms.

## Translator's notes

1. The response speaks in the plural for the “company of pastors of this church.” Neither the body nor CO's source note identifies Calvin as sole author. It is retained as a Calvin-associated corporate consilium and should be cited that way.
2. *Conseillier* and *iuge* are distinguished throughout. The counselor can normally decline a commission before accepting it; the judge's public office may constrain him to hear disputes within his jurisdiction.
3. “Benefice” retains the technical ecclesiastical and legal sense: an office carrying a right to revenue or property. The response denies either litigant a sound claim by divine right while describing a limited civil ruling under the king-approved rules of the Roman court.
4. *Practiques* can mean cases, proceedings, or legal business. “Leave the cases” fits the proposed delegation to a papist deputy without implying approval by the faithful judge.
5. *Destroictz* most likely denotes districts or territorial jurisdictions. Because the original letter did not identify them, the response itself refuses a more particular answer.
