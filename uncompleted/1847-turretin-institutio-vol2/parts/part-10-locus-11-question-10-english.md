## QUESTION X

Is only the worship, or also the formation and use of religious images in
sacred places, prohibited by the second commandment?

It is affirmed, against the Lutherans.

I. The preceding question treated the *worship of images*. It remains now to
inquire further into their *use*: in addition to the adoration of images, does
the commandment concerning them also prohibit the very making of them? Here
our dispute will be not only with the Roman Catholics but also with the
Lutherans. Although the latter abhor and condemn the worship of images as
unlawful and superstitious, they attempt to defend εἰκονοποιΐα, *image-making*,
or the formation and use of images in sacred places as lawful—if not for
worship, at least for history and the memory of deeds.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

II. The question is not whether every image of every kind whatsoever is
prohibited by God, even in civil and domestic use, as though the art of
πλαστική, *modeling*, and every kind of painting were condemned equally with
sculpture. Although this seemed right to some of the ancients, both Jews and
Christians, as can be established from various passages in Clement of
Alexandria, Tertullian, and others, they judged every use of images absolutely
forbidden so as to draw Christians away more readily from the Gentiles'
horrible image-mania. But the construction of the tabernacle and temple alone
can teach that this position is wrongly maintained. In them various figures
of cherubim, oxen, and other things were seen, skillfully fashioned by expert
craftsmen whom God had given the mind for this work. Thus we do not condemn
*historical* images of deeds or great men, *symbolic* images by which virtues
and vices are represented, or *civil* images stamped on coins. The subject
here, however, is *sacred and religious images*, which are thought to
contribute something toward stirring up piety.

III. The question is not whether it is lawful to paint creatures and depict
histories of deeds with a brush, whether for adornment, for enjoyment, or even
for instruction and as a μνημόσυνον, *memorial*, of past things. None of us
denies this. Rather, it is whether it is lawful to represent God himself and
the persons of the Trinity by some image—if not by an immediate and proper
likeness expressing a perfect image of God's nature, which the Roman
Catholics confess cannot be done, then at least by analogy or by metaphorical
and mystical significations. The adversaries affirm this; we deny it.

IV. Finally, the question is not whether it is lawful to keep images of holy
men at home for devout remembrance of them and as an example for imitation.
Rather, it is whether it is right to place them in sacred locations, such as
churches and oratories, not for worship and veneration but to remind the
faithful and stir their affections through the memory of past things. The
Lutherans maintain this with the Council of Frankfurt; we deny it.

[Marginal heading: The use of images is proved unlawful. 1. From the second
commandment, Exodus 20.]

V. The reasons are as follows. First, because God expressly forbade this in
the second commandment, where two things are prohibited: both *making images*
for the sake of religion and *worshiping* them. Nor should it be answered,
first, that the commandment means such images by which people attempt to
express God's essence, but not those in which either God or the saints are
represented in their appearances. The falsity of this

[Printed p. 57 | Internet Archive PDF 85 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

is evident from the fact that there would be no need to prohibit this, because
no one is so foolish and insane as to wish to represent God's spiritual
essence itself by some external and bodily symbol. Indeed, if we wish to speak
accurately and philosophically, not even the essence of the least creature
can be expressed, but only its external outlines. Nor, second, should it be
answered that only images of false gods are designated. For Moses himself
clearly explains that God is not to be represented (Deut. 4:12), and God
himself, the best interpreter of his law, intimates this in Isaiah 40:18.
Hence the Israelites, who represented God by the image of a calf, were
severely rebuked and punished (Exod. 32). The godly kings likewise removed the
idols of Jews no less than those of Gentiles, just as God commanded his people
both to demolish the altars of the Canaanites, break their statues, and not
make molten gods for themselves (Exod. 34:13, 17).

[Marginal heading: 2. From the nature of God.]

VI. Because God, as ἄπειρος, *infinite*, and ἀόρατος, *invisible*, can be
represented by no image. Isaiah 40:18 says: “To whom will you liken the mighty
God, and what likeness”—or *image*, as the Vulgate Version has it—“will you set
up for him?” Looking back to this, Paul says in Acts 17:29: “Since we are the
offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Deity is like gold or silver
or stone, a sculpture of human craft and invention.” Hence, when promulgating
the law, God willed to present no likeness of himself, so that the people
might understand that they must abstain from every image of him as a thing not
only unlawful but also impossible. Deuteronomy 4:15 says: “Therefore take
great care for yourselves, for you perceived no likeness on the day when
Jehovah spoke to you,” and so forth, “lest you corrupt yourselves and make for
yourselves a carved image, the likeness of any representation, the form of a
male or female,” and so forth. This is what the apostle condemns in the
Gentiles in Romans 1:23: “They exchanged the glory of the incorruptible God
for a fashioned image of corruptible man, birds, quadrupeds, and reptiles.”

Yet even this was not hidden from various Gentiles themselves, who judged it
impious to wish to represent the Deity by any image. Plutarch says in *Numa*:
“He”—namely, Numa—“forbade any image of God resembling a human being or any
animal. Nor before that time was there among them any sculpted or molded
representation of God. Rather, throughout the 160 preceding years they were
continually building temples and erecting sacred huts or shrines, but they
made no bodily representation, judging it unholy to liken better things to
worse and that we can apprehend God in no way except by the mind alone.”
Likewise Antiphanes says in *On God*: “God is not known from an image, is not
perceived by the eyes, and is like none; therefore, no one can learn of him
from an image.” And Herodotus says in book 1: “The Persians have neither
statues nor altars and think those who make them insane, because they do not
believe, as the Greeks do, that the gods have arisen from human beings.”

[Marginal heading: 3. Because it is joined with the danger of idolatry.]

VII. Third, because whatever does not pertain to the worship of God and is
joined with the danger of idolatry ought to be absent from sacred places. But
images in sacred places do not pertain to the worship of God, since God has
expressly removed them from his worship by the law. They are also joined with
the most immediate danger of idolatry, because people—especially the common
people, who are naturally prone to superstition—are moved to worship them by
the very reverence of the place, as experience testifies. For, as Brochmannus
rightly acknowledges in chapter 7, question 1, *On the Law*: “Any images
should be removed rather than be allowed to remain on display for the sake of
religious worship against God's express command.”

It is objected in vain here that an occasion of sin in itself is indeed
prohibited, but not one that is such by accident; otherwise the sun, which has
given innumerable persons an occasion for idolatry, would have to be removed
from heaven. Therefore, it is said, the abuse should be abolished, but not the
lawful use of images. An abuse certainly ought not to abolish

[Printed p. 58 | Internet Archive PDF 86 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

a lawful use, if some such use is given by God's institution. The adversaries
suppose this; we deny it. Second, it is objected that worship alone renders
images unlawful, and the Lutherans profess to abhor such worship. Reply:
although they do not expressly worship images as the Roman Catholics do—by
falling on their knees, burning incense to them, or pouring out prayers—they
nevertheless cannot be said to be free from all worship. If it is not direct,
it is at least indirect and participated, because they contend that through
images and at the sight of them they conceive holy thoughts about God and
Christ. These thoughts cannot but pertain to the worship of God, so that in
this way they truly worship God through images. Then, even if images are not
worshiped by the Lutherans themselves, they can be worshiped by others—for
example, by Roman Catholics if they enter Lutheran churches. This makes the
use of images in churches unlawful, since it is exposed to the danger of
idolatry, by which image-worshipers are confirmed in their error and
innumerable people are caused to stumble: not only unbelieving Jews and
Muslims but also faithful Christians.

VIII. Therefore, it cannot be imputed as a fault to our forefathers that at
the time of the Reformation they took care to remove all images from sacred
places. Nothing was done here that was not prescribed by God in Numbers 33:52,
Deuteronomy 7:5, and Ezekiel 20:7 and confirmed by the various examples of
kings and emperors who diligently labored to destroy idols and cleanse sacred
places of every appearance of idolatry. Thus Hezekiah “removed the high
places, broke the statues, cut down the grove, and also crushed the bronze
serpent that Moses had made, because down to those days the Israelites burned
incense to it” (2 Kings 18:4). The same was done by various emperors, who
therefore received the name *iconoclasts*.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

IX. Although God sometimes appeared under a visible form and is described to
us under such an appearance in Scripture when bodily members and actions are
ascribed to him, it does not follow that it is lawful to represent him in an
image. First, the same God who appeared in this way nevertheless severely
prohibited the Israelites from fashioning any image of him. God certainly
could make for himself speech, bodies, and symbols by which he attested his
singular presence, but a human being cannot therefore make for God an image
and statue in which God presents himself to humanity. Second, those bodily
appearances were presented only in a vision. They depicted to some degree not
God's essence but his works and external glory; they were extraordinary, not
ordinary; temporary, not perpetual; and offered not publicly to all but to
this or that person, chiefly in the spirit. They therefore have nothing in
common with images. Third, it is one thing to speak metaphorically about God
according to the measure of our capacity; it is another to fashion his
visible appearance as though it were true and proper and set it publicly
before everyone's eyes.

X. The making of images is not absolutely forbidden, but is forbidden with a
twofold limitation: images must not be made to represent God (Deut. 4:16), nor
employed for his worship. Therefore, making images and worshiping them do not
stand in the second commandment merely as means and end, but as two parts of
the divine prohibition. For images are forbidden not only insofar as they are
an object or means of worship, but insofar as they are made simply for the
sake of religion or are placed in sacred locations.

XI. The inference from a *mental image* to a *sculpted or painted image* is
invalid, because the former is a matter of necessity, since I cannot
contemplate any thing without some appearance or idea of it fashioned in the
mind. But this image is always joined with a spirit of discernment, by which
we distinguish truth from falsehood so that there is no danger of idolatry.
The latter image, however, is a work of mere choice and will, which God has
expressly prohibited and which is always joined with great danger of
idolatry. Hence it is no less wrongly said to be a sin to set images of
certain things before the mind, or to

[Printed p. 59 | Internet Archive PDF 87 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

comprehend them in writings and present them to be read, than to place them
before the sight delineated on a panel, since there is a great difference
between these things.

XII. The inference from the figures of the Jerusalem temple to Christian
images is invalid. Those figures were commanded, but these images are not;
those were typological and fulfilled in the New Testament, but these are not;
those were placed almost entirely out of the people's sight and beyond the
danger of adoration, which cannot be said of these. Nor should Christian
liberty be offered as a pretext here. It is not license to do anything
whatever concerning the worship of God, but immunity from the curse of the
law and the bondage of ceremonies. Since those figures belonged to the
ceremonies, they must surely be judged abrogated in the New Testament.

XIII. Images are so far from being rightly called *books of the unlearned*
and aids to religious piety and devotion that the Holy Spirit testifies that
they are *teachers of vanity and falsehood* (Jer. 10:8; Hab. 2:18). There is
another book to be consulted by everyone, skilled and unskilled alike, which
makes people skilled and learned: namely, Scripture, which the faithful must
continually read and meditate upon so that they may be made wise for
salvation. But the pope takes this book from the people so that they may be
entangled in inextricable error and so that he may not be refuted by it. He
substitutes other, mute books, by which ignorance is fostered rather than
removed, because he does not fear that they will mutter anything against that
ignorance. Thus, while he gives stones as teachers, the people are turned into
stones and become no more learned than their teachers.

Hence Augustine, in book 1 of *The Harmony of the Evangelists*, where he
treats images of Peter and Paul through which certain people had fallen into
error, says: “Thus indeed they deserved to err, who sought Christ and his
apostles not in the sacred books but on painted walls.” Second, God would have
dealt badly with the Jews, to whom he denied these books, although, as simpler
people, they had greater need of them.

XIV. Whatever may be said about the usefulness of images in sacred places
cannot and ought not be opposed to God's command prohibiting them; and this
usefulness is assumed without reason, not proved. The sacred signs are the
sacraments, not images. The adornments of churches are the pure preaching of
the Word, the lawful administration of the sacraments, and holiness of
discipline. The means of keeping minds attentive are God's own presence and
majesty and the difficulty and excellence of the sacred mysteries.

XV. It is not sufficient to cast images out of hearts through the preaching
of the Word unless they are also removed from sacred places, where they cannot
remain without danger of idolatry.
