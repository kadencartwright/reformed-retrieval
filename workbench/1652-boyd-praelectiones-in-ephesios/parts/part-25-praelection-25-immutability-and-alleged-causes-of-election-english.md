Now let us bring forward the fourth objection, which belongs to this same
class, an objection drawn from the nature of the good, in the following form.
Whatever God has decreed, especially concerning the supernatural end and
eternal condition of rational creatures, he has decreed most excellently and
wisely. But to have elected a few to salvation while reprobating the majority
(may blasphemy be far from the expression) would not have been to establish
his decree concerning humanity in the best way. This is shown as follows:
it would have been better for all, without exception, to be elected to
salvation. This in turn is proved thus: the more common a good is and the more
people to whom it extends, the better and greater it is, and the more divine.
But the election of human beings to eternal life is a good, and indeed the
greatest good, I say, a good beyond nature and divine. Therefore, the more
common it is—that is, the more people to whom it belongs—the better it is;
consequently, if it were most common, that is, if it extended to everyone,
then it would become by far the greatest and most divine good.

[Marginalia: Objection 4. Lecture XXV.]

We answer this briefly by applying a twofold distinction to the maxim on which
the whole objection rests—“the more common a good, the better it is.” First,
we distinguish a simple good from a comparative good. We call that good
simple which belongs to someone absolutely, without connoting a relation to
another person who is excluded from it. A comparative good, however, has
regard to others who lack that same good—that is, it belongs to one or more
people in such a way that others are barred from it, and thus its nature
consists in a certain selection and separation. Examples of the former kind
are life, light, health, salvation, and so forth. Examples of the latter are
being made a magistrate or a senator, being summoned from the crowd of others
and enrolled by a prince among the number of his friends, and likewise being
chosen for some honor or dignity or for a rank higher than that of others.
In these cases the stated maxim can have no place, because to will to make all
these things common to all and extend them equally to everyone is utterly to
overturn their nature. Here the apostle’s saying in 1 Corinthians 12 applies:
“If the whole body were an eye, where would hearing be? And if the whole were
hearing, where would smell be?” Likewise, “Are all apostles? Are all prophets?
Are all evangelists and teachers?” It is self-evident that the election of
which we speak is of this kind, since by its nature it signifies the selection
of some from among many. Where everyone is assumed without distinction,
therefore, election can have no place.

[Marginalia: Answer 1. The former distinction. Application of the
distinction. 1 Corinthians 12:17 and 29.]

As for goods of the former kind, whose nature is simple and absolute, another
distinction seems necessary. Someone could always insist that at least life
and eternal salvation itself, because it is a simple good, will be a greater
and better good the more common it is—which, if conceded by us, would suffice
to prove the point at issue. We must therefore distinguish the highest good,
that is, the supreme end of all, from intermediate goods. When compared with
that highest end, the latter have the character of means, whether or not they
also have the character of an end in other respects. As for the highest and
supreme end, we grant that the greater it is in every respect, both in
intension and extension, so much the better and more divine it is. It is not
measured by any superior good or end that sets its measure and limit; rather,
it is its own measure and the measure of itself. But with respect to
intermediate goods destined for that end, the stated maxim is by no means
true. Their end is their measure, and they possess the character of good only
insofar as they are subordinated to that end and serve its attainment. If they
exceed the relation and proportion they ought to have to the end and do not
keep within the bounds prescribed to them by the end, they cease to possess
the character of good, because ordered things become disordered. In the
present matter, the manifestation of God’s glory alone has the character of
the end and supreme good. The life and salvation of the elect, however, has
in relation to it the character of a means destined to that end. It therefore
neither can nor ought to extend beyond the measure and relation prescribed to
it by the end, as it plainly would if everyone were saved and life extended
to all.

This is evident from the fact that the end requires an adequate means by which
God’s glory may be manifested both in justice and in mercy. God wills to be
celebrated and glorified in both, and thus, as the royal psalmist says in
Psalm 101:1, he wills “judgment and mercy to be sung to you,” so that his
glory may shine perfectly and fittingly in his creatures. But the salvation
of the elect, in which God’s grace and mercy shine, is not an adequate means
for displaying the divine glory. Therefore, if all were preserved, that means
would exceed the relation it ought to have to its end and would consequently
lose the character of good, because it did not keep its due order toward its
end. Because God in his most wise counsel wills to be displayed and to reveal
himself gloriously in his creatures not only as merciful and kind but also as
just and holy, he therefore decreed neither to have mercy upon everyone nor
to elect everyone to life, but, after electing some, to pass over the rest.
He decreed that these should be more numerous because, as we taught above in
the proper place, this too serves to commend his grace.

[Marginalia: The latter distinction. Note its application.]

[Marginalia: Assumption 1. Proof. Psalm 101:1.]

Finally, as regards the two maxims from which this objection was deduced—“the
greater and better a good is, the more communicative of itself it is, and
therefore the highest good is supremely communicative of itself,” and “a good
is better the more people among whom it is diffused”—each may be suitably
distinguished as follows, in addition to what has already been said above for
their proper understanding. First, if they are understood of extension, so
that the highest good extends most of all and diffuses itself most widely of
all, we confess them to be true. Indeed, we affirm that there is no creature
at all in the world that does not in some measure participate in the divine
goodness, even by the very fact that it is something in the nature of things.
The highest good therefore pours and extends itself most widely of all. But
if you look to intension, so that it should extend to all things equally and
in the same degree, we reject and repudiate this as most absurd and wholly
contrary to divine wisdom. For God willed wisely to arrange many ranks and
orders of things in the world: to grant to some only the lowest degree of
being and to others a more perfect degree; to grant to some only natural,
perishable, and bodily goods, but to others spiritual, eternal, and saving
goods as well, according to his wise and free good pleasure.

[Marginalia: Third distinction. Member 1.]

Next, as regards the maxim taken from Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1.2,
besides what has already been answered above, we may reply that Aristotle
there speaks only of natural and civil good—that is, the good

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 143 | De Prædestinatione p. 99]

that belongs to a human being insofar as he is by nature a political animal,
fitted and born to cultivate society. He is not speaking of the good that belongs to a person
by reason of mind and intellect, insofar as he has something divine within
him and therefore passes a life according to wisdom in contemplation—a life,
I say, more suited to blessed minds than to a human being in this life, as
Aristotle himself acknowledges in the final chapter of book 10 of the same
work. How much less, then, will we estimate the greatness of this eternal good
by its being shared with a greater number? We will recognize it as perfect
and consummated in its highest degree not when it has been extended to
absolutely everyone, including the Devil and the reprobate, but when the
gathering of all the elect for whom this good has been prepared by God from
eternity has been completed.

[Marginalia: Member 2.]

But what need is there to linger over distinctions in matters of this kind?
The divine will alone must be for us the sole and supreme rule of everything
right, equitable, and good. Something must accordingly be called good when
and insofar as God willed and decreed it to be so, and that must be called the
highest good which God willed to be the highest. It is finally perfect, in
respect either of intension or of extension, when it has reached that degree
of perfection and that number of human beings and angels which God determined
to make participants in it. In this way we will both vindicate the glory of
the divine will and always shut the mouth of every person’s unbridled
audacity, whatever they may snarl against it.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the answer. By way of correction.]

The final objection or accusation of this class that we will take up is
directed against the absolute immutability of this divine decree. The earlier
objections were brought forward for the most part to establish a certain
universal election. Just as they wished to break through the walls and
barriers of this decree, abolish its bounds and limits, and expand the sacred
boundary of this city without measure, so this objection attempts to
undermine its very foundations—but with an entirely vain and ineffectual
effort. They say that, once this infallible and immutable decree is posited,
we introduce a certain Stoic fate and establish fatal necessity. Consequently,
all place and use in the Church is taken away from our diligence and labor,
from preaching, admonition, counsel, prayers, rebukes, exhortations, and so
forth, since by all these things the divine will cannot be changed, but God’s
sentence concerning every person always stands firm and unmoved, and his
decree and unavoidable fate remain. Since these things are absurd, such a
doctrine must not be introduced into the Church.

[Marginalia: Objection 5. Force of the argument.]

But since we anticipated this accusation above, at the very threshold and
entrance of this disputation, from the letters of Prosper and Hilary to
Augustine, there is no need to dwell upon it any longer now. Here we say only
that the alleged absurdities do not follow from the stated antecedent and that
the infallible necessity of the divine decree is fully consistent with human
freedom and the contingency of secondary causes. In the same way, the
immutability of the divine will is fully consistent and harmonious with its
supreme freedom. Because God is supremely wise, he wills immutably whatever
he wills; yet he wills and has willed that very thing most freely, because he
alone is self-determining and supremely his own master, independent of all,
so that he depends upon nothing outside himself. We therefore affirm that his
decree, however immutable, is nonetheless most free, because whatever he
determined, he could have left undetermined or determined otherwise if he had
willed. We also affirm that his decree brings upon things themselves a
necessity of such a kind that it does no injury to their free or contingent
nature, provided that they are things that act freely or contingently. It
does not at all take away that freedom or contingency, much less compel
secondary causes or inflict any violence upon them.

This is amply sufficient to banish that Stoic fate, together with Epicurus’s
fortune, from the universe. For just as fortune is defeated by the immutable
and infallible decree of divine providence, so the Stoics’ fate also departs
when the freedom of secondary causes and the contingency appropriate to their
nature are preserved, and especially when supreme freedom in willing and
decreeing and independence from all other causes are vindicated for the first
cause, that is, for God himself. In other words, this fate departs when we
reverently vindicate God’s glory in his supreme right and absolute power over
all things. Even when that fate is rightly understood—for we confess that
some explain it suitably and skillfully enough that it contains nothing false
or impious—we nevertheless abhor and reject the name itself, lest it offend
Christian ears. Together with the term “fortune,” we send it down to the
underworld with the pagans, so that both the name and the doctrine of divine
counsel and providence may take the place of each.

[Marginalia: Member 1 of the answer. Argument from an equal case. Corollary.
Thus Lipsius.]

There will be a more suitable place below to examine the various degrees and
senses of necessity adapted to illustrate this answer, when the ordering of
the first sin or fall and its dependence upon the divine decree must be
treated in the third class of objections.

[Marginalia: Postponement.]

Here we teach only why the other absurdity does not follow from our doctrine
of God’s immutable decree: namely, that there is no use in our studies,
labors, and prayers, or in the admonitions, counsels, exhortations, and
persuasion of others to whom God has committed this duty or upon whom this
office rests according to the rule of charity. They infer as follows: “God’s
decree, which remains firm and fixed, cannot be changed by these things;
therefore, none of them has any use.” The inference is plainly foolish and
frivolous. We do not employ all these things to change God’s decree, but
rather to execute it; not to delay God’s will, but rather to advance and
promote its effect. For when God decreed the end, by one and the same act he
also decreed the means through which he wills to bring us to that end, and he
willed us ourselves to be instruments of those means. He therefore disclosed
those means to us in his word, and disclosed them in such a way that he willed
us to be bound and obligated to them, so that unless we earnestly and
sincerely apply ourselves to those means, we have no reason to hope for the
end.

Therefore, when in the fear and reverence of God we have made our calling and
election firm and sure to ourselves, as Peter says in his latter epistle, by
employing the means prescribed in God’s word and diligently applying
ourselves to them—that is, when by those means we have obtained certain
earnests and pledges of our salvation and election—far be it from us to
suppose that in this way we have changed the divine decree and thereby made
ourselves elect out of the reprobate. Far be so foolish and absurd a thought
from our minds. Instead, let us know that by this very thing God’s counsel
concerning us, appointed from eternity, has actually obtained its effect, and
that from all eternity God wisely ordained us both to the end and to these
very means by which he actually brought us to the end.

[Marginalia: Member 2 of the answer. Confirmation. 1 Peter 1:9b.]

Let this suffice concerning the first class of these objections, which
concerned the end itself and the species or parts of the divine decree.

[Marginalia: Transition to the second class.]

The second class of these objections now follows, concerning the impelling
cause of this decree. We shall not linger over these as we did over the
preceding ones, because we hope that they will be readily and promptly
answered, or will prove so to anyone familiar with the preceding discussion.
Nor shall we bring forward all the objections that the adversaries of the
truth customarily raise, but from the heap of others we shall select only
some of the less frivolous and foolish ones, whether arguments or ineptly
cited testimonies of sacred Scripture.

[Marginalia: Subdivision.]

First, as regards the decree of election, the adversaries have brought
forward only three impelling causes besides that true cause which has already
been set forth and proved above, for which any appearance or reason can be
devised: foreseen faith, good works, and Christ’s own merit. We shall take
these up in order.

First, they claim that the foreseen faith of the elect is an impelling cause
of election itself—if not its principal cause, at least an instrumental and
secondary one. Some of them speak in this most foolish and absurd way, as
though God Most Good and Great had used some instrument to determine whatever
he decreed from eternity. We do indeed confess that he uses instruments to
execute his decree, but to say that he used any instruments to make the
decree itself, which he made within himself, to form the counsel itself, which
he formed within himself, or to determine what he determined in his own mind,
is most absurd and foolish even to imagine. Nevertheless, omitting most of
their other wholly senseless and foolish sophisms, as I warned just now, they
attempt to prove their claim as follows. First, God elected us from eternity
in the same way that he preserves us in time. This is based on the maxim that
in whatever way God executes something in time, in that way he also decreed
from eternity to execute it. But he preserves us by faith, at least as an
instrumental cause. Therefore, he also elected us through faith as an
instrumental cause—that is, on account of foreseen faith as an impelling
cause.

[Marginalia: Part 1, Member 1. Objection 1.]

Answer: The consequence may rightly be denied, at least as regards the
explanatory clause added to the conclusion. For although we are said to be
justified and preserved by faith or through faith as an instrumental cause,
we are not justified and preserved because of faith or on account of faith
as an impelling or meritorious cause. The only exception would be if, by a
metonymy of the adjunct for the subject possessing it, we understood by the
name “faith” Christ himself apprehended by faith. How much less, then, are we
said to be elected on account of foreseen faith as an impelling or meritorious
cause, when we cannot rightly be said in this sense even to be elected by
faith or through faith?

As for the major premise, however, we answer by denying it as false and
absurd. It neither follows nor depends upon that foundation or maxim. Only
this will follow from it: in the same way that God preserves us in time, he
decreed from eternity to preserve us. But he preserves us in time through
faith as an instrumental cause of salvation. Therefore, he decreed from
eternity to preserve us through faith as an instrumental cause of salvation,
and consequently as an effect of that decree, not as its antecedent cause.
We freely confess all of this, and we ourselves taught it above.

[Marginalia: Solution.]

These two statements are very different and have entirely different
meanings: “In whatever way God effects or executes something, in the same way
he decreed it,” and, “In whatever way God effects something, in the same way
he decreed to effect or execute it.” We confess the latter to be true and
have ourselves posited it above as a foundation; we reject the former as
false. For someone could deduce many extreme absurdities from it. For example:
God effects or executes something through many instruments; therefore he also
decreed through the same instruments. Again, he executes in time; therefore
he also decreed in time. Again, he executes successively, one thing after
another, and does not cease executing until all things have been completed
at the resurrection; therefore he also decrees successively and will only
then cease to decree. This is most absurd.

[Marginalia: Note the distinction between these two maxims.]

Second, God foreknew who would believe, as Augustine testifies in Tractate 42
on John, on the words, “He who is from God hears God’s words.” Therefore, he
elected them on account of foreknown faith. Answer: We deny the consequence.
This argument could be brought forward for every future thing God foreknew,
for example even for the good works he foreknew some would perform, though
they themselves confess that claim to be Pelagian. Moreover, as we warned
above in the proper place, Augustine himself, when expounding Romans 11,
“God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew,” interprets “foreknew” not
of bare foresight but of God’s decree, as if Paul had said, “whom he had
ordained and appointed beforehand for grace.”

[Marginalia: Objection 2, with its solution.]

Third, whoever are elect in Christ are elect through faith. But all the elect
are elect in Christ. Therefore, all are elect through faith. The major premise
is proved as follows: no one is in Christ except through faith; therefore, no
one is elect in Christ except through faith. Answer: We deny the consequence,
because these two things are entirely different: to be in Christ and to be
elect in Christ. Only those who actually believe in Christ and are engrafted
into him through faith are said to be in Christ. But all those are elect in
Christ who will ever afterward be engrafted into him and believe in him,
whether they have already been born or have not yet come into the light.

[Marginalia: Objection 3, with its solution.]

Fourth, they also customarily distort several testimonies of sacred Scripture
here no less foolishly. For example, first, in 2 Thessalonians 2 the apostle
gives thanks to God for the Thessalonians as brothers beloved by the Lord,
because God elected them from the beginning to salvation through
sanctification of the Spirit and faith in the truth. Therefore, the foreknown
faith of the Thessalonians was the cause through which they were elected to
salvation. Answer: This is the fallacy of composition and division. The phrase
“through faith” is joined to the verb “elected,” with which it ought not to be
joined, instead of with the nearer word “salvation,” as if Paul had said, “to
that salvation which is through sanctification and faith in the truth”—that
is, whose means of attainment are sanctification and faith. It is therefore
clear that in a thoroughly analytical order the apostle traces the end back
into its subordinate means: resolving from the end to the means,
sanctification is nearest to salvation, and faith in Christ in turn is nearest
to sanctification. Otherwise, because the apostle joined sanctification with
faith, this passage would also make foreseen sanctification an impelling cause
of election. Even the adversaries would not dare profess this, since the
apostle testifies that we have been both elected and called to sanctification.

[Marginalia: Objection 4, from testimonies of sacred Scripture.
2 Thessalonians 2:13. Solution or refutation.]

Second, “Those whom he foreknew, he also predestined.” Therefore, foreknown
faith is an antecedent cause of predestination. Answer: We deny that this
follows, and the passage does nothing to advance their purpose. The apostle
does not speak there about foreseen faith but about the knowledge of approval,
in accordance with the language of Scripture, by which God is said to know
those whom he loves and approves and, conversely, not to know those whom he
hates and rejects. Thus this foreknowledge includes God’s benevolent

[Marginalia: Romans 8:29. Refutation.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 145 | De Prædestinatione p. 101]

purpose of conferring grace and glory, as we taught more fully above from
Augustine, Cyril, and Thomas himself; therefore we do not linger over it here.

Third, Hebrews 11 says that it is impossible for anyone to please God without
faith. From this one might argue as follows. Those whom God elects must be
pleasing to God. But it is only through faith, at least as an instrumental
cause, that people please God and become welcome and acceptable to him.
Therefore, it is at least through faith, as the instrument of election, that
they are elected by God. Answer: We distinguish the ambiguity of the middle
term. “To please God” may be understood in two ways. First, it may mean to be
an object of the divine favor and benevolence as still considered in God’s
eternal purpose—that is, to be regarded graciously and benevolently by God
and taken up into his love and favor. In this sense we grant the major premise
and utterly deny the minor. We say that God found absolutely nothing in the
elect themselves why they should please him in this way above others, but
that from mere grace and gratuitous benevolence he deigned to elect them.
Indeed, he was not even moved or impelled by Christ’s death or merit, much
less induced by any foreseen faith of theirs to pursue them with this wholly
gratuitous favor. But the apostle is not speaking there of what I might call
this “pleasingness,” that is, this way in which someone is said to please God,
for it is a less proper sense and wholly foreign to his purpose.

[Marginalia: Hebrews 11:6. Refutation. Note this distinction. Rejection.]

Second, the phrase may mean actually to be reconciled to God, so that both we
ourselves and our persons, so to speak, as well as our works, are actually
welcome and acceptable to God. The apostle speaks there only of this
acceptance, as is clear to anyone who considers his purpose. In this sense we
utterly deny the major premise while granting the minor. For it is only
through faith, as the instrument that apprehends Christ’s righteousness and
merit, that we are actually reconciled to God, justified, and so forth. But
we were elect not merely before we were justified or reconciled—and therefore
before we were pleasing to God in this sense—but even before we existed at
all. Since it is clear that the apostle’s meaning in this passage and the
sense of these words are different from what would suit the present purpose,
it is manifestly wrong to cite them as proof that we were elected through
faith or on account of foreseen faith.

Finally, although we rely upon the divine authority of sacred Scripture alone
and not upon any person, however deserving of God’s Church, Augustine
nevertheless excellently defends this same truth with us from sacred Scripture
in various places. Therefore, no one should hesitate or take offense if he
encounters a contrary assertion somewhere in Augustine. For example, in
chapter 3 of *On the Predestination of the Saints*, he says that he had
developed an argument so as to say, “God did not therefore elect anyone’s
works in his foreknowledge, which he himself was going to give, but he elected
faith in his foreknowledge, so that he elected the person whom he foreknew
would believe in him.” By these words he frankly confesses that he was once
entangled in the error of supposing foreseen faith to have been the moving
cause of election. We therefore thought it right to note in passing that
afterward, when God revealed it to him, that holy father saw and acknowledged
the truth on this point and consequently retracted that former opinion in
book 1, chapter 23 of the *Retractions* as a remnant of Pelagian assertions.
Indeed, in that same chapter 3 of *On the Predestination of the Saints*, he
immediately adds a refutation of that same false doctrine in these words:
“I had not yet investigated more diligently, nor had I discovered the nature
of election by grace. I would not have said this if I had known that faith
itself is found among God’s gifts. For it is said, ‘I obtained mercy so that
I might be faithful,’ not, ‘because I was faithful.’” Let this suffice for the
objections that can or customarily are brought forward for foreseen faith.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On the Predestination of the Saints*, chapter 3.]

As for foreseen merits of works, which Catharinus, Pighius, and other coarser
and more absurd papal theologians once established as the cause of election,
the Jesuits themselves condemn this error today. They reject as wrongly cited,
or answer and dissolve, the testimonies brought forward by others to support
it, as is clear from Bellarmine, book 2, chapters 9 and following of *On Grace
and Free Choice*. Such are, for example, first, the apostle’s words in
2 Timothy 2: “If anyone therefore purges himself from these, he will be a
vessel for honor, sanctified and useful to the Lord, prepared for every good
work.” From this they reason as follows. If those who devote themselves to
holiness by purging themselves from sins become vessels for honor, then God
predestined people to glory on the basis of foreseen good works. But the former
is true; therefore, the latter is also true.

[Marginalia: Member 2. 2 Timothy 2:21.]

Answer: We deny the consequence, or rather the connection in the major
premise. The apostle’s intention in this passage is not to teach us the
antecedent cause of election, but rather the effect that necessarily follows
it, through which God executes his counsel in the elect. That effect occurs
when by his Spirit of sanctification he grants them the grace by which they
separate themselves from the corruptions of ungodly people, whether in morals
and life or even in doctrine, and, by putting off the old person, devote
themselves to a new purity of mind and obedience of life. The apostle’s only
purpose there, therefore, was to teach us how vessels for honor may be
recognized—that is, who are truly elect from eternity in that hidden counsel
of God which he had called above God’s foundation sealed with a seal. They
are recognized from soundness and holiness of faith and life, according to
Christ’s rule, “You will recognize them by their fruits.”

[Marginalia: Refutation. Matthew 7:20.]

Therefore, when he says, “he will be a vessel for honor,” these words must not
be understood to mean that this person will only then and by this means become
elect in God’s counsel, or that on this account God will make him a vessel for
honor. These are absurd inventions of perverse people and manifest corruptions
of the passage. Rather, only by this means will he declare himself to be truly
elect, and by this most certain sign and proof will he confirm his election
to everyone, as well as the truth of his calling, if he has separated himself
both from the corruption of nature by denying himself and from the leaven of
heresies, and has kept sound faith in a pure heart and good conscience. We
commonly speak in this same sense: “If a thing has this or that mark, it will
be this or that”; “if someone has these or those indications, he will have
this or that disposition, character, or temperament.” A physiognomist speaks
this way, not explaining the causes why someone will be such, but providing
signs suitable for discerning people’s various characters and dispositions.

[Marginalia: Confirmation.]

So too the pagan poet speaks most elegantly of the citron tree, which we now
call *citria* or *citrium*, describing it by its outward form and the
appearance of its leaves: “The tree itself is huge and very like a laurel in
appearance; and if it did not spread abroad a different scent, it would be a
laurel”—“would be” for “would have been,” by poetic alteration—“with leaves
that fall in no winds,” and so forth. Does he therefore say that if this tree
did not breathe a scent different from the laurel, it would become a laurel,
and that nothing except its scent prevents the citron tree from being

[Marginalia: Virgil, *Georgics* 2.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 146 | De Prædestinatione p. 102]

transformed into a laurel? Certainly not. The highly discerning Virgil was not
so absurd and senseless. Rather, he says that for those inspecting the tree
from the outside, this indication of its scent alone discloses that it is not
a laurel; otherwise, by its remaining outward shape and appearance it could
easily deceive anyone into taking it for a laurel. “He will therefore be a
vessel for honor” means: by this mark and indication he will disclose himself
to be among the number of the elect.

Second, in the parable of the talents in Matthew 25, God is said to have
distributed talents to each person “according to his own ability,” as they
interpret it—that is, according to each person’s capacity, “according to his
own power.” Therefore, they say, from foreseen merits of works, or at least
from each person’s foreseen natural disposition, he distributes to everyone
the gifts of his grace, and consequently decreed to give to each on the basis
of the same foresight.

[Marginalia: Matthew 25:15. The former refutation.]

Answer: First, the corrupt old translation gave them an occasion to wander
from the point. In the Greek, as we have rightly translated it, the words
contain no ambiguity, for they mean “according to each person’s ability,”
that is, his skill in doing business. Who does not see that this is said
anthropopathically and in a human manner, so that the Lord might show all the
more clearly that the likeness is drawn from common experience and ordinary
human practice? It is as though everything said in parables ought immediately
to be applied to the matter itself outside or beyond—much less contrary to—the
purpose and intention of the teacher. Otherwise, what is added immediately
below in the same parable, when the Lord says to the wicked and lazy servant
that he ought to have entrusted his money to bankers so that the Lord might
receive it back with interest at his coming, could be taken to establish the
bankers’ profit, though that was by no means in the Lord’s mind in that
passage. He merely explains the matter he undertook to teach and illustrate
in the most suitable way, by a likeness drawn from things customarily done
among people, whether he approves or disapproves the human manner of acting in
itself. The same judgment must be made concerning the music and dancing in
the parable of the prodigal son in Luke 15, which the elder brother is said
to have heard as he approached the house, and other things of this kind.

[Marginalia: Confirmation. Luke 15:25.]

Next, in that passage the Lord does not express even the impelling cause of
the distribution of gifts, much less the cause of eternal election. He merely
indicates the measure of his gifts: as the most prudent father of a household,
he entrusted more or fewer gifts to his servants according as he himself
gave ability to each—that is, skill, prudence, faithfulness, and dexterity in
discharging his calling. Just as he himself supplies the strength and ability
to bear some burden, so he himself places the load upon each and assigns to
each one a duty to administer. It is therefore clear that not only the gifts
themselves, but also the ability to employ and exercise them rightly and
profitably, come from God’s mere grace and gratuitous gift. No one has by
nature that ability to use God’s gifts rightly and holily, as the adversaries
claimed. Along with the gifts, the Lord gives his servants the gift of
exercising them well. In proportion to this gift he measures out to each the
amount of another gift and also lays upon each his own office and calling,
whether in the Church or in the commonwealth. But what does this have to do
with the impelling cause of eternal election?

[Marginalia: The latter answer.]

Third, they reason from the passage containing the words of the apostle Peter
in Acts 10, where Peter plainly said, “Truly I perceive that God does not
regard the person, but in every nation anyone who fears him and works
righteousness is acceptable to him.” They attempt to argue thus: if God does
not regard the person—that is, any race, form, nation, quality, or outward
condition—but regards a person’s own righteousness and holiness, approves
someone, and bestows his saving grace upon him, it will follow that he elected
people on the basis of the foreseen merits of their works. But Peter teaches
and professes the former in the cited passage, and confirms it by the example
of Cornelius called to faith in Christ. Therefore, the latter is also true.

[Marginalia: Acts 10:34 and 35. The objection itself.]

But this argument, both of whose premises are faulty, is easily answered.
First, the prior connection must be denied. Even granting, though not
conceding, the antecedent that in calling people to himself God regards their
righteousness and holiness of life, it still would not follow that he also
elected them from eternity on account of the same quality foreseen. Even if
he called none but those who were already holy, righteous, and feared him—a
claim that is nevertheless utterly false—it still could not immediately be
inferred that he had elected none but those whom he foresaw would be such
before he elected them. God could have decreed from eternity to endow with
holiness and righteousness those whom he had freely elected, and then to
receive them into his Church and call them when they had already been made
such by his gratuitous gift according to the purpose of that eternal election,
without regard to nation or race but only to that holiness and righteousness
freely given to them. The connection of the hypothetical proposition is
therefore plainly false.

[Marginalia: The former part of the answer.]

Next, the assumption is nothing other than a corruption of the cited passage
from Acts, or a false interpretation and distortion into an alien sense. To
make this clearer, we must note in passing the true and genuine sense of the
saying so often inculcated in sacred Scripture, that there is no acceptance
of persons with God. It may be gathered specifically from our apostle in
Romans 2. The impartiality customarily attributed to God is intended to
demonstrate divine justice and the equity of his justice, according to which
he is said to render to everyone according to his works. Therefore, although
the acceptance of persons mentioned here is by no means incompatible with
the utterly free grace of divine election, as will presently be said, it must
nevertheless by no means be extended to election or to its impelling cause.
For election, as we heard above in the proper place, is not a work of justice
but rather of freedom, self-determination, and God’s gratuitous goodness and
mercy.

[Marginalia: The latter part. Refutation. Romans 2:11. The true exposition.]

The sense of passages of this kind, in which God is denied to be an accepter
of persons, is therefore this. Just as an equitable and just judge, in
examining and judging cases, does not regard conditions of the persons upon
whom he is to pronounce sentence that are foreign and extraneous to the case
itself—for example, he does not favor a rich person more than a poor one, or
a friend or acquaintance more than an enemy or stranger, but judges each
person according to the tenor of the laws and the prescription of equity and
assigns to each either punishments or rewards according to his merits—so God
Most Good and Great, the supreme Judge of the world, exercises his judgment
justly toward

[Marginalia: Protasis. Apodosis.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 147 | De Prædestinatione p. 103]

human beings without any acceptance of persons, rendering to each according
to his works, whether by merit, as to the reprobate, or by grace, as to the
elect. He does this without regard to nation, origin, native land, or other
circumstances of this kind that have no bearing on either covenant—whether
people are Jews or Gentiles, Greeks or barbarians, servants or free, kings or
oarsmen. As the just Judge of humanity, God will regard nothing of this kind
in exercising his judgment and in this way will show himself entirely
impartial. He will judge people either according to the justice of the natural
or legal covenant of works, or according to the justice of the gratuitous
gospel covenant. In the former way he will judge all the reprobate who were
never outwardly called to Christ through knowledge of the gospel. In the
latter way he will judge all the rest, both elect and reprobate, who knew
Christ in this life, convicting the reprobate from their evil works of
unbelief but disclosing the faith of believers from their good works. Thus,
according to gospel justice, he will sentence unbelievers to death and
believers to eternal life.

I say gospel justice, not legal justice. It fastens upon the unbeliever the
sentence of eternal death but ascribes to the believer the gratuitous gift of
eternal life. Consequently, to the guilt of evil works it adds the extremely
heavy weight of unbelief as a kind of superior form, increasing and exceeding
by infinite degrees the guilt of all legal sins. Yet it wholly excludes every
merit of good works, which can in no way consist with this gospel justice,
since gospel justice depends only upon God’s gratuitous promise in that
gospel pact and covenant. It can be called justice not in relation to the
person working, who could not properly merit anything from God even if he had
remained upright and incorrupt, but in relation to God who promises and Christ
who merits. For it is just for God to perform and fulfill his gratuitous
promise and to render to the believer, in return for his Son’s death and
merit of infinite worth, a reward of likewise infinite weight—namely, that
eternal weight of surpassingly excellent glory.

[Marginalia: Note the distinction. Note Corollary 1.]

In this respect God’s judgment differs from human judgments. People who wish
to discharge the office of a good judge exercise only legal justice according
to the prescription of laws and equity. They cannot pardon the sins of every
penitent or condemn an innocent person to the punishment of the guilty.
Moreover, they render not only punishments but also rewards as a debt and on
the basis of the merit of works. God, however, can actually render or owe
nothing to anyone by merit except punishments. Through his Son, the innocent
surety substituted for the guilty, he has fully and perfectly satisfied his
justice in a gospel manner and by a gospel means rather than in a legal way.

[Marginalia: Corollary 2.]

As for Peter’s passage, therefore, when he says that he perceives from the
calling of Cornelius that God is by no means an accepter of persons but holds
as acceptable in every nation anyone who fears him and is devoted to
righteousness, he abolishes nothing other than the distinction especially
between Jew and Gentile, circumcision and uncircumcision. Until that time,
even believing Jews had suffered from a perverse opinion about this
distinction, and Peter himself had once been imbued with it. No one was to
suppose from then on that uncircumcision, or what I might call Gentile status,
would hinder anyone from being honored by God with his grace and brought to
eternal salvation, provided that he devoted his mind to true piety and
holiness of life. Peter does not, however, mean there that anyone can perform
this by the powers of nature before being effectually called by God. Far be it
from us to suppose that Cornelius only began to be called into the communion
of the Church when he summoned Peter. He was indeed called through Peter’s
ministry only then to a distinct, and so to speak formed and articulated,
knowledge and faith in Christ. But he had undoubtedly already belonged to
God’s covenant made with the Jews and to the commonwealth of Israel, together
with the rest of the faithful Jews who had lived before Christ’s coming, from
the time when he began to acknowledge and invoke the true God. Although his
faith was still rudimentary and begun, and therefore only formed but not yet
perfect, it was nevertheless true and sincere, and he actually professed and
attested it by those exercises of piety and charity.

[Marginalia: Application to the subject. By recapitulation. Note the
distinction.]

We therefore say briefly that God regards piety and holiness of life as
fruits and indications of faith, on the basis of which he actually holds as
welcome and acceptable to himself in Jesus Christ a believer originating
from any nation. This alone can be gathered from Peter’s words. But what does
it have to do with the antecedent cause of election? To conclude in a word,
when they argue from divine acceptance to eternal election from this passage,
the same distinction may be applied which you remember us using above in
solving the sophism brought forward for foreseen faith from the apostle’s
words in Hebrews 11, that it is impossible for anyone to please God without
faith.

[Marginalia: Conclusion of the answer. See page 101, column 1, A. Rejection.]

As for the remaining passages they misuse in this matter, such as Matthew
20:8, “Call the laborers and pay them their wage,” and Matthew 25:34,
“Possess the kingdom, and so forth, for I was hungry and you gave me something
to eat,” and Revelation 3:11, “Hold fast what you have, so that no one may
take your crown,” the adversaries customarily cite them for the righteousness
of works, for which alone they might have some appearance of support, not to
establish election on the basis of foreseen works. We therefore deliberately
refrain from refuting their distortions of these passages here. We bring
forward only testimonies from which at least some not wholly unreasonable
appearance of an argument may seem to be gathered.

[Marginalia: Add also Revelation 3:4: They will walk with me clothed in white,
for they are worthy.]

As for Christ’s merit, nothing can be brought forward to establish it as this
cause which has not already been answered in our refutation of foreseen faith.
Among other examples are these two. First, we are said in Ephesians 1 and
elsewhere to be elect in Christ. Therefore, we were elected on account of
Christ’s merit. The fault in the consequence is obvious to anyone. In the
constructive part of our treatment of this passage, we taught sufficiently
and at length above how great a difference there is between these two things:
to be elected in Christ and to be elected on account of Christ’s merit. The
latter expression, which we reject, signifies an antecedent cause of election,
which we deny Christ’s merit to be. The former, which we gladly and rightly
admit, designates not the cause but the order of election, as well as the
bond, means, or foundation of its execution, and consequently the head into
whom we who are elected as members must be engrafted. The apostle thus wishes
to teach, first, that the head himself is elect, and then that we are elect in
him as members in the head. In ourselves we were not capable of so great a
dignity and excellence, nor could we have been eternally united to God the
Father and enjoyed his blessed communion except through this intervening bond.

[Marginalia: Member 3. Objection 1. Refutation. Explanation.]

Next, they say that in whatever way we are justified in time, in that same
way we were also elected from eternity. But we are justified in time on
account of Christ’s merit. The major premise must be denied, for it does not
follow from the maxim, “In whatever way God executes something in time, in
that same way he decreed from eternity to execute it.” Only this can be
deduced from it: God justifies us in time on account of

[Marginalia: Objection 2. Rejection.]

Christ’s merit and obedience; therefore, he decreed from eternity to justify
us on account of the same. We freely concede all of this, because it follows
that God decreed that this merit would be the cause of our gratuitous
justification, not that it was an antecedent cause of the decree itself, but
rather its effect or consequence, as is self-evident.
