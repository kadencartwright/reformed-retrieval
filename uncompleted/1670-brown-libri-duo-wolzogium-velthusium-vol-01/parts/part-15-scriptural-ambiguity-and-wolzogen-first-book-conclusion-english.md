XXVII. To such men the things he has on page 73 cannot be displeasing:
*[We confess*, he says, *that many things in the Scriptures are not understood
because of that ambiguity which God did not leave without intention.]* All
acknowledge that very many things are contained in the Scriptures which are
difficult to understand—not because Scripture is obscure in itself, much less
because it is ambiguous, but because such is the weakness of our understanding
that we cannot perceive them without a greater measure of the Holy Spirit.
Our writers place all the blame upon us ourselves and none upon the Scriptures.
Here, however, he openly says that ambiguity is found in the Scriptures and
that God did not leave it without intention. What else is this than what the
papists say and what our writers refute in the disputation *On the Perspicuity
of Scripture? Polanus*, above,

[Page 131 | ONB canvas 185 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000185]

page 311: *[Words*, he says, *and discourses occur as ambiguous in Scripture,
not with respect to God, who dictated them, nor with respect to Scripture,
which is the voice of God, but with respect to people who do not attain or
investigate the meaning of God speaking in Scripture. Thus that ambiguity is
not in Scripture but in the mind of man.]* What else is this than what the
*Socinians* say? See *Smalcius against Frantzius*, page 81. Here, therefore, he
manifestly abandons the Reformed and flies over to the adversaries. Moreover,
the things which follow later on pages 73, 74, and 75 confirm this: *[But it
suffices for us to hold firmly and to be able to learn with certainty those
things which are required for salvation by a certain necessity. In these,
indeed—though it may obtain in some other things also, yet not in all—God has
expressed his will so that we cannot doubt it.]* And again, a little later,
after saying that many uncertain things are found *in other languages*, he
says *[that the account of the Scriptures is no different in many things, so
that we ought to believe we have made no small progress if we now have at
least something ascertained.]* From this it may be gathered that, according to
*Wolzogen*, the Scriptures are for the most part obscure and ambiguous. For as
to those things which are required for salvation by a certain necessity, he
says that they are fewer than is commonly believed and acknowledges that the
Scriptures are perspicuous only in these and in certain others. Indeed, he
wishes them to be so obscure in themselves that we ought to believe we have
made no small progress if we now have at least something ascertained. But what
more insulting thing could any *papist* or *Socinian* have vomited against the
Sacred Writings than what the insolent *Wolzogen* spits out here? He cannot say
that he is speaking here about the perspicuity or obscurity of Scripture with
respect to us, for his purpose would not permit it; and the analogy drawn from
other languages must be understood of the perspicuity or obscurity of a
language in itself, as is clear even at first sight.

[Page 132 | ONB canvas 186 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000186]

XXVIII. *[But*, says *Wolzogen* on page 75, *because not all things are bathed
in so great a light that no study at least and industry are needed to see
through them, it must be investigated what usage can accomplish here. I do
not wish to copy out all the laws of interpretation.]* Answer: Our writers
distinguish between these questions: What is the norm for interpreting the
Scriptures? And what are the means? See *Polanus* above. He, however, confuses
them. When the question concerns the norm, rule, the principle by which, from
which, and through which interpretation and judgment concerning the meaning
ought to be made, he speaks about laws of interpretation and investigates
what usage can accomplish. Our writers grant that this usage can accomplish
many things in the category of a means when, among the means, they number the
inspection of the original languages; the consideration whether words are
taken properly or figuratively; the comparison of one passage with other
parallel and similar passages; and the retention of the words and phrases
Sacred Scripture employs. But they distinguish these from the Norm, Rule,
Measure, Plumb Line, or Principle. Perhaps, however, he therefore insinuates
that they have explained themselves either badly or not fully enough; but I
leave it for the reader to judge whether he or they have more obscured this
whole matter with confused notions. In what follows I see nothing new said by
him: the same things others have said concerning the varied acceptation of
words and the collation of various passages. They have spoken, he says, about
the circumstances of the passage, the antecedents and consequents, the aim of
the chapter or of the general book or of the matter explained, the connection
of words with other words, and the construction itself. Besides these he adds
(what they too do not omit when they enumerate the means) a third thing, which
he locates outside the Scriptures—namely, that *it contains all history, every
art, every science; and whatever is, in short, established outside the
boundaries of Scripture can nevertheless be employed to illustrate it.* But
these things pertain to another question, not to the one now before us. The
present inquiry is not into the means of discover-*

[Page 133 | ONB canvas 187 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000187]

*ing the true meaning, but into the norm itself, or that by which, being moved,
we can establish for certain among ourselves that this and no other is the
genuine meaning of this passage. All these things may perhaps pertain remotely
to that, especially this one which contains all the others within itself:
histories, arts, and sciences. I therefore do not see by what method this man,
who wishes to seem to surpass all others in an accurate manner of speaking,
can refer all these things so generally and confusedly to usage of speech. For
although one who wishes to search out the meaning of some passage must
consider and weigh both the antecedents and the consequents, the aim of the
chapter, the aim of the text itself, and the other circumstances, it can
nevertheless sometimes happen that usage of speech in that particular passage
borrows little or nothing from them. Thus parallel passages do not always
mutually explain the words, syntax, or usage of speech, but sometimes the
subject matter itself which is treated; and therefore he would refer these
more accurately to reason than to usage. Finally, although Grammar, Rhetoric,
or logic can be referred here because they serve to explain and illustrate
usage of speech, all the other arts, sciences, and histories which Scripture
either presupposes or to which it alludes appear able to explain and
illustrate the subject matter itself rather than usage of speech. Let
*Wolzogen* read what *Velthuysen* has above on pages 125, 126, 127, and 128.

XXIX. Besides these things which he has concerning usage of speech, there are
also others which must not be passed over without some observation. Such are
these:

1. What he has on page 81: *[I nevertheless think*, he says, *that all*
(namely, the Scriptures) *must be referred to the same author, because they
all proceed from God; or the scribe, who perhaps sometimes did not know what
he was writing, will have to be confused with the author.]* Answer: The
*Socinians* are accustomed to speak this way. Thus the *Montanists,
Phrygasts, and Cataphrygians* spoke in former times, as *Rivet* rightly says
in his *Introduction to Sacred*

[Page 134 | ONB canvas 188 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000188]

*Scripture*, chapter 11, section 5: *[Nevertheless*, he says, *when the
authors of the Scriptures are said to have prophesied and written, not by
their own will but as the divine Spirit operated by moving them, it must not
be supposed that after the manner of those carried away they did not
understand the things about which they spoke and wrote, or that the same thing
happened to them as to Caiaphas, who unknowingly prophesied that Jesus would
die for the nation, John 11:51. This was the error of the Montanists, or
Phrygists, or Cataphrygians, which the ancients deservedly refuted,
acknowledging that it is repugnant to the mode of the divine presence for it
to render mad or ignorant the one whom it fills with wisdom, and for one who
provides benefit to others to take no benefit from his own discourses.]* He
also adds certain things from *Jerome's prologue to Isaiah* and from
*Augustine, On the Literal Meaning of Genesis*, book 12, chapter 9; see these.
Your elder says the same thing above on pages 85–86: *[I do not doubt*, he
says, *that the prophets themselves often did not clearly perceive the things
about which they prophesied]*; and from this he concludes that the obscurity
of the prophets arose in great part. I ask this one thing: Did those scribes
not sufficiently understand the usage of speech which they employed? If not,
how is it probable that we can understand what they too did not understand?
If they were skilled in that usage and yet did not understand the meaning,
the foremost part in interpreting the Scriptures must not be assigned to
this usage.

2. Pages 86–87: *[Thus*, he says, *after all the laws of interpretation which
the nature of the text supplies have been employed, if in accordance with
them I discover the meaning of the Scriptures, either that meaning is true or
the Scriptures did not proceed from a perfectly good God.]* Answer: Here he
either supposes that we cannot err in employing those laws, or he supposes
that we can. If he supposes that we can err, how will it follow either that
the meaning which we discover while erring is true or that the Scriptures did
not proceed from a good God? If he should say that in that case we do not
discover the meaning

[Page 135 | ONB canvas 189 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000189]

of the Scriptures, which is what he supposes, he argues quite learnedly and
cleverly by making that supposition. For when we perceive the meaning of the
Scriptures, we perceive the true meaning, since the Scriptures do not possess
a false meaning. What! Is *Wolzogen* dealing with children? We are asking
whether through these rules we can always perceive the meaning of Scripture.
If he grants this, then there will be no need of the Spirit's aid, for he
himself acknowledges on page 109 of the *Censure of the Censure* that this aid
must not be numbered among the Rules. But he says in the same place that *when
we err, the Rules are not correctly applied.* Quite right. But are they
correctly applied as often as we employ them? Or must they never be said to be
employed unless they are correctly employed? An excellent escape indeed, but
a ridiculous one! The question always remains: How will we know that they are
correctly employed? I would also like him to explain what he has on pages
111–112 of the *Censure of the Censure*: *[Labadie does not grasp*, he says,
*that to employ all the laws of Interpretation means that everything is
performed, and in the way in which it ought to be performed by man. Then no
one ought to say, “I err.” For that would be a blasphemy redounding against
the Holy Spirit, by whose authorship, in short, I correctly employ all those
laws.]* If the meaning is that so long as and insofar as the Spirit leads us,
then we are not deceived and then we correctly employ those rules, who will
contradict him? For that is a most true proposition, being identical: When I
am not deceived, I am not deceived; and when I correctly employ the Rules, I
correctly employ them. Do you argue thus, *Wolzogen?*

3. On pages 87–88 he has this: *[It may now suffice to have advised by way of
conclusion that everything which has hitherto been discussed tends to this:
Scripture, through the efficacious Spirit, is the interpreter of Scripture.]*
Answer: Fine words, indeed, so that he might pretend that he is proposing no
other position here than that which is defended by the orthodox. But it is
already established in part, and will be established more clearly afterward,
that the matter stands otherwise; and the words immediately following teach
us this well enough: *[But by Scripture*, he says, *in the fir-*

[Page 136 | ONB canvas 190 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000190]

*st place I understand both the discourse of Scripture and the subject matters
themselves which underlie that discourse, insofar as their nature can bear it.
To discourse I refer usage; to the subject matters, reason. But I maintain
that the Spirit causes us to use both correctly. We nevertheless wish this to
be understood in such a way that, from the nature of interpretation, we always
assign the foremost part to usage.]* For although he says that Scripture is
the norm, he nevertheless understands not Scripture but Reason and usage of
speech. He wishes Reason to be regarded as the norm insofar as the nature of
the subject matters can bear it, and usage of speech with respect to
discourse; and he says that the foremost part of interpretation must be
attributed to this usage of speech. But does anyone among the Reformed admit
reason as the norm for interpreting the Scriptures? Do not all who are not
planning a defection to the *Socinians* deny this with one voice? Who,
therefore, will not regard you as a betrayer, since by this account you abandon
the orthodox cause and even flee to the adversaries? Moreover, in the words
just cited it must be noted that he contradicts himself when he says that by
Scripture—when he makes it the interpreter of Scripture or the norm of
interpretation—he understands both the discourse of Scripture and the subject
matters themselves which underlie that discourse. For on page 205 he says
that *those who make Scripture the interpreter of Scripture, if they
understand themselves, can understand nothing other than Scripture's usage of
speech.* But usage here, as he himself says, is referred only to the discourse;
where, then, are the subject matters which underlie that discourse? The
Reformed therefore understand themselves well enough when by the Scriptures
in this passage they understand not the bark, not that material part, not the
discourse alone, but also the formal part—that is, the whole of Scripture
consisting of matter and form, namely both the discourse and the subject
matters underlying the discourse. Or else *Wolzogen* does not sufficiently
understand himself when he says that *Scripture is the interpreter of
Scripture* and that *by Scripture he understands in the first place both the
discourse of Scrip-*

[Page 137 | ONB canvas 191 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000191]

*ture and the subject matters themselves which underlie that discourse.* For
this is something other than Scripture's usage of speech.

4. And these are the things with which he ends his first book and appears to
explain or confirm what precedes: *[Because*, he says, *not all subject matters
admit the examination of reason, and someone who has been aided by no singular
efficacy of the Spirit can truly interpret Scripture in many things. But this
is the special privilege of believers: whenever need exists, they are never
left without divine aid.]* Answer: By this account he appears to prove that
usage of speech must be preferred in interpreting the Scriptures not only to
Reason but also to the Holy Spirit, and that the foremost part must always be
attributed to it. For, of course, not all subject matters admit the examination
of reason (in which, to note this in passing, he contradicts himself, for on
page 68 above he was saying that Reason calls all things, of whatever kind, to
examination), and someone can interpret Scripture well in many things without
being aided by any singular efficacy of the Spirit. It is no answer for him to
say on page 113 of the *Censure of the Censure*: *The superiority of one over
the other is not treated here, because the Spirit and usage of speech are
placed in different genera of cause: the one refers to the object, the other
to the subject. The Spirit holds the foremost part in man; usage, in
Scripture.* For although these things should be true, usage of speech will
hold the foremost part in interpretation, not the Spirit; and thus, insofar as
interpretation is concerned, usage of speech will be preferred to the Holy
Spirit. Then how perfectly do these statements agree with those of the
*Socinians*, if they are not the same? *Smalcius, On the Divinity of Christ*,
chapter 8, says these same things: *[Therefore*, he says, *a man plainly can
accomplish much without that singular work of the Holy Spirit---but God uses
that singular aid of his to assist believers.---Thus also, whenever believers
have need, God has that aid most ready; and if upright men lack anything, he
supplies the whole of it by that aid.]*

[Page 138 | ONB canvas 192 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000192]

From this we can better understand what *Wolzogen's* meaning is: namely, that
we can do many things without the Spirit and that the Spirit merely supplies
our defects. Moreover, when he says that someone can truly interpret the
Scriptures in many things without the special aid of the Spirit, he is
undoubtedly not speaking about saving interpretation; nor ought he to speak
about this saving interpretation in the latter part of the sentence when he
says this is the special privilege of believers. Otherwise this sentence will
cohere badly and will be unintelligible. The course of the discourse requires
him to speak about the same kind of interpretation in both places. From this
it follows that every believer can interpret all Scripture, if the things
*Wolzogen* says here are true. In vain does he reply on pages 114, 115, and
following of the *Censure of the Censure* that by these words, *whenever need
exists*, he understands the things necessary for salvation. For by this
phrase, *whenever need exists*, he cannot understand those things. There is no
particular discussion of these here, but one about Scripture in general; and
he says that anyone can interpret it in many things without the Spirit's aid
or singular efficacy, but that the special privilege of believers is that
whenever need exists, they are never left without divine aid. Who, according
to the common usage of speech, will not interpret these things in this way:
that the meaning is that the ungodly can interpret very many passages of
Scripture without the Spirit, though not all; but that the privilege of
believers is that, whenever need exists—that is, for explaining those passages
which they cannot explain without the efficacy of the Spirit—they are never
left without divine aid. Therefore, because of this privilege, all believers
can interpret all Scripture.
