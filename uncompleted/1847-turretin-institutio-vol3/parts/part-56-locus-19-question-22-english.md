QUESTION XXII.

Why was the Holy Supper instituted by the Lord, and of what parts does it
consist?

I. Since the nature and use of this sacrament cannot be rightly perceived
unless we understand the purpose for which it was instituted by Christ and
what it presents to our mind, before treating the controversies that are
commonly raised in great number concerning it, a few things must be said about
the end of this sacred institution and its various parts, so that we may have
the idea of this entire mystery before our eyes.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 366 | IA leaf 400 | PDF p. 400]

[Marginalia: Why the Supper was instituted.]

II. Once God has received us into his family, not merely to regard us as
servants but as sons, he assumes the role of the best Father, concerned for
his offspring, and undertakes to feed us throughout the continuous course of
life, so that he may continually preserve and refresh the life he once gave,
until we arrive at heavenly immortality. Not content with this, he wished to
make us more certain of this liberality by giving us a pledge. To this end he
gave the other sacrament of his church through his only-begotten Son as a
spiritual banquet, in which Christ testifies that he is the life-giving bread
by which our souls are fed unto true and blessed immortality. For because this
mystery of Christ’s hidden union with us is incomprehensible to our mind, he
presents its figure and image in visible signs most suitably adapted to our
measure. Indeed, by giving us what are, as it were, earnest pledges and tokens,
he makes it as certain to us as if it were seen with the eyes. Therefore, just
as in baptism, the sacrament of our initiation and entrance into the church,
he wished to depict our regeneration, so in the Holy Supper he seals our
nourishment and spiritual sustenance through Christ, in remembrance of the
death in which the food of life by which we are sustained was prepared for us.

[Marginalia: The threefold end.]

[Marginalia: 1. Commemoration of Christ’s death.]

III. The end of this entire institution can therefore be threefold. 1. The
commemoration of Christ and his death. “Do this in remembrance of me” (Luke
22:19), and, “As often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you will
proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor. 2:26 [sic]). This commemoration is
not only theoretical but also practical, a commemoration of the things Christ
did and suffered for us, so that with a grateful mind we may proclaim the
immense love of Christ, who did not refuse to suffer a dreadful death for us
and shed his blood. In the same way, in the ancient Passover, which was a
memorial of deliverance from Egyptian bondage, they celebrated the marvelous
benefit received from God. He wished to give the most suitable symbol of this
in the broken bread and poured-out wine—not only broken and poured out, but
distributed to us—so that even from this it might be evident that all this was
intended and obtained for us, and that Christ offered himself to God as a
victim to expiate our sins for no other end than that he might afterward
become food for us, on which we feed and by which we are sustained unto
eternal life.

[Marginalia: 2. Our union with Christ and communion in his benefits.]

2. Our union with Christ and communion in his benefits, which are represented
to us most fittingly through eating and drinking, since food eaten by us is
united most closely to our nature and coalesces with it into one. Hence
participation in the symbols is called communion in the body and blood of
Christ (1 Cor. 10:16), and Christ is said “to abide in us, and we in him” (John
6:56). And he so abides and is united with us that we may call whatever is his
our own.

[Marginalia: 3. Assurance of the remission of sins and eternal salvation.]

3. From this follows assurance of the remission of sins (Matt. 26:28) and of
the eternal salvation obtained for us through Christ’s death, which can no
more fall away from us than the merit of his death and the efficacy of his
Spirit can be vain and ineffectual.

[Marginalia: The matter consists of two symbols. Why they were instituted.]

IV. All these things are presented to us most suitably in this sacrament, as
can easily be evident to one who considers each of its parts more attentively,
whether we look to the symbols instituted by Christ, the rites and ceremonies
employed about them, or the mystery hidden beneath them and the thing
signified with its various effects. For as regards the symbols, which occupy
the place of external matter together with the actions performed about them,
two things, bread and wine, were instituted by Christ to occupy the place of
elements—neither more nor fewer. He wished them to be so joined that they
cannot be separated without sacrilege. For although in baptism Christ
instituted only one elemental sign, because water is sufficient for washing
away bodily filth, in the Supper he wished to employ a

[1847 vol. III printed p. 367 | IA leaf 401 | PDF p. 401]

twofold sign, both for a clearer representation of Christ’s death and the
separation of his body and blood in it, and to signify complete nourishment,
which needs drink no less than food, so that it might be evident that in
Christ we have whatever is required for full nourishment and for life and
sustenance. Therefore he wished to institute two signs, not signs that make a
twofold sacrament—although the ancients often speak here in the plural as if
of two sacraments—but one, because there is one formal rationale of each,
namely, the signification of full nourishment through Christ, dead and
crucified for us. Hence, although he is presented to us in the word under
various other ideas—the foundation by which we are supported, the head by
which we are governed, the garment by which we are covered, the light by
which we are illumined, and other things of this kind—in the Supper he is
presented to us solely under the idea and symbol of food, because the matter
at hand is the sustaining and refreshing of the life given to us through the
regeneration depicted in baptism.

[Marginalia: The bread should not be unleavened.]

V. Christ used bread because by divine blessing it is the most effective,
most common, most familiar, and everywhere available of all the elements for
nourishing and strengthening the body; it is easily obtained and causes the
least distaste. The bread he used was unleavened, not from the necessity of
the thing, but from the accidental circumstance of the time, on account of
the feast of Passover, during which it was lawful by the law neither to use
another kind nor to have any other (Exod. 12:19). At other times it was always
leavened, both in Judea whenever the Supper was celebrated outside that season
(Acts 2:42), and among the Gentiles, who used common bread, not unleavened
loaves. Hence it was called simply “the breaking of bread” (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor.
10:16; 11:26, 27). Thus a most bitter controversy arose wrongly over this
head between the Greeks and Latins, the former insisting on the necessity of
leavened bread and the latter of unleavened loaves. Hence the former were
given the name “Fermentarians” by the latter, while the latter were called
“Azymites” by the former. Yet the thing is in itself indifferent, as most of
the papists acknowledge, so the peace of the churches should not be disturbed
on its account; it should be left to their liberty, since it is evident that
the practice varied both long ago and even today. Nevertheless, we do not
deny that the use of leavened bread appears more fitting to us: first, because
it accords better with Christ’s purpose, which was to use common and ordinary
bread that would be available everywhere; second, because it is better suited
to sustain the sacramental analogy, which consists in signifying our
communion with Christ through the likeness of bodily nourishment; third,
because the necessity of unleavened bread belongs to the Jewish ceremonies,
which have been abrogated and cannot be retained without a certain “Jewish
affectation” (*kakozēlia*, κακοζηλία); and fourth, because in the entire
ancient church no traces appear of the common use of unleavened loaves in the
Eucharist before the ninth or tenth century. Indeed, it is evident that in
former times the eucharistic symbols were taken from the offerings of bread
and wine made by believers, which without doubt were of ordinary, leavened
bread, as James Sirmond the Jesuit candidly acknowledges and solidly confirms
with many arguments in his book *On Unleavened Bread*. Nor can or should
Christ’s example be alleged here, because, as was said, there was a unique
reason that moved him to use unleavened loaves, and it no longer obtains.
Thus his example binds us with regard to the essence of the thing itself, so
that we do all the things he did—taking, blessing, and breaking bread, and
other things of this kind recorded by the sacred writers—but not immediately
with regard to every circumstance that does not pertain to the thing. Suarez
observes this in his commentary on the third part of Thomas, *On the
Sacraments*: “Christ’s deed alone,” he says, “is not sufficient to introduce a
precept; for although, for example, he consecrated in white wine or at an hour
of the night, he did not thereby command us to consecrate in this way.” Nor,
if unleavened loaves have a mystical signification, are they immediately of
divine institution, since it is evident that the reason for their institution
was the memorial of the hurried departure from Egypt.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 368 | IA leaf 402 | PDF p. 402]

[Marginalia: The wine is common wine, not diluted with water.]

VI. Common wine is instituted, its color being indifferent, not diluted with
water, because it is called simply the produce or fruit of the vine (Matt.
26:29; Mark 14:25). Thus the papists here unreasonably insist on mixing water
with the wine. According to them, although this does not belong to the
integrity of the sacrament, it does belong to its fittingness and cannot be
omitted without mortal sin, as Bellarmine maintains. Although the matter
appears indifferent, and therefore can be omitted or retained provided the
order of the church is not disturbed, and although it is clear that the
ancients mixed water with wine, because the use of pure, unmixed wine is rare
among eastern peoples, they are nevertheless rightly reproved because, with
no word of Scripture preceding them, they invent mortal sins and condemn the
liberty of others.

[Marginalia: The rites concerning the symbols are either Christ’s deeds or
words. His deeds are the taking and blessing of the bread.]

VII. The rites employed concerning these symbols in the institution are
either Christ’s deeds or his words. The deeds contain the norm and example of
administration; the words, the doctrine of administration and use. The deeds
are (1) the taking of the bread and its blessing, for he is said to have taken
bread and to have blessed or given thanks. Matthew 26:26 has *labōn ton arton
kai eulogēsas eklase* (λαβὼν τὸν ἄρτον καὶ εὐλογήσας ἔκλασε), “having taken
the bread and blessed it, he broke it,” whom Mark follows. Luke 22:29 [sic] has *kai
labōn arton eucharistēsas eklase* (καὶ λαβὼν ἄρτον εὐχαριστήσας ἔκλασε), “and
having taken bread and given thanks, he broke it,” whom Paul follows (1 Cor.
11:24). This shows that these two verbs, *eulogein* (εὐλογεῖν), “to bless,” and
*eucharistein* (εὐχαριστεῖν), “to give thanks,” are taken in the same sense,
as is evident from Matthew 14:19 and Mark 6:41. Thus the consecration of the
bread and wine is denoted, or their preparation for sacred use through
prayers and thanksgiving. This was drawn from the common rite of the Jews,
who offered thanksgiving before taking food and ate nothing that they had not
previously consecrated with prayers and blessings, whether for the use of this
life or for sacred use, as is evident from Jewish rituals. Christ is recorded
as having done this more than once in the miracle of the loaves (Matt. 14:19;
Mark 6:41; John 6:11). Looking to this, Paul says, “Every creature is good and
nothing is to be rejected, provided it is received with thanksgiving, because
it is sanctified through the word and prayers” (1 Tim. 4:4): through the word
on God’s part, who grants us its use and gives it the power of nourishing;
through prayers on our part, insofar as by blessing God through them and
seeking his grace we obtain a blessing upon the food set before us and the
right to use it. But this rite, customary in the ordinary use of food, is far
more necessary in sacred use, and was observed more diligently by the Jews at
sacred banquets. Thus in eating the Passover there was a particular formula
of blessing or thanksgiving in the consecration of the unleavened loaves:
“Blessed be the Lord our God, king of the world, who redeemed us and our
fathers from Egypt and commanded us to eat unleavened loaves.” And in the
consecration of the cup: “Blessed is the Lord our God, king of the world, who
created the fruit of the vine.” It is altogether likely that Christ drew from
this the designation “fruit of the vine,” which he gives to wine when speaking
of the cup. Therefore Christ, who readily accommodated himself to the custom
of the Jews whenever there was nothing contrary to truth and piety, suitably
employed this mode of consecration as altogether necessary to the institution
of the sacrament, so that the symbols might be transferred from common and
profane use to sacred and mystical use. Hence Œcumenius, on 1 Corinthians
10:16, most fittingly explains *ho eulogoumen* (ὃ εὐλογοῦμεν), “which we
bless,” as *ho eulogountes kataskeuazomen* (ὃ εὐλογοῦντες κατασκευάζομεν),
“which we prepare by blessing.” The change made through this consecration was
not a real change of substance, but only a moral change with respect to use,
so that it might be the sacrament of his body and blood. Moreover, this
blessing primarily regarded God, to whom blessing and thanksgiving are
rendered for the benefit of redemption, in such a way that it was also
extended to the symbols themselves, upon which God’s blessing was called down
by such prayers, just as we bless the symbols through prayers that we offer to
God for their consecration. Thus the symbols themselves can rightly be said to
be blessed. Although

[1847 vol. III printed p. 369 | IA leaf 403 | PDF p. 403]

the evangelists do not recount any express formula employed by Christ—so that
the difference between this consecration and pagan or magical consecrations,
which consist of a fixed number of words, might thereby be evident, and so
that the church might be left at liberty to employ whatever formula it
pleased, provided it was suited to the mystery—there is nevertheless no doubt
that he used words most suitably adapted to the nature of the thing.

[Marginalia: 2. The breaking of the bread.]

VIII. The second rite was the breaking of this blessed bread, for “Christ”
*eulogēsas arton eklase* (εὐλογήσας ἄρτον ἔκλασε), “broke the bread after he
had blessed it.” Although this rite was also drawn from the Jews, among whom
the head of the household, after pronouncing the blessing, broke the bread
and distributed it to the guests, there is no doubt that something unique and
“mysterious” (*mystēriōdes*, μυστηριῶδες) occurred here. For Christ wished to
break bread after the supper had already ended, when not only did the guests
no longer need food, but this was also forbidden to them by the custom
observed at Passover. Therefore this rite of breaking bread was carefully
noted by all the evangelists and by Paul. Indeed, the entire action of the Holy
Supper is named from this ceremony as “the breaking of bread.” From this too
it may be evident that the rite is not free and indifferent but essential and
necessary. Although it does not properly pertain to the essence of the
sacrament itself, it nevertheless pertains to its integrity, not only because
of the example of Christ (Matt. 26) and the apostles (1 Cor. 10:16), but also
from the mystery of its signification, according to which it pertains to the
very matter and form of the Supper: to the matter because, as a part, it
completes it; to the form because it represents the crucifixion of Christ and
the tearing of his body, as well as the mystical communion of believers from
one body. Therefore this breaking must not be thought simply to regard the
necessity of distribution, because the Jews’ loaves, which were broad like
flat cakes rather than thick, were customarily broken, not cut. Rather, it
further contained the mystical signification of the broken body and the
poured-out blood, as Christ himself explains.

[Marginalia: 3. Distribution.]

IX. The third rite is the distribution of the broken bread and poured-out
wine, by which believers are taught that Christ with his own hand gives
himself, with all his benefits, to be used or enjoyed by them. No other
minister of this distribution at the first Supper is to be sought than Christ
himself, who alone was the dispenser of this mystery and who first sanctioned
and sanctified its dispensation by his example. Although the rite of
distribution varied among the ancients, without doubt the mode Christ used is
to be judged best: that the people receive the sacred signs from the hand of
the presiding minister.

[Marginalia: Christ’s words.]

X. These are Christ’s deeds concerning the symbols. To them must be added the
words or sayings that accompanied those deeds, which are of three kinds. Some
contain a command, others a promise, and others an explanation of this
mystery.

[Marginalia: They are, 1. Of command.]

The command concerns either the administration or the use and participation
of this sacrament; the former pertains to its dispensers, the latter to the
communicants. For it imposes upon ministers the necessity of dispensing this
sacrament and upon everyone the necessity of receiving it, adding the norm
and form to be observed by both in celebrating this mystery. These words
pertain to the former part of the command: “Do this,” that is, do what Christ
did, setting his example before us for imitation—take bread and wine,
sanctify them by thanksgiving and prayers, break the bread, pour out the wine,
and present both. Thus the norm and necessity of administration are
prescribed to those who dispense it.

XI. These words pertain to the latter part: “Take, eat, and drink.” In them he
commands two things to the communicants when he wills them to do what Christ
had commanded: that they “take,” and then that they “eat and drink.”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 370 | IA leaf 404 | PDF p. 404]

The word “take” is properly to be understood of the hand, which the force of the
verb *lambanō* (λαμβάνω), used by Christ, implies. Hence it is inferred that
Christ held out the bread to be grasped by hand, but did not put morsels of it
into their mouths, as is customary among the papists. Second, he commands them
to “eat” and “drink.” Here the communion of the sacrament and its use to
signify our communion with Christ are denoted, by virtue of which we become
participants in all his benefits. For just as no closer conjunction is given
than that between food and the body nourished by it, so our communion with
Christ could not be designated more suitably than by eating, through which
Christ, the food of our souls, when apprehended by faith, becomes one with us.
There is this difference, however: bodily food is converted into our
substance, but spiritual food, because it is more powerful and perfect,
changes us in a certain manner into its own substance, so that we may be
conformed to Christ, live the life of Christ, and be participants in the
divine nature (2 Pet. 1:4). And just as food is twofold, namely, bodily and
spiritual, so a twofold eating must be understood here: the oral eating of the
bodily signs, and the spiritual eating of the thing signified, or Christ’s
body and blood. Therefore Christ wills believers to eat this sacrament in
both ways: in the former with the mouth of the body as regards the sign, and
in the latter with the mouth of the soul, namely faith, as regards the thing
signified. They are to eat this bread not merely orally, “insofar as it is
bread,” but also spiritually, “insofar as it is a sacrament,” by meditating
upon and applying to themselves all the mysteries represented beneath these
symbols. What is said of the bread must also be understood of the cup, which
is the other symbol and participation in which is equally commanded to
everyone, as will be proved in its own place.

[Marginalia: 2. Of promise.]

XII. The word of promise is that which declares the thing signified: “This is
my body, which is broken for you; this is my blood, which is poured out for
many for the remission of sins.” For the sake of explanation, we briefly
distinguish three things in these words: (1) the subject; (2) the predicate;
and (3) the mode of predication made by the copula “is.” In the words about
the bread and the cup, the subject in each is the demonstrative pronoun
“this,” taken not adjectivally but substantively. Since the pronoun does not
stand for itself but for another noun either expressed or understood, it is
evident that this pronoun refers to some noun in the text, expressed or
understood. This can be nothing other than bread and wine, because it can
demonstrate nothing other than that which Christ had taken and broken,
namely, the bread. The attribute is Christ’s true and natural body, which was
delivered for us—not simply as the body and blood of Christ, for as such they
are neither presented to us nor benefit us (John 6:63), but his body insofar
as it was given unto death for us and broken on the cross, and his blood
insofar as it was poured out for the remission of sins. For only in this way
were they made the food and drink of life for us, according to the saying,
“The bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the
world” (John 6:51). The mode of predication indicated by the copula is not
proper and substantial, because the bread and wine are not properly Christ’s
body and blood, nor is the substance of Christ’s body and blood the substance
of bread and wine. Nor is it synecdochical, as though the body were contained
in the bread and the blood in the wine. For Christ does not teach where his
body and blood are or in what receptacles they are contained, but what use and
office they have in the Supper. Rather, it is the sacramental and metonymical
mode customarily used in all the sacraments, in which the name of the thing
signified is predicated of the sign. Thus those figurative words are to be
resolved into proper ones: the bread and wine are signs and seals of Christ’s
body and blood. This accords with Paul’s exposition (1 Cor. 10:16), where he
calls the bread the communion of Christ’s body and the cup the communion of
his blood. In this way the trope is located neither in the subject nor in the
predicate,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 371 | IA leaf 405 | PDF p. 405]

but in the copula “is,” where a mystical and sacramental being is understood,
in which one considers not what the thing is in itself but what it signifies.

[Marginalia: The form of the Supper.]

XIII. From this is gathered the true form of the Holy Supper, which consists
in the conjunction of the signs and the thing signified, through which the
sign and the thing signified are one certain thing by divine ordination
declared in the word of the sacrament. They are one, however, not in genus,
species, or number, but by analogy. Hence it follows that this union is not
natural, occurring through physical contact, nor local, through absence of
distance and the inherence or coexistence of one in the other, nor
hypostatic, but analogical, relative, and sacramental. It consists especially
in three things. 1. In signification, through which the external symbols
function as representative and commemorative signs, while the internal thing
functions as represented and commemorated. 2. In the sealing of the thing
signified, through which the elements function as a seal, pledge, and earnest,
while Christ’s body and blood function as the thing sealed. 3. In the
presentation of the thing signified and sealed, insofar as the external thing
is a moral instrument through which and with which God wills to act
effectively and really communicate the internal thing to the believer. The
signification consists in the likeness between the properties and effects of
the sign and of the thing signified, which have a great affinity with one
another. As bread and wine are the most sufficient and effective means for
sustaining and preserving bodily life, so Christ’s body and blood furnish
nourishment to the soul and are the most sufficient and effective means for
fostering and preserving spiritual life. As the bread and wine are separated
in the Supper, so Christ’s body and blood were separated on the cross. As the
bread is broken and the wine poured out, so Christ’s body was broken on the
cross by pains and torments, when his heart was torn by the sense of God’s
wrath, his hands and feet were pierced with nails, his side was transfixed by
a spear, and his blood was poured out there. As bread and wine do not nourish
unless they are received orally, so Christ’s body and blood do not nourish
the soul when they are considered only speculatively, but when they are
received practically by faith and applied to us. Finally, as the communicants
all participate in one bread, so believers participate in one Christ and
coalesce with him and with one another into one body through faith and love
(1 Cor. 10:16, 17).

XIV. Second, the form consists in sealing, through which God not only
represents these mysteries through the likeness of the sign to the thing
signified, but also seals them by the force of God’s institution, so that
believers are rendered certain of their possession through the application of
this divine seal to the communicants. So Christ gave the bread to be eaten and
the wine to be drunk individually to the disciples, so that they would have a
divine testimony of the remission of their sins and the salvation acquired by
the crucifixion of Christ’s body and the shedding of his blood. Third, it
consists in the presentation of the thing signified and sealed. For these
symbols do not merely represent the things or only seal them; they also
present them in their own manner. So by the breath of his mouth Christ not
only signified but also presented and communicated his Spirit to the
disciples (John 20:22). This presentation, however, occurs not by the force of
the signs but by God’s order; not to the tooth but to the mind; not to the
mouth but to faith. Therefore, when faith is present, spiritual presentation
is joined with the oral reception of bread and wine. Hence follows Christ’s
presence in the Supper, which is indeed true and real, not fictitious and
imaginary, but not bodily, as if Christ were enclosed in the substance or
accidents of bread and wine. For although Christ’s body and blood are
received with the signs by believers *hama* (ἅμα), that is, simultaneously in
time, they are not presented *homou* (ὁμοῦ), that is, simultaneously in
place. Christ’s body and blood are therefore present, offered and presented
in the accompanying promise of grace received by faith. Faith, however, does
not effect this presence; rather, the divine institution and promise of grace
do so. Thus, properly speaking, the truth

[1847 vol. III printed p. 372 | IA leaf 406 | PDF p. 406]

of the presence of Christ’s body and blood comes from the promise of grace,
but its reception comes from faith. Thus Christ’s presence in the Supper can
be fourfold: relative in the signs, objective in the mind, a presence of union
in the heart, and a presence of efficacy in the whole soul, as will be treated
more fully in its own place.

[Marginalia: 3. Of institution or explanation.]

XV. We said third that in Christ’s words one must attend to the institution
or explanation of this mystery, which he adds to the word of command and
promise in order to expound the end of this entire sacred action when he says,
“Do this in remembrance of me.” The apostle explains this as “the proclamation
of his death” (1 Cor. 11:26). Here remembrance is understood not as bare and
merely historical, but as a living and effective practical application of
Christ’s merit and its apprehension through faith. The proclamation of
Christ’s death also signifies not the simple narration of it, but public and
solemn thanksgiving for so great a benefit, which is performed when we recall
Christ’s obedience and benefits with a grateful mind. Hence it is evident
that the Supper was not instituted to be a real and propitiatory sacrifice for
our sins, but rather to be a spiritual and eucharistic sacrifice, consisting
in solemn thanksgiving for Christ’s incarnation, death, resurrection, and all
the benefits acquired for us through him, as will be shown in its own place.

XVI. From all these things it is gathered that the Holy Supper can be
considered in two ways. It can be considered simply and separately with
respect to the external symbols and the rites employed about them. In this
way it is called a sacrament instituted by Christ for the commemoration of
Christ’s body delivered unto death and his blood poured out, and for the
confirmation of our faith. Or it can be considered compositely and conjointly
with respect to the complex of the signs and the thing signified. In this way
it is called the other sacrament of the New Testament, in which, through the
eating of broken bread and drinking of poured-out wine, communion in the body
and blood of Christ and in all his benefits is signified and sealed to
believers for the commemoration of his death and the confirmation and increase
of faith.
