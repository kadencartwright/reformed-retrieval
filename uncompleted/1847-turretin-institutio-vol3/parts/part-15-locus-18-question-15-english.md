QUESTION XV.

Are the Evangelical and Reformed churches the true church of Christ? We
affirm.

[Marginalia: The Reformed churches are proved to be the true church.]

I. Since it is evident from what was said in the preceding question that the
present-day Roman church is not the true church of Christ, it can be no less
clear from the rule of opposites that this title cannot be denied to the
Reformed and Evangelical churches, which separated from the Roman church, and
that salvation can therefore certainly be obtained in their communion,
whatever the papists may determine to the contrary by their rash and most
unjust judgments.

II. Nor is it difficult to prove this further from the things said above
concerning the marks of the church. For since it has been established that the
true church is precisely the one in which the pure preaching of the word and
the religion instituted by Christ and the apostles obtain, it cannot be denied
that our churches are rightly designated by this name if it is true, as we
contend, that our faith and religion are no different from the faith and
religion of Christ and the apostles.

III. That the matter truly stands this way is plainly evident from an
examination of the religion itself. However it is viewed—whether with respect
to faith, worship, or government, the three things it usually embraces—nothing
occurs in it which is not supported by God’s word and was not handed down and
instituted by Christ and the apostles, as can be established by an examination
of each head. This is the primary foundation of our faith, to which we have
always appealed and still appeal. Nor do we plead against our churches being
held false by that very fact if anything is taught by us beyond or contrary to
God’s word.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 118 | IA leaf 152 | PDF p. 152]

But if, on the contrary, it is certain that nothing is handed down by us,
whether concerning faith or concerning morals and worship, which does not
agree with the doctrine handed down in the word by Christ and the apostles, no
one can doubt that the truth of faith and religion necessarily draws after it
the truth of the church which professes them. If the adversaries wish to deny
this or call it into doubt, we must come to the examination of doctrine which
they avoid so zealously.

IV. I know that the papists fault various things in our doctrine which they
persuade themselves are opposed to truth as well as godliness and on account of
which they customarily fasten the mark of heresy upon us. But a discussion of
the matters themselves can make it clearer than the light of midday that this
accusation is most unjust. From it the following is evident. 1. Some things are
fastened upon us by slander and most falsely, although we are entirely foreign
to them and detest them with our whole heart: that we make God the author of
sin because we assert that his providence concerning sin is efficacious; that
we deny God’s omnipotence because we teach that God cannot bring it about that
Christ’s body is essentially present in several places at once; that we
overturn the equality of the divine persons because we attribute a
*preeminence* of deity to the Father but refuse to say that the Son is *God of
himself*; that from divine providence we impose an absolute, fatal, and Stoic
necessity upon all things; that we make all sins equal because we contend that
all are mortal and none venial; that we deny Christ’s true humanity by taking
personality away from it; that we assert that Christ despaired on the cross;
that we deny inherent righteousness and acknowledge no necessity of good
works; and other things of this kind. No one who knows our faith and has read
our public confessions and symbolic books can be unaware that these charges are
most false and were attached to us by mere slander, as we have shown throughout
this work under each particular head. 2. Other things are wrongly drawn by
most false consequences from our true principles: that the doctrine of
predestination as handed down by us extinguishes all religion in human hearts
and teaches either profanity or despair; that we remove the moral law from our
midst by removing the necessity of good works; that in calling we make man a
log and a block, since according to us he is merely passive in the first moment
of calling; that in justification we judge that man is not made righteous in
himself but remains a sinful, unrighteous, and unclean man and is only reckoned
righteous in Christ; that however much people sin, they ought nevertheless to
be certain of their perseverance; that through the doctrine of the certainty
of salvation and inamissible righteousness we corrupt the idea of true
perseverance, abolish every use of fear, and destroy the nature of the
Christian virtues; and quite a few other things of this kind. Anyone who
weighs them more attentively will find that they are entirely foreign to our
doctrine rightly understood and can be drawn only by violent consequences
twisted against the nature of the matters themselves, as we have proved in
their proper places. Or, 3. things which are most true in themselves and
agreeable to God’s word are most unjustly accused of falsehood, such as the
things we teach against the invocation of saints and the worship of images,
against purgatory and human satisfactions, against transubstantiation and the
sacrifice of the Mass, against the primacy of the pope, and the rest by which
we oppose papal errors. Or, 4. the harsher words and private opinions of
certain doctors, never received by the church, are alleged as its public and
constant faith.

V. But these things will shine forth more clearly if we conceive in the mind
the true and genuine idea of our religion, opposed to the false and deceptive
ideas which the adversaries customarily fashion through mere slander,
ignorance, and blind prejudices in order to defame it, not only as absurd and a
mere skeleton without nourishment and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 119 | IA leaf 153 | PDF p. 153]

unction, stripped of every ornament, but also as ungodly and impure, worthy of
everyone’s hatred and detestation. Yet in reality nothing holier and purer,
nothing worthier of human love and veneration, can be conceived. Our religion
is that which is entirely occupied with knowing the one and triune God,
Creator, Preserver, and Redeemer, and duly worshiping him according to his own
prescription. It credits the glory of our salvation entirely to God alone and
ascribes solely to man the true cause of his sin and ruin. Our religion
acknowledges no other rule of faith and morals besides sacred Scripture, no
other Mediator and Head of the church than Christ, no other propitiatory
sacrifice than his death, no other purgatory than his blood, no other merit
than his obedience, and no other intercession than his prayers. Our religion
determines that God alone is to be adored and invoked, and does not allow the
glory and religious worship owed to him to be transferred to creatures. Our
religion humbles man as much as possible by taking from him every presumption
of his own powers and merits; and it supremely exalts God’s grace and mercy by
proclaiming it the sole cause of salvation, with respect both to its
acquisition and to its application. Our religion declares war on every vice,
commends every virtue, and urges the necessity of holiness and good works for
salvation. It locates godliness and worship not in bodily exercises, which
profit little—such as distinctions of foods, observance of festivals, fasts,
pilgrimages, scourgings, and other external ceremonies and forms of
*will-worship* which God never commanded—but in worship in spirit and truth,
which consists in a pure heart, a good conscience, unfeigned faith, love, and
the practice of good works. Our religion brings firm peace and consolation in
life and death to the faithful soul through the true confidence which it
commands to be placed, not in the uncertainty and vanity of one’s own
righteousness or human satisfactions, but in God’s mercy alone and Christ’s
most perfect righteousness. When applied to the heart through faith, this
removes doubt and distrust and engenders a living persuasion of salvation
after this life. Our religion not only does not forbid the reading of sacred
Scripture as dangerous, but commands it as most useful and supremely necessary.
It does not will sacred rites to be performed in a foreign language through
which the wretched people neither understand God speaking nor understand
themselves and are kept in blind ignorance, far removed from the mysteries;
rather, it commends the use of the vernacular language known to everyone so
that the edification and instruction of all may be served. Our religion
imposes upon everyone the obedience owed to higher powers, and judges that no
mortal can claim for himself the right to depose kings and absolve subjects
from their oath of fidelity without great wickedness and sacrilegious audacity.
Our religion, content with the two sacraments instituted by Christ, baptism and
the Supper, rejects all the others as inventions of human ingenuity. It
acknowledges Christ’s true, spiritual, and alone saving presence in the Supper,
but cannot admit a bodily and Capernaite presence, by which God is believed not
only to be made by man but also to be eaten, since it is opposed to sense,
reason, and faith and is full of a thousand contradictions. What either false
or ungodly can be found in all these things? On the contrary, what in them does
not breathe truth and holiness and agree with God’s word and the spirit of
Christianity? Can anyone be so shameless as to dare to say that those who
truly believe and sincerely observe such things are consigned to eternal
flames and must be condemned without hope of salvation?

VI. But if one wished to institute a comparison of the Roman religion with
ours, it could readily be gathered which is holier and worthier of human zeal
and veneration and which, on the other hand, is more impure and should make us
ashamed. Is it

[1847 vol. III printed p. 120 | IA leaf 154 | PDF p. 154]

the Roman religion, which transfers to creatures the worship owed to the
Creator alone, God blessed forever, and commands rational man to prostrate
himself before mute and lifeless statues and pictures? Or ours, which commands
that the immortal, true, and living God alone be adored and worshiped? Is it
the Roman religion, which divides the glory of our redemption, conversion, and
salvation between God and man? Or ours, which attributes it uniquely to God?
Is it the Roman religion, which acknowledges and venerates a frail, sinful,
and mortal man as the head of the church and as the supreme judge of
controversies and consciences? Or ours, which subjects neither Christ’s
mystical body nor its individual members to anyone other than Christ alone,
the Son of the blessed God and *of one substance* with him? Is it the Roman
religion, which still seeks Christ on earth under the appearances of a
perishable thing, contrary to the testimony of the senses, reason, and
Scripture? Or ours, which seeks him only in heaven, seated on the Father’s
throne, and uses the sacrament not to create Christ but to honor him, not to
bring his body down into ours but to lift our hearts up to him? Is it the
Roman religion, which, in order to rule more easily in the darkness, puts the
candle under a bushel by forbidding the reading of Scripture and the testament
of our Father and by prescribing the use of a foreign language in sacred rites,
and which contends that faith is defined by ignorance and blind obedience
rather than by knowledge and understanding? Or ours, which exhorts everyone to
the reading and study of Scripture, commends itself through the manifestation
of truth, and locates faith in the knowledge of the sacred mysteries? Is it the
Roman religion, which equates human traditions with God’s word, corrupts the
precepts of the law, and mutilates the sacrament in one of its parts? Or ours,
which, content with the inspired word of God alone, permits nothing to be added
to or taken from it? From these points, without pursuing others, everyone sees
what judgment must be made concerning the truth and falsehood of each religion
and church.

VII. But to press the adversaries more closely, we gather the truth of our
religion and church from their own confession. For with respect to merely
affirmative articles, which contain things to be believed, whether concerning
faith or concerning morals and worship, we believe nothing which they do not
profess with us to believe as most true. They must therefore either admit that
our faith is true or that their own faith, which they hold in common with us,
is false. This is confirmed by the fact that our entire dispute with the
papists does not concern those affirmative articles which the catholic church
of every age has constantly taught and which Rome itself even now receives and
professes. Rather, it concerns the negative and exclusive articles which it
alleges must be believed by us as necessary, but which we constantly reject as
false and erroneous. For example, the question between us is not whether
Scripture is the inspired word of God and the rule of faith and morals—which is
our belief and which they also admit—but whether besides Scripture there are
*unwritten* traditions to be received as the rule of faith with an equal
feeling of piety and reverence, which they would have but we deny. It is not
disputed whether Christ is our Mediator before God and his death a
*propitiatory* sacrifice for our sins, which both sides confess, but whether
besides Christ there are other mediators, whether of redemption or of
intercession, and whether besides the sacrifice of the cross another truly
propitiatory sacrifice must be admitted, which they contend but we reject. It
is not disputed whether God is to be worshiped and adored, about which both
sides agree, but whether besides God we can and ought lawfully to worship and
invoke creatures. The question is not whether Christ is the head of the
church, which both sides assert, but whether besides Christ the pope is also a
secondary head, which is their error and one we abhor. Finally, not to pursue
more heads, it is not disputed whether we are justified through faith
apprehending Christ’s merit, which we maintain with Scripture, but whether we
are also justified through works, which they urge but we reject. From these
things it is

[1847 vol. III printed p. 121 | IA leaf 155 | PDF p. 155]

plain that these heads of our faith do not come into controversy with the
adversaries. It cannot be denied that our religion, which is contained in
these heads, is true and consequently that the church which professes it is
true. Nor can its falsehood be gathered with respect to the negative articles
because we refuse to receive and believe everything they impose upon us as
things to be believed. For it is one thing not to believe everything one is
ordered to believe, another to believe what is false. The papists can reproach
us with the former but not the latter. Nor can the lack of that faith be
charged to us as a fault unless it is first shown that such articles as are
imposed for belief are necessary and handed down in Scripture, which they will
never accomplish.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions. 1. The charge of schism.]

VIII. Because they try to overwhelm the truth of our religion and church with
various prejudices, their vanity must be shown briefly. The first is the
*charge of schism*: that we separated from the Roman church and were thus torn
away from the true church. But various things can be answered. (1.) Not every
separation is evil and produces schism, but only one made rashly and unjustly.
Since this cannot be said of ours, which had the gravest and most necessary
causes, neither can schism be fastened upon us, as we have proved elsewhere in
Disputation I, *On Separation*. (2.) It is assumed without basis that to
separate from the Roman church and to separate from the true church are the
same thing. Yet there is a vast difference between them. We did indeed
separate from the Roman and papal church, but we did not therefore separate
from Christ’s true church. On the contrary, we left Roman communion precisely
so that we might retain communion with Christ, with which the former was
*incompatible*. (3.) Since the privilege of infallibility was granted neither
to Rome nor to any particular visible church, as we proved above, none can
claim that separation from its communion is unjust unless it has first shown
that the truth of doctrine resides with it. For if it is true that a church is
heretical or idolatrous, who can doubt that a believer must necessarily
separate from it if he wishes to provide for his salvation, as he is altogether
indispensably bound to do? I do not think anyone of sound mind would wish to
adopt that crude and ungodly paradox found in the author’s book *On
Prejudices*, chapter 7: “One must never separate from the Roman church, even if
it were heretical and idolatrous,” as though heresy and idolatry were not
*incompatible* with salvation, and as though Paul had spoken falsely when he
said that Christ has no communion with Belial, light with darkness, or the
temple of God with idols, and that idolaters must be excluded from God’s
kingdom.

[Marginalia: 2. The novelty of the Reformation.]

IX. 2. The *novelty of the Reformation* is urged to show the novelty of the
religion. But here reformation is wrongly confused with religion. Religion is
the body of doctrine handed down by Christ which contains whatever must be
believed or done by us for salvation. Reformation is nothing other than the
cleansing away of errors and corruptions which had been introduced by the
papacy into the doctrine of faith and morals handed down by Christ. The
Reformation is indeed new, that is, recently performed, since it presupposes a
preceding corrupt state of the church. But through this Reformation a new
religion or church which did not exist before was not therefore instituted;
rather, the one which already existed was restored to a better condition by
the ancient rule, namely, God’s word. Besides, it is no new thing for truth to
be accused of novelty and for falsehood to cloak itself in sacred and venerable
antiquity. The Jews objected to Christ that he was novel, while boasting that
they themselves were lovers and followers of antiquity (Mark 7:5; Matthew
15:2). The Gentiles used the appearance of novelty to bring the first
Christians into hatred and displayed the monuments of their own antiquity as
falsely as proudly. But just as Christ refuted the falsity of this accusation
by appealing to the Scriptures which testified about him

[1847 vol. III printed p. 122 | IA leaf 156 | PDF p. 156]

(John 5:39) and confirmed the doctrine handed down by him, and just as the
Christians defended themselves against the Gentiles with the same weapons, so
the same rationale for clearing ourselves and answering the charge should
prevail. We ask that the antiquity not of persons but of doctrine be
considered, which we rightly contend stands on our side.

[Marginalia: 3. Lack of calling and mission.]

X. 3. The prejudice is a *lack of calling and mission*: that we are
*self-called* men who had no authority or right to reform the church and thus
must be condemned even unheard on this very ground. But (1.) this is assumed
without basis, not proved. For we assert neither that our Reformers lacked a
lawful calling nor that we ourselves lack one, as will be demonstrated in the
following discussion when we dispute about the calling of pastors. (2.) It is
again wrongly assumed that an ordinary calling is always necessary for the
exercise of ministry and the reformation of the church. The rationale of a
church to be instituted and preserved is one thing; that of one to be restored
and reformed another. In a well-ordered commonwealth, no one is permitted to
rise up against the king’s ordinary prefects, and if anyone attempts it, he is
prosecuted as guilty of sedition and injured majesty. But if in a disturbed
commonwealth it occurs in order to avert some treachery stirred up against the
king and kingdom, it is then judged not a crime deserving punishment but a
heroic and praiseworthy action. In the same way, if anyone, out of contempt for
the order of a well-constituted church, wishes to invade the sacred office
without a calling, as is done by the Anabaptists and similar disturbers, he is
rightly to be condemned. But where the ministry itself is corrupt, where
everything in the church is disturbed, and where the most immediate danger of
destruction threatens, if someone wishes to provide for his own salvation and
his neighbors’, he does not need a special calling or any other authority
beyond zeal for God’s glory and desire for his salvation, which everyone is
bound by a general calling to secure. We contend that this was the state of
the Roman church and are prepared to prove it from the supreme corruption of
religion which prevailed there. If we assert this falsely, we do not plead
against being prosecuted as guilty of rashness and insolence. But if the matter
is proved to stand this way, who can hold it against us if we wished to provide
for our salvation in good time by rejecting falsehood and error and publicly
professing the truth? (3.) They most unjustly contend that we can be condemned
even unheard for lack of mission, although the laws themselves constantly
forbid this. Thus Nicodemus says, “Does our law condemn anyone unless it first
hears him and knows what he has done?” (John 7:51). For how can what is not
known be condemned without the greatest rashness? And how can it be known
unless it is examined? Add that because truth secures a hearing for itself
from whomever it is brought to us, if our doctrine is true we must be heard
even if we had no external mission; but if it is false, however much mission we
had, we must be detested, not heard. This is all the more so because mission
must be proved from doctrine, not doctrine from mission.

[Marginalia: 4. Dissensions.]

XI. 4. The prejudice is the *dissensions* from which the Evangelical churches
suffer. But these cannot prevent them from retaining the name of true
churches, because they agree in the foundation. If any dissensions remain,
which God has willed to permit for the testing of our faith, they concern less
necessary articles on which disagreement can exist without loss of the essence
of religion. Thus the apostolic churches once suffered from their own
dissensions and blemishes, as is evident from Acts and the Pauline Epistles;
nor were the eastern and western, Latin and Greek, African and Italian churches
free from them, although they did not therefore cease to be true churches.
Moreover, the Evangelicals’ contentions and dissensions are far smaller than
those agitated among the papists, who, as seen above, not rarely accuse one
another of heresy.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 123 | IA leaf 157 | PDF p. 157]

Nor are we detained by the harsher judgments which some of those who take
their name from the great Luther, carried away by hostile prejudices, are
accustomed to render against us. However harsh we find them, we do not cease
to embrace them with brotherly affection. And if, setting aside prejudices and
private affections, they were seriously to weigh the matter itself according
to the law of Christian love, truth, and prudence, they would not so greatly
shrink from a godly union and reconciliation with us, or at least from mutual
toleration, to which quite a few of their more moderate men are not averse.

[Marginalia: 5. The lives of the Reformers.]

XII. 5. The prejudice is the *lives of the Reformers*, which are reported to
have been defiled by so many vices that no one could easily believe God wished
to use such instruments to perform so great a work. But many considerations
demonstrate the vanity of this prejudice. (1.) It assumes that the truth of
religion depends upon the lives and morals of its pastors. Yet religion is not
to be measured from the lives of sinful men, who are nothing other than the
instruments God uses, but from the revelation and truth of God alone, its
author. (2.) The Pharisees and Sadducees once tried to overwhelm Christ and his
doctrine by the same prejudice, accusing him of blasphemy, sedition, imposture,
gluttony, and diabolical arts. Nor was their manner of acting different with
the apostles and God’s most godly servants. To be made like them in this
respect should be not only no reproach to us but glorious. (3.) Although we
believe that our Reformers were neither infallible nor impeccable, but were men
*subject to the same passions* as others, to whom nothing human was foreign and
who did not lack their own blemishes and faults, nevertheless their signal
godliness, singular integrity of life and morals, and rare virtues by which
they commended themselves and obtained testimony from the adversaries
themselves speak sufficiently to show that they were entirely foreign to the
very charges fastened upon them. And those most foul slanders have been
refuted and convicted of falsehood so many times that those who are still not
ashamed to repeat them must have lost all shame. See the APOLOGIES made by
RIVET, DU MOULIN, DRELINCOURT, and others for Zwingli, Luther, Martyr, Calvin,
and Beza, and, above all, the recently published RESPONSE TO THE HISTORY OF
CALVINISM, in which the author carefully pursues this argument in Part One,
chapters 1, 3, 4, 8, 15, and following. (4.) If one wished to return the
accusation, how much more rightly and truly could they be answered with the
vices and crimes with which the Roman curia abounded at the time of the
Reformation? Not a few of the papists graphically describe its most corrupt
state, as seen above, on account of which Reformation in both head and members
was so often sought with such zeal by emperors and kings.

[Marginalia: 6. Violence.]

XIII. 6. There is the slander concerning *violence and cruelty* fastened upon
our religion, as though it had been established by sword, blood, and force of
arms, with religious wars therefore stirred up. Thus the most abusive
Maimbourg, driven by insane fury, in the very opening of his *History of
Calvinism*, written not so much with ink as with blood, rages against it most
intemperately. He pursues this one purpose insatiably: by monstrous falsehoods
and hideous slanders not only to expose to the mockery of the world the pious
shades of holy men who deserved well of God’s church, with their reputation
wickedly torn to pieces, but especially to expose the doctrine itself, built
solely upon the foundation of the prophets and apostles, to everyone’s hatred
and execration, assailing it as the fertile mother of ungodliness, disturbances,
and every kind of rebellion. Thus he says that Calvinism renewed “whatever
rebellion, treachery, avarice, ambition, cruelty, and the most savage passions
of fury and rage once inspired in the most criminal men, so that it might
establish itself by sword and flames.” But the matter itself cries out that
nothing is falser than this most atrocious lie, nor can those who know the
history of the preceding century, in which the Reformation occurred, be
unaware of it. Christ’s religion is always like itself. Its weapons are not
carnal but mighty through God for the destruction of strongholds and
reasonings which rise up against

[1847 vol. III printed p. 124 | IA leaf 158 | PDF p. 158]

the knowledge of God, and for taking every thought captive to the obedience of
Christ (2 Corinthians 10:4). Just as at the beginning the church was founded
not by arms but by the preaching of the word alone, the blood of the apostles,
and the sufferings of the martyrs, so it was restored to its former splendor in
no other way: not by the arm of flesh, but by the arm of the Lord and the
invincible scepter of the gospel, which is the power of God for salvation to
everyone who believes. As the church was founded in blood, so it grew by blood,
bearing injuries, not inflicting them. “The church’s weapon is faith; the
church’s weapon is prayer, which conquers the adversary,” says Ambrose. If any
disturbances occurred or wars were stirred up on this occasion, they must not
be ascribed to religion, which advises altogether different things, but to the
fury and cruelty of the adversaries, who tried to destroy by sword and flames
what they could not destroy by reasons. Nor must civil wars, often initiated
by princes for reasons of their own, even if under a pretext of religion, be
immediately imputed to religion. See the RESPONSE TO THE HISTORY OF CALVINISM,
Part II, where the author, tracing the history of the Reformation in
Switzerland, England, Scotland, France, Belgium, and other places, demonstrates
most clearly that it was entirely foreign to the violence and cruelty ascribed
to it. But he proves with the surest arguments in Part III, in the *History of
the Papacy*, that this charge can most justly be turned back upon papism, whose
savagery and cruelty are monstrous.

[Marginalia: 7. Confusion.]

XIV. 7. There is the slander concerning the *confusion* and manifold
*disorder* said to have arisen in the world from the Reformation. But neither
should this most false accusation move us. For Elijah was once accused as the
troubler of Israel (1 Kings 18), and all the evils and calamities which befell
the Roman Empire were imputed to the first Christians. But just as Elijah did
not trouble Israel and Christianity was not the cause of the empire’s
miseries, so our religion, which agrees with that purer Christianity, cannot be
called the cause of the confusions which reign in the world. It breathes
nothing but peace and concord, believes nothing is more dangerous and more to
be avoided than confusion and anarchy, and commends nothing more efficaciously
than good order and good laws. And if any confusion has arisen because of it,
this does not follow in itself from its doctrine, but only accidentally on
account of the stubbornness and corruption of people who, unable to bear that
light, tried in every way to extinguish it. It is in this way that Christ
professes that he “came not to send peace upon the earth, but rather fire and a
sword” (Matthew 10:34).

[Marginalia: 8. Independency.]

XV. 8. There is the slander concerning *independency*, both in the church and
in the commonwealth, by which it comes about that we acknowledge no authority
in the church and are enemies of higher powers. But nothing more unjust or
more foreign to our religion could have been said. Who that has even greeted
it from the threshold can fail to know how little it favors independency, and
with how much zeal it labors to the contrary so that everything may be done
decently and according to order in the church through the institution of
lawful authority and government? Nor is it unknown that the obedience owed to
higher powers is numbered among the chief heads of our faith. We are bound to
obey them not only because of wrath but also because of conscience, so that it
always wills these two things to be joined by an indissoluble bond: “Fear God
and honor the king; render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God
the things that are God’s.” We leave independency to those who withdraw
themselves from the magistrate’s jurisdiction and think themselves so exalted
above kings that their crowns depend upon them, crowns which it rests with them
to place or remove at pleasure.

[Marginalia: 9. Fanaticism and libertinism.]

XVI. 9. There is the slander concerning *fanaticism and libertinism*, which
the adversaries would have to be an offshoot of our Reformation because we
shook off the pontiff’s yoke and introduce a private spirit. But the matter
itself, even if we were silent, plainly proves that our Reformation has nothing
in common with sectarians of that kind.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 125 | IA leaf 159 | PDF p. 159]

Indeed, nothing is more opposed to fanaticism and libertinism than the spirit
of the Reformation, which urges nothing more strongly than zeal for holiness
and a well-ordered manner of life drawn from God’s word. And it pursues nothing
more than that furious and fanatical sect of impure people abandoned to every
lust. Who opposed them more powerfully than our Calvin? Who exposed more
clearly the ungodliness and impurity of its doctrines? And since the primary
foundation of our faith is to adhere to Scripture alone, setting aside every
secret and immediate inspiration and revelation, who does not see how foreign
it is to fanaticism, which repeatedly boasts of new revelations and
inspirations? Nor do we introduce a private and fanatical spirit if we hold
that every believer can judge the doctrine proposed according to the rule of
the word by a spirit of discernment. Nor, if we shook off the tyrannical yoke
of the papacy, did we wish to free the believer from every yoke so that he
should plunge headlong into every crime; rather, we intended to subject him
more rightly to Christ’s law and his easy yoke. But what they rashly and
falsely fasten upon our Reformation we most truly and justly turn back upon the
Roman church, which is like the throne and empire of fanaticism, from whose
bosom all fanatics have gone out. There men resort daily to new inspirations
and visions if some doctrine or worship is to be newly instituted, some
miracle confirmed, or some religious order established. This is evident even
from the founders of the orders, Dominic, Francis, Ignatius, and others, who
repeatedly boasted of revelations and visions to secure belief in themselves,
as has been demonstrated at length by Dr. STILLINGFLEET in *On the Fanaticism
of the Roman Church*, in the question, *Can a Protestant Who Joins the Roman
Church Be Saved in It?*, and by the author of the *Response to the History of
Calvinism*, Part 1, chapter 6.
