# CHAPTER XVIII

## Whether, because God created the impious for the day of evil and permissively
preordained them to sin, it follows that God is the author of sin. Proverbs
16:4, “God made all things for himself,” and Romans 9, “For this very purpose I
raised you up,” are examined and vindicated. Bellarmine, Ruiz, Leonardo
Maeratius, Arubal, Fasolus, and the Arminians are also summoned concerning
1 Peter 2:8.

This question might appear more appropriate to a disputation about eternal
reprobation. But because Bellarmine and the Remonstrants raise this controversy
in their questions about providence, their method must be followed.

Before I address the question, however, I propose our second argument for
God's active providence concerning sin.

[Marginal summary: Second argument.]

Whoever by his wise and just ordinance established that all Adam's descendants
—neither knowing nor willing it and having no personal act capable of avoiding
it—should be born stained by original sin, willed that sin to occur by his
permission.

But God by his just ordinance established that all Adam's descendants should
be born in this way.

Therefore God willed that sin occur by his permission.

[Marginal summary: Sin's entrance into all Adam's descendants proves that God
wills sin to occur, willing and ordaining it permissively.]

The major premise is certain, because whoever wills the penal flow of sin to
pass from Adam into all Adam's descendants who were not yet born wills that
sin exist in all of them, by a permissive ordinance, as the punishment of sin.
But God does this. Therefore, and so forth. The major is proved again, and the
minor premise of the first syllogism is also confirmed. For in the first
covenant, which God made with Adam by his entirely free and wise choice alone,
God enacted this law: if Adam sinned, all his descendants would sin in his sin.
But God freely enacted this law. (1) God did not make this law by any necessity
of nature. (2) God could, if it had so seemed good to him, have enacted a law
by which, if Ad-*

[Chapter XVIII; printed page 212; IA PDF page 263; BSB canvas 264]

*am sinned, he alone would be guilty and all his descendants would be born
innocent. And since we were not yet born, it is evident that we could not
prevent that penal flow by any personal act; for it is impossible for a
creature to escape a punishment inflicted by the Creator. If someone says that
this flow of sin into all Adam's descendants arose from the communion of human
nature which existed between Adam and his descendants, this is irrelevant.
For a communion of natures does not necessarily create a communion of law and
sin between father and offspring. It is not by necessity but by God's free
institution that the son bears the father's iniquity with respect to either
the guilt of fault or punishment, as one can see in Ezekiel 18 and Exodus 20.

Hence the third argument. Whoever permits sin not toward an uncertain but
toward a fixed and determinate end which he does not attain without what he
permits does not stand negatively toward what he permits; rather, by a
permissive will he wills what he permits to occur. But God permits sin not
toward an uncertain but toward a fixed and determinate end—namely, the glory
of vindicatory justice and pardoning mercy.

Therefore God wills sin to occur by his permission.

[Marginal summary: Third argument. Permission of sin toward a determinate end
proves that sin occurs by God's will.]

The major premise is evident. Since sin is the most certain road to a
creature's destruction, God could not have permitted so noble a creature to
rush to destruction except toward a fixed and determinate, and indeed by far
the best, end: the manifestation of God's glory. The reason is that God himself
and God's glory are the end of all God's actions, both immanent and internal,
and transient and external. Hence Solomon says: “God made all things for
himself, even the impious for the day of evil” (Proverbs 16:4). Romans 9:17:
ὅτι εἰς αὐτὸ τοῦτο ἐξήγειρά σε, ὅπως ἐνδείξωμαι ἐν σοὶ τὴν δύναμίν μου, καὶ ὅπως
διαγγελῇ τὸ ὄνομά μου ἐν πάσῃ τῇ γῇ, “For this very purpose I raised you up, so
that I might display my power in you and that my name might be proclaimed in
all the earth.” Moses, Exodus 9:16: “For this reason I caused you to remain.”
Arias Montanus: וְאוּלָם בַּעֲבוּר זֹאת הֶעֱמַדְתִּיךָ. Junius: “For this reason
I caused you to remain, that I might display my power in you and that my name
might be proclaimed throughout the whole earth.” Two questions emerge from
this. The first is whether God creates people for destruction. Others carry
the question higher, to the decree: whether people are predestined by God for
destruction. The second is whether people are preordained to sin. The former
must be treated here. Hence the following distinctions must be observed.

[Marginal summary: Distinctions concerning God's actions in destining a person
for destruction.]

1. Distinction.-*

[Chapter XVIII; printed page 213; IA PDF page 264; BSB canvas 265]

1. It is one thing to predestine a person to destruction and another to
predestine an obedient and upright person.

2. Distinction. Predestination according to an act of absolute good pleasure
is one thing; predestination according to an act of justice is another.

3. It is one thing for God to predestine a person simply to destruction and
another to predestine him to destruction for God's glory.

4. The end of God as creator and craftsman is one thing, while the end of the
creature or of God's workmanship is another, just as the end of the agent is
one thing and the end of the work another.

5. Natural providence is one thing and supernatural providence another.

6. It is one thing to predestine a person to destruction and another to
predestine to destruction someone who conducts himself passively like a stake
or log while incurring condemnation by a demeritorious act.

7. A conditional end, if Adam had never fallen, is one thing, and the absolute
end in God's eternal election and reprobation another.

With these premises I will do three things. (1) I will set forth our doctrine
and confirm it by conclusions. (2) I will discuss the second question with the
modesty that befits it. (3) I will answer the opponents' cavils. Let the first
assertion therefore be this.

Assertion 1. God's primary and ultimate intention is not a person's
destruction, as though God created for this reason and purpose: to have some
whom he could destroy and torment forever. (1) In so great and inexplicable a
mystery, we must speak with Scripture. But Scripture does not say without
qualification that God created people in order to have some whom he could
torment, as though God's pious will were ultimately terminated upon the
creature's torments. Rather, it adds that God created them “for himself”
(Proverbs 16:4), and preserved Pharaoh not simply in order to destroy him but,
as Romans 9 says, “that God's name and power might be proclaimed throughout
the whole earth.” Vessels are fitted for destruction by God not simply, but by
God “willing to display his wrath and make his power known” (verse 22). See
Beza. (2) God does all things “for himself,” not simply and ultimately for the
creature's good or evil. No creature can exercise an act of imposing a final
end upon God's will. (3) Our theologians Beza and Calvin speak this way. I have
never encountered among them the formula, “The end of creation is a person's
eternal destruction.”

Assertion 2. These propositions differ greatly: “God destines and creates his
creatures for eternal destruction,” and “God creates and destines for eternal
destruction free creatures who incur the punishment of sin by a demeritorious
act.”-*

*The former proposition contains nothing in itself contrary to divine justice,
but we do not in fact assert it. The latter, however, is ours. (1) For God to
destine creatures simply—or creatures devoid of freedom and conducting
themselves passively on the way to destruction—to eternal ruin would make God
the primary author of his creatures' ruin. But our destruction is from
ourselves, and salvation is from God. Yet for God to intend to deny a creature
grace which he in no way owes it, so that the creature thus abandoned most
justly merits eternal ruin, and thus to intend that the glory of justice be
manifested in a creature freely sinning, contains nothing contrary to God's
goodness. (1) Pharaoh is raised up by God so that the glory of his power may be
manifested in him (Romans 9:17). (2) Such a display of power would be lawful
for an earthly potter. Therefore, much more is it not unworthy of God. (3) God
has mercy on whom he wills, and hardens whom he wills.

Assertion 3. God destines Pharaoh, and creates the wicked for the day of evil,
so that the glory of his power, justice, and mercy may be displayed, by an
absolute will—that is, one free from every condition that would condition,
restrict, and determine such a will of God. (1) God's will no more has a cause,
or a condition that conditions it, than God himself does. (2) There is no
condition or rationale in the clay that determines the potter's will to fashion
this vessel for honor and that one for dishonor. Yet God does not destine
people to destruction by a will so free from every condition that no condition
must necessarily—though also freely—be present in the person who actually
reaches the goal, terminus, or object of that destination. For God does not
destine people to destruction whether or not they are going to be sinners.
Indeed, these two acts are included in the same decree: the destination of a
person to eternal destruction, and the willing to permit that person finally
to sin. God's destination is therefore not absolute on the side either of its
matter or of its object or terminus toward which it tends. For God destines no
one to destruction except a person who will be finally impenitent, nor does he
destine anyone except to a conditioned destruction—that is, the sort of
destruction which the person so destined most justly procures for himself by
an evil, demeritorious act and by his wicked life.

all were created by God for eternal salvation, but some for destruction and
others for eternal salvation.

Assertion 5. The act of GOD destining some to destruction is not a juridical
act of GOD sitting upon the bench of justice and pronouncing sentence after a
case has been judicially heard. That act presupposes the demerit of a sinful
creature. Rather, it is an act of absolute good pleasure by which God, in the
manner of the supreme potter, determined within himself by a perfectly free
and wise decree what should be done concerning the final state of the rational
creature, and what evil of fault he would or would not permit to be committed.
For before the supreme potter created this earthen and fragile vessel, reason
requires that God, since he is an intelligent and supremely wise agent, should
determine something concerning the end preappointed in the fashioning of such
a creature. (1) No wise person builds a house or constructs a work except for
a rational end. (2) Romans 9 expressly says that GOD makes some vessels for
honor and others for dishonor.

[Marginal summary: The act of destining creatures to destruction is not a
juridical and forensic act of God.]

Assertion 6. We do not teach that GOD destines an upright, just, and obedient
person to destruction—and that by natural providence, or the providence of the
natural order. Rather, we teach that, by the providence of the supernatural
order, God ordained the human being simply for the manifestation of the glory
of his vindicatory justice. For if we consider that most eminent providence of
GOD above the ordinary providence that God exercises over brute animals and
inanimate things, it is fitting that, concerning a person not yet created, at
a stage of reasoning prior to his creation, he should determine something:
namely, that he should neither create so noble a creature for no end nor create
it for an end that he foreknows he will never attain. Neither alternative can
befall a wise man, much less the supremely wise God. Hence our Calvin argues as
follows:

the human being for his own glory, for both sides grant that. The question is
whether God created the human being for his glory as a determinate end—namely,
the glory of punishing justice—if God destined no one to destruction, as
Bellarmine and his party say. For they teach that God destined no one for the
display of a determinate glory, namely that of vindicatory justice. Instead,
they say that in creating the human being God intended his own glory: either a
glory to be declared speculatively apart from permitted sin, or a glory to be
manifested in experiential practice, but under a confused disjunction—through
the declaration of the glory of mercy if people believed and repented, or
through the disclosure of the glory of divine justice if they neither believed
nor repented. According to them, however, believing or not believing rests in
the indifferent choice of human beings.

(2) Bellarmine grants that *it was in some manner uncertain whether a person
would experience the glory of justice* if he disobeyed, or mercy if he obeyed.
But Pareus rightly observes there: *This is contrary to the immeasurable and
immutable wisdom of GOD.* Psalm 147: “There is no number to his wisdom.” Romans
11: “O the depths of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of GOD!” Something
can be uncertain to a human being, but nothing is uncertain to the omniscient
God—unless something knowable escapes God's infinite knowledge.

[Marginal reference: Pareus, *Critique of Bellarmine*, book 2, on the loss of
grace, chapter 17, lecture 59.]

(3) It is absurd that God should have created the human being as though the
Almighty were like an archer aiming at two targets who could not direct his
arrow in an undeviating motion, but, if it missed this target, might happen by
chance to strike the other. Or it is as though he were much like an earthly
shipmaster who could by no means determine the movements and blasts of the
winds and therefore, if adverse and ill-fated winds prevented him from
reaching this harbor, would be driven to some other shore, not without the loss
of a shipwreck. This is utterly unworthy of God.

(4) Bellarmine wrongly denies that *it follows that GOD created the human being
for an uncertain end* if God did not foreknow in his permissive decree what the
person would do if created. For contingent and free acts, which are equally
able to be or not to be, are not knowable except in some determinate cause. But
that cause is nothing other than GOD's free decree.
