ON

GABRIEL PENOTTUS’S

*Bulwark of Human Liberty*

BOOK III, CHAPTER XVIII.

Whether the argument taken from the presentiality of things, explained in this way, is sufficient.

In the preceding chapter the Author himself has already discussed the argument in question, explained
according to Penottus’s contrived invention, as now sufficient. Yet in order to add weight and
importance to his explanation, he makes a show of working out the matter more laboriously. To establish
it, however, he contributes nothing more than what he has already dispatched in very few words. Be that
as it may, we shall weigh whatever pertains to the present matter. We omit the first three numbered
sections from examination, since they contain nothing that does not wander wholly outside the course;
I turn to the fourth.

PENOTTUS.

It must be noted that future contingents do not have determinate truth in themselves according to the
being which they have in their contingent causes not yet determined and ultimately completed. For from
the very fact that some cause is formally contingent, it implies by its intrinsic nature a manifest
contradiction that the effect, so long as it is in the cause itself, should have being so determined
that it is true to say, “This effect will determinately and infallibly occur.” For by that very fact the
contingency of the cause would be removed if we were to say that the effect, insofar as it is in that
cause, is certainly and infallibly future. This is what Thomas always taught in express words,
especially in the cited q. 14, art. 13, when he says: “In another way a contingent can be considered as
it is in its cause, and thus it is considered as future and contingent, not yet determined to one
outcome, because a contingent cause is open to opposites; and thus the contingent does not fall with
certainty under any cognition (therefore not under divine cognition either). Hence one who knows a
contingent effect only in its cause has no more than conjectural cognition of it.” This is what
Aristotle intended in saying that future contingents do not have determinate truth. Thomas repeats the
same in *On Truth*, q. 2, art. 12, reply 1. Future contingents therefore have determinate truth not
according to the being which they have in their contingent causes, but only according to the being
which they have when they have been produced in the nature of things outside their causes. For then
they have determinate and indeed necessary being, in the way in which we say, “While something is, it
necessarily is.” Yet that necessity does not alter the nature of the contingent thing but only alters
its being, as Cajetan rightly noted.

RESPONSE.

I readily acknowledge that future contingents do not have determinate truth in contingent causes not
yet determined. For contingent causes are called contingent not because they exist contingently, but
because they operate contingently—that is, they operate indifferently both as to exercise and as to the
specification of the act. From a cause standing indifferently toward acting or not acting, and toward
doing this or that, it no more follows that it will act than that it will not act, and no more follows
that it will do this than that it will do that. Nevertheless, it is also true that, given the
indetermination of causes acting contingently, a contingent thing has absolutely no determinate truth
in any manner whatever other than in its causes. It follows in turn that, given the indetermination of
the causes, the things Penottus calls future contingents should no more be called future contingents
than non-futures, although contingents of this kind are everywhere called “future” by Penottus, in a
phraseology prone to error. For if they are future, it must by no means be doubted that they have
determinate truth and can be known by the infinite intellect according to their determinate truth. I
therefore acknowledge that it cannot be affirmed without contradiction that a cause is undetermined
and that the effect of that cause is determined, since the effect has no determination except from the
determination of the cause. But I acknowledge no contradiction between the contingency of the cause
and the determination of the effect, because the contingency of the cause is no more opposed to the
determination of the effect than to the determination of the cause. Yet nothing prevents the
determination of the cause, for a contingent cause, such as a rational will, contingently determines
itself—that is, freely. Consequently, the act itself, or effect, is also a free act. It is therefore
manifest that this determination does not at all oppose contingency. And just as an act elicited by
the will is free, so too no one, so far as I know, has ever called into controversy whether a commanded
act is also free, although it certainly and infallibly follows from the elicited act that the commanded
act will occur—provided that the will which determines itself through the elicited act to an outward
act that it will subsequently command is both intrinsically immutable and extrinsically irresistible.
This alone is the point toward which Aquinas tends in part 1, q. 14, art. 13: to prove that the
determinate truth of a future contingent cannot stand with a secondary cause’s being undetermined as
to acting, not with its being contingent or operating contingently. Penottus’s subsequent distinction
is therefore worthless: future contingents have determinate truth not according to the being which
they have in their contingent causes, but only according to the being which they have when produced in
the nature of things outside their causes—as though it were possible for things to be produced in the
nature of things outside their causes without the contingent causes’ determination to one outcome.
On the contrary, it is rather most true that future contingents have determinate truth by nature first
in their contingent causes (not insofar as those causes are contingent, I grant, but insofar as they
are determined to one outcome) and only afterward insofar as they are produced in the nature of things
outside their causes. For by nature it is prior for a cause

[Book I, printed p. 105; BSB canvas 00131]

to cause than for an effect to be produced. Certainly a necessity consequent upon the existence of a
thing does no injury to the thing’s contingency. But Bradwardine too, a Scholastic of great name, has
already established by various arguments that not even every antecedent necessity does any injury to
the contingency of a thing. Therefore, when future contingents are said not to have determinate truth
according to the being which they have in contingent causes, this must be understood of contingent
causes not yet determined. For Penottus himself proposed it this way at the very threshold of the
present dissertation: “It must be noted that future contingents do not have determinate truth in
themselves according to the being which they have in their contingent causes not yet determined and
ultimately completed.” By this very statement he also taught how, in his judgment, Thomas is to be
understood in this passage. And because in the conclusion he draws from Thomas he also mentions
secondary causes, he thereby signifies that, in Penottus’s own judgment, Thomas’s statements concern
secondary causes alone—namely, when Aquinas says that “a future contingent can be considered as it is
in its cause and thus considered as future and contingent, not yet determined to one outcome.” I
therefore gladly grant that we owe Penottus thanks under two heads for illuminating Aquinas with these
commentaries. First, Penottus interprets the cause in which, according to Thomas, the effect is
considered as something future and contingent not yet determined to one outcome as a cause not yet
determined to one outcome. Second, whatever Aquinas discusses about a cause in this passage must be
understood of a secondary cause. Nor is that surprising, since it is well agreed between the parties
that the first cause was determined from eternity to one side of future contingents. But I seem to see
the design with which Penottus shared this interpretation of Aquinas with us: these words should
concern a secondary cause, evidently, unless I am mistaken, so as to clear every obstacle from the path
of the conclusion that follows. It runs thus: “Hence it comes about that the divine intellect does not
have certain knowledge of future contingents by attaining only the being which contingents have in
secondary causes” (here again he interprets Thomas as though he must be understood of secondary
causes), “unless it simultaneously attains the being of actual existence of those same contingents
outside their causes. And this,” he says, “is what Thomas means when he says that future contingents
are not known infallibly by God except insofar as they are subject to the divine gaze according to
their presence—that is, according to the present being of actual existence.” This, I say, must also be
understood by setting the cognition of a thing according to its being of actual existence outside its
causes against cognition of it according to the being which it has in causes, but in secondary causes,
so that the first cause of all things does not enter into consideration here at all. For since to see a
thing according to the being which it has when produced outside its causes is nothing other than to see
it according to the being which it will have, as Penottus very often explains his meaning in this
argument, and since to see a thing according to the being which it will have is nothing other than to
see it as future, who would be so foolish as to deny that God also knows a future thing infallibly as
it is in the first cause—that is, in God himself, namely in the determination of the divine will? For
what sober person would doubt that from eternity God knew that the world would be, and knew it
infallibly, for this very reason: God himself had decreed from eternity that the world would be made?

PENOTTUS.

And this doctrine is most true not only when speaking of absolute future contingents but also of
conditional ones. For from the fact that some contingent is future only upon a hypothesis, it does not
follow that it can be known certainly by seeing only the being it has in its causes, since there is no
difference between an absolute future and a future upon a hypothesis except that, in the case of an
absolute future, the being of actual existence which it will have absolutely is seen, while in the
case of a future upon a hypothesis the being it will have is seen not absolutely but upon the
supposition that God willed to constitute such causes in such an order.

Hence Molina does not seem consistent with himself in the cited disputation 14, in the second argument
for the first conclusion, when he teaches that from the comprehension of created free choice alone the
divine intellect can know whatever it would do upon the hypothesis that it were constituted in this or
that order. For either he speaks of the comprehension of free choice as determined to the production
of this or that effect, and then one who knows free choice as determined to this or that effect also
sees the effects themselves as actually emanating from that free choice—and thus he returns to the
presentiality which he there attempts to demonstrate useless. Or he speaks of the comprehension of the
same free choice as not yet determined to this or that effect, and thus it is false that from its
comprehension one can understand what it would do upon the hypothesis that it were constituted in this
or that order. Indeed, it seems to involve a manifest contradiction that one who knows a cause as
undetermined to produce an effect should certainly know the effect which will proceed from it. For no
matter how many circumstances are applied, so long as it remains undetermined as such it cannot be
known that it will produce a determinate effect. Add that, since on his opinion not only infinity of
light and intellect but also determinate truth in the object is required for perfect knowledge, future
contingents do not have determinate truth by reason of the being they have in secondary causes, but
only by reason of the being which they would have in themselves upon the hypothesis that their causes
were constituted in such an order, and so forth.

RESPONSE.

First, I answer that Penottus’s distinction is very foreign to the present undertaking. For he
discusses the cognition of future contingents according to the being they have in secondary causes,
which he denies to be certain whether the question concerns absolute future contingents or conditional
ones. But, I say, all future contingents are conditional with respect to secondary causes, and none are
absolute. For example, the human will is a cause of future contingents, but with respect to the human
will no contingents are future absolutely; they are future conditionally, namely upon the hypothesis
that it is constituted in this or that order in which it is suitably moved to do what it will do.
Second, Penottus proceeds to indulge himself in his dreams when he distinguishes cognition of future
contingents according to the being they have in their causes from cognition of the being of actual

[Book I, printed p. 106; BSB canvas 00132]

existence which they will have—as though the being they will have could be anything other than future
being, or future things as future could be seen otherwise than in their causes. Again, to see a future
thing in its causes is to see the being of actual existence which it will have; this is nothing other
than to see the being of some thing’s existence as future. Thus in every respect it is altogether the
same as seeing a future thing in its causes. Penottus everywhere relies upon this thoroughly watery and
senseless distinction. Yet equipped with this alone, when he approaches this controversy debated among
the greatest intellects and is going to decide it with some authority and command unknown to me, he
boasts wonderfully. One may deservedly marvel that a man could rave to such a degree and indulge his
dreams so far as to wish his futile and empty speculations to be put up for sale in the schools of the
theologians—speculations more insane than any I believe the Christian world has ever seen. I grant that
he offers some interpretation of Aquinas in this sense, devoid of all sound and sober sense. Yet I do
not readily believe that anyone among the learned Roman Catholics, whether Dominican, Jesuit, or of
whatever order, has either agreed with this opinion of Penottus thus far or will ever agree with it. I
say nothing of those among our English theologians who plunge headlong into the Arminian party and
eagerly seize for themselves any refuse whatever, provided it somehow suits their corrupted palate.

Molina undoubtedly speaks of the comprehension of free choice as not yet determined to this or that
effect, and I do not at all doubt that Penottus knew this perfectly well. I too reject Molina’s and the
Jesuits’ opinion in this respect as foreign to sober reason. Nevertheless, I judge that opinion far
more probable and more worthy of a theologian than Penottus’s opinion. For despite freedom and
indetermination of choice, prudent people not only have good hope from their counsels but also place
confidence in them, and very often prove not to have been false prophets. How much more, and infinitely
more certainly, must this be judged true of God’s judgment, since the inmost inclinations of human
beings are most thoroughly perceived by him, as are the weights of every reason that can be proposed
and that is supremely effective for persuasion. For it is far easier to discern what a person will do
from the congruity of the counsels proposed with the inclination of the people to whom they are
proposed than from no foundation at all. Yet the latter is the manner in which Penottus has invented
for us God’s intuitive knowledge of future contingents, devised without any sense or brain. For when
he maintains that God sees the being of actual existence which things have or will have, he varies his
phraseology in this way as occasion offers, as though he had until now been uncertain where he might
at last come to rest more conveniently. If, according to Penottus, future contingents have had the
being of actual existence from eternity, he falls into the Dominican opinion which he attacks. But if
God sees no being of things’ existence other than what they will have, then he does not see their
present being but only their future being. Yet one who sees things in their causes sees the being which
they will have, and sees it determinately and infallibly provided the causes have been determined, and
determined immutably and irresistibly—as is most true of the determination of the divine will. If the
causes are undetermined, then by reason of those causes the future things themselves will also be
undetermined and will occur only contingently, though they will occur if they are determined through
another cause, namely the divine will. Hence it will come about that they will occur infallibly by
reason of the first cause, yet contingently by reason of secondary causes, since with respect to the
latter they will always occur with indifference toward the opposite. It is certainly the disgrace of
the Jesuits’ opinion that they place the futurition of things before divine cognition without
indicating any sufficient cause by which those things have passed from the condition of merely
possible things into the condition of future things. And how, I ask, does Penottus remedy this
difficulty? With no remedy at all. He holds that from eternity God sees the being of actual existence
which future contingents will have, that is, a future being of actual existence. But he nowhere
explains—and cannot explain—why that being will be rather than will not be, or why future contingents
will have being of actual existence rather than not have it. Indeed, immediately afterward in numbered
section 7 he professes that the difficulty of this matter is supreme and is an immense sea of divine
wisdom, which, he says, cannot be satisfied by what Molina has near the end of disputation 14, when he
says that divine knowledge of those things which are still contingently future is not properly the
knowledge of vision so long as they are still future, but properly has the character of the knowledge
of simple intelligence. He also says that Thomas’s argument by no means appears to demonstrate
sufficiently how this occurs—so much so that he wishes it to appear sincerely said that perhaps this
knot cannot be solved evidently in the present life, and that perhaps in this sense the opinion
reported in Chapter 11 from Gabriel, Ockham, and Marsilius is true. Or, he thinks, it must be said that
the divine essence, besides the modes of representing creatures explained above, also has another
mode: namely, that of representing creatures in the manner of a measure representing the measured
thing. Thus the divine essence simultaneously represents all time and the things which occur
successively in every difference of time, because it is the measure containing time itself and all
things that occur in every difference of time; and perhaps Boethius and Thomas intended to intimate
this. He behaves most ridiculously when, with a great effort and exertion of intellect, he wishes to
seem to bring forth some new and excellent argument by which the commotions of minds in so obscure a
controversy may be settled, although he gives birth to nothing new but only to what has already been
received by everyone: namely, that God’s essence represents to God whatever God knows, and does so as
a measure represents the things measured, or rather, far better. Nevertheless, this makes no progress
toward attributing to God cognition of things future only contingently. For things which are only
contingently future are, as such, no more future than non-future. Consequently, there is no greater
reason why their being should be measured by the essence of God than why their nonbeing should be. And
this is precisely the difficulty which Penottus had objected in numbered section 7 in order to
overturn and devalue Molina’s opinion: “This opinion,” he says, “does not assign a reason how God
understands from eternity the future being of a contingent thing which that thing does not yet have.”
Thus, I say, the divine essence will not be able to represent the future thing’s being as a measured
thing, since it no more measures that being as yet than it measures the same thing’s nonbeing. It is
most certain that the essence of God considered simply

[Book I, printed p. 107; BSB canvas 00133]

represents only possible things. But if the essence of God is considered as willing, as Durandus
speaks, or as joined with his will and its determination, in the way Aquinas acknowledges that God’s
knowledge is the cause of things, it will immediately be clear how his essence represents to God not
only all possible things but also all future things. Yet whatever there is of mystery here, Penottus
can digest it no more than the Jesuits themselves. This is the truth which both parties especially
hate, and in hating it they cultivate the fullest communion, although when they devote their utmost
effort to obscuring it they are struck with dizziness and, like the earthborn Cadmean brothers, attack
one another.

PENOTTUS.

From these observations it can easily be gathered how fitting, proper, and solid is the argument
assigned by Thomas both for demonstrating the certainty of divine knowledge concerning future
contingents and for reconciling the certainty of that same divine knowledge with the freedom of human
choice. For of all the arguments assigned thus far by the Fathers or Scholastics it is both the
clearest and the most solid, and, once its terms are understood, it can scarcely—indeed, scarcely at
all—be denied except by an obstinate person. It is as follows: Although one who sees only the being
which a future contingent has in its undetermined contingent causes cannot in this way have certain
knowledge of it, nevertheless one who also sees the present being of the actual existence of that same
contingent outside its causes in the nature of things can have certain and infallible knowledge of it.
From eternity, in the *now* of eternity, God sees not only the being which future contingents have in
their causes but also the actual being which each one has or will have in itself outside its causes.
Therefore he has certain and infallible knowledge of all future contingents. This argument, I say, when
held up to the light, is so clear and solid that, if compared with the others, it appears sufficient by
itself. It is an argument received not only from Thomas but from the holy Fathers and almost all the
Scholastics.

RESPONSE.

We shall receive nothing more from this argument which Penottus sells as proper and solid than we
already received in the preceding chapter. It is a merely tautological argument and altogether
unworthy to be attributed either to Aquinas or to any serious Scholastic as its author. I readily grant
that it cannot be denied, since the same thing is proved through itself; but I cannot sufficiently
marvel at the man’s folly when he displays an argument by the discovery of which in Aquinas he very
greatly wishes the fame of his name to be commended to the Reader. For the argument comes down to this:
“One who sees the present being of the actual existence of that same contingent outside its causes in
the nature of things can have certain and infallible knowledge of it.” But, I say, this proposition is
completely identical, as will become clear if only we do not allow an empty sound of words to make
sport of us. First, the question is not about the power to attain certain knowledge of the thing but
about the attainment itself. Again, the question is about knowledge of the thing not in the class of
the possible but in the class of the future. Yet for God, seeing a future thing and having certain
knowledge of it are altogether the same. Again, one who sees a thing’s present being does not have
certain knowledge of it unless he sees it certainly. But to infer certain knowledge of a thing from
the fact that it is seen certainly is to prove the same through itself. Indeed, let a still greater
incongruity be noticed. Penottus professes that the thing to be demonstrated by this argument is the
certainty of divine knowledge concerning future contingents. Why, then, does the syllogism not issue
in some such conclusion as: “Therefore God has certain knowledge of future contingents”? Plainly, if
he had honestly proposed such a conclusion, there would have been no coherence at all between the
premises and the conclusion. For it would have proceeded thus: “One who certainly sees the present
being of the actual existence of some thing certainly sees that thing to be future. But God certainly
sees the present being of actual existence,” and so forth—as though he had framed it thus: “Whoever
certainly sees a thing to be present certainly sees the same thing to be future. But God certainly
sees a thing to be present. Therefore he certainly sees the same thing to be future.” Let the Jesuits
hear this, let the Dominicans hear it, let every Roman Catholic without exception hear it—especially
the more learned and those practiced in Scholastic Theology—and at last, having employed this Novarian
doctor as their master, let them learn to think soberly. For he promises you an eminent demonstration,
the clearest and most solid of all those assigned thus far by Fathers or Scholastics, which, once its
terms are understood, can scarcely—indeed, scarcely at all—be denied except by an obstinate person. It
is therefore right that, setting aside the mutual contentions by which you destroy one another, you
should thank this Penottus for labor so happily expended, through which you may agree amicably,
provided you are not willing to be obstinate, and should gratefully embrace so splendid an
interpretation of Aquinas’s opinion—one which until now has lain hidden from the Christian world and
escaped even Cajetan, although he searched for it in fifteen years of study. Its genius is that, from
God’s knowing with complete certainty that a thing is present, it is inferred that God knows with
complete certainty that the same thing is future. And do not let envy so blind you that, scorning and
holding for nothing the fruitful night studies of so great a man, you exclaim, “What worthy of so great
a gape has this promiser produced?” For although it is not so easy for bleary eyes to behold its clear
constitution, if you are willing to hold this argument, explained in this way, up to the light (and not
to the gallows), it will be found so clear—look to Penottus—and so solid that, if compared with the
others, it appears sufficient by itself. Nevertheless, I grant that a certain irregularity occurs
here. In the major premise mention is made only of seeing the present being of the actual existence of
a contingent thing. But in the minor God is said to see the actual being which that same contingent
thing has or will have. Since according to Penottus a contingent thing has not had actual being from
eternity either in time or in eternity, this must be understood of the actual being which the thing
will have. Yet the actual being which a thing will have is not so much the thing’s present being as its
future being. Therefore, so that the argument might proceed more fittingly, it should have been framed
in this way: “One who certainly sees the actual being which a thing will have certainly sees the
actual being which the thing will have. But God certainly sees the actual being which the thing will
have. Therefore he certainly sees the actual being which the thing will have”—that is, he has certain
and infallible knowledge of the contingent thing. I decided to lend Penottus my services as midwife in
bringing this most

[Book I, printed p. 108; BSB canvas 00134]

exquisite argument of his into the open, so that the man’s acumen might be prostituted to each and
every person and those who wish might come to embrace and admire it. And I readily believe that a
reader, though not excessively sagacious, will easily sniff out how truly Penottus said that this
argument is of such a kind that, once the terms are understood, it can scarcely—indeed, scarcely at
all—be denied except by an obstinate person. To confirm its clarity and solidity he adds that this
argument was received not only from St. Thomas but also from the holy Fathers and almost all the
Scholastics. But, to state openly what I think, I judge that this argument of Penottus surpasses all
the others in clarity. Thomas, however, so far as I grasp him, gathers the certainty of God’s knowledge
of future contingents from the presence of things before God from eternity. In my judgment no such
presence before God is prior to divine cognition except in willed being. We have already examined in
the same passage the texts of Augustine brought forward in Chapters 5 and 16. As for Scotus, nothing
is clearer than that in the cited question Scotus proves that God knows future contingents from the
determination of his will—not indeed through discursive reasoning, but insofar as through the
determination of the divine will a possible thing becomes future and consequently can be known by the
divine intellect as future. In the passage designated by Penottus, however, he says only that “all the
authorities of the Saints which appear to sound as though all things are present to eternity must be
understood of presence under the aspect of being known, and not merely of being knowable, as though by
abstract cognition (just as a nonexistent rose is present to my intellect through a species), but of
true intellective intuitive cognition, because God knows things made no differently from things to be
made, and thus things to be made are known by the divine intellect just as perfectly and presently as
things made.” These statements agree excellently with the cognition of future contingents as they are
before God in willed being according to Scotus’s own opinion. But Scotus by no means signifies that
those ancient Fathers—who conducted themselves much more simply—attained this Novarian’s subtlety,
which he himself so loudly proclaims, and from which they were undoubtedly very far removed. Rather,
they left such minutiae of demonstrations entirely to this inquisitive age. As for Durandus, in book
1, distinction 38, q. 3, no. 11, he teaches there only the sense in which it is true that God knows
future contingents with respect to their actual existence. It is not in the sense that he knows them
to be actually existing, since an intellect which says that what does not exist exists is false, which
he says is impossible. Hence he concludes that God does not know future contingents with respect to
their actual existence in such a way that he knows them to be actually existing. Rather, he knows them
in this sense: from eternity God knew the proper, distinct, and actual existence which they will have
in themselves in the course of time. He then supplies a rationale very different from Penottus’s
argument in these words: “It is agreed that God knows a contingent when it is present—for example,
that Socrates is running. Otherwise I would know what God did not know. Therefore, if he did not know
this before, when it was future, it would follow that something had accrued to his knowledge, which is
impossible.” Thus Durandus’s argument is this: Now God knows that Socrates is running; therefore he
knew this from eternity, for otherwise something would have accrued to his knowledge. Durandus says
nothing about the certain and infallible condition of divine knowledge, which is the sole concern of
Penottus’s argument. But perhaps someone will reply: It will amount to the same thing, provided that
from eternity God knew Socrates to be running. I answer that Durandus has already professed that God by
no means knew future contingents from eternity with respect to their actual existence in such a way as
to know that they then existed. Therefore he did not know from eternity in this sense that Socrates
was running—that is, that Socrates’s running actually existed from eternity. But he knew both the
existence of Socrates and the running which Socrates would have in himself in the course of time.
According to Durandus, and also according to sound reason, before the time at which Socrates runs has
arrived, to know that Socrates is running at that time is to know that Socrates will run; and after
that time has passed, it is to know that Socrates has run. Hence in distinction 39, q. 1, when asking
whether God could fail to know what he knows and know what he does not know, Durandus proposes this
argument for the affirmative: “God does not know that Antichrist has been born, but at some point he
will know that he has been born; therefore at some point he will know what he now does not know.
Likewise, God knows that Antichrist will be born, but at some point he will not know that he will be
born, namely when he has been born; therefore at some point God will not know what he knows.” Observe
how he judges this argument must be met in no. 14: “It must be said that God knows from eternity that
Antichrist will be born for one time, is being born for another, and has been born for another. But the
fact that this proposition, ‘God knows that Antichrist has been born,’ is not now verified, but rather
its contradictory, namely, ‘God does not know that Antichrist has been born,’ is not because of the
principal composition of the verb with the subject but because of the time connoted in the matter of
the verb. For ‘has been born’ includes past time with respect to Antichrist’s birth, and this inclusion
is false for the present because the birth is not now past. Therefore, ‘God knows that Antichrist has
been born’ is said to be false not because God does not know Antichrist’s birth as past for the time
when it will be past, but because it is falsely implied that it is past now. Therefore God will
subsequently know nothing that he does not know now, nor conversely, although some propositions
concerning God’s knowledge are verified now and others are not verified afterward, not because of
variation in God’s knowledge but because of the varying implication of times in the matter of the
verb.” This matter seems easy for us to dispatch by saying that God beholds things in no other way than
in his essence, whether they are future, present, or past; and they are seen in God’s essence insofar
as it is willing. For a certain time, to be willed in this manner is both to be future until that time
has arrived, to be present as soon as it has arrived, and to be past as soon as that time has ceased to
be. Is this not Durandus’s very opinion, namely that God sees future things through his essence insofar
as it is willing? Indeed, the very question Penottus cites makes manifest that this is his opinion. For
he writes thus in no. 25: “God’s foreknowing that A will be co-requires God’s willing that A will be.”
But he is still much clearer in book 1, distinction 39, q. 1, no. 11: “That a thing will or will not be
is represented through the divine essence, not as it is merely an essence virtually containing every

Penottus adds that the difficulty is not whether one who sees the present being of the actual
existence of a

[Book I, printed p. 109; BSB canvas 00135; upper section]

contingent thing can have certain and infallible knowledge of it—that is, whether one who sees the
thing’s present being sees its future being certainly and infallibly. This is the sober acumen of this
Novarian in which he boasts so greatly. But since the present being of the actual existence of some
thing is the sort of present being which the thing will have or is going to have, his subtlety should
more fittingly be reduced to this: “One who certainly knows the present being of the actual existence
of some thing certainly knows that thing to be future.” And since for a thing to be future is nothing
other than for it to be going to have the being of actual existence at some point, the fine point
comes down to this: “One who certainly sees the being of actual existence which a thing will have
certainly sees the being of actual existence which the thing will have.” Or in fewer words: “One who
certainly knows that a thing will be certainly knows that the thing will be.” What else is denying
this than denying sense itself, as Penottus speaks in order to magnify the acumen with which he devised
this subtlety? “But,” he says, “the whole difficulty of this matter lies in assigning the reason why
God, from eternity preceding and anticipating the differences of time, sees things which are future
before they come to be, and how by his intuitive cognition he attains the very being of actual
existence which contingent things will have when they actually exist in the world, before they really
exist in the world.” But, I say, it is no more difficult to assign the reason why he sees things which
are past than why he sees things which are future, and how by his intuitive cognition he attains the
very being of actual existence which contingent things had when they actually existed in the world,
after they really exist in the world. I cannot sufficiently marvel that he has not magnified the
difficulty of the matter equally from this side too. Unless my mind deceives me, this delineates for
us clearly enough the superficial intellect of this great busybody. But we assign this reason: God
beholds all future contingents before himself in willed being, whether they are future, present, or
past. Yet Penottus, together with all the Jesuits, cannot digest this, lest their bulwarks of human
liberty thereby cover them with shame and leave them miserably confounded.
