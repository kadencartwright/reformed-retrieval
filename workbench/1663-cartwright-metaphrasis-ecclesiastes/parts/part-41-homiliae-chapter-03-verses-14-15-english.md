VERSE 14. I know that whatever God does endures forever; nothing can be added
to it and nothing can be taken from it, and God himself brings it about that
men fear before his face.

VERSE 15. That which has been now is, and that which will be has already been;
finally, God himself brings back the things which follow one another.

The third objection is based upon disparate things. In this exceedingly
mobile and changeable revolution of all affairs, some mark of imperfection is
cast upon God's administration. The answer is drawn from an attribute. On the
contrary, God's administration is most perfect, as is confirmed by an
enumeration of its parts.

First, whatever God does endures and will endure forever. Although some things
pass away in a moment and are involved in the greatest mobility, they are
nevertheless eternal: first in God's decree, which was established even for
the smallest things before the foundations of the world were laid; and then
in their use and end, since prosperous and adverse things are directed either
to the salvation of the godly or to the destruction of the ungodly. That
blasphemous saying belongs to the pagans: the gods toss men this way and that
like a ball and recklessly roll and roll them back. Consequently, nothing so
abject and worthless can happen in human life that it ought to be passed over
carelessly by us, since it bears upon either our happy or our unhappy
condition.

Second, whatever God does is complete in every respect. Solomon proves this
from the parts of completeness, because nothing can be added to it or taken
away from it. Just as God's Word, and therefore all God's sayings, are perfect
in all their parts, so that nothing can be added to or taken away from them,
so too are all his works. It must therefore be attributed to men's malice or
ignorance if they find something wanting in God's administration. Just as all
things are exceedingly perfect in creation, so too are they perfect in their
administration.

Therefore, when we judge God's works and government, let us set this
commendation of the Holy Spirit concerning them like a rampart against our
own rashness: nothing can be added to them and nothing taken from them. For
this reason we must labor to search out and investigate the perfection and
wisdom within them. If we are not able to attain it, let us nevertheless show
them the honor which we render to mortals endowed with wisdom: let us look up
with wonder at what we do not understand and, moved by reverence, judge it to
have been done wisely.

In the third place, he confirms God's perfection from the end of his works,
namely, that men should fear and revere God. Because of human perversity, men
cast aside all fear of God and loosen the reins upon their desires on account
of this teaching about the eternal decree of all things and the fact that
each thing is beautiful in its own time. They recklessly mix everything up,
as though they would waste labor and oil by walking carefully and
tremblingly before God, since things must necessarily happen and, when they
happen, happen in their own beautiful time.

The Holy Spirit, however, uses this argument for the contrary purpose: to
stir men to fear and reverence of God, to preserve them in it once stirred,
and to humble them beneath God's powerful hand—not so that they may rush
recklessly and without deliberation in the conduct of affairs or carelessly
pass over the means. For when they acknowledge that everything is in God's
hand and that willing and running do not belong to them, they return to God
in prayer to entreat his aid. If they enjoy good, they are grateful to God; if
they suffer evils, they take them in good part. For just as God determined to
do everything certainly and definitely, so he uses men's prudent and
provident diligence to bless them mercifully and affect them with benefits;
or he uses their rashness and wickedness to punish them by just judgment and
thrust them down to hell.

Fourth and finally, he confirms the perfection of divine administration from
the accompanying order and most beautiful sequence by which God does all
things: the individual things successively

follow one another, just as in the revolution and turning of heaven. There,
what is now seen beneath our hemisphere and the heavenly body passes to the
other hemisphere, returns again at its own time, and presents itself to be
seen by us. Consequently, just as the spheres of heaven turn not recklessly
or confusedly but in a most beautiful and orderly course, so too each event,
directed by God, follows at its proper time and place. Just as the same face
of heaven repeatedly returns at the appointed time, if that which once was
has now existed, and that which will be has now existed. See chapter 1, verse
9. Thus a certain constant administration shines forth amid the greatest
apparent inconstancy of all things. The things done seem fleeting and
inconstant if they are considered separately and apart from what precedes and
follows them; but when the individual things are composed and compared with
one another, they are proved to possess order and fitting relation.

*Translator's notes:*

1. The third objection alleges that change implies imperfection in God's
   administration. Its four-part answer argues from the eternal decree and
   ends of events, their completeness, their purpose of producing reverence,
   and their ordered sequence.
2. The p. 160 division `con- / sequuntur` crosses the image boundary. The
   paired texts put the PA161 marker before the complete normalized word
   `consequuntur`.
3. `Occupatio` and `subjectio` are rhetorical terms for an objection and its
   answer. `Absolutum omnibus suis numeris` means complete in every respect;
   the related printed phrase `absolutionis partibus` has been retained
   closely as “parts of completeness.”
4. The edition's `omnia temere permisceant` and the construction beginning
   `tanquam operam & oleum perderent` are unusual. The English renders the
   evident warning against recklessness while retaining the latter's idiom of
   wasted labor and lamp oil.
5. The Latin preserves `ut malitiæ aut inscitiæ hominum tribuendum sit, quod
   ... aliquid requirant`, the shifts of subject in `omnia ... eorumque`, the
   printed `si quod`, and both occurrences of `jam extitit`. The translation
   conveys the contextual sense without silently altering the transcription.
6. Running heads, printed page numbers, catchwords, and the initial lemmata for
   verses 16–22 are outside this unit. Those lemmata begin the next complete
   analytical section.
