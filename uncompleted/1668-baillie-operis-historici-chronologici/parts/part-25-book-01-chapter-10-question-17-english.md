### QUESTION XVII

#### What should be determined about the periods of the final three Prophets, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi?

Answer. It is established that the first two exercised their offices in the
second year of Darius, while Joshua and Zerubbabel were building the second
temple. But the great controversy concerning which Darius this was also makes
the period of these Prophets controversial. There are three opinions on the
matter.

First, Tertullian, Clement of Jerusalem, Genebrardus, and others understand
Darius the Mede. Their anachronism is manifest and reveals an extreme
inattention to the explicit Scriptures. Haggai and Zechariah prophesied under
the Darius in whose sixth year the construction of the second temple was
completed. But Darius the Mede reigned in Babylon scarcely two years. In any
event, his rule ended before Cyrus’s began, and not only was no stone laid in
the temple before Cyrus’s reign, but none was laid before his decree to restore
Jerusalem. The temple’s construction was also obstructed throughout the days
of both Cyrus and his son Cambyses.

> Marginalia, printed p. 187: *Tertullian’s shameless anachronism*.

Once this thoroughly shameless opinion about Darius the Mede is rejected,
many in the second place favor Darius Nothus. Without any suitable argument,
they hold that the temple lay in ruins for more than a hundred years after
Cyrus’s edict and extend the lifespans of Zerubbabel, Joshua, Haggai,
Zechariah, and many of the people beyond the measure of human life. We refuted
this great anachronism of Scaliger, Junius, and others at length in Question
VI.

> Marginalia, printed p. 187: *Not Darius Nothus but Darius Hystaspes built the
> temple*.

We confirmed the third opinion, concerning Darius Hystaspes, with various
arguments in that question. We therefore do not postulate it here but assume
it as previously proved: the Prophet Haggai began his work in the second year
of Darius Hystaspes, in that year’s sixth month, as we read in Haggai 1:1.
Zechariah entered his office in the same year only two months later, as is
clear from his chapter 1, verse 1.

Beware, therefore, the most learned Junius’s annotations on those passages,
and likewise on Ezra 4:24. There, contrary to the truth, he makes the Darius
under whom the Prophets before us lived Nothus. Diodati guarded sufficiently
against this error in his first annotations but fell into it in his later
Italian edition, now rendered in English. The English Geneva Annotators were
learned and excellent men. During the Marian period they were

intimates of Calvin and Beza. They were reprinted thirty times in London
during Elizabeth’s reign. Although they were suppressed as far as possible
without commotion during James’s reign, through the hostility of bishops who
supported their own cause much too zealously, they correctly identify this
Darius as Hystaspes in all their notes. But the recent Annotators are entirely
inconsistent with themselves in their first edition. On Haggai 1:1 and
Zechariah 1:1 they expressly call this
Darius Hystaspes the third king of the Persians, yet on Ezra 4:24 the same men
will not have him be Hystaspes but Nothus.

The preface to their final edition does not excuse this great ἀσυνέχεια,
“inconsistency.” It declares that one of the group of eight colleagues, who
was responsible for commenting on Ezekiel, Daniel, and the Minor Prophets—I
think Pemberton or Reading, for I knew the other six, Featley, Gouge, Gataker,
Downham, Ley, and Taylor, as men of such learning and magnanimity that I dare
pronounce them free of every reproach—was an infamous plagiarist of Diodati.
Yet the notes on the cited passages of Haggai and Zechariah were not stolen
from Diodati, since Diodati was silent on those passages in his first Italian
edition and spoke badly in the later one. The intermediate English Annotators
spoke correctly on Haggai and Zechariah. The error we note is in the author of
the notes on Ezra, whom the colleagues’ preface absolves from every charge.
Whatever the fault here, the English did not entirely correct it in their
final, quite extensive revision—unless the liberty to assert contradictory
opinions counts as correcting the error found in one of the alternatives. In
the final edition, on Haggai 1:1, Ezra 4:7, and Ezra 6:15, although they incline
toward Darius Hystaspes, they do not reject the alternative opinion concerning
Darius Nothus. They instead present arguments for both and refuse to determine
anything themselves. Such ἀδιαφορεῖν, “indifference,” in serious commentators
on such an uncommon question is scarcely sufficiently commendable.

To determine the period of the third Prophet, Malachi, one must first know who
this Malachi was. I find four opinions on the matter. The first is Origen’s:
he thinks Malachi was an angel, not a man. Origen adduces nothing for this
ἀτοπώτατον, “most absurd,” fancy other than the translation of the
seventy-two interpreters, who rendered the word *Malachi* as “his angel.”
Jerome, and after him all other Christian teachers, reject this foul error as
impossible. Although, by the power of divine omnipotence, they concede a
personal union of human nature with the second person of the Trinity, they
deny that such a union is possible for any other created suppositum. Thus the
Prophet Malachi cannot be a person composed of an angel and a man. An argument
from etymology is highly presumptive and contingent when applied to common
nouns, and much more so when applied to proper names. Malachi himself calls
John the Baptist מלאכי, “my angel,” gives him the name Elijah, and even calls
all priests God’s angels. Must it therefore be denied that Elijah, John the
Baptist, and all the priests were men? Moreover, the seventy interpreters
should not have interpreted a proper name as a common noun. Their authority
here should be valued even less because they make another manifest error in
this very word. At the end of *Malachi* they substitute “his” for *yod*. If
they wished to translate it, they should have rendered it “my angel” in the
first person, not “his angel” in the third.

> Marginalia, printed p. 188: *Origen’s absurd opinion about Malachi*.

The second opinion is that of both recent and ancient Jews. Jerome and Ribera
report it expressly, and Calvin in his French prologue to Malachi comes close
to following it: namely, that Malachi is Ezra because he discusses only the
things Ezra discusses. If this opinion were true, Malachi’s period would be
clear because Ezra’s is clear. But most Roman Catholics and Protestants reject
this opinion. Shared subject matter does not make writers identical. Moreover,
Malachi treats various matters about which Ezra says nothing. Finally, to
what end would Ezra have concealed his own name? Since he bore the name Ezra
in one book, why would he assume the title Malachi in another?

> Marginalia, printed p. 188: *Ribera’s and Calvin’s opinion about Malachi is
> refuted*.

With the same arguments we refute those who have thought that Elijah, Haggai,
or Zechariah was Malachi.

The third opinion is that of the very solid Gualter in his prologue to
Malachi. He thinks this Prophet was the same Malchijah who, according to
Nehemiah 8:4, stood on Ezra’s left on the day of the fast. But this opinion
cannot be accepted either. It is not that the periods could not agree
sufficiently, but that, quite apart from the singular nature of the
conjecture, the names themselves differ in their constituent letters.
*Malachi* has in its middle not only the vowel *qamets* but also the letter
*aleph*, both absent from *Malchijah*. *Malchijah* has a *dagesh* in *kaph*
with *hiriq* beneath it, and in *yod* a *mappiq* with *qamets* beneath and
*he* appended, all of which are absent from *Malachi*. These seven differences
make the words *Malachi* and *Malchijah* differ so greatly that they cannot be
one man’s single name.

> Marginalia, printed p. 188: *Gualter’s opinion*.

There remains the fourth, common opinion, which is our own: Malachi was a
Prophet distinct from Ezra and everyone else. No arguments beyond those with
which we refuted the other opinions are needed to prove this.

Nothing certain about his particular period is found in Scripture, but from
the subject matter

[Printed p. 189 | IA leaf 219 | access PDF 219 | OCR object 218]

of the book we draw the following general conclusions. First, he wrote after
Ezra, Haggai, and Zechariah, and was therefore the last of all the Prophets in
time. We do not infer this so much from his ancient placement after all the
other Old Testament writers, although that argument is usually, if not always,
quite probable. Nor do we infer it from Jerome’s rule, following the Jews,
that every biblical writing bearing no indication of its time was
contemporary with the preceding book that does bear such an indication, or at
least not earlier than it. Leaving these aside, we derive three points from
the book’s subject matter.

First, Malachi wrote after Haggai and Zechariah. Through their labors the
interrupted construction of the temple was completed, whereas Malachi acted
as Prophet when the temple had already been completed. For he rebukes the
priests and people for the impieties committed in their rites in the completed
temple. Second, he wrote after Ezra and Nehemiah. He sought to correct more
carefully the renewed abuses in foreign marriages, divorces, and polygamy that
they had reformed. Third, he wrote last of all the Prophets because he
prophesied far more distinctly about the Messiah who would come soon and his
forerunner John the Baptist. Thus there was no need for another Prophet to
give warning of the Messiah’s days, when he stood, as it were, at the door and
was certainly just about to come.

We do not dare descend to greater particularity. Cornelius à Lapide assigns
this Prophet to the seventh year of Artaxerxes Longimanus, when Ezra was
correcting the temple’s pollutions. He does so on his own conjecture, without
anyone’s authority or even a suitable argument. Claims of this kind are
rejected with as much ease as the boldness with which they are proposed.

> Marginalia, printed p. 189: *The rashness of Cornelius à Lapide*.

We place in the same category what Epiphanius records about Malachi in the
last of his books on the lives of the Prophets, without any witness or
argument: that he was a Zebulunite born at Sophe; that he was buried there
with his parents; that he died in his youth; that he possessed a remarkable,
angelic bodily form; and indeed that he was adorned above all others with this
privilege—whatever he prophesied, an angel from heaven always appeared on the
same day, repeating and confirming it to the people. See how ancient the
monks’ traditions are. These bold little tales were not born in the church
yesterday or the day before.
