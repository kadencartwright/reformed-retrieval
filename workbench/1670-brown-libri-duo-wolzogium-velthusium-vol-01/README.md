# John Brown of Wamphray, *Libri duo* against Wolzogen and Velthuysen, volume I (1670)

> **Working edition in progress:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,

> human editor or prepared for publication.

## Work and edition

- **Author:** John Brown of Wamphray (c. 1610–1679)
- **Short title:** *Libri duo: in priori Wolzogium ... in posteriori Lamberti
  Velthusii sententia Libertino-Erastiana ...*
- **Edition:** Amsterdam, Henricus ab Aquisgrano, 1670
- **Volume:** I of the ONB two-volume set
- **Contents:** Brown's dedicatory epistle and ecclesiological preface, followed
  by the first book, an examination of Ludwig Wolzogen's two books on the
  interpreter of Scripture

The title page advertises two books, but the Austrian National Library binds
and digitizes them as two volumes. Volume I contains the preliminaries and the
complete book against Wolzogen; volume II contains the book against Lambert
van Velthuysen. This directory is explicitly volume-specific so that the
second volume can later receive a separate, unambiguous workbench.

## Controlling witness and rights

The controlling witness is the Austrian National Library copy, barcode
`Z176010803`, exposed through the stable
[`IIIF Presentation manifest`](https://iiif.onb.ac.at/presentation/ABO/Z176010803/manifest).
The manifest supplies 468 canvases, stable image services, and page-level OCR.
Its rights field reports `http://rightsstatements.org/vocab/NoC-NC/1.0/`.
Only the public-domain 1670 text is reproduced here; no ONB images or OCR
files are redistributed. The page images, not the machine OCR, govern the
transcription. ONB OCR is used only as a navigation and collation aid.

## Exact volume map

- ONB canvases 1–6: cover, ownership/endpaper, and blank preliminary matter;
  excluded.
- Canvas 7: title page.
- Canvas 8: blank title verso.
- Canvases 9–15: Brown's dedicatory epistle to John Livingstone, Robert
  Trail, John Nevay, Robert MacWard, and James Garner.
- Canvases 16–49: Brown's authorial *Ad Lectorem Praefatio*, including its
  thirty-two numbered ecclesiological assertions and concluding directions
  concerning separation.
- Canvases 50–53: separately attributed commendatory poems, including a Greek
  epigram; not Brown's preface.
- Canvas 54: blank/offset leaf between preliminaries and body.
- Canvases 55–461: Brown's complete first book against Wolzogen, continuously
  printed pp. 1–407.
- Canvases 462–468: terminal blank, ownership, binding, and back-cover matter;
  excluded.

No missing or duplicated textual leaf was detected in the mapped sequence.
The ONB manifest's automated `CHAPTER_START` tags on many rectos are not
authorial structural data and are not used as boundaries.

## Editorial method

Long *s*, ligatures, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v` are normalized.
Historical spelling, capitalization where significant, numbered assertions,
biblical and bibliographical references, and Brown's quotations are retained.
Running heads, catchwords, signatures, ornaments, and purely typographical
lineation are omitted. Source markers identify the unnumbered preliminary
page, exact ONB canvas, barcode, and image number. When a word crosses a
source-image boundary, the marker is placed at the printed break and the two
halves are marked with asterisks.

The English is a complete translation of the paired Latin, not a summary. A
modern translation of an extract from Assertion 23 was identified during the
source audit but is not a wording source for any part here.

XXXVIII. In the remainder of chapter three the *Paradoxical Exercitator*
expounds the obscurity and ambiguity of Discourse at great length, so as to
infer from it the obscurity and ambiguity of the Sacred Writings. To this end
he gathers Latin authors with great effort and adduces several examples from
the Scriptures. In reply to these *Wolzogen* had indeed said rightly that
examples from Latin could be transferred only remotely to the Scriptures,
which were composed in Hebrew and Greek. But who will regard *Wolzogen* as a
faithful champion of the Orthodox cause, when he avoids examining most of the
Passages of Scripture collected by the *Exercitator* and does not answer those
examples with so much as a grunt? Ought he not to have vindicated them from
the ambiguity which *Paradox* had tried to fasten upon them, if he did not
agree with *Paradox*, accuse them of ambiguity in the same way, or abandon the
cause of the Reformed? For who, apart from *Wolzogen*, would tolerate: 1. This
*Paradox*, when from Hebraisms, Graecisms, or Latinisms he infers the obscurity
of those Scriptures in which such foreign words are found, although those
very words which are derived from a foreign language and introduced into the
vernacular become through daily use as familiar as any others, and anyone
skilled in the languages from which those words are taken can easily
understand them? 2. Did *Wolzogen* have nothing to reply to what *Paradox* had
said about the distinction between *believing that God is, believing in God*,
and *believing God*, a distinction sometimes employed by Theologians, not
without reason, since an emphasis very frequently appears in the New
Testament in the prepositions εἰς and ἐπί? See John 1:12; 3:36; 14:1; Romans
10:14; 1 Peter 1:21; 2:6; John 2:11; 4:39; and 6:35, 40, 47. And *believing in
God* or *in Christ* comprehends within itself also *believing*

*God* and *Christ*, and presupposes *believing that God* or *Christ exists*.
The phrase *believing that God is* does not signify the same thing as
*believing in God*; nor does the phrase *believing God* or *Christ* always
imply the same thing as *believing in God* or *in Christ*, although the matter
can sometimes stand so, as in 1 Timothy 1:12 and John 8:31, where the
circumstances confirm this, and in Romans 4:3, 17 and Galatians 3:6, where the
Hebrew text from which they are taken—namely, Genesis 15:6, where *Abraham* is
said to have believed בַּיהוָה—clearly indicates it. Yet even if *Wolzogen* was
unwilling to defend Theologians of the greatest renown, he could nevertheless,
if it had been a matter of concern to him, have offered some defense of the
Dutch Theologians who translated the Bible and added marginal notes. For
*Paradox* objects that they translate Exodus 14:31 thus: *they believed in
Jehovah and Moses his servant*, and 2 Chronicles 20:20 thus: *believe in
Jehovah your God, and believe his Prophets*. Yet in their commentary on the
first passage they expressly confess: *The Hebrew mode of expression is
indeed the same, but the difference is derived from the nature of the thing.*
Is this the faithful brother of the Orthodox, who hears even the Dutch—men who
have rendered outstanding service to Christ's Church—being so undeservedly
thrashed by *Paradox*, and will not support them with so much as a finger as
they labor under that burden? 3. *Paradox* maintained in § 13 that Luke 13:9,
2 Corinthians 5:13, and very many other unnamed passages were obscure because
certain words had been omitted. Was it no concern of *Wolzogen's* to remove
this obscurity, when he could very easily have shown from even a single word
in the preceding context how that Ellipsis was to be supplied? 4. Could no
consideration of his reputation or office move *Wolzogen* to free 2 Kings
18:5–7 and 23:25 and 1 Kings 3:12 from amphiboly and hence from the ambiguity
which *Paradox* dares to smear upon them in § 21, by showing how the things
said there, both about *Solomon* and about

respects, without any hyperbole at all, much less amphiboly or ambiguity; and
by showing how one surpassed the others—*Solomon*, namely, with respect to

skillfully; *Hezekiah*, with respect to zeal and spiritual fortitude in
extirpating idolatry and restoring the worship of God; and finally *Josiah*,

would *Wolzogen* not vindicate the passage in Luke 7:22, *the blind see, etc.*,
from the ambiguity which *Paradox* had said was in it on page 22 because it

have done so by saying that from the circumstances and purpose of the passage
it is entirely certain that those words cannot be taken in a composite sense,
and that this is evident even from the common usage of speech. 6. Did
*Wolzogen* have nothing to reply to the *Exercitator's* claim in § 24, pages

the Evangelists and Apostles did not punctuate their writings or distinguish
them with the marks and signs of commas, colons, and periods, although this
has most dangerous consequences? 7. Did *Wolzogen* not think it worth the
effort to vindicate other passages from the ambiguity smeared upon them by
*Paradox* because of the absence of punctuation, question marks, and the like?
For example, in John 21:15–17 the interrogation is plainly apparent even from
*Peter's* Answer; in John 5:29 the circumstances of the text show that the
verb ἐρευνᾶτε is taken as an imperative; in Matthew 26:45 and Mark 14:41 an

clear that *Pilate* did not pose the question in earnest, for he neither
received nor awaited an answer. In Colossians 2:21, *Do not touch, etc.*, the

XXXIX. *Paradox* said in chapter 4, § 1 that Sacred Scripture is both obscure
and ambiguous in many passages; that this was abundantly evident from the
examples adduced above and from the Theologians' notes, explanations,
observations, commentaries, paraphrases, and interpretations. He then cited
*Luther's On the Bondage of the Will* and *Chamier's Panstratia Catholica*,
volume 1, book 15, chapter 4, § 5. He adds further: *[There is not the least
doubt, not only that Scripture is obscure and ambiguous, but also that,
because it consists of words, all the enumerated species both of obscurities
and of ambiguities can find a place in it; indeed, if not all of them, at least
the greatest part, and specifically the principal ones. Besides these, other
kinds of no slight importance also occur (such as, by the Theologians' own
admission, mystical predication, both typological and Sacramental,
anthropopathism and theoprepy, prolepsis, inversion or anticipation, variant
readings of both the Old and New Testaments, and similar things).]* Here
*Wolzogen* had an exceedingly opportune occasion to vindicate and defend both
orthodox truth and the Reformed Theologians. Let us see how he discharged his
office, on page 122, 122. He answers thus: *[From the things which he disputed
in the preceding chapter, the conclusion is that certain passages of Scripture
are obscure and ambiguous. Who ever denied this? Why did he strive so hard to
prove what he knew no one called into doubt?]* Really, *Wolzogen*? *Paradox*
had said that Scripture is both obscure and ambiguous in many passages. Do you
concede this to him? Do you confess it? But what Reformed man agrees with you?

[Page 157 | ONB canvas 211 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000211]

*Paradox* had tried to prove this from the examples mentioned in chapter 3.
Do you also concede that all the passages he adduced are obscure or ambiguous?
But what Reformed man will confess this with you? *Paradox* had said that this
is clearly established from the Theologians' notes, explanations, and so
forth. Do you approve this argument? What Reformed man will grant that
inference with you? Who would not say instead that those things entail the
perspicuity of Scripture, since all those notes, explanations, paraphrases,
commentaries, and the like are drawn from the Scriptures themselves—provided,
of course, that they are true and genuine? Hence it is manifestly clear that
things which appear somewhat obscure in one passage are expressed more
clearly elsewhere. The Orthodox indeed admit that certain passages of
Scripture are obscure with respect to us, on account of our blindness and
ignorance. But *Paradox* wishes them to be exceedingly obscure not only in
relation to us but also in themselves. For, as we have just heard, he wishes
nothing to be obscure and ambiguous in relation to us which is not also
obscure and ambiguous in itself and absolutely. Does *Wolzogen* think and say
the same thing? Indeed he does, as we heard above, and he gives no different
account of himself here. Who, then, will regard *Wolzogen* as Orthodox and as
a Patron of the Orthodox? Did *Wolzogen* have nothing to adduce in vindication
of *Luther* and *Chamier*, to whom the *Exercitator* does an intolerable
injury? Or did he think that *Paradox* cited them appropriately and faithfully?
If so, he is as injurious to those Orthodox writers and to the truth as
*Paradox* himself. For *Luther* in that passage speaks only about the obscurity
which flows from ignorance of vocabulary and grammar, but which nevertheless
does not prevent knowledge of the matters that are all set forth in the
Scriptures. He expressly adds: *[Yet it is foolish and impious—to call the
matters obscure because of a few obscure words. If the words are obscure in
one place, they are clear in another—it now makes no difference whether the
thing is in the light while some*

[Page 158 | ONB canvas 212 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000212]

*sign of it is in darkness, since meanwhile many other signs of it are in the
light.]* And in chapter 9: *[As for the fact that many things remain hidden
from many people, this does not arise from the obscurity of Scripture but from
their blindness or indolence, since they make no effort to see the clearest
truth.]* *Chamier* likewise has a magnificent view of the perspicuity of
Scripture, as is evident from the same chapter cited. For although he admits
that certain passages of Scripture are obscure if they are considered alone,
by themselves, and separately, he nevertheless affirms that from Scripture
considered ὅλως, or in its whole body, all Christians can certainly learn what
God's sure and constant will is concerning the whole of religion, or
concerning all the heads necessary to religion. Does *Wolzogen* believe that
all those species of obscurities and ambiguities enumerated by *Paradox* in
chapter 3—or at least the greatest part, and specifically the principal
ones—are found in the Sacred Writings? Finally, does *Wolzogen* believe that
the Scriptures are exceedingly obscure because mystical predication,
anthropopathism, or prolepsis sometimes occurs? If not, did he show himself a
faithful patron of the Orthodox cause and of the Orthodox when he answered
absolutely none of these things? Or rather an accomplice?
