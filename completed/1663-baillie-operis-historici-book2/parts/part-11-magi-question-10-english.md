## QUESTION X

### At what time did the Magi worship Christ as an infant?

A controversy arose long ago among theologians, both ancient and more recent, over precisely when the Magi, led by the star, came to Bethlehem and worshiped Christ in his cradle. Matthew 2:1 places the Magi’s journey from the East immediately after the account of the Nativity and, after their return, Christ’s descent into Egypt at verse 13. But after the events performed in the Jerusalem temple on the day of purification—which according to the law was the fortieth day from the virgin’s delivery—Luke 2:39 has Joseph travel with Mary and Christ not into Egypt but to Nazareth. Every appearance of contradiction here is easily removed. The later evangelists omit many things which the earlier ones related fully. Matthew had described the history of the Magi and Christ’s descent into Egypt so clearly that Mark, Luke, and John pass those things over entirely. Luke’s placing of the return to Nazareth after the purification, while Matthew connects the descent into Egypt with the Magi’s worship at Bethlehem, does not support those who suppose that on the very day of purification Joseph was warned by the angel at Jerusalem and hastened into Egypt without visiting either Bethlehem or Nazareth. For nothing prevents Joseph from having gone to Nazareth after the purification and then, when warned by the angel, fleeing into Egypt. This whole dispute seems very groundless, but the time of the worship at Bethlehem is not so clear, either among the ancients or among more recent writers. I find three or four principal opinions.

The first is that of Epiphanius, Eusebius, Augustine, and others, who hold that the Magi came to Bethlehem two full years after the virgin’s delivery. They rest upon the foundation that Herod killed the children two years old and under according to the time which he had ascertained from the Magi. —hem and spoke with Herod only two years after Christ’s birth. This opinion is commonly rejected by Protestants and papists alike, and its foundation is not sound. For the two years which Herod had ascertained from the Magi must be referred not to Christ’s birth or the Magi’s arrival, but to the time of the massacre of the infants, which happened long after the Magi’s departure.

Epiphanius indeed does rightly refute, in his fifty-first heresy, that of the *Alogi*, number 8, the objections which Porphyry, Celsus, and other enemies of the Christian name made against the evangelists: that they simultaneously recount Christ’s birth and circumcision, the Magi’s worship, the child Jesus’s appearance in the temple, and the descent into Egypt, as though all these things happened on one day and sacred history were therefore impossible and false. Epiphanius answers that the apostates were palpably blind, since they did not discern the distinct times of this history. Although the circumcision, the Nativity, and the purification are joined in the narrative, they are shown to have occurred at different intervals: the circumcision on the eighth day after the birth, and the presentation of every male in the temple on the fortieth. Thus far this most learned father is correct and sufficient for beating back the enemies’ slanders against the truth. But his addition that the Magi’s arrival happened in the second year after the Nativity, and the return from Egypt in the fourth, has no follower among more recent writers.

The Jesuit Maldonatus, at Matthew 2, is not content to reject and refute Epiphanius’s opinion. He adds that Epiphanius asserts that every year Joseph brought Mary and Christ back from Egypt to Bethlehem, and that this is how the Magi found Christ at Bethlehem when he was two years old. Our Casaubon censures Maldonatus for wrongfully imputing this error to Epiphanius. Petavius candidly admits that his colleague Maldonatus’s error was deservedly noted, for anyone who reads Epiphanius’s text seriously will free him from this absurdity. He does indeed have something similar: namely, that each year Joseph went from Nazareth to Bethlehem in order to proceed from there to Jerusalem. Epiphanius certainly asserts this gratuitously and falsely. Nevertheless, Maldonatus’s mistake was to substitute Egypt for Nazareth, and here Casaubon’s censure is entirely just; Petavius does not dare oppose it. But lest Casaubon carry off with impunity any censure of the Jesuits, however deserved, Petavius attacks him as a heretic and a producer of books sewn together from abuse and slanders. Anger is the source of this black cuttlefish ink, because Casaubon had called Maldonatus an abusive and virulent writer. Maldonatus very richly deserved these epithets for his exceedingly virulent abuse of the venerable Beza. In his annotations at Matthew 2, Maldonatus argues against the Magi’s kingship and thinks that Psalm 71:10, about the kings of Tarshish and the islands and about Saba and Sheba, is badly wrested to the evangelists’ Magi. Maldonatus admits that passages of Scripture are sometimes applied in Roman missals and liturgies to persons of whom they are not literally true. For example, the church sings of Mary the Lord’s mother the words of Luke 10:42, “Mary has chosen the good part, which shall not be taken away from her,” although it is evident in Luke that the Lord speaks these words not about his mother, but about Mary the sister of Lazarus and Martha. Maldonatus also grants that Mantuanus and other learned men, among whom I find Jansenius and Estius, argue by many sound reasons against the kingship of the Magi. Yet he is unwilling to permit Beza this liberty from his cartload of abuse, one of the lighter pieces of which is his repeated charge that Beza mocked the ancient fathers. Jansenius, Estius, and other papists certainly deny that the fathers regarded the Magi as kings in any proper sense, and Beza cites various fathers in support of this opinion; far from ever mocking them, he offers for very well-deserved ridicule the recent Jesuit superstition and palpable idolatry by which people at Cologne religiously worship the undoubtedly spurious bodies of the three kings, as the Jesuit Cornelius admits in his comments on this passage.

But there is no reason to complain of the abuse of Maldonatus, Petavius, or any Jesuit, since Richard Montagu, a leader in the Laudian ranks, far surpasses all the Jesuits in exceedingly virulent attacks upon the leading Protestants in this very argument, in the former part of his *Origins*, number 70.

Compared with him, the Jesuit Cano is a most learned and moderate theologian. Yet against Protestants who do not think the Magi’s feast should be celebrated with solemn worship, he hurls the charges “reformation of madmen” and “Cathar Puritanism.” These are Genevan censors, long-winded men, Myconian innovators, little noses, terribly clever sages, for whom nothing has savor unless it was first brought forth from Lake Geneva. What is the very summit of their fury and the furthest limit of their madness is the most dreadful curse by which he devotes that whole company of Reformers, as though they were Puritans, to final perdition, at number 62.

“Father Zeus, how the race of the young has perished!” From this it is transparently clear enough what was to be expected from those Bacchants in their frenzy, had not the merciful God confounded and shattered the Laudian sect.

—ion as a matter of divine faith, since it was supposedly established not only by church tradition but also by public practice for many centuries, by which the day of Epiphany, or the Magi’s feast, is celebrated on that date and its observance is necessarily imposed upon all Christians in the public liturgy. Protestants argue as follows against their tyranny in this opinion, in order to remove this bond of the Roman Church’s authority from their consciences.

First. It cannot be proved by any firm argument from Scripture or any certain tradition of the primitive church that the Magi worshiped Christ on the thirteenth day after his birth. The present Roman Church indeed places the day of Epiphany on January 6, but, as Casaubon demonstrates in *Exercises* 2, number 36, on that same day the ancient Roman Church once used to celebrate Christ’s Nativity, although for some centuries they have assigned the solemnity of the Nativity to December 25. Today they also commemorate Christ’s baptism and the first miracle at Cana on January 6 along with the Magi’s worship. Just as the Roman Church’s ancient practice in assigning the manifestation at Christ’s birth to January 6, and its present practice in assigning Christ’s baptism and the first miracle at Cana to the same day, do not prove that all those things happened on that day, so the church’s assignment of the Magi’s feast to that date proves no more that the Magi’s worship occurred then. This argument of Estius in his *Difficult Passages* at Matthew 2 seems firm and irrefutable.

Second. The Magi’s journey from the East to Jerusalem could not be completed in so few days. For first, of those thirteen days at least five must be set aside from the journey: one for the journey from Jerusalem to Bethlehem, and another two during which they remained in Jerusalem and spoke with Herod and with the council of priests and scribes. Of the ten days remaining, at least two must necessarily be allowed after the star was seen and its portent understood, so that they could prepare the things required by such a long journey. Thus only eight remain, or only seven even if you grant that they rested on the Sabbath.

Now the Magi’s abode in the East is thought by very many to be so many miles distant from Jerusalem that far more than seven days must be assigned to cover them. It is true that if, with Remigius, you employ the angels’ service to carry the Magi through the air, fewer days would suffice for the longer journey, although the lives of animals during excessively rapid motion are thought incapable of being preserved by any created power. But none of the doctors approved this miracle of Remigius for the many reasons which Tostado and, after him, Salmeron and others relate.

The fable recounted by Navarrus, Osório, and the Jesuit Maffei is also commonly rejected by the papists: namely, that the Magi’s abode was at the furthest East; that one of the three Magi was Perimal, king of Coulão or Cranganore or Calicut far beyond the Ganges; and that after his return from Jerusalem to India he built at Calicut a splendid temple with an altar and border in honor of the virgin Mary. See how much trust is to be placed in Osório, Maffei, and other papist historians’ very grandiose accounts—marvelous if they were true, or rather monstrous. In Mary’s days and for some centuries afterward, no public house was open for Christian worship even in Judea itself, within the Roman world itself. After temples began to be erected, for many years no altars were seen in them, while images crept into temples only at a late date. Yet if we trust those good men, before Mary died one of the Magi had erected at Calicut in eastern India a magnificent palace with an altar and an image. Away with such nonsense!

Some of the fathers do indeed place the Magi far beyond the Ganges, but these allow two full years for their journey. An unfinished Latin work on Matthew among Chrysostom’s works, though by no means Chrysostom’s—yet by a sufficiently ancient, learned, and pious author—takes this course, but it has few followers. Besides unnecessary miracles, the account supposes that the Magi, traveling on foot for two full years and as many on their return, kept their clothes and shoes unchanged and intact without wear, just like Israel in the wilderness. Luke’s text refutes this invention, for the star was not that of the king of the Jews who was going to be born but of the king who had been born. Thus the Magi could not begin their journey at the star’s warning two years beforehand, but only some days after the Lord’s birth. Nor is the furthest East so widely distant from the West, or the entire hemisphere of the earth so broad, that a moderately swift pedestrian would not need far fewer days than there are in two years to traverse it.

What Jerome says about Persia as the Magi’s homeland is embraced by Maldonatus, Calvin, and many others, but most of these, following Jerome, employ dromedaries for the Magi’s journey. Although this kind of camel is much swifter than the fastest horses, it could not in seven days—or even twice seven—carry even courier messengers over as much territory as lies between Pasargadae in Persia and Jerusalem, much less be suited to carrying kings gently within that time. Indeed, those who assign to the Magi the East closest to the Jews as their abode, whether in Mesopotamia or in Arabia, as a great many learned men do, will not easily persuade us that even this journey could be completed suitably and as became kings in seven days. There was no need here for troublesome and undignified haste.

Third argument. The Magi did not come to Bethlehem at a time which would have brought Christ’s infant life into manifest danger from the tyrant Herod. Yet this would have happened if the Magi had come to Bethlehem on the thirteenth day after the virgin’s delivery, for there are twenty-seven days between the thirteenth day from her delivery and the child’s presentation in the temple. Hearing nothing from the Magi throughout all that time—although they had declared that they were setting out over scarcely seven or eight miles, the short distance from Jerusalem to Bethlehem—Herod would easily have become suspicious that the Magi had deceived him and that a danger truly threatened both him and his kingdom from the newly born king Messiah. He would have resolved that it must immediately be driven away by the crime of infanticide, even before Christ left Bethlehem, at least as soon as he learned what great things the shepherds had proclaimed at the birth, and Simeon and Anna at the Jerusalem temple. This danger from an aroused Herod is avoided by a later arrival of the Magi at Bethlehem. By this and other arguments, the aforementioned most learned man William Estius, primary professor of theology at Douai, professes that he abandons the Roman Church’s common opinion and teaches that the Magi came to Bethlehem not on the thirteenth day from the birth but some time after the infant’s presentation in the temple.

The positive part of this third opinion, that of Estius, does not please me. First, if the Magi had come after the presentation in the temple, they would not have found the child with his mother at Bethlehem. For immediately, as Luke’s account indicates, Joseph departed Jerusalem for Nazareth with the child and his mother; he did not return to Bethlehem. Estius’s claim that Joseph returned to Bethlehem so that he could gather his few belongings there before setting out for Nazareth is without any argument or testimony.

Richard Montagu, with Estius, seems to postpone the Magi’s arrival until after the fortieth day of purification. Yet he clings tenaciously to the feast of Epiphany on January 6, when the infant Son of God was revealed to the Magi at Bethlehem. Let him see for himself how these two truly contrary things agree.

The fourth opinion, in which we rest, is the common Protestant view: that the Magi came after the thirteenth day from the birth, but nevertheless before the fortieth day of purification. What we have brought forward to overthrow the three preceding opinions serves to establish this fourth one, and we shall not repeat it. Let us answer briefly only what some object.

If the Magi’s arrival had preceded the purification, they say, then the gold and the Magi’s gifts would easily have enabled Joseph and Mary to offer a more costly sacrifice for Christ in the temple than a pair of turtledoves. Reply: although the gifts were brought out from the Magi’s treasures, they were not so great as to change Joseph and Mary’s station in life. They, and Christ himself, certainly remained throughout their lives in very straitened circumstances, so that the sacrifice enjoined by God upon the poor was entirely suitable for Mary despite the Magi’s gifts. I omit Joseph’s necessary care to provide supplies for the journey to Egypt, and also the alms from those gifts which charity required to be given to poorer people.

When I had written these things, but not before, I came upon Spanheim’s most learned dissertation on the Magi in part 2 of his *Gospel Difficulties*, page 255 and following. I rejoiced that I agreed with that eminent man on almost everything, but after reading him I thought nothing in what I had written above should be changed.
