The second corollary will concern the things conjoined with election—that is,
the elect themselves. It is evident that in God’s counsel there is a certain
and definite number of the elect which can neither be increased nor
diminished. If they are considered simply and absolutely, they are many; but
if they are compared with the contrary multitude of the reprobate, they can
rightly be called few. The two latter parts of this assertion—for the first
has been sufficiently demonstrated thus far—must be supported for us by the
plain authority of Scripture, in order to blunt the calumnies of those who
maliciously object against us in this doctrine, among other things, that we
teach not only that certain people have been elected to salvation, whose
number is so fixed that it can neither be enlarged nor reduced, but even that
they are exceedingly few, while all the rest are devoted and destined to
eternal destruction. These supposedly gentle people denounce this violently
as an impious and cruel dogma. Is that really so, good sirs? Would God be
ignorant of the number of his own, when the Apostle testifies that this is the
seal of election, that “the Lord knows those who are his,” and Christ himself
says, “I am not speaking about all; I know whom I have chosen”? Or do you
suppose that God’s eternal counsel is deceitful and changeable from day to day
and hour to hour?

Second, we do not deny that they can be called many when considered in
themselves, since the Lord himself says in Matthew 8, “Many will come from east
and west and recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven.”
Likewise Revelation 7 says, “After this I saw, and behold, a great multitude
which no one could number, from every nation, tribe, people, and language;
they stood before the throne and in the sight of the Lamb, clothed in white
robes, with palms in their hands.” Therefore, even apart from the holy and
elect angels, that company of people gathered from every part of the world
for the triumph of eternal life is great. Hence Augustine says, “The elect
are, as it were, a world of their own; likewise, the Church without spot or
wrinkle, gathered from every nation and destined to reign with Christ
forever, is itself the land of the blessed, the land of the living.”

Yet if they are compared with the crowd of the reprobate, does the Lord not
plainly declare in Matthew 7, “Narrow is the gate and confined is the way which
leads to life, and few are those who find it”? And in chapter 16 he says,
“Many are called, but few are elect.” In the same way, in Luke 12 he calls his
Church a little flock. Paul in Romans 9, following Isaiah as he cries out over
Israel, says, “Even if the number of the children of Israel should be as the
sand of the sea, a remnant will be saved.” Finally, Christ himself predicts
that in the final times before his coming he will scarcely find faith upon the
earth when he comes. This proves sufficiently how few, even in these final
ages, belong to his election. Let each person therefore be stirred up even by
this to undertake that confined and difficult way and to enter through that
narrow gate with the utmost effort of soul.

[Marginalia: Lecture XXII. Objection. Answer. Matthew 8:11. Revelation 7:9.
Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, book 3, chapter 34. Matthew 7:13 and 16:26.
Luke 12:32. Romans 9. Isaiah 10:22. And Romans 9:27. Luke 18:8. Caution.]

The third inference which follows from this is the following: just as the
elect can be known to God alone in this life, so to them alone belong living
and saving faith, Acts 13 and Titus 1; likewise the power and efficacy of the
saving promises; Christ’s redemption, merit, mediation, righteousness, and
eternal life itself. They alone are dear to God; they alone are true and
living members of that catholic and invisible Church, Psalm 65. Consequently,
they cannot persevere to the end in unbelief, impatience, and sins; nor can
they finally and totally fall away from faith and grace. Rather, final
perseverance in faith and holiness belongs to them alone. Therefore, they can
never become reprobate, be erased from the book of life, or be distinguished
and separated from the rest by any merit of nature or any natural worth and
excellence. They are distinguished instead by God’s mere and gratuitous
grace, which through his eternal predestination made this whole distinction
between them and the rest, so that all the honor, praise, and glory for their
salvation may be rendered entirely to his glorious grace alone through Jesus
Christ. AMEN.

[Marginalia: Third corollary.]

Let this suffice concerning election itself, the first part or species of
predestination.

It follows now that we should touch more briefly upon the other part or
species of the same thing, namely reprobation. Its doctrine is not so
necessary to be believed for salvation, since it does not pertain to us by a
special application, as the preceding doctrine of election does. Moreover, it
becomes clear partly from the contrary things already taught, and, finally,
many things have already been said in explaining the nature of election which
are common to each part. It is therefore unnecessary to repeat them here.
First, then, as regards

[Marginalia: Transition. On reprobation.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 127 | De Prædestinatione p. 85]

the question whether it exists: although some reject the very word and also
pervert and corrupt its doctrine, we rightly must admit and receive both,
since both are supported by the sacred authority of Scripture. We have the
word itself in Jeremiah 6. Speaking there of those most obstinate and
stiff-necked despisers of the divine law for whom no hope of correction
remained, the prophet concludes: “They are called reprobate silver, because
Jehovah has rejected them.” Here the prophet uses the same word that is
contrary to election, as is clear from the evident antithesis of these words
which we have in Isaiah 7: “He will make us eat butter and honey, so that this
child may know how to despise, or reject and reprobate, evil, and to choose
good.” So also Hebrews 6 says: “The land which has often drunk the rain that
comes upon it and nevertheless produces only thorns and thistles is reprobate
or rejected and near to a curse; its end tends toward burning.”

[Marginalia: Concerning the name. Jeremiah 6:30. מאם. Isaiah 7:15. Hebrews
6:8.]

As for the thing itself, no matter how much human reason and the stubborn
sense of the flesh—which cannot subject itself to divine wisdom—resist it, it
is set forth still much more clearly and openly in the divine oracles. In
particular, Romans 9 says: “For this very purpose I raised you up, that I might
show my power in you and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth.
Therefore, he has mercy on whom he wills, but whom he wills he hardens.” And
again: “Does not the potter have power over the clay to make from the same
lump one vessel for honor and another for dishonor? What if, willing to show
his wrath and make his power known, he endured with great patience”—or
forbearance and restraint of wrath—“vessels of wrath, fitted or joined
together for destruction?”

In 1 Peter 2 he says that the unbelieving Jews who stumble and take offense at
Christ were also appointed to this; and he contrasts them with the faithful
as elect, adding, “But you are an elect race,” and so forth. What, then, was
plainly lacking except to say that those others of whom he had just spoken
were a reprobate and rejected race? Likewise, speaking in 2 Peter 2 about
those pseudo-teachers and false doctors who would trouble the Christian
Church, he says that “their condemnation from long ago is not idle, nor does
their destruction slumber.” Speaking of the same people, the Apostle Jude
says that they were “written beforehand, or previously prescribed and
appointed for this condemnation.”

From these passages it is evident that there is a decree of reprobation just
as there is of election. One of these can no more exist in the divine mind
without the other than heat can be introduced into some subject without cold
being expelled, or the air be illuminated without darkness fleeing. We have
also taught above that this act of the divine decree is comprehended under the
term predestination. This is evident even from the words which Holy Scripture
uses to designate it for us—appointment, prior inscription, fitting, raising
up, and others like them—besides the testimonies of the ancients which we
adduced above for this purpose.

[Marginalia: Concerning the thing itself. Romans 9:17, 18, 22. 1 Peter 2:8.
2 Peter 2:3. Jude 4. Analogy.]

It follows now that we should see what reprobation is and how it ought to be
defined or described by us. Reprobation is that part of the divine decree
opposed to election by which God decreed to pass over certain people with
respect to supernatural grace and not to take them up into love and eternal
life, in order thereby to declare his wrath, holiness, and justice in their
deserved destruction. Or it is God’s will concerning the permission of
humanity’s fall into sin and the consigning of certain people, left to
themselves because of sin, to eternal death and punishments. Or it is God’s
counsel by which, from his entirely free will, he determined from eternity to
pass over the greatest part of humanity and justly to leave them in that
eternal destruction into which they would fall through their own sin, for the
glorious manifestation of his justice. Or, finally—and this amounts in
reality to the same thing—it is that eternal counsel of God by which he
freely, wisely, and immutably purposed within himself and decreed with himself
not to choose certain ones out of the whole body of rational creatures—that
is, of angels and people—but to pass them over and not to take them up into
eternal grace and glory; and at last, when they had not continued in their
integrity, to condemn them most justly because of their sins—that is, to
consign them to everlasting death and punishments—in order to display the
glory of his justice in them and in their destruction.

[Marginalia: What it is. Perkins. Trelcatius.]

As you see, this description is broader and more extensive than the preceding
ones with regard to its object—that is, the reprobate themselves—since it
comprehends within its scope not only reprobate people but also the devil
himself with his angels. Their eternal reprobation in the divine counsel is
evident from Matthew 25 alone. There the just Judge will say to reprobate
people: “Depart from me, you accursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the
devil and his angels.” The reprobation of the evil angels is therefore that
eternal decree of God concerning their abandonment and their not being
confirmed in good; the permission of their fall and defection; and their
consequent expulsion from heaven and casting down into eternal destruction,
in order to manifest his power and glorious justice in them. But let us omit
them and speak only of human beings and of God’s counsel insofar as it
properly concerns them, since in this respect it is set forth more clearly and
distinctly for us in Holy Scripture.

[Marginalia: Explanation of the object. Matthew 25:41.]

Just as we taught about election in its proper place above, so here we must
hold that these two acts are reverently to be distinguished and observed in
the decree of reprobation—even though on God’s side it is perfectly simple,
the distinction arises from the manner of our understanding. The first in the
order of nature is the purpose and decree to abandon certain people and to
declare his justice in them. It may be called passing over, or non-enrollment
in the book of life; and the scholastics usually call it passive or negative
reprobation. A final cause can indeed be given for this act, namely the
glorious manifestation of divine justice. But no antecedent or impelling
cause can be given outside God himself and his entirely free will. For it is
wholly absolute and bears no regard to good or evil in the creature, since it
does not descend as far as the means for
executing this decree, which are included in their own way in the latter act,
as will soon be clear. This is what

[Marginalia: Distinction of the two acts. Polanus improperly reverses the
order of these acts. The first.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 128 | De Prædestinatione p. 86]

the Lord and Savior himself teaches us in Matthew 11. He gives no other reason
why the heavenly Father had concealed the mysteries of his kingdom from the
wise and intelligent of this age than that it had seemed good to him. He thus
teaches that here we must contract every sail of pride and submit in this
respect to God’s entirely free good pleasure. Since his will is the cause of
causes, we must seek nothing above it and nothing beyond it.

[Marginalia: Matthew 11:26.]

The Apostle plainly teaches the same thing in Romans 9 by the example of a
potter making different vessels from the same lump of clay. It is therefore
evident that God also makes vessels for wrath and does not merely find them
already made. But if we say that in his eternal counsel he regarded and
rejected, or passed over, people only *as* sinners and not absolutely *as*
human beings, then it will surely follow that he did not so much make vessels
for wrath and dishonor as find them already made by themselves or by the
devil, and merely left them as such—contrary to the Apostle’s meaning and
judgment.

Yet the source and root of every human contradiction on this point is the
pride of our disposition. Through it we imagine ourselves to be something,
even when compared with that infinite Majesty, and consequently suppose that
people must not be abandoned or passed over for the purpose of God’s
gloriously manifesting his freedom, justice, and holiness, as though too
precious a commodity would thereby be lost. Yet we utterly foolish little
people—how much greater a right do we claim over creatures belonging to
another, that is, to our God? We make nothing of their killing, butchering,
and every kind of slaughter, and do not think it should be imputed to us as a
fault when we arrange a procession, festival, or feast, receive friends
lavishly, or on any similar occasion display, if God pleases, our wealth,
generosity, or magnificence. And shall we complain that God created some to
declare his own glory in them not only mercifully but also justly? What if he
had ordained not only this one entire world but many worlds of this sort to a
just destruction? Why could he not justly, rightly, and wisely have done with
his own what seemed good to him?

[Marginalia: Confirmation. Amplification from the unequal comparison.]

Therefore, just as there is no need for us to seek another cause why he made
only one world and not several, so no reason besides his will should or can be
assigned by us for why he passed over those people and chose these. It must be
firmly established that God does not will something because it is already
just and holy in itself; rather, it is just and holy because God has willed
it, since his will is not only the supreme cause of things but also the
supreme rule of all holiness and justice.

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

Its latter act is ordination to punishment, or the decree to condemn because
of sins. This in turn can be considered by us in two ways, either
comparatively or absolutely. If it is regarded comparatively—that is, if we
compare the reprobate with the elect—then no cause for this can be given other
than God’s will alone: why it seemed good to him to distinguish from one
another those placed in the same condition and in the same condemnation, so
that he deigned to take some into grace but passed over others. Augustine
rightly says, “Let whoever can search the great depth of his judgments to
discover why he delivers this one rather than that one.” And again: “Why,
then, is it so with this person and not so with that one? O human being, who
are you to answer back to God?” Likewise, the reason why, in the same
condition, he ordained this one to a greater punishment and that one to a
lesser serves especially to display the divine freedom, and therefore also
perfection, by which the divine will can distribute those eternal and
supernatural goods as it wills and, like the householder in the parable of
the laborers hired for the vineyard, do as he chooses with his own.

[Marginalia: Act 2. Subdivision. Member 1. Augustine, book 1, *To Simplician*,
question 2.]

If this same ordination is considered absolutely or simply—that is, if regard
is had only for the person himself who is ordained to punishment, without
comparing him with others in the same natural condition—we say that it indeed
occurs by God’s perfectly just will, but not absolutely as the first act did,
with all regard to sin excluded. Instead, it necessarily includes regard for
either original sin alone, as in infants, or for actual sin as well. Just as
people are condemned in actuality—that is, consigned to eternal
punishments—only because of sins, so God certainly did not decree to condemn
them except because of sin.

The reason for the distinction between these two acts is clear. That
rejection or passing over which is the first act, as well as this latter act
when understood comparatively insofar as it concerns this person and not that
one in the same state of nature, comes from the free will of God the Creator,
insofar as he has the supreme right and dominion over all creatures to
determine concerning them as he chooses. He can therefore deny his eternal
goods or confer them on those whom he wills. But this latter act, understood
simply apart from a comparison between one person and another, comes from the
just will of God the Creator—or rather, of the just Judge. Justice regards
each person’s desert and therefore justly repays each one according to
innocence or to sins. The decree to condemn therefore necessarily entails and
presupposes regard for sins.

[Marginalia: Member 2. Confirmation.]

Yet this distinction must be observed: although sin is the proper and
proximate cause of the condemnation and punishment of the reprobate, it
cannot properly be called the cause of reprobation itself or of either act of
its decree. Sin is the reason why he condemns, but not the reason why he
decreed to condemn. We nevertheless confess that, according to the order of
nature, sin precedes this act of the decree to condemn in the divine
foreknowledge. Those whom God passed over he decreed both to create and to
permit to fall into sin, and then determined to condemn them because of sin.
What, then, is the cause of this act? If we regard this decree to condemn
simply and apart from comparison with others, let us surely say that it is
divine justice. This can be gathered from the contrary. God mercifully, and
from mere and supreme mercy, decreed to deliver some from sin. Conversely, he
decreed justly to punish and condemn some because of that sin—that is, from
his supreme and holy justice, which appointed eternal punishment and
condemnation as sin’s fair and equal wage.

[Marginalia: The distinction to be observed. Note the true cause.]

From this, incidentally, a difference between election and reprobation is
clear. Election is the true and proper cause of salvation and life, so that
it can rightly be said that we are saved because of the decree of election.
But the decree of reprobation cannot properly or fittingly be called the cause
of condemnation and death, nor can people be said to be condemned because of
reprobation. For, as we will shortly say, reprobation is not the cause of sin,
which is properly the cause of death and condemnation. People do not perish
because they have been reprobated by God,

[Marginalia: Corollary.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 129 | De Prædestinatione p. 87]

even though he rightly destined them to destruction, but because of their own
sins, for which God devoted them to destruction—that is, decreed to condemn
them. In his infinite wisdom God arranged this whole counsel concerning
humanity’s eternal state within himself so that, just as he willed no one to
be saved except by his mere grace and mercy, so that no flesh might boast in
his sight, he likewise willed no one to perish except by his own fault, so
that no flesh might complain before his tribunal. He willed every cause of
life and salvation to reside in himself. He willed all blame for eternal
death and condemnation to rest with the sinner himself, so that although he
determined freely concerning him, he determined nothing except justly.

[Marginalia: Note the reason for this difference.]

Thus we have examined both the efficient cause of reprobation—both principal
and antecedent—and its form, which consists in those two acts. As we noted
above concerning election, we recognize no matter here
in the proper sense, but only the subject about which it is concerned, or the
subject of its occupation—that is, the reprobate themselves, about whom we
will speak below in the proper place.

[Marginalia: Transition.]

There remains, then, one final cause of reprobation to be considered, which we
touched upon above in the definition. This is the supreme end which we said
was the display of divine glory. Reprobation serves this end in three ways.
First, it reveals God’s free power and supreme right to determine concerning
his creatures according to his will. Thus he says that he “raised up Pharaoh
so that he might show his power in him and make his name known throughout the
whole earth.” Second, it demonstrates his justice, the supreme holiness of his
nature, and his hatred and rejection of sin. Therefore, he says that, “willing
to show his wrath, he endured the reprobate with great patience,” that is, in
order to show his justice and judgment in punishing their sins. Third, when
regarded comparatively, it commends the glory of his mercy toward the elect.

Hence in Psalm 147, after saying that God reveals his words to Jacob and his
statutes and judgments to Israel, the prophet immediately adds, to commend
this grace and mercy of God, “He has not dealt in this way with the other
nations, and therefore they have not known those judgments.” He then
concludes, “Hallelujah,” intimating that from this a most abundant subject
arises for the elect to celebrate God’s mercy toward them, even from the
passing over and rejection of others from grace. In passing over others God
shows what he could justly have done to both groups, and therefore how great
is that benefit which he freely confers upon the elect. Hence Luther, looking
to this very thing, said that the highest degree of faith is to believe and
acknowledge that he who saves so few and condemns so many is merciful.

[Marginalia: On the twofold final cause. The first. 1. 2. 3. Psalm 147:18 and
19. Luther.]

Another end is subordinated to this supreme end, the manifestation of divine
glory: namely, the salvation of the elect themselves. Reprobation serves to
advance this salvation in many ways, as Augustine teaches in his book *On
Predestination and Grace*: “God willed the destruction of those who will
perish to be a proof of salvation to the vessels of mercy predestined by him,
and used the destruction of some for the salvation of others, because the
potter has power over the clay to make from the same lump one vessel for honor
and another for dishonor.”

[Marginalia: The subordinate end. Augustine, *On Predestination and Grace*,
chapter 6, near the end.]

First, when the elect behold God’s justice toward the reprobate, they are
stirred to fear and reverence him. They are not only restrained but driven
away from the contagion and fellowship of sin; they cling through Christ all
the more closely and firmly to God and persist all the more attentively and
constantly in his ways. Hence the psalmist says in Psalm 119, “You have
consumed all the ungodly of the earth like dross, or reduced them to nothing;
therefore I love your testimonies. My flesh shudders with fear of you; so
greatly do I fear your judgments.” Indeed, Paul himself, the holy teacher and
Apostle of the Gentiles, says in 2 Corinthians 5, “Knowing that terror of the
Lord, we lead people to faith.”

Augustine also teaches this very neatly in his book of *Questions to
Simplician*: “For what benefit does it bring to vessels fitted for
destruction”—that is, joined together for destruction—“that God patiently
endures them in order to destroy them in due order and uses them as an
instrument for the salvation of others on whom he has mercy? Surely it
benefits those for whose salvation he uses them in this way, so that, as it is
written”—namely in Psalm 58:11—“the righteous may wash his hands in the blood
of the sinner,” that is, may be cleansed from evil works through the fear of
God when he sees the punishment of the ungodly. Although Scripture says that
he will wash his feet, not his hands, it amounts to the same thing, since we
must be cleansed not only from evil works but also from evil affections. To
this end it is profitable to behold and consider the just punishments of the
ungodly. This, of course, is an allegorical exposition of the saying; we are
not unaware that its real sense is different.

[Marginalia: 1. Psalm 119:119 and 120. 2 Corinthians 5:11. Augustine, tome 4,
book 1, *To Simplician*, question 2.]

Second, by earnestly meditating on the contrary reprobation of others, the
elect and faithful perceive more powerfully the magnitude of God’s grace
declared toward themselves, when they consider that by nature they are no
better or worthier than the reprobate. Thus the grace which they see denied to
others both shines more brightly before them and tastes sweeter to their
hearts. So too, in earthly and perishable affairs, we do not value as highly
those goods which are granted equally and commonly to everyone as those which
are bestowed upon only a few and therefore bear the mark and testimony of a
particular favor and grace.

[Marginalia: 2. Analogy.]

Finally, from this we are both kindled with a vehement and sincere love of our
God and kept within modesty, submission of soul, and humility by the
recognition of his immense mercy and of our own vileness and unworthiness. By
nature we were equal to the reprobate themselves. Although our God could by
his perfectly just judgment have left us with them in the same lot and
eternal death, he preferred that we should be vessels of election and willed
us to belong to the number of those in whom he had decreed from eternity to
reveal the glory not of his justice but of his mercy and grace. Therefore,
whenever the thought occurs to us that our most merciful God has exempted us
from the lot and number of the reprobate and made us fit to share the
inheritance of the saints in light, we cannot but be continually impelled and
stirred to thank him with all our strength and to declare our soul’s gratitude
in every way.

[Marginalia: 3.]

From this it is evident that this doctrine of reprobation can be a scandal or
stumbling block only to those who as yet possess no certainty or full
persuasion concerning their own salvation. But it is no wonder if neither
election itself, nor the gospel, nor any other part of saving doctrine—much
less reprobation—brings any joy or comfort to them. Therefore, as I admonished
you so strongly above, in order for us to receive any fruit and consolation
from this doctrine, we must strive before all things to possess firm and valid
earnests of our own election. Then, beyond all doubt, everything Holy
Scripture teaches us about the eternal reprobation of others will serve our
remarkable use and comfort. All who desire to banish this truth from God’s
Church or suppress it in silence deprive and despoil the Church of that
comfort, so far as it lies within their power;

[Marginalia: Conclusion concerning the final cause.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 130 | De Prædestinatione p. 88 (through Lecture XXII close)]

for the elect perceive that not only God’s glory—which is dearest of all
things to them and of the greatest weight and value—but also their own good
and sweetest joy arise clearly even from this part of the divine decree. To
conclude this passage in the Apostle’s words, we see him aptly join both ends
in Romans 9, when he says that “God endured with great patience vessels of
wrath fitted together for destruction, willing to show his wrath and make his
power known, and in order that he might make known the riches of his glory
toward the vessels of mercy which he prepared for glory.” Let this suffice
concerning the final cause of reprobation.
