QUESTION V.

CHRIST’S THREEFOLD OFFICE

*Why did Christ the mediator have to sustain a threefold office?*

[Marginal heading: What is Christ’s mediatorial office?]

I. Christ’s office is nothing other than mediation between God and human
beings, for whose accomplishment he was sent by the Father into the world and
anointed by the Holy Spirit. It embraces everything which, by virtue of his
mission and calling, Christ had to do toward the offended God and offending
human beings, in order to reconcile them and unite them with each other again.

[Marginal heading: And through how many stages was it instituted?]

[Marginal heading: 1. Predestination;]

II. The institution of this office is completed especially in three stages:
1. Christ’s predestination; 2. his mission; and his calling. The first stage is
the eternal predestination of the mediator, by which God chose and appointed
his Son as mediator

[Printed p. 345 | Internet Archive PDF 373 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to reconcile the elect to himself and execute the work of salvation. To this
pertain 1 Peter 1:20, Acts 2:23, and Revelation 13:8.

[Marginal heading: 2. Mission;]

The second stage is his mission. According to the eternal decree of
predestination, he was not only promised under the Old Testament, foretold by
various oracles, foreshadowed by types, and presented as the church’s mediator;
but in the fullness of time he was also sent into the world to assume our
nature, so that in it he could accomplish the work entrusted to him. This
mission is mentioned very frequently in the gospel. It did not occur through
a change of place, as though he came where he had not been before, but through
an extraordinary manifestation of his presence in the flesh, in which he
visibly appeared and worked.

[Marginal heading: 3. Calling;]

III. The third stage consists in his calling, by which he was consecrated to
discharge that office. Isaiah 42:6 treats this: “I, Jehovah, have called you in
righteousness,” and so forth; as do Psalm 110:4 and Christ himself in John
10:36. This calling embraces two parts, anointing and inauguration.

[Marginal heading: Which consists of anointing;]

Anointing, from which the names *Messiah* and *Christ* arose, belonged
preeminently (κατ’ ἐξοχήν) to our redeemer, although others too are improperly
called anointed ones and christs by reason of the office of prophet, priest,
or king. It denotes two things: 1. Consecration to the office, which belongs to
Christ’s person even according to the divine nature, through which he was
actually consecrated to the office of mediator, Psalm 2:6. Scripture customarily
designates this by the name *sanctification,* John 10:36. 2. The conferral of
all graces and gifts necessary for the execution of that office, which pertains
only to the human nature. Through it he is said to have been anointed with the
oil of gladness above his companions and endowed with the Spirit without
measure (ἀμέτρως), Psalm 45:7, Isaiah 61:1–2, and John 3:34.

[Marginal heading: And inauguration.]

IV. Inauguration is that by which, although he was actually Lord and heir of
all things by right of birth and the benefit of the hypostatic union, he
nevertheless willed to be solemnly inaugurated into his office, just as even
hereditary kings are customarily installed in full possession of the kingdom
by a solemn inauguration so that all may acknowledge them. First, this occurred
at Christ’s baptism, Matthew 3:17, where in an illustrious and altogether
extraordinary manner he was consecrated to the public exercise of his office.
This was confirmed not only by the opening of the heavens and the mission of
the Holy Spirit upon him, but also by the heavenly voice, which testified to
the excellence of his person and office. It testified to his person when he
was called the Son of God—not adoptive, of whom God has many, but natural,
only-begotten, and proper—and therefore called beloved (ἀγαπητός) and most
dear. It testified to his office when it is said that the Father is well
pleased (εὐδοκεῖται) in him. That is, not only does the Father wholly rest and
delight in Christ, but in him and because of him we also please God and are
accepted, Ephesians 1:6. Moreover, the excellence of this office is clearly
demonstrated by the fact that he was constituted our mediator not without an
oath, Psalm 110:4: “The Lord has sworn,” and so forth. From this Paul infers
the superiority and eternity of Christ’s priesthood over the Levitical in
Hebrews 7. Second, at the transfiguration he was likewise confirmed in his
threefold office by the heavenly voice, Matthew 17. Third, this inauguration
occurred fully after his resurrection and exaltation, when “he was made Lord
and Christ,” Acts 2:36, and “the name above every name was given to him, that
at the name of Jesus every knee should bow,” and so forth, Philippians 2:9–10;
when all power in heaven and earth was given to him, Matthew 28; and when he
sat at the right hand of the Majesty on high, Hebrews 1:3.

[Marginal heading: The rationale of Christ’s threefold office.]

V. This mediatorial office of Christ is distributed into three offices, which
are its three corresponding parts: prophecy, priesthood, and kingdom. Christ
sustained these not separately but conjointly, something given to him alone.
For what was divided in others because of

[Printed p. 346 | Internet Archive PDF 374 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

weakness is united in Christ because of his supreme perfection. So great was
the dignity and burden of these offices that no mortal could discharge them
alone. There could indeed be kings and priests, such as Melchizedek; kings and
prophets, such as David; and priests and prophets, as the high priests
sometimes were. But no one ever appeared who alone perfectly fulfilled all
three. This was reserved for Christ alone, who could sustain the reality
foreshadowed by those various types. If Moses is read as having foreshadowed
him in some manner, because he was not only God’s prophet and the people’s
leader and liberator but also sometimes performed the priesthood before Aaron
was inaugurated, as in Exodus 24, this was outside the order and done only as
a single act; it cannot and ought not to be drawn into a precedent.

[Marginal heading: It is founded 1. In Scripture;]

VI. Various reasons can be assigned for this distribution: 1. Scripture,
which customarily points out Christ by this threefold name. At one time it
presents him as prophet, Deuteronomy 18 and Isaiah 61:1–2, as the Father’s
wisdom and the Word (Λόγον) through whom God speaks to us, Proverbs 8 and John
1:1. At another it presents him as priest, Psalm 110:4 and repeatedly in the
epistle to the Hebrews; and at another as king, Psalms 2 and 110:1, Isaiah 9,
and Luke 1. Nor are these three offices only described separately; they are
also presented conjointly. Thus in Isaiah 9:6 the one called a child by reason
of his human conception and a son by reason of his eternal generation is
called *Wonderful* by reason of his person, *Counselor* by reason of prophecy,
*Father of Eternity* by reason of priesthood, and *Prince of Peace,* upon whose
shoulders dominion is placed, by reason of kingship. Likewise in chapter 61 a
threefold end of Christ’s anointing is noted: “To evangelize the poor,” which
belongs to the prophet; “to heal the brokenhearted and console those who
mourn,” which belongs to the priest; and “to bring liberty to the captives and
execute the Lord’s vengeance,” which belongs to the king. Throughout Psalm
110 Christ’s royal dignity and his victory and triumph over his enemies are
asserted. Prophecy is established in verse 2, when the scepter of the word is
said to go forth from Zion, and the priesthood is sanctioned in verse 4: “The
Lord has sworn and will not repent, You are a priest forever,” and so forth.
In Zechariah 6:12–14, the man who is the Branch and is said to build the
Lord’s temple through the word of the gospel—which belongs to the prophet—is
said to rule on his throne, which belongs to the king. The priest who will sit
on his throne, the very one who will be king, will also be priest, and “the
counsel of peace will be between these two,” that is, between those two royal
and priestly dignities. For one and the same will be king and priest, who will
acquire the church’s peace through the priesthood and defend it through the
kingdom.

VII. Thus in the New Testament these three offices are often joined, as in
John 14:6, when Christ is called the way, the truth, and the life: the way
leading, the truth teaching, and the life saving. He is the way in the
priesthood, when through his blood he made our way into heaven, Hebrews 10:20;
the truth in prophecy, because he reveals to us the word of the gospel, which
alone is saving truth; and the life in the kingdom, through which by his
efficacy he vivifies and protects us. He is the way in death, the truth in the
word, and the life in the Spirit. Hence Emissenus says on this passage: “I am
the way, because no one comes to the Father except through me. I am the truth,
because no one knows the Father except from me. I am the life, because no one
lives except in me.” The same is shown in the celebrated passage 1 Corinthians
1:30, where Christ is said to have been made for us by the Father wisdom,
righteousness, sanctification, and redemption. He is said to have been *made,*
that is, constituted by God, to be wisdom for us—that is, the author and
infallible teacher of heavenly wisdom through prophecy; righteousness—that is,
the giver of eternal and infinite righteousness, through which we are freed
from guilt and justified before God through the priesthood; sanctification—that
is, the cause of true and spiritual holiness, through which we are cleansed
from the dominion and defilements of sin through the kingdom; and redemption,
which exerts itself in all three. Or, if redemption is taken strictly, it is
redemption from

misery and death. Thus in Hebrews 1:2–3 God is said to have spoken to us in
the Son as the supreme prophet, who, after he had made purification for our
sins as priest, sat at the right hand of the throne of Majesty on high as king.

[Marginal heading: 2. In humanity’s threefold misery;]

VIII. Second, the threefold misery brought upon humanity by sin required this
conjunction of the threefold office: ignorance, guilt, and the tyranny and
servitude of sin. Ignorance is healed through prophecy, guilt through the
priesthood, and the tyranny and corruption of sin through the kingdom. The
prophetic light dispels the darkness of error; the priest’s merit takes away
guilt and procures reconciliation for us; the king’s power removes the
servitude of sin and death. The prophet shows God to us, the priest brings us
to God, and the king unites and glorifies us with God. By the Spirit of
illumination the prophet enlightens the intellect; by the Spirit of consolation
the priest calms the heart and conscience; by the Spirit of sanctification the
king subdues rebellious affections.

[Marginal heading: 3. In the nature of the salvation to be conferred;]

IX. Third, the nature of the salvation to be conferred upon us required the
same. Three things were altogether required for it: announcement, acquisition,
and application. It had to be revealed to us, to whom it is unknown by nature;
it had to be acquired for us; and once acquired, it had to be applied. Thus by
his threefold office Christ had to perform these three things: announce
salvation to us through the prophet’s doctrine, acquire the promised salvation
through the priest’s merit, and apply the acquired salvation through the
efficacy of the royal Spirit.

[Marginal heading: 4. In the mediator’s acts;]

X. Fourth, the mediator’s acts could not be accomplished otherwise. For two
things were necessary: that he act on our side before God and on God’s side
toward us—the things toward God (τὰ πρὸς τὸν θεὸν) and the things toward us
(τὰ πρὸς ἡμᾶς). He acted for us toward God by satisfying and interceding,
which he did as priest: “For every priest is appointed in the things which
pertain to God, offering gifts and sacrifices,” Hebrews 5:1. He acted for God
toward us by speaking and working: first, through the announcement of God’s
grace and our duty, which he did as prophet; and afterward, through powerful
redemption from all our enemies and the efficacious procurement of full
salvation, which he did as king.

[Marginal heading: 5. In the fulfillment of the types;]

XI. Fifth, the symbolic anointing of the Old Testament. Three kinds of human
beings under the Old Testament had to be consecrated by sacred anointing,
namely prophets, priests, and kings, as was seen in Elisha, 1 Kings 19:15, in
Aaron, Exodus 29, and repeatedly in the kings. Now since all the types looked
to the one Christ and received their fulfillment in him, Christ had to exhibit
in himself the reality of this threefold office, but far more perfectly than
in others. This was not only by reason of the conjunction of these three parts,
which no human being could fulfill simultaneously, as said above, but also by
reason of the preeminence and dignity both of the office and of the gifts.

[Marginal heading: 6. In God’s three attributes.]

XII. Sixth, this was also not obscurely intimated by the three principal
attributes of God which exercised themselves toward us in the work of
redemption: wisdom, mercy, and power. For the Son as prophet reveals God’s
wisdom to us, as priest merits his mercy for us, and as our king brings forth
power for our liberation and salvation. From this arises the object of the
three cardinal virtues, faith, hope, and love. Faith embraces the prophet’s
doctrine, hope rests upon the priest’s merit, and love subjects itself through
voluntary obedience to the king’s scepter.

XIII. These three offices are so joined in Christ that he not only exercises
their distinct operations, but the same action also proceeds from all three
at once, which considerably increases the wonder of the matter. Thus Christ’s
cross, which is

the priest’s altar upon which he offered himself as a victim to God, is also
the prophet’s school in which he teaches us the mystery of salvation, whence
the gospel is called the word of the cross; and the king’s trophy, upon which
he triumphed over principalities and powers, Colossians 2:15. The gospel is the
prophet’s law, Isaiah 2:2–3; the king’s scepter, Psalm 110:2; the priest’s
sword, which penetrates to the inmost divisions of the heart, Hebrews 4:12;
and the altar upon which the sacrifice of our faith must be placed. Thus the
Spirit, as the Spirit of wisdom, is the effect of prophecy, as the Spirit
of consolation is the fruit of the priesthood, and as the Spirit of strength
and glory is the king’s gift.
