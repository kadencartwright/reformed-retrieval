PROPOSITION TWELVE.

SECTION ONE.

ARM.

Thirdly, concerning non-election or preterition. Non-election is an act of the divine good pleasure
by which God determined from eternity not to communicate supernatural happiness to certain people,
but to give them only natural happiness if they lived agreeably to nature. But in an act of this
kind God does not deal with people considered as in their purely natural condition. 1. Because
there is no natural happiness of this kind which is man's end and ultimate goal, either actually or
potentially. 2. Because sin is the meritorious cause of the act of the divine good pleasure by
which God determines to deny supernatural blessedness to someone.

JUN.

The definition of non-election or preterition is by no means correct, for three reasons. 1. Because
the differentia which is given inheres only as an accident. 2. Because the thing defined is not
related to the earlier end—namely adoption—but to the later one, namely happiness. 3. Because the
definition is redundant: something positive is added, although the thing defined is purely
negative.

ARM.

I did not labor to conform the definition of non-election to the rule of art. If the things which
you think should be substituted are substituted, my proof will remain firm, and that correction was
not necessary. I am treating predestination to glory, not predestination to grace. I therefore had
to refrain from those things which are common to grace and glory, among which is adoption as sons.

2. I remember no mention of an end in my definition of non-election. For supernatural happiness is
not the end of election but its matter. In your theses you include that matter under the name of
blessing and divide it into grace and glory.

3. To the third point: that phrase denotes not an affirmative but a negative act, and the emphasis
is on the sign of exclusion, “only.”

RESPONSE I.

Let us consider Arminius's argument. The decree to communicate supernatural happiness does not deal
with people considered as in their purely natural condition. 1. Because there is no natural
happiness of this kind which is man's end. But this reasoning is founded solely upon that part of
the description which he enclosed in parentheses. It contributes absolutely nothing to the
definition of preterition—namely, the part about giving natural happiness—which Arminius afterward
professes does not pertain to preterition at all, except by reason of the exclusive particle
“only.” Thus it falls back upon the decree to deny supernatural happiness. Meanwhile, it by no
means pleases me to say that God deals with people considered in their purely natural condition,
whether by not destining supernatural happiness for them or by destining natural happiness for
them, although Arminius appears likely to affirm the latter. For the destination of natural
happiness does not presuppose the decree to create. Otherwise creation would be held to be prior to
natural happiness in God's intention, from which it would follow both that creation has the
character of an end and that it must occupy the later place when execution is reached. But people
are not adorned with natural happiness for the end that they might be created, nor can anyone
attain any happiness before he is created. It can still be said in two senses that in passing over
people God deals with them as considered in their purely natural condition. First, if the purely
natural condition were considered not actually but potentially. That is, when a person is passed
over, he is considered by God according to all the natural things required for his nature, but only
as possible, not as actually existing. 2. Secondly, even with respect to actual existence—not as
though actual existence or the consideration of it preceded preterition, but because that will by
which the immutable God passes people over remains until the person exists in his purely natural
condition and thence to eternity. And while he exists in that purely natural condition, he is
considered by God, and God's will by which he wills eternally to deny him supernatural happiness
still remains. 3. Let us examine the other reason. It is this: “Because sin is the meritorious cause
of the act of the divine good pleasure by which God determines to deny someone spiritual or
supernatural blessedness. Isaiah 59: Your iniquities separate between you and your God. That denial
of happiness made to a person can only be considered as punishment, which is necessarily preceded
by actual sin, while the destination of that punishment is preceded by foresight of future sin.” I
congratulate Arminius upon so open and candid a profession of his opinion, since he plainly admits
that sin is the meritorious cause of the divine act by which God determines—that is, the meritorious
cause of the divine decree or will. Until now he has refrained from so open a profession. For in his
reply to Proposition 9, Section 1, he had said that the question is not: “Whether in this decree God
dealt with people in the contemplation of sin as a cause—a cause, I say, why he chose these and
passed over those. For Augustine also admits this, although he presupposes sin to that decree as a
condition required in the object.” “But this,” he says, “is the question: Is sin a condition
required in the object about which God will be engaged in electing and passing over, or not?” And
shortly afterward: “Augustine excludes sin as a cause why God elects or reprobates, but includes it
as a cause why he can pass over and reprobate, or as a condition required in the object of election
and reprobation.” By comparing these passages with the one now before us, should we infer that by a
meritorious cause Arminius understands a cause, not why God passes over or reprobates, but why he is
able to reprobate or pass over? Yet in the present passage he openly professes it to be the cause of
the divine act by which God determines, and so forth; he does not call it the cause why God is able
so to determine. Let us leave that contradiction. We utterly deny that sin can be a cause of the
divine decree to deny supernatural happiness to someone. For sin is something temporal, whereas
every decree of God is eternal. But the temporal cannot be the cause of the eternal. At this point
they customarily have recourse to the consideration of sin. But far be it from us to judge the
consideration of sin a meritorious cause, for consideration is the most holy action of the Creator,
not an action of the sinful creature. They will therefore retreat to sin as considered. But if
neither sin nor God's consideration of sin can be called a meritorious cause, how will sin
considered by God be able to be called a meritorious cause? Yet grant, if you please, that
`φάρμακον`; to blunt the edge of that argument, behold another. If sin is a meritorious cause, and
so forth, then the decree to permit sin must be prior to the decree

to deny supernatural happiness. Therefore, the permission of sin was prior in God's intention to
the denial of supernatural happiness. Therefore, I say, it will have the character of an end and
will have to come later in execution. But the denial of supernatural happiness does not occur for
the end that sin might be permitted, nor can supernatural happiness, according to Arminius, be
denied before sin is permitted, since on his account that denial has the character of punishment.

But let us examine Arminius's arguments to the contrary. The passage in Isaiah 59 proves, I admit,
that iniquities are the cause of separation between us and God; it does not prove that they are the
cause of the decree or will to separate. And I admit that sins precede separation, but they cannot
have preceded the decree to separate. Grant that the denial of happiness is considered as
punishment. From this, I admit, it follows that this punishment has a preceding transgression, but
it does not follow that the destination of this punishment presupposes the foresight of future
transgression. Arminius is accustomed to dictate that consequence and assume it as valid, but so far
he has never attempted to prove it. We, on the contrary, demonstrate its manifest invalidity. For
just as the denial of happiness is considered as punishment for sin, so the granting of happiness
is considered as the reward of good works. Hence it follows that, as the former presupposes sin,
the latter presupposes good works. But from this it is in no way concluded that the destination of
happiness presupposes the foresight of good works, lest we admit—even against the Apostle's
will—that election is from works.

2. I turn to Junius. First, his response by no means pleases me. It is not required that the
proposition have the character of a definition. And even if it had been proposed as a definition,
Junius's exception rests upon a peculiar subtlety of his own, in which he has found neither a guide
nor, thus far, a follower. That subtlety is neither necessary nor at all true, as has been said
sufficiently above. His second exception is of the same character throughout. But the third
exception is just, and I have indeed been unable to comprehend any reason why Arminius referred the
giving of natural happiness to preterition when he says that preterition supposes, in addition,
that God does not give natural happiness to those to whom it has already been given. I do not care
to inquire what he means, lest by understanding it we attain the result that we understand nothing.

3. I proceed to Arminius. I pass over the first two members of his response as sufficiently sound
and come to the third. “It is evident,” he says, “that the things inserted in parentheses were added
for the sake of explanation, and do not enter the definition in the same order and manner as the
other things.” But your first reason is founded solely upon that insertion. “They denote,” he says,
“not an affirmative but a negative act, and the emphasis is on the sign of exclusion, ‘only.’” On
the contrary, I say, they denote both. The exclusive particle indeed denotes the negative act, but
the whole preceding sentence also denotes an affirmative act. Moreover, that negative act had
already been expressly proposed in the preceding words, “by which God determined not to communicate
supernatural happiness to certain people.” Whichever way you turn, therefore, you are constrained
here, and the things you append are of no value in excusing those words, which certainly introduce
a marvelous confusion into the preceding proposition. For although natural happiness would have
been given, according to your and Junius's opinion, if a person lived obediently, it by no means
follows from this that it would have been given by preterition, as your definition of preterition or
non-election declares. It is therefore false that this can be excused by the example of Junius's
theses. Rather, they reveal a harlot's brow while you prefer to excuse an error instead of confessing
it candidly. In Junius's definition of preterition there is nothing that offers even a semblance of
your absurdity of which you have been convicted. In adapting the definition to the preterition by
which glory is denied, a certain natural happiness to which supernatural happiness is denied is
introduced absurdly and without foundation. These words tend only to keep him from blushing,
although he multiplies other things for which he should blush more.
