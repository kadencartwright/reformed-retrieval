### Objections

**Objection 1.** From Hebrews 11:6. When people first draw near to God, in the
first movement by which they begin to approach him, they believe that he
exists. Faith is therefore only the beginning of justification, and
consequently we are not justified by faith alone.

**Response.** 1. I deny the inference. Although the first movement of drawing
near is attributed to faith, that does not remove faith’s subsequent movements
and acts, since the beginning, progress, and completion of justification are
by faith alone—that is, apart from faith there is nothing in us which
contributes to faith’s completing the work. The case is similar to arguing,
“The beginning of good is from God; therefore its progress and end are not.”

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 106 | image 15575078 | printed p. 104]

2. In the same chapter the apostle teaches the remaining acts of faith by
which justification is completed, as may be seen in all the examples.

**Objection 2.** From Romans 10:14–15. The apostle teaches that salvation—that
is, justification, which is the soul’s healing from the disease of sin—begins
with faith, which is followed in its proper order by invocation and so forth.
Faith alone therefore does not justify.

**Response.** 1. It is a false interpretation to make salvation identical
with justification.

2. The inference does not follow: salvation begins with faith and faith is
followed by the same, therefore faith is only the beginning of justification;
or invocation, together with faith, prepares a person for justification.
Invocation indeed follows faith as an effect follows its cause. It does not
therefore follow that whatever faith does, love does also.

3. Nor does this follow: whoever calls upon God will be saved, therefore
invocation is a cause of salvation in us. Rather, this is reasoning from an
effect of a necessary cause. Finally, when we are said to be justified by faith
alone, the existence of other virtues in us is not excluded; what is taken
from them is the power to cause justification by their own merit or efficacy.

**Objection 3.** John 1:12. That which grants the ability to become children of
God, but does not actually constitute people as God’s children, justifies only
as the beginning of justification, and by it alone we are not justified. But
those who receive Christ through faith are not yet God’s children; they can
become such if they proceed further to love, hope, and so forth. Therefore the
conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. I deny the minor premise, because it plainly contradicts Holy
Scripture in 1 John 5:1.

2. The passage is falsely interpreted. Those who believe are said to have
*exousia* to become God’s children—that is, authority, dignity, and power—and
to have it in actuality.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 107 | image 15575079 | printed p. 105]

Nor does that word signify mere potential, as Bellarmine wishes. The same
verse also teaches that by this faith they received Christ. But those who
received Christ are God’s children not merely potentially but actually, and
they are actually justified.

3. The following does not follow: although faith is the beginning of
justification and grants us the ability to be God’s children if we proceed
further, our being God’s children therefore comes from love and the other
virtues. For as the beginning of our adoption is from God, so also are its
progress and completion.

**Objection 4.** From 1 John 2:29: “Everyone who does righteousness has been
born of God.” Love therefore properly makes people God’s children.

**Response.** This commits the fallacy of treating what is not a cause as a
cause, for the passage does not deal with the efficient cause of spiritual
birth, but with the signs by which we understand that we are God’s children or
belong to life. Nothing follows from the passage except that by love we can
know that we are God’s children. It is one thing for works to show who God’s
children are, and another for those works to be the cause why they are God’s
children. The case is similar to arguing, “By its light and heat we know that
the sun is shining; therefore these things are the cause of the sun, or the
cause why it shines.” The inference does not follow.

**Objection 5.** From natural reason. Someone can believe something for which
he neither hopes nor has love, but he cannot hope for or love what he does not
believe. Faith is therefore the foundation of hope and love, and not the
reverse.

**Response.** 1. This is the fallacy of an irrelevant conclusion, because it
does not conclude what is denied. It is indeed true that faith is naturally
prior to hope and love, and is even their cause.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 108 | image 15575080 | printed p. 106]

It does not therefore follow that faith alone does not justify.

2. The antecedent is false, for we hope for nothing which we do not believe,
Hebrews 11:1, and we also love the things which we believe.

**Objection 6.** From a comparison. In bodily diseases the beginning of health
is believing oneself to be sick and trusting a physician who is willing to
cure, yet that faith is not complete health. Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. In bodily diseases that faith is not a part of health, but
only a producing cause. So too, in healing from sin—that is, in
justification—faith is not a part of health, but a producing cause and the
instrument by which we apprehend Christ.

2. Justifying faith is badly defined as believing oneself to be sick and
trusting someone willing to cure. Its chief office in justification is to act
as the instrument which apprehends and applies the medicine.

**Objection 7.** From Proverbs 1:7: “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of
wisdom,” where wisdom signifies perfect justification. Fear is therefore no
less the beginning of justification than faith is.

**Response.** 1. I deny the minor premise. Wisdom is falsely interpreted as
justification, because wisdom is a quality in the human mind, whereas
justification is an act of God himself, by which he makes and pronounces a
person righteous.

2. Spiritual wisdom is distinguished from justification in 1 Corinthians
1:30. Finally, one thing has only one beginning. Therefore, if faith is the
beginning of justification, fear is not.

**Objection 8.** From Psalms 78:34 and 83:17 and Jonah 3. Faith justifies
because by faith we seek God and draw near to him. Fear does the same.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 109 | image 15575081 | printed p. 107]

**Response.** 1. I distinguish the major premise. If seeking and drawing near
to God is understood as apprehending and applying Christ’s merit by faith as
an instrument, then in this sense the major premise is true and the minor is
false. But if seeking and drawing near is understood as something in us by
which we either prepare ourselves to draw near to God and seek him, or by
whose efficacy we reconcile an offended God and obtain his favor, then in this
sense both the major and the minor premises are false.

2. We seek God by invocation arising from faith, and we draw near to God with
filial fear. It does not therefore follow that invocation or fear prepares us
for justification. For we draw near to God in one way by faith and in another
by invocation and fear: by faith, in apprehending and applying Christ’s merit;
by invocation, in imploring his benefits; and by filial fear, in humbling
ourselves. The cited passages speak of drawing near to God by invocation and
fear. Finally, invocation and filial fear come after justification and are its
effects.

**Objection 9.** Faith justifies because through it Christ is formed in us,
Galatians 4:19. Fear does the same, Isaiah 26:28: “From fear of you we conceived
and brought forth the spirit of salvation.”

**Response.** 1. The passage in Galatians does not speak about faith, but about
the preaching of the gospel, by which Paul wished to form Christ in the
Galatians.

2. The passage of Isaiah is falsely quoted. Its words are, “We were pregnant,
as though we were bringing forth wind,” where there is nothing about fear or
the spirit of salvation. The prophet is speaking about the afflictions of the
church and teaches that she could not deliver herself. This is signified by a
metaphor from childbirth: she was tormented in the manner of those

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 110 | image 15575082 | printed p. 108]

who feel in the womb the pangs of winds struggling against one another.

**Objection 10.** Faith justifies because the righteous person lives by faith.
But Proverbs 14:27 says, “The fear of the Lord is a fountain of life.” Fear
therefore justifies as faith does.

**Response.** 1. Faith does not justify because it is a fountain of life, but
because it is the instrument which apprehends and applies Christ’s merit. Nor
is faith the fountain of life simply because the righteous person lives by
faith. Rather, he lives by faith because through faith he draws life-giving
righteousness from the fountain of grace.

2. Fear is a fountain of life, though not the first fountain in us. That
passage speaks of spiritual actions and the flight from sin which proceed from
filial fear.

**Objection 11.** Faith justifies because it purges sins, Acts 13:9. But
Ecclesiasticus 21:7 says that the fear of the Lord drives out sins. Therefore
the conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. Faith purges not by its own efficacy or merit, but
instrumentally.

2. The passage about fear is apocryphal and therefore proves nothing.

3. Fear is not said to purge or make satisfaction for sin by its own efficacy
or merit, nor instrumentally as faith does by applying Christ’s merit. Rather,
it is said to drive out sin because filial fear causes us to do good and flee
evil. It does not follow that fear therefore justifies.

**Objection 12.** From Proverbs 28:18, Psalms 37:9 and 91:14. Those who hope in
God are preserved; therefore we are justified by hope no less than by faith.

**Response.** 1. I deny the inference, because those passages do not speak of
justification but of deliverance from dangers.

2. This does not follow: those who hope in God are preserved; therefore hope
is either a preparation for justification or

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 111 | image 15575083 | printed p. 109]

in any way a cause of justification, or God confers salvation on account of
hope.

**Objection 13.** From Matthew 9:2. Christ said to the paralytic, “Take heart”;
that is, he raised him up to hope for salvation. And when he saw him raised up
by hope, he then said, “Your sins are forgiven”—that is, he justified him.
Hope is therefore a preparation for justification.

**Response.** 1. “Take heart” is falsely interpreted as referring to the hope
of salvation. It means the same as “Believe firmly,” for Christ exhorts the
man, who was beginning to believe, to believe more fully.

2. This does not follow: after Christ saw him raised up to hope for salvation,
he said, “Your sins are forgiven”; therefore hope was a preparation before
justification. On the contrary, Christ said, “Take heart,” because his sins
were forgiven, reasoning from cause to effect. Thus even if “Take heart” were
interpreted as referring to hope, it does not follow that hope preceded
justification.

3. *Aphiontai* is in the past tense. Therefore his sins had been forgiven
before Christ said, “Take heart,” and consequently he had been justified.

**Objection 14.** From Luke 7:47. That on account of which sins are forgiven is
prior to the forgiveness of sins, and therefore also to justification. But
sins are forgiven on account of love. Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. The major premise is not always true if it is understood as
priority in time, because the sins of the Old Testament fathers had been
forgiven on account of Christ’s satisfaction, although that satisfaction was
later in time.

2. I deny the minor premise. The passage does not say, “Her sins are forgiven
on account of love,” but “because she loved,” reasoning from effect to cause.
The cause on account of which her sins were forgiven is taught in verse 50.

**Further instance.** But *hoti* is a causal particle. Therefore it indicates
that love is the cause of

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 112 | image 15575084 | printed p. 110]

forgiveness.

**Response.** It is not always causal, but can give a reason and denote an
argument from effect to cause no less than from cause to effect. “The sun has
risen, because it is day; therefore the day is the cause of the sun’s rising”
commits the fallacy of affirming the consequent. Not everything which is the
cause of a consequence is the cause of its consequent. It does not therefore
follow, “Her sins are forgiven because she loved; therefore love is the cause
of forgiveness,” any more than this follows: “You are alive because you walk;
therefore walking is the cause of life.”

**Objection 15.** From Galatians 5:6. Faith made effective through love
justifies. Therefore faith alone does not justify; rather, faith does so only
as the beginning of justification, and love justifies no less than faith.

**Response.** I deny the inference. Faith made effective through love
justifies, not because love is a cause of justification, but because justifying
faith is a true and living faith. It is therefore said to be effective through
love, and by this mark justifying faith is distinguished from hypocritical
faith. Paul does not say that faith justifies by means of love, but that the
faith which works through love justifies—that is, faith which shows and
declares itself true through works of love. To speak about the truth and
effects of faith is one thing; to speak about the cause of justification is
another.

**Objection 16.** From 1 John 3:14: “We have passed from death to life because
we love the brothers.” Faith alone therefore does not justify, and love is
prior to justification, since to pass from death to life is to be justified.

**Response.** 1. This commits the fallacy of treating what is not a cause as a
cause, for the passage does not speak about the cause of justification and
salvation, but about a later evidence and its effects. From love we know that
we have passed from death to life. John therefore argues not from a cause, but
from an infallible sign, as if someone were to argue,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 113 | image 15575085 | printed p. 111]

“This person neither breathes nor moves, and therefore he is not alive,” and
the reverse.

2. John himself denies that our love is the cause and merit of salvation in 1
John 4:10, 19, and attributes these instead to Christ’s death and to faith in 1
John 1:7; 2:1–2; Galatians 2:20. Finally, our love has only begun and is
therefore unable to be the cause of our justification, 1 John 1:10.

**Objection 17.** From Acts 11:18. If God grants repentance unto life, then
faith alone does not justify, but repentance does so together with faith. The
former is stated in the cited passage. Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Response.** I deny the major premise, for the passage does not speak about
justification but about eternal life. In believers repentance is antecedent to
eternal life, but not to justification; it is a consequence of justification.
Moreover, the passage does not assert that repentance is an efficient or
meritorious cause of life, but that it is a means tending toward that end. It
therefore says that repentance unto eternal life is granted.

**Objection 18.** From 2 Corinthians 7:10. Sorrow for sins works repentance
unto salvation; therefore we are justified by faith together with repentance.

**Response.** 1. I deny the inference. The apostle does not assign the cause
of salvation, but shows the consequence of true repentance—namely, that like a
road it leads to salvation.

2. He is not speaking about justification but about salvation. Finally,
although God does not forgive sins except in those who repent, he does not
forgive them because they are such people or on account of repentance, but on
account of Christ’s merit.

**Objection 19.** From Ezekiel 18:27. That which makes a soul alive justifies
it. But one who repents makes his soul alive. Therefore the conclusion follows.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 114 | image 15575086 | printed p. 112]

**Response.** I deny the minor premise, for the words are falsely quoted. They
read, “He preserves his soul alive.” As spiritual life is freely given in
Christ through faith alone, so it is preserved and fostered through
invocation, repentance, and the other spiritual exercises. But this does not
follow: repentance preserves the soul alive, therefore it is a cause of
justification and life. The case is similar to arguing, “Food and drink
preserve natural life; therefore they are the meritorious and efficient causes
of life.”

**Objection 20.** From John 3:5 and Acts 2:38. Baptism is a necessary medicine
for curing sins. Therefore, without the intention and desire to receive it, we
cannot be prepared for justification.

**Response.** 1. Baptism is a necessary medicine, not because it works by its
own efficacy or effects the healing of sin, but because it testifies and seals
that sins have been forgiven.

2. Baptism is useful and necessary, but not absolutely, as though healing from
sin could not occur without it, as Bernard testified above.

3. This does not follow: there must be a desire to receive the sacrament;
therefore this desire prepares us for justification or effects justification
as a cause. For an actual desire to receive the sacrament can exist in those
already justified, as it did in the eunuch, Acts 8, and Cornelius, Acts 10.

**Objection 21.** From Ezekiel 18:31. If we must cast away our iniquities and
make a new heart for ourselves in order that we may live, then the resolve to
live a new life prepares us for justification. The former is stated in the
cited passage. Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. I deny the major premise, because that passage does not speak
about justification but sanctification.

2. It does not follow that because we ought to make a new heart, we are able to
do so; for we ought to do many things

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 115 | image 15575087 | printed p. 113]

which we cannot. 3. Nor does this follow: we make a new heart; therefore the
resolve to live a new life is a preparation for justification. Rather, it is a
consequence of justification and its effect in us. For those whom God
justifies he renews by his Spirit and gives them a new heart, so that they may
walk according to the Spirit and not according to the flesh, Romans 8:10.

**Objection 22.** Faith separated from love and the other virtues cannot
justify. Therefore faith alone does not justify.

**Response.** 1. I distinguish the antecedent. It is true that faith separated
from the other virtues with respect to existence does not justify, because
true faith cannot exist without them. But it is false that faith separated
from the other virtues with respect to the act of justification does not
justify.

2. As to the consequence: it is true that faith does not justify when it
exists alone without the other virtues, but false with respect to the act of
justifying. The case is similar to arguing, “A hand or an eye separated from
the rest of the body apprehends or sees nothing; therefore the hand or eye,
when joined to the body, does not alone perform this act of seeing or
apprehending.” The inference does not follow.

**Further instance.** If the entire power of justifying were in faith alone,
so that the remaining virtues, although present, contributed nothing to
justification, faith would justify whether they were present or absent. But it
cannot justify when they are absent. Therefore the entire power of justifying
is not in faith alone, but partly in faith and partly in the other virtues.

**Response.** Faith justifies when the other virtues are absent from the act
or cause of justification, because it is faith’s peculiar office and proper
act to apply

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 116 | image 15575088 | printed p. 114]

Christ’s merit. But faith does not justify when they are absent with respect
to their existence or their act as effects, since the other virtues can
concur with justification in this manner, as good works and love do. The
argument therefore commits the fallacy of equivocation: in the major premise
it speaks about absence with respect to the act, but in the minor about
absence with respect to existence. If both premises are understood as absence
with respect to the act, the major is true and the minor false. But if they are
understood as absence with respect to existence, the major is false and the
minor true.
