QUESTION EIGHT

ON THE PERPETUITY OF THE CHURCH

Is the true church indefectible, so that it has always existed in the world
and must always exist until the consummation of the ages? We affirm against
the Socinians.

I. The question concerning the church’s perpetuity can be considered under a
twofold relation: either as the church is visible and conspicuous to human
eyes through the profession of the truth and communion in sacred things, or
as it is invisible and consists of true believers. In the former respect it
belongs to the church’s outward state, and a controversy about it exists
between us and the papists, who contend for its perpetual conspicuousness and
eminence. That will be discussed later. In the latter sense the question
belongs to this place. It is raised against us by the Socinians, who deny that
the church’s existence on earth is necessary and insist that it can fail
utterly.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

II. First, it is not asked here whether the church can fail from the church’s
side, or whether such failure conflicts with the church’s nature. For no one
denies that this can all too readily happen, and that it certainly would if
the church were left to itself. Rather, it is asked whether the church can
fail from God’s side. We deny this, because it is contrary to his decree. Thus
the necessity of the church’s existence is not absolute but only
hypothetical. Second, the question does not concern a failure from its
flourishing and eminence through obscuration, which will be discussed in what
follows, but a failure from life through total destruction and extinction.

III. Third, it is not asked whether this or that visible particular church can
fail, whether through the death of the faithful who are in it, through the
violence of persecutions by which a flock that existed somewhere is entirely
scattered, or through corruption of doctrine and morals and disturbance of
government. It cannot be doubted that this has often happened and can happen.
Rather, the question is whether the catholic church, which is the whole body
of believers dispersed throughout the entire world, ever fails. Has it ever
happened that there was no church on earth? Could it formerly have happened,
or can it happen in the future, that the church utterly fails and that no one
is left on earth who worships God rightly? We deny this. For if particular
churches fail, it does not therefore follow that this church immediately
fails utterly. It always remains in its true members dispersed throughout the
world.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 37 | IA leaf 71 | PDF p. 71]

IV. Properly speaking, we have no controversy here with the papists. They do
indeed gravely err by referring this perpetuity to the visible church, and to
the Roman church in particular to the exclusion of the others. They have
little concern for other particular churches and little concern for the
church’s inward faith and form, which they deny is necessary for a true
definition. Their one concern is to defend their own see and their own curia.
They place the church’s chief interest in that and thus deny that it can fail.
We will demonstrate the vanity of this fiction in its proper place.
Nevertheless, they agree with us in asserting the church’s perpetual duration
in general.

It is true that some of our opponents, such as Stapleton and others, expend
much effort proving against us that the catholic church does not fail, as
though we said that it does. They do this wrongly and without reason, as
Bellarmine himself acknowledges in book 3, chapter 13, *On the Church*: “It
must be noted that many of our writers waste time when they prove without
qualification that the church cannot fail, for Calvin and the others concede
it.” But here our dispute is with the Socinians and other enemies of the
perseverance of the saints. In defending the apostasy of the saints, they
contend that the church’s perpetual existence in the world is contingent, not
necessary. Thus Ostorod, *Institutes*, chapter 42, and Smalcius, Disputation 8,
*On the Church*. “No one,” he says, “denies precisely that there could always
have existed some who were the true church of Christ; it is only denied
precisely that it had to remain.”

V. The orthodox, on the contrary, teach by broad agreement that some church
of Christ has always existed and always will exist on earth. Its perpetual
existence is not contingent, as though it could utterly fail, but plainly
necessary—not absolutely, but hypothetically. It is necessary both on the
part of the first cause, namely, the immutable will of God (Romans 9:11), and
on the part of the second and proximate cause, namely, the word of God and the
Holy Spirit, who works effectually through it (Isaiah 59:21).

[Marginalia: The church’s perpetuity is proved. 1. Because it is Christ’s
body.]

VI. The reasons are as follows. First, from the nature of the church, because
the church is Christ’s body (Ephesians 1:23), which cannot be torn away from
him and will endure as long as the head himself, who cannot exist without his
body. That the body will remain such until the consummation of the ages is
evident even from the fact that it grows and increases daily toward its
perfection, which will come at last on the final day (Ephesians 4:15–16).

The title *bride of Christ*, which is attributed to the church, also belongs
here. For he has betrothed her to himself forever according to the oracle
(Hosea 2:19), and he also pursues her with perpetual love until he has
perfectly sanctified her and presented her blameless and without spot before
God in heaven (Ephesians 5:26–27). There this mystical marriage must be
consummated through the marriage supper of the Lamb, which must be celebrated
on the final day (Revelation 19:9). Everyone therefore sees that the church’s
duration must be perpetual so that she may enjoy these benefits.

[Marginalia: 2. From the nature of the covenant of grace.]

VII. Second, from the nature of the covenant of grace that God made with the
church. Since it is eternal and never to be abrogated according to the sacred
oracles (Isaiah 59:21; 61:8; Jeremiah 31; 32; Ezekiel 37:26; Psalm 89:30,
etc.), the church, which is the other covenanting party, must endure
perpetually. By receiving the covenant’s benefits, it must also discharge its
duty toward God, to whom it is bound by the covenant.

This is all the more so because the preservation of the world depends upon
the preservation of the church. Indeed, God sustains the world for no other
reason than to gather from it the number of the elect from whom the church’s
body is composed. The church therefore could not utterly perish without the
world itself, which is preserved for the church’s sake, also perishing.

[Marginalia: 3. From the divine promises.]

VIII. Third, from the divine promises that assert the perpetuity of God’s
presence and grace toward the church in the world. Christ promises that “he
will build his church upon the rock and the gates of hell will not prevail
against it” (Matthew 16:18). Here

[1847 vol. III printed p. 38 | IA leaf 72 | PDF p. 72]

Christ declares that whatever Satan’s attempts and endeavors to attack the
church may be, and whatever may be the power of death and the grave, which
are often designated by the gates of hell (Job 38:17; Psalm 9:14; Isaiah
38:10), they will nevertheless never prevail to overthrow it utterly, because
it is built upon the eternal and unconquered rock. This could not be said if
the church could fail completely and perish in the world. For then it would
truly be said that the gates of hell had prevailed against it and that the
standards of hell, the devil, and death had been victorious.

Nor can it be objected that “the promise here is only that the church,
insofar as it is the church, will always remain unconquered by death and
condemnation.” For the promise is made to the church not only insofar as it
is the church—that is, for as long as it remains the church, which is the
force of their reduplicative expression—but also that it may perpetually be
the church. The gates of hell are said not to prevail against the church for
no other reason than to signify that the attempts by which they strive to
oppress and destroy it are vain and futile.

IX. Christ confirms the same thing with the splendid promise, “I am with you
until the consummation of the ages” (Matthew 28:20). Although Christ first
regards the apostles, promising them the perpetual presence of his grace in
the exercise of their calling throughout their lives, even if life were to
last until the end of the world—in the same sense Paul commands Timothy to
keep the commandment until Christ’s coming (1 Timothy 6:14)—the promise can
also be referred to the true and faithful ministers of Christ, who in the
ordinary office of pastors can be said to have succeeded the apostles. For
just as the preceding command belonged equally to all pastors, so the promise
must certainly be extended to them, so that Christ’s perpetual presence is
promised to his faithful servants who conduct themselves in holiness in their
office.

There is also no doubt that it is rightly extended to the whole church.
Augustine does this more than once in order to teach that Christ did indeed
withdraw from them in bodily presence, but is present and will always be
present spiritually. Hence Jansenius says, “In the apostles themselves he
speaks to all the faithful,” because he addresses the disciples here not only
as apostles but also as God’s elect and true believers representing the whole
body of the faithful. Christ remains with them until the end of the ages in
accordance with the promise given elsewhere to the church (Isaiah 49:14;
54:8–10). For that reason he asks for the Father’s protection and keeping in
John 17, not only for the apostles but also for all who would believe through
their word and who were to be sanctified in the truth.

It is therefore futile for those who wish “to restrict this promise to the
apostles and their successors in the ministry of the gospel, with whom Christ
will be, not absolutely, but on the condition that they conduct themselves
worthily in their office.” For it is given to the apostles in such a way that
it belongs no less to the church by the nature of correlatives. When Christ
promises that he will be with the apostles—namely, effectual through their
ministry for the faith and conversion of the elect—by that very fact he
supposes that believers will always exist in the world to whom the gospel is
preached and in whom he exercises its power for salvation. Mark 16:20 and
Paul in 1 Timothy 3:16 explain the promise from its fulfillment. Moreover, the
condition that is proposed, besides not appearing in the text, is fulfilled
by God himself in his faithful servants, whom he renders fit for his work (2
Corinthians 3:5–6).

It is also wrongly said that this promise regarded the visible church because
it regards the apostles and the pastors who succeed them. For it cannot
regard the visible church except insofar as the invisible church is contained
within it. Otherwise it would follow that no visible assembly founded by the
apostles could have perished, whereas experience teaches that this has
happened.

[Marginalia: 4. From Christ’s mediatorial office.]

X. Fourth, from Christ’s mediatorial office, which must be perpetual with
respect to its three parts. 1. With respect to the kingdom, which is called
eternal and without end: “A kingdom that will not be corrupted or overthrown
forever” (Daniel 2:44), and whose end will never

[1847 vol. III printed p. 39 | IA leaf 73 | PDF p. 73]

come (Luke 1:33). In accordance with this the apostle says that “he must
reign until he has put his enemies beneath his feet, but the last enemy to be
destroyed is death” (1 Corinthians 15:25–26). Therefore, if he must reign
until death is destroyed, which will happen on the final day, the church in
which he reigns must also endure, unless we wish to imagine a king without
subjects and a shepherd without sheep, which is incoherent.

2. With respect to the priesthood, for since he has been appointed “a priest
in perpetuity” (Psalm 110:4), and cannot discharge this office perpetually
except with respect to intercession, there would be no place for his
intercession if the church militant, for which he must intercede, utterly
perished.

3. With respect to prophecy, because this office pertains to the conversion
of elect people and the preservation of those converted through the same
proclamation of the gospel, namely, for the joining together of Christ’s
body. If there were no longer any elect in the world, the need for preaching
would also cease, though the apostle testifies that it must endure until the
end of the world (Ephesians 4:13). Therefore, just as the ministry of the word
and use of the sacraments must be perpetual in the church, so the church
itself must be perpetual.

[Marginalia: 5. From the perpetuity of the Spirit’s presence.]

XI. Fifth, from the perpetuity of the Spirit’s presence in the church:
“This is the covenant that I will make with them: My Spirit who is upon you,
and my words that I have put in your mouth, will not depart from your mouth or
from the mouth of your offspring, from now and forever” (Isaiah 59:21). Here
God promises the perpetual presence of the word and Spirit through every age
until the end of the world. But how could this take place if the church could
utterly perish and fail?

The same is proved by Christ’s promise of the Comforter, who will remain with
the faithful “forever” (John 14:16)—that is, not only for a few years, as he
had been with them, but perpetually, so that neither enemies nor death itself
could take him away from them. Nor can it be said that this is promised under
the tacit condition that God’s commandments are observed, and that if this
condition fails, the Spirit can depart and be driven away. For then all
certainty in laying the church’s foundations would depend not upon the Spirit
but upon the will of human beings, and the Spirit would not be the one who
determines and establishes, but would instead be determined and established.

Add that Christ wished to comfort his people here. The person who says, “The
Spirit does not depart unless you will it,” does not comfort someone liable to
sin, because that person fears no one but himself. The person who says, “The
Spirit will also bring it about that you will it,” does comfort him. Both our
prayers and thanksgivings rest upon this foundation.

[Marginalia: 6. From the experience of all ages.]

XII. Sixth, from the experience of all ages, because the church has always
endured from the beginning of the world until the present time and has never
utterly failed. This can easily be shown age by age. In every age God has had
his faithful people, who at times lay hidden in greater obscurity but who had
nevertheless truly been called out of the world and received into communion
and covenant with God.

As regards the Old Testament from Moses to Christ, the matter is established
beyond every hazard of doubt, since the church was no longer enclosed within
one family but, diffused into a great nation, was exposed to human eyes. As
regards the preceding ages, although the church was sometimes more obscure
and reduced to a very small number, as in the time of Noah and in Abraham’s
time before his calling from Ur of the Chaldeans, many things nevertheless
prove that it always existed.

In the first interval of the first world, from Adam to Noah, the church began
in Adam and his family. Thus Abel’s faith is celebrated (Hebrews 11:4), as are
the piety of Seth and Enosh, in whose time “people began to call upon the name
of the Lord” (Genesis 4:26), namely, with public and solemn worship because of
the plurality of families, which celebrated divine worship in a larger
assembly. It was then continued in Enoch and his descendants until Noah, and
passed from Noah to Shem, who lived until the time of Abraham. God wished to
make his covenant with Abraham in a more illustrious manner, and thus
preserved the church in his

[1847 vol. III printed p. 40 | IA leaf 74 | PDF p. 74]

family. It increased remarkably in Egypt until it grew into a very numerous
nation. God gathered it into the form of a republic, gave it his statutes and
laws, and ordained that it should subsist under that government until Christ,
though with various changes.

XIII. Concerning the times of the New Testament, it is no less clear from the
testimony of the histories themselves that the church never utterly failed.
Indeed, if it ever failed, this should especially have happened either during
the Roman emperors’ most severe persecutions, at the time of the Arian heresy,
which had occupied nearly the whole world, or under papal tyranny before the
Reformation occurred. Yet nothing could be further from the truth. Far from
failing under the emperors’ persecutions, the church plainly grew and was
propagated as God made the blood and ashes of the martyrs fruitful like seed.

The plague of Arianism pervaded many parts of the world, but not all of them;
not only many individuals but also several churches remained free of it.
Papal tyranny did not rage so thoroughly that God did not always preserve his
people under it, whom he later wished to call out of Babylon. Since it is
therefore established that the church has never failed until now, it is
certainly inferred from this that it will never perish in the future, because
the same reasons demand its duration in each case.

[Marginalia: 7. From the preserving causes.]

XIV. Seventh, from the causes that preserve the church, because all causes of
failure are removed from it and all causes of perpetuity are affirmed of it.
For if the church could fail, this would occur either because it could be
conquered by Satan, seduced by the world, or hurled by the flesh into reigning
sin and death. But it cannot be overcome by Satan, because he has been
conquered and triumphed over by Christ (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14).
Consequently, he can neither bring about through sifting that our faith fails
(Luke 22:32), nor secure the prevalence of all his arts and endeavors against
the church (Matthew 16:18).

Nor can the church be seduced by the world, because our faith is the victory
that has overcome the world (1 John 5:4), and the elect cannot be seduced
(Matthew 24:24), or be brought under sin’s dominion again by the flesh (1 John
3:9). Indeed, from God’s immutable purpose (Romans 8:28; 2 Timothy 2:19), from
Christ’s merit and intercession (1 John 2:1–2; Romans 8:33–34), and from the
Spirit’s power and protection, the faithful are kept by faith for salvation
(John 14:16–17; 1 Peter 1:5), as was demonstrated more fully in the locus on
perseverance.

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XV. It is one thing that the visible and outward church of the called can fail
and often does fail in its various parts. We concede this, and it is evident
from the example of the Jewish church and various Christian churches. The
taking away of the kingdom and removal of the lampstand of which Christ
speaks belong here (Matthew 21:43; Revelation 2:5). It is another thing that
the invisible church of the elect can fail, which we deny. The perpetual
agreement of Scripture and experience establishes that even in the most
disturbed and corrupt times, some remnant of the church has always been
preserved according to gracious election (Romans 11:5).

XVI. It is said that the situation of the catholic church is the same as that
of all particular churches, which can fail, because the situation of the whole
is the same as that of the parts. But this argument holds only within the same
order, not in different orders—namely, with respect to the catholic church of
the called and visible particular churches, but not with respect to the
invisible church of the elect. Furthermore, it cannot be admitted absolutely
and without qualification that each and every particular church can fail on
earth simultaneously and at once, but only successively and at different
times.

XVII. Christ’s statement, “When the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on
earth?” (Luke 18:8), must not be understood absolutely, as though no faithful
people would remain in the world. Paul declares that at the Lord’s coming
those found alive, among whom he also

[1847 vol. III printed p. 41 | IA leaf 75 | PDF p. 75]

names himself, will not precede those who sleep, and that after their
resurrection they will be caught up to the Lord in the clouds (1 Thessalonians
4:15). He could not have said this unless he thought that some faithful people
would then exist. Rather, Christ spoke hyperbolically and comparatively,
because the church will be very small in comparison with its state in former
ages, and very few faithful people will survive when he comes. Thus these
words indeed mark the church’s future obscuration, but not its destruction.

XVIII. It is one thing for the church to be obscured and hidden so that it
does not appear, and another for it to fail and be extinguished so that it
ceases to exist. We grant the former, but not the latter. It is one thing for
the church to experience various conditions and assume a different face—now
splendid and serene, now deformed and sorrowful—which we admit belongs to it,
and another for it to be abolished with respect to its existence, which we
deny.

XIX. A universal apostasy is predicted (2 Thessalonians 2:3), not absolutely,
but in a qualified sense, because a few witnesses would remain (Revelation
11:3). The apostle therefore mentions this so that the faithful of that time
may guard themselves more carefully from seduction. When “the whole earth” is
said to “worship the beast” (Revelation 13:3), the whole earth means classes
of every sort, not every member of each class—namely, all the earth’s
inhabitants indiscriminately, of whatever nation, condition, or sex, whose
names are not written in the Lamb’s book of life, as is explained in verse 8;
that is, those who are reprobate in contrast to the elect and faithful.

XX. If Revelation predicts many extremely severe persecutions threatening the
church in the last times, nothing about its total destruction can be inferred
from them. The woman’s flight into the wilderness (Revelation 12:6–14) indeed
denotes the church’s obscuration and dispersion because of the dragon’s
persecutions, against which she must seek safety in flight. It does not,
however, signify her abolition and destruction. Indeed, so that she may not
perish, the wings of an eagle are fitted to her so that she may suddenly fly
away from the dragon and withdraw into the wilderness, where she can lie
hidden safely.

XXI. The state of nature is one thing and the state of grace another. In the
former the church was mutable and could fail, and in fact did fail. But in the
latter it is immutable and can fail neither totally nor finally, just as
individual believers cannot.

XXII. Although this or that particular church can fail, so that in a place
where God today gathers a church for himself through the preaching of the
word and administration of the sacraments, the purity of the divine word may
in the course of time be driven out through heresies and persecutions—and
although no absolute promise has been given to any particular church that it
will be perpetual and in no way liable to failure—nevertheless, speaking
absolutely and without qualification, the church never fails. For God gathers
the church for himself out of the human race until the end of the world, if
not among this people and in this place, then among another people and in
another place.
