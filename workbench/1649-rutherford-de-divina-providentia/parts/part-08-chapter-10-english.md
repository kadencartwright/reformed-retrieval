## Chapter X

### Whether any Christian fate ought to be admitted

The Jesuits and Arminians call God’s absolute decree—which from eternity
predetermines all things, causes, counsels, actions, and events—and also God’s
predetermination to the material acts of sin the most fatal fate of the
Stoics. They do so especially because these savor of the supreme necessity
flowing from God’s irrevocable will: by the eternal decree the free will is
predetermined to one thing, and by God’s temporal predetermination it is
necessitated to that one thing, since predetermination, no less essentially
than the decree, is an efficacious impulse of the will to this material act of
sin rather than to that one. Something, therefore, must be said about fate,
because of the ill-fated slanders of the adversaries, for whom barking against
God’s truth is exceedingly fateful. Accordingly, we must speak first about the
name “fate”; 2. about the thing; 3. about Christian fate, insofar as it differs
from pagan fate; and 4. about the friendly accord and association between the
necessity of divine providence and human liberty.

*[Marginal note and references: Providence is a certain kind of fate,
although we shrink from the word; Calvin, Institutes, book 1, chapter 16,
section 8; Thomas Aquinas, part 1, question 116, article 1; Augustine, City of
God, book 5, chapter 9.]*

Our Calvin speaks reverently about the name. “Although,” he says, “we
reluctantly dispute about the word, we nevertheless do not accept the term
*fate*: first, because it is among that class of profane novelties which Paul
teaches us to shun; and second, because by the odium attached to it they try
to burden God’s truth.” Augustine nevertheless speaks correctly—as the Jesuit
Suárez does not deny, nor even Thomas himself: “If anyone calls God’s will or
power by the name *fate*, let him retain the opinion and correct his
language.” Indeed, I add this other statement of Augustine, as Bradwardine
teaches and Diego Ruiz acknowledges: “Where the name *fate* is customarily
used by

*[Marginal references: Augustine, City of God, book 5, chapter 5; Suárez,
Metaphysics, vol. 1, disputation 19, section 11, no. 11; Bradwardine, On the
Cause of God, book 1, chapter 28; Diego Ruiz, On Providence and
Predetermination, disputation 4, section 3, no. 8.]*

[PDF p. 151; printed p. 100]

speakers—that is, for the configuration of the stars under which each person
is conceived or born—because the thing itself is empty, we demonstrate that
it has no force. But the order of causes, in which God’s will has the greatest
power, we neither deny nor call by the name *fate*, unless perhaps we
understand *fate* to be derived from *fando*, that is, from speaking. For we
cannot deny that it is written in the sacred Scriptures, ‘God spoke,’” and so
forth.

Among the papists, however, Jovius, Bishop of Nocera, does not shrink from the
terms fate, Mars, fortune, and genius. Nor does Lipsius. “But God,” he says,
“surpasses even what we call fate.” Daniel Chamier also observes this. So does
Augustine: “But as for those who call by the name *fate* the connection and
series of all causes by which everything that occurs comes about, one need
not contend and labor greatly with them in a controversy over a word, since
they attribute the necessary order itself, and a certain connection, to the
supreme will of God.”

*[Marginal references: Jovius, in his History, book of the author, century 3,
letter 20; Chamier, Catholic Panstratia, vol. 1, On Providence, book 3,
chapter 11; Augustine, City of God, book 5, chapter 7.]*

Therefore we do not call God’s providence *fatal* or *fate*, because, as
Suárez says, “from its earliest signification, *fate* was instituted to
signify the root of a fateful necessity, which those who invented the name
*fate* considered to be absolute necessity.” Thus, “insofar as all these
things,” says Thomas, “which are done here are subjected to divine providence,
as things ordered and, as it were, spoken beforehand by it”—before they came
to pass and before they were decreed by God—“we can posit fate, although the
holy doctors refused to use this name because of those who twisted the name
to mean the force of the stars’ position.”

*[Marginal references: Suárez, Metaphysics, vol. 1, disputation 19, section
11, no. 12; Thomas Aquinas, part 1, question 116, article 1.]*

“Fate is fourfold,” says Giovanni Pico della Mirandola. “1. Fate is nature
itself; or the series and order of causes producing all things by a necessary
nexus; or the course and configuration of the stars when something is born or
conceived; or, finally, the certain execution of the divine counsel and its
inevitable success. The first fate is natural. The second is Stoic. The third
is mathematical or astrological. The fourth is Christian and divine.” Hence
it is not easy to define what fate is.

*[Marginal note and reference: Fate is fourfold; Giovanni Pico della
Mirandola, Against Judicial Astrology, book 4, chapter 4.]*

But something must be said about each of them. Natural fate is nature itself.
Thus Piccolomini says: “According to Aristotle, fate is fundamentally the
same as nature. It is called *nature* insofar as it is the essence of a thing
and a principle of acting; but *fate* more expressly displays an order
dependent on some principle, as though pronounced, commanded, and declared.
For just as in civil government one considers a prince, to whom wisdom and
providence belong, followed by the establishment, promulgation, and execution
of laws, so in the universe the prince of all is God, to whom wisdom and
providence belong as the establishment of the universe’s laws. Fate follows
providence as the promulgation of laws,

*[Marginal reference: Piccolomini, Natural Philosophy, book 2, consideration
14, p. 253.]*

[PDF p. 152; printed p. 101]

and nature follows fate as the execution of laws, so that fate especially
preserves nature’s relation to the first principle.” So much for him.

Perhaps it was for this reason that Posidonius, as Pontanus reports and the
Complutensian Doctors observe, posited three principles of all things: “he
assigned the first parts to God, the second to nature,” say the
Complutensians, “and the third to fate.” He considered fate a necessary and
unavoidable cause. But this fate must be rejected, because the defenders of
fate said that everything is conducted by fate; yet not everything occurs by
nature or by nature’s impulse, but many things happen freely and
contingently. 2. Indeed, even natural effects in this lower world, such as
rains and winds, do not happen by the necessity of natural fate, as Suárez
rightly says, because they can often be impeded and often occur by the
intervention of a free cause, namely through the ministry of angels.
Alexander of Aphrodisias does say that “fate sometimes signifies the nature
of each thing,” and this is gathered from Aristotle. 3. Many things occur by
the command of plainly extraordinary and supernatural providence: the death,
resurrection, and ascension of Christ into heaven; the destruction of Sodom
by fire sent down from heaven; and the destruction of the Jewish people and
of the Babylonian, Assyrian, and Persian kingdoms. None of these things happen
by fate—that is, by nature or natural necessity.

*[Marginal references: Posidonius; Pontanus, On Fortune, book 1, chapter 13;
the Complutensian College, commentary on Aristotle’s Physics, disputation 13,
On Fortune and Fate, question 4; Suárez, Metaphysics, vol. 1, disputation 19,
section 11, no. 12; Alexander of Aphrodisias, On Fate, book 3; Aristotle,
Physics, book 5, chapter 6, texts 5 and 7.]*

The second fate is that of the Stoics. It is such a series of causes as is
absolutely necessary, binding all causes together and necessitating God
himself by fate. This opinion is attributed to the Stoics because of certain
rhetorical and poetic expressions they employed; and in this sense the
Fathers and Doctors of the Church most rightly condemn the Stoic opinion when
explained in this way. For this is astronomical or mathematical fate. In
truth, however, this opinion may perhaps be attributed to the Stoics
undeservedly and without grounds. Lipsius, who is by no means unpracticed in
their doctrine, denies that this was their meaning. 2. A man of greater
learning as well as authority thinks that the Stoics appear to deserve
acquittal from this charge. “For Boethius appears,” says Thomas Bradwardine,
“deliberately to accuse the Stoics in his exposition of the first [doubtful
reading: *Peri hermeneias*] because they posited that all things come about by
fate. For they spoke about fate in accordance with the efficacy of the divine
will, and perhaps they are free from every real error, even if not from a
verbal one; for this word *fate* is suspect among Catholics, although the
thing itself is sound.” 3. Plutarch himself says: “Fate contains all things,
as indeed it appears, and nevertheless all things do not on that account
happen necessarily, but each according to the manner of its own nature.” 4.
Augustine, drawing from Seneca,

*[Marginal references: Lipsius, Guide to Stoic Philosophy, book 1, chapter
29; Bradwardine, On the Cause of God, book 1, chapter 30; Plutarch, On Fate;
Augustine, City of God, book 5, chapter 8; Seneca.]*

[PDF p. 153; printed p. 102]

Homer, and the Stoics, cites these verses. Bradwardine supports this, whatever
Grevinchovius may say against it:

> Lead me, supreme Father and ruler of lofty heaven,
>
> Wherever it has pleased you; there is no delay in obeying.
>
> Here I am, eager; make me unwilling, and I shall accompany you groaning;
>
> And as an evil man I shall suffer to do what a good man was permitted to do.
>
> The fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling.

*[Marginal references: Bradwardine, place cited; Grevinchovius, Against Ames,
p. 64.]*

By these words the truth establishes—and Augustine proves—that the Stoics
held Jupiter to be superior to fate and to rule over fate. 5. Isidore’s
positing of three Fates proves the same thing. For they posit three sisters,
whom they call *Parcae* by a contrary sense or antiphrasis, as Servius says,
because they spare (*parcant*) no one—although Varro derives their name from
*parcendo*, sparing. They imagine these three sitting on a throne, dressed in
white and crowned upon the head. I would venture to say that this is because
they signify the self-determining authority (*autexousia*) of God, who sits
upon the throne, governs the times of human life, and fixes beforehand the
limits of the months of life which a person cannot pass beyond, as Job says.
They imagine them in white garments because this self-determining authority
of God is white—that is, free from the stain of injustice, innocent, and most
equitable—as Natalis Comes rightly says. The first, Lachesis, speaks and sings
of things past, and signifies things situated beneath the moon. The second is
called Clotho; she writes and sings of things present, and signifies the seven
planets. The third is called Atropos; she sings of things future, draws out
the threads, and signifies the fixed sphere. Plato, however, says that these
three sisters are daughters of the fateful God himself, or of the soul of the
world. I know that Piccolomini calls this the opinion not of the Stoics but of
the Academics, but he is mistaken, for the Stoics acknowledge the Fates in
Seneca.

*[Marginal references: Isidore, Etymologies, book 8, section 47; Servius,
“The Three Fates”; Varro in Gellius, book 3; Job 14:5; Natalis Comes,
Mythology; Plato, Republic, book 10; Piccolomini, Physics, book 2,
consideration 4, p. 252; Seneca.]*

But what are all these things other than God, as Bradwardine says and Boethius
explains as follows? “Every generation of things, the whole advance of
mutable natures, and whatever else is moved in any manner, receives its
causes, order, and forms from the stability of the divine mind.”

*[Marginal note and references: Bradwardine, On the Cause of God, book 1,
chapter 28; Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy, book 4, prose 6; God’s will
is fate.]*

What else, then, did the poets, the Stoics, and the Academics establish here
but an absolute decree concerning the boundaries of human life? Or why would
they solicit Jupiter with prayers if he himself were bound by fate? I answer,
1. “They meant only this,” says the most learned Dr Andrew Rivet, “that God
does not change what he has once established—not because there is some
necessity in him or because fate rules over him, but because it pleased him
to stand by his decree.” Hence God’s immutability—by which he can no more
retract or rescind what he has once

*[Marginal reference: Rivet, Catholic Orthodoxy, vol. 2, tractate 4, question
6.]*

[PDF p. 154; printed p. 103]

decreed than he can lie, change, or deny himself—is like fate, which by an
iron and adamantine law stands firm and irrevocable, never to be revoked, not
even in eternity. This was the reason the Stoics imagined God and fate to be
the same. 2. From this immutability of God’s decree, the poets—for whom it is
safe to lie without penalty—and the orators who imitated them fashioned, in
their hyperbolic rhetorical flourishes, a God who was fate’s servant, fate
ruling over God, God bound by fate, and their God and Jupiter grieving over
Sarpedon’s death. Scripture, however, attributes repentance, grief, joy, and
desires to God, but in the manner of human affections. Thus they inferred the
erroneous consequence—if their words are interpreted literally—that crimes
are excused by fate. So Seneca:

> That fault belongs to fate; let no one be guilty because of fate.

And likewise this:

> He whom the fates compel is wretched with a pardon.

*[Marginal references: Genesis 6:6; Judges 10:16; Deuteronomy 5:29; Isaiah
5:4; Seneca, Hippolytus.]*

It is on account of these consequences of the Stoics, interpreted literally
in this way by the Doctors and, in the strict sense, heterodox, that our
theologians condemned Stoic fate. Thus Calvin: “For we do not invent a
necessity with the Stoics from a perpetual nexus of causes,” and so forth,
“but we establish God as the arbiter and governor of all things,” and so
forth. Beza speaks likewise.

How perversely, therefore, Nicolaas Grevinchovius says: “Although fate is
established by these verses”—“Lead me, Father,” and so forth, discussed
above—“its governance is nevertheless placed in God’s hand. For the poet
invokes the supreme Father and desires to be led by him, while at the same
time showing that his will is certain and infallible.” But Scripture
acknowledges such a fate, and so does Augustine. This man denies fate—that is,
such a series and connection of things as is in God’s hand. Yet, since such a
fate exists, the connection of things will be either in God’s hand and his
powerful providence or in fortune’s hand. Thus, while he overturns fate, he
builds up fortune. But he denies that the connection of things is in God’s
hand. 2. With this aim, as he denies fate, he condemns in the Stoics the fact
that they affirm God’s will to be certain and infallible. In Grevinchovius’s
view, plainly, God’s will is uncertain, wavering, suspended, and fallible;
otherwise, God’s certain will is Stoic fate. The third fate is mathematical
and astrological. This is the fate

*[Marginal references: Calvin, Institutes, book 1, chapter 16, section 8;
Beza, Against Castellio, article 6; Grevinchovius, Against Ames, p. 94; Isaiah
15:17 [as printed]; Augustine, City of God, book 5, chapter 7.]*

[PDF p. 155; printed p. 104]

which we detest and the Fathers opposed, together with the fate which some
perhaps imputed to the Stoics not without injustice. For perhaps some
individuals, rather than the whole school, added the rhetorical and
hyperbolic consequence that God himself is bound by such a fatality of the
connection of things. We oppose this as well, since our God does whatever he
has willed without any external necessity which in any manner necessitates or
determines him.

Whether the Pharisees held this fate is uncertain; some papists assert that
they did. Josephus speaks of them as follows: “[doubtful Greek reading:] The
Pharisees say that certain things, but not all things, belong to fate.” It is
likely that by fate they understood God’s will. Elsewhere they say: “[doubtful
Greek reading:] The Pharisees, while assigning all things to fate, do not
remove from human beings the voluntary impulse that lies within their power.”
Rufinus translates: “They believe all things are conducted by fate, but do
not take away man’s free choice.” It is therefore unlikely that they took
away God’s free choice. Elsewhere, however, the Pharisees praise fate and
astronomy, as Epiphanius says.

*[Marginal references: Josephus, Antiquities, book 13, section 9; the same,
Jewish War, book 2, chapter 12; Rufinus’s Epiphanius, on the Pharisees.]*

But the Fathers condemn a fate which, by the influence of the stars, imposes
necessity upon all lower causes, including the free wills of human beings:
the Council of Braga, Eusebius, John of Damascus, Augustine, Clement, Gregory
of Nyssa or Nemesius, and Boethius. And we embrace with open arms the
condemnation of the errors in this fate which the Fathers condemned. For 1.
they taught that the stars rule over the free wills of human beings, and thus
overturn man’s free choice. Eusebius says in a [doubtful Greek reading], which
is rendered: “If an inexorable necessity is located in the stars and fate,
not only for those things outside us which are to be imputed, but even for
rational inclinations themselves—a necessity which compels human
consciences—philosophy has perished and religion has perished.” Clement says:
“Since God is just and himself made human nature, how could it be that he
himself placed over us a nativity opposed to us, which would compel us to
sin?” Augustine says that if the fate of the stars is posited, free choice is
destroyed in human beings, in God, and in Christ. So also Jerome, Gregory, or
Nemesius in book 6 of his Philosophy; Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy,
book 6; Eusebius in the place cited; Clement, Recognitions, book 9; Augustine,
Questions of the Old and New Testaments, question 115; Jerome, book 2 on
Isaiah 3:11; and Gregory, homily 10, which concerns Epiphany.

*[Marginal references: Council of Braga I, canon 9; Eusebius, Preparation for
the Gospel, book 6, chapter 5; first error of astronomical fate; John of
Damascus, On the Faith, book 2, chapter 7; Augustine, City of God, book 5,
chapter 8; Clement, Recognitions, book 3.]*

[PDF p. 156; printed p. 105]

Ambrose. To these may be added the philosophers Aristotle, Ammonius,
Simplicius, Themistius, the Complutensian College, the College of Coimbra,
Suárez, Vásquez, and Piccolomini. One could also add Thomas Aquinas,
Bradwardine, and Diego Ruiz.

Ruiz strives to impute this error to us in the following words: “A decree
which predetermines the material acts of sin will be worse than this fate, if
it is not entirely similar to it. The Fathers,” he says, “wrote against Simon
Magus, the Manichaean, Marcion, Apollinaris, and the Priscillianists. For
those who believe that the stars possess such powers of influence as
necessitate human beings to sins, fate will be at least the mediate author of
each sin which arises from their influence. Therefore, since God alone fixed
beforehand the predetermination to the act of hatred of God and bestowed all
its force upon it, he will be at least mediately the first author of the
hatred against God that necessarily arises from that determination. —— For
those who left nothing in the power of the stars attributed to the stars a
mere execution of the divine will of this kind. Augustine says that a decree
of determining to crimes is intrinsically evil, and that what is unworthy of
the heavens is unworthy of the divine will. And either the Priscillianists or
the Manichaeans likewise cared nothing about the formal character of malice,
provided they established that certain stars have such a power that by
necessity they drive those born beneath them to the material acts of theft,
adultery,” and so forth. “It is not a satisfactory answer to say that the
stars have no malice or guilt because the stars determine only the material
works of sins and determine to them according to their character as universal
causes. For someone will say that the stars do not determine to malice because
they are not moral but physical causes,” Ruiz adds. “The Fathers infer, as an
unacceptable consequence of the necessity of the stars, that a person is
excused from guilt. But as to whether the stars intend or do not intend the
formal character of malice when they impel someone to hatred of God, this
makes no difference to whether a person is or is not excused from guilt. Yet
it would make the greatest difference if the stars necessitated human beings
to the material acts of sins.”

*[Marginal references: Ambrose, Hexaemeron, work of the fourth day;
Aristotle, Ethics, book 3, chapter 1; Ammonius, Physics, book 2, chapter 6;
Simplicius; Themistius; the Complutensian College on Aristotle’s Physics,
disputation 13, On Chance and Fortune, question 4; the College of Coimbra,
commentary, book 2, chapter 6, question 1, article 2; Suárez, Metaphysics,
vol. 1, disputation 19, On Necessity and Contingency, section 11, no. 7;
Vásquez, on 1.2, disputation 28, chapter 4, nos. 16–17; Piccolomini, Physics,
book 2, consideration 4, pp. 253, 255; Thomas Aquinas, part 1, question 9,
article 6; Bradwardine, On the Cause of God, book 1, chapter 20; Ruiz, volume
On Providence and volume On the Will of God, disputation 38, section 2; Diego
Ruiz, place cited; Augustine, on Psalm 91; Augustine, ibid.; Nemesius, On
Human Nature, chapter 35.]*

I answer, 1. The Jesuit’s argument is as follows. Whoever is the creator and
author of those stars which necessitate to sin is the mediate cause and at
least the mediate author of sin. The reason is that the author of a thing
which fatefully necessitates

[PDF p. 157; printed p. 106]

to sin is mediately the fateful author of sin. But when God creates and
produces the predetermination to the material act of hatred of God, he creates
and produces that which fatefully necessitates to sin. Therefore, God, in
creating and producing the predetermination to the material act of sin, will
be the author or mediate cause of sin.

I answer, 1. The minor premise is openly false. For in producing the
predetermination to the material act of hatred of God, God does not produce
something that fatefully necessitates to sin. Rather, he produces something
which, in a manner suited to the nature of free choice, bends the will as it
freely inclines itself toward that to which it is predetermined by God.

*[Marginal note: Diego Ruiz, though God does not create it, makes the
predetermination to the material acts of sin at least a mediate cause of
sin.]*

2. To the proof: those who placed nothing in the power of the stars and left
to the stars only the execution of the divine will do injury both to the
heavens and to God. They injure the heavens because, as Augustine rightly
says, “if they think these same stars possess this power delivered to them by
the supreme power of him”—God—“so that they decide these things by their own
will, they do a great injury to heaven.” And they injure God because, as
Nemesius says, “the stars are unjust, because they make some adulterers and
others murderers, and God is their chief Architect.” For the creator of the
stars who implanted in them such a power as would compel human beings to sin
will be unjust.

3. It is false that Augustine said that “the decree of determining to
crimes”—that is, to the material acts of sin—“is intrinsically evil.”
Augustine says the opposite: “Nothing occurs unless the omnipotent wills it
to occur”—and thus has decreed it—“either by allowing it to occur or by doing
it himself.” It is true that Augustine says, “We must not think about God in
the same way as it seemed most unworthy to think about the stars.” We likewise
say that it is unworthy both of God the Creator and of the stars, God’s
creatures, that a power of compelling and physically necessitating the free
will should be impressed and implanted in the stars, as the defenders of
astronomical fate taught.

*[Marginal references and note: Augustine, Enchiridion, chapter 10; there is
a vast difference between astronomical fate and physical
predetermination.]*

4. It is false that the Priscillianists and astrologers “cared nothing about
the formal character of malice, provided they established that the stars
necessitate human beings to the material acts of sin.” For 1. precisely by
saying that the stars necessitate human beings to the material acts of sin—by
impressing upon the free will a physical force of such a kind as removes the
elective power of liberty and reason’s indifferent judgment—they cared very
much about the formal character of malice. For if the stars thus necessitate
to the material acts of sin, they remove the power of free choice. Then the
stars will be the causes of sins, God will be the creator of sin-causing
stars, and human beings will sin by fate. Although God by his own

[PDF p. 158; printed p. 107]

predeterminative concurrence, which is tempered to the disposition and
natural manner in which liberty acts, necessitates to the material acts of
sin, it by no means follows that God is the author of sin in the way in which
they said that the stars cause sins. For the stars’ necessitation of liberty
is physical and destructive of its connatural manner of acting, whereas God’s
necessitation is tempered to the free will’s natural manner of acting and is
not at all destructive of liberty.

Thus Augustine: “They say of heaven, ‘You have an unavoidable necessity of
sinning, and Venus did this, or Saturn, or Mars,’ namely, so that the human
being might be without blame while the Creator and Ordainer of heaven and the
stars is blameworthy.” And Basil: “The evil stars transfer the cause of their
own malignity to their maker; for if evil-doing belongs to the stars by
nature, surely the Creator himself will be the producer of malignity.” Cyril
of Alexandria says that “through these people God is accused as the one who
implanted in heaven that force which impels human beings to evils.” And in the
same place: “Since heaven impels human beings to act wickedly, they cannot
themselves avoid that evil; for no one is rightly blamed for what is not
placed within his own will.” “Those who argue in this way,” says Nemesius,
“take away the nature of the contingent together with free will.” Cyril of
Jerusalem says that “they taught that the inclinations of the stars compel us
to embrace impiety.” And Simon Magus, as Vincent of Lérins says, “taught that
God had created human nature by his own hands in such a condition that, by
its own motion indeed and by the necessary impulse of a certain will, it could
do nothing else and would will nothing else but to sin.”

*[Marginal references: Augustine, discourse 14, chapter 3; Basil,
Hexaemeron, homily 6; Cyril of Alexandria, book 5 on John, chapter 6;
Nemesius, On Human Nature, chapter 35; Cyril of Jerusalem, On the State of
Man, Catechesis 4; Vincent of Lérins, For the Antiquity of the Catholic Faith,
chapter 17.]*

This is the second difference between us and the defenders of astronomical
or Manichaean fate. We sin not by a necessity of the nature created by God,
but by a necessity of corruption innate from the first sin and acquired from
outside. That follower of Pelagius, Nicolaas Grevinchovius, dared to impute to
us a Manichaean necessity of sinning. But Euodius the Manichaean said, “We
live or act not by will but by necessity.” “It belongs to the Manichaeans,”
says Jerome, “to condemn human nature and take away free choice.”

*[Marginal note and references: Second difference between us and the
defenders of astronomical or Manichaean fate; Grevinchovius, Against Ames,
pp. 61–62; Augustine, On Free Choice, book 3, chapter 3; Jerome, preface
against the Pelagians.]*

3. The proponents of astronomical fate bound God himself by their fate. We
maintain that God, utterly free in all his external operations, acts without
any necessity. Herodotus says in a [doubtful Greek reading], “It is impossible
even for a god to escape the destined lot.” And Eusebius says in another
[doubtful Greek reading], and so forth: “To such a degree do the high-born
gods fear tightly knotted fate that they confess themselves unable to bring
aid even when their own temples have been seized by heavenly

*[Marginal note and references: Third difference between us and the defenders
of mathematical fate; Herodotus, Clio; Eusebius, Preparation for the Gospel,
book 6; ‘to such a degree tightly knotted’; Against the Pelagians.]*

[PDF p. 159; printed p. 108]

fire.” Yet it is easy to prove that the Jesuits and Arminians bind God with a
truly most fatal fate. For all contingent future things are absolutely able
to be or not to be, so that they might be truly knowable and might determine
and ground the certainty of divine knowledge. These connections are
determinately true before God’s middle knowledge and before God’s conditional
decree:

“If the Sabeans plunder Job, then Job will humbly submit himself to God.”
Again: “If the patriarchs sell Joseph, Pharaoh will exalt Joseph gloriously.”
Again: “If Judas betrays the Savior, Judas will end his life by a noose.”
Again: “If Peter and the apostles are thrown into prison and danger of death,
then Peter and the apostles will pray, refer the life they have received to
God, and give thanks.” Again: “If three thousand people hear Peter preaching,
those three thousand people will be converted to faith in Jesus Christ.”

All these connections precede God’s decree and are true in themselves by
nature before God determines and decrees the truth of the connection, because
they are the object of middle knowledge. Yet because these connections are
full of grace, mercy, goodness, and God’s freely given love—and indeed some of
them are full of divine justice—God will be necessitated to will, or rather to
decree afterward, will afterward, or approve these just and gracious
connections. And this will occur after they are already going to be future
and to have existence before every decree of God. God will be necessitated by
a fate or fateful necessity which he can in no way prevent, because these
connections are naturally prior to God’s knowledge and decree.

Indeed, says the Jesuit Fasiolus, “Here the created will determines these
things to either alternative; and a decree of God concerning the connected
extremes is indeed granted, but we deny a third decree concerning the
connection itself. For God does not say, ‘I will that Judas should be
converted if he is called in such a manner.’” Hence we have the defendant
yielding in court. For either Job’s patience is connected with the calamities
he suffers from the Sabeans, and Lydia’s conversion is connected with God’s
gracious calling, by its own nature or from the nature of the thing—which the
Jesuits do not say; or by God’s free and gracious will—which this Jesuit
denies here; or at fortune’s discretion—which is atheistic; or by fateful
necessity—which they must necessarily say.

*[Marginal reference and note: Fasiolus, vol. 1, question 14, article 13,
doubt 14, no. 265, and doubt 16, no. 275; the Jesuits and Arminians have bound
God by their own choice and by infallible things [the final wording of this
marginal note is textually doubtful].]*

Why does Joseph give thanks to God for this connection? “If, when imprisoned,
I interpret Pharaoh’s dream, he will establish me as a prince to feed God’s
Church.” Surely he will not give thanks to God, because this connection exists
before and apart from every decree

[PDF p. 160; printed p. 109]

of God (says Fasiolus), but God approves and afterward decrees the connection once
this connection exists. So be it. He cannot fail to will and approve the
existence of a thing which exists apart from his will and decree. These
things, then, exist when God does not will them. Indeed, even supposing that
God by his omnipotence should will to impede them, they occur by a fate that
binds God. Why is this? Because they occur apart from his will. Thus all
adverse things—untimely death, poisons, the burning of houses, wheels,
gallows, nooses, axes, exile, and burning alive—which occur through
contingently acting causes; and all favorable things—honor, glory, courtly
offices, wealth, victory, golden pleasures, a prosperous voyage, magnificent
houses, numerous offspring, and broad companies of friends—which happen
through contingent causes, are future and are to have existence before every
decree of God and, in relation to God, happen by the most fateful fate.

4. “The proponents of astronomical fate,” as the Complutensian College
states, “said that there is a fateful series of causes by which a cause,
connected to another cause, produces the thing from itself. They nevertheless
placed this series and order in the heavenly spheres, positing certain images
and configurations of heaven and the stars under which each thing is born.
The force and power of these is such that nothing can change them.” So taught
Ptolemy and Apuleius, as Albert says. For they divided the members of the
human body into twelve parts according to the number of the twelve signs of
the zodiac, so that each part of the zodiac ruled over some bodily part and
member with such force and necessity that nothing could occur in the human
body which had not been preordained in stars or constellations of this kind.
Therefore they imagined a nativity and a planetary hour of birth which ruled
over all the actions of life. Against this Clement says: “Since God is just
and himself made human nature, how could it be that he placed over us a
nativity opposed to us which would compel us to sin?” Hence come cruel and
unlucky stars, and good and lucky constellations.

*[Marginal note and references: Fourth difference between us and the
proponents of astronomical fate; the Complutensian College, commentary on the
Physics, disputation 13, question 4; Albert, Physics, book 2, tractate 2,
chapter 19; Clement, Recognitions, book 9, heresies 10.2–3.]*

We teach here, 1. that celestial bodies act upon man as a physical body, as a
living thing, and as an animal, but not upon man insofar as he is human, nor
upon his rational soul or free choice. Thus a human being does not exercise
the free actions of acting well or wickedly, speaking freely, walking,
believing, repenting, and praying by the fate or command of a fateful nativity
or planetary birth. 2. We teach that celestial bodies act indirectly, by
concomitance and accidentally, upon the rational soul,

[PDF p. 161; printed p. 110]

because animal and physical inclinations of a physical temperament disposed
in one way or another by celestial bodies incline the free choice in some
measure. Thus we see choleric persons given to anger and phlegmatic persons
exercise even moral actions suited to their temperament, but not by a
necessity of fate. In this sense the prophet forbids us to fear the signs of
heaven or the stars; this was once the superstitious fear of the pagans. “From
this arose the genethliacs,” says Calvin, “and they maintain
that life depends upon the horoscope, that both good and bad fortune can be
foretold from the stars, and that someone born while the sun is in the head or
tail of a certain sign awaits good or bad fortune.” The Chaldeans likewise
foretold for themselves from the stars the prosperous and flourishing
condition of their nation, victories, and the subjugation of nations—a manner
in which reverent fear of God and confidence in prayer would be utterly
extinguished. For this reason Augustine denies that there is any natural
cause of good fortune, and says that God’s providence reigns.

*[Marginal references: Calvin, commentary on Jeremiah 10:2–3; Augustine,
City of God, book 5, chapter 9; Vásquez, on 1.2, vol. 1, disputation 38,
chapter 4; Francesco Silvestri of Ferrara, Against the Gentiles, book 3,
chapter 92; Henry of Ghent, Quodlibet 6, question 10; Pico della Mirandola,
Against Astrology, book 4, chapter 2.]*

3. No accidental effect comes from the fate of the stars, “nor is there any
constellation,” as Vásquez rightly says, “which inclines a person to leave a
house when its collapse is imminent for the reason that the collapse is then
imminent; for that collapse is accidentally conjoined with his departure from
the house.” Ferrara, Henry, and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola may be consulted
and read on this matter.

It is therefore ridiculous that they refer Christ’s spiritual kingdom to his
birth under the new star seen by the Magi in the East. Calvin rightly says
“that the star was wholly extraordinary and arose beyond the customary
course of nature.” And Cajetan says that “it was not a star, but a fiery comet
in the air which led them onward by its progressive motion to Christ” and
rested over Bethlehem where he was born. Thus Gregory of Nyssa, Chrysostom,
Augustine, Isidore, Ambrose, Jerome, and Fulgentius say that “the star was not
the child’s fate, but the child was the star’s fate.” Certainly the child was
the Creator of that star. We consider such a fate impious and execrable.

*[Marginal references: Calvin, commentary on Matthew 2:2; Cajetan,
commentary on Matthew 2:2; Gregory of Nyssa, oration On the Nativity of
Christ; Chrysostom, homily 6 on Matthew; Augustine, Against Faustus, book 2,
chapter 5; Isidore, On the Lord’s Passion, chapter 13; Ambrose, book 2 on
Luke; Jerome, on Isaiah 47; Fulgentius, sermon On Epiphany.]*

The fourth fate is Christian and divine. “Divine fate,” says Bradwardine,
“must undoubtedly be granted. Is it not written at the beginning of creation,
‘God said’—that is, *fated*? This very thing is divine fate and belongs most
especially to the divine will, which is the efficacious cause of things.”

*[Marginal note and reference: Fourth fate; Bradwardine, On the Cause of God,
book 1, chapter 28.]*

[PDF p. 162; printed p. 111]

Augustine says: “All things are established by divine providence. If anyone
attributes them to fate for this reason, because he calls God’s will or divine
power itself by that name, let him retain his opinion and correct his
language.”

*[Marginal reference: Augustine, City of God, book 5, chapter 1.]*

I would hold, however, that this fate should be considered in three ways: 1.
actively; 2. passively; and 3. relatively, insofar as it signifies the middle
relation between the two. Active fate is God himself, insofar as, having
pronounced it, he appoints and decrees all things which are, were, or will be.
Thus God as decreeing is improperly called fate. Passive fate is all things
themselves, whether necessary or contingent, insofar as they have been
established and decreed by God’s pronouncement, word, and decree, as it were,
and fixed and settled by God’s immutable and eternal constitution. In this
sense fate is in things rather than in God, who pronounces and irrevocably
decrees. 3. Fate is taken relatively for the ordered connection of things,
causes, and effects—not considered in itself and in comparison with secondary
causes, for this connection can in that respect be broken and dissolved,
since in very many things it is contingent rather than necessary—but in
relation to God’s decree and to God’s irresistible and immutable constitution
and providence.

*[Marginal references: Genesis 21:1; 24:51; 28:15; Exodus 9:12, 23; 32:13;
Numbers 16:29; 23:19; Deuteronomy 26:19; 6:19; Joshua 21:45; 1 Samuel 25:30;
2 Samuel 3:18; 7:19, 25; 2 Chronicles 6:1.]*

And although the name *fate* is not Christian, the thing itself, understood
in an orthodox sense, cannot be denied. 1. For this is the Holy Spirit’s
utterly invincible inference: “Jehovah pronounced this; Jehovah spoke this;
Jehovah adopted this counsel.” Therefore this or that thing which he
pronounced or concerning which Jehovah adopted counsel, though in itself
utterly contingent—if we may speak this way—is necessarily going to happen.
Or this thing, insofar as it is subject to God’s pronouncement and declaration,
decree and counsel, cannot fail to be. So it is everywhere in sacred
Scripture: “He has thus spoken or pronounced it, and he will do it. Would God
speak and not do it?” Again, “I have spoken and I shall do it.” But God does
not pronounce or say that a thing will be unless he has established by an
immutable decree that it will be: Numbers 23:19. 2. For the things which God
has once decreed to occur, insofar as they are subject to God’s decree, can no
more fail to exist than God’s decree can be rescinded and changed. 3. Since
God’s will is the efficacious cause of all things,

*[Marginal references: 2 Chronicles 18:22; Psalms 50:1; 60:6; Isaiah 1:20;
21:17; 25:8; 40:5; 46:11; Jeremiah 48:8; 51:62; Joel 3:8; Obadiah 18; Micah
4:4; Matthew 3:3; Acts 3:21; Isaiah 14:24–27; Jeremiah 51:29; Isaiah 19:12;
Isaiah 23:18–19; Jeremiah 4:28; 49:20; 50:45; Lamentations 2:8.]*

[PDF p. 163; printed p. 112]

“and works all things according to the counsel of his will,” Ephesians 1:11,
nothing will exist in the nature of things unless its future existence is
necessary, once it is posited that God, who is the first cause of all beings,
decreed that it would be. 4. For the dominion of God’s providence would be
nothing, and there would be no infallible reason in God’s will why Christ’s
bones should not be broken rather than broken; why the apostles should preach
the gospel rather than not preach it; and why the nations should be converted
to the faith rather than not converted—if the existence of things that will
or will not be were not necessarily dependent upon God’s will.

5. For faith, hope, and confidence in God would collapse; there would be no
place for prayers and thanksgiving, and no use for Christian patience and
consolation, if contingently occurring goods and evils, favorable and adverse
things, poverty and wealth, honor and disgrace, joy and sorrow did not
necessarily flow from God’s free will and decree, but could indifferently
befall us or not befall us, just as fickle secondary causes saw fit, apart
from the efficacious determination of the divine will.

*[Marginal references: Augustine, City of God, book 21, chapter 8; new star,
the year 1572; Fonseca, Metaphysics, book 6, chapter 2, question 2,
subquestion 5; Thomas Aquinas, part 1, question 116, article 2; the
Complutensian College, Physics, disputation 13, On Lot and Fate, question 4,
no. 17.]*

6. For things which are necessary by their own nature receive from God’s free
decree a necessity to that which is beyond or contrary to nature. Thus it was
necessary for the Red Sea to be divided because God willed it so, and by this
free decree it happened “that the star of Venus under King Osroes changed its
color, size, shape, and course.” Not only Varro, but also Adrastus, Cyzicenus,
and Dion of Naples, notable mathematicians, called this a *portent*. And as
Augustine says, “When God wills, he changes his laws of nature.” The new
apparition in Cassiopeia occurred by the divine will alone. Hence, contrary to
nature, intense cold frequently occurs while the sun is in the Dog Star, and
violent heat while it is in Capricorn. Thus Fonseca says that “true fate is
nothing other than the infallible and gentle execution of divine providence,
in accordance with the nature of each thing.” Thomas and the Complutensian
College speak likewise.

*[Marginal references: Suárez, On the Work of the Six Days, tractate 1, book
1, chapter 1, section 2, no. 3; Augustine, City of God, book 5, chapter 8;
Seneca, On Benefits and letter 18.]*

Suárez asserts the same. “Calvin,” he says, “and other heretics deny that the
will operates freely because it needs a divine motion which determines it to
act, or certainly because it is subject to the ordering of the divine will by
which all things are so arranged before they occur that they cannot occur or
happen otherwise.” But the man is bold! He clears us of the charge that human
liberty has been taken away and stripped, or else he fastens that stain upon
Augustine. Indeed, the Jesuit adds, “Augustine, drawing from Seneca, explains
the Stoic opinion in such a way that by fate they understood this disposition

[PDF p. 164; printed p. 113]

of the divine will, and he judges that in this sense their opinion should not
be condemned.”

Finally, as I have often admonished, concerning the accord of things which
happen contingently with the invincible decree, I here briefly repeat the
following points. 1. This most friendly association of the creature and its
manner of acting, whether freely or necessarily, with the Creator’s
invincible and supremely efficacious will must not be denied, even though we
little human beings are wholly unable to grasp with the mind or comprehend
with the intellect such a mystery.

*[Marginal note: Propositions concerning the association of God’s decree with
contingent things.]*

2. It is atheistic to deny—and indeed most contradictory to Scripture, God’s
nature and attributes, sound reason, and orthodox theology—that all beings,
future, necessary, contingent, and free things, thoughts, cares, counsels,
events, actions, circumstances, manners of acting, both good and bad fortune
(as they call them), birth and destruction, peace and war, sin and justice,
are subject to God as governor, Lord, decreer, establisher, creator, ruler,
supremely efficacious worker, invincible and irrevocable willer, or
permissively establishing—as occurs in the wicked actions of human beings and
angels—and are governed and determined by him who made the world and governs
it once made.

3. By its implanted elective power, the will cannot fail to accord with and
to furnish an interpretive consent to that very thing which God from eternity
immutably decreed by an external, secret necessity most amicably tempered to
the creature’s disposition. The free manner of acting and the native
character of the will or of a contingently acting cause is no more injured in
attaining its object than if, by an impossibility, we imagined that there
were no providence, no decree, and no determination of God.

4. Indeed, the very manner of acting freely and contingently, no less than the
action itself, is immutably established and determined by God.

5. Nor do these statements conflict with each other, as the adversaries
dream: “Something is future under a disjunction—it can happen and it can fail
to happen”; and, “This is in itself and on the side of the thing necessarily
future,” namely by a qualified necessity. Thus the breaking of Christ’s bones
by wicked murderers, who are more than sufficiently inclined to it and swell
with hatred against Christ, can altogether occur or not occur under a
disjunction: 1. in relation to the free cause, which remains a free cause
whether it bursts forth into this effect

[PDF p. 165; printed p. 114]

so that they break Christ’s bones, or abstains for another cause and motive
which is not in the least religious and pious. 2. But in relation to the
nature of the event itself, either breaking or non-breaking is necessary. For
it is conjunctively impossible that both the breaking of Christ’s bones and
the non-breaking of Christ’s bones should exist at the same time. Yet from
the nature of the thing it is sufficient—and indeed necessary—that only one
or the other exist on the side of the thing, namely, either the breaking of
the bones, in which case non-breaking is impossible by the necessity of
nonexistence or by qualified impossibility if breaking exists, as is known
from logic; or if breaking exists, non-breaking also is necessary from the
hypothesis and in a qualified sense. [The witness appears internally
inconsistent in this last clause, printing *fractio* and then *non-fractio*.]

In exactly the same way, the breaking of Christ’s bones can be contingent in
itself: both in relation to the free cause, whose essence and manner of acting
remain intact whether the breaking exists or does not exist, and with respect
to the nature of the thing itself, which by reason of the disjunction can be
a free effect actually existing or can be a free effect never actually
existing. But in relation to the first cause, which does not give being to
events absolutely and under an indeterminate and changeable disjunction, but
gives them determinate, fixed, and necessary being by a qualified necessity,
non-breaking is necessary in the same way as either part of the disjunction
is necessary on the side of the thing, namely by a qualified necessity. For
everything that is, when it is, necessarily is; and everything future, when
it is future, is necessarily future.

*[Marginal note: Future contingents are determined on the side of the thing
disjunctively, not conjunctively.]*

6. Nothing is easier than to demonstrate that God absolutely and without any
condition decreed and established from eternity each and every thing that
happens. It is easy to establish from Scripture the entire member of the
disjunction that happens. 2. For Scripture argues invincibly from God’s
decree to the necessary existence and futurition of the thing, or to its
nonexistence and non-futurition. Thus Jehovah adopted counsel and decreed
this; therefore the Chaldeans and Medes will be devastated, because Jehovah’s
counsel will stand immutably and irrevocably forever. Consult Isaiah 14:27;
Psalm 33:10–11; Isaiah 46:10–11; Jeremiah 4:28; Ephesians 1:9; Jeremiah 49:20;
51:29. 2. Jehovah of Hosts swore that he would do many things—and the same
reason applies to all—which occur through the ministry of contingently acting
causes. Therefore he decreed absolutely that he would bring those contingent
things into effect: Isaiah 14:24; Hebrews 6:16–18; Genesis 26:3; 24:7; Exodus
13:5; 21:13; Joshua 23:13; Numbers 14:16, 23, 29; 32:10–11; Deuteronomy 1:8;
9:5; 26:3; Joshua 21:43–44; Psalm 95:11.

*[Marginal note: Future contingents are determined by God from eternity.]*

[PDF p. 166; printed p. 115]

The reason for the inference is that whatever God swore he would do, he
decreed and willed from eternity to do, and that absolutely, with no condition
suspending the act of willing. For it is impossible that there should be an
immanent act in God which has some cause or condition in the creature.

3. The same is demonstrated from God’s promise. For when God promises many
things, and thus wills and decrees to give and externally bring about things
which occur through the ministry of contingent causes, he cannot decree them
on the hypothesis that they will be future—that would be ridiculous—nor on
the hypothesis that free causes will determine themselves to such events or
free effects. For the free determination of the nations to obey Christ’s
calling, of Mary to contract marriage with Joseph, and of Christ’s ancestors
from the seed of Abraham, David, and others to marriage and the procreation of
children, and many other merely contingent things of that kind, are things
promised by God. They therefore cannot be conditions of conditional promises.
The Scriptures cry aloud that such contingent things were promised and thus
absolutely decreed: Genesis 3:16; 9:17; 15:4–5; 22:16–17; Isaiah 54:1–2, 10;
44:1–4; Zechariah 12:10; 12:1–7; Isaiah 64:1–2; Ezekiel 36:26–27;
Deuteronomy 30:6; Ezekiel 11:19–20; Jeremiah 32:33–39; 34:39–40; Psalms 2:8–9;
71:7–12; Hosea 1:10–11; 1 Peter 2:9–10.

4. All predictions which contain contingently future goods or even
punishments and penalties and other things indifferent in themselves were
decreed and absolutely established by God. For God does not predict that this
will or will not be unless he has decreed that it will or will not be. There
are many such things in the Scriptures, as it is written: John 15:25; Matthew
2:5; 26:24; Mark 1:2; Romans 8:36; 1 Corinthians 10:7; John 12:14; 19:36;
Mark 15:27–28; Matthew 27:35.

5. I wholly deny a conditional decree of certain future contingents if the
condition is referred to God’s decreeing will, as though God’s decreeing will
were suspended upon some condition, cause, work, or motive in the creature.
For a will or decree suspended in the proper sense is something ambiguous and
doubtful which presupposes ignorance or a defect of judgment in God; to me
this is blasphemous.

*[Marginal note: In what manner God’s decree concerning future contingents is
absolute, and in what manner conditional.]*

[PDF p. 167; printed p. 116]

For he whose knowledge is infinite establishes everything as fixed,
determinate, and irrevocable. An earthly judge who decrees something if this
or that should occur, but wills nothing decreed and nothing fixed if it should
not occur, can be just in such a decree; but a decree of this kind manifestly
involves a defect of wisdom, knowledge, power, mercy, goodness, and so forth,
which it is impious to think of God. Therefore I add no more to what I have
said elsewhere and to which no answer has been made. For I have demonstrated
from Scripture that such a decree is unworthy of God, and I trust by God’s
grace that against the adversaries I shall fight for and defend this truth of
God to the last breath of my life.

*[Marginal reference: Apologetic Exercises for Divine Grace, exercise 1,
chapter 3, pp. 57–59, and following.]*

I do not, however, deny a conditional decree on the side of the object. God
decreed that Saul’s kingdom would be established if he obeyed; but this decree
is not ambiguous or suspended, because God willed, fixedly, definitively, and
irrevocably, that the establishment of Saul’s kingdom should be linked and
connected with the obedience Saul owed, and God neither retracted nor can
retract this decree. But because God never decreed to produce such obedience
in Saul through efficacious grace, it is properly an absolute and immutably
fixed decree, not a conditional one except in an improper and metonymic manner
of speaking, because only the object is conditional.

Among our people, no one has attacked providence and the immutability of the
divine decrees more obstinately than Thomas Jackson, a fierce follower of
Arminius, in a book written in English on providence, chapters 17 and 20–23.
“In many human actions,” he says in chapter 17, section 2, “God decreed a
mixture and multitude of possible beings. Our meaning is that the eternal
condition of the omnipotent God’s fate”—or word or decree—“from which all
things have both their law and manner of being, so that being itself with
respect to the outcome of possible decrees was decreed not conjunctively and
categorically but disjunctively.” His
reason: “God can conceive and make genuinely disjunctive propositions just as
well as we can, and this decree concerning possibilities under a disjunction
is always fulfilled.”

*[Marginal note: Thomas Jackson, an English follower of Arminius, taught that
all decrees are under a disjunction.]*

I answer: it is absurd to say that God decrees possibilities under a
disjunction, just as it is absurd to say that God decreed that man should be
an animal, because it is impossible for man not to be an animal. In the same
way, it is impossible for possibilities under a disjunction not to be future
or non-future. We must not imagine so

[PDF p. 168; printed p. 117]

suspended and ambiguous a decree in God, by which he established that
Christ’s bones would or would not be broken; that the owner of the donkey
would or would not grant Christ the donkey, according to the indifference of
choice; that the children would or would not cry “Hosanna”; that the soldiers
would or would not divide Christ’s garments; and that the disciples would or
would not desert Christ. 1. For these are simply necessary, on pain of an
eternal contradiction. Christ’s bones must be either broken or not broken,
even if by an impossibility we supposed that there were no providence and no
wise decree.

2. God has something more in his decree than a blind man and blind fortune.
Ignorant and foolish people can decree all contingent things under a
disjunction. Can the demons not answer, I ask, and all the gods and idols of
the nations, concerning future contingents such as Ahab’s future death, the
destruction of the Israelites, Job’s illness, and infinitely many events of
that kind, that all of these will or will not be future? On that account, my
Jackson, are idols, fortune, and human beings gods who can predict future
things as our Jehovah can?

3. From God’s decree Scripture infers that a thing will determinately be, not
that it lies under a suspended and ambiguous disjunction: namely, “The Medes
will devastate the Babylonians,” because Jehovah adopted this counsel. See
Isaiah 14:27; Psalm 33:10–11; Isaiah 46:10–11; Jeremiah 49:20. But he did not
decree that the Medes either would or would not devastate the Babylonians. On
Jackson’s account, therefore, 1. God decreed nothing fixed or certain; 2. he
can predict nothing certain from those suspended and disjunctive decrees; and
3. God can establish no material or support for human hope, faith, patience,
or consolation concerning future things, since God decreed either to afflict
you by the instruments of the Devil or not to afflict you, either to feed you
or not to feed you, and either to deliver you from the force of the sword or
not to deliver you.

Finally, because Jehovah knows that discretive propositions will be true and
will be fulfilled or have been fulfilled, it does not follow that God decrees
them. He knows it to be true that he is the omniscient, just, and merciful
God. Did he therefore freely decree that he would or would not be the
omniscient God, and that he would be just or not just? For not everything God
knows immediately falls under God’s free decree. He knows that twice three is
six, but he did not decree that twice three is six.

Jackson objects in the same place: “God’s decree with respect to the whole
and to every

[PDF p. 169; printed p. 118]

part of its proper object is equally omnipotent. Therefore it is just as
impossible for any necessity”—under any pretext or in relation to any
cause—“to creep into such contingent events, whose manner and law, as regards
their production, God decreed would be contingent, as it is for necessary
effects to be made contingent.”

I answer, 1. God indeed decreed that some causes would act contingently and
others necessarily. But the question concerns the nature of a contingently
acting cause. The Jesuits say that for the will to act contingently is for the
will to be absolutely able to act or not to act. But this must be proved, not
assumed without proof. For we teach that God’s determination, which restricts
the will to one side of a contradiction, does not in the least take away the
nature of contingency, but is entirely consistent with it, provided that it
acts in such a way that, in relation to the secondary cause, not the first, it
can act in this way or not—in the divided sense, not in the composite sense.
