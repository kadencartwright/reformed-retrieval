### QUESTION VI

#### Of What Period Does Daniel Speak in Chapter 11, from Verse 36 to the End?

We shall not raise more questions concerning Daniel than this one. Yet there
are very diverse opinions about it, both recent and ancient. Our principal
aim is to oppose the fantasies of the millenarians, who take from this passage
as well a principal support for those dreams of theirs by which, to our great
harm, they recently stirred up the British people to expect—and, so far as it
lay in them, to promote—the greatest changes in civil governments throughout
the world. They firmly believed that, once Antichrist and the Turk had been
abolished, the Jews converted, and the Saints among the nations had advanced
far beyond every previous reformation of the Church, the fifth monarchy of
the whole world would be delivered into their hands.

> Marginalia, printed p. 264: *The Fifth Monarchists*.

Leaving the others aside, we shall consider only five principal opinions. The
first is that of the Jews; the second, Calvin’s; the third, Brightman’s and the
millenarians’; the fourth, that of the Roman Catholics and also of many
Protestants; and the fifth, finally, that of Junius. This last is the common
opinion of Protestants and the one we embrace.

The Jews’ understanding may be seen in Jacchiades’ commentary on this passage,
from which we make the following observations. First, the Jews rightly refer
the earlier portion of the chapter to the Greek kings, the Seleucids and the
Lagids. Yet with great want of method they apply many things most badly,
attributing to Antiochus’s son Epiphanes what belongs to Antiochus the Great,
and to Epiphanes’ successor Demetrius what is proper to Epiphanes, while
interjecting several utterly stale fables.

Second. What most clearly concerns Epiphanes in verse 31 and those following,
they apply, contrary to the truth of history, to Titus Vespasian, as far as
verse 37.

Third. With skipping fickleness they leap from Titus to Constantine the Great
and, with the greatest blasphemy, attribute to him all the impieties of
Antiochus Epiphanes because of his faith in Jesus Christ.

Fourth. Not content with having transformed Antiochus, the king of the north,
into Constantine in verse 40, they transform both the kings of the south and
of the north into one Turk, whom they make emperor of the whole world. Yet
they exempt the Edomites, Moabites, and Ammonites from his dominion.

Finally, altogether in accordance with the thinking of the English
millenarians, they affirm from verses 44 and 45 that, because of a rebellion
of the Jews in the north and east, the Turks will gather their forces in
Judea, pitch camp there, and be defeated and destroyed there, so that the
monarchy of the world may at last pass to the Jews. We shall not linger over
refuting these ravings; to have indicated them is to have refuted them.

> Marginalia, printed p. 264: *Points to note in the Jews’ opinion*.

Calvin’s opinion is the second: that from verse 36 to the end the prophet
speaks about the Romans, in the period after Antiochus Epiphanes but not beyond
the birth of Christ. In this opinion we approve his meaning that Antiochus
Epiphanes is indicated in the verses before 36. We also approve his intent
that no periods after Christ’s birth are marked at the end of the chapter,
and that he not only excludes but also explodes the fables concerning the
Turks and the monarchy of the Jews.

But in this commentary of Calvin’s we reject several excessively subtle
points, in which the words of the text are turned from Antiochus to the Romans
not without some twisting. Look at verse 36.

From it I argue as follows. First. The king in verse 36 is the same one who
was treated in the preceding verses. There is no sign in the text of a change
of persons; indeed, the demonstrative pronoun ה prefixed to “king” appears
expressly to establish that precisely the same king is meant there—the one
who alone was being discussed in many preceding verses. Now Calvin grants
that the preceding verses speak of Antiochus Epiphanes, not of the Romans.

Second. Even if we are unwilling to contend about words, the name “king” is
far more fittingly attributed to Antiochus, who was one person and truly a
king, than to the Roman senate and people, especially at a time when the name
of king had become an abomination to them.

Third. Although in their pride the Romans did very many things in the
provinces according to their pleasure, at that time the tyranny of Antiochus,
especially against the Jews, was far more lawless and arbitrary than that of
the Romans. For the Roman senate at that time proceeded with some appearance
of law and kindness toward provincials who had not provoked it by any injury.
But Antiochus’s cruelty toward his subjects, and especially toward the Jews,
was so monstrous and insolent that this exceedingly hot-headed man justly
acquired among everyone the infamous name *Epimanius*, that is, “mad and
insane.”

Fourth. Although the Romans were very impious and preferred their own desire
to all the gods, and although Marcus Tullius and other Roman writers belched
out various taunts and blasphemies against the God of Israel, they were far
surpassed in this atheism by Antiochus Epiphanes. For he commanded all the
nations in his empire to cast away all their ancestral gods on pain of death
and to worship the foreign gods of the Greeks. He was so blasphemous against
the God of Israel that all the sacred books were to be destroyed in the
flames

[Printed p. 265 | IA leaf 295 | access PDF 295 | OCR object 294]

at his command, and he would not permit even the slightest worship of Jehovah
to be celebrated anywhere. Nothing similar was ever perpetrated by the Romans
anywhere in the world before the birth of Christ, much less among the Jews.

Fifth. The king of whom Daniel speaks in that verse acted prosperously until
God’s wrath against the Jews was completed. Then divine wrath was turned from
the Jews against that king. This was manifestly seen in Antiochus, but by no
means in the Romans. For until the persecution of the Jews had reached its
height, everything in Judea and all Syria flowed according to Epiphanes’ wish.
But once the Jews had drunk the cup of divine wrath, all Epiphanes’ affairs
immediately began to go backward until the divine hand shattered him most
miserably. As for the Romans, they never enjoyed a more prosperous fortune
than after the destruction of Jerusalem and the Jews’ final dispersion. But
if you interpret the wrath to be completed as the divine vengeance upon the
Jews, which lasts to the present day and will last until their conversion,
Roman prosperity certainly did not continue that long. For many centuries
ago the Roman people were broken by every kind of calamity and truly
destroyed. Those who now stable themselves in the corpse of the ancient city
have no connection whatsoever with the blood of the ancient Romans.

From verses 37, 38, and 39 we argue as follows against the opinion before us.
The king of whom the prophet speaks there despised his ancestral gods. But
Antiochus did this more than the Romans. Under pain of death he forbade the
worship both of all the Syrian gods whom his forefathers, the kings of Syria,
had worshiped in a long succession and of the God of Israel, whom his
ancestors had often honored with many gifts and sacrifices. The god whose
worship he chiefly professed was foreign to him—Jupiter Olympius. Although he
was worshiped as an ancestral god by the Athenians and by certain
Peloponnesians who lived by the city Olympia near Elis in Achaea, neither
Mount Olympus, the city Olympia, nor Jupiter Olympius had any such relation
to the Macedonians. Alexander, to be sure, from a peculiar superstition—or
was it ambition?—cultivated Jupiter Ammon, but by no means Olympius, although
his mother was Olympias. As for the Romans, their superstition was so great
that they summoned into their city almost all the gods of their neighbors,
but never removed any of their own gods from the city before Christian times.

Second. Calvin’s observation about the Romans’ contempt for women in their
frequent divorces gives that nation no crime peculiar to it rather than to
others. Antiochus, however, can by singular right be called a despiser of
women. He was so far from being moved by the Jewish women’s lamentations and
monstrous cries that he suspended very many of them from high trees together
with their circumcised infants fastened to their mothers’ breasts.

Third. The god whom the Romans worshiped above all others was Jupiter
Capitolinus, in reality the same as Antiochus’s Jupiter Olympius. But to the
Romans he was not a foreign god, but especially their native god and oldest
father. For the first enrolled fathers themselves, soon after the city’s
foundation, were laying the foundations of Jupiter’s temple on the Tarpeian
Hill when they found the buried head of a certain Tolus. From this they gave
the Tarpeian rock the name Capitolium, whereas the hill had previously been
called Tarpeian after Tarpeia, the Vestal virgin whom the Sabines killed there
by crushing her with their shields. Thus from their city’s earliest infancy
and the Capitolium’s first naming, the Romans acknowledged Jupiter Capitolinus
as the highest of the gods. Therefore he was never a foreigner at Rome, but
always a citizen and foremost of its civic gods.

From verses 40, 41, 42, and 43 I oppose the following. First. The Egyptians
made war upon the king of whom Daniel speaks there. But after Antiochus
Epiphanes the Egyptians never made war upon the Romans. Such was Roman power
and Egyptian weakness in those centuries that the latter willingly lay hidden
under the former’s protection and never rebelled. As for Cleopatra’s arms
against Caesar Augustus, they were taken up under Antony’s auspices and Roman
eagles on behalf of the Roman commonwealth, as though against the tyranny of a
faction introduced into the commonwealth by force and artifice.

Second. The one of whom Daniel speaks was the king of the north against whom
the southern Egyptians fought. But the title “king of the north” does not
belong to the Romans; it belongs rightly to Antiochus, by Calvin’s own
judgment, for he admits that in the preceding verses Antiochus is indicated by
the name “king of the north.” Calvin indeed wishes the king of the north here
to be understood as the kings Mithridates of Bithynia and Tigranes of Armenia,
but without reason. They did indeed fight against the Romans, but by no means
against the king of the south. Yet here, just as often in the preceding
verses, Daniel sets the king of the south and the king of the north against
one another as mortal and hereditary enemies.

Third. After defeating Egypt, the king of whom Daniel speaks there subjugated
Judea, but not Edom, the Moabites, or the Ammonites. These things fit
Antiochus, as is clear from the histories of Josephus and the Maccabees, but
they do not fit the Romans. For the Romans did not cross from Egypt into
Judea. Egypt was reduced to a province by Augustus long after Judea and Syria
had been subjugated by Pompey. As for Edomites, Ammonites, or Moabites having
been free from the Roman yoke, or Ethiopia and Libya having been subject to
the Romans

[Printed p. 266 | IA leaf 296 | access PDF 296 | OCR object 295]

before the birth of Christ, I do not know which of the ancients testifies to
it.

Finally, from verses 44 and 45 we make the following objections. First. The
king of whom Daniel speaks was drawn away from Egypt by adverse reports from
the north and east. This is true of Antiochus, not the Romans. His final
campaign after the third invasion of Egypt was against Artaxias the Armenian
in the north and the eastern Persians. The Romans indeed suffered great loss
at the hands of the Parthians in Crassus’s defeat, but were not disturbed by
the news of this defeat, nor at that time had they set a hostile foot in
Egypt.

Second. Moved by those sinister reports, the king of whom Daniel speaks there
invaded the east and north with great force. But before the birth of Christ
the Romans moved no arms against the Parthians to avenge Crassus. Antony’s
incursions against the Armenian, undertaken on his own initiative without any
mandate from the senate and accomplished with great loss to himself and his
allies, were of little moment.

Third. The king of whom Daniel speaks there pitched his tents in the mountains
of Judea. Antiochus did this, but the Romans did not. While Antiochus went into
Persia with half his army, he sent Lysias, his prefect of Syria, into Judea
with his son Eupator and the other half of the army, an immense number of
soldiers. There at Emmaus in the mountains of Judea not far from
Jerusalem, between the two seas—the Mediterranean and the Dead Sea—Lysias
pitched Antiochus’s royal pavilions, as the books of Maccabees and Josephus
testify. Calvin attributes these things to the Romans only by asserting that
their dominion and tents were fixed from the Euxine Sea as far as the Persian,
Egyptian, and Red Seas. But this is not to pitch tents on the holy mountain of
glory, or in Judea between two seas.

Finally. While the king of whom Daniel speaks there advanced toward the north
and east, and while he also had a royal army in Judea, he was so struck by the
divine hand that, with no human power able to aid him, he perished miserably.
Everyone knows this is true of Antiochus; no one knows it of the Romans. These
and so many other arguments against Calvin are obvious that he has few
followers of his opinion here, if any.

Brightman follows in the third place, the leader of the millenarians here,
from whom the rest borrow their principal arguments against the common
opinion. Like Calvin, he interprets verses 36 through 40 of the Romans, but in
so different, distorted, and peculiar a sense that his own disciples have not
followed him thus far. Yet from verse 40 to the end he deserts Calvin. By the
king of the south he understands the Saracens, and by the king of the north
the Turks, whom he thinks Daniel here pronounces will at last be destroyed by
the Jews in the land of Canaan.

> Marginalia, printed p. 266: *Arguments against Brightman*.

Most of the points we brought against Calvin also count against Brightman, but
in addition we make the following objections peculiar to him. He interprets
the king in verse 36 who exalts himself above all gods as the Romans exalting
themselves above kings and magistrates. We grant that Scripture sometimes
understands magistrates by the word “gods,” but by no means in this passage.
For Brightman himself is forced to interpret the “God of gods” that follows
in that verse as the heavenly God. Therefore, the immediately preceding words
also speak of that same God, not earthly gods. Furthermore, the Romans did not
at that time, or ever, exalt themselves above all the kings of the world, but
only above some neighboring kings whom they had subdued. These were few in
comparison with the rest of the world’s kings, with whom the Romans had no
dealings at all.

Brightman interprets verse 37 of the Roman emperors, who overthrew all their
ancestral gods or magistrates and, contrary to their wives’ desire, neglected
to leave the empire as an inheritance to their children. Who will swallow
these audacious novelties? Why does he skip from the Roman senate to the
emperors contrary to Calvin? Then, so far were the emperors from despising the
ancient magistracies that for many centuries they eagerly displayed both the
names and the functions of dictators, consuls, pontiffs, tribunes of the
people, and other supreme magistrates. Inferior magistrates, meanwhile,
discharged their offices without hostility or hindrance under the emperors
down to late posterity, just as they had formerly under the senate. All the
emperors, moreover, no less of their own motion than from their wives’ wishes,
were most eager to leave the imperial scepter to their sons when they had
them, and, when sons were lacking, to their nearest kinsmen.

He interprets verses 38 and 39 of Pompey, who offered sacrifices in the temple
at Jerusalem, and especially of Caesar Augustus, who through the service of
his client Herod adorned the Jerusalem temple with the greatest resources,
gold, silver, and jewels. But truly, did any mortal ever dream such
commentaries either before or after Brightman? The prophet certainly means
that the king whom he discusses acknowledged that foreign god and worshiped
him with his own resources, not those of another. But did Augustus Caesar,
much less Pompey, ever acknowledge the God of Israel? Or did he spend a single
penny of his own upon his worship? Away with such trifles! They are

[Printed p. 267 | IA leaf 297 | access PDF 297 | OCR object 296]

so extravagant that Brightman’s principal followers, Parker and Huit, reject
them. Each turns from the Roman empire to the Roman Antichrist, but with this
difference: Parker introduces Antichrist from verse 21, thereafter wholly
excluding Antiochus, and indeed the Roman empire; Huit, however, admits
Antiochus down to verse 36, and from there introduces the Roman emperors
together with Antichrist, although in verses 38 and 39 he means Antichrist
alone, to the exclusion of every other.

There is greater agreement in the later verses, for Brightman, Huit, and
Parker understand the Saracens by the king of the south. They inflicted great
evils upon the Roman empire, or the king of the north.

Against this commentary I argue from the text itself as follows. First. The
king of the south takes up arms at the end of times. But the Saracens’ arms
were first moved against the Roman empire under Muhammad in the year of Christ
630. The end of either the Roman emperors or pontiffs was then far distant.
More than a thousand years have now passed since that time, and yet no end of
either the Roman empire or the Roman Antichrist has appeared.

Second. Daniel’s king of the south is attacked by the arms of the king of the
north. But the Saracens were not attacked by the arms of the Turks, whom they
repeatedly take to be the kingdom of the north.

Third. If we believe Parker, verse 21 repeatedly sets out the arms of the
Saracens and Turks against the Roman pope. Why then, as Huit puts it, would
the warmed-over cabbage be served again afterward?

In the final verses they hold that the arms of the Turks are described
together with their destruction at the hands of the Jews. We cannot approve
this commentary. First. Because it is new and exceedingly peculiar.

Second. It expounds the greatest changes of empires in so many clear details
and circumstances far more plainly than prophecies of this kind are
accustomed to be narrated before the event.

Third. Audacious conjectures of this kind about future events are as easily
rejected as asserted.

Fourth. If we believe Parker, the king of the north warring against the king
of the south is the Roman Antichrist in his holy war against the Saracens. But
if we believe Brightman and Huit, it is the Turk fighting against the Mamluks
in Egypt. These two commentaries cannot stand together.

Fifth. The Turks subjugated the Mamluks’ Egypt before Judea, although on their
first passage they grievously ravaged Syria and Judea, and the Edomites,
Moabites, and Ammonites did not escape the hands of the Turks on the approach
to Arabia. But Daniel says everything contrary to this concerning his king of
the north.

Finally, they hold that the reports from the north and east are news of an
armed insurrection by northern and eastern Jews converted to Christianity;
that the encampment of the king of the north on the holy mountain is the
Turkish army in Judea; and that his destruction by God is the fifth monarchy
given by God to the Jews—and all this, according to these authors, in the very
year of Christ 1650. All these things are the purest rubbish, founded upon no
testimony and no sound reason. Time itself has now inflicted upon them the
blush they deserved. Yet the millenarians wish to persist in causing trouble,
and when one course has failed, they continually take other paths in their
attempt to prop up their fantasies.

The fourth opinion is that of those who interpret this passage of Daniel as
Antichrist, excluding Antiochus entirely. This is the position of Jerome and
Theodoret, and of almost all the Roman Catholics; also of Luther, Bullinger,
and other Protestants. We do not wish to dispute against this opinion here,
both to avoid becoming more prolix and because it is quite pleasing to us that
many of Daniel’s words here should be applied to Antichrist. What displeases
us is that Antiochus should be entirely excluded as a type of Antichrist,
rather than that both type and antitype should be included, as the ordinary
gloss and various interpreters do.

> Marginalia, printed p. 267: *Against the Roman Catholics*.

Huit denies that Antiochus can be called a type of Antichrist because all
types refer to Christ alone, but this is excessively subtle. For what
theologian of any party would deny that Pharaoh is a type of the devil,
Solomon’s temple a type of the Christian Church, and earthly Jerusalem a type
of the city above? And if someone more scrupulous rejects the word “type”
after straining it, call Antiochus an image, exemplar, and living likeness of
Antichrist.

Furthermore, some of Daniel’s verses are so difficult to accommodate to
Antichrist that Luther and Graser resort to subtler allegories than Parker
himself is able to digest. The Roman Catholics indeed embrace this passage as
speaking of Antichrist alone all the more readily after the Fathers, because
they hope by this means more easily to evade our arguments concerning the
pope’s Antichristianism. Yet Luther, and Polanus especially, bring forth from
this very passage as applied to Antichrist many difficult and pressing
arguments which demonstrate that the Roman pontiff is *the Antichrist*.

The final opinion is that of those who interpret the whole passage of
Antiochus Epiphanes, yet without denying that it can be accommodated to the
Roman pontiff. This position

[Printed p. 268 | IA leaf 298 | access PDF 298 | OCR object 297]

is that of Pellicanus, as well as Junius, Polanus, Rollock, Piscator, Diodati,
all the English interpreters, and—to one’s surprise—the Jesuits Maldonatus,
Sanctius, and A Lapide. In support they cite Chrysostom and others who expressly
interpret this passage not of Antichrist but of Antiochus. The following are
the reasons for the affirmative. First. In the preceding verses the prophet
treats Antiochus Epiphanes, and there is no sign at all in the text that verse
36 even faintly suggests a change of person.

Parker, apparently overcome by this argument, concedes that the prophet must
be interpreted from verse 36 onward as speaking of the same person of whom
the preceding verses speak. Parker indeed holds that in the preceding verses,
namely from verse 21, Daniel speaks of the Roman Antichrist and by no means of
Antiochus. But in this he stands alone, having, so far as I know, neither an
authority nor a follower for his opinion.

Second. The demonstrative article ה, which they call the marking article,
ordinarily marks the subject previously treated, and does so with some
emphasis. Therefore, “the king” mentioned in this passage, having this marking
article prefixed to it, ought not without necessity be taken for a king other
than the one of whom it is known and clear that Daniel spoke in the preceding
verses.

Third. Daniel speaks of the person to whom the history of the deeds recounted
in that passage corresponds. But that entire history corresponds more
correctly to Antiochus Epiphanes than to anyone else, as will be clear both
from what has been said and from what is to be said.

Fourth. It is incongruous to break off the prophet’s narrative and interrupt
it with a gap of very many centuries. Yet the opinion we refute does this, for
it holds that the prophet leaps immediately from Antiochus’s persecution to
the last period of Antichrist’s persecutions and to Antichrist’s destruction
at the very end of the world, omitting the principal fortunes of the Church
that have occurred during nearly two thousand years from the days of
Antiochus to the present.

Fifth. Antiochus Epiphanes made a third campaign into Egypt after the second
one, from which the Romans deterred him. For verse 29 implies three campaigns
of Antiochus into Egypt. In the first and last his affairs prospered; in the
middle one they went worse for him. After verse 36 the prophet deals with
this third campaign, which was happier than the second. The chief question
here will concern the correct translation of verse 29. These are the Hebrew
words: ולא תהיה כראשונה וכאחרונה. Pagninus translates, “And it shall not be
as before and so afterward.” More correctly, the Septuagint interpreters give
it word for word without any change: οὐκ ἔσται ὡς ἡ πρώτη καὶ ἡ ἐσχάτη,
as if to say, “this second campaign will not be as successful as either the
first or the last.” So our common Bibles read, “But it shall not be as the
former, or as the latter,” here following Junius: “But it shall not be as the
earlier campaign or as the later.” Piscator also follows him: “But it will not
happen as on the first occasion or as on the other occasion.” Diodati likewise
has, “But the matter will not succeed as on the first and the last occasion.”
Brightman acknowledges that according to this translation there must have
been three campaigns of Epiphanes into Egypt. But to evade the force of the
argument, he contends that Junius’s translation is false on this basis: that
when the adverb כ, the sign of comparison, is repeated, the first must always
be translated with “as” and the latter with “so.” He proves this with three
examples and, from this hypothesis, translates the passage as follows: “and
it shall not be as before, so also afterward,” thereby avoiding the necessity
of a third campaign.

But this argument of Brightman’s does not appear sound. We acknowledge that in
the passages he cites and in several others, when *caph* is repeated, the
latter is to be translated, as he puts it, responsively by “so,” not
propositively, while the first is translated by “as.” But we deny that this
can be done either always or for the most part, and especially in the passage
before us. For in this passage, besides *caph*, the conjunction *vau* precedes
the adverb *caph*. Brightman and the interpreters he follows here either omit
it entirely, translating, “and it shall not be as before, so afterward,” or
place the conjunction after the adverb as follows: “and it shall not be as
before, so and afterward.” Each of these corrupts the original text. Junius
therefore rightly recalls the Latin versions to the original here, and
Brightman wrongly attempts to correct Junius’s version.

I admit that Piscator, who follows Junius here, opens another escape for
evading our argument. He acknowledges three campaigns of Antiochus from this
passage, but holds that two preceded the one which the Roman legate Popilius
Laenas prevented. But this commentary—although the third English Annotators
embrace it far too rashly—is empty and frivolous. For in the verses before
the twenty-ninth, no campaign of Antiochus into Egypt appears apart from one;
nor do any approved authorities place two before Antiochus’s encounter with
Popilius. Indeed, Brightman and his followers, with whom alone we are dealing
here, hold that there was no more than one.

Many sufficiently difficult objections are raised against this opinion of
ours concerning Antiochus. We answered most of them above when we disputed
against Calvin. The principal additions Brightman, Huit, and Parker take from
Graser are as follows.

First. No authorities mention any war of Antiochus against the Egyptians
after Popilius Laenas compelled him to depart from Egypt, nor did he have any
war either in the east or in the north shortly before his death. Response.
Although the English Annotators, Polanus, and others repeat word for word on
Junius’s authority—though suppressing his name—what Junius brings from
Zonaras, Livy, Florus, and Justin, yet when I examine and reexamine those
historians, I find nothing in any of them that contributes anything to our
case. These things do, however, occur in other authorities.

Cornelius Tacitus in the fifth book of his *Histories*, as Grotius correctly
cites him, expressly relates that after his wars in Egypt and Judea Antiochus
Epiphanes fought in the east and north, namely against the Parthians, and was
prevented by this Parthian war from wholly destroying the Jews’ religion.
These statements cannot be interpreted of the much later expedition of
Antiochus Sidetes against the Persians, as our opponents wrongly attempt to
evade them, nor can they be understood of any Antiochus other than Epiphanes,
and that shortly before his death, after all his campaigns against the
Egyptians and Jews.

Furthermore, Appian in his *Syriaca*, as Ussher correctly cites him with
Grotius, affirms Antiochus’s victory against Artaxias the Armenian, the king
of the north.

Finally, in Jerome on this passage we read Porphyry asserting that Epiphanes,
in the penultimate year of his reign, which followed the two aforesaid
campaigns into Egypt, moved arms against Philometor but was forced to divert
them against new enemies in the east and north. Jerome does not reject these
statements of Porphyry, although he deservedly censures him there on many
other points.

They object, second, that the king of the south in verse 40 provokes the king
of the north, who sweeps through the whole southern region like a whirlwind.
But Philometor never provoked Epiphanes, and after the Romans ordered
Antiochus to keep away from Egypt he never dared invade it; nor does any
ancient historian mention anything of the sort about him. Response. The
history of those centuries concerning events in the east and south is very
meager. Indeed, the sources even of Roman and Greek history—Polybius, Livy,
and Dionysius of Halicarnassus—have almost entirely perished. Josephus and
the Maccabees also touch upon very little concerning Syria and Egypt, while
the slightly earlier authors who wrote in great numbers in that very century
have perished altogether. If their narratives from which we have cited
Porphyry, Appian, and Tacitus survived, what now lies hidden would be openly
known. Epiphanes, certainly, had been taught by his inward misfortune always
to fear the Romans greatly. Yet despite this fear he dared to invade Egypt
more than once under various pretexts. After his second campaign, from which
the Roman legate drew him back, a new discord between the brothers—by which
Physcon was forced to flee Egypt and finally to appear at Rome to beg for aid
against his brother Philometor—seemed to open a broad gate for Antiochus. By
it, as though to assist Physcon and not displease the Romans, he might invade
Philometor a third time.

The third objection. Before verse 36 the history is clearly that of Epiphanes;
therefore in the later verses someone other than Epiphanes is treated.
Response. If with the Jesuit Sanctius we say that the prophet repeats himself
to relate more clearly what he had presented more briefly above—as is quite
customary in Holy Scripture—we shall escape the preceding difficulties about
Epiphanes’ third campaign into Egypt after the second, from which he was
repulsed by Roman authority. But leaving this aside, we deny that Epiphanes’
history was finished before verse 36. For before that passage nothing was
heard concerning his various deeds in his final years, and especially
concerning God’s most glorious vengeance upon him for his monstrous cruelty
against Israel. To have passed these things over in silence would have
greatly obscured God’s glory and the people’s consolation.

They object, fourth, that the king of whom Daniel speaks did everything
according to his own pleasure, in the manner of a supreme emperor, not a
tyrant. But Epiphanes, besides being a most monstrous tyrant, was prevented by
Maccabaeus from accomplishing many things according to his own pleasure even
in Judea itself. Response. Tyrants act according to their own pleasure no less
than just emperors do, and indeed more than they, since they are more released
by their own pleasure from every restraint of law. Then distinguish places
and times in Antiochus’s case. In Egypt, when the Romans were present, he
accomplished nothing according to his pleasure; but in Syria and Judea he
acted for some time as an absolute lord and tyrant. The prophet does not,
however, speak of every period, nor of the time when the fortitude of Judas
Maccabaeus had put a clasp upon Antiochus’s raging madness.

Fifth. Paul applies this passage of Daniel in 2 Thessalonians 2:4 to
Antichrist. Therefore Daniel speaks about Antichrist, not Antiochus. Response.
There is no inference, for the prophet speaks of Antiochus, even though Paul
applies to Antichrist what Daniel has concerning Antiochus. As for their
reply that Antichrist is not the antitype of Antiochus because Christ alone is
the antitype of all types, we said above that this new doctrine about types is
a paradox contrary to all the interpreters of the passage, both the ancient

Fathers and Protestant and Roman Catholic doctors, who extend types and
antitypes beyond the person of Christ to many other things, and especially to
Antiochus and Antichrist. In any case, even granted that Antiochus is not a
type, he will nevertheless be, as no one will deny, a very living image and
likeness of Antichrist. Certainly, because of their great similarity,
Antiochus’s attributes can be applied to Antichrist by the apostle or anyone
else.

Finally. Antiochus did not pitch his tents, nor did he die, on the holy
mountain of glory. Response. The prophet does not speak there about the place
of Antiochus’s death, but only about his tents. Antiochus is rightly said to
have pitched his tents there, when Lysias, his legate of Syria, at his command
pitched the royal pavilion and surrounded it on the mountain by Jerusalem
with half of the entire Syrian army, in the presence of Eupator, the only son
of Epimanius. The remaining objections are easier, and so we omit them.
