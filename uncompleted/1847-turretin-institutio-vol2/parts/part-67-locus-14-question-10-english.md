QUESTION X.

THE NECESSITY OF SATISFACTION

*Was it necessary for Christ to satisfy divine justice for us? Affirmative,
against the Socinians.*

I. Just as Christ’s priesthood, according to the apostle’s description and
the Levitical foreshadowing, is comprised chiefly of two parts, ἱλασμῷ, or the
*satisfaction* given for us on the cross, and ἐμφανισμῷ, or his *appearance and
intercession* for us in heaven (1 John 2:2; Heb. 9:12), so the doctrine of
*Christ’s satisfaction*, as the primary and most important part of the great
mystery of godliness and the principal foundation of our faith and hope, must
be established above all and vindicated and confirmed against the flaming
darts of Satan with which innumerable adversaries customarily attack it.

II. Whatever concerning it comes into controversy can be referred to five
heads: 1. Its *necessity*. 2. Its *truth*. 3. Its *unity and perfection*. 4.
Its *mode and parts*. 5. Its *object*. These must be treated distinctly in as
many questions.

[Marginal heading: In what sense is satisfaction taken here?]

III. The satisfaction in question here is not taken *broadly* for the simple
and any sort of *repair of an offense*, when someone clears and excuses
himself before one who has suffered some offense. Rather, it is taken
*strictly for the payment of a debt*, by which what another owes is paid and
satisfaction is made to a creditor or judge demanding a debt or penalty.

[Marginal heading: State of the question concerning its necessity.]

IV. There are three principal opinions of theologians concerning the
necessity of satisfaction. The first is of those *who simply deny it*, such
as the Socinians, who not only deny that Christ made satisfaction for us, but
also contend that there was no need for it, since God both can and wills to
remit sins freely without satisfaction (Socinus, *On the Savior* 1.1). The
second is of those *who distinguish absolute and hypothetical necessity*.
With respect to the latter they steadfastly assert against the Socinians the
necessity of satisfaction because God so decreed it, to which they add a
*necessity of fittingness* because it was most fitting that divine justice be
satisfied, lest its precepts be said to be violated with impunity. But with
respect to the former they deny that satisfaction was absolutely necessary,
because if it had pleased God, other possible modes of deliverance would not
have been lacking to him. Augustine appears to have held this opinion (*On
the Trinity* 13.10), as did some of our theologians, especially those who
wrote before Socinus. The third is of those *who affirm it*, who establish
absolute necessity in such a way that God not only did not will to remit our
sins without satisfaction, but could not have done so consistently with
justice. This is the common opinion of the orthodox, which we follow.

V. Because the adversaries err in many ways here by perversely explaining our
opinion, certain foundations must first be laid in advance, from which the
whole doctrine of Christ’s satisfaction can be more readily understood and
great light shed both on this question and those which follow. These concern
either the *sin* for which satisfaction was required, the *satisfaction*
itself which had to be given, *God* to whom satisfaction had to be rendered,
or *Christ* who had to make satisfaction.

VI. First, *sin*, which draws guilt after it, by which we are rendered hateful
to God and constituted debtors to punishment, can be viewed under a threefold
σχέσει: either as a *debt* by which we are bound to divine justice for its
payment, in which sense the law is called a handwriting (Col. 2:14); or as
*enmity*, through which, not

[Printed p. 369 | Internet Archive PDF 397 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

only are we θεοστυγεῖς, “haters of God,” but God himself hates us and regards
us with indignation; or as a *crime*, by which we are guilty of death and
eternal curse before God, the ruler of the world and supreme judge. Hence
sinners are sometimes called *debtors* (Matt. 6:12), sometimes ἐχθροὶ,
*enemies* of God, both actively and passively (Col. 1:21), and sometimes
*guilty* and ὑπόδικοι τῷ Θεῷ (Rom. 3:19). Hence it is gathered that three
things were required for our redemption: that the debt of sin be paid, that
God’s hatred and wrath be appeased, and that guilt be expiated.

VII. From this, second, the nature of the *satisfaction* which had to be
rendered for sin can be perceived, namely, one in which these three σχέσεις
concur together: the *payment of the debt*, the *appeasement of divine wrath*
through our reconciliation with him, and the *expiation of guilt* through the
endurance of punishment. But just as the chief aspect to be considered in sin
is the σχέσις of *crime*, so satisfaction has the character of a punishment
imposed by the supreme judge.

VIII. Here, however, the twofold *payment* recognized among jurists must be
accurately distinguished. One *liberates the guilty person and debtor by the
very fact* when precisely what is owed under the obligation is paid, whether
by the debtor himself or by another in his name. The other *does not liberate
by the very fact* when not precisely what is owed under the obligation but an
equivalent is paid. Although this does not extinguish the obligation by the
very fact, nevertheless, when it is admitted although it could have been
refused, it is regarded as satisfaction. This is evident in pecuniary and
penal debt. In pecuniary debt, payment of the debt liberates the debtor by the
very fact, whoever makes it, because here attention is paid not to *who pays*,
but only to *what is paid*. Hence, when the creditor has received payment, he
is not said to exercise any indulgence and remission toward the debtor,
because he received as much as was owed. But the rationale is not the same in
penal debt, because the obligation concerns not only the thing but the person;
that is, attention is paid not only to *what is paid*, but also to *who pays*,
namely, that the punishment be borne by the person who sinned. For just as the
law demands proper and personal obedience, so it also exacts such a
punishment. Therefore, for the guilty person to be freed when another’s or a
vicarious satisfaction is supplied, an act of the ruler and judge must
intervene. With respect to the law this is called *relaxation*; with respect
to the debtor and guilty person, *remission*, because his own punishment is
remitted and a vicarious one is admitted and accepted in its place. In this
matter, because sin has the character not only of a debt but also of a crime,
the satisfaction is not of the kind which can liberate the debtor by the very
fact unless the judge’s ἐπιείκεια and remission are added. For precisely what
is owed under the obligation is not paid, namely, the offender’s own
punishment, which the law demanded according to the ἀκριβοδίκαιον, but only a
vicarious punishment. Hence it is readily evident how well *remission* and
*satisfaction* agree here, whatever the adversaries may cry out, because in
the endurance of the punishment borne by Christ there is *satisfaction*, but
in the admission and acceptance of the vicar there is *remission*. The former
regards Christ, from whom God exacts a punishment the same not numerically but
specifically as that which was owed by us. The latter regards believers,
whose own punishment God remits when he admits the vicarious one. Thus there
appears a marvelous tempering of justice and mercy, for justice exerts itself
against sin and mercy toward the sinner. Satisfaction is made to God’s justice
by the sponsor, and remission is granted to us by God.

IX. This is confirmed, third, from the σχέσει of God to whom satisfaction
must be given. For just as there is this threefold σχέσις of sin, as we have
said, so God can be viewed either as creditor, as lord and offended party, or
as judge and ruler. In this matter, although the first two also have their
place, yet the third must be considered chiefly. For God here acts not only as
a creditor who can remit his right at will, nor only as the offended party and
lord who can do what he wills with his own without

[Printed p. 370 | Internet Archive PDF 398 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

injury to anyone, but as *judge and ruler of the universe*, to whom alone
belongs the infliction of punishment or liberation from it. For as all jurists
acknowledge, this belongs only to the supreme magistrate and τῆς ὑπερεχούσης
ἐξουσίας. Although a creditor can demand the debt and an offended party has
the right to demand restitution and compensation for loss, no one except the
judge constituted in authority has the power to compel or take punishment.
This must be properly maintained against the adversaries, whose πρῶτον ψεῦδος
is that sin must be regarded here as a simple debt and God as a mere creditor
who can exact or remit punishment at his discretion without satisfaction. But
it is certain that God here assumes the σχέσιν of judge and ruler of the world,
who upholds the right of majesty and professes himself guardian and avenger of
the laws. Thus he possesses not only the right of credit or dominion, which he
can or cannot use at will, but the *right of government* and punishment, which
is natural and indispensable.

X. But here again *the punishment itself* which the judge exacts must be
accurately distinguished from the *mode and circumstances of the punishment*,
for the rationale of the two is not the same. As regards punishment itself in
general, God’s right in this respect is plainly indispensable, being founded
in God’s justice itself. But as regards the mode and circumstances of this
punishment, that it be exacted from such a person and in such a manner, it is
not likewise a matter of natural right, but of positive and free right. For a
certain ἐπιείκεια can be admitted here from the goodness of the most wise God:
with respect to *time*, by delaying punishment; with respect to *degrees*, by
mitigating it; or with respect to *persons*, by substitution. Although the
sinning person altogether deserves punishment and can justly be punished, it
is nevertheless not so necessary and indispensable that, for certain weighty
reasons, the punishment cannot be transferred to a surety. In this sense
theologians say that punishment must necessarily be inflicted
*impersonally* upon every sin, but not immediately *personally* upon every
sinner, since by singular grace God can exempt some from it by substituting a
surety in their place.

XI. For us to conceive that God can accomplish this, he must be regarded not
as an inferior and subordinate judge constituted under the law who could not
relax the law’s rigor by transferring the punishment to another, but as the
supreme and ἀνυπεύθυνος judge. Just as he wills his justice to be satisfied by
the punishment of sin, so according to his supreme wisdom and mercy he could
relax the law’s ἀκριβοδικαίᾳ by exempting sinners from the punishment due and
transferring it to a sponsor. Hence it is evident to whom this satisfaction
had to be paid: to the devil who detained us, as Socinus cavils, or to God the
supreme judge? Since the devil here is only God’s minister and the keeper of
the prison, who has no power over sinners except from God’s just judgment,
satisfaction did not have to be rendered to him, but to God the judge, who
primarily and principally detained us. Add that it is wrongly supposed here
that in the infliction of punishments there must be some person to whom
payment is made, as money is counted out to the creditor in a pecuniary debt.
It suffices that there be a judge who exacts punishments to preserve the
majesty of the laws safe and intact.

XII. Fourth, *the person himself who makes satisfaction* must be considered.
For just as sins had the character of debt, enmity, and crime, and God acted
as creditor, offended party, and judge, so Christ had to assume a threefold
relation: that of a *sponsor*, who would pay the debt for us; of a *Mediator
εἰρηνοποιοῦ*, who would remove enmities and reconcile God to us; and of a
*priest and victim*, who would substitute himself in our place for penal
satisfaction. Again, because the satisfaction exacted by God’s justice
principally required two things—1. that it be paid by the same nature which
had sinned, and 2. that it nevertheless be of infinite value and price to
remove the infinite demerit of sin—two natures were necessary in Christ for
paying satisfaction:

[Printed p. 371 | Internet Archive PDF 399 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the human, which would suffer, and the divine, which would add infinite value
and worth to his sufferings.

XIII. Finally, because Christ’s *substitution* had to be made in our place,
its justice must be demonstrated, so that it may be evident in what case and
under what conditions such a substitution of the innocent for the guilty
could legitimately be made, which at first sight appears not only unusual but
also unjust. Although this substitution, which occasionally occurs in a
pecuniary debt, occurs more rarely in a penal debt and indeed is sometimes
prohibited—as among the Romans suretyship was forbidden in a capital penalty,
because no one is lord of his own members and this cannot be done without
some injury to the commonwealth—nevertheless, that it was not absolutely
unknown to the pagans is sufficiently attested by the examples of the friends
Damon and Pythias, one of whom presented himself before Dionysius as surety
for the other in a capital case, and of Curtius, Codrus, and Decius, who
devoted themselves for their country. The right of killing hostages, which
was customary among the most civilized peoples when their principals failed
in their promises, also attests it; for this reason they were called
ἀντίψυχοι. Paul, looking to this, says that perhaps someone might dare to die
for a good man (Rom. 5:7). Scripture certainly establishes it often, not only
from the imputation of sin, in which one customarily bears another’s
punishment, but also from the public use of sacrifices, in which the victim
had to take the sinner’s place and undergo death for him. For this purpose
hands were laid on its head and confession of sin was made.

XIV. But for this substitution to be made legitimately and without any mark
of injustice, various conditions are required in the sponsor, all of which
belong perfectly to Christ. 1. *Communion of nature*, so that sin might be
punished in the nature which was guilty (Heb. 2:14). 2. *Consent of will*, so
that freely and voluntarily, with no one compelling him, he might take that
burden upon himself (Heb. 10:9): “Behold, I come to do your will.” 3. *Power
and dominion over his own members*, so that he might be his own master and
able to determine concerning himself (John 10:18): “No one takes my life from
me, but I lay it down by myself; I have authority to lay it down and to take
it up again.” 4. *The power to bear all the punishments owed by us and to
remove them both from himself and from us*. Otherwise, if he could have been
held by death, he could have freed no one from it. No one can doubt that this
belonged to Christ θεανθρώπῳ. 5. *Undefiled holiness and purity*, so that,
stained by no sin, he would not need to offer for himself but only for us
(Heb. 7:25–27).

XV. When these conditions were posited, it was not unjust for the just Christ
to be substituted for us, the unjust. For here no injury was done to anyone.
Not to *Christ himself*, both because he willingly received this punishment
upon himself and because he had power to determine concerning himself and
power to raise himself from the dead. Not to *God the judge*, because he
willed and commanded it, nor to his natural right, which was secured by the
sponsor’s punishment. Not to the *commonwealth of the world* through the death
of the innocent, as if it were deprived of its best citizen, for Christ,
freed from death, lives forever; nor through the life of the guilty, as if
they survived to the destruction of the commonwealth, because through Christ
they are converted and become new creatures. Not to the *divine law*, for
which provision was made by its most perfect fulfillment by Christ and by our
twofold *union* with Christ, *natural and forensic or mystical*. Through it,
as he became one with us and we with him, so he could receive our sin and
evils upon himself and communicate his righteousness and benefits to us. Thus
neither abrogation nor derogation of the law was made, but an explanation κατ’
ἐπιείκειαν, so that what was owed by us according to the rigor of right might
by God’s special grace be transferred to Christ.

XVI. With these things laid down in advance, let us descend to the proposed
question. It is asked whether it was necessary for Christ to satisfy for us,
both absolutely on the part of justice and hypothetically by virtue of God’s
decree; or whether it was either

[Printed p. 372 | Internet Archive PDF 400 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

not necessary at all for Christ to satisfy for us, because God was able to
remit sins freely without any satisfaction,
or necessary only hypothetically on account of God’s decree. The Socinians
indeed acknowledge no necessity here. Some of the ancients and of our own
urge only hypothetical necessity and judge it sufficient for refuting the
heretic. But we, with most, retain both and maintain that Christ’s satisfaction
was necessary both on the part of God’s justice and of his will. Yet we do not
urge a simply natural necessity, such as that in fire for burning, which is
neither voluntary nor admits any modification in its exercise, but a *moral
and rational* necessity. Just as this flows from God’s holiness and justice
and cannot be exercised except by the intervention of free will, so it can
admit various modifications according to God’s wisdom and ἐπιείκεια, provided
his natural right remains intact.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of satisfaction is proved: 1. From God’s justice.]

XVII. Many things prove that such is the *necessity of Christ’s satisfaction*.
First, *God’s avenging justice*. Since it is natural and essential to God, as
was proved in Part I, Locus III, Question 19, and God’s eternal and natural
right as judge is founded in it, God can no more dispense with it than deny
himself. Its exercise cannot but be necessary once sin is posited, although it
occurs with free will intervening. This is especially so because it does not
consist in a merely gratuitous and undue act, which is the nature of mercy—
whether it is exercised or not, injury is done to no one—but in the due act
of rendering to each what is his, from which God cannot abstain without some
detriment to justice. Since this justice is nothing other than the constant
will to punish, and this will cannot be ineffectual in God, whose majesty is
supreme and power infinite, it necessarily requires the infliction of
punishment, either upon the sinner himself or upon a surety substituted in his
place. Neither can the consideration of God’s freedom interfere with this
right, because it exerts itself only in positive, not natural, right; nor can
the consideration of mercy, because this indeed consists in the sinner’s
being withdrawn from punishment, but not in sin itself remaining unpunished.
The exceptions brought forward were examined in the place cited.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the nature of sin.]

XVIII. Second, *the nature of sin*. Since it is a moral evil which intrinsically
and in the nature of the case differs from holiness, as was shown above in
Locus XI, Question 2 on the law, a necessary and natural connection must also
exist between it and physical evil, so that they necessarily follow each
other. Just as physical and penal evil cannot exist without moral evil,
whether proper or imputed, so moral evil cannot exist without drawing
physical evil after it. Ethical good is joined with physical good, and
holiness with happiness, by an indissoluble bond, not only by reason of divine
wisdom, to which it is agreeable that those things which agree in the genus
of good or evil be joined together, but also by reason of goodness and
justice. For just as by his goodness God cannot bring himself to allow it not
to go well with the good, because he is good and beneficent to his image, so
he cannot allow it not to go badly with the evil, because he is just. This is
all the more so because if he repays any good to the creature’s holiness, he
does it from mere liberality, without any merit of the creature; but if he
punishes the sinner, he renders to him what he owes and what he has deserved
by his own fault.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the sanction of the law.]

XIX. Third, *the sanction of the law*. The law threatens the sinner with the
punishment of death (Gen. 2:17; Deut. 27:26; Ezek. 18:20; Rom. 1:18, 32; 6:23).
Since God is truthful and cannot lie, it must finally be fulfilled either in
the sinner himself or in a surety. It is replied in vain that the threat is
hypothetical, not absolute, that is, that it can be relaxed upon the presence
of repentance. For

[Printed p. 373 | Internet Archive PDF 401 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

it is gratuitously assumed, when it should have been proved, that any such
condition, either tacit or express, exists. Indeed, since the act of the legal
sanction can be none other than that of the law itself, and since the law
itself is of natural and indispensable right, as was proved in its place, the
sanction itself must also be immutable. Nor should *particular and economic
threats*, whether paternal or evangelical, which by a special dispensation are
denounced to men to recall them to repentance and can be revoked when
repentance is present—such as that proposed to Hezekiah (Isa. 38) and to the
Ninevites (Jonah 3)—be confused with *judicial threats* and the immovable
sanction of the law, which is founded in God’s very nature and to which the
human conscience assents.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the proclamation of the gospel.]

XX. Fourth, *the proclamation of the gospel*, which announces the bloody and
dreadful death suffered for us by the mediator and sponsor, confirms the same
most solidly from the *event a posteriori*. Since God does not multiply
entities without necessity, and his goodness and wisdom do not permit us to
believe that the Father wished to expose his most innocent and beloved Son to
the most cruel and ignominious death without some most weighty and
indispensable necessity, and since no rationale of necessity for so unusual a
dispensation can be conceived and given besides the satisfaction which had to
be paid for us to his justice, anyone can see that it was absolutely
necessary. I know that the adversaries bring forward other causes for so
dreadful a death: the confirmation of doctrine and examples of every kind of
virtue, especially love and constancy. But since he had confirmed his doctrine
by various miracles and throughout his whole life had given manifold examples
of these virtues, who could persuade himself that God for this reason alone
would have exposed his only-begotten Son to so many dreadful torments?
Therefore, without doubt another cause had to underlie this dispensation,
namely, the consideration of his justice which had to be satisfied. Paul
testifies to this in Romans 3:25 when he says that Christ was appointed by God
as a propitiatory εἰς ἔνδειξιν τῆς δικαιοσύνης αὐτοῦ, *for the demonstration
of his justice*, which was so inexorable that it did not permit our sins to be
remitted except through the intervention of Christ’s death.

XXI. Again, if God by a word alone could and willed to remit our sins without
the intervention of any satisfaction, how is it that Paul so often ascribes
our justification and salvation to Christ’s blood when he declares that *we
are justified through the redemption which is in his blood* (Rom. 3:24), that
*we have redemption in his blood, namely, the remission of sins* (Eph. 1:7),
and that *he reconciled all things to God through the blood of the cross*
(Col. 1:20)? There was no need for his blood to be shed for this if it depended
on God’s will alone. Likewise, the apostle would have wrongly insisted so
often in the epistle to the Hebrews that the blood of bulls and goats, that
is, the legal sacrifices, could not take away sins—that is, abolish guilt and
obtain pardon—but only Christ’s offering, if there was no need at all for any
purification and if repentance alone could suffice to remove guilt without
any sacrifice. For why could that which could readily be taken away with no
sacrifice at all not be taken away by the blood of the legal victims? And if
God’s will alone intervened here, why does Paul not touch upon it, but rise to
the nature of the thing itself when he says that it is impossible for the
blood of bulls to take away sins? Sin is so abominable and hateful to God that
its stain could be washed away by nothing except the blood of the Son of God
himself.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the magnitude of God’s love.]

XXII. Fifth, if it was in no way necessary for Christ to die in order to
satisfy justice, the magnitude of that love through which he did not spare his
own Son but delivered him for us, which the apostle so highly commends in
Romans 5 and 8, would seem to be diminished not a little. For if no obstacle
on the part of justice stood in the way of our salvation,

[Printed p. 374 | Internet Archive PDF 402 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the grace would indeed have been immense if God had willed to pardon our sins,
but it would nevertheless have fallen far below the ὑπερβολὴν of love by
which, although justice inexorably demanded the punishment of sin, the desire
for our salvation nevertheless prevailed, an admirable tempering of justice
with mercy having been found for this purpose in the treasury of wisdom. Nor
could Christ be said to have appeased wrath if God himself, by requiring no
satisfaction, could readily have laid aside his wrath by himself.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the glory of the divine virtues.]

XXIII. Finally, 1. The opinion concerning the necessity of satisfaction
detracts from none of the divine perfections. Not from *absolute power*,
because God cannot deny himself and his virtues, nor so conduct himself as if
he delighted in sin by granting the sinner communion with himself. Not from
the most free *will*, because he wills and can will nothing contrary to his
justice and holiness, which would be harmed not a little if sin remained
unpunished. Not from immense *mercy*, because a place is always given to it
toward the sinner, although satisfaction is exacted from the surety. Rather,
it serves to illustrate the glory of God’s most excellent virtues: his
*holiness*, through which he can maintain no communion with the sinner without
a preceding satisfaction by which his guilt is blotted out and the washing
away of his stain procured; his *justice*, which so inexorably demands the
punishment of sin that God willed to punish it even in his own Son lest it
remain unpunished; his *wisdom*, which found what was impossible to every
created intellect, an admirable tempering of justice and mercy by which a
place could be given to mercy toward us without any prejudice to justice; and
his *love*, by which he did not spare his own Son so that he might spare us.
2. It serves to increase piety by augmenting the horror of sin, which was of
such atrocity that it could be washed away by nothing other than the blood of
the Son of God, and by inflaming our hearts with reciprocal love toward God,
so that according to our strength we may love again the one who so greatly
loved us.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXIV. Although God necessarily exacted satisfaction, it does not follow that
he did not do so freely. For this necessity was not physical, but ethical and
rational, as was said. It arose from the hypothesis both of sin, which he
willed to permit, and of God’s decree concerning human salvation. Once this
was posited, he could not fail to act in agreement with his justice, just as,
if God wills to speak to men, he cannot do so otherwise than truthfully,
although he nevertheless does so voluntarily.

XXV. It is one thing to remit from one’s *right*, another from *justice*. God
can remit from his right, but not absolutely; only insofar as the rationale of
justice permits it, lest anything unjust be done. Justice does not permit sin
to be remitted without satisfaction, because thus the majesty of the laws is
violated and what is due is not rendered to sin. 2. It is not always lawful to
remit from one’s right if the right is public, not private; judicial, not
domestic; natural, not free; a matter of uprightness, not utility. But the
right of punishment in God is not the private right of credit or dominion,
not free and positive, not of private utility, but of public uprightness, the
right of the ruler and judge, which is founded in nature itself, as was seen
above. Therefore God could not dispense with that right, although he is not
subject to the law because he is a law to himself. Otherwise, he could also
remit sins to the impenitent insofar as they remain such, which even the
heretic denies. Nor, if injury would have been done to no one through such a
dispensation, could God have done this, because God’s justice and the majesty
of the laws would be harmed.

XXVI. *Satisfaction* and *remission* are incompatible when the payment of a
pecuniary debt is treated, whether it is made by the debtor himself or by
another, because in it attention is paid not to who pays but to what is paid,
provided as much is paid as is owed. But this likewise has no place in the
*payment of a penal debt*, because here attention is paid not only to the
thing paid but also to

[Printed p. 375 | Internet Archive PDF 403 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the person himself who pays. This is also granted concerning proper payment,
because one who personally pays what he owes has no need of remission. But
the point concerns another’s and vicarious payment, which has place in this
matter. Although justice is satisfied by the satisfaction, yet because it is
another’s satisfaction, not the proper satisfaction which the law exacted
according to the rigor of right, an act of the judge is required here. By
accepting the surety’s vicarious punishment, he remits to the guilty person
the proper punishment which he owed.

XXVII. God’s grace and Christ’s merit are not opposed, but subordinated,
because they are regarded here κατ’ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο. Grace is considered with
respect to us, both in the gift of the surety and in the acceptance of his
satisfaction for our justification. But merit is considered with respect to
God’s justice, which could not leave sin unpunished. The apostle asserts that
these agree most perfectly with each other (Rom. 3:24–25).

XXVIII. Although *the remission of God and of men* are alike in various
respects as to the thing, especially in the sincere and unfeigned pardon of
offenses, it does not follow that there is a complete parity, because men are
private persons to whom the right of punishment therefore does not belong,

XXIX. If God promises the remission of sins in the new covenant, this does
not deny but presupposes Christ’s satisfaction because it is promised in
Christ’s name (Acts 10:43), that is, through his power and merit. Nor is there
any remission without αἱματεκχυσίᾳ (Heb. 9:22). And if the ancients are said
to have obtained pardon through faith (Heb. 11), Christ is necessarily
included, whom faith regards as its proper object, and indeed insofar as he is
crucified, who had to lay down his life as a sacrifice for sin (Isa. 53:10)
and become sin and κατάρα (2 Cor. 5:21; Gal. 3:13). The other matters
pertaining here should be sought in Part I, Locus III, Question 19 and in
Disputations 1 and 2 on the necessity of satisfaction.
