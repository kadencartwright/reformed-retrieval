# Preface to Beza's Book against Baudouin

1563

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin preface printed in
*Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. IX (Corpus Reformatorum
XXXVII), pp. 859–862. It was translated from the normalized Latin and checked
against the page images. Square brackets mark a supplied clarification.

The CO title is editorial and is translated here only to identify the text.
Calvin's own heading is “John Calvin to the Reader.” The page markers follow
the printed Latin rather than modern paragraph boundaries.

## Translation

### [CO IX, p. 859]

JOHN CALVIN TO THE READER.

The poets imagine that Hecuba, when Troy had been overthrown and she gave
unrestrained indulgence to her grief by hurling curses everywhere at her
enemies, was finally turned into a dog. The reason, no doubt, was that this
immoderate rush of abuse came to be valued no more than a dog's bared jaws. If
this was said of a most noble queen, whose womanly grief still deserved some
excuse even if so many bitter disasters drove her to madness, what must be
said of a worthless man who, struck by blind frenzy rather than anger, not
only vomits recklessly invented insults upon everyone he meets, but eagerly
attacks the best men so that he may rail wantonly at them?

Although many people have thus far urged me to curb François Baudouin's
insolence, I thought it best to despise the beast, lest I seem to be contending
with a barking dog. I do not yet regret that decision. For what in his stinking
slanders deserves refutation? He lived in my house and was treated kindly and
generously. To prove himself worse than a barbarian, he seizes upon a foolishly
fabricated lie: that, while criminals are being condemned, I sit as judge on
the tribunal. Heshusius made this worthless claim. I had previously supposed
that it had been dictated by some wandering beggar in order to squeeze a penny
out of him. But now that my good guest Baudouin, who was Heshusius's colleague,
makes the same charge against me, who will seek another architect of the tale?
Yet at public trials everyone has always seen me standing beside the accused
man who is to be led to punishment. Am I really to exercise my pen on such
trifles?

He reproaches me for having said that the bishop of Nenetensis sat at the
Council of Trent in place of the bishop of Rennes. It is a capital crime,
apparently, that I was deceived by taking one egg in place of another. From my
letters and those of the man of blessed memory, my revered brother Bucer, he
has concocted for me what he at least regards as the greatest odium and
disgrace.

Before I reply, readers must be warned that nothing is more thievish than this
little jackdaw, so that in this respect he

### [CO IX, p. 860]

surpasses his own cousin Antoine Baudouin, whom his schoolmates nicknamed “the
Ablative” for his skill in stealing. I was so indulgent toward him that, in my
absence, he freely rummaged through every paper in my library. That he stole
whatever he thought would serve his purpose needs no other proof to make it
plain than his own publication, in which he has exposed himself splendidly.
Here, certainly, one sees his good faith and his conduct as a guest.

But now let us consider the matter itself. Since it lay in his power to bring
something forth almost from my innermost parts, had he discovered any fault in
me, he found nothing more shocking than my having confessed to Bucer that I am
irascible by nature, that I struggle constantly with this fault, and yet do
not make as much progress as I wish. He fabricated for himself the other
things that he mixes in. What, then, does he mean by this accusation, unless
in his judgment only those people deserve praise who not only indulge and
pardon their own faults but brazenly cast far from themselves the faults of
which they know themselves guilty? We learned something different in Christ's
school.

Still, if he had remembered Cicero's observation that the dispositions of
good men are readily provoked, perhaps he would not have puffed up his pipes so
absurdly. And if he is to have a plausible pretext for taunting me, he will
have to produce either some insulting remark or cruel deed that anger wrung
from me.

But what of Bucer? Because he thought that I had written too harshly about
certain ministers of God's word, he took up their cause and rebuked my
vehemence. These are his words: “We judge according as we love or hate.”
Baudouin, that skillful interpreter, changes the person and twists this against
me so that the reproach may be more hateful. Granted that Bucer—a man otherwise
of the gentlest disposition—was then more agitated against me because of our
intimacy, why does Baudouin not equally relate what Bucer wrote about the same
matter? After asking Christ's forgiveness and offering several excuses, Bucer
closed his discourse with this exclamation: “But what am I doing, minimizing
my sin

### [CO IX, p. 861]

when my own conscience condemns me?” I can certainly rest safely in this
defense from Bucer. Modesty prevents me from recounting how honorably he
always spoke and wrote of me. I have no desire to imitate Baudouin's
foolishness: he boasts grandly of letters full of flattering compliments that
he extracted from Philip Melanchthon, of the sort that man customarily wrote
even to complete strangers. Melanchthon often said that, because the practice
had become widespread of calling people who fell below mediocrity “most
learned,” “most illustrious,” and “most distinguished,” he was generous with
those titles, but did not so prostitute the designation “good man.” What if he
called me not only “revered” but “an excellent man” in his letters? Away with
such trifles.

Baudouin produces a memorable example of my pride and even my tyranny: I
supposedly assailed a friend of mine with almost implacable savagery merely
because he wished one word erased from the church's public prayers, in the
place where the new covenant is said to be far superior to the old. But this
gross lie will be refuted by the records both of the Consistory and of the
Council. That man had rashly and ignorantly

### [CO IX, p. 862]

spread the claim that it should in no way be called “better,” because it was
the same covenant. This had circulated among many people before it became
known to us. It was of course easy to shame the man by producing the apostle's
testimony. Convicted of rashness, he asked my pardon and obtained it
immediately. I even interceded with the Council so that it would not decide
anything too severe against him. From that time on he frequented my house just
as he had before. Whenever he wrote to me from France, where he died, he was
the most obedient son and I the revered father. Let Baudouin's impudence rely
on this witness, certainly—but am I to toil over it? Still less does his
stupidity move me in the frivolous boasts he puffs out.

Moreover, although I decided that I should remain silent, I am so far from
censuring the plan of my excellent and thoroughly upright brother Beza that
his apt response, at once free from all artificial coloring, has delighted me
considerably. I wish he had begun sooner, because unbelievable haste did not
allow him to carry the whole case through as he wished. It will nevertheless
be enough that the truth shines so brightly in this sincere and candid defense
that it rises victorious above all Baudouin's clouds.

## Translator's notes

1. Hecuba's transformation into a dog is the classical myth behind Calvin's
   opening comparison. *Canis rictus* is literally a dog's open or bared jaws;
   the following sentence develops the image as barking.
2. “The bishop of Nenetensis” transliterates the edition's elliptical
   *Nenetensem*. Calvin is contrasting two episcopal sees, but the printed form
   is not silently corrected here. His “one egg for another” is a sarcastic
   claim that the error was trivial.
3. A jackdaw was proverbial for theft. “The Ablative” is a grammatical pun on
   Latin *ablativus*, associated here with taking things away.
4. The CO text reads *luculentiae probatio* in the sentence about proof of
   theft; its apparatus reports Beza's later *luculentior* (“clearer”). The
   translation gives the intelligible force of the printed construction
   without silently inserting the later reading.
5. *Fides eius et hospitalitas* is sarcastic. *Hospitalitas* denotes the
   obligations or relationship of host and guest here; Calvin, not Baudouin,
   had supplied the lodging.
6. “Puffed up his pipes” renders *tibias inflasset*. The image can also suggest
   puffed cheeks or inflated rhetorical display.
7. Bucer's two statements are quotations embedded by Calvin. The source
   edition prints them continuously with the surrounding prose rather than as
   a modern block quotation.
8. The last sentence plays on Baudouin's name and *nebulae*, “clouds” or
   “mists.” The English retains the image without attempting a new name-pun.
