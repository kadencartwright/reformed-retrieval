# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 17.* Romans 6:14, 15: *For you are not under law, but under grace.*

*Answer.* 1. It is certain that the apostle uses the term *law* in various
ways, even when he speaks about its abrogation. Sometimes it denotes the
Mosaic economy, as in the epistles to the Galatians and the Hebrews; sometimes
it is taken for the ceremonial law alone. It is also used by synecdoche for
some part or effect of the law, or it denotes the law insofar as it convicts
people of guilt for sin and therefore condemns them, as in Galatians 5:23:
*Against such there is no law.* Our opponents, however, admit no distinction

[Printed p. 71 | IA PDF 79 | IA leaf 79 | IA decausadei02brow]

wherever there is a statement about the law that seems to favor its abolition
or abrogation. They take *law* for the moral law, and that insofar as it
prescribes our duty and requires obedience, without any consideration of the
purpose or circumstances of the text. We shall make this evident here.

2. A distinction must be made between the phrases *not to be under law* and
*to be without law*, or *not to have law*. For the latter designates either
those to whom the law has not yet become known, and who are thus without law
because they do not know the law and it has not been revealed to them—as Paul
says in Romans 2:14 that the Gentiles did not have the law—or those whom the
law does not bind, as in 1 Corinthians 9:21, where Gentiles are said to be
without law, namely the ceremonial law, because they were not bound to observe
that law, as is clear from Acts 15:28 and 21:25. Or it designates those who
have not yet experienced the law's terror and accusation. Thus in Romans 7:9
Paul was alive without the law, but when the law came, sin revived and he
died. The Antinomians, however, want *not to be under law* to mean the same as
*to be without law*, or *not to have law*, when they insist that the meaning
of the words *you are not under law* is that believers are not bound to obey
the law. But Scripture nowhere says that believers are *without law*, or that
they *do not have law*.

3. Some observe that whenever *law* is opposed to Christ, faith, grace, or the
promise, it always denotes the covenant of works. They therefore hold that it
is taken in this sense here, where it is opposed to *grace*, or the covenant
of grace. Consequently, this inference does not follow: believers are not
under the law insofar as it is the covenant of works, or insofar as it demands
perfect obedience complete in every respect with a view to justification;
therefore, they are not under the law insofar as it is the rule of conduct.

4. Whether or not the term *law* is taken in this sense here, it is certain
that it cannot be taken in the Antinomians' sense, as though these words meant
the same thing as *to be freed from obligation to the law*, or said that the
law has been abrogated insofar as it is the norm of life. The first reason is
that this interpretation conflicts with the apostle's general purpose in this
passage. Throughout this chapter he urges holiness and moves believers with
many arguments to pursue sanctity. But to say that the moral law has been
abrogated and has no force as law or norm, or that believers have been freed
from its yoke so that they are no longer bound to observe it, is not to urge
holiness but rather to loosen the reins for every vice. What sort of argument,
I ask, would this be: Pursue holiness, because you have no law to show you the
way of holiness and press you to obedience under any penalty whatsoever? Who
does not see that this would be the same as if the apostle had said, Observe
the law and conform your life to it, because the law has now been abrogated
and you are not bound to observe it? The second reason is that it conflicts
with what was said immediately before, where he urges them into battle with
the certain hope of victory. *Do not,* he says, *present your members to sin
as instruments of unrighteousness, because sin will not have dominion over
you.* He confirms this reasoning with the statement, *for you are not under
law*, and so forth. Let us therefore take these words in the Antinomians'
sense. Where, I ask, is the connection or force of that reasoning? Is this a
good inference: Sin will not have dominion over you because the law has been
abrogated? Indeed, from the abrogation of the law it would follow not merely
that sin does not exercise dominion, but rather that sin does not exist at
all, for where there is no law there is no sin, which is transgression of the
law. How could the conclusion that sin or corruption will not exercise
dominion in believers follow from their not being bound to observe the moral
law? When every rein is loosened,

[Printed p. 72 | IA PDF 80 | IA leaf 80 | IA decausadei02brow]

does a fierce and untamed horse grow gentle? Does it not, on the contrary,
throw its rider all the more easily?

5. The commentators reject this interpretation. Peter Martyr has the
following on the passage: [*"We should therefore distinguish in this way:
with respect to justification, we are said to be exempt from the law. On the
other hand, we are not released from obedience toward God, for we must
necessarily do the things prescribed in the Decalogue, and so forth. Hence
the law, even with respect to the moral part, is said to be abrogated insofar
as it neither condemns nor accuses nor compels the elect."*] And a little
later: [*"But some wonder how it happens that, although all those parts have
been abrogated so that nothing remains of the ceremonial and civil laws, the
whole Decalogue nevertheless keeps us bound as far as obedience to it is
concerned, as we have already taught. I answer: the Decalogue is fulfilled by
no one, however holy, for all our works are so defective that they do not
answer to its prescription and norm. Nevertheless, the precepts described
there accord with the law of nature, and so forth."*] Pareus has the following
on the passage: [*"We are also not under the moral law. But this is the eternal
rule of God's wisdom and righteousness, perpetually binding all people to
obedience and conformity with God. This is certainly so, and in this respect
we are not exempt from the law."*] Piscator has this observation on the
passage: [*"We must take care not to misuse these words to establish the error
of the Antinomians, who think that the doctrine of the law should be banished
from the Christian church. Paul does not intend that, but means that believers
have been freed from the curse of the law, as he himself declares in Galatians
3:13. But that does not prevent the law from remaining the perpetual rule of
believers' lives, from which they may advance daily in the knowledge of sin
and the pursuit of godliness. Certainly the apostle does not contradict
himself, for at the end of chapter 3 above he said that through faith he does
not abolish the law, but establishes it. This also occurs because it is only
the obedience toward the law which proceeds from those justified by faith
that pleases God."*]

6. The commentators explain these words very differently. Gualther says:
[*"The condition is no longer what it once was. Now is the time of grace,
which Christ merited for us. By the term *grace* he means the forgiveness of
sins and the Spirit of sanctification, and the fact that the Father has been
reconciled to us in Christ."*] And a little later: [*"That is, the condition
is not what it once was, when the law condemned all who had failed in even the
least matter. Rather, you now live under the reign of grace, which Christ
merited, so that sins are not imputed to us and at the same time we can do all
things in him (John 15; Philippians 4)."*] Pareus says: [*"We have indeed been
exempted and freed: 1. From the curse of the law, which says, 'Cursed is
everyone who does not continue,' and so forth. Christ freed us from this by
becoming a curse for us. 2. From justification by the law, which it cannot
confer on us because it was weakened through the flesh, but it compels us to
seek that justification outside itself in Christ. 3. From the demand for the
first obedience, which we violated before the state of grace, while in Christ
we made satisfaction for the violation. 4. From compulsion to the second or
new obedience, which the law indeed requires from the regenerate in the state
of grace, but does not compel with threats and terrors as before, since it is
so written by the Spirit of God in the hearts of the justified that they are
ready to show God spontaneous gratitude according to the law. This is the
principal part of Christian liberty, of which it is said, 'You are not under
law.'"*] Piscator takes the meaning to be: *You are not under the curse of the
law.*

[Printed p. 73 | IA PDF 81 | IA leaf 81 | IA decausadei02brow]

Bullinger gives this interpretation of the phrase in the passage: [*"That is,
after faith in the Lord Jesus has been received, we are no longer such as we
were when we lived under the law—that is, in our former life. For now, having
been made new people, we can do all things through him who strengthens us by
his Spirit and has called us into this grace. To be under law is nothing other
than to live the life lived by those who are still under law: fearful,
thunderstruck, relying upon their own powers, and meanwhile accomplishing
nothing. Or to be under law is to be under the dominion of sin, if *law* is
taken for sin, which is made known when the law points it out."*] The Belgian
Annotations say that we are not under a law [*"which indeed commands what must
be done but grants no power to perform what it commands; which terrifies us
with threats but gives us no courage and does not rouse us to conquer sin,
but instead convicts us of sin more and more (Romans 7; 2 Corinthians 4)."*]
Martyr comments as follows: [*"You are not,"* he says, *"under a law which
merely shows what must be done but supplies no help. Chrysostom observes on
this passage that the law merely indicates what must be done and what must be
avoided, but provides no aid to those who struggle and sets forth only a bare
exhortation in words. The Gospel, however, presents Christ, from whom are
supplied the Spirit and the strength to perform those good things which we
have known through faith. From this it comes about that people do their duty,
not driven by fear, but voluntarily and willingly. His statements should not
be understood as though the fathers who lived long ago under the law were
entirely without grace, for they too believed in Christ. But here Paul speaks
about the law by distinguishing it from the Gospel and Christ, as the Hebrews
were doing at that time. Thus if the fathers were strengthened by the support
of grace and the Spirit, they did not have this from the law. Paul therefore
says: so far are you from having to be subjected to sin that you have also
been freed from the law itself. He appears to argue from effects. For since
the law was enacted solely so that sin might be perceived, once it has been
removed it is evident, as far as believers are concerned, that the powers of
sin have now been broken so that it can no longer have dominion in us."*] And
a little later: [*"Paul appears by these words to anticipate an objection and
console the Romans. For they could have said: Although we do not present
ourselves to God as those alive from the dead, or our members to God as
instruments of righteousness, we nevertheless cannot prevent ourselves from
sinning at times while we are in this life. Paul answers: Although an
implanted and innate desire is found in you, it nevertheless will not reign
in you. For since you are not under law, sin will not be able to have dominion
over you. Through the grace under which you live through Christ, it will not
be imputed to you; and your obedience, although begun only imperfectly, will
nevertheless please God even if the law is not satisfied, for you are not
under law."*] Beza explains the passage in this way, rejecting the sense in
which *not to be under law* would mean the same as no longer to be liable to
the law's curse, because the dominion of sin is in view here—not whether sin
is or is not imputed, but whether or not it is vigorous in us. [*"And so,"* he
says, *"others prefer to explain it thus: You are no longer in the condition
in which you formerly were, when the law made you guilty by commanding but
not assisting, as Augustine explains. I embrace this opinion as the simplest
and most suitable, but I apply it a little differently than they usually do.
The apostle's purpose, then, is to exhort us to resist sin courageously. For,"*
he says, *"it will certainly happen that, once it is overcome, you will carry
away the victory. But from where does he infer so confidently that this will
happen? Because we are not under law, but under grace. For the law,"* as he
will explain in the next chapter, *"is that instrument through which our
husband—that is, sin—used to beget those vicious desires and actions in us as
children. That, I say, is the source from which sin, having seized an
opportunity, revives though otherwise as if dead and kills us. Finally, the
law is the power of sin,*

[Printed p. 74 | IA PDF 82 | IA leaf 82 | IA decausadei02brow]

*as is written in 1 Corinthians 15:56. Therefore the inference holds that we
will obtain victory over sin, since we are not under law insofar as it is the
power of sin, but under grace—that is, endowed with the Spirit of Christ—and,
relying upon his power, will utterly crush even the now-unarmed remnants of
sin. That this is the proper explanation is clear from Galatians 5:23 and
24."*] See the other writers in Marlorat on the passage. Whichever of these
two interpretations we accept, it is clear that nothing favorable to
Antinomianism can be inferred from these words.

*Reply.* But the objection which the apostle anticipates in the following
verses sufficiently indicates what he understood by the words *you are not
under law*: namely, that they had been freed from obligation to the law. It
was indeed from this that the false apostles could seize an opportunity to
object that Paul was loosening the reins for every wickedness and granting
Christians license to do whatever they pleased.

*Answer.* 1. If the meaning of the words *you are not under law* here is, You
are no longer under the curse of the law; your sins have been forgiven by
grace through Christ; you have satisfied the law in Christ; you have been
justified by faith and sanctified by the Holy Spirit—then it is obvious how
easy it would be for detractors and malicious people to raise some such
objection against Paul's doctrine: namely, that it was then quite permissible
to sin without concern. [*"Nevertheless,"* Pareus says on the passage,
*"because he saw that the preceding statement could be seized by his
opponents for a slander similar to the earlier one, he anticipates it and
shows that it is empty: 'I said that we are not under law, but under grace.
Shall we therefore sin?' It is as though he were saying, 'Does an open door to
wickedness follow from this for us? Has license to sin been granted? By no
means.' The objection will be: Those who are not under law, but under grace,
may sin with impunity, for without law sin is not imputed, and when sin
increases grace increases. We are not under law, but under grace. Therefore,
and so forth. The apostle charges the major premise with falsehood and denies
the inference, because not to be under law is not to be exempt from obedience
to the law or released from every law so as to have license to sin; it is not
to be under the law's dominion, threats, and compulsion."*] It is therefore
clear that objectors of this kind were led by the spirit of the Antinomians
and argued as the Antinomians now do, while the apostle was entirely hostile
to them and did not favor this error in the least. It is consequently evident
that the words *you are not under law* have a far different sense from that
which our opponents try to attach to them. Otherwise the apostle would never
have rejected that objection as we see him reject it. See the Belgian
Annotations on the passage. [*"It is therefore an anthypophora,"* Martyr says
on the passage. *"If it is as you say, that we have been released from the
law, we shall sin, and indeed with impunity. But those who object in this way
are greatly mistaken. For freedom from the law was not given for license, but
rather for a more perfect absolution. Paul therefore adds, 'By no means,'
signifying that he vehemently abhors such an opinion. This absurd conclusion
could have followed if Paul had merely asserted that we were freed from the
law and added nothing else. But since he added, 'But you are under grace,' he
shows that they were so freed from the law that they lived under a better
teacher. For by grace and the Holy Spirit we are not only enlightened to see
what things must be done, but are also powerfully urged and kindled to perform
them."*] Bullinger says on the passage: [*"From this it is clear what it is to
be without law: namely, to be freed from sin, and to be under grace—that is,
to be devoted to righteousness. Nor is Christian liberty anything else.
Certain people who dispute about it too curiously come to the point of
thinking that the law has been removed, the Scriptures abolished, and
everything made permissible."*] Calvin, as quoted by

[Printed p. 75 | IA PDF 83 | IA leaf 83 | IA decausadei02brow]

Marlorat, [*"says that they err in thinking that the abolition of the
righteousness which the Lord commends in the law is contained in the
abrogation of the law, when only the curse to which all mortals outside grace
are liable is abrogated."*] From this objection, thus anticipated and rejected
by the apostle not without indignation, it is sufficiently evident that the
apostle's words have a far different sense than the Antinomians dream, and
that nothing was further from the apostle's meaning than that believers are
exempt from obligation to the law, or that the moral law no longer has the
force of a norm to whose observance we are strictly bound. If we look more
deeply into the apostle's answer, by which he more fully refutes the proposed
objection, we shall see that our opponents' gloss is perverse. For it is clear
from verse 16 that *to be under law* is the same as *to be slaves of sin unto
death*, while conversely *to be under grace* is *to present ourselves as
slaves of obedience unto righteousness*. From verses 17 and 18 it is evident
that *not to be under law, but under grace* is the same as *to be freed from
sin and made slaves of righteousness*; verses 19, 20, and 22 also confirm
this. Paul himself indicates in the following words what the expression *you
are not under law, but under grace* means when he addresses them thus: *You
were indeed slaves of sin, but you obeyed that form of doctrine—and having
been freed from sin, you were made slaves of righteousness—you present your
members as slaves to impurity and lawlessness unto lawlessness; so now present
your members as slaves to righteousness unto holiness—when you were slaves of
sin, you were free from righteousness—but now, having been freed from sin, you
have been made slaves of God.*
