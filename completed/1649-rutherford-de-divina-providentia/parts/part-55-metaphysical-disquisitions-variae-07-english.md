Question 32. Does orderability to good belong to sin in itself or accidentally?
The Disputant thinks it absurd for good to belong in itself to sin or evil, and
therefore thinks that both orderability and being actually ordered belong to sin
accidentally. For if the capacity for laughter belongs to a human being in
itself, so too does laughing.

[Marginalia: Being orderable to good belongs to sin in itself.]

Answer. I cannot accept this Arminian opinion. First, nothing prevents useful
good—which, in its own way, is good in itself—from belonging in itself to the
lancing of a vein or to a very painful medicine. And nothing prevents
orderability, and being actually ordered to manifest the glory of punishing
justice and sparing mercy, from belonging to sin in itself. For nothing except
sin is in itself orderable to the glory of avenging justice, just as every sin,
sin alone, and sin always, is punishable by justice or pardonable by mercy. I
admit that sin is neither intrinsically nor accidentally a morally worthy good,
but is plainly opposed to it.

Second, what prevents sin from being good in itself to the sensory appetite,
and pleasant to the affections and the corrupt will?

Third, orderability is so called from the act of ordering in GOD. Now GOD is
said to order sin in two ways: either from eternity or in time. From eternity,
moreover, GOD orders sin in two further ways: either that sin should exist, or
that sin—now presupposed by the eternal ordering and decree—should be punished
or pardoned. The Arminians do not admit, in the former way, that GOD orders or
decrees that sin should exist, although they ought not deny that GOD
permissively orders that sin should exist. And in my judgment nothing can be
the intrinsic object of a permissive decree except sin alone. For GOD decrees
or wills nothing, either permissively or effectively, by accident; otherwise,
another and prior GOD who decreed it in itself would necessarily have to be
posited. Let the Opponents teach us who that GOD might be.

But if *to order* is understood as the eternal decree by which GOD resolved to
punish sin or pardon the sinner, then GOD must by no means be said accidentally
to decree that sin be punished, whether the expression *by accident* is
referred to the act of decreeing or to the act of punishing or pardoning, for
the reasons given above. But if *to order* denotes a temporal act and GOD’s
actual direction of sin to the good of divine glory—both the glory of justice
and that of mercy—this ordering is not an action

distinct from the act of punishing and pardoning sins. For this ordering is
GOD’s actual drawing forth of his glory from sin; but that very thing is the
action of punishing and pardoning. And thus sin is punishable or pardonable in
itself and because it is sin. But sin is not punished or pardoned by accident
(for this ought not be said, since sin alone is punished or pardoned). It
necessarily follows that GOD punishes and pardons sin in itself, and not by
accident.

Question 33. Is the will of the sign improperly and metonymically the will of
GOD? Answer. The will of the sign, insofar as it denotes GOD’s pleasing and
acceptable will which reveals to us what our duty is, is called the approving
will. It is distinguished by contrast from the will of good pleasure, and it
relates to two things. First, it relates to what we are bound to believe or do,
as something that binds consciences, provided that we wish to please GOD and
obtain the reward of obedience. Second, it relates to this obedience insofar as
GOD actually produces it in us, whether by special grace or by a more common
concurrence. The former will is a will of complacency and is properly called
will; its object is the rational creature’s duty. In this way GOD sincerely
approves and wills obedience, and takes pleasure in this moral good as in a
rule and norm; it is no less a will than the one called the will of good
pleasure.

But in the latter way it is not an executory will, as is the will of good
pleasure. Nor has GOD willed, intended, or decreed to produce in creatures,
through grace or concurrence, what he commands or forbids merely because he
commands or forbids it. And in this sense the will of the sign is called a will
just as improperly as punishment is called the wrath of GOD. For GOD, in
punishing, does what angry people, aroused and moved by the passion of anger,
are accustomed to do, although passions do not befall GOD.

Question. What kind of being does a future thing have from eternity? Answer. It
is not the being of the thing, real being, or actual being. For although
futurity is a certain mode of being, a mode of being cannot be real or actual,
and indeed so from eternity—as the futurity of the world, or the world’s being
future, was from eternity—when the thing which is future is by no means itself
from eternity. The world itself certainly did not exist from eternity, but was
created in time. Or put it this way: the futurity of a thing is not
[Marginalia: What kind of being future things have from eternity: namely,
objective, not real, being.]

the future thing itself, because futurity

(misprinted “605”); IA PDF page 680; BSB canvas 656]

is from eternity, whatever it may ultimately be—whether a real being, a mode of
being, or an objective being—whereas the future thing itself is a created being
which arose in time. It is therefore not absurd for an objective and potential
mode of being to exist from eternity in the intellect and will of GOD, while
the real being itself did not exist from eternity but arose in time and was
created by GOD.

Indeed, the futurity of a chair not yet made can exist for many years in the
craftsman’s mind and intention or will before the chair itself, or the wood from
which the chair is to be made, exists, is present, or has sprung from the seed
of a tree. And nothing is more often repeated than this: a thing’s being
desired, willed, and intended, and its ideal and intellectual formation in the
mind, precede—in the mind and will of an intellectual agent, whether by many
years or from eternity—the real thing itself which arose in time. Therefore,
the being which a future thing had from eternity is not a real being, but
ideal, objective, representative, and intellectual being in the
[Marginalia: Futurity and the future thing differ.]

intellect and will of an intellectual agent.

Thus the possibilities and futurities of created things can be modified from
eternity by Eternity and encompassed by it, while the things themselves,
according to their real being, are by no means encompassed by Eternity. For
nothing is encompassed or modified by Eternity except what is actual and what
exists. Yet a future thing that arose in time was no such thing from eternity;
only that most blessed uncreated being was.
[Marginalia: Futurity is eternal, while the future thing arose in time.]

Nevertheless, I do not think one should say that the futurities of created
things from eternity are beings of reason, mere fictions, or imaginary beings.

Question. Does futurity, then, signify nothing real at all? Answer. Futurity
taken passively, or the thing which is future, is nothing real. For whatever is
formally predicated by “it will be” or “it is future” does not yet exist outside
its cause. But “it will be” and “it is future” can be, and are, predicated of a
future thing—of the world which is to be created and has not yet been created.
That is: the world, not yet created but to be created, will be the world, but is
not yet the world. The reason for the major premise is that, in the case of
created beings, “it is” and “it will be,” or “it is present” and “it is
future,” cannot be predicated at the same time, for the coexistence of present
and future implies a contradiction.

Futurity taken actively, however, is not altogether devoid of anything real,
because it signifies the action of GOD, or the intellectual action of a
[Marginalia: Futurity taken actively and passively differ.]

created intellectual agent who forms the idea of a future thing, intends it,
and determines that it be made. And this is why, against the Jesuits, Arminians,
and

page 681; BSB canvas 657]

Socinians, we assert that GOD’s free will alone was the cause why all things
were future from eternity.

Question. Was a future thing future from eternity because of a second cause or
because of the first cause? Or is a thing future for this reason: that a second
cause gives it the fact that it will eventually become present? That is, is the
sun going to rise because the sun now rises by the impulse of nature? Answer.
The Jesuits and Socinians, and their offspring, the tribe of Arminians, teach
that a second cause is the cause both of a present thing and of a future thing.
But without doubt a second cause cannot be the adequate cause of a future thing,
for this is impossible in the case of all things that come to pass through
creation and GOD’s miraculous operation. On this account, the cause why the
world was to be future from eternity through creation would be the world itself
created in time.

But this is impossible, for the world is not a cause in the present which
confers being upon itself through creation. In that case the world would have
created itself, or would have supplied some causal influence toward creating
itself, which is monstrous. Therefore, the world cannot be the cause why it was
itself to be future through creation. Nor can water or any creature be the cause
why the conversion of water into wine was to be future. For at present there is
no cause in the water, none in the wine, and none in any created thing why water
should at present be transubstantiated into wine—unless water were to transform
itself into wine by the power of nature. Therefore, there was no cause in the
water or wine of that future transformation of water into wine. Nor can the
Jesuits dream this about their own transubstantiation. I would like those who
imagine a second cause to be the cause of things future from eternity to try to
solve this argument.

[Marginalia: Things created in time cannot be the causes why created things
were future from eternity.]

Second, *future* essentially signifies the determination of a thing, for what
is future is determinately future. Otherwise, if something were future only
possibly, and not determinately and indefectibly future, “not future” could be
predicated of what is future. For what is possibly future, and future by the
Opponents’ loose contingency, is just as much not future as it is future. Thus
what is future would be not future, and the distinction between future and
possible, as well as between future and contingent, would be overthrown. For
without doubt *future* adds to *possible* a determination toward “it will be,”
whereas the possible or contingent (for these differ very little in my
judgment) is what can be or not be. It is neither properly future nor nonfuture,
but formally indifferent to each.

[Marginalia: What is future is determinately and necessarily future.]

page 682; BSB canvas 658]

Now no action is determinately future in relation to a second cause, because it
is proper to a creature to act defectibly, just as the Creator alone produces
his effects indefectibly. For he is an efficacious, insuperable, and
irresistible cause, and indeed the cause of things, of being, and of acting.
For notwithstanding that a second cause exists and is poised to produce an
effect, the action may never be future. A creature is not the absolute,
efficacious, and insuperable mistress of things or actions. It therefore cannot
be the cause of future things, especially from eternity.

GOD, however, is the absolute Lord of creatures’ volitions, desires, and
intentions. For he promises that he will restrain the inward desires and
intentions of the nations, so that they will not covet, will, or intend to
invade the land of GOD’s people while the people are intent upon the worship
established by GOD at Jerusalem. Similarly, he is the unconquered and
insuperable cause of all things and actions, and therefore of all future things
as well. A chair which a craftsman intends to make in the following year is not
simply and determinately future. For even when it is posited that a craftsman
exists who intends to make it, it can nevertheless happen that it is never
made. But if GOD intends to do something, that thing is insuperably future.

Third, a being does not receive simply from a second cause the fact that it is
a present being, because a second cause is not a cause in itself or a being in
itself. Therefore, a being does not receive from it the fact that it is a future
being. But since GOD is a cause in himself and a being in himself, a being
receives from him being simply, and the fact that it is a present being and a
future being.

Question. According to the doctrines of the Jesuits, was anything future from
eternity? Answer. Nothing whatever. For since they deny that GOD’s free will is
the cause of what is future, Omnipotence is the cause of possible things, but
by no means of future things. And what has no cause has neither real nor
objective being. Therefore, if the Opponents are to be believed, what is future
from eternity is purely nothing.
