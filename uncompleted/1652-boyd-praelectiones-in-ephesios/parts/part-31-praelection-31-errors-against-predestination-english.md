Lecture XXXI.

Only this now remains: before we conclude this whole theological commonplace,
we should not so much formally refute as indicate in passing certain errors
contrary to the truth established above, errors upon which some people, either
long ago or recently, have struck as upon so many rocks, and show that they
cannot coexist with the truth thus far demonstrated from Holy Scripture.

[Marginalia: The refutative part.]

1. First, the doctrine explained thus far demonstrates that the Pelagians
committed a most destructive error. They confused the grace of election with
nature, whose fall and corruption they denied, and consequently rejected this
whole gratuitous decree of predestination, or sought its entire cause in the
foreseen merits of good works and the right use of free choice.

2. From this the error is also convicted of those who make foreseen faith and
unbelief the impelling causes—the former of election, the latter of
reprobation.

3. There is also the error of the Schoolmen, who assert that without some
special revelation no one can be certain of his election.

4. And there is that of other followers of the Roman Pontiff, who most
absurdly rave that by any mortal sin all faith and love are extinguished and
every gift of righteousness and the grace of the Holy Spirit are entirely
lost.

5. Then there is the truly disguised unbelief of those who mock with satire
and jeers every confidence of the pious concerning final perseverance and
God’s help in preserving them in grace—a help which will never fail and
utterly desert them—even when that confidence rests upon his own most sure
promise and is joined with its companion: zeal for godliness, newness of life,
and repentance, together with caution, prudence, and humility. This was
observed above when we unraveled Bellarmine’s sophistical darts.

6. Some think that this doctrine, as dangerous and either rocky or scrupulous
for these or those people, should be buried in silence in the Church. Its most
wholesome use, explained above in its proper place, sufficiently refutes them
and convicts them of ignorance or malice.

7. Finally, the same orthodox doctrine convicts of various errors the opinion
which I reserved for the last place and which must be examined somewhat more
fully. The Ubiquitarians embrace it for the most part with every effort, after
having devised it themselves recently or not very long ago. They say: 1. God
first determined to create the whole human race for eternal life, for they
shrink from the thought that anyone was created for death as very absurd. 2.
He then foresaw the fall, but by no means ordained it beforehand. 3. After all
people had fallen, he seriously willed that they should be saved and come to
the knowledge of the truth, and he redeemed them all in Christ, whom they hold
to have died for each and every human being universally. They thus establish
a kind of universal election as well as universal redemption. 4. From the
same supreme goodness of his nature, he determined to confer upon everyone
those aids of nature and grace by which they could be saved, and consequently
actually to call everyone. Their opinion thus posits a certain universal
calling of all people. Last, because he foresaw that some would embrace the
gospel by faith while others would despise or reject it, he rejected the
latter because of that contempt and unbelief and chose the former on account
of their foreseen faith. Yet they maintain that the latter can fall totally
and finally from that faith—that is, become reprobate after being elect.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the consubstantiators concerning predestination.]

I confess, however, that on this point it is scarcely consistent with itself
and that their opinion cannot be gathered with sufficient certainty. Some
frankly and openly confess that sin and unbelief are the cause of reprobation,
but hesitate to say similarly that foreseen faith is the cause of election.
Others acknowledge no other eternal will in God—that is, no decree concerning
human salvation—apart from what is proclaimed in the gospel, namely, “If you
believe, you will be saved.” Thus they establish only a conditional election,
one dependent upon human faith. Others make faith at least the instrumental
cause of election, so that God chose people because of faith, if not primarily
then certainly secondarily and with regard to it as election’s instrumental
cause. Finally, others say that from eternity God neither chose nor rejected
anyone, but daily chooses or rejects these or those people—that is, believers
or unbelievers. We have abundantly refuted all these things above in their
proper places.

[Marginalia: Diversity of opinions.]

They devised this absurd invention of human ingenuity to clear the divine
Majesty, as they thought, both from every stain of

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 172 | De Prædestinatione p. 128]

cruelty and injustice and, so to speak, from the efficient causation of sin.
They suppose that these disadvantages necessarily follow from the true
doctrine which we have asserted. In reality, however, their invention is
nothing but a jumble of various errors, as the following enumeration will
make evident.

[Marginalia: Refutation.]

First, since this opinion posits a certain universal election of everyone if
they believe, it follows that all election is abolished. Indeed, a universal
reprobation contrary to that election is posited as well—the reprobation,
namely, of every human being if they do not believe. This is in reality to
erase both election and reprobation completely and to maintain that God is
disposed with equal affection toward everyone and seriously wills everyone’s
salvation and conversion, yet neither chooses nor rejects anyone, but remains
as if suspended in mind and hesitating at a fork in the road. Nothing could be
more absurd to think.

[Marginalia: Error 1.]

Next, this same dogma posits in God a certain idle and ineffectual will to save
absolutely everyone and impart glory and eternal life to them, although this
by no means happens in most people and very many fall into death and eternal
destruction. Far be it from us to think any such thing about God, whose will
nothing can resist. Yet Damascene falls into this error in book 2 of *On the
Faith* when he distinguishes God’s will into antecedent and consequent. He
calls the former the will by which God, as supremely good, wills to confer
every good and blessedness itself upon the creature, and by this will wills
all people universally to be saved and attain his kingdom, because he did not
form—that is, fashion or create—us in order to punish us, but, because he is
good, to make us participants in his goodness. He calls the latter the will
by which, because of certain circumstances of the creature, God wills this or
that absolutely; and according to it God wills a person to be condemned for
sin because he is just.

[Marginalia: Error 2. Damascene, On the Faith, book 2, chapter 29.]

Whatever finally must be said about that distinction of the divine will into
antecedent and consequent, considered absolutely and in itself—whether it
should be approved or disapproved—we at least deservedly condemn and reject
the hypothesis of the eternal salvation of each and every person to which
Damascene applied it. We deny that any such will is to be posited in God by
which, either indefinitely or even on a condition, he wills each and every
person of every age to be saved, whether you prefer to call it antecedent,
consequent, or by any other name.

[Marginalia: Refutation.]

For 1. what does a will—or, so to speak, a mere inclination—indicate in the
one who wills except impotence, when it is a wish, desire, or a certain
disposition of mind of this kind which no effect follows? Whatever someone
earnestly longs for, wills, and desires, he will certainly accomplish if he
can and is not prevented. For example, a merchant seriously wills—that is,
longs and desires—to preserve his goods, and it is not because of him that
this fails to happen. But because he cannot withstand a greater force, he is
compelled by a storm to cast away his goods rather than his life. Thus what he
wills absolutely is what he actually accomplishes and carries out. We see
that antecedent will prevented in him, which very plainly exposes the
impotence of one unable to accomplish what he wills. The same thing would
necessarily happen to God if there were in him the kind of will which these
people rashly and impiously fabricate for him.

[Marginalia: Argument 1. Example.]

If they object that this is only a conditional will—namely, that God wills
people to be saved if they believe—this certainly merely exchanges one
absurdity for another and falls from a fever into consumption rather than
escaping. For it would follow that God’s will hangs in uncertainty, wavering
and hesitating while it awaits the outcome of the matter itself, uncertain
whether the condition will be fulfilled. Nothing more absurd can be imagined,
as was just said. Moreover, on this reasoning people will be assigned a free
choice either to believe or not believe, a faculty ready for either outcome,
although they possess it neither through nature nor through grace. They do not
possess it by nature, because that would abolish the grace of effectual
calling and openly contradict Scripture. They do not possess it by grace,
because this grace is not common to everyone; otherwise it would be nature,
not grace, as will presently be said. To those to whom it is given, the actual
willing, following, and believing are given—not merely a free choice to
believe or not believe. Hence it is evident that no such will, either apart
from a condition or dependent upon a condition, must be posited in God.

[Marginalia: The objection rejected.]

Next, it is foolish and ridiculous to posit a conditioned will in God’s
purpose when the condition itself, and whether it is fulfilled or not,
depends upon his will alone. His will does not, conversely, depend upon the
condition. For since he possesses infinite power, he will accomplish whatever
he wills without labor or expense; and since he possesses infinite wisdom and
is the first and supreme cause of all, he will subordinate every other cause,
condition, means, and other such inferior thing to his will.

[Marginalia: Argument 2 and the remaining considerations by accumulation.]

Finally, I omit the evil angels, of whom it cannot be said that God wills them
to be saved. I omit that when the apostle says that no one resists his will,
he speaks of the antecedent will—that is, the will which precedes every cause;
for it follows that it is identical with the absolute will, contrary to their
distinction. I omit that it also follows that God wills all people, insofar as
they are sinners, to be condemned eternally, by the same reasoning by which he
is said to will all people, insofar as they are human, to be saved. These are
absurdities. Does not the outcome itself sufficiently refute this impious
invention? We see some people truly believe and persevere, and consequently be
saved, while others either never believe or do not persist constantly, and
consequently are not saved. From where does this difference arise, unless
because God determined to accomplish this in believers but to desert the
others and not fulfill in them the same condition of salvation? Thus he willed
with his first and absolute will, for which no reason can be given, that these
should believe and persevere but the others should not. Accordingly, that
universal antecedent will concerning the salvation of each and every person
in Christ is utterly false and fictitious, and its assertion is impious
toward God himself.

[Marginalia: Romans 9:19. Proleptically.]

As for the passage in 2 Timothy and other similar places, from which they seem
to have gathered that opinion by misunderstanding them and upon which they
try to found their error, we answered them abundantly above and supplied their
true meaning.

3. This opinion not only overturns the entire order of causes by making the
first depend upon the second—that is, making it depend upon the human will,
which on this reasoning would direct and dispose God’s will in its second act
to will this or that according as the human will first chose to believe or not
believe. The contrary had to occur: the will of God, upon which all things
depend, had to direct and dispose every act of the creature, lest we raise the
creature onto God’s own throne and make it the artisan

[Marginalia: Error 3. 1.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 173 | De Prædestinatione p. 129]

and author of its own condition. Otherwise we would be compelled to ascribe
the glory of salvation and election not so much to God as to the person who
spontaneously embraces the grace offered to him. It also overturns the order
and sequence of the things themselves which are objects of the
divine will, since they posit that God foresaw the human fall before he
established any end toward which to direct it, when God had not yet decreed
what outcome that human fall would have. This directly conflicts with his
supreme wisdom.

[Marginalia: 2.]

[Marginalia: Amplification.]

Indeed, only bare foreknowledge or foresight of the human fall is established
there, without any antecedent decree by which God ordered it or decreed to
permit it and so willed its outcome. This is very absurd. That divine
foreknowledge or foresight is not the cause of events; on the contrary, God
infallibly foreknows and foresees that they will be because they are future.
They are future because he decreed that they would be, either so that he
himself would produce them if they are good in themselves and in their own
nature, or so that he would permit them to be done by others and order them
well toward a good end if they are simply evil in their own nature. Otherwise
something would certainly exist from itself, unless its occurrence, position,
or existence in the nature of things depends upon the divine will—that is,
unless we say that it exists and occurs because God willed it to exist and
occur.

[Marginalia: Confirmation.]

Let us omit foreknowledge—or rather what others call intuitive knowledge—by
which God sees in himself whatever is possible and likewise what is
incompatible with the nature of things, and thus sees not only what is or will
be future but also whatever could exist or happen if he willed it, since this
is not our present subject. What they call definitive foreknowledge, by which
God knows what will infallibly be future, must be preceded in the order of
nature and according to our manner of conceiving and understanding by his
effectual decree concerning the infallible occurrence of everything that will
be future—that is, concerning either its production, or its permission and
ordination to its ends. Their absurd hypothesis entirely subverts and
overturns this order.

[Marginalia: Note the distinction of foreknowledge.]

4. What a monstrous opinion is that universal redemption of everyone
accomplished through Christ, who on his part supposedly redeemed each and
every person and reconciled them to God, although as an outcome or by accident
very many happen to be condemned and perish. This is nothing other than
asserting that the Devil, sin, and so forth are stronger than Christ and God
himself, and, as Augustine aptly says in book 7 of *On Rebuke and Grace*, that
God is conquered by human vice, or at least changes and retracts his counsel
and will. That is what follows if at first he seriously willed that everyone
should be redeemed and saved through Christ, but afterward nevertheless
passed a different sentence and determined to destroy all who were unwilling
to embrace this benefit and bend and incline themselves to his will.

[Marginalia: Error 4. Augustine, On Rebuke and Grace, chapter 7.]

We answered universal redemption above in its proper place, showing that it
is inept to say that Christ died for each and every person. We acknowledge
that in Christ and his death are found not only a price and merit, but also an
efficacy, power, and strength more than sufficient to redeem a thousand
worlds. Yet it is one thing to say that Christ’s death is sufficient for the
redemption of everyone and quite another to say that Christ underwent death
with the design of redeeming absolutely everyone. The former statement
indicates only the magnitude of the merit and price, about which we have no
controversy. The latter implies God’s counsel and destination, as though the
will and counsel of God and of Christ the Redeemer himself were that he should
die also to redeem the reprobate themselves. From the outcome itself, the
plain authority of Holy Scripture, and various arguments, we have rejected
this as utterly false and absurd.

[Marginalia: Note the difference between the phrases.]

5. Along with that universal redemption, the same opinion carries another
falsehood with it no less: a universal calling, namely, or some grace common
to absolutely everyone. This truly abolishes and subverts the whole nature of
grace and cannot coexist with the mystery of the calling of the Gentiles, the
covenant which God formerly made with Abraham alone and his descendants
arising from Isaac and Jacob, or experience itself.

[Marginalia: Error 5.]

First, if from the very beginning of the world everyone has been called to
the knowledge of Christ by certain means, even if those means are unknown to
us, and if absolutely everyone is furnished by God with aids and supports of
grace by which they can attain the knowledge of the truth and therefore
eternal salvation, why, I ask, was it necessary to make a special covenant
with the Israelites, by which they above the other nations were chosen and
adopted as God’s peculiar people, while the rest were left in the ignorance
of their nature for a time appointed by God’s own counsel? Or what benefit was
conferred upon the Gentiles by Christ’s coming? Concerning them we see what
the apostle plainly testifies in Ephesians 2 before the completion appointed
by God of that acceptable time and of their calling and adoption into the
Church: they lived alienated from Christ, estranged from the commonwealth of
Israel and the promises of the covenants, without hope and without God in the
world, and consequently very far removed from all salvation and saving grace.
In Acts 17, moreover, the apostle says that God overlooked with forbearance
the times of that ignorance which had passed before Christ’s coming in the
flesh. Hence it is evident that this absurd invention plainly conflicts with
the clearest testimonies of Scripture, the covenant made with Abraham, and
the calling of the Gentiles from the advent of Christ.

[Marginalia: The first part of the refutation. Ephesians 2:12. Acts 17:30.]

Next, as for experience, why linger here and seek to demonstrate through an
enumeration of individual people—indeed, of whole nations—that this saving
grace by which they acknowledge Christ as Savior and Redeemer was not divinely
conferred upon everyone? I omit the many people who throughout their whole
lives are foolish, insane, and deprived of the right use of reason. Even the
weak and half-blind eye of human understanding is not open in them concerning
civil and moral matters and things belonging to this present life, since by a
hidden direction of divine providence the use of reason is hindered in such
people—much less has that divine and supernatural light of Christ ever shone
upon them. What, I ask, shall we say about the most acute philosophers of the
Gentiles? Some expressly attacked the immortality of the soul; others doubted
it throughout their whole lives. Some denied the creation of the world and
others divine

[Marginalia: The latter part.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 174 | De Prædestinatione p. 130]

providence. Some openly professed suspension of judgment and the
incomprehensibility of
all things—that is, they removed all truth either from the nature of things or
at least from our minds. Finally, although some had lived a life highly
praised among human beings, when they stood at its final boundary, they
frankly professed that they did not know whether it would be better for them
when departing from here to the majority or for the others whom they left
alive. Indeed, they thought the matter known and ascertained by no mortal, but
only by the gods. Those are the final words of Socrates speaking before his
judges in Plato’s *Apology*.

[Marginalia: The exhortatory conclusion.]

What, then, must be judged about the remaining mass of those people, enslaved
to the most foolish and grossest idolatry? What about those who throughout
their whole lives never so much as heard by report about the true God, Christ,
God’s word, or the whole mystery of human salvation, but passed their wretched
age in the thickest darkness of pagan errors? Shall it be said that they
lacked nothing necessary to obtain salvation? Far be such foolish and impious
madness. Not only experience itself but also the divine voice of Christ the
Savior attests the contrary: it is not given to everyone to know the mysteries
of the kingdom of heaven; the Spirit breathes where he wills; the Son gives
life to whom he wills and likewise reveals his Father to whom he wills; and
that saving doctrine of heavenly wisdom is hidden from the wise and
intelligent of this age. Innumerable other things of this kind could be
adduced from Isaiah 52, 55, and 65; Hosea 1; Acts 14; Psalms 79 and 147; and
similar passages. Finally, what would this be except openly bringing
Pelagianism back from hell—the claim that someone can attain eternal salvation
by a grace of this kind which is universal and common to everyone, that is,
by the powers of nature alone?

There is therefore that notable statement of Augustine writing against a man
of that faction: “You introduce,” he says, “a class of human beings which can
please God without faith in Christ, by the law of nature. This is the point
for which the Christian Church most of all detests you.”

[Marginalia: Augustine against Julian.]

Nor should anyone object to the promise concerning the seed of the woman, as
though it had been made in Adam to each and every human being. It is evident
that it was made indefinitely to Adam’s descendants and must be understood
first concerning Christ as the head, and then concerning all and only his
members—that is, each and every one of the elect comprehended in him as their
head. Otherwise we would have to assert that the reprobate too have conquered
the Devil and crushed his head, although it is certain that they remain
eternally bound in his chains—that is, sin and eternal death.

[Marginalia: Objection 1.]

Nor does Romans 1 help them by saying that God’s power is known to the
Gentiles from the very light of nature and the open book of creation, while
the apostle says in 1 Corinthians 1 that Christ is the power of God. It is
evident that in Romans the apostle means the power of creation, not of
redemption, which alone could in some measure become known to the Gentiles
from created things. Christ, however, is the power of God with regard to
redemption, as the apostle considers him in Corinthians, where he speaks about
Christ as Mediator and as crucified, whose preaching is foolishness to the
world.

[Marginalia: Objection 2.]

The ancients also acknowledged and taught with us that the grace of Christ
and the outward calling are not common to everyone. How could they have been
blind in so clear a light? I will adduce one passage of Augustine in place of
all, both because our memory might not be sufficient for more and because in
a matter so clear and obvious there is no need to pile up many. See his
treatise on Christ’s words in John 15, “But now they have nothing with which
to cover their sin.” He adds: “What he says, ‘But now they have no excuse for
their sin,’ can move those asking whether the people to whom Christ did not
come and to whom he did not speak have an excuse for their sin, and so forth.
To these inquiries I reply according to my understanding: they have an excuse
not for every sin, but for this sin, that they did not believe in Christ, when
Christ did not come or speak to them. But the people to whom he spoke in his
disciples and through his disciples are not in that number, which he also
does now, and so forth. It remains to ask whether those who, before Christ
came in the Church to the Gentiles and before they heard his gospel, were or
are overtaken by the end of this life can have this excuse. They plainly can,
but they cannot therefore escape condemnation. For all who sinned without the
law will perish without the law.”

[Marginalia: Augustine, Tractate 89 on John. John 15:22.]

But if the grace of the calling to faith in and knowledge of Christ were as
general as nature itself, those very people of whom Augustine speaks would
certainly have no excuse for the sin of not believing in Christ. Yet he
teaches how those who do not believe or did not believe in Christ are excused
from that sin: they were never called through the proclamation of the gospel,
as he gathers in that place from those words of the Lord.

If the fathers sometimes seem to assert universal grace and extend it to
everyone, their statements must be understood in a sound manner. We must
carefully attend whether they speak about that grace of the last time, whose
right, as it were, was extended to all nations by Christ’s coming; about
temporal gifts and benefits concerning the state of this present life, to
which they sometimes extend the name of grace because they are so many
monuments and testimonies of divine goodness and providence toward the human
race; or about that universal aptitude, or remote capacity, by which the human
will, although enslaved to sin, is nevertheless capable of divine grace and
faith if one regards its nature and not the blemish and corruption of nature.
This cannot be attributed to a stump, stone, or brute animal. Prosper
therefore says correctly, “To be capable of having faith belongs to the nature
of human beings, but actually to have faith belongs to the grace of the
faithful.” His teacher Augustine says, “This difference exists between evil
human beings and demons: even for very evil human beings reconciliation
remains if God shows mercy, but no conversion is reserved for demons.”

[Marginalia: Objection 3. Prosper, On Genesis against the Manicheans, book 1,
chapter 3. Augustine, To the Articles Falsely Imputed to Him, response 6.]

The fathers’ words must be weighed in this way, I say, and their meaning
sought from the purpose and sequence of the passage itself. It never occurred
to the fathers to make the grace of the faithful—namely, actually to will to
believe in Christ—common to all people universally. Indeed, it never occurred
to them to make common even the grace by which it is asserted that someone
can believe if he wills, in the sense that willing or refusing is placed in
his choice.

In the sixth place, this same opinion falls into

[Marginalia: Error 6.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 175 | De Prædestinatione p. 131 (through Prælectio XXXI close)]

the error we rejected above, by which election or reprobation is established
according to foreseen faith or unbelief, contrary to the sovereign freedom of
the divine will and grace, the order of causes, the truth of the things
themselves, and the manifest truth of Holy Scripture, as was clearly shown
above in its proper place.

7. Some who assert this dogma maintain not only that true faith can be lost,
but also that election itself can be changed and turned into reprobation in
accordance with a person’s apostasy and loss of faith. We also refuted these
blasphemous impieties abundantly above.

[Marginalia: Error 7.]

8. This opinion assigns to everyone a free choice made flexible in either
direction by grace. A person could supposedly either apply himself to the
grace set before him and embrace it by the aid of that universal grace, or
spurn and reject it out of nature’s corrupted impotence. But with this one
argument I will sufficiently prove that this is utterly false. It would
follow that by that universal grace alone everyone—even people who have never
heard of Christ so much as in a dream—could nevertheless come to Christ. Yet
Christ teaches in John 6 that this is false when he denies that anyone can
come to him unless the Father draws him. That is, he asserts that only the
elect, who are effectually called, can believe in him. About the unbelieving
Jews, however, he had said in the preceding chapter, “How can you believe,
when you seek glory from one another?” And in chapter 12, “Therefore they
could not believe, because Isaiah said again, ‘He has blinded their eyes and
hardened their hearts,’” and so forth. Where, then, is that universal grace by
which everyone can believe if they will, when Christ denies it even to those
to whom he deigned to preach his own gospel?

[Marginalia: Error 8. Refutation. John 6:44; 5:44; and 12:39.]

It is therefore evident that the sufficient grace spoken of by the papists is
an utterly false invention of the human brain. No grace is sufficient for our
salvation except one which is effectual for the same salvation, one which
overcomes and fills up all the incapacity of our nature and, with a contrary
power and indeed act, equips and enables us for salvation. It gives us the
capacity to receive the grace of Christ offered in the gospel, its right use,
and the grace of perseverance. Thus it works both in us, as it goes before,
prepares, renews, restores, softens, and—to say it in one word—makes alive our
souls which were previously dead in sin, and with us, as it brings that life
forth into act and work, fosters it in us, increases it, advances it, and
finally perfects it.

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

Augustine therefore rightly says: “If in the great weakness of this life the
renewed will were left to the regenerate so that they would remain in God’s
help if they willed, but God did not work in them so that they willed, the
will would succumb through its weakness amid so many and such great
temptations. Human weakness was therefore assisted so that it would be moved
inseparably by divine grace and, although weak, would therefore not fail or be
conquered by adversity.” Nothing could be said more plainly in
refutation of the invention of sufficient grace which nevertheless is by no
means effectual in the great majority of people. For in this passage he denies
that renewing grace itself, which restores to our wills the liberty lost in
Adam, is sufficient for the salvation even of the elect without confirming
grace which fortifies them in temptations so that the wills of regenerate
people never fall entirely from the good. How much less will the grace with
which they pretend those are endowed who are never actually converted to
Christ—indeed, who are never even called or invited—be called sufficient?

[Marginalia: Augustine, On Rebuke and Grace, chapter 12.]

And how much more rightly and truly does the same author say in chapter 17 of
the same book? “So that we may will, he works without us. But when we will,
and so will that we act, he cooperates with us. Yet without him working so
that we will, or cooperating when we will, we can do nothing toward good works
of godliness.”

[Marginalia: And chapter 17.]

But here they will object to passages of Scripture which seem to indicate the
contrary, of which we will adduce only these three examples.

[Marginalia: Objection 1.]

[Explicit Lecture XXXI. The following `First, Isaiah 5` and `Lecture XXXII`
are excluded.]
