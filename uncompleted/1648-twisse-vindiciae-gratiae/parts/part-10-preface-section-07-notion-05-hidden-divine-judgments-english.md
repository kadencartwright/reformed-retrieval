SECTION SEVEN NOTION V

PERKINS

God’s hidden and inscrutable judgments are to be honored and acknowledged. Augustine says: “It troubles me, you say, that one person perishes while another is baptized. It troubles me; it troubles me as a man. If you wish to hear the truth, it troubles me too, because I am a man. But if you are a man and I am a man, let us both hear the one who says, ‘O man!’ Surely, if we are troubled for this reason, that we are human beings, the Apostle is addressing human nature itself, weak and frail, when he says: ‘O man! Who are you to answer back to God? Does the thing formed say to the one who formed it, “Why have you made me thus?”’ If a beast could speak and say to God, ‘Why did you make that one a man, and me a beast?’ would you not rightly grow angry and say, ‘O beast, who are you?’ And you are a man, but in relation to God you are a beast.”

ARMINIUS

1. All God’s judgments, of whatever kind they are, whether hidden or in part known to us, are to be honored and adorned with praise for their justice, provided only that it is established that they are God’s judgments. But under this pretext no judgments are to be attributed to God which Scripture itself does not assign to him, and much less judgments which are contrary to God’s justice as disclosed in the Scriptures.

2. As to Augustine’s statement: the more like God a person is, the more the damnation of those who perish troubles him. It also troubled our Savior himself and more than once drew tears from him. It even troubled God himself, for he says, “What shall I do for my vineyard?” Isaiah 5. “O that my people had obeyed me,” Psalm 81. “I do not desire the death of the sinner,” Ezekiel 18. Yet God is so troubled that he nevertheless delights in the destruction of his stubborn enemies who do not know how to repent. For his justice requires this. I say he is troubled because they are unwilling to be saved; he is not troubled because, when they are unwilling, he consigns them to a just destruction. Christ the Savior is so troubled that he is nevertheless willing to banish unbelievers and evildoers and sentence them to eternal fire. For the office of Judge requires this. A godly person is so troubled that he does not contradict God when God determines otherwise and exercises the judgments of his justice upon the obstinate. The obedience which a creature owes to its Creator and Redeemer requires this.

3. But as to that objection, with Augustine’s kind permission let me say that it is not characteristic of weak and frail human nature, but of the stubborn disposition of the Jews and people like them, with whom the Apostle is dealing.

4. It is indeed true that, compared with God, we are like locusts and are even reckoned as nothing before him, Isaiah 40. But in these amplifications of human littleness, care must be taken that we do no injury to God’s creation. For a human being was made in the image of God and thus is, even to God himself, a human being and not a beast—the noblest creature, with whom God’s own wisdom says that she has her delights, Proverbs 8.

RESPONSE 1

We accept the condition. Meanwhile, see to it that, if the judgments which Scripture assigns to God happen to displease human judgment, you do not at once cry out that they are contrary to God’s justice disclosed in the Scriptures. For example: that God made “all things for his own sake, even the wicked for the day of evil”; that God commanded Pharaoh to let the Israelite people go, while in the meantime he hardened Pharaoh’s heart so that he would not let them go; that God sent an evil disposition between Abimelech and the Shechemites so that the Shechemites acted treacherously against Abimelech, and thus the injury inflicted upon the seventy sons of Jerubbaal—that is, their slaughter—might be repaid to their brother Abimelech, who had killed them, Judges 9:23–24; that the revolt of the ten tribes from Solomon’s descendants had its cause from Jehovah, so that he might fulfill the word which Jehovah had spoken through Ahijah the Shilonite to Jeroboam son of Nebal, 1 Kings 12:15, and again verse 24, “For this thing was done by me”; that Jehovah put a spirit of falsehood

into the mouth of all Ahab’s prophets, 1 Kings 21:23; that God declared to David that he would take David’s wives before his eyes and give them to his neighbor, who would lie with his wives in the sight of the sun, adding, “Because you did this secretly, I will do this thing before all Israel and before the sun,” 2 Samuel 12:11; that the Babylonians themselves, when they invaded Judaea, were like an axe and a saw in the Lord’s hand, Isaiah 10:15; and finally, that Herod and Pilate, together with the Gentiles and the assembly of Israel, gathered against Jesus to do whatever God’s hand and will had determined beforehand should be done, Acts 4:27–28.

2. Here, reader, you have, if you please, a specimen of a shifty mind. In response to Augustine’s statement, Arminius says that the more like God a person is, the more he is troubled by the damnation of those who perish. But tell me, I say, with what kind of emotion? Obviously with an emotion of compassion and mercy. But this has nothing to do with Augustine’s statement. For Augustine speaks not of an emotion of mercy toward a human being, but of an emotion of consternation toward God because of the inscrutable nature of his judgments, from which human beings are very commonly prone to indignation and even to imputing injustice to God. For Augustine understands the kind of emotion which the Apostle wishes to restrain lest it break out into blasphemy: “O man, who are you to answer back to God?” “It troubles him,” Arminius says, “that they are unwilling to be saved.” But what has this to do with Augustine, who is speaking of the kind of emotion which arises from consideration of God’s hidden judgments, not when others are unwilling to be saved, but when they cannot be saved—that is, whenever they are found to perish, not because they themselves are unwilling to be saved, but because God is unwilling to save them?

As for Arminius’s statement that God is troubled because they are unwilling to be saved, tell me: with what kind of emotion is he troubled here? There are no passions in God. Let us ascend to first principles. He wills all people to be saved, you will say, in the sense that he is said to desire their salvation. But how is it proved that God wills those people to be saved who have neither obtained salvation nor ever will obtain it? If he willed it, could he not easily grant them efficacious grace by which they would be converted and saved, and do so without any injury to the freedom of the will? I ask this to close off that customary refuge of yours. If he willed it, why did he hate Esau before he was born or had done anything evil? The words in Isaiah 5, “What shall I do for my vineyard?” do not prove that God willed the salvation of all those who were in that vineyard. It is sufficient if he willed the salvation of some of the elect, whom he also wished to arouse to repentance by this declaration of his concern, while rendering others all the more inexcusable, as is asserted in its proper place. Likewise, the words “O that my people had,” and so forth, when their anthropopathic phrasing is set aside, mean only that God would have delighted in his people’s obedience if it had been rendered and would have rewarded them magnificently for it. Finally, “I do not desire the death of the sinner” means a repentant sinner. Thus in these extravagances of his Arminius speaks with insufficient appositeness and coherence—to say nothing of what these things have to do with the emotion by which the man in Augustine is troubled. They are as far apart from one another as heaven and earth. For I do not think anyone will say that God is troubled and, as it were, dumbfounded at his own judgments.

Christ, I grant, was moved as a man, for he was a man and a minister of the circumcision. From the love by which as a man he was borne toward all human beings, especially toward his brethren, his people, and his own countrymen, he was held by grief—as in fact he was capable of grief—also on account of their sins and especially their unbelief. But this emotion has no affinity at all with the emotion of which Augustine speaks.

When Arminius finally turns to the godly person whom he also says is troubled, he comes nearer to the emotion of which Augustine speaks, for he says that this person is so troubled that he does not contradict God when God determines otherwise. Yet Arminius still conducts himself without candor or any tinge of modesty. For the coherence of these words with those preceding requires that the reason why this godly person is troubled is that people are unwilling to be saved. Augustine, however, when he speaks of a troubled person, understands him to be troubled not for this reason but by God’s hidden judgments, when some people perish not because they themselves are unwilling to be saved but because God is unwilling to save them. Next, when Arminius says, “A godly person is so troubled that he does not contradict,” where, I ask, does any occasion for contradicting the divine judgments arise whenever they are exercised only upon people who are stubborn and unwilling to be saved? Finally, the person of whom Augustine speaks is so troubled that he is on the verge of contradicting and would indeed contradict unless he were restrained by some such saying as, “O man, who are you to answer back to God?”

3. In his interpretation of the apostolic passage, Arminius opposes his own judgment to Augustine’s, but it is supported by no reason. There may be some who grant too much to Augustine, but those who dissent from him without reason assuredly grant too much to themselves and not enough to the judgment of so great a Father. Moreover, I have never yet encountered any tolerable probability by which I might be induced to believe what Arminius drives home by bare authority: that in Romans 9 the Apostle was dealing with stubborn Jews. But since Arminius and his followers think this foundation suitable for the structure of the rest of their interpretation, they immediately assume it on some kind of dictatorial authority, for they are people to whom it is permitted to thrust anything upon others without anything to support it. Behold craftsmen in their own field much more powerful than Archimedes, who could move anything, but not without something. Writers of every kind are accustomed to anticipate and resolve objections—not objections, I say, which necessarily are being raised by others, but those which they perceive can arise from what has been said. Thus Cajetan too observes that the objection which the Apostle meets with the words “O man, who are you?” arises from what has been said. Because of the weakness of our mind, which is as dim-sighted in heavenly things as an owl usually is before the sun, none of us is so far from formulating this objection that Augustine’s assertion is by any means foreign to reason: namely, that the Apostle addresses our weak and frail human nature when he says, “O man, who are you to answer back to God?” So too in the *Enchiridion*, chapter 98. Perceiving how what had been said could trouble those who cannot penetrate this depth of grace by their understanding, Augustine says: “What then shall we say? Is there injustice with God? By no means.” For it seems unjust that, without any merits of good or evil works, God should choose one person and hate the other.

4. Finally, when Augustine says that a human being is a beast in relation to God, Arminius assails him here too, so that you may understand how little he is moved by Augustine’s authority, since he proceeds to rebuke him in a matter where this is scarcely necessary. But let us see his reasons. “Care must be taken,” he says, “that in these amplifications of human littleness we do no injury to God’s creation.” But, I say, one who regards the creature as a beast by comparison with the Creator does no injury to creation. If Isaiah were alive today, would Arminius use the same admonitions to compose him to sobriety and rebuke him for saying that human beings are like locusts when compared with God? Would he therefore at the same time join in teaching the Holy Spirit τὸ σωφρονεῖν—to be sober-minded? If we can be called like locusts in relation to God without injury, why not like beasts? Augustine means nothing else. “He was made in the image of God.” I acknowledge it; and yet, according to the testimony of the Holy Spirit, he is like a locust in relation to God, so why not also like a beast in relation to God? Indeed, human beings are called like locusts in relation to God not only by comparison of littleness; by comparison of morals they are also called lions, bears, leopards, and even asps and basilisks in the sacred writings. “He is the noblest creature,” you say. Grant that after the angels he is the noblest, even so to God himself; I do not deny it. Meanwhile he can be called a beast in relation to God. See what the holy Prophet professes about himself: “I am a worm and not a man.” Observe in passing this Doctor’s customary deceit and crafty disposition. He says that a human being is a human being and not a beast to God himself, and says this as though resisting Augustine. But Augustine himself does not say that a human being is a beast to God, but a beast in relation to God—that is, by comparison with God he is as distant from God as a beast is from us. This is entirely true and would still have been true even if Augustine had added, “Indeed, much more.” For the vilest beast, indeed the vilest tiny creature, has some proportion in dignity to us; we have none to God.
