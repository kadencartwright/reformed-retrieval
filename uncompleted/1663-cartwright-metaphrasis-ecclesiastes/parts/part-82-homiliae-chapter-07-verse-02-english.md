VERSE 2. It is better to go to a house of mourning than to go to a house of
feasting, because the former is the end of every person; and one who lives
will lay it to heart.

From the circumstance accompanying each of the two things—riches and death,
which have been compared with one another in their unequal value—Solomon
confirms his teaching about the excellence of death over riches. For
lamentation, which friends undertake for the dead person, accompanies death;
but a feast is ordinarily furnished only by those who abound in resources.
Thus the very rich have also been accustomed to display their wealth in
costly feasts, as we read that Ahasuerus did, Esther 1. Solomon therefore
says that it is better to go to a house of mourning than to a house of
feasting.

He gives the reason for going to the house of mourning: there is the end and
final act of every person. Here it should be noted that death is the way of
all flesh, as is said in Joshua. Genesis 3. For it is God's eternal and
irrevocable decree that what was taken from dust must be reduced again to
dust. Psalm 90. God therefore declares to all without exception, “Return, or
turn back, you children of men.”

People are consequently foolish to invent for themselves a place of
permanent residence here on earth. Those who nearly reached their thousandth
year, who had stronger bodies and used nourishment more wholesome than ours,
were nevertheless cut down by the scythe of death. Yet even when people have
by God's kindness reached a decrepit age, they are never so spent with age
that they do not believe they can extend their life into yet another year.
When that year has passed, they conceive a new hope of life for the following
year. What else is this but to fashion a kind of immortality for themselves
in their own mind?

Next, by calling it the end, Solomon teaches that each person will remain such
as the day of his death finds him. This is contrary to the papal purgatory,
masses, and indulgences, by which people persuade themselves that they can
obtain some good after death. How desirable death is to the dying person will
be explained later in its proper place. Solomon now shows how useful it can be
to the living, so that the excellence of death over riches may shine out more
clearly from the usefulness that flows both to the dying and to the living.

The fruit that the living receive is that they reflect inwardly upon the
death of the deceased and apply their mind to its consideration. If it is
asked what someone can draw from meditation upon death for his own
edification, I reply that the fruit is so manifold that a lengthy discourse
would not suffice to enumerate it. Moreover, the matter is readily at hand,
and Scripture, as the Rabbis say, rests upon an intelligent reader.

The living, therefore, will consider the stench and deformity of sin: a body
created in the highest beauty and so handsome and pleasant to behold becomes
through the contagion of sin so hateful to every eye that its face must be
covered with a cloth. The sight of the cloth, though unclean, is less
nauseating and repulsive than the sight of by far the loveliest member in the
whole body of the dead. If this were carefully weighed, it would diminish the
vain fastidiousness of some in adorning and grooming themselves and putting
their beauty on display.

From the manner of death, or the disease that caused death, one may learn
God's justice, since the manner of death often answers to the character of
the life. If death is sudden, one may meditate upon the vanity of life and
take care that it never finds us unprepared, and learn almost infinitely many
other lessons. At a feast, besides the fact that lessons are not supplied so
abundantly, even those it does furnish are seldom

learned. Instead, people pour themselves out into every dissolution of mind
and body. And it rarely happens that a feast furnished lavishly and
magnificently passes without some great misfortune, or that what began like a
comedy does not end like a tragedy. Examples are the feast that Absalom
furnished, 2 Samuel 13; that of Ahasuerus, Esther 1; that of Herod, Mark 6;
and those of others. Nor does this happen only among the wicked, but also
among those who worship God.

Hence Moses warns God's people to take care that, when they have drunk and
eaten to satiety, they do not consign God to oblivion. Deuteronomy 8. From the
same source comes the fact that Job, though blessed with children who had
been instructed well and piously, was exceedingly anxious lest something
might have escaped them during their feasting that would provoke God against
them. Job 1. On this account he also called them to sacrifice and perform
sacred rites.

This should warn us not to devote ourselves to frequent feasts and, whenever
we attend them—especially if we furnish and set them before others—to employ
great caution, lest, with God provoked against us, our honey be turned into
gall and our gladness into sorrow.
