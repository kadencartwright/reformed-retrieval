SECTION TWO.

JUN.

The minor is denied, for in this act God regards mankind collectively. To confirm the minor, you
use two arguments.

ARM.

God did not will for any person considered in the first state of nature natural blessedness alone,
to the exclusion of supernatural blessedness. For all people are considered equally in Adam,
whether in the primeval state of nature or in the state of sin, unless a distinction is introduced
by the will of God. But I deny that the distinction was made on account of the first state, and you
establish the first reason for the denial when you say that both kinds of blessedness were prepared
for man.

Next, what God prepared for man by his providence he does not deny to him through preterition,
unless from foresight that the man will not attain it under the guidance of providence but will
freely turn aside of his own accord. God prepared supernatural blessedness for the first man and,
in him, for all people. For he gave him means sufficient to acquire it, with the added assistance
of divine grace—if indeed that is necessary in this state—which he does not deny him unless he has
first been deserted by him. Whatever truth there may be in what Junius discusses here, it
nevertheless wanders entirely beyond the bounds of the subject.

RESPONSE.

This is what you mean: God did not will supernatural blessedness to be denied to anyone considered
in the first state of nature. We deny that proposition. We say that when Adam and all people
created in him were upright, God had already willed supernatural blessedness to be denied to some.
All people in Adam are considered equally, I admit, and no distinction arises except by the will of
God. For this is precisely what we assert: by his mere good pleasure God destines supernatural
blessedness for some and denies it to others. Arminius has therefore thus far accomplished nothing
by which he might strive after even the appearance of a reason. But perhaps what follows will
supply what was lacking. “I deny that the distinction was made on account of the first state.”
Unless Arminius plays the sophist, he has no way to proceed.

Those who say that God, at his own discretion, either destines or denies supernatural blessedness
to a person considered in the first state of nature do not at all mean that some reason is found in
the consideration of the first nature why God either denies or destines supernatural blessedness.
Rather, they derive this distinction from God's mere good pleasure. Junius says so expressly and
repeats more than once that nothing considered in them is a cause. When Arminius himself required
sin before the decree, he did not wish to appear to require it as a cause, but only as a condition.
Here, however, he quite craftily leaps from a condition to a reason. “I deny that the distinction
was made on account of the first state,” he says; for to give a reason is to give a motivating cause
of the decree.

Likewise, when God decreed to create, how, I ask, did he consider people before he created them?
Whatever consideration you imagine, no reason arises from it which moves God to create. By his mere
good pleasure he creates whom he wills and such as he wills; those whom he does not will and such
as he does not will, he does not create. God considered the angels as already created. Whatever
consideration of them you imagine to be presented to God, he nevertheless gives efficacious grace
for perseverance to those to whom he wills, and does not give it to those to whom he wills not.
Likewise, of people fallen into sin, he mercifully calls whom he wills to faith and repentance and
hardens whom he wills. If, therefore, you philosophize about a consideration which discovers
something in man that assumes the character of a cause moving God to elect or reprobate, we wholly
deny that man is or can be considered by God in that way. Arminius adds that Junius establishes
this reason of his when he says, “Both kinds of blessedness were prepared for man.”

Junius certainly had said that God destined it for Adam under the condition of keeping to the way.
We too profess that even now supernatural blessedness is destined for all under the condition of
faith and repentance. But those words must be received correctly, not understood perversely, as
Arminius is accustomed to corrupt everything with his breath. God destined blessedness to be
possessed

under the condition of keeping to the way. That is, the possession of blessedness—and that by
God's destination—depended on obedience, but the destination itself did not. In the same way today,
the attainment of salvation—and that by God's destination—depends not only on faith but also on
good works, yet the destination itself does not, lest election be established as being from works
while the Apostle raises no protest. Thus the weakness of the condition in Arminius's reasoning
has been exposed; we shall now contend against him with a reason. If the foresight of Adam's
obedience, or of our faith and repentance, preceded destination to salvation, then certainly the
destination of efficacious grace for obedience, or even for faith and repentance, would precede
destination to salvation. But this is impossible: the intention of obedience cannot precede the
intention of salvation, since obedience does not assume the character of an end, nor salvation the
character of a means to an end. For that reason alone, the intention of obedience would be earlier
than the intention of salvation. We do not attain salvation in order to obey God. On the contrary,
by God's grace we obey God in order to attain salvation. If God's granting of efficacious grace for
obedience were intended before the conferral of salvation, it would certainly be later in
execution; that is, people would attain salvation before obeying God.

What Arminius submits in the second place is therefore false: namely, that through preterition God
does not deny blessedness to a man except from foresight of his sin. For if sin were foreseen before
the denial of blessedness was destined, then the permission of sin would be decreed by God before
the denial of blessedness. The permission of sin would therefore have the character of an end and
the denial of blessedness the character of a means to that end, so that blessedness would be denied
to a man for the purpose of permitting him to sin—than which nothing is more absurd to say. It is
far more plausible to say that God permits a man's sin in order that blessedness may be denied to
him, though it by no means pleases me to affirm even that. Again, if the permission of sin were
intended by God before the denial of blessedness, it would certainly take effect later: blessedness
would first be denied to them and their sin permitted afterward.

The argument on which Arminius relies has no force unless it is proposed sophistically. But neither
will his sophistry help him. The denial of salvation is properly opposed to the conferral of
salvation, not to its destination. Yet to make his sophism proceed more successfully, he restricts
the denial by calling it denial “through preterition,” when he should instead have said that God
did not destine its denial, and so forth. With respect to the form of the argument, therefore, we
grant that whatever God prepared for man to obtain under a condition—and I add those words to
explain the proposition—he does not deny to him unless God not only foresees but the man's sin
actually exists in his failure to perform the condition prescribed for him. The sum is this: not
only foresight of the sin committed by failing to perform the stipulated condition, but its real
existence, precedes the denial of the salvation which God prepared for the man to obtain under that
condition. But it by no means follows from this that foresight of sin precedes the destination of
this salvation. Arminius is always straying on this string and thereby raving in his customary
fashion. Good works too precede the conferral of salvation, but the foresight of good works does
not on that account precede the destination to confer salvation. Therefore, although God destined
the first man and all people in him to attain supernatural blessedness under a certain condition,
and although he also gave means sufficient to perform that condition, so that unless the man used
them rightly to perform the condition for obtaining blessedness he would by no means obtain it;
nevertheless, unless God granted him efficacious assistance to perform the condition, he would
never perform it and so would never obtain blessedness by those means. The absolute destination of
blessedness to be obtained by those means depends on the absolute destination of this assistance.
Therefore, according as God either absolutely destines this assistance for people or does not
destine it, he will be judged either to have absolutely destined them to obtain salvation by those
means or not to have destined it.

Arminius had seen this: namely, that from the absolute destination or non-destination of assistance
follows the absolute destination or non-destination of blessedness. To prop up his collapsing cause
at this point, therefore, he pretends that this assistance too was to be granted under a condition:
unless God had first been deserted by Adam. This is the customary solecism most commonly employed
by the Arminians. For since the stipulated condition is the condition of obedience, which cannot be
performed without God's assistance, Arminius maintains that God denied Adam his assistance for
obeying God because Adam had already deserted God. What else is that than because he had already
failed to obey God? Hence it follows that Adam renounced God by two degrees before he renounced
him—that is, deserted God before he deserted him. But Arminius preferred, so that the matter might
be concealed more conveniently, to confuse things that should have been distinguished. He says
that God “gave him means sufficient to acquire it”—namely, blessedness—“with the added assistance
of divine grace,” as if the assistance of divine grace contributed immediately to attaining
blessedness rather than to performing obedience. If he had stated it in that way, as he should have
done, the irrationality would have been far more apparent. For when he places Adam's sin of
deserting God before the denial of divine assistance, it is manifest how absurdly the denial of
divine assistance is made to follow Adam's disobedience. For according to Arminius that
disobedience can occur even without the denial of divine assistance; he maintains that God's
assistance was denied to Adam only once Adam had already deserted God.

But unless I am mistaken, Arminius had also perceived the nicety involved in constructing this
opinion, and therefore wished to hunt elsewhere for support for himself and his collapsing
opinion. He eagerly seeks to establish that divine assistance was not necessary in that state—that
is, while Adam was still upright—although he only suggests a suspicion of this in passing, as if in
parentheses. He evidently thinks it could have happened that Adam persevered in that state without
the assistance of grace, and Arminius judged it more probable that the good angels in fact
persevered in their primeval state without the assistance of grace. Let the angels therefore
attribute to themselves, and not to the assistance of divine grace, the fact that they remained
unmoved in their obedience toward God—if only they allow themselves to be instructed by our
Arminians! Yet Arminius had already professed that God made manifest in the angels the benefit of
his preserving grace.
