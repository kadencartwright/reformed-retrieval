VERSE 6. For if they fall, one of them raises up his companion; but woe to
him, to the one man who has fallen, when there is no second to raise him.

So far the reward of human society has been discussed in general. Its parts
must now be considered. The first benefit of human society to occur is the
raising up of the man who falls, which is attached to it as a circumstance.
It often happens that men making a journey fall so dangerously, without
anyone's push or the application of force, that unless someone is present to
raise them, the fall is fatal and leads to destruction. If a man has no
traveling companion,
it is entirely over with him. Once he himself is removed by death, he is
immediately deprived of the use and enjoyment of the resources he acquired by
his labor. Thus it is a great aid toward receiving the fruit of one's labors
if one joins a companion to oneself in conducting business.

What is stated is illustrated by its opposite, namely, the curse which
usually befalls the solitary man: “Woe,” Solomon says, “to the man alone, who
has no one standing by to raise a fallen man to his feet and restore him.”

This fall is harmful to the body, but it is much more destructive when someone
falls in spirit because of misfortune or some grievous calamity.

It is more harmful still if someone falls into sin and has no one at hand to
draw him out of the pit of sin into which he has fallen. In a bodily fall it
is considered shameful and childish for someone who is stretched upon the
ground to remain lying there. He therefore rises as quickly as possible so
that no one may discover that he fell. In a spiritual fall, however, the
matter is far different: after falling headlong a man is scarcely, if ever,
raised and restored to his feet unless he is recovered by the extended hand
of chastisement

and exhortation. On this matter, see the example of David in 2 Samuel 12. He
slept in the sin of adultery until, at the arrival of Nathan the prophet, he
was awakened from the sleep of sin. See the same in the example of the godly
king Jehoshaphat, 2 Chronicles 19–20.

Christ appears to have regarded this usefulness in the principle of civil and
spiritual society when
he sent first the twelve apostles and then the seventy disciples only in
pairs, with the purpose that they might mutually aid one another with the
supports and helps both of the present and of the future life. Matthew 10;
Luke 10.

The Holy Spirit also had the same end in view when he established two
ministers in each church: the one a doctor who would devote himself to
doctrine, the other a pastor who would attend to exhortation. Thus, when one
was sick with disease or occupied in the necessary business of providing for
his household, he would be assisted by the other.

By far the safest and surest remedy for the evil which threatens because of
solitude is for a man to be united to God by true faith. Whatever
blood relationships, relationships by marriage, friendships, and patronage he
has either obtained by nature or acquired by effort, times nevertheless
sometimes occur when, however greatly they may wish it, these people cannot
aid their laboring friend or relative. When men are shut up in prisons and
torn away from everyone, God alone both can and wills to be present with us.
On this matter, see the example in Genesis 39:21.

Indeed, it happens not infrequently that men appear surrounded and fortified
by a multitude of friends, but are nevertheless deserted by their own people
if it happens that they are placed in some graver danger. On this, see 2
Timothy 4:16–17.
