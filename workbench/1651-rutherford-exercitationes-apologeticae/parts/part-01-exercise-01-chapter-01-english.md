EXERCISES IN DEFENSE OF DIVINE GRACE, against the JESUITS and ARMINIANS.

EXERCISE I

On the friendly association of the divine decrees and divine will with human
freedom.

CHAPTER I

On the nature of human freedom, and whether Suarez and the Arminians give a
sufficiently philosophical and theological account of its nature as a power to
act or not to act when all the requisites for acting have been posited.

THESIS I. According to the common opinion, one cause is natural—one “whose
principle is in itself,” as the Philosopher has it—and it acts necessarily, as
fire does. Another is free, and can either act or not act, in this way or in
another, as human freedom can. Although the latter acts naturally, from an
internal principle—that is, in opposition to what is violent and

[Marginalia: Aristotle, *Physics*, book 2, text 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 2 | IA leaf 20 | PDF p. 20]

fortuitous—or even acts toward something supernatural, and therefore ought in
this sense to be called a natural cause, it is nevertheless not determined to
produce one effect. It is accordingly regarded by right as not a natural but a
free cause. The will, then, is not contingently but necessarily free, as Thomas
Jackson fabulously claims, although it acts contingently; for freedom concerns
not *being* but *acting*. Natural freedom is a property of the will: the latter
regards the end, and indeed necessarily, while the former regards the means
contingently and freely. I do not know whether the Scholastics are right to
teach that the will does not lack freedom of exercise. For although it could
not refrain from willing blessedness if it elicited an act concerning it, it
can nevertheless elicit no act concerning it by calling the intellect away
from actually thinking about it. For freedom, as a commanding power, applies
both the will and the intellect to acting, or calls them back from acting; it
is not the will that does this.

[Marginalia: The Englishman Jackson, on the divine essence.]

2. Thus the will, by which it acts freely, is in itself—so far as it is
will—determined by nature to willing the good. And this freedom in first act is
neither radically nor formally in the intellect, since this appetitive faculty
immediately inheres in the soul without any intervening mental reasoning. I am
not a little surprised how a free power could belong to the will through the
mediation of an intellective power, since the will is just as immediately and
intrinsically free as the mind is cognitive. But is freedom radically in the
intellect because judgment is the foundation of freedom?

[1651 logical printed p. 3 | IA leaf 21 | PDF p. 21]

By an equal line of reasoning, say that cognitive power is radically in the
will—which is unheard of—because the intellect does not cognize unless the will
applies it to acting. Consequently, this argument establishes that the
exercise and use of freedom are radically—or, more properly, directively—in
the intellect, while formally and effectively they are in the will. This I
readily concede.

3. The object of free choice is twofold, either material or formal. The
material object is the good, the evil, or the indifferent. The formal object
is this whole eligible thing insofar as it is a good conducing to the end: “For
no one wills anything except what he supposes to be good.” Nor is this good
one that is such to the senses; it is morally good. For we do not properly
choose to live, to exist, or to sleep—nature, not our freedom, seeks these
things—except insofar as they are brought within moral being. This good is also
known; yet I do not believe that cognition belongs to the formal account of
the object, but is only a condition required in the object. Thus the good that
is in the mind is the object of the agent who directs something to an end, not
insofar as it is in the mind. See Scheibler and Rutgers on metaphysics.

[Marginalia: Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, book 1, chapter 10.]

[Marginalia: Scheibler, *Metaphysics*, book 1, chapter 22, title 4; Rutgers,
*Metaphysics*, book 2, question 11.]

4. The assertion that evil as evil is desired can be understood either with
the qualification referred back to the evil or with it referred to the one
desiring. In the former way it is only too true, alas, that an evil which is
formally and intrinsically evil—such as adultery, murder, and so forth—is
desired. In the latter way it cannot be desired, for in every desire for evil
(even if the sin is against the Holy Spirit) there intervenes, if not formal

[1651 logical printed p. 4 | IA leaf 22 | PDF p. 22]

ignorance, at least interpretive ignorance, which is the free failure to
consider the object.

5. The will is considered: first, as nature, and it has this in common with
other agents, that it tends toward good; second, as such a nature, and in this
respect it tends toward blessedness by an essential inclination antecedent to
freedom, as Thomas intimates, not by mental deliberation or freely; third, as
free; and fourth, as good or evil. Considered simply as free, its acts are
indifferent; considered as good or evil, it is subject to law. A physical
freedom to act or not to act belongs to freedom in every state, although it is
always restricted to willing well or badly. An inclination indifferent to good
or evil, however, is never found in nature, whatever the sophists dream to the
contrary. For after the fall there is an inclination to evil in the
unregenerate; and in the regenerate there is an inclination to good insofar as
they are spirit, and to evil insofar as the flesh dwells in them. Yet here
there is no pure Arminian indifference. An indifference unrestricted by flesh
and spirit no more exists than a generic animal nature exists apart from a
human being or a brute animal.

Before the fall, however, there was no inclination to evil in Adam, for his
holy will inclined toward fulfilling God’s law. But you will say: there was
actual sin in Adam, and therefore there was beforehand in him an inclination
and power to sin, since an act in creatures flows only from a preexisting
power. I answer: there was in him a natural power for eating,

[Marginalia: Aquinas, *Summa*, I–II, question 83, article 1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 5 | IA leaf 23 | PDF p. 23]

and for not eating the fruit, which were acts merely indifferent by nature. It
was added to those acts as something extrinsic that they were restrained or
determined by law. Adam, however, merely put a natural power into act. For the
power to sin either regards the entitative act of sin—and this in itself was
not an indifferent moral power—or it regards lawlessness itself, and this was
not properly a power, but the natural power itself insofar as it was subjected
to law. Indeed, that power would not have existed if God had not prescribed a
law to the human being, as he was free not to do, since he freely imposes laws
upon his creature. Bondage and a vicious inclination to sin are indeed present
in a human being after the fall, but this is not so much a power as a curvature
of the power. It no more belongs to the nature of free choice than a power to
limp belongs to the formal account of the locomotive faculty.

I infer from this that the freedom of grace does not add a new freedom to the
freedom of nature, but only a new power which qualifies and elevates natural
freedom. For if it were a new freedom, the regenerate person would have two
distinct freedoms, and thus two wills—and why not also two intellects? For if
properties are multiplied, their subjects are multiplied too. Second, freedom
properly is a vital faculty for refusing and willing and, insofar as it is
subject to law, for sinning or not sinning. Yet who would say that grace gives
the regenerate person a power to sin? For grace disposes one not to sin, not to
sin. Third, just as willing and willing

[Marginalia: Adam sinned in this, not because he simply put a natural power
into act, but because he put into act a power subjected to and restricted by
law through God’s prohibition, from a free failure to consider the forbidden
object.]

[1651 logical printed p. 6 | IA leaf 24 | PDF p. 24]

the good are not two distinct acts, so too the freedom of willing and the
freedom of willing the good are not two distinct freedoms. The reasoning is
the same in both cases; and for these reasons the freedom of nature and the
freedom from sin are not two distinct freedoms.

6. The form of free choice is not spontaneity or absence of coercion, for this
belongs to the intellect and will in their relation to the highest good. And
although Calvin uses this expression, for lack of words, to explain the hidden
nature of freedom, he nevertheless adds to non-coercion a power to approve,
reject, and exert oneself. But let the papists also charge their angelic
doctors with this fault. Thus Aquinas says that humanity lost freedom, not with
respect to natural freedom, which is freedom from coercion, but with respect to
freedom from guilt. Likewise Lombard says that to move freely is to move
voluntarily and by a spontaneous motion. “The necessity of coercion,” says
Bonaventure, “is incompatible with free choice, but the necessity of
immutability is not.” “There have been,” says Francisco Suarez, “some modern
Catholics who denied that indifference, or the absence of necessity, is
required for an act’s freedom, but held that the absence of coercion is
enough.” Indeed, Augustine explains freedom by absence of coercion. Estius
excuses him; in the same way Bellarmine excuses Prosper against Cassian; yet
Calvin will be accused, even though here he adheres to Scripture, 1 Peter 5:2:
“shepherd the flock, not under compulsion, but willingly.”

[Marginalia: Calvin, *Institutes*, book 1, chapter 5, section 14.]

[Marginalia: Aquinas, *Summa*, part 1, question 83, article 2.]

[Marginalia: Lombard, book 2, distinction 25.]

[Marginalia: Bonaventure, book 2, distinction 25, question 1, article 2.]

[Marginalia: Suarez, *On Grace*, part 1, prologue 1, chapter 1, § 9.]

[Marginalia: Augustine, *City of God*, book 22, chapter 30; Prosper, *Against
the Collator*, chapter 7.]

7. Our theologians maintain that freedom’s nature consists in immunity from
coercion

[1651 logical printed p. 7 | IA leaf 25 | PDF p. 25]

and also from natural necessity arising from the judgment of practical reason;
in this they obey the truth. For in reality this commanding, internal, vital,
and elective power—by which free choice, when an object is presented through
the intellect under the aspect of good, applies itself to acting—is the formal
account of free choice.

8. The Jesuits Suarez, Vasquez, Fonseca, Luis de Molina, Becanus, and Jacob
Arminius; the Remonstrants’ writings for the Synod; and Corvinus hold that its
nature is a power to act or not act even when all the requisites for operation
have been posited. With God’s gracious help, we shall see whether they advance
this without sacrilegious boldness against God’s providence and grace. In the
meantime I wish the following points to be observed.

First note: the power to will or refuse is one simple power. For if there were
two powers—or one power regarding both objects conjunctively—then nothing would
prevent the will, by the same power, from willing and, by the same power,
refusing; from refusing and willing the same thing, in the same respect, toward
the same object, and at the same time. This is a grave absurdity.

Second note: a free power is considered in two ways. First, as an individual
thing and the individual terminus of divine creation, insofar as creation is
distinguished from the act of providence; so considered it is simply
indifferent to refusing and willing, because according to its natural mode of
acting, willing no more than not willing belongs to the essence of freedom.
Second, it is considered as that same power capable of being subjected to
providence, and

[Marginalia: Suarez, *On Divine Grace*, prologue 1, chapter 2; Vasquez, on I–II,
disputation 189, chapter 14; Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 6, chapter 2,
question 5, section 8; Molina, *On the Concord*, disputation 14, article 13,
question 10; Becanus, *Theology*, part 2, treatise 1, chapter 1; Jacob Arminius,
*Declaration of Sentiments*, article 8, p. 107, Bertius edition; Remonstrants,
*Acts of the Synod of Dort*, articles 3 and 4; Corvinus against Bogerman, part
2, chapter 7, p. 239.]

[1651 logical printed p. 8 | IA leaf 26 | PDF p. 26]

in this respect the disjunctive power is determined to one side, with the
nature of the power preserved, and not to the other. For in relation to God,
whether we consider his knowledge—since he knows everything—or his will, which
does not hang upon anything created or creatable but is determined from
eternity, there is no disjunctive power. A created power can indeed be
considered without considering the divine decree, and in a conceptual instant
antecedent to God’s decree; but in such a chimerical consideration our
opponents vainly bring us a quarrel not worth buying even at the price of a
rotten nut. Yet there is no created power except one that is subject to God’s
eternal decree, unless someone wishes, along with the atheists, to renounce
divine providence.

On the former consideration, the power is indeed an actually determinate
being, but its acts are only possible and have being only in relation to
omnipotence, not in relation to God’s decree. Once the decree is posited,
however, especially a conditional one, the question whether a free power is
indifferent to acting or not acting is vain. For under that condition the power
is determined, and the question is the same as asking whether a power which is
not indifferent but determined is indifferent and undetermined. And once an
absolute decree concerning all things is posited, it is foolish to ask whether
a free power under that decree is indifferent. For the absolute decree posits
the futurity of the determination and that indifference will be removed.
Therefore, in the Jesuit-Arminian sense—namely, as they use the word
*indifference*—the question is empty and vain; but the word

[1651 logical printed p. 9 | IA leaf 27 | PDF p. 27]

*indifference*, if referred to the object, denotes the objective indifference of the
judgment that presents the object—namely, that by its nature the will can will
or refuse it, since the essence of freedom would be no more impaired if freedom
embraced this rather than that. The question would then be easily resolved. But
because it is difficult to fix the limits of the question with obstinate
opponents, we must follow and pursue our adversaries wherever the course of the
disputation leads us.

But they will object against this indifference that such indifference belongs
to a natural cause: not heating impairs the essence and first act of fire no
more than heating does. I answer: although the essence of fire is preserved
under non-heating in the case of a miracle, its natural mode of acting is not
preserved. Yet neither the first nor the second act of a free power is impaired
whether the will wills this or not. We shall therefore grant a twofold
indifference in a free power. One is objective, when the intellect presents to
freedom an eligible object as a means not absolutely necessary to the highest
good intended. The other is internal, vital, and elective, by which the free
will is able of itself both to will and to refuse this, while both the essence
of freedom and its most natural mode of acting remain intact.

[Marginalia: See the Remonstrants, in the *Apology: Examination of the
Censure*, chapter 7, folio 81.]

9. Hence our first assertion: this objective and internal indifference belongs
intrinsically and formally to a free will; and through it freedom is formally
distinguished from absence of coercion, from spontaneity, and from all natural
causes.

[1651 logical printed p. 10 | IA leaf 28 | PDF p. 28]

This indifference belongs to freedom in itself and in its primary conception.
For if you ask what freedom is, the answer is: an elective power which, of
itself and with respect to its natural mode of acting, by its own internal and
vital command, is equally able to choose or not choose this means; and by this
it is distinguished from other causes. Therefore this is the formal account of
freedom.

Second, that is the formal account of freedom through which such mastery of
its act as befits a created being is left to the will. But through these kinds
of indifference freedom has such mastery of its own act. Therefore it is the
formal account. The opponents will concede the major premise. I prove the
minor. When these kinds of indifference are posited, no external agent, not
even God, can overthrow freedom in its natural mode of acting. Therefore,
mastery of its own act will always be left to the will. I prove the antecedent.
The formal account of choice is the objective union with a good presented by
the intellect, not as necessary to the end intended. But freedom itself is the
intrinsic and vital—and indeed the sole—cause of this union.

I illustrate this by a hypothesis. Cyrus freely wills the liberation of God’s
people. God indeed contributes efficient being to this volition of Cyrus,
since Jehovah stirred up the spirit of Cyrus; but God contributes no vital or
formal being to that volition. This is because an efficient cause, as external,
cannot contribute formal or internal being to the effect unless the efficient
cause becomes a formal cause or co-cause. But God was not the formal cause or
formal co-cause of Cyrus’s volition.

[Marginalia: Ezra 1:1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 11 | IA leaf 29 | PDF p. 29]

For then it would not have been the will of Cyrus, but God in Cyrus, that
formally and vitally willed that liberation—which is plainly atheological and
repugnant to reason. Furthermore, God creates no greater objective union
between Cyrus’s freedom and the liberation of the people than effectively
bringing that indifferent object near to its vital cause, by stirring up
effective reasons for that volition and physically predetermining Cyrus’s
freedom so that he wills it. Yet Cyrus regarded that liberation only as a means
to his happiness, and his freedom would have remained uninjured both in first
act and in its natural mode of acting, whether he had willed that liberation or
not.

Indeed, such is my ignorance that I confess myself unable to see how an
infinite efficient cause could impair a formal cause in acting unless the
efficient cause became a formal cause. Take as an example a dyer, who is an
efficient cause in dyeing wool by applying active things to passive ones. Even
if he applies himself more vigorously and intensely than usual and adds
stronger and more penetrating ingredients, the wool is nevertheless always
colored in the mode most natural to it. In precisely the same way, even if
Jehovah creates the objective union between Cyrus’s will and the people’s
liberation, he does not extend objective being beyond the degrees of formal
causation. Freedom therefore always acts in a mode natural to itself. I do not
deny, however, that a finite cause can impair a formal cause in acting—for
example, if someone threw a heavy body from a high tower more forcefully than
the nature of its gravity carries it—but

[1651 logical printed p. 12 | IA leaf 30 | PDF p. 30]

even then the one throwing is an efficient cause, not the formal cause of that
motion.

10. Second assertion: bare objective indifference is not the formal account of
freedom. Yet the arguments of Suarez in *On Divine Grace* and in his
*Metaphysics* contend against us in this sense. He objects as follows: the
origin and root of freedom is not the formal account of freedom, but objective
indifference is only the root and origin of freedom. I answer by conceding the
major and denying the minor. For objective indifference alone is not the origin
of freedom. Rather, we say that this objective indifference together with the
vital and internal indifference by which freedom can choose or not choose this
without injury to itself, whether in first act or second act, is the form of
free choice.

Second objection. The will, in relation to blessedness, can elicit an act
concerning it or refrain from eliciting one, since sometimes the intellect is
not actually thinking about blessedness. Therefore, to be indifferent to
refusing or willing without injury to the essence and the natural mode of
acting is not the distinctive account of freedom. Answer: I concede the whole.
For in addition to an internal indifference to refusing and willing, an
external objective indifference is also required; but blessedness is not such
an indifferent object, but a necessary one.

Third objection. God can present freedom with a means to happiness in such
clear and evident light that the object cannot fail to appear good; yet in this
case the will would choose freely. Therefore this objective indifference is not

[1651 logical printed p. 13 | IA leaf 31 | PDF p. 31]

necessary to the nature of freedom. Answer: we do not deny that God can present
an object to freedom in this way, but we deny that the act of the will would
then be free. Second, internal indifference does not merely regard refusing and
willing indifferently, without violating the will. It also adds to this
indifference a certain commanding and vital power by which freedom applies
itself and every other power to its work through a thoroughly vital command.
Hence we say that the following are included in the formal account of freedom:
first, non-coercion; second, the addition of objective indifference to
non-coercion; third, the further addition of an internal and vital indifference
by which the free will can will or refuse this without damage and loss to its
first or second act; and fourth, the addition to all these of that vital,
elective, and commanding power flowing from the very inward parts of freedom,
so to speak. By these things freedom is distinguished from every other power.

11. The indifference of the Jesuits and Arminians—by which they hold that, once
all antecedent requisites for acting have been posited, a free power can still
either bring refusing or willing into existence or not—is in no way part of
the formal account of freedom. Indeed, it meets divine providence head-on. I
prove this first from the prayers of the saints, in which they ask, from faith
and God’s Word, that Jehovah incline their heart to keep God’s commandments,
give them a sound heart, direct their steps according to his Word, lead them in
the way of righteousness, and bind their heart to the fear of God. Hence

[1651 logical printed p. 14 | IA leaf 32 | PDF p. 32]

I argue: what the saints pray for from God’s Word and faith, God graciously
grants them. This major premise is Scripture’s, John 14:13–14: “Whatever you
ask in my name will be given”; and Matthew 7:7. But by these testimonies and
countless others, the saints pray from God’s Word and faith for a thoroughly
free obedience, not for one in which the will can indifferently obey or not
obey. Therefore God grants them such obedience, and consequently a freedom by
which they obey; such absolute indifference is incompatible with it.

I prove the minor. First, the saints do not ask God to destroy freedom—which
they would necessarily be asking, according to the Jesuit-Arminian, if they
asked for obedience flowing from such indifference. Such petitions would be
requests for miracles apart from faith or God’s Word, and indeed utterly
opposed to the gentle providence whose office is to preserve, not overturn,
the natures of things.

Second, the words “Bind my heart to fear you” do not bear this ridiculous
sense: “Jehovah, bend my heart toward obedience so that I can either fear you,
or, by the indifference of my freedom and with reverence for your divine
majesty cast aside, entirely desist from fearing your name.” For then the
Prophet would be praying for disobedience just as much as obedience. Indeed,
who does not see that such petitions are diametrically opposed to the vows,
holy desires, pious intentions, faith, and consolation of the saints? For then
the saints would have to believe God as likely not to hear them as to hear
them. “What, then, do the Jesuits ask?” says Gabriel Vasquez. “That God stir the

[Marginalia: See Vasquez, volume 1, question 38, point 5.]

[1651 logical printed p. 15 | IA leaf 33 | PDF p. 33]

will by a congruous motion so that they actually adhere to God, although they
remain free either to comply with or resist such a motion.” Answer: I hear a
Jesuit chimera. Congruous motion, as Petrus Fonseca understands it, includes in
its essential concept the obedience of the person congruously moved. Thus the
person prays as follows: “When you foreknow, Jehovah, antecedently to the act of
your will, that my heart will be bound to you, I ask that you will to bind it.”
Indeed, this asks for what already exists with just as sound a rationale as if
the Jesuits today importuned God with prayers for the creation of the world
that occurred many centuries ago.

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 7.]

12. I prove the same point, second, from the divine promises in which God
promises that he will produce thoroughly free obedience in us. Ezekiel 36:27:
“I shall cause you to walk in my statutes.” The Arminians’ solemn refrain is
that God produces these things in us from his promises not absolutely but
conditionally: “I shall cause you to walk, provided that you are willing to
walk.” Twisse, that unblemished flower of scholastic minds, rightly asks for the
condition here, especially in elicited acts of the will: “I shall cause you,”
God would say, “to be willing to walk, provided that you are willing.” An
answer worthy of the enemies of grace!

But it is certain that the will to walk in God’s commandments is promised. For
the object of the promise, or the thing promised, to precede the promise itself
is the same as if God today promised yesterday’s sunrise or the creation of the
world. Are the minds of such Davuses quite sound? Yet I detect what they will
answer here: God promises to produce in

[Marginalia: See Jeremiah 32:4; Ezekiel 11:19–20; Hosea 14:5–6; Zechariah
12:10.]

[Marginalia: Twisse, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius*, *Antiperse
section*, preface, section 9, note 7, p. 14.]

[1651 logical printed p. 16 | IA leaf 34 | PDF p. 34]

us the will, not on the hypothesis that we are willing—for this would be an
excessively crude philosophy, especially for the great Arminius—but on the
hypothesis that our will begins, by a collateral influx, to accommodate its
own influx to God, who wills to concur. But let it be so.

First, whoever distinguished between the influx of our will and the volition
itself would indeed savor of a remarkably metaphysical spirit and would be a
great Apollo to me. Second, if Arminius is to be believed, this influx is
exempted from God’s providence. For by nature this influx flows into
supernatural acts of faith, love, hope, and so forth before God begins to
concur, and indeed it does so from the mere decision of nature without any
motion of divine grace. Thus God does not work willing in us according to his
good pleasure, as the Apostle teaches, but according to the good pleasure of a
human natural influx.

Third, if the will independently of God flows into an act conforming to the law
and gospel, that influx will conform to the law and gospel and will therefore
be supernatural. Thus there will be a supernatural act without the assistance
of grace and without the concurrence of the first cause; there will even be a
positive act underlying sin without the action of the first cause. Fourth, the
act itself is free because of the freedom of the influx; therefore the influx
itself is more free. For that on account of which each thing is such is itself
more such. It follows that all supernatural obedience is primarily from mere
nature. Fifth, since those influxes are positive entities, there are as many
positive beings as there are acts

[Marginalia: Philippians 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 17 | IA leaf 35 | PDF p. 35]

contingent acts of human beings and angels, and they will exist without the
concurrence of the first cause. I fear, however, that in their own minds the
Arminians deny God's concurrence in acts of the creature, so that the new
goddess Contingency might more easily be raised to the throne of God.

13. Third, if that is formally a free power which, when all the requisites for
acting have been posited, can act or not act, God's dominion over created
entities will be abolished. But this is absurd; therefore the antecedent is
also absurd. Proof of the major: if *the willing* or *the not willing*, even
when God's decree has been posited, lies in Peter's will, it will lie in the
power of man, not God, whether Christ is begotten from David's seed or not
through free acts of begetting; likewise whether any believers exist in the
world; likewise whether Cyrus frees God's people; and whether Babylonia is
devastated by the Medes, the Tyrians by the Babylonians, and the Jews by the
Assyrians. For God can neither bring about nor avert those things which are
done through free agents when they are absolutely indifferent matters of
human choice. Thus providence, trust in God for some good or deliverance
through the ministry of freely acting human beings, gratitude, patience, hope,
and so forth would be entirely abolished. For whether human beings freely do
something or do not is either within the control of indifferent freedom or is
within God's control.

[Marginalia: Suarez, *De auxiliis*, book 1, chapter 13, §§ 19–20;
Grevinchovius, *Against Ames*, p. 277, §.]

Suarez answers, and with him that verbose declaimer Nicholas Grevinchovius:
“For God to concur with the will is not for him to exercise his own dominion,
but to allow the will to exercise its own dominion. It is enough for God's
dominion that he can annihilate the creature and that acts of the creature
once produced

[1651 logical printed p. 18 | IA leaf 36 | PDF p. 36]

are subject to God's dominion.” Reply 1. Paul extends God's dominion to things
that do not yet exist, which he calls as though they already existed, Romans
4:16. Cannot God, then, by equal right call forth free acts from the
potentiality of the will? But they do not want God to exercise his dominion
over future free acts, lest an injustice be done to Queen Freedom.

2. They take as opposites things which are not opposed, namely God's exercise
of his dominion over creatures and his assistance of those creatures in their
acts. For when God concurs with rain, snow, hail, and falling showers, the
sacred oracles attest and everywhere proclaim that God exercises his dominion
toward human beings, either by conferring benefits or by inflicting
punishments. 4. God's dominion is perceived more often in the preservation of
things than in their annihilation. Elsewhere we shall add other arguments. I
thought it advisable to establish these preliminary points, so that a road
might be laid to deeper matters.
