# On the Name of God and His Invocation

*A fresh translation of the complete French-form consilium in CO X/1
153–156.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 153]

When we speak of God alone, without adding anything, we must consider him in
his simple essence as one God.

When we speak of Jesus Christ alone, without mentioning the Father, we must
know him as God manifested in the flesh. That is, God took our nature in order
to dwell in it as in a temple of his majesty, yet in such a union that Jesus
Christ in his divine essence and in his human nature is only one person.

When we speak of God and of Jesus Christ together, then under this name *God*
we must comprehend the Father and comprehend Jesus Christ as Lord and head,
whom the Father has appointed to rule over us and to have all preeminence in
his name.

However that may be, when we think about God or wish to speak about him, we
must not occupy ourselves with considering his infinite essence. Such a
thought is dangerous, because the human understanding is confounded in it.
Instead, we must immediately return to Jesus Christ, in whom the Father has
revealed himself to us.

### [CO X/1, p. 154]

When we speak of Scripture, we must distinguish between the Old Testament and
the New. As for the Law and the Prophets, although the whole proceeded from
Jesus Christ insofar as he has always been the sovereign Angel and the chief
ambassador through whom God communicated himself to human beings, nevertheless
the common usage is not to say that he said this through Moses or that through
David, but rather simply that God did. The reason is that at that time he had
not yet been manifested. Thus, following Saint Peter's teaching, we must firmly
believe that all the holy prophets spoke through the Spirit of Jesus Christ;
and, following Saint Paul's teaching, that he was the guide of the people of
Israel and even that the Law was given by his hand (1 Pet. 1:11; 1 Cor. 10:4
and following). Nevertheless, following Scripture's common style, when citing
something from the Old Testament we will not say that Jesus Christ said it in
this way through the mouth of Isaiah or Moses. We will say that God said it,
or else the Spirit of God.

The New Testament presents a different case. For Jesus Christ, when he came
into this world, was appointed by the Father as teacher and master to announce
all truth to us. He must therefore be held and called by us the author of all
the apostles' doctrine, as Saint Paul says that Christ speaks in him.

Concerning prayers in which we invoke God alone without expressly mentioning
Jesus Christ, or in which we add that God the Father hears us in his name,
setting him before the Father as

### [CO X/1, p. 155]

mediator—or prayers that we address to Christ himself—if we do not express the
name of Jesus Christ with our mouth, we must nevertheless supply it in our
mind. This does not mean that we are required always to have present the
thought that we address the Father through this advocate. Rather, we must have
this foundation and settled principle within us at all times: we have no other
access for praying to God than to be conducted to his majesty by the one who
humbled himself to us in order to accommodate himself to our littleness.

If we expressly petition the Father, praying that he hear us in the name of
his Son, we must then think that, just as our Lord Jesus obtained for us by the
sacrifice of his death an entrance through which we may present ourselves
boldly to God, so now by virtue of that sacrifice he intercedes for us. He
thereby makes our prayers acceptable to God and obtains our requests. We need
not imagine him prostrate on his knees, entreating in the manner of human
beings. Let it suffice us to have him as advocate, as though he spoke in our
name, and to know that he receives our prayers in order to present them to God
and obtain for us both audience and favor. I say *audience*, so that God
permits us and gives us leave to invoke him and listens to us. I say *favor*,
so that by his grace he grants us what we ask of him.

This, then, is the disposition a Christian ought to have when praying to God
in the name of his Son: “If I had to go to God without any other guidance or
means, I could never reach his majesty, so lofty is it. But since I have the
promise of my Savior, who extends his hand to me, access to it will be very
easy.”

If this thought intervenes—“How can our Lord Jesus, who is God, intercede with
God? For it seems that he prays to himself, which is contrary to all
reason”—two considerations must be used to resist it. The first is that,
although there is only one God, within this one Deity there is the distinction
of the Father and the Son, of which we have spoken. Thus there is no
incongruity in our looking especially to the Father when we pray, directing
ourselves particularly to his person. The second is that our Lord Jesus,
insofar as he took human flesh to accomplish our redemption, has this office

### [CO X/1, p. 156]

of mediator and advocate, interceding for us in the nature he shares with us.

When we address our prayer to Jesus Christ, either we ask him to conduct us to
the Father as mediator—and so this is, as it were, a preparation for coming to
the Father—or we ask him to help us by his own power, as the one to whom all
power has been given, in whom the whole fullness of Deity dwells, and who is
himself our eternal God manifested in the flesh. Both ways are lawful and
holy.
