Verse 9. Do not speak in the ears of a fool, because he will despise, despise, I say, [the wisdom] of your words.

In באזני (*in the ears*) there is a metonymy of the subject for hearing itself.

The sum is that we must exercise choice among the hearers to whom we present teaching or wholesome counsel, and that it requires no less prudence to know when to be silent than to speak prudently and considerately.

In chapter 9, verse 8, he forbade the rebuke of a scoffer. But this proverb is more general, and therefore more instructive and worthy of greater attention. For whereas there the concern is with rebuke alone, here a precept is given concerning every kind of counsel and prudent discourse. Again, whereas that proverb is restricted to one kind of fool, this extends to all. Nor is it unlike that command of the Savior, Matthew 10:14: “Whoever does not receive you or listen to you, the dust,” and so forth; except that what was there said to ministers of the word is here said to all believers.

Within the compass and scope of this proverb are included scoffers, Proverbs 9:8, which is why Christ gave Herod no answer, Luke 23:9; swine, who value sound counsels at nothing, such as the Gadarenes, who valued their swine more than the preaching of the Gospel and at whose request Christ also departed; dogs, who bite and persecute those who teach; much more blasphemers, which is why Hezekiah commanded his servants not to answer the blasphemies of Rabshakeh, 2 Kings 18:36; those stupefied by drink, since they are deprived of the use of reason for a time, 1 Samuel 25:36; and finally those whose mind and senses are wholly intent upon other matters. Such are misers, whose minds, if they ever provide a feast, we have just heard to be so occupied with the dishes that they think of nothing but the food, lest the guests consume it. Hence this proverb also appears to have some connection with the preceding one. It embraces all these within its scope. Pearls must not be cast before such swine; the children's bread must not be given to these dogs; nor must the precious seed of God's word be scattered upon this brambly and thorny soil.

Nevertheless, since the designation “fool” denotes not these alone but all the ungodly, as has often been said, everyone who leads a shameful and wicked life seems to be barred from Christ's school. In this way we shall close off the path of repentance and condemn the Apostles themselves, who proclaimed the Gospel to nations estranged from God, and indeed Christ himself, who associated with tax collectors and sinners to win them for God. I answer that it is one thing to associate with the dead, another with the sick in order to restore them to health; and secondly, that the calling of Christ and the Apostles was singular and altogether extraordinary. Moreover, since contempt is assigned as the cause of this prohibition, and we cannot recognize it without making a trial, even in those whom we certainly know to be ungodly, it follows that we ought not to abstain from instructing anyone, so far as the nature of our calling allows and opportunity is given, except those whom we have found to be obstinate.

Those, however, who are of such a disposition that the heavenly rain must be withheld from them ought to consider how desperate their condition is. But let the others see how calmly rebukes ought to be borne, since they bring testimony of God's favor toward us.

### Translator's note

The verse lemma prints *prudentium verborum tuorum*, retained in the Latin. The context suggests *prudentiam verborum tuorum*, “the wisdom of your words”; the English explicitly brackets “[the wisdom]” as the probable intended reading. Cartwright's abbreviated quotation of Matthew 10:14 breaks off at “the dust”; the English preserves that abbreviation instead of silently supplying the rest of the biblical verse. The metaphorical descriptions of different hearers are the author's exposition, not independent classifications added by the translation.
