QUESTION XXIII.

THE ABROGATION OF THE MORAL LAW.

Has the moral law been utterly abrogated under the New Testament, or does it
still apply to Christians in some respect? The former is denied; the latter is
affirmed, against the Antinomians.

I. The proposed question arises from the third use of the law, which we called
*directive*. It is disputed between us and the Antinomians, who maintain that
the law no longer has any use under the New Testament and therefore do not
wish the Scripture of the Old Testament to apply to Christians. This was
treated in Part I, Locus II, Question 8.

II. To establish the state of the question properly, we must see in what sense
the law is said to have been abrogated and in what sense it has not.

First, the question does not concern the law’s use for *justification*: whether
we are still bound to the law in order to acquire life. Since this is
impossible after sin, the law is said to be abrogated in this sense also:
“from the works of the law no flesh will be justified” (Rom. 3:20), and

[Printed p. 125 | Internet Archive PDF 153 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

“as many as are under the law are under the curse” (Gal. 3:10).

Second, the question does not concern the *curse*, because through Christ the
law has been abrogated under that σχέσει, *relation*, with respect to
believers: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a
curse for us” (Gal. 3:13).

Third, it does not concern the *coercion and rigor* which obtained under the
Old Testament, because we are no longer under that pedagogy and servile
dispensation in which the Spirit of bondage prevailed. Rather, we are under
the evangelical economy in which the Spirit of adoption reigns (Rom. 8:15),
through whom Christ’s yoke becomes easy (Matt. 11:30) and his commandments are
not burdensome (1 John 5:3).

The question concerns only the *directive use*: whether we have now been freed
from the direction and observance of the law. Our adversaries would affirm
this; we deny it.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that the law has not been abrogated with
respect to direction.]

III. First, “Christ did not come to dissolve the law, but to fulfill it”
(Matt. 5:17). Therefore, just as the law was not abolished through Christ but
fulfilled, neither should its use among us be abolished.

Likewise, in Luke 16:29 he wishes Moses and the Prophets to be heard: “They
have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them.” If they must be heard, their
commands must also be observed. Although Christ says this to the Jews of his
own time, it should not at once be understood *exclusively* of others. No
special precept is given here, but a general one for all who desire to escape
the place of torments. What applies in this respect to the Jews applies also
to Christians, and Peter generally commends to Christians the practice of
what is proposed here as a precept (2 Pet. 1:19), as he does frequently
elsewhere.

IV. Second, Christ and the apostles confirm and commend this law as one to be
observed by all (Matt. 22:36–37, and so forth). Paul likewise says: “Owe
nothing to anyone except to love one another, for the one who loves his
neighbor has fulfilled the law,” and so forth (Rom. 13:8–9). John calls love
both an old and a new commandment (1 John 2:7–8).

Third, “faith does not abolish the law, but establishes it” (Rom. 3:31). Hence
the gospel is often designated by the name *law*: “the law of the Spirit of
life” (Rom. 8:2) and “the royal law of liberty” (James 2:8, 12). Galatians
2:19 says, “Through the law I died to the law so that I might live to God,”
that is, through the law of Christ I died to the law of Moses insofar as it has
the character of a covenant.

This must not be understood only μιμητικῶς, *imitatively*, in relation to the
Jews’ thought, because the gospel truly and perfectly gives us what the Jews
falsely sought in the law, namely justification and salvation. In this sense
faith also is called *the work of God* (John 6:29). It must also be understood
properly, because the gospel is not without law. The law is now indeed
presented in a sweeter manner, but it nevertheless always binds the human
being to obedience.

Fourth, the moral law belongs to natural and immutable right, as was proved
above. It therefore applies equally to all human beings in whatever state they
may be, so that they cannot be exempted from its subjection.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of the law is proved. 1. By reason of the
covenant of grace.]

V. Finally, the law is necessary to the Christian in many ways.

1. By reason of the *covenant of grace* under which believers live. This
covenant not only has a promise of grace and salvation on God’s part, but also
entails a stipulation of obedience on the human being’s part. Just as God
promises that he will be our God in love and protection, so we in turn are to
be his people in worship and obedience (Jer. 31:33; 2 Cor. 6:16–17).

[Marginal heading: 2. By reason of God the Father.]

2. By reason of *God the Father*, who receives us into his family and assumes
toward us the σχέσιν, *relation*, of Father and Lord, to whom we indispensably
owe honor and worship (Mal. 1:6; 1 Pet. 1:15–16).

[Marginal heading: 3. By reason of Christ.]

3. By reason of *Christ*. Just as he sustains a twofold character toward
us—the character of *Surety and Priest*, who satisfies for us by fulfilling
the law, and of *Head and King*, who works in us by his Spirit and fulfills
the law—so he
requires a twofold virtue from his faithful people, that they may be united
and conformed to him: *faith*,

[Printed p. 126 | Internet Archive PDF 154 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

which embraces the promise of grace and the merit of the Surety, and *love*,
which imitates the holiness of the Head by obeying his commands. Hence his
*death* is not only the λύτρον, *ransom*, of our *redemption*, by which he
fully satisfied for us, but also the *pattern for our imitation*, set before
us so that *we may follow in his footsteps* (1 Pet. 2:21).

[Marginal heading: 4. By reason of the Spirit.]

4. By reason of the *Holy Spirit*, who consecrates us to himself as temples in
which he may dwell (1 Cor. 3 and 6), and who has the name and office of
*Comforter and Sanctifier*. Just as by the office of Advocate he comforts us
against the curse of the law, so as the Spirit of sanctification he confirms
and establishes the necessity of the obedience owed to the law.

[Marginal heading: 5. By reason of grace.]

VI. 5. By reason of the *grace* now conferred upon us, which requires this
obedience as the *fruit* of that seed, as *gratitude* for benefits received
(Ps. 116:12; 130:4; Luke 1:74), and as the *goal* to which grace looks. This
is evident in *election* (Eph. 1:4), *redemption* (Titus 2:14), *calling*
(1 Pet. 1:15; 2:9), *justification* (Ps. 85:9; Gal. 2:20; Ps. 130:4), and
*regeneration* (2 Cor. 5:17; Rom. 6:14).

[Marginal heading: 6. By reason of glory.]

6. By reason of the *glory* for which we hope. The obedience owed to the law
stands in relation to that glory as a *means* to its end, without which the end
is not reached (John 3:5; Matt. 5:8; Heb. 12:14); as the *way* to the goal
(Eph. 2:10; Phil. 3:14); as the *seed* to the harvest (Gal. 6:7–8); and as
the *firstfruits* to the whole mass (Rom. 8:23). Indeed, it stands as a most
important part of felicity.

Hence arises the *necessity* of good works for glory—not a necessity of
*merit*, but of the *means*. No one can be glorified in heaven who has not
been sanctified on earth through the pursuit of piety and obedience to the
law.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VII. It is one thing to be under the *law* as under a *covenant* for acquiring
life from it, as Adam was, or as under a pedagogue and prison for guarding the
human being until Christ’s advent. It is another not to be under the law as a
*rule of life* for ordering one’s conduct piously and holily.

It is one thing to be under the law insofar as it is opposed to the gospel
with respect to its rigorous demand for perfect obedience and the terrible
curse which it threatens against sinners. It is another to be under the law
insofar as it is subordinated to the gospel with respect to its gentle
direction.

In the former sense Paul says that “we are not under law but under grace”
(Rom. 6:14), with respect to the covenantal relation and the curse and rigor,
because Christ by his merit has freed us from the former and by his Spirit
exempts us from the latter. But in the latter sense we always remain bound to
the law, although for a different end. In the first covenant the human being
had to *do this so that he might live*, in order to merit life. In this
covenant he is bound to perform the same thing, not so that he may live, but
*because he lives*, in order to possess the life acquired through Christ and
to testify his gratitude. Thus in the same passage the apostle exhorts
believers to obedience.

VIII. Paul compares the *law* to a *dead husband* (Rom. 7:2–3), not simply,
but in a qualified sense with respect to the authority and rigorous dominion
it exercised over us and the curse to which it enslaved sinners. He does not
make the comparison with respect to release from the duty of acting according
to it.

Likewise, in 1 Timothy 1:9 the law which threatens, compels, and condemns “is
*not laid down for a righteous person*,” because the righteous person is
spontaneously impelled to duty and is no longer led by the spirit of bondage
and fear of punishment (Rom. 8:15; Ps. 110:3). But the law which directs and
regulates conduct is always laid down for him, and he must be subject to it.
“Those who are led by the Spirit are no longer under the law” (Gal. 5:18),
that is, under the law as compelling and cursing, but they are under it as
directing. The Spirit inscribes this law upon our hearts (2 Cor. 3:2; Jer.
31), and it is therefore called the law of the Spirit of life (Rom. 8:2).

IX. It is one thing to speak of the duration of the legal economy, and another
to speak of the

[Printed p. 127 | Internet Archive PDF 155 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

duration and use of the doctrine delivered in it. The former had to change and
cease, and it did in fact cease; but the latter did not. Christ speaks in the
former sense when he says that “the law lasted until John” (Luke 16:16), but
not in the latter sense. Nor, if the economy is removed, must the doctrine
accommodated to that economy also be removed in every respect. It is removed
only according to the respect in which it was proper to that economy.

X. It is one thing that the law should not be imposed upon Christians, and
another that a yoke ἀβάστακτον, *unbearable*, should not be imposed, namely,
the observance of circumcision and the legal ceremonies. The former is not
removed from Christians, but only the latter: “Why do you test God by placing
a yoke upon the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we were
able to bear?” (Acts 15:10).

The occasion of these words, noted in verse 5, makes their meaning evident.
Some had risen up who said that it was necessary “to circumcise Christians
and command them to observe the law of Moses,” namely, the ceremonial law, as
is gathered from verses 20, 28, and 29. Elsewhere this is called a ζυγὸς
δουλείας, *yoke of bondage* (Gal. 5:1). Or it refers to the moral law directed
to an improper end, as though Christians had to seek life from it, either
entirely or at least in part. In this sense it can properly be called a ζυγὸς
ἀβάστακτος, *unbearable yoke*, because of the weakness of the flesh, since no
one can be justified through it (Rom. 8:3).

Thus that yoke can be removed according to that in which it is ἀβάστακτον,
*unbearable*, namely, with respect to the law’s τὸ ἀκριβοδίκαιον, *demand for
exact justice*—that is, its coercion and the curse annexed to it—although it
remains with respect to the necessity of obedience.

XI. The use of that which is in itself and by its nature a cause of the
increase and multiplication of transgression ought not to be retained among
Christians. But the law does not increase sin in this sense, because sin
follows not from the establishment of the law as such, but only from its
transgression.

The law is therefore said to increase sin by accident (Rom. 5:20), not only
because it manifests it *declaratively*, but also *occasionally*, insofar as
sin, seizing an occasion through the commandment, produces covetousness
(Rom. 7:8). It does so by provoking covetousness to rage still more against
the restraining command, like a restive horse which rages all the more against
its rider the more tightly it is constrained by a sharper bit. Hence the
violation of the known law merits a graver guilt.

XII. When the law is called “the letter that kills and the ministry of death
and condemnation” (2 Cor. 3:6–9), it must be understood in this way not of
itself and by its nature, but by accident because of human corruption. It must
be understood not absolutely and simply, but in a qualified sense when it is
regarded as the covenant of works in opposition to the covenant of grace.

Or the terms refer to the legal economy and pedagogy considered precisely in
itself, apart from the promises of grace and in contradistinction to the
evangelical ministry with which the apostle compares it there. In this sense
it is properly called the *letter*, because it indeed shows the duty but does
not fulfill it, commands but does not help, and gives orders but does not work.
It is said καταργεῖσθαι, *to be abolished*, with respect to that economy,
because that old covenant had to be abrogated. But it cannot be called these
things absolutely under the character of a rule and norm.

XIII. Christ’s liberty frees us from the yoke of the law which curses, from
the tyranny of the devil, and from bondage to sin. It does not exempt us from
the necessity of rendering obedience to God, which is indispensable for every
creature as such and especially for redeemed children. Indeed, the bond of our
duty binds us more strongly in proportion to the greater number of grounds on
which we are obligated to God.

Thus liberty from sin necessarily entails service to grace: freed from sin,
we become servants of righteousness (Rom. 6:18). Indeed, true liberty consists
in our being subject to God, for *to serve God is to reign*. Therefore, the
*liberty of the spirit* is one thing and the *license of the flesh* another.
The latter is truly ἀσύστατος, *incompatible*, with the law, but the former
accords with it perfectly and is inseparable from it: “as free, but not as
those who have liberty as a covering for malice, but as servants of God”
(1 Pet. 2:16), and in Galatians 5:13,

“You have been called to liberty; only do not give liberty as an occasion to
the flesh.”

XIV. What was given to the Jews insofar as they were Jews can be useful only
to the Jews. But what was given to the Jews insofar as they were in covenant,
or simply insofar as they were God’s people, applies not only to them but to
all who have the same character as the people of God. The law was not given to
the Jews in every respect in the former sense, but in the latter, for it still
speaks to us today (Rom. 7:7; 1 Cor. 9:8).

Nor, if the law is said to have been given through Moses (John 1:17), does it
follow that it has no place under the gospel of Christ. Moses is not opposed
to Christ but subordinated to him, as a servant to his lord, as the one who is
in the house to the one who has a right over the house as the one who built
and governs it.

XV. *Moses* can also be regarded in two ways: either generally and
*indefinitely*, as the *Teacher of the whole Church*, or particularly and
*definitely*, as the *leader of the people* and the Lawgiver of Israel, who
looked to its advantage. In the latter sense the law given by Moses applied
only to the Jews, but in the former it extends to all, no less than the law of
nature, whose compendium it is.

In this way the different opinions of the orthodox concerning the use and
obligation of the Mosaic law can be reconciled. Some, such as *Zanchi* and
*Musculus*, hold that it applied only to the Jews, to the exclusion of others.
Others, such as *Pareus* and others, assert that it applied to everyone. The
latter consider Moses and the law under the first conception, while the
former consider them under the second.
