## Book III

### On Grace

The manner of proceeding which Bellarmine employs concerning grace and free
will is astonishing. For although he professes to treat the controversies
against the heretics of this age, and in his preface threatens our theologians
with a great gape—or rather a wide-open mouth—nevertheless in these six books
we have scarcely any weighty controversy properly formulated and arranged
which is legitimately treated against us. Instead, a great clamor is made
about Scholastic questions, the domestic disputes of the Roman Catholics, and
Pelagian errors, in order that their renovators may remove from themselves any
share in those errors, while our positions are for the most part noted only
obliquely and incidentally. The true cause of this perplexed cir-*

[Printed p. 257 | Google Books PA257 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*cumlocution was that so many disputes about these matters exist among the
Roman Catholics, and have existed for a long time, and are carried on without
any papal definition, that Bellarmine had no certain and received position of
the Roman Church to oppose to our doctrine. “Behold a reef,” Mariana cries in
*On Death and Immortality*, book 3, chapter 8, “upon which many ships have been
shattered. Behold a swift tide-race, the sea turned in different and contrary
directions, like a torrent hurtling down a steep mountain, seething with
quarrels and disputes. Nowhere in these years has the contest been conducted
with greater spirit, nor with a more implacable hatred of factions, to such a
point that they have come before severe tribunals, while each side fastens
upon its opponent the charge of heresy, than which nothing is graver. The
Roman Pontiff himself, appealed to for some years and constrained by this
controversy, has not yet set the disputants at peace by promulgating a new
decree.” Nor has this vacillation of the Roman Catholics lasted merely a few
years. Nearly three hundred years ago, our Bradwardine, in the conclusion of
the work he wrote *On the Cause of God against Pelagius*, vehemently implored
the Roman Church to deign to determine these questions: “Simon, are you
asleep? Arise, I beg you,” and so forth. Yet the pope has still not been roused
from this sleep; and Bellarmine did not think it prudent to disturb his rest,
but rather to measure his step cautiously and tread with foot poised among the
disagreements of his own party, so that he might not greatly trouble any of
them and might appear instead to have refuted us while doing something else.
It will therefore be necessary for us, for the most part contrary to the plan
of the undertaking, to excerpt from Bellarmine those things which are worthy
of notice and briefly point them out, without an orderly disputation through a
statement of the case and an examination of the arguments on each side.

### Chapter I

#### On the Meaning and Division of Grace

##### Thesis I

Bellarmine. 1. *The word “grace” has three meanings in Holy Scripture. For it
is taken: 1. For any benefit of God. 2. For a supernatural benefit alone. 3.
Most properly, for only those supernatural benefits which are conferred upon
unworthy men from Christ's merit.*

Protestant reply. I. Although the first two meanings are not foreign to the
word “grace,” they nevertheless do not occur in Holy Scripture. II. Although
the third meaning is true and drawn from Scripture, it is not, as Bellarmine
wishes, the most pro-*

[Printed p. 258 | Google Books PA258 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*per. Rather, by the metonymy in which the cause is put for its effect, it is
derived from that meaning in which God's grace, as to the thing itself,
signifies the same as God's goodness, kindness, favor, love, and mercy. The
more learned Roman Catholics are not ignorant of this. For Thomas himself,
part 1 of the second part, question 110, article 1, not only presents that
meaning of grace, but shows elegantly enough that this is its principal
meaning, upon which the others depend. “Grace,” he says, “is customarily taken
in three ways: 1. For the love of someone. 2. For some gift freely given. 3.
For the recompense of a benefit freely given. Of these three, the second
depends upon the first. For from the love by which someone holds another dear
it proceeds that he freely bestows something upon him. From the second,
however, proceeds the third, because thanksgiving arises from benefits freely
bestowed.” Filincius the Jesuit also, treatise 21, final chapter, section 442,
acknowledges that “grace is taken first for love.” But among the patrons of
righteousness by works, mention is rarely and incidentally made of this
meaning, which is most celebrated in Scripture and which above all others
serves God's glory and the consolation of the faithful. For they see that all
their meritorious notions are utterly overthrown by this one meaning of grace.

2. Bellarmine. *The first division of grace, taken in the second and third
senses, is into eternal grace, which is God's eternal love, and temporal
grace, consisting in the benefits of calling and justification, which are the
effects of eternal grace.*

Protestant reply. I. This division refutes the preceding distinction of
meanings, because eternal grace confers all benefits; it is not a benefit
which is conferred upon us and therefore is not contained among those
meanings. II. It is not a division of the thing, as it is taken by the Roman
Catholics, but merely a distinction of the name into its proper and figurative
significations.

3. Bellarmine. *Temporal grace is either grace freely given (to which the name
of the genus is left as its proper name), bestowed chiefly for the salvation
of others; or grace making acceptable, given principally for each person's
own salvation.*

Protestant reply. The title “grace making acceptable”: 1. Is without warrant
restricted to God's temporal gifts, though it belongs primarily to God's
eternal grace, Ephesians 1:6. 2. Is wrongly extended indiscriminately to all
gifts which chiefly concern our salvation, since Christ alone, given to us in
justification, makes us acceptable to God as the meritorious cause. The other
gifts do so either as means disposing us to receive Christ, or as signs of the
grace in him which makes us acceptable and as objects of God's gratuitous
good pleasure—not as causes of admission into favor or of reconciliation.

4. Bellarmine. *Grace making acceptable is either abiding grace,*

[Printed p. 259 | Google Books PA259 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*that is, an infused habit; or the grace of special assistance, that is, a
special motion.*

Protestant reply. We do not reject the substance of this distinction, but we
say that neither part pertains to grace which properly makes one acceptable.
And since God's assistance has our souls as its object and not as its subject,
it is evident that it does not formally make anyone acceptable.

5. Bellarmine. *Special assistance is either awakening, to which divine
illuminations and inspirations pertain; or assisting, to which divine
cooperation, direction, and protection pertain.*

Protestant reply. This distinction can be admitted as to its terms. But
Bellarmine errs gravely in explaining awakening grace if he wishes it to
consist only in the illumination and inspiration of the mind, and not
principally in a certain supernatural power received in the will.

6. Bellarmine. *The fifth division is of assistance, or of grace, into
sufficient and efficacious assistance or grace.*

Protestant reply. This division will have to be discussed a little later. In
the meantime it will suffice to note two things. 1. Bellarmine hesitates here,
uncertain by what name he should designate the whole which contains these
parts: “It is a division,” he says, “of assistance or of grace.” The reason is
that the Roman Catholics have not yet settled whether that sufficient grace
which they wish to be given to all is habitual grace, or only assistance and
a special motion. 2. Bellarmine here breaks the thread of the divisions of
grace which he has woven thus far, since he refers sufficient and efficacious
grace neither to awakening grace nor to assisting grace. The reason is that he
places awakening grace in the illumination of the intellect, and assisting
grace in God's cooperation, direction, and protection; but in neither of
these could he find the sufficiency and efficacy of grace for conversion. In
reality, however, grace by awakening and assisting at the same time is
sufficient and efficacious, as Bellarmine himself acknowledges a little later
in chapter II: “The fifth division is of both awakening and assisting grace
into sufficient and efficacious grace.”

6. Bellarmine. *Efficacious grace is either operating, by which it causes us
to will; or cooperating, by which it causes us to accomplish what we have
willed.*

Protestant reply. Thomas himself, part 1 of the second part, question 111,
article 2, teaches in explicit words that “operating and cooperating grace are
the same grace, but are distinguished according to their different effects.”
Concerning these divisions of grace, Bellarmine raises various questions
about which he disputes with other Roman Catholics; but he proposes no
controversy in which we stand on the opposing side, except concerning
sufficient and efficacious grace, and even in these matters he evades a proper
disputation.
