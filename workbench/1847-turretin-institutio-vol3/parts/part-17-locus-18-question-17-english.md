QUESTION XVII.

ON THE PRIMACY OF PETER.

Was Peter the ecumenical pontiff, the Head of the church, and the Vicar of
Christ? We deny against the papists.

I. Although the preceding question demonstrated in the thesis the vanity of
an ecumenical pontificate, nevertheless, because the adversaries attempt to
establish it with various arguments in the hypothesis concerning Peter and
the Roman pontiffs, who are called his successors, this subject must be
disputed in a separate controversy so that the truth may be fully confirmed
against falsehood.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

II. Concerning the state of the question, we observe: 1. The question does not
concern just any primacy, whether of order and calling, age, or dignity and
gifts. We do not deny that such a primacy can belong to Peter, either because
he was called first by Christ, as is evident from Matthew 4:18, and thus was
first in order, as often occurs among those who are equal in power in order to
avoid confusion, in which sense he is called “first” (Matt. 10:2); or by
reason of gifts and dignity, as Nicodemus is called *a ruler of the Jews*
(John 3:1), not because he ruled the Jews but because he possessed great
authority among them. Thus Gamaliel is called *honored by all the people*
(Acts 5:34). Therefore, since Peter excelled others in gifts, and especially
in that fervor of spirit by which he was moved to answer first for the rest,
he not unreasonably occupied the first place. Hence the fathers more than once
call him the Prince of the Apostles, that is, the first and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 133 | IA leaf 167 | PDF p. 167]

foremost, just as Cicero was the prince of orators and Virgil of poets,
although they had no rule over orators or poets but held that title by reason
of dignity. Rather, the question concerns a primacy of authority and
jurisdiction: whether some degree of authority and jurisdiction over the
other apostles was given to Peter, by which he was constituted the ecumenical
pontiff, Vicar of Christ, and Head of the church with the most absolute power,
so that all the faithful individually and the apostles themselves with the
rest, as subjects, had to bow their submissive heads to him. The papists
contend for this. Bellarmine says in *On the Roman Pontiff* 1.10: “Catholics
affirm that the apostle Peter was the Head and Prince of the whole church,
appointed in Christ’s place by Christ himself; and to deny the primacy of
Peter instituted by Christ is not a simple error but heresy.” The orthodox
steadfastly deny it.

[Marginalia: It is proved that no primacy of Peter is given. 1. From the
nature of the office.]

III. There are various reasons. 1. The first is drawn from the nature of the
apostolic office, which, since it is supreme in the ecclesiastical order,
acknowledges no higher and greater office. Thus each apostle possessed the
supreme, most ample, and immediate power and jurisdiction by which they could
give laws to the whole church, so that no appeal from them was given; and they
received immediately from Christ that power which contains every other
ecclesiastical power. Consequently, they acknowledged no superior on earth by
whom they were appointed, for nothing greater is given above what is supreme,
and things which are supreme must be equal. Bellarmine does not deny in *On
the Roman Pontiff* 1.9 that supreme power was given not only to Peter but also
to the other apostles; that they received their mission and jurisdiction
immediately from Christ; that as apostles they possessed supreme and most
ample power and the fullness of power; and that with respect to government
they were Heads, Rulers, and Pastors of the universal church (chapter 11).
Hence the writings of each are canonical and their doctrine infallible
without any confirmation by Peter.

IV. But he replies that “Peter possessed the pontificate as an ordinary
pastor who would have a successor, while the others possessed it as delegates
who would have no succession.” Yet it gratuitously assumes what had to be
proved: that Peter was called and sent in a different manner from the others
and was an ordinary pastor who would have a successor, while the rest were
extraordinary, especially since Bellarmine says that what was promised to
Peter by the words “I will give you the keys” and bestowed by the words “Feed
my sheep” was also given to the apostles. Add that since they contend that the
supreme pontificate was expressly set down by Paul in the word *apostles*
(Eph. 4), which is common to all, how can that office be attributed to Peter
alone and not to all the others? Again, if Peter had a successor, it was
either insofar as he was an apostle or insofar as he was a teacher. If the
former, apostles as such have successors; if the latter, why are there not
also successors to the rest, who likewise were teachers? Nor should it be
said that Peter possessed a principate, because that principate is either a
power distinct from the apostolic power or the same power. If it is the same,
then the apostles likewise ought to have had successors; if distinct, then an
apostolic power will be given which is greater than the one the adversaries
declare to be supreme. Finally, since the apostolate as such was an
extraordinary and temporary office which had to cease in the church, it ought
not to have successors.

[Marginalia: 2. From the equality of the apostles.]

V. 2. From the equality of the apostles. If Peter was constituted the
ecumenical pontiff, he was greater than and superior to the others. But we do
not read that any such superiority over the others was conferred upon Peter
by Christ, claimed by Peter himself, or acknowledged by the apostles. Christ
sends them on equal terms: “As the Father sent me, so I send you” (John
20:21–22). On equal terms he breathes the Spirit upon them and gives them the
power of remitting and retaining sins: “Whosesoever sins you remit, they will
be remitted; whosesoever sins you retain, they will be retained.” On equal
terms he gives all of them the command to preach and baptize (Matt. 28:19):
“Go, teach all nations,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 134 | IA leaf 168 | PDF p. 168]

etc.” Hence in Matthew 19:28 he promises them equality of thrones: “In the
regeneration”—that is, in the renewal of the church accomplished through the
gospel—“you will sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of
Israel.” But if Peter had to be prince of the others, why is his throne not
distinguished and raised above the others? Why did Christ never say a single
word about such a monarchy and principate? For in what follows, the things
brought forward by the papists to prove it will be shown to be vain and
fictitious. After Christ’s ascension the same equality is evident from
apostolic practice. (1.) When Matthias is chosen in the place of Judas, this
does not occur at Peter’s decision, as would have suited a monarchy, but by
lot with the common consent of the whole assembly. And when Peter speaks, his
aim is nothing other than to exhort the assembly to make such an election,
which was an effect either of his zeal or of the order which he held among
them because of the priority of his calling. (2.) The Holy Spirit descends
upon all equally, without any prerogative belonging to anyone. (3.) When the
appointment of the deacons is treated (Acts 6), the twelve apostles are said
to have summoned the multitude for it, and those who were chosen are said to
have been presented to the apostles so that hands might be laid upon them.
This proves that there was then no Head of the church, for otherwise he would
have discharged this office before the others. Hence Chrysostom says in
Homily 3 on Acts: “See him doing all things in common”—*nothing
authoritatively, nothing imperiously*. (4.) Peter is sent to the Samaritans
by the other apostles (Acts 8:14). Therefore, he could not be the ecumenical
pontiff, because being delegated by others is incompatible with that office.
Indeed, from this it is clearly gathered that authority belonged to the whole
body, not to some private person or specifically to Peter, who is subject to
the whole college. Would the pontiff readily tolerate being sent somewhere to
preach the gospel by the cardinals or bishops? Nor can it be said with Perron
that this mission was undertaken at the request of the apostles and not by
their authority. Nothing like this appears in the text; it says only that it
was done by their common and harmonious counsel, in the same manner as
Barnabas is elsewhere said to have been sent (Acts 11:22), and Silas and Judas
with Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:22). Although an equal may be sent by an
equal, it does not follow that a superior can be sent by an inferior, which is
an act of authority, or that the one sent can possess rule over those by whom
he is sent.

[Marginalia: 3. From Acts 15.]

VII. 3. From the council of Jerusalem (Acts 15), from which various arguments
against Peter’s primacy are drawn. (1.) Paul and Barnabas are sent to the
apostles in general, not to Peter (verse 2), although this certainly ought to
have been done if he had been acknowledged as the Head and Prince of the
others. (2.) They bring back a resolution from the apostles and elders
(verses 23–25) by synodical letters written in the name of all the apostles,
not of Peter alone: “The apostles and elders, etc.” (verse 23), and “It seemed
good to us” (verse 25). (3.) James’s authority appears no less than Peter’s
here, because he qualifies Peter’s opinion and concludes in such a way that
the whole assembly passed over to his judgment and the letters were written.
Nor, if Peter spoke first after the dispute in order to state his opinion,
does it follow that he gave a definitive judgment or made the decree itself.
James spoke last and delivered the judgment which was received. Hence if the
decree is to be attributed to any one of the apostles, it belongs to James
rather than Peter, because he speaks after Peter’s address, which would not
have been necessary after a decree had been sanctioned; nor was the decree
made according to Peter’s words but according to James’s opinion. James’s own
manner of speaking pertains to this (verse 19): “Therefore I judge, etc.”
This is not the language merely of one offering an opinion but of one decreeing
and defining. Hence Chrysostom in Homily 33 on Acts gathers that the
principate was entrusted to James, *for he had been entrusted with the rule*;
and later he asks what “I judge” means: *I say that this is so with authority*.
And Hesychius in Photius says, “Peter

[1847 vol. III printed p. 135 | IA leaf 169 | PDF p. 169]

preaches, but James decrees, etc. How shall I proclaim the servant of Christ
and brother of the new Jerusalem, the supreme leader, prince of priests, Head
of the apostles, summit among heads, outshining among stones, and most
illustrious among the stars?” What more illustrious statement about Peter
occurs in all antiquity? Lorinus labors in vain in the preface to his
commentary on James, chapter 3, when “he contends that these things do not
injure Peter’s primacy but only indicate that the prerogative of the episcopate
of Jerusalem was granted to James.” For if, in Hesychius’s judgment, James was
the Head of the apostles and presided over the council of Jerusalem in Peter’s
presence, so that he was legislator and judge in the council while Peter was
the orator, how could Hesychius acknowledge Peter’s primacy? Would the pope
today allow a bishop in his diocese to be distinguished by these praises, or
such authority to be attributed to any bishop if a council were celebrated in
that diocese with the pope himself present? Tostatus does not differ from
these writers in his comments on Matthew 17, question 13, reply 1, when he
says: “All the apostles, while they lived in Judea, were in a certain manner
subject to James, bishop of Jerusalem; he was greater than they, even Peter;
and in that council what the apostles and the whole church did was definitively
pronounced not by Peter but by him, as the organ of the whole church and one
greater than each of those present.”

[Marginalia: 4. From a comparison of Peter and Paul.]

VIII. 4. From a comparison of Peter and Paul. Paul testifies that he was
inferior in nothing to “the most eminent apostles” (2 Cor. 11:5; 12:11). It
must be observed that one man is not constituted supreme, but several, and
Paul equates himself with these supreme apostles. Thus Paul silences the
detractors who defended themselves against him by the authority of Peter,
John, and James, intimating that they should not be esteemed so highly that he
would be despised on their account, since he was inferior to them in no
respect whatever. Could any bishop speak in this way about the pope today?
The same is the point of what he says in Galatians 2:6: “From those who were
thought to be something—whatever they were makes no difference to me; God
does not accept the person of man—for those held in esteem contributed
nothing to me.” If they contributed nothing to Paul, he must therefore be
regarded as inferior to them neither with respect to knowledge nor with
respect to authority, but as equal in all things. This is further confirmed
by “the right hand of fellowship which they gave” (Gal. 2:9): “For James,
Cephas, and John, who were regarded as pillars, gave to me and Barnabas the
right hands of fellowship,” not to enter fellowship but to attest and confirm
that they were partners in the apostolic office and in purity of doctrine.
The sense is this: those Jews were so far from contributing any authority or
knowledge to me that those very men who were foremost in the church—James,
John, and Peter—acknowledged me not as unequal but as a colleague and gave me
their right hand as such. For among the Hebrews giving the right hand is a
sign of fellowship (Jer. 50:15; Ezek. 17:18). Therefore, if Paul was the
partner and colleague of the others, what room remains for Peter’s primacy?
Nor can it be said here that “they were indeed partners in the office of
preaching, but this did not prevent Peter from always being superior in the
office of governing.” This is an open begging of the question, contrary to
Paul’s purpose, who defends not only the truth of his doctrine but the dignity
and authority of his apostolate by parity of the same right. And this
*fellowship* indicates not only association but also participation in the
same thing, so that Paul discharged the apostolate among the Gentiles and
Peter among the Jews. Therefore, just as Peter had to govern the Jews, so Paul
had to govern the Gentiles. Hence he says “the gospel of the uncircumcision
was entrusted to me, as the gospel of the circumcision was to Peter” (verse
7). But this division of provinces could not have occurred while Peter’s
primacy remained intact, just as no such division could occur today between
the pope and the bishops. Although we do not deny that it was arranged so
that each refrained from the other’s apostolate, it was nevertheless arranged
so that Paul’s authority in his apostolate was no less than Peter’s in his.
To this also must be referred what the apostle professes in chapter 1, verse
1: that he did not have his

[1847 vol. III printed p. 136 | IA leaf 170 | PDF p. 170]

calling from men as its authors, nor through men, that is, by their ministry
and mediately through them, but immediately from Christ and God. For if after
Christ’s ascension there was a visible Head in the church, no one could be
admitted to ministry in the church without his intervention. And in verse 16
he says that after his conversion he “did not confer with flesh and blood,”
nor return to Jerusalem to the apostles to receive the authority of his
ministry from them or to confer with them about his calling. This certainly
ought to have occurred if Peter was the visible Head in the church upon whom
the others had to depend.

[Marginalia: 5. From Peter’s rebuke.]

IX. 5. From Peter’s rebuke by Paul. For an ecumenical pontiff is
*unaccountable*: he must judge everyone and be judged or rebuked by no one.
But we read that Peter was judged and rebuked by Paul (Gal. 2:11): “But when
Peter came to Antioch, I withstood him before all, because he was blameworthy,
etc.” This denotes not only an *accusation* but also a *rebuke* and a *more
severe censure*. This passage troubles the adversaries. Baronius calls it a
stone of offense, a rock of scandal, and a history difficult to comprehend.
He therefore leaves nothing untried to remove this stumbling stone, but in
vain. It cannot be said, following Chrysostom and Jerome, that these things
occurred by pretense and by an arrangement between Peter and Paul. For in his
letter to Jerome, Augustine rightly notes that this pretense, which would have
been a true falsehood, is unworthy of the sanctity of Scripture and especially
of this epistle, in which Paul protests in chapter 1 “that he writes the truth
and does not lie.” The phrase “withstood him to the face” cannot be drawn into
fiction and pretense, because nowhere in the New Testament is it taken in that
sense. Rather, it intimates something done in a person’s presence and before
him, as in Luke 2:31, Acts 3:13, and Acts 25:16. And in verse 14 Paul explains
“to the face” by “before all,” because the sin was public, known to everyone,
and harmful. Hence Thomas renders it “openly,” and Emmanuel Sa, “publicly.”
Although Peter was not ignorant that the church had been freed from the yoke
of the Mosaic law and had delivered a judgment about it in the council of
Jerusalem, it does not follow that he could not sin in practice. He could
think rightly and act wrongly. Nor did Paul rebuke Peter because he taught
wrongly but because he did not walk uprightly according to the truth which he
knew: before the Jews arrived, he freely associated with the Gentiles in
accordance with Christian liberty; after they arrived, overcome by timidity,
he withdrew from them and drew others after himself into the same hypocrisy.
We do not deny that Paul still observed certain legal rites, nor did he rebuke
Peter for this. Rather, he rebuked him because in the absence of the Jews he
freely ate with the Gentiles but in their presence withdrew from them, which
Paul never did. Therefore, one thing is for Paul, while living among the Jews,
to accommodate himself to Jewish rites which it was not yet lawful to regard
as deadly but which were gradually to be omitted as *indifferent*. Another is
for him, while living among the Gentiles and enjoying Christian liberty, to
withdraw suddenly from their fellowship through fear of the Jews as though it
were an unlawful thing.

X. Perron fares no better in holding that it was a rebuke of charity, not of
authority. Although it was a rebuke of charity and not of the authority of a
superior over an inferior, it was nevertheless an act of just severity which
could not have been lawful without equality between Peter and Paul. For if
Peter was the Head and Prince of the church, how could Paul have censured him
so freely before everyone without violating the obedience owed to him? Nor
should it be said with Bellarmine that a superior can be rebuked by an
inferior, provided it is done with due caution, reverence, and the honor owed
to a superior. Paul denies that he is inferior to Peter, and there is no
indication of such reverence if by it is understood

[1847 vol. III printed p. 137 | IA leaf 171 | PDF p. 171]

a testimony of subjection, since Paul is not said simply to have rebuked him
but to have withstood him to the face. But if modesty and brotherly charity
are understood by it, we concede that Paul employed them here, although they
are those of an equal toward an equal, indeed of a greater toward a lesser.
Then the question is not whether Peter was greater but whether he was the
ecumenical pontiff. For although the papists concede that a greater man can be
rebuked by a lesser, they nevertheless deny in their canons that the
ecumenical pontiff can be rebuked, even if he drags peoples into hell in
crowds, according to the canon *Si Papa*, Distinction 40. Would the pope
tolerate anyone withstanding him to his face before everyone and accusing him
of hypocrisy because he did not walk uprightly, and that without any preface
of honor? Even if Peter had permitted himself to be accused in this way out
of humility, though he was greater, it would not have been lawful for Paul to
undertake it. Nor do we argue from Peter’s fault or indulgence but from Paul’s
act and the authority to rebuke Peter which he assumed. For he necessarily
did this either by right or contrary to duty. No one will dare say the latter;
therefore, the former is certain. But if Paul could rebuke Peter by right,
while no one can rebuke the ecumenical pontiff, it follows that Peter was not
such a pontiff as the adversaries contend.

[Marginalia: 6. From Peter’s declaration, 1 Peter 5:1.]

XI. 6. From Peter’s declaration, for he never claimed this primacy for
himself but places himself in the same rank as others and prohibits all
lordship in the church. In 1 Peter 5:1–4 he says: “I exhort the elders among
you as a *fellow elder*, an elder with you, a witness of Christ’s sufferings,
and a partaker of the future glory: feed God’s flock among you, not by
compulsion but willingly, nor as lording it over the allotted portions, but as
examples of the flock, etc.” But if he had obtained the primacy from Christ,
why does he not mention it in order to add greater weight to his exhortations?
Why does he place himself in the same order as the others? Why does he so
carefully prohibit lordship to others as a thing unworthy of Christ’s pastors
if he had received this office from Christ? Far be it from us to say that
Peter did what he prohibits to others with such earnestness. Add that Peter
performed no act of an ecumenical papacy, whether by commanding the apostles,
sending them, making laws, judging controversies, reserving certain cases to
himself, usurping both swords, convening councils, arrogating to himself the
power of transferring kingdoms and making right out of non-right, or the many
other things of this kind which the Roman pontiff claims for himself today.
Let the Gregorian dictates be compared with Peter’s entire life and
institutions, and it will be clearly evident how completely they differ from
one another.

[Marginalia: 7. From the silence of Scripture.]

XII. 7. From the silence of Scripture. If such a primacy, in which the
principal foundation of the church lay, had been instituted by Christ, should
not Christ or his apostles have made express mention of it somewhere, so that
the faithful, once instructed, would pursue it with due obedience? This was
especially necessary in those passages which seemed necessarily to require
it, where ecclesiastical offices or the authority granted to pastors by Christ
are treated, such as Luke 22, Matthew 28, Ephesians 4:11, and 1 Corinthians
12. But since nothing of this kind occurs, and indeed Scripture always speaks
of the apostles as equals, both with respect to the office of preaching and
governing and with respect to authority, it is evident from this that such a
primacy is fictitious and unknown to Christ. This argument is confirmed from
Christ’s administration after his ascension toward the seven churches of Asia
designated by the seven golden lampstands, to which he writes letters through
the apostle John to exhort, correct, and comfort them. For if the Roman church
had been made by Christ the Mistress of the churches and its pontiff the
visible Head and Vicar of Christ, why did Christ write no epistle to it as the
center in which the others had to be united, but direct his discourse to the
others and address them in the plural, not the singular, although he should
have done the latter if all had to be subject to one? Why does he not use this
Vicar of his rather than John to write

[1847 vol. III printed p. 138 | IA leaf 172 | PDF p. 172]

to those churches, when by his command all had to be subject to that Vicar,
while John held no such office? This clearly proves that such a primacy was
not instituted by Christ.

[Marginalia: 8. From the testimonies of the fathers.]

XIII. 8. From the testimonies of the fathers, not a few of whom overthrow
this primacy. Cyprian says in Treatise 3, *On the Simplicity of Prelates*:
“Certainly the other apostles were what Peter was, endowed with an equal
partnership both of honor and of power, but the beginning proceeds from unity.
Primacy is given to Peter so that one church of Christ and one chair may be
shown; and all are pastors, and one flock is shown which is fed by all the
apostles with harmonious agreement.” He says not only that the others were
the same as Peter but also that they had partnership with one another. And
because sometimes a certain kind of partnership can exist between unequals,
he adds that it was an equal partnership both of honor and of power, lest
anyone understand the others to remain in some lower rank. In the same place
he notes that “after his resurrection Christ gave equal power to all the
apostles.” Nor should his mention there of Peter’s primacy be understood in
any way other than a primacy of time and origin, so that Peter is said to have
obtained his dignity before the others obtained theirs—namely, before the
resurrection, while the others obtained theirs afterward. It is not a primacy
of dignity, as if he were said to have possessed a greater dignity than the
others possessed, for in that respect Cyprian says that they were equal in
honor and power. Thus Jerome says in Book 1 *Against Jovinian*: “But you say
that the church is founded upon Peter, although elsewhere the same thing is
done upon all the apostles, and the strength of the church is established
equally upon them.” He indeed adds a little later, “One among the twelve is
chosen so that, a Head having been appointed, the occasion for schism may be
removed,” but only by reason of order, not of authority and jurisdiction.
Ambrose says in Sermon 66, *On the Nativity of Peter and Paul*: “Therefore,
blessed Peter and Paul stand out among all the apostles and excel by a certain
special prerogative, but which of the two is to be preferred to the other is
uncertain.” And in *On the Holy Spirit* 2.12 he says: “Nor is Paul inferior to
Peter, etc. Paul was never unworthy of the college of the apostles, being
readily compared even with the first and second to none.” Gregory of Nyssa
says in *The Life of Moses*: “Peter, James, and John are not the pillars of
the church, but all who sustain the church are.”

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XIV. The passage in Matthew 16:18 does not favor Peter’s primacy. First,
because the passage is allegorical and metaphorical and admits various
interpretations; consequently, it cannot found the ecumenical pontificate,
which ought to be proved by some express passage. Second, these things are
indeed said to Peter but not about Peter. There are various reasons. (1.)
From the words themselves, because the rock is expressly distinguished from
Peter both in person and gender, as the denominating thing from the thing
denominated. But if Christ had wished to indicate Peter, why did he use an
indirect expression? Why did he not say, “You are Peter and upon you,” which
would have removed every difficulty, but “and upon this rock”? Although
Christ spoke in Syriac, in which language *Cepha* indiscriminately signifies
Peter and a rock, nevertheless the difference is not removed, both because
the gender differs—for when it is taken for a rock it is feminine, but when
for a person it is masculine—and because Matthew, who without doubt knew
Christ’s intention very well and whose text is authentic, wished to
distinguish between *Petros* and *petra*, although from the usage of the Greeks
he could have called each by the same sound and gender, *Petros*. (2.) From
the nature of the thing, because Peter cannot be called the foundation of the
church, since no other foundation besides Christ can be laid (1 Cor. 3:11)
and Peter himself is a member of the church who must rest upon Christ with the
others, as he himself explains (1 Pet. 2:5–6). Nor, if the church is said to
be built upon the foundation of the apostles, is this understood with respect
to their persons but to their doctrine. (3.) Peter could not at that time be
called the rock in

[1847 vol. III printed p. 139 | IA leaf 173 | PDF p. 173]

the present, since afterward he was going to deny Christ, sufficiently proving
by his fall that he had not yet been so confirmed that he could be called the
*unshaken rock* treated here. Indeed, in what follows Christ is compelled to
call him Satan. (4.) The founding of both the militant and triumphant church
is treated, and no one would say that Peter is its foundation. Nor should the
pronoun “this,” which appears to be referred to the immediately preceding
word, be objected here, because it is known that relatives and demonstratives
are not always referred to the nearest and immediately preceding words, but
often to more distant words when the nature of the thing demands it. Thus in
Acts 3:15–16 the pronoun “him” must twice be referred to the more distant
“Author of life,” not to “God,” which is nearer. The same occurs elsewhere
frequently. Add that the Holy Spirit does not set down the bare pronoun but
adds the noun itself, removing all ambiguity: “upon this rock.” Thus in
Matthew 26:28–29: “This is my blood, the blood of the New Testament, which is
poured out for many, etc. I tell you that from this time I will not drink of
this fruit of the vine.” If he had said simply “of this,” it could have been
referred to “blood”; therefore, he adds “fruit of the vine” to remove the
ambiguity. In the same way, lest anyone refer what Christ adds about the
building of the church upon the rock to the person of Peter, he was not
content with the demonstrative alone but distinctly designates the rock as
different from Peter, which the Vulgate translator retained: “You are Peter,
and upon this rock.” I indeed acknowledge that there is some *relation* to
Peter here, not to intimate that his person would be the rock upon which the
church was to be built, which we proved false above, but by reason of the
imposition of the name. For Christ, wishing to favor Peter, gives him a new
name derived by paronomasia from that Rock, as if he were to say: You are
called Peter by me, the Rock whom you confessed, upon whom I will build my
church. Cephas is therefore denominatively named from Cepha, the Rock, just
as Peter himself names the faithful living stones from Christ, the living
stone (1 Pet. 2:5).

XV. It is added in vain that Christ here promises something singular and
excellent to Peter’s person as a reward for the excellent confession which he
had made, which would have no place if Peter were not understood by the rock.
For first, just as Peter answered not only in his own name but in the name of
the other apostles as a sign of the unity of the faith of all, since Christ
had questioned them all in verse 15—“But who do you say that I am?”—so
Christ’s answer which is added must apply to all by reason of their common
doctrine and apostolate. Yet this does not prevent Christ from also having a
singular regard here for Peter, who had spoken in the name of all, in order to
bestow some privilege upon him before the rest: not a fictitious monarchy and
ecumenical pontificate, which is *incompatible* with Christ’s priesthood and
royal authority, but the glorious change of his name as a symbol of the
firmness of the faith which Christ was going to give him (Luke 22:32) and of
the faithful labor which he would perform in discharging his office. I know
that some of the ancients, followed by some more recent writers, hold that a
singular privilege and office given to Peter was thus designated: that he
would preach the gospel first both among the Jews and among the Gentiles and
so found and gather the Christian church (Acts 2:14; 10:34). But although
this is true, it cannot pertain here. For besides the fact that this would be
a personal privilege of Peter which could not belong to the pope, he would in
this way indeed be called the Architect and Founder through whom the church
was to be built, but not its foundation upon which it was to be built, as
Christ speaks here.

XVI. Third, Scripture hands us the key to this mystery when it everywhere
presents Christ to us under the figure of the rock. Thus under the Old
Testament God bears the name of the rock which sustains the church (Deut.
32:4; Isa. 26:4). Christ himself explains it in this way in Matthew 21:42:
“The stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner.”
Peter follows Christ and confirms this (Acts 4:11; 1 Pet. 2:5). Thus Paul in
Romans 9:33 and 1

[1847 vol. III printed p. 140 | IA leaf 174 | PDF p. 174]

Corinthians 3:11 calls Christ the cornerstone and the sole foundation besides
which no other can be laid. With this in view, most of the fathers understand
this rock of Christ, whom Peter had confessed. Augustine says in Sermon 13,
*On the Words of the Lord in Matthew*: “You are therefore Peter, and upon
this rock which you confessed, upon this rock which you acknowledged when you
said, ‘You are Christ, the Son of the living God,’ I will build my church.
Upon myself, who am the Son of the living God, I will build my church; I will
build you upon me, not me upon you, etc.” He confirmed this elsewhere more
than once, in *Retractions* 1.21 and Tractate 124 on John. Hilary says in *On
the Trinity* 2: “There is therefore one immovable foundation of the faith,
this one blessed rock confessed by Peter’s mouth: ‘You are the Son of the
living God.’” Various adversaries follow this interpretation here. Lyra says,
“Upon this rock which you confessed, that is, upon Christ.” The interlinear
gloss explains the rock as “Christ, in whom you believe.” The gloss on the
Roman Decretals, part 1, Distinction 9, chapter 7, says: “By this distinction,
‘and upon this rock,’ I do not believe that the Lord demonstrated anything
other than these words which Peter answered to the Lord when he said, ‘You
are Christ, the Son of the living God,’ because the church was founded upon
that article. Therefore, God founded the church upon himself.” Thus Tostatus,
bishop of Ávila, says on Matthew 16, question 67: “When Christ says, ‘Upon this
rock I will build my church,’ it is asked what this rock is. Some say it is
Peter, namely, that the church was built upon him; that is, he is the
foundation of the church. But it must be said that, taking the founding or
building of the church properly, it is not true that it was built upon Peter.
For that upon which something is built is the foundation, and thus Peter would
be called the foundation of the church. But this is false, because Christ
alone is the foundation (1 Cor. 3:11). He alone is also the Head of the
church. But we are all members, as is gathered from 1 Corinthians 12 and
Ephesians 4. Therefore, Peter is a member, not the Head or the foundation of
the church.” Peter of Ailly, the cardinal of Cambrai, and others do not differ
from him.

XVII. Nor do those who wish to refer the rock to Peter’s confession and faith
depart far from this. Chrysostom says on this passage, “Upon this rock, that
is, upon the faith of the confession.” Theophylact says, “This confession
which you confessed will be the foundation of believers.” Basil of Seleucia
says on this passage: “When Christ had called this confession the rock, he
named the one who first confessed it Peter, giving him this designation as a
token and monument of this confession. For this truly is the rock of piety,
this the basis of salvation, this the wall of faith, this the foundation of
truth; for no one can lay another foundation besides that which is laid, Jesus
Christ.” Among the adversaries, John of Torquemada embraces this opinion in
*Summary of the Church* 2.10: “The church is founded upon the rock, that is,
the faith of Christ.” So also Dionysius the Carthusian on this passage,
Gorranus, and others. For since faith and confession must be understood not
properly and subjectively but metonymically and objectively by reason of the
Christ whom they embrace, the matter comes to the same thing whether by the
rock we understand Christ himself or faith in him. This faith is to be
considered not so much subjectively with respect to itself as objectively with
respect to the Christ whom it embraces. With this in view, Ferus gives an
excellent explanation of this passage in his comments on Matthew 16: “The
rock is primarily Christ, upon whom the whole church has always been built;
no one can lay another foundation besides that which is laid, namely Christ.
Then, because through true faith we are joined to Christ and thus in a certain
manner become rock, if it is permissible to speak this way, Christian faith
itself and the truth of the gospel are also that firm and unshaken rock upon
which Christ built the church.”

XVIII. Some keys are keys of dominion and principate, such as the keys

[1847 vol. III printed p. 141 | IA leaf 175 | PDF p. 175]

of cities which are given by subjects to a prince as a sign of homage. In
this sense “Christ” is said “to have the key of David, who opens and no one
shuts, who shuts and no one opens” (Rev. 3:7), that is, he whose power is
supreme and *unaccountable*. Other keys are keys of ministry and subjection,
such as those given by a prince to an official and prefect of a household, and
by a head of household to a steward and storekeeper. These infer no primacy,
but only *service* and office. Such are the keys which Christ promises to
give Peter: “I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven.” These in turn
are twofold. First are the keys of wisdom and knowledge, by which through the
preaching of the word the treasures of the heavenly mysteries and the
*storehouses* of Scripture, in which the bread of life is kept for sustaining
the faithful, are opened. Thus the office of teaching is well intimated, as
among the Jews teachers were customarily inaugurated by the handing over of
keys. With this in view, Christ rebukes the Pharisees and scribes in Luke
11:52 because they take the key of knowledge away from the people, so that
they neither enter themselves nor permit others to enter. He indicates that
the scribes and Pharisees must be removed from this power so that it may pass
into the hands of the apostles, who had to handle these keys properly by
opening to people through their proclamation the door of faith and salvation.
Second are the keys of government and discipline, by which heaven is opened
to the penitent and closed to the rebellious and unbelieving. Both are
promised here by Christ, and their exercise is designated in the following
words: “Whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you
loose will be loosed.” Here Christ passes from one metaphor to another, as it
is not unusual for the Holy Spirit to join different likenesses for a clearer
explanation of one and the same mystery. It might have seemed more fitting to
say “open and shut,” which pertains to keys, than “bind and loose.” Yet he
preferred to use the language of binding and loosing, both to signify the
extent of the power which he was going to give Peter, by which he could not
only open and shut but also loose and bind, and to show how wretched is the
condition of those to whom heaven is shut, who remain bound by the bonds of
sin and divine justice. This could be done all the more suitably because the
likeness of binding and loosing most aptly designates both offices of the keys
described above. For it is referred both to preaching and precepts and to
discipline and government, so that what is permitted and allowed is said to be
*loosed*, while what is prohibited and forbidden is *bound*. Hence in the
very common phraseology of the rabbis, what is unlawful and what they wished
to be forbidden is called *bound*, while what is lawful by right is called
*loosed*. With this manner of speaking in view, Christ says in Matthew 23:4
that the Pharisees bind heavy burdens upon people, not only by their exaction
of the law, which was an *unbearable* yoke, but also by the rigid imposition
of various ceremonies and human traditions, by which the people were
oppressed as by the harshest burdens. It pertains to government and
discipline, however, when pastors are said to bind sinners with ecclesiastical
censures and penalties, whom they loose when they relax those penalties. For
those to whom heaven is shut by the denunciation of God’s wrath and whose
sins are retained are, as it were, bound so that they may be cast into hell,
just as elsewhere the hypocrites are said to be bound hand and foot and cast
into hell. Conversely, those to whom heaven is opened by the announcement of
God’s grace and the remission of sins are loosed from the bonds of sin and
God’s judgment so that they may enter heaven without hindrance.

XIX. But this power cannot be regarded as absolute and unlimited, which
belongs to God alone, to whom alone it pertains to forgive sins (Matt. 9:3).
Rather, it is only ministerial and dependent, insofar as in God’s name and by
his authority they declare from his word what the faithful and penitent should
hope for and what the obstinate and impenitent should fear. Nor can Bellarmine
deny this, for in *On the Roman Pontiff* 1.12 he acknowledges that the Lord
speaks about the power of the keys by which the apostles and their successors
bind or absolve

[1847 vol. III printed p. 142 | IA leaf 176 | PDF p. 176]

sinners. Hence it is evident that Peter’s primacy is sought here in vain.
Although something is promised here specifically to Peter, nevertheless
nothing peculiar is promised to him here. The promise itself, or the act of
promising, was indeed peculiar to him insofar as Christ addresses him by name,
but the thing promised was common and pertained also to the others in whose
name Peter spoke. This is evident from Matthew 18:18 and John 20:23, where the
same power which is designated here by binding and loosing is described by
the retaining and remitting of sins and is attributed equally to all the
apostles: “Whosesoever sins you retain, etc.”

XX. Although Peter is commanded to feed Christ’s sheep (John 21:15–16), he is
not thereby immediately constituted the Monarch of the church or ecumenical
pontiff. (1.) This is an exhortation, not the institution of a new office.
(2.) Feeding is one thing, ruling another. Although a pastoral act sometimes
extends to royal rule and denotes dominion—in which sense kings are called by
Homer “shepherds of the peoples,” and Christ is called the shepherd of his
sheep, who feeds them not only by the Word but rules them by his eye and hand,
after laying down his life for them and feeding them with his flesh and giving
them his blood to drink, which belongs to no other pastors, whence he is
preeminently called the “Chief Shepherd,” the Prince of Pastors (1 Pet. 2:25;
5:4), and the “great Shepherd” (Heb. 13:20)—nevertheless, this meaning cannot
have a place here when the apostles and ministers are treated. For the office
of feeding is expressly opposed to lordship: “Feed Christ’s flock, not
lording it over the allotted portions” (1 Pet. 5:3). This lordship is also
expressly forbidden by Christ (Luke 22:26). Finally, Peter is commanded to
feed Christ’s sheep, not his own, intimating that he must discharge this
office not as lord and prince of the sheep but as Christ’s minister. (3.)
This office is imposed not upon Peter alone but upon the other apostles and
indeed upon all ministers. Acts 20:28 says, “Take heed to yourselves and to
the whole flock in which Christ appointed you bishops to feed the church of
Christ.” Therefore, not everyone commanded to feed the sheep is a Monarch,
or else the church would have as many Monarchs as ministers. Thus the word is
not to be taken here in all its breadth but according to the subject matter.
And just as anyone would be insane who, from the fact that kings are called
shepherds of peoples, wished the preaching of the gospel to pertain to them,
so those who contend that rule was given to the man upon whom the office of
feeding was imposed are little removed from insanity. Therefore, he is
commanded to feed not by exercising lordship but by teaching and governing,
feeding with knowledge and understanding (Jer. 3:15) and ruling with a
shepherd’s staff.

XXI. Nor should it be objected that something is attributed preeminently to
Peter here because he alone is addressed, and indeed three times. What is
said to Peter individually is not said so that he would be constituted the
first and Prince of the Apostles, but so that his triple restoration with a
pledge of love and duty would be opposed to his threefold denial, by which he
could have seemed to have fallen from his apostolate, and so that by his
threefold confession he might wash away his threefold denial. Thus Augustine
explains in Tractate 123 on John, following Cyril and others, whom many of the
papists follow, including Jansen, Toletus, Maldonatus, and others. Thus Christ
wished solemnly to restore the one who alone had sinned; thus Peter had to
make satisfaction to Christ, whom he had denied three times, and to his
fellow disciples, to whom he had preferred himself (Luke 22:33). Nor, if
Peter appears already to have been restored to his former state by Christ’s
breathing upon him and by an equal mission with the others (John 20:22), does
it follow that he could not be confirmed by Christ in a special manner.
Because he had sinned more grievously, he had need of greater confirmation
and consolation. The other apostles indeed had abandoned Christ and fled,
but Peter alone denied Christ, and that with an oath invoking a curse upon
himself. Moreover, no mystery is to be sought in the distinction between
sheep and lambs which Christ makes here. Toletus judges this reading more
probable than the one which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 143 | IA leaf 177 | PDF p. 177]

Bellarmine wished to introduce against the great consensus of copies and the
authority of the Vulgate version, which makes only two ranks, not the three
which the cardinal contends for: lambs, that is, the nations; little sheep,
that is, the bishops; and sheep, that is, the apostles. Jansen teaches, I say,
that no mystery is to be sought here, and Maldonatus teaches the same:
“It must not be subtly disputed,” he says, “why he called them lambs rather
than sheep. Let the one who does this take care again and again not to make
learned people laugh.” A comparison of the passages shows that these terms
are used interchangeably (Matt. 10:6; Luke 10:3; Isa. 53:7; Acts 8:32). Thus
the fathers explain them, Chrysostom in Homily 87 on John and Augustine in
Tractate 123 on John.

XXII. Whatever the prerogatives attributed to Peter may be, they do not infer
the primacy of authority of which we speak but only a primacy of order and
dignity, which does not prevent Paul from acknowledging himself inferior to
Peter in nothing. Paul and John also had their prerogatives, from which an
ecumenical pontificate would nevertheless be ridiculously inferred. These
prerogatives are either uncertain and false or vain and useless, and prove the
primacy neither separately nor jointly. Not the “change of name,” because a
new name was not so much substituted for his as added to it (Matt. 17:25; 2
Pet. 1:1); add that this imposition of a new name was common to the sons of
Zebedee, who were called Boanerges. Not that “he is placed first” (Matt. 10:2;
Mark 3; Luke 6), for besides the fact that this is not perpetual, others are
placed before him elsewhere: Andrew in John 1; Paul and Apollos in 1
Corinthians 3; James in Galatians 2:9. He could have been so called by reason
of order, age, or gifts, not by reason of power and jurisdiction, just as the
first consul in the city is not the Prince of the senate. Not his “walking
upon the waters,” which indicates only his fervor and *impetuosity*.
Chrysostom says in Homily 51 on Matthew: “Peter, who is fervent everywhere
and always leaps forward before the others.” Not the “revelation of the great
mysteries of the faith,” because this was neither made to him alone nor first.
Not the “payment of the tribute for Christ and Peter,” but not for the others,
because there was a definite reason why it was paid for both: they lived in
the same city, namely Capernaum, which is called Christ’s city and where Peter
had a house. Not the “catch of many fish” (John 21), because this indicates
his ministry, not rule, as a sign of the gospel fishing (Luke 5) by which he
had to call innumerable people out of the world. Not “Christ’s prayer for
him,” which is rather a testimony of his weakness and tends to nothing other
than perseverance in faith. The strengthening of his brothers which is
imposed upon him indicates not primacy but ministry and office, which is
elsewhere claimed for Peter and Barnabas (Acts 14:22; 18:23). Not that “Christ
appeared first to Peter after the resurrection” (Luke 24; 1 Cor. 15:5),
because he appeared first to the women. Not that “he first washed his feet,”
or “foretold his death,” or “he himself first addressed the assembled
apostles” (Acts 1:15), or “first preached the gospel,” or “spoke first in the
council” (Acts 15). For these and the like which are brought forward are
either uncertain, confer no prerogative, or convey only a primacy of order.

XXIII. If the fathers sometimes call Peter the Head and Prince of the
apostles, they do not understand this by reason of an ecumenical pontificate
or monarchy but by reason of a primacy of order and dignity, or because of the
gifts in which he excelled the others. In this sense the apostle calls certain
men “the most eminent apostles” in 2 Corinthians 2 and elsewhere “pillars”
in Galatians 2. Thus Cyril of Jerusalem calls Peter “the most excellent Prince
of the Apostles”; Cyril of Alexandria, “Prince and Head of the others”;
Theophylact, “Prince of the disciples”; and others, “Leader of the apostles.”
But Eusebius notes in *Ecclesiastical History* 2.14 the sense in which these
praises must be understood when he says that Peter was “on account of his
virtue, before the rest

[1847 vol. III printed p. 144 | IA leaf 178 | PDF p. 178]

of them all as their leader”—that is, he was easily “the Prince of all the other
apostles because of the greatness of his virtue.” Thus among writers the
names *leader* and *chief* often occur in this sense. But the fathers not
infrequently attribute this also to others. Hence Peter and Paul are often
called by the fathers “the Princes of the Apostles.”
