As to the third, namely delegation, the English bishop, after stripping
consistories and the deaneries' classis

[1623 printed p. 314 | IA leaf 326 | PDF p. 326]

presbyteries of their power, and even the chapter, which the hierarchists call
his senate and presbytery, has claimed all their power for himself alone,
although he cannot
exercise it alone. But because he is unequal to the burden, he must communicate
the power to others. He does not restore their rights to the presbyteries and
churches, but transfers them to a certain novel brood unknown to purer ages:
vicars, chancellors, archdeacons, officials, and commissaries, the excrement of
the Roman pontiff's Decretals. He has taken the pastoral staff from the proper
pastors and delivered it into the hands of strangers who have no care for the
sheep. But let the statesman proceed in his critique. *As to the second
circumstance, namely the delegation of their authority, I see no sure and firm
foundation for it, since it departs somewhat from the examples and rules of
right government. A bishop exercises his jurisdiction through a chancellor,
commissary, officials, and so forth. We see in laws throughout the world that
offices requiring skill and trust are not transferred or exercised through a
deputy unless that is expressly contained in the original grant and is
accounted lawful in that case. No judge in any court has ever appointed a
vicar. A bishop is a judge, and indeed a judge of a more sublime kind. Whence,
then, does it arise that he substitutes another, since every trust, as has been
said, is personal and inherent and cannot be transferred to another? Certainly
in this matter also it was not so from the beginning. But it is probable that,
when bishops devoted themselves too much to the pomp of this age and became
magnates in kingdoms and counselors of princes, they delegated the
jurisdictions that were more properly their own, as matters of a condition far
beneath those which would display their grandeur; and then, after the likeness
of kings and counts palatine, they wished to have chancellors and judges. But
they cannot lawfully defend themselves by the examples of kings and magnates.
For there are two reasons why kings administer justice through their judges,
although they are themselves the supreme judges. The first is that the offices
of kings are for the most part hereditary. And it is a rule of law that
hereditary offices are things which savor more of an interest than of a trust,
since they can devolve upon women, infants, lunatics, idiots, and others unfit
to exercise jurisdiction in their own persons; and therefore, under all laws,
such offices can be administered through delegates. The second reason is the
breadth of their jurisdiction, which is as great as either descent from their
ancestors or the sword-right from God has made it. Accordingly Moses, who was
governor of a people not so numerous and assembled together in one camp rather
than scattered through provinces and cities, and who was himself furnished
with the Spirit in an extraordinary manner, was nevertheless unequal to
governing the people by himself in person and administering judgment. Instead,
by the counsel of his father-in-law Jethro, which was approved by God, he
appointed elders and judges; much more can other kings and princes do so. There
is also a third reason not very remote from the present subject: kings, either
with respect to their commonwealths or by reason of the extent of their own
patrimonies, are commonly parties in causes, and then the judges stand as
intermediaries between them and their*

[1623 printed p. 315 | IA leaf 327 | PDF p. 327]

*subjects. But none of these considerations is firm in the case of a bishop.
For first, his office is elective and for life, not patrimonial or hereditary,
an office worthy of skill, trust, and suitable gifts. As to the second reason,
it is true that their jurisdiction is broad and spacious, and that they must
divide their hours according to the labor to be bestowed both in the word and
doctrine and in jurisdiction and government. Yet, granting that judgments in
their courts are administered sincerely and without indirect devices contrived
to multiply causes for the gain of fees or salaries, I do not see why a bishop
cannot nevertheless discharge the judicial office in person in causes of
greater moment. For we see with our own eyes that the chancellor of England
dispatches the equity causes of the whole kingdom. This does not arise from the
excellence of the most honorable person who now holds that place, but it has
always been so, although the office was more or less burdensome to the litigant
according as the holder was more or less capable of dispatching causes. If the
point made before—that a bishop ought to devote most of his hours to labor in
the word and doctrine—is turned back against me, I reply that chancellors have
for the most part devoted their leisure to the affairs of the kingdom; for they
were generally such men that our kings relied especially upon their counsels.
But a bishop, whose territory is not so broad and whose suits are not so
numerous, can discharge his office himself, since there are readily available
the benefit of reports, fuller information through papers sent back and forth
among chosen and suitable men to mature the proceedings of actions, and the
other aids that ordinarily occur in jurisdiction. There is another assistance
as well. The causes brought before him concern tithes, legacies, administration
of goods, and other testamentary matters; matrimonial matters; accusations of
ministers tending to suspension, deprivation, or degradation; simony,
incontinence, heresy, profanation of the Sabbath, and other things of that kind
which are a stumbling block to others. The first two of these, in my judgment,
are unlike the rest—namely, causes concerning tithes and testamentary matters.
For they are temporal matters by their nature and bring profit, although by the
kindness and indulgence of the temporal judges the ecclesiastical courts have
been permitted to involve themselves in them: in the former so that clerics
could contend before their own judges for what had been given as assistance
for sustaining life; in the latter because care for putting the testaments of
the dead into execution was considered a pious work. But as to those two, the
bishop can disburden himself upon his ordinary judges without any graver
danger. I also think that in this way those suits would make up a greater
number. As to the rest, which require skill and spiritual adjudication by
reason of their own nature or of the stumbling block that arises from them, it
is equitable, in my judgment, that an audience—so the canonists call episcopal
judgment—should be granted only by the bishop himself, with assessors employed,
as was suggested before. It would also be necessary that the chancellor, or
another who performs a similar office under the bishop, being skilled in civil
law, should attend upon him to instruct him in legal formulas and the forensic
practice of the court. If this were done, the Official's Court, about which so
many complaints are now raised, would be less necessary.*

[1623 printed p. 316 | IA leaf 328 | PDF p. 328]

*If causes of the kind mentioned above were referred to the episcopal
audience, frivolous suits would be suppressed and competent causes would be
tried with greater gravity and sincerity.* Without doubt that statesman would
have said more against this delegation if he had not thought it more prudent
to guard himself, lest excessive freedom create danger for him. He would have
said that dioceses ought to be divided and contracted, so that bishops could
more easily discharge their office in person together with others as in a
senate. Dioceses are too extensive for them to embrace in their arms, yet they
hold on to them stubbornly, lest anything be lacking for luxury and pomp.
Duarenus says, *But neither is that jurisdiction now exercised by the bishops
themselves, as it once was not only with great gravity and dignity but also
with gentleness and simplicity; rather, it is exercised by certain juridical
vicars who are commonly called officials. Their courts far surpass every royal
and secular tribunal in calumnies, impostures, and forensic tricks.*

[Marginalia: *De minist. & benefic.* l. 6. 1. ca. 4.]

I do not doubt, however, that if the bishops themselves exercised jurisdiction
in their consistories, a graver tyranny would rage under a mask of gravity.
Pontifical bishops both now and in former ages were no whit inferior to our
English bishops in affected gravity, and yet they were cruel tyrants, of whom
Luther says, *It is just the same with evil and impious bishops as if Satan
himself, wearing miter and ring and sitting in the chair, were ruling the
people.*

[Marginalia: *Adversi falso nominatum ordinem Episcopor.* tom. 2. 318.]

English bishops, then, can sit in their consistories and courts with greater
gravity than their chancellors or vicars, because they do so with greater pomp
and Pharisaical attire, but not with less tyranny, as they show when and where
they exercise jurisdiction themselves; nor with greater sincerity, because
perhaps a chancellor's penny does not befit the bishop's dignity. There would
be greater and more genuine gravity and greater sincerity if he presided over
an ecclesiastical senate. The statesman's further counsel that a bishop use a
chancellor or official skilled in civil law as an adviser to instruct him, and
his assertion that this is necessary, is the same as what Bilson once answered.
*What madness is this,* he says, *to give your presbyteries the administration
of laws which they can never read or understand? First hand them over to be
taught in schools; then, when they have acquired knowledge of those laws, lead
them to the academies; finally, when they have advanced so far in study that
they can pronounce law according to them, interpose a petition for them to be
substituted for a civil court in every parish.*

[Marginalia: *De gubern.* cap. 14. pag. 364. 365.]

He imagines an objector: as if bishops anywhere possessed such great knowledge
of law. He answers, *All the more do they need chancellors and other jurists.* I
reply: all the more do presbyteries need chancellors and other jurists. For by
the same right presbyteries can use a chancellor as adviser by which the bishop
does so. Indeed, the bishop uses him not only as an adviser but also as a
judge. For in the bishop's absence the chancellor judges, and in the
archdeacon's absence the official does. Furthermore, the comparison is made
not only between the bishop's consistory and a parochial consistory,

[1623 printed p. 317 | IA leaf 329 | PDF p. 329]

but also between the bishop's consistory and classis presbyteries, and between
the bishop's consistory and the common presbytery of the whole diocese, which
should rather be called a council. Can these not use a chancellor or another
jurist as adviser? But Bilson trifles. Let judgments of causes concerning
tithes, testaments, legacies, and like matters be removed from the
ecclesiastical court, and then neither parochial nor classis presbyteries will
need the counsel of jurists. The canons concerning ecclesiastical order, and
especially the removal of stumbling blocks and the cleansing of the church,
are few; all pastors, including elders, can easily become skilled in them. What
sort of canons of ecclesiastical government, I ask, are these of which the
bishops themselves are ignorant, though they claim the government of the
church for themselves alone and consequently need jurists as advisers?
*In the same article a doubt arises,* Cajetan says: *Ought a bishop to be
learned, and a doctor of theology or of canon law? To this some say that,
although in former times theological knowledge was more fitting for bishops
than knowledge of law, because it was necessary to strike heretics with the
sword of theology, nevertheless today, because questions concerning law arise
more frequently than questions concerning faith, it is more fitting that
bishops be skilled in law. But these men wander far astray. First, the office
laid upon bishops in their consecration is to preach, and the subject of
preaching is not law but the gospel, for the Lord says, Preach the gospel. Holy
Scripture is comprehended under the gospel, and it is the true and proper
knowledge of theology. Bishops are no less bound to preach today than formerly,
because this is laid upon them no less today than formerly, although the abuse
is so great today that scarcely a single one does it. Second, a bishop is more
bound to teach the people to observe those things that concern good morals and
which Jesus Christ commanded to be observed than to teach men the sacred
canons, which were issued not by the Lord himself but by men.* And below:
*Therefore I say that, even if questions of faith had ceased today,
nevertheless there is the greatest necessity for teaching morals.*

[Marginalia: In 2. 2. qu. 85. art. 3.]

But since bishops are rulers and sit at the helm of the church, it is expedient
that they be skilled not only in theology but also in ecclesiastical law—we
banish the dung-law of the Decretals to Rome—so that with the pastoral staff a
bishop can rule the sheep entrusted to him. Otherwise, why should the staff be
taken from the other pastors and handed to a bishop who does not know how to
use it unless advisers skilled in canon or civil law are employed? The
episcopate is not a hereditary kingdom, so that skill in ecclesiastical law
would therefore be unnecessary for him, as in hereditary kingdoms sometimes a
man, sometimes a woman, and sometimes a boy rules and consequently skill in
law is not necessarily required. Instead, in electing a bishop a person's
industry and skill are chosen. English bishops rarely preach, and *indeed they
have ceased from this work because they consider it no part, or only the least
part, of their office,* as the Replier reports in the second Reply against
Downham.

[Marginalia: Part. 1 pag. 26.]

Let us return to the point from which we digressed. English bishops

[1623 printed p. 318 | IA leaf 330 | PDF p. 330 | ends before `Quod ad quartum`]

not only use chancellors and jurists as advisers but also substitute them as
judges. And so much for the delegation of jurisdiction.
