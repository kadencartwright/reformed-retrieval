QUESTION FOUR

Do unbaptized catechumens, the excommunicated, and schismatics belong to the
church? A distinction is required.

I. This second question about the parts of the church is also raised by the
papists. Their opinions concerning it vary, as will be evident from a distinct
consideration of the question’s several parts.

[Marginalia: Do catechumens and the unbaptized belong to the church?]

II. The first inquiry concerns catechumens and the unbaptized: Are they
members of the church? The papists do not agree among themselves about this.
Bellarmine maintains that such people “are not in the church actually and
properly, but only potentially, in the way that a human being has been
conceived but has not yet been formed and born,” in *On the Church*, book 3,
chapter 3. Cano, by contrast, according to Bellarmine’s testimony, maintains
that “they belong to the church—not properly to the Christian church, but to
the one that encloses within its compass all the faithful and that also
embraces Abel.” Torquemada and Báñez maintain that catechumens “belong to the
church not in number, but in merit.”

[Marginalia: In what sense they belong.]

III. We judge that the question can be resolved by a twofold distinction.
First, unbaptized people alien to faith and religion, whom Paul calls “those
outside” (1 Corinthians 5:12), must be distinguished from unbaptized believers
or those already disposed toward faith, although they have not yet received
the outward sacrament of baptism. We consider only the latter, not the former,
to belong to the church.

Second, the church can be considered in two ways: either with respect to its
outward state and visible form, or with respect to its inward and invisible
form. Catechumens and the unbaptized are said to belong to the church not in
the former sense and with respect to its outward state, but only in the latter
sense, so that they belong to Christ’s mystical body. They have not yet been
called outwardly to outward and visible communion, and they have no right to
the sacred rites.

IV. The reasons are as follows. 1. Unbaptized catechumens can be saved in that
state; therefore they are of the church and in the church. There is no
salvation outside the church, as our adversaries themselves do not deny, but
it is evident from the converted thief that such people are saved. Although
he was never baptized, he nevertheless was saved.

Bellarmine’s escape does not succeed—that such people are in the church by
desire and potentially, though not actually and properly. If no one can
partake of salvation except insofar as he is in the church, someone who is
only potentially in the church must be said to be saved only potentially, not
actually and in reality.

But if by *desire* you understand the inward act of communion by which someone
already assents to the faith in spirit and desires to be joined to outward
communion, we do not deny that catechumens and the unbaptized can be said to
be in the outward and visible church by desire, since they already truly
belong to inward

[1847 vol. III printed p. 22 | IA leaf 56 | PDF p. 56]

communion. But in the other sense, if *being in the church by desire and
potentially* is understood to mean only potential communion with the invisible
church, it is false that they can be saved actually. No one is actually saved
who has not been given to and engrafted into Christ and who is not a true and
living member of Christ. But whoever is actually a true member of Christ must
by that very fact also be a true member of the church, which is Christ’s body.

V. 2. Catechumens and the unbaptized can be in God’s covenant and be
participants in Christ and his benefits before they have received the
covenant’s symbols. This is evident in those who, though strangers according
to the flesh from the commonwealth of Israel, were enrolled in the body of the
spiritual people and enjoyed the same blessings through faith. Such were
Melchizedek, Job, and others in the Old Testament, and in the New Testament
the centurion, the converted thief (Luke 23), the eunuch (Acts 8), and
countless martyrs who died before receiving baptism.

VI. 3. God’s grace is not bound to the sacraments, as Ferus observes on John
3. Our adversaries themselves do not deny that baptism is not absolutely
necessary for salvation but can be supplied by desire and martyrdom. But if
baptism, which is the entrance into the church, is not so strictly and
precisely necessary, it is readily evident that those who are not enrolled in
the church’s outward form do not for that reason simply perish. Nor is the
church’s order thereby overthrown, because the same judgment must be made
about the church’s outward form as about baptism: not its mere deprivation,
but contempt and disdain for it, condemns.

[Marginalia: Sources of the answers.]

VII. If baptism is called the entrance into the church, when this is said of
outward baptism, it is understood only of the visible church. If it is
referred to inward baptism, it also pertains to the invisible church. But then
those who have not been baptized outwardly, whom we are discussing here, can
be called baptized inwardly, as is seen in the thief and various unbaptized
martyrs. In this sense those who are baptized truly and inwardly always put on
Christ, as Paul says (Galatians 3:27); but in the other sense they are said to
put him on only sacramentally and symbolically.

VIII. Those who are added to the church either believe and receive baptism at
one and the same time, or they believe but nevertheless do not receive
baptism. The latter either are constrained by necessity, as was the thief on
the cross, or are led by their free will, as were the catechumens whose
baptism in the primitive church was delayed until the feast of Easter, and
sometimes even until the end of life.

Those discussed in Acts 2:41, who were added to the church through baptism,
belonged to the former class. But this does not prevent those who receive
Christ by true faith but do not immediately receive baptism, provided this
happens without contempt for the sacrament, from already belonging to the
church, because through faith they become children of God (Galatians 3:26).
It is one thing to be added to the visible church, which happens through
baptism; it is another to be added to the invisible church, which happens
through faith.

IX. If the ancients distinguished catechumens from the faithful, they used
the word *faithful* strictly for those who not only believed but had also
already made a profession of faith in the church’s outward order and polity.
But this does not prevent those same catechumens from already having faith and
having to be numbered among true believers. They were already endowed with
faith inwardly, although they did not yet profess it publicly.

X. It is one thing to have a right to the church’s outward sacred rites, which
belong to profession; it is another to have a right to the inward things of
faith. Believing catechumens actually have a right to the latter because they
are already participants in Christ and his benefits, although they do not yet
have a right to the former unless due order has been observed and baptism has
been administered.

Thus circumcision was the sign of the covenant. Those who lacked it could not
be regarded as belonging to God’s people with respect to outward form,
although they could already belong to God’s people according to election, as
is seen in Job and Melchizedek. In the same way, baptism makes someone a
Christian by profession, but not in inward reality.

[Marginalia: Are the excommunicated members of the church?]

XI. Second, concerning the excommunicated, whom Bellarmine likewise excludes
from the church, we say that an excommunication is either unjust, being made
with an erring key. Such an excommunication is not ratified before God and
cannot cut off from Christ’s body and the church’s communion someone whom
Christ has not rejected. Such was the excommunication pronounced against
Christ and those who followed him, who were put out of the synagogue.

Or an excommunication is just and legitimate. It is either partial and
relative in a certain degree, by which the excommunicated person is barred
from the Lord’s table, prayers, and assemblies, or it is absolute and total,
which is called the greater excommunication, by which the excommunicated
person is cut off from the society of the church itself.

XII. Once this has been established, those excommunicated people are outside
the church who, content for some time with outward profession, had no true
communion of faith and love with Christ and the faithful. When they are
excommunicated, what John says about apostates must be said of them: “They
went out from us, but they were not of us” (1 John 2:19).

Others, however, though cast out of outward communion, retain inward communion
and do not cast away the seed of faith and repentance. They are rightly said
to be of the church. Even if it could happen that before death they were not
readmitted to the church’s peace, having encountered harsh pastors, God does
not favor human faults. Nor can it happen that someone once admitted by faith
into the church’s body is ever cast out of it.

XIII. There are several reasons. 1. Excommunication is never absolute but
conditional, presupposing impenitence and obstinacy. 2. It is not so much an
exclusion from Christ’s mystical body as from the church’s outward communion
and the use of its sacred rites. If the excommunicated person is declared
separated from God and Christ, this must be understood with respect to the
intrinsic desert of the fault, the use of God’s benefits, and the consolation
and peace of conscience that follow the persuasion and sense of the communion
of saints, rather than of an actual severance from Christ, which is
impossible.

3. Since excommunication is a part of the polity and government of particular
churches, it is not exclusion from the catholic church, but only from a
particular and visible church. It is directed not to destruction but to
salvation, and is temporary, not eternal, so that it is a suspension and
banishment, not an absolute cutting up and tearing of the records.

XIV. No member of the church is outside that church of which he is called a
member. But an excommunicated person, although outside the particular visible
church of which he was previously called a member, does not cease to be a
member of the invisible church even after excommunication. Thus Augustine
says in *Against the Donatists*, chapter 20, “For we do not separate from the
people of God those whom, either by degradation or excommunication, we reduce
to a lower place for doing penance.”

XV. A person in whom faith ceases with respect to act and habit together
cannot be a member of the church. But someone in whom it ceases only with
respect to the second act and exercise, as in an excommunicated person, can be
a member, since it does not cease with respect to the first act and habit.

XVI. It is one thing to treat someone as a Gentile and a tax collector—that
is, to regard him as alien to religion and the church’s communion, as Gentiles
and tax collectors were regarded among the Jews. It is another thing for him
to be such in reality. The former can be said of the excommunicated, but not
the latter.

Nor, if they are not severed from Christ’s body, do they therefore suffer no
harm. It is a great harm to be cast out of the visible church and deprived of
the communion of the sacred rites, by which Christ confirms and seals his
grace in us.

XVII. When Bellarmine says in chapter 6, “An excommunicated person is in the
church in soul, or by desire, which is sufficient for his salvation, but he is
not in it in body, or by outward communion, which properly makes a person
belong to this visible church that is on earth,” he approaches our position
quite plainly. He maintains that it is sufficient for someone’s salvation to
be in the church in soul, though not in body. But since

[1847 vol. III printed p. 24 | IA leaf 58 | PDF p. 58]

there is no salvation outside the church, some invisible church besides the
visible church must be acknowledged, in which people can be in soul, if not
in body, and be saved. He confirms this more clearly when he says in the same
place, “A person unjustly excommunicated can be a friend of God and belong to
the soul of the church.”

XVIII. To say a word about schismatics, we readily grant that those who rashly
and unjustly withdraw from the true church, tearing its unity, do not belong
to the church. But we deny that those who are falsely branded with this
hateful name because, as Bellarmine says, they are unwilling to submit to the
Roman pontiff are excluded from it.
