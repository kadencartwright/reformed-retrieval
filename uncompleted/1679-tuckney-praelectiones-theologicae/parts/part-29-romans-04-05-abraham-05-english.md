ROMANS 4:2

Εἰ γὰρ Ἀβραὰμ ἐξ ἔργων ἐδικαιώθη, ἔχει καύχημα· ἀλλ’ οὐ πρὸς τὸν Θεόν.

For if Abraham was justified by works, he has glory, but not before God. —
Vulgate

For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something about which to
boast, but not before God. — Beza

Chrysostom aptly observes that this is an obscure saying which requires
explanation. We approach it as follows.

The proposition asserted in the first clause was that Abraham was justified
not by works but by faith. Paul now confirms it by an argument from boasting,
as the words “for if” demonstrate.

Adams and Estius deny that “for” is causal here, taking it instead as
assertive or supplementary and as the beginning of Paul's answer to the
preceding verse's question, “What shall we say that Abraham found by works?”
It would mean, “Certainly not by works, because then he would have possessed
boasting,” and so forth. We already showed, however, that the question in the
first clause itself supplied the answer. This verse begins not the answer
but its proof, as the ordinary meaning and Pauline use of “for if” plainly
proclaim (Romans 3:7; 4:14; 5:10, 17; 6:5; 8:3; 11:15; 21:24). Adams's
invented Hebraism is unnecessary.

We may therefore take it as established that this clause contains not the
answer but the proof of the answer included in the preceding question. Its
argument proceeds to an impossibility or absurdity, as Bucer says, or from
denial of the consequent to denial of the antecedent, as Aquinas, Pareus,
and others say. Every writer I have read agrees on that general point. The
interpreters divide completely, however, over the argument's particular
meaning and the consequent Paul seeks to destroy.

Since the argument is an epichireme, or incomplete reasoning, as Calvin says,
they complete its syllogism in different and even directly contrary senses.
Some formulate it: “If Abraham was justified by works, he had something
about which to boast before people, though not before God. But he did have
something about which to boast even before God. Therefore he was not
justified by works.” On this view the present verse expresses only the
syllogism's major proposition. Verse 3 supplies and proves the minor, that
Abraham possessed glory before God, because Scripture says he believed God
and it was credited to him as righteousness.

[1679 printed p. 187 | IA leaf 195]

Others formulate the syllogism: “If Abraham was justified by works, he had
something about which he could boast. But he had nothing about which he
could boast, at least not before God. Therefore he was not justified by
works.” The present verse thus contains both major and minor propositions.
They hold that verse 3 proves the minor—Abraham had nothing about which to
boast before God—because Scripture says that Abraham believed God and it was
credited to him as righteousness. As Olevianus devoutly says, one to whom
faith is freely credited has nothing about which to boast before God, but
does have reason to glorify God.

Thus, under these different expositions, verse 3 proves entirely contrary
positions. Because faith was credited to Abraham as righteousness, on the
former account he had something about which to boast before God, while on
the latter he did not. We must return to this below.

Origen was, I think, the first to dictate the former view. He writes: “The
apostle says that if Abraham was justified by works, he has a reward, but
not before God. He argues this with dialectical skill. If he was justified
by works, he has no glory before God. But it is established that Abraham has
glory before God. Therefore Abraham was justified not by works but by faith,
so that he necessarily has glory before God. Scripture declares that
Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness. If God
reckoned it to him as righteousness, his glory is undoubtedly before God.”

Origen is followed by many Fathers—Augustine, Ambrose, Chrysostom, and
Theophylact—all the Roman writers I have seen, and even some of ours,
including Bucer, Peter Martyr, and Ludovicus de Dieu. They understand Paul
to attribute boasting before God to Abraham and thereby prove that he was
justified not by works of law, which produce boasting only before people,
but by faith, which alone causes Abraham and other believers to boast before
God. They do not all understand this in the same sense.

Origen and later Roman writers say that Abraham could have glory from works
before people because works are public and visible, but not before God, who
sees and searches the heart and knows who believes in secret.

Chrysostom posits two kinds of boasting, by works and by faith. He holds that
the boasting of faith is far superior and valid before God for two reasons.
First, it glorifies God more by thinking greater and more excellent things
of him and believing he can do the impossible. Second, one who boasts in his
works boasts in or before himself and has his own labors to present, whereas
one justified by faith boasts in God as saved by him and his grace, as
Theophylact likewise says.

[1679 printed p. 188 | IA leaf 196]

The boasting of faith before God which they understand Paul to attribute to
Abraham is boasting not in himself, his merits, or his works, but in the
Lord alone and his free mercy, in accordance with Jeremiah 9:23–24 and 1
Corinthians 1:30–31. Bucer, Peter Martyr, and De Dieu accept the former
construction of Paul's argument only in this sense. They formulate it as
follows:

“If Abraham was justified by works, he possessed only carnal boasting, which
has no validity before God. But Abraham had something about which to boast
even before God. All the Jews claimed this, and Paul admits it when he so
highly praises Abraham's faith. Abraham was called God's friend, made father
of many nations and heir of the world, and all the nations of the earth were
to be blessed in his seed. He certainly had reason to boast before God in
this grace. Therefore he was not justified by works and did not find
righteousness according to the flesh. Flesh has nothing about which to boast
before God; faith does, since in embracing God's power and his Son's death
it rightly boasts of having found eternal redemption. Because this boasting
is in the Lord, it is most pleasing to the Lord.”

Although this first interpretation and construction of Paul's argument has
great names supporting it, with their leave it does not satisfy me or
several of our writers. They formulate Paul: “If Abraham was justified by
works, he did not have glory before God; but he did have glory before God;
therefore he was not justified by works.” This representation of the
argument errs in several respects.

First, Paul would express here only the major proposition of the entire
syllogism: “If he was justified by works, he did not possess glory before
God,” while wholly leaving unspoken the assumption, “But he did possess
glory before God.” Paul's discourse would gape open, and the connection
between this clause and verse 3 would be unclear. How do these statements
cohere: “If Abraham was justified by works, he would not possess glory
before God” in verse 2, and “For Scripture says, Abraham believed God, and it
was credited to him as righteousness” in verse 3? How does the latter prove
the consequent which completes verse 2?

If verse 3 is said to prove the implied minor, that Abraham possessed glory
before God, I answer that this minor is false, as will shortly appear. Even
if it were true, it appears nowhere and is merely assumed. Rather, the
obscure and unstable consequent should have been proved, while Paul's
opponents would readily concede the minor. On the alternative
interpretation all is plain: “If Abraham was justified by works, he would
have something about which to boast”; that is the major, followed by the
minor, “But he has nothing about which to boast before God.”

[1679 printed p. 189 | IA leaf 197]

Verse 3 solidly proves this minor because Scripture says faith was credited
as righteousness, which excludes boasting. No one can boast of something
freely credited.

Second, the former construction also errs because its major proposition is
not true: “If Abraham was justified by works, he did not possess boasting
before God.” There is no such consequence. Indeed, the contrary follows. If
he was justified by works, he possessed matter for boasting before God. Paul
himself states in Romans 3:27 that boasting is excluded by faith alone and
expressly denies that it is excluded by works: “Boasting is excluded. By
what law? By that of works? Not at all, but by the law of faith.”

Absolutely considered, not even the most perfect works of the holiest people
supply matter for boasting before God. First, compared with his infinite
majesty and holiness, they are defiled and unworthy: “The heavens are not
pure in his sight” (Job 15:15), and “he finds perversity in his angels” (Job
4:18). Even the greatest stars grow ashamed before the splendor of this sun;
how much more our little lamps? They are far from boasting. Second, the best
works of the holiest people, however great, must be referred to God as the
source and author of all being and goodness and are consequently owed to him
by every right. “When we have done everything commanded, we have nothing
about which to boast but should say, We are unprofitable servants, because
we have done what we were obligated to do” (Luke 17:10).

Thus, considered absolutely as supreme Creator and creatures, God and we
stand in a relation that makes us incapable of merit or boasting.
Nevertheless, insofar as God was pleased to enter a covenant of works with
us in which eternal life should accrue to the worker as wages for completed
work by distributive justice, such a worker could in that respect merit and
boast, especially if he performed the work wholly or partly by the power of
his free will. The major's consequence is therefore false: “If Abraham was
justified by works, he would not have had boasting before God.” He would
rather have had it.

Third, the invented minor, “But Abraham possessed boasting before God,” is
also false. He did not. Paul denies it in express terms: “but not before
God.” He proves this in the next clause because Abraham was justified through
faith and its gracious crediting, for the law of faith excludes boasting, as
Paul had said in chapter 3.

Let us briefly examine the reasons which led advocates of the former view to
believe Paul asserts that Abraham possessed boasting before God.

Roman writers can be assigned two reasons. The first arises from their
common doctrine. Although Cardinal Toledo's purple blushes somewhat here
and he admits the proposition, “A person has nothing about which to boast,
because he has nothing he did not receive,” the rest commonly and vigorously
maintain that a person can, at least in part, turn himself to God by the
power of free will and, once made righteous, perfectly fulfill God's law so
as to merit eternal life by condignity.

[1679 printed p. 190 | IA leaf 198]

They claim this from the strictness of justice, even commutative justice, so
that God would be unjust if he did not pay the promised reward. Why could
such a worker not truly and properly boast even before God? Since we have
already refuted this bold and blasphemous position more fully as to merit
and attacked it as to boasting, it should not be pursued further but rather
detested.

Their second reason is that they want the Greek word to be rendered “glory,”
as the Old Latin does, rather than “boasting.” Cardinal Toledo insists upon
this after rejecting the first reason, because he distinguishes the term
Paul supposedly attributes to Abraham here from the word for boasting which
Paul excluded in the preceding chapter. He concedes that the latter often
means boasting and ostentation but contends the former is nearly equivalent
to praise and approval. The cardinal plays the subtle critic in vain.

First, such words are often confused in meaning and used equivalently. The
word in the present verse denotes matter for boasting in 2 Corinthians 1:12
and Romans 15:17, while the related word denotes the act of boasting in 1
Corinthians 5:6; 9:15; 2 Corinthians 5:12; and Philippians 1:26. The Old
Latin itself, though rendering the word here as “glory,” renders it
“boasting” in 1 Corinthians 5:6; Cajetan likewise affirms that it properly
means boasting in this passage.

Second, even if in the strictest grammar the terms differ, the former
meaning the object or matter of boasting and the latter the act itself, the
distinction gives no help to Toledo's view. If Abraham possessed matter for
boasting in Toledo's sense, he also possessed the act of boasting, unless
Toledo thinks being able but unwilling nobler, so that Abraham refused out
of modesty and humility to do what he could rightly and meritoriously do.
Paul, however, removes from us not merely every act of boasting but also its
matter, both here and in the preceding chapter. He cannot consistently
attribute it to Abraham here.

Third, the present word does not properly mean glory, as the Old Latin
renders it, nor praise and approval, as Toledo says. If its related verb can
mean praise or approval at all, it means not those things absolutely but
praising oneself. Even advocates of this view do not claim Abraham possessed
glory before God by praising himself before God. They mean only that God
praised and approved him as righteous, a meaning the word cannot bear.
These are the Roman reasons for claiming that Paul attributes glory or
boasting to Abraham, and their falsity and weakness are now evident.

[1679 printed p. 191 | IA leaf 199]

Chrysostom's first reason is that Abraham's faith, like anyone else's,
possesses boasting because it thinks the greatest things about God and thus
glorifies him more. This is true, but it does not fit the scope of the
passage or the word's meaning. At the end of the chapter Paul praises
Abraham's faith because he believed in hope against hope and gave glory to
God (verses 19–21). At the chapter's beginning, however, Paul treats another
matter and removes from Abraham every boasting before God.

Moreover, even if Paul had said Abraham possessed boasting before God—which
he expressly denies—this could not be explained by saying Abraham's faith
thought magnificently about God. That would be to glorify God, not to boast
before him. To boast before God and to glorify God are far different. The
former is “to boast before God,” as here; the latter “to boast in the Lord,”
as in 1 Corinthians 1:31. Chrysostom's first explanation therefore wavers or
at least does not fit the passage and its phrasing.

His second reason fits better. Abraham possessed boasting by faith in this
sense: those seeking justification by works display their works and boast
in themselves, while faith sends a person wholly outside himself. It claims
nothing for itself but ascribes everything it is and has to God, his free
goodwill, and Christ's imputed righteousness, and thus boasts not in itself
but in God alone. The following verses point this way: verse 3's testimony
about faith freely credited to Abraham as righteousness, and verses 4–5's
reason that wages are credited to a worker according to debt, while faith is
credited as righteousness according to God's gracious purpose to the one
who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly. This alone
explains why Bucer, Peter Martyr, De Dieu, and certain others formulate and
interpret Paul's argument in the former way.

I willingly acknowledge three things. First, as to its substance, this
interpretation contains a devout position most agreeable to the truth and
consolation of the passage, displaying faith's genuine nature admirably.
Second, it can fit the following verses reasonably well. The gracious
crediting of faith there, opposed to workers claiming wages according to
debt, transfers all boasting from self to God alone and his free mercy.
Third, as to the substance expressed, it can agree perfectly with the other
interpretation even though their constructions of Paul's argument are
directly contrary. Those who make the syllogism's assumption affirmative,
“But Abraham had something about which to boast before God,” explain it by
saying Abraham's faith claimed nothing for itself but attributed everything
to God and his grace and therefore boasted before God—that is, in God alone.

[1679 printed p. 192 | IA leaf 200]

For the very same reason, those who make the assumption negative according
to Paul's plain words—“But Abraham had nothing about which to boast before
God”—say that faith, being freely credited as righteousness, ascribes
nothing to itself or its works but everything to God and his grace. They
therefore say and mean the same thing in substance. It is regrettable that
such agreement in the matter produces such opposed and contradictory forms
of Paul's argument. The former says, “If Abraham was justified by works, he
had nothing about which to boast before God; but he did. Therefore...” The
latter says, “If Abraham was justified by works, he had something about
which to boast before God; but he did not. Therefore...” “He had” and “he did
not have” are contradictory. We may truly say here that none are so near,
and none so far, from one another.

Yet despite agreeing wholly or partly in substance, advocates of the former
view seem less correct about the form of the argument, the phraseology, and
perhaps the truth of the matter itself.

As to phraseology, “boasting before God” does not properly express what they
intend: boasting in God, Christ, and his righteousness rather than one's
own. Scripture expresses that as boasting “in God” or “in the Lord,” as in
Romans 2:17 and 1 Corinthians 1:31. The Hebrew likewise says, “My soul shall
boast in the Lord” (Psalm 34:2).

“Boasting before God” means boasting in the presence of God as conscious
witness, arbiter, and judge, in contrast with boasting before human
witnesses. That is the interpreters' common judgment here. Romans 5:1
similarly says that those justified by faith have peace “with God,” before
God the judge. Second Corinthians 3:4 says that we possess confidence through
Christ “toward God.” First John 3:21 says that if our heart does not condemn
us, “we have confidence toward God”—before his tribunal.

It may be replied that believers do possess boasting before God through
faith because they boast in another's righteousness rather than their own:
not in their merits and works before or after faith, but in God's free mercy
and Christ's imputed righteousness. They can present this as a covering
before God's tribunal and boast in it modestly, humbly, gratefully, yet
confidently. First Corinthians 1:30–31 says that Christ became wisdom,
righteousness, sanctification, and redemption from God so that the one who
boasts may boast in the Lord. Thus justification excludes only carnal
boasting resting on human excellence and flowing from works, which is
hostile to grace, not all boasting. Does it detract even minimally from grace
to boast that we are justified by God's grace alone and Christ's imputed
righteousness alone?

[1679 printed p. 193 | IA leaf 201]

I reply first that this statement, in that sense, is most true and contains
the sweetest doctrine. It contains no Roman leaven about the merit of works
done without or from faith, which are either sins or sinful. It boasts in
neither works nor faith itself but only Christ's righteousness apprehended
through faith. This is faith's only boasting, belonging to no merit of works
or faith but wholly to free grace. We boast not at all in ourselves but in
the Lord alone. “Let the one who boasts boast in the Lord”: let him
acknowledge his nothingness and ascribe his righteousness and salvation
wholly to God's grace, as Pareus rightly explains. Peter Martyr consequently
understands boasting here as righteousness liberally granted by God through
Christ—Christ's own righteousness. Chrysostom rightly notes that people have
greater boasting by faith than works because the things faith apprehends far
excel our works. I concede that this is entirely sound and orthodox and that
Abraham and every believer possess matter for boasting even regarding
justification: God's grace and Christ's righteousness freely imputed to
them.

Second, I am less persuaded that Paul discusses this boasting here or
asserts that Abraham possessed something about which to boast in
justification.

First, nothing of the kind appears or is expressly stated here. Even
opponents admit it must be implied.

Second, Ephesians 2:8–9 states the contrary: “By grace you have been saved
through faith, not from works, lest anyone boast.” More decisively, six
verses before the present clause in the same argument, Romans 3:27 openly
excluded boasting from justification. This strongly prejudices the contrary
view. Paul does not discuss one kind of boasting there and another here—our
works there, God's grace here—but the same boasting in both places. There he
excludes it universally from justification; here he denies it particularly
of Abraham.

Third, what follows supports this. Verse 3's testimony that God credited
faith to Abraham as righteousness does not, as some claim, prove the boasting
they find in verse 2. Instead, it directly refutes the boasting excluded
above: Abraham had nothing about which to boast because he was justified by
freely credited faith.

[1679 printed p. 194 | IA leaf 202]

Verses 4–5 further establish this from the matter of boasting. Its matter is
not God's free mercy in Christ, which Paul would prove or approve to have
been in Abraham, but something resting upon Abraham himself and his works,
for which wages are credited according to debt in verse 4. Verse 5 shows
faith has no commerce with this because Abraham believes in the one who
justifies the ungodly and faith is credited to him as righteousness
according to gracious purpose, removing every occasion for such boasting.
As Calvin devoutly asks, if Paul removes it from Abraham, who among us will
arrogate to himself the glory of merit or boasting?

These reasons lead me to reject the first construction, which makes the
assumption affirmative—“But Abraham had something about which to boast”—and
to adopt the second view held by many of our writers. The assumption is not
implied but expressly and negatively stated: “But Abraham had nothing about
which to boast.” The words read: “If he was justified by works, he has
something about which to boast, but not before God.”

Paul anticipates the Jewish objection: “But Abraham found the glory of
righteousness, and therefore could rightly boast.” He answers by
distinguishing two kinds of boasting, before people and before God. He
concedes the former. Since Abraham was truly faithful and godly and
performed so many excellent deeds, he had something to oppose to slanderers
and something about which he could boast confidently, though not arrogantly
or ostentatiously, before people who cannot see inward things and whom he
served well (Galatians 6:4). But he possessed no such boasting before God,
who searches everything and perceives the smallest defects continually
present in our best works, by whose judgment we must stand or fall, and
before whom even the most perfect works could merit absolutely nothing.

Most of our writers formulate Paul's syllogism this way, so that Paul removes
rather than attributes boasting to Abraham. The Arabic interpreter likewise
reads: “If Abraham had been justified by works, he would have possessed
manifest boasting in them. But it is not so before God.”

I will not labor further to confirm this view. Enough has been done, and it
is more than sufficiently established by our interpreters' common judgment,
Paul's words themselves, and especially what precedes and follows: they
assert the gracious justification of the ungodly and consequent imputation,
deny every consideration of debt and merit, and thus exclude boasting.

It remains only to append a few corollaries, as we did for the preceding
clause.

First, if Paul removes all matter of boasting from Abraham, the holiest of
all, who among us, as Calvin says, “will arrogate even a drop of merit to
himself”? There can be no merit where there is no boasting. Aquinas dared
admit merit not in itself but only by supposition of divine ordination.

[1679 printed p. 195 | IA leaf 203]

But no such divine ordination exists; rather, God has ordained the contrary:
the one who boasts must boast in the Lord. Bernard therefore devoutly says
in Sermon 13 on the Song of Songs: “Let us say it, crying more with the
marrow of our hearts than the lips of our mouths: Save us, O Lord our God,
and gather us from the nations, that we may confess your holy name, not our
own, and glory in your praise, not ours, for ever and ever.”

Second, we can derive a mark of Pauline and truly apostolic doctrine: it
devotes itself to humbling a person in and before himself and removes every
occasion for thinking magnificently of and boasting in himself. We can
therefore readily and certainly judge the perverse and proud doctrine of
both Roman Catholics and numerous innovators which flourishes excessively
today even in Reformed churches: doctrines which abolish original sin
altogether or dilute it; justification by works; perfect fulfillment of the
divine law in this life; merit; free will; human reason's judgment in
gospel matters; the moral righteousness of the unregenerate; the salvation
of pagans; and similar things. All inflate a person as though he possessed
something in himself, diminish grace, and begrudge Christ being “all in all”
(Colossians 3:11). We may meanwhile rejoice in our lot: while they, like the
Pelagians of old, elevate and advertise self, humanity, and nature, we join
Bradwardine in pleading God's cause.

Third, this does not prevent us from heaping deserved praises upon all godly
people and those who have served us or others well. “Render to all what is
due; honor to whom honor is due,” Paul commands in Romans 13:7. Remembering
this precept, though he removes boasting before God from Abraham, Paul
willingly acknowledges worthy praise and boasting before people for his
excellent deeds. Paul himself boasts abundantly of his sincerity before
suspicious Corinthians in 2 Corinthians 1:12 and of his apostolic authority
against boastful false apostles in 2 Corinthians 11:8.

Fourth, boasting before God differs immeasurably from boasting before
people. Paul clearly implies both kinds: “He has boasting, but not before
God”—that is, he had boasting before people but not before God. What is
exalted among people can be abominable before God, who knows hearts (Luke
16:15). It is not the one who commends himself who is approved, but the one
whom the Lord commends (2 Corinthians 10:18), whose praise is not from
people but from God (Romans 2:29).
