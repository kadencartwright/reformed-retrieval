CHAPTER XIV.

Verse 1. Of wise women [each] builds her house, but a foolish woman destroys it with her own hands.

In חכמות (*wise women*) and אולת (*foolish woman*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct. In בנתה (*builds*) there is a synecdoche of the part for every good thing that befalls a household, whether by building it new, preserving it once built, or restoring it once fallen. In בית (*house*) there is also a synecdoche of the part. For he understands not only the material house, but further the children, who are like pillars of the building. Thus it also appears to be understood in Psalm 127—that is, as is evident from verse 3—and 2 Samuel 7:16. In בידיה there is a metonymy of the effect for works.

The sum is that men ought not so to trust their own prudence that they have no need of the aid of a prudent wife toward the family's advantageous

[1632 witness | PDF p. 232 | printed columns 438–439]

management. For since Adam's uncorrupted condition could not conveniently lack this consolation of life, much less should his condition when corrupted, and certainly weakened by sin, be deprived of it.

From the Holy Spirit's customary manner of speaking in this book, a wise woman is one who is godly and fears God, not one who has a sharp, acute, and crafty disposition. Of this woman it is said that she builds her house. First, she is instructed from the word of God how she should conduct herself in the whole management of the household; how toward her husband; in what manner toward her children; in what way toward her male and female servants; and, finally, how toward the members of the household. For Scripture, which fully instructs ministers and kings in every part of their office, does not leave the mother of a household naked and unarmed in the matters that belong to her. Although it supplies this instruction here and there throughout all the books of Scripture, it does so most fully in the last chapter of this book. Next, God's blessing contributes much to this matter. Since it alone is effectual in the bearing of children, it also possesses a certain principal power in the acquisition of riches. For since parents are accustomed to be generous in giving their daughters in marriage, it is certainly incredible that a godly woman—who is likewise God's daughter—should come from God undowered into the hand of her husband. Add to this that, although the procreation of children is generally common to godly and ungodly women, it very rarely befalls any but those who fear God, from God's covenant, to receive excellent offspring ennobled by virtues. And in offspring of this kind the hope of a more enduring posterity is nourished, while those who are propagated by ungodly parents generally die out by the third generation. Indeed, to those who survey and review the excellent and distinguished men in the Church and commonwealth, it will be plain and evident to know that nearly all of them were brought into this light by women who feared God.

And since a servant who fears God builds up the house of a heathen man, as is evident from the examples of Joseph and Jacob, who enriched the households of Potiphar, Pharaoh, and Laban, how much more will a godly wife accomplish this, who, joined to her husband by the closest bond, draws to him a more abundant blessing of God than a servant does. Therefore, by this proverb men are admonished that in choosing a spouse and inseparable companion of life, they should have principal regard not for beauty, riches, stock, and lineage, but for godliness, unless they wish to experience at a great price their own ignorance and rashness in complying with the desires of their soul.

The truth of this proverb is confirmed by many examples. A splendid example appears in Rahab the harlot, who by her faith and charity toward God's servants preserved not only her own life but that of her whole kindred, and rescued it from Jericho's πανωλεθρίᾳ (*utter destruction*). Then, by this same obedience, she was also joined in marriage to a prince from the tribe of Judah, from which she ennobled her family. Joshua 2. Another memorable example is presented for consideration in the Shunammite woman, 2 Kings 4. Because of the humane hospitality she showed to the prophet Elisha, first, although barren, she received a son; second, she received the same son back alive from the dead; third, being forewarned of the approaching famine, by migration to another region she delivered herself and her household from destruction; fourth, upon returning to her homeland, she recovered both her son's inheritance and the estates, together with the revenues, that had been taken from her during the time of her sojourn. To this may suitably be referred the fact that the Israelite midwives who feared God were the reason God established numberless families, Exodus 1:21.

In the latter half-verse he illustrates

[1632 witness | PDF p. 233 | printed columns 440–441]

the former doctrine from the contraries: [a foolish or ungodly woman destroys and casts down her house with her own hands. For she not only permits it to collapse and fall—which is sufficiently grievous in itself—but throws it down; nor does she assign to others these parts in destroying the house, but she herself assails it by her own deeds as though both her hands were laid upon it. She does so not only when she squanders and dissipates the goods of the household, but even when she applies her mind to accumulating wealth in order to shore up the falling house. For by her ungodliness she summons God's curse, which alone, like a burning fire, consumes everything easily and without any difficulty. See Judges 14 and 16 concerning Samson's wives; see 2 Kings 8:20. Eve's example in Genesis 3 can be referred to this.

Verse 2. He who walks uprightly reveres Jehovah, but one perverse in his ways despises him.

In הולך, *walks*, and דרכיו, *in his ways*, there is an allegory, but one exceedingly familiar and common.

The head and sum of this proverb is that each person's worship and religion are to be estimated from his works, whether good or evil, and that the surest judgment among men concerning godliness is to be sought from the pattern of his life. Here, first, a caution must be applied: we who have been corrupted from the cradle must not suppose that any uprightness inheres naturally in our minds. But our uprightness, which is mentioned in this place, is not one that we either drew by nature from our parents or acquired by our own labor and industry. Rather, it is called ours because it rests upon us, is demanded from us, and is given to us freely. Moreover, it is said that he walks *in his own uprightness*, rather than simply *in uprightness*, because according to the consideration and condition of each person's calling, so one kind of uprightness and then another exists. The uprightness of a prince is one thing, that of a subject another; that of a minister one thing, that of one ministered to another; that of a father of a household one thing, that of the household members another. Therefore, in order to prove ourselves to be those who truly fear God, we must not only do the things common to all Christians universally, but also zealously pursue the things that rest upon us from our singular or proper calling.

Next, it is further required that we should not merely begin obedience, but should persist in it to our final breath. This is designated both by the word *walking* and especially by the employment of the tense. We must not, therefore, begin by the Spirit and finish in the flesh; rather, the course of obedience once begun must be completed by us, if we do not wish the prize to be snatched away. For we must run in such a way that we may obtain it, 1 Corinthians 9.

In this passage godliness and the worship of God are defined by the fear of God. This fear is not, indeed, the servile fear with which it is agreed that the wicked and even demons are suffused and imbued. Rather, it designates the filial and freeborn fear with which men, out of love for God, fear for themselves lest they admit or omit things that they know to be hostile and ungrateful to God. Thus those are truly said to fear God who fear for themselves concerning a forbidden oath, Ecclesiastes 9. Let the same be transferred to all other crimes forbidden by God's commandment. By the same reasoning, let him be regarded as righteous who fears for himself lest he commit adultery, theft, or murder, or fears for himself because of the omission or interruption of things commanded. In this man's soul the fear of God is so βεβαίως (*firmly*) established that he resolves with every zeal and exertion of mind to undertake the things commanded by God and to flee the things forbidden.

By contrast, the man who is stubborn and perverse in the pattern of his life—that is, the man who complies with the desires of the flesh

[1632 witness | PDF p. 234 | printed columns 442–443]

which is his own, and obeys them—whatever godliness he holds out, and whatever appearance of divine worship he presents, so that he speaks every honorable and glorious thing about God, is nevertheless pronounced by the Prophet to be reckoned and numbered among those who despise God. Here Solomon argues from contempt for God's commandments to contempt for God himself. Hence in 2 Samuel 12 David, said in verse 9 to have despised the word of the Lord, is said in the following verse, verse 10, to have despised God; and Eli and his sons are affirmed to have despised God, 1 Samuel 2:30. But this is most clearly established in Malachi 1:6–7, where it is treated διαλογιστικῶς (*in the form of a dialogue*). For God complains that he is despised by the priests. The priests ask by what right God says this. God replies that he is despised in this: they hold his worship in contempt and, through avarice, admit worthless things into divine worship.

But God uses the word *despised* in this passage in order to frighten men away from evil deeds. For would it not stupefy a man to consider within himself that almighty God, full of majesty, has been despised—when he would shudder at contempt for mortal princes? In Psalm 8 David is carried away into wonder that the infinitely glorious God does not disdain man, but has some regard for him and numbers him for something; and that because of man's low and abject condition, by this very fact that he was created from dust. On this see Abraham, Genesis 18:27. If God rightly despises us because we were born from dust, how much more because of the stain of sin? But most of all, and with the greatest justice, may he do this if we are contemptuous toward God. Who, therefore, does not rightly deserve to expect that God will despise him, if he first provoked God by despising his majesty?

Verse 3. In the mouth of a fool is the rod of pride, but the lips of the wise are their preservation.

In חטר (*rod*) there is a metaphor by which harmful speeches are compared with a rod, with which the fool injures both others and, chiefly, himself.

The purpose of this proverb is to indicate the harms of a proud tongue both to its owner and to others, so that they may beware wherever they are not fortified by heavenly wisdom.

Although Solomon in many proverbs before this has censured the errors of the tongue, and from that point onward has proclaimed its virtues, in this passage he indicates the disease of the tongue that flows from pride, together with the medicine by which we may be safe from it. The tongue has many kinds of errors according to the variety of causes that impel us to speak wrongly: sometimes from a depraved habit, from carelessness and drowsiness; sometimes from anger; not seldom from envy; and so forth. But from the fact that the rod is especially attached by the Prophet to proud speeches, we are taught how hostile and hateful to God the crime of pride is, since by breaking off God's forbearance and patience it in some manner sounds the signal for him to take up arms. See Psalm 12:4–5. For the most exact examples of this, see Pharaoh in Exodus 1; see the history of Rabshakeh in 2 Kings; likewise Nebuchadnezzar in Daniel 4; Herod in Acts 12; and Matthew 11:23.

Next, in order to declare how swift and hastened God's vengeance is against proud and fierce words, he places the rod of divine vengeance in the mouth itself and in the words, as if it sprang from those very inflated and swollen speeches, like a flower from a bud and a berry from a flower. For, like Jonah's gourd, it germinates, blossoms, and reaches maturity in a single night. Thus in a proud mouth, as in marshy and irrigated, or exceedingly rich, soil, God's vengeance grows. Nor is what we received above in chapter 13, verse 21 dissimilar, where evil is said

[1632 witness | PDF p. 235 | printed columns 444–445]

to pursue sinners and press them as though following their very tracks.

Next, by attaching a staff, not a switch, he appears to indicate that God prepares a hard wedge for a hard knot. But the fact that he threatens proud tongues with a staff—or rather a little club—not with a sword, spear, or instrument of war, may perhaps pertain to contempt and disdain for that person, according to the custom and manner of action of men who, when they wish to treat an adversary with the greatest contempt, do not employ a sword but a little club or whip, with which they beat him as though deeming him unworthy of the honor of a fair fight. This manner of action is supremely fitting for the proud. Since they exalt themselves above everyone—not only men but sometimes angels and even God himself—it is most fitting that their confidence and unbridled spirits should be beaten back and broken by a contemptuous kind of punishment. See Psalm 123:4 and the last two words of the verse. God has rather often imitated this custom of men, as when he cast the most powerful kings, Pharaoh and Herod, to death by the most abject little beasts; and cast Nebuchadnezzar, the most august emperor of the whole world, to feed among dumb beasts.

Since in the former part we were taught what evils redound upon the proud tongue, in this latter part we are taught how we ought to be disposed so that we neither bring this loss upon ourselves nor create any danger for ourselves from what is inflicted upon us by the proud. This will come about in no other way than by wisdom granted from heaven—that is, by true godliness—which alone knows how to govern the tongue, James 1:26. Although among unregenerate men there are some of so moderate and placid a disposition that they do not easily provoke others with proud and swollen speeches, nevertheless, for those who have been provoked to contain themselves steadfastly so that they are not led by the example of others into the same crime requires a certain greater strength than can fall within our depraved disposition.

Verse 4. When oxen are absent, the manger [is] clean; but the abundance of produce [is] by the strength of an ox.

In אלפים (*oxen*) and שור (*ox*) there is a synecdoche of the part for every instrument necessary for obtaining sustenance; likewise in אבוס (*manger*) for every place where fodder is set out.

The aim and mark at which this proverb directs itself is to teach that, among things pertaining to the present life, those things which have a necessary use are chiefly to be sought and procured, not those which pertain to pleasure or relaxation of the soul. Out of all the instruments of necessary use he selects the ox, by which he would designate all the others, because an ox is by far the most useful for the support of a household. Hence in Deuteronomy 33 Moses calls Joseph an ox, because he had abundantly nourished the whole family of his father Jacob in the season of famine. And in Psalm 144, when describing the prosperous condition of the commonwealth, he places a good part of it in the fatness and strength of oxen.

He says, therefore, that the manger is clean—that is, that there is no chaff in the manger, much less grain in the granaries—where there are no oxen: fodder does not abound for beasts of burden, much less food for men. This proverb refutes the ignorance of those men who apply their own zeal and that of everyone else to obtaining delights before provision has been made for the things necessary to sustain life. They are concerned first about noble horses for racing, a numerous retinue, splendid clothing, dogs and hawks for hunting and fowling, before they are concerned about the equipment necessary for agriculture and other labors and arts. For just as these easily without those
[1632 witness | PDF p. 236 | printed columns 446–447]

can subsist, nor can they preserve or sustain enjoyment for long. For what profit would there be in having a face anointed with costly ointment when meanwhile there is no bread by which we may be strengthened? God summons us to this matter and to provident care when he forbids the giving in pledge, or the taking as a pledge, of a millstone or its dish, Deuteronomy 24:6. This kind of ignorance is not rarely perceived in heavenly agriculture, where no few persons are found who weary themselves and others with the most difficult questions although they have not yet tasted the first elements of the Christian religion. They pass over neglectfully the doctrine of faith, repentance, and the other things enumerated in Hebrews 6, while meanwhile they inquire with greater curiosity about the degrees of happiness in heaven. Such are the schismatics of our times, who have all their thoughts and senses so thoroughly fixed upon the government of the Church that they never apply their minds to learning the foundations of religion. They act just like people who prepare ointment while meanwhile the bread by which they might sustain themselves is lacking.

In the latter half-verse he argues the former part from the contraries. Namely, all things necessary for life abound for those who use lawful aids granted by God for procuring sustenance. At the same time he also seems to indicate that oxen and instruments of this kind must be well cared for and fed, not only so that they may be strong for accomplishing agricultural labors, as in Psalm 144, but also so that we may be far removed from even the appearance of cruelty. To this pertains the provision sanctioned by the Law that they should not muzzle the mouth of an ox while it treads out grain.

Verse 5. A witness of the highest trustworthiness does not lie, but a false witness breathes out lies.

At first sight no sharp point seems to be inherent in this proverb by reason of which it should be reckoned among the Proverbs of Solomon—and especially of the Holy Spirit. Yet one who looks more closely will perceive that it gives forth the fragrance of the Spirit’s wisdom. For it offers us a lesson eminently suited to discerning human vanity. It teaches that a witness summoned to settle a dispute, who testifies truthfully, will not be empty and mendacious in ordinary and common speech. For no one ought to doubt that the person who is faithful in a great matter will be faithful in lesser matters as well, and therefore that one who has shown himself upright and incorrupt in the greatest matters has acquired great trust and authority for himself among everyone in the common intercourse of life—especially if the testimony which he gave was joined with some inconvenience or danger to himself or to his own people. This is confirmed by Paul’s example. When he was accused of fickleness by the Corinthians because he had not come to them as they had understood from the former Epistle that he would do, he refuted their slander from the fact that in the ministry of the word he had always maintained a faithful and constant course in a matter far more weighty. And this part of the proverb is so evident that it needs no fuller testimony. But what follows seems further removed from the truth. For it does not appear to follow that one who breaks his word in ordinary and common speech will immediately prove faithless in a weightier matter, especially a judicial one. Nor does the proverb affirm that a person who lies once or twice in a small matter will break his word when employed as a witness. Rather, it speaks of one who breathes out lies in ordinary speech, has grown accustomed to lying, and has often trodden the path of lying. Christ confirms the truth of this proverb in Luke 16: one who is not faithful in the least matter will not be faithful in a great one either. Therefore, if we do not wish trust in weightier matters to be denied us, let us

[1632 witness | PDF p. 237 | printed columns 448–449]

train ourselves to speak the truth in the smallest matters. And just as we should preserve to the end the faith pledged to God in the profession of religion, so let us preserve inviolate and entire the faith we have pledged to human beings.

But if one prefers to follow in this passage the sense that is most evident, something is nevertheless also contained in these words which is not so obvious and exposed to everyone: namely, that the person to be regarded as a true witness is not one who never lies at all—for what mortal is there in whose tongue no deceit is found?—but that the truthful witness is the person who does not repeat and redouble lies or do so deliberately, as the verb of the third conjugation indicates. By contrast, the one to be regarded as false and mendacious is not the person who lies once or, through weakness, says something false as Sarah did in Genesis 17, but the one who grows accustomed to empty speeches.

Verse 6. For a mocker who seeks wisdom, it is not to be found; but knowledge is easy for a prudent person.

In קל, *light*, there appears to be a metaphor transferred from earthly things weighed upon a scale to the comprehension of heavenly things. It is just as if he were saying: as things of light weight are easily carried by a strong and robust person, so heavenly wisdom is perceived by a godly and prudent man without any trouble or difficulty.

Here it is asked, first, who is the mocker? The mocker is a sinner who, through pride and arrogance, refuses to be corrected and brought to good conduct by bare instructions and verbal chastisements, and treats with mockery everything contrary to his soul’s desire. See Proverbs 9:7; 13:1; and 15:12.

It is first affirmed of him that he seeks wisdom. But why does he who seeks not find it according to God’s promise? Namely, because he does not seek it as the word of God commands. He does not seek earnestly and diligently, as one seeks gold and gems, and so forth. Next, he does not seek with the purpose of putting himself upon the way of God’s commandments; instead, he seeks God’s will in the hope of finding it favorable to his own desires, so that he may proceed more fearlessly and blamelessly in his begun course of wickedness, without any protest or sting of conscience, or so that he may delight his mind with the sweet speech and elegant diction of ministers. For the former kind see Jeremiah 42:1–2 and following, and likewise Ezekiel 20:1–2 and following. For the latter kind see the example in Ezekiel 33:31–32. See the example of Herod in Matthew 26, who desired to see Christ.

By contrast, God will teach concerning his will the person who seeks God’s will with the purpose of giving himself over to obedience, John 7:17. Next, Psalm 25:8–9 teaches that [God will indeed instruct sinners and lead them in his ways], but not all sinners: only those who become humble and meek through consciousness of their sins, and likewise those who fear God in the same passage. These are the people for whom Solomon affirms that knowledge is ready at hand and easy. For only those are to be accounted prudent who take leave of their own prudence and the reasoning of their reason, give themselves over to God for teaching and discipline, and are no wiser than God’s utterances permit them to be—or, to use the Apostle’s words, beyond what is written, 1 Corinthians 4:6. Proverbs 8:8–9 accords with this statement of Solomon, and likewise 2 Peter 3, where he teaches that only the unlearned twist the Scriptures to their own destruction. Therefore, the papists who say that Scripture is difficult to understand and covered in darkness thereby confess themselves to be foolish and unskilled, proud and devoid of the fear of God.

Verse 7. Go away from the presence of a

[1632 witness | PDF p. 238 | printed columns 450–451]

foolish man; and [one whom] you do not [know to be endowed] with words of knowledge.

In לך there is a synecdoche by which not only bodily departure but also aversion of mind is designated. For bodily withdrawal from fools is not always permitted, as when a wife is not permitted to depart from her husband, a servant from his master, or a son from his parents. In שפתי, *lips*, there is a metonymy of the efficient cause.

This proverb is intended to teach that avoiding the company of the wicked is a great defense of piety. Previously, at chapter 13, verse 20, he taught that a great increase of piety comes from fellowship and association with the godly; now he teaches that no little strength for the confirmation of piety lies in shunning the company of the ungodly. This proverb therefore meets the evasion of those who will profess that they delight in the company of the godly and eagerly seek it, but that for this reason they must not bring about a complete separation and divorce from the wicked. It is sufficient, they say, if our principal familiarity is with the godly, without our wholly separating ourselves from the wicked. But from these two proverbs, set together and compared with each other, it is established that association and familiarity must be cultivated only with the godly. For just as from these propositions—*we are not justified by works, and we are justified by faith; we ought to worship God, and we ought not to worship idols*—it is rightly concluded that we are justified by faith alone and that God alone must be worshiped, so also from these axioms—*we must associate with the godly, and we must not associate with the ungodly*—it is concluded that we must associate only with the godly.

But here a cunning and crafty mind rises up and says that it need not fear for itself because of the company of the wicked: it can associate with them and meanwhile preserve itself untouched and pure from their contagion. Solomon blocks this hiding place when he commands distinctly that we withdraw ourselves from the company of the wicked. [*For what has righteousness to do with unrighteousness?*] 2 Corinthians 6. [*A little leaven leavens the whole lump*], 1 Corinthians 5:6. *Touch no unclean thing*, 2 Corinthians 6. For just as under the law a thing regarded as unclean rendered all other things unclean that were affected by contact with it, so the dregs and stain of the wicked spread to others who in some fashion handle them through association. But they proceed further to object that they will have no dealings with them in matters of faith, but will deal with them only in matters bounded by the circuit of the present life. Yet in these very unnecessary dealings people are held entangled by the wickedness of those with whom they have entered covenants pertaining to the advantages of this life. See Ezekiel 23, where it is expressly indicated that the Israelites joined themselves to every kind of idolatry practiced by those with whom they made covenants of association and mutual aid. Hence that excellent profession in David, Psalm 26:4–6.

And even if they were masters of themselves, knew how to command their own wills and affections, and remained untouched and immune from the vices of others, account must nevertheless also be taken of other people: first, of the weak and those who readily fall, lest they plunge headlong by our example; next, of the wicked, lest they sleep on in their sins when they understand that people renowned for the profession of religion desire their company. It is therefore established that the company of the good with the wicked and dealings between them must be avoided, insofar as it can conveniently be done. Nor is a way of escape opened to them by the fact that Christ dined with publicans and sinners. First, the condition of Christ and our own are far different, because he was always superior to and unconquerable by every sin, which has befallen no mortal person once Christ is excepted. Second, it was for no other reason than this that he joined himself to their

[1632 witness | PDF p. 239 | printed columns 452–453]

society: to recall them to health of faith and to the good fruit of morals. If they should do this with conscience borne up to the tribunal and presence of God as witness, I certainly do not refuse that they do the same.

In the latter half-verse he describes the foolish man as one whom we recognize not to have lips of knowledge, or polished speech, so that the person spoke acutely who said: [*Speak, so that I may see who you are.*] In this way—that is, from the use of the tongue—the surest and easiest indication of piety and impiety is sought. Hence Christ says, [*By your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned*], Matthew 12:37. And 1 Corinthians 15: *Evil communications corrupt good manners*, so that if one must live with the wicked, those are especially to be chosen from whom every use of tongue and speech has been snatched away.

Verse 8. The wisdom of a shrewd person [is] to attend to his way, but the folly of foolish people [is directed toward] deceit.

This proverb is referred to the eighth commandment and explains that part of the commandment which does not so much prohibit idleness as, by commanding orderly and rightly appointed work, forbid the contrary.

The Apostle in 1 Corinthians 7 wishes each person to remain in the calling in which he was called, and in 1 Thessalonians 4:11 he exhorts everyone to attend to their own affairs and to occupy themselves only in those things incumbent upon them by their calling. In order to do this, it is required that each person have his way and calling well known and understood. This is perceived from Solomon in the present passage and from the Apostle in Ephesians 5:15: περιπατεῖτε ὡς σοφοὶ μὴ ὡς ἄσοφοι, *walk as wise people, not as unwise*.

This doctrine is illustrated by a likeness drawn from travelers, who—unless perhaps they are deranged—have known and understood the road that leads to the place they have fixed in mind and toward which they direct their journey. For that reason they carry a map of the region through which they travel and inquire of passersby which road is most passable; finally, they carefully employ every aid that may preserve them from errors, especially since, as strangers, they do not know the twists and meanders of the region through which they travel. For such darkness has been spread over our minds that we do not know not only what duty is incumbent upon us toward God, but also what is just and due toward other human beings and even toward ourselves. For although by the guidance even of depraved nature we may appear sufficiently well instructed in how to conduct ourselves in human affairs, nevertheless not only the experience of our common weakness but also the Law of God laid down concerning the duties that touch ourselves and others declares that we are deceived. God would not have enacted that Law if these things had been sufficiently comprehended by us.

In this kind of matter people commonly sin in two ways. First, trusting in the powers of our own reason and disposition and not considering the magnitude of the matter and of our calling, we have followed the way without inquiry and investigation. To this heading may also not inconveniently be referred the fact that the tyranny of custom holds us so captive that it does not permit us to extend our thought beyond what has been received by custom. The other stone against which we stumble is that we busy ourselves with the affairs of others and with things that do not concern us; Scripture intended to mark this by the name ἀλλοτριοεπισκοπίας, *meddling in another’s affairs*, 1 Peter 4:15; 1 Timothy 5:13; and 2 Thessalonians 3:11. Hence a servant commonly knows better what belongs to his master than what belongs to his own proper duty; and, conversely, a son knows better what ought to be furnished to him by his father than what ought to be rendered to his father by himself.

In the latter half-verse it is taught how the wicked, passing over investigation of their own duty and of those things which fall upon them from their

[1632 witness | PDF p. 240 | printed columns 454–455]

calling, devote themselves wholly to devising frauds and byways, Proverbs 2:15. This also returns upon them for evil and destruction. For the fraud placed here refers not only to the one devised by the wicked person, but can also embrace the one by which he himself is held entangled. See Proverbs 28:18. And thereby he teaches that, for a good man who devotes great effort to understanding the way and thoroughly knowing his own affairs, all things will fall out rightly and succeed splendidly. For he does not seek it with such diligence unless he means to express it in deeds.

It must further be noted that Solomon brands ingeniously devised frauds with the designation *folly*, though people boast expansively of them by that name and hold up to ridicule the righteous, who shrink from those hiding places.

Verse 9. Guilt reconciles fools; but among the upright, that which is acceptable [produces reconciliation].

In אשם, *guilt*, there is a metonymy of the adjunct for the sin from which guilt is contracted. Likewise, רצון, *delights*, stands for an agreeable and acceptable thing.

This proverb is referred to obedience to the whole law, not to any single commandment of the law as most proverbs are, all of which appear able to be referred to two commandments as to chief heads.

Not unlike this proverb is the one found at 10:23, but—as happens in the repetitions employed by Scripture—not without ample profit. Here he depicts the disposition of the wicked, who receive no trouble or sorrow from the very thing from which they ought to derive the greatest grief. As long as they do nothing but contract guilt and are not called to account, or afflicted with punishment, because of their offense, they laugh at the sin and hold their reprovers up to ridicule; they are moved only at last, when punishment is imposed. In their folly they imitate those who bind and render themselves liable with countless written bonds yet are not concerned in the least about the contracted debt until they have been summoned before the law and condemned to prisons, from which they are not permitted to depart until they have paid the last penny. But if it belongs to prudence to take care that you do not become liable to human beings, how much more that you not sink into debt to almighty God. You might perhaps deceive a human being; you will never deceive God. When the human being is dead, perhaps the debt is extinguished at once; but God remains forever.

But here they bark that an injury is done to them because they are barred from their relaxation and recreation; that the severity of certain people which deprives them of their jests and delights is intolerable. For they are so perversely disposed that, although they are kept from illicit and degrading exercises, they immediately cry out that they are being deprived of every right and liberty of refreshment and relaxation. Solomon meets them in the latter half-verse when he says that upright and blameless people do not lack something by which to refresh and delight themselves while meanwhile they walk in the right way of virtue and piety. See Psalm 119, section 2.

Verse 10. The soul knows its own bitterness; and no stranger [at all] intermingles himself in its joy.

In מרה, *bitterness*, and שמחה, *gladness*, there appears to be a synecdoche of parts for all the faculties of the soul.

The purpose is that, after properly considering the magnitude of each affection—sorrow and joy, and the other disturbances of the soul—we may prepare ourselves for their right and prudent use.

What is said here is confirmed by the Apostle’s testimony in 1 Corinthians 2: [*No one knows the things of a man except the spirit of the man,*
[1632 witness | PDF p. 241 | printed columns 456–457]

who is in him;] Therefore [no one knows] either grief or joy. For apart from the fact that there is great dissimulation in man, by which he bears one thing in his heart, another on his countenance, another in his mind, and another on his brow, there is also such great weakness in nature, especially corrupted nature, that even when we make the attempt and exert the powers which we keep enclosed within, we cannot disclose and bring forth the emotions of joy and grief. Hence comes that saying, 1 Peter 1:8 and Revelation 2:17.

From this axiom we learn first that matter for humiliation is supplied to us in the impotence of our tongue. Whatever it attempts, it nevertheless cannot bring forth the sentiments and conceptions of our mind, and what it does express it pronounces corruptly and depravedly in comparison with what it did before it was infected—or rather imbued—with the stain of sin. Indeed, what is more, the more grief or joy we conceive within and embrace in our mind, the less we are able to express our mind's sentiments and conceptions. See 1 Samuel 1:10 and 13. From this it is gathered that no one except the one God is to be invoked, since he alone knows the heart and understands the things which we are unable to express. No saint or angel can perform this.

Then, since the magnitude of grief arises from sin, but inexpressible joy from holiness and integrity of life, we must strive with every power and sinew to forsake sin and pursue holiness of life, unless, after rejecting even in this life the firstfruits of eternal and undefiled felicity, we wish to experience a foretaste and, as it were, a pledge of the punishment of hell.

Furthermore, since our infancy in speaking and in expressing the sentiments of our mind is so great, charity dictates that we not take hold of the words and discourses of others tenaciously and with excessive strictness, so that harm or disadvantage returns upon them, but rather allow them a more fully unfolded explanation of their mind, especially when in the rest of their manner of life they are found to be strangers to and far removed from fraud.

Moreover, since it is commanded that [we weep with those who weep and rejoice with those who rejoice], this doctrine should admonish us to conceive in our mind something greater and graver in the grief of others than they are able to express; thus we should stir ourselves to greater compassion, bear more equably with their impatience, if any should break forth, and interpret it with the least possible loss to their reputation. That Job's friends sinned in this respect is evident from that history. By contrast, in another person's joy let us also sharpen our gratitude to God.

But in unlawful griefs and joys let this doctrine render us cautious, so that we set a limit upon them, lest, if we indulge them, they drive us headlong into great danger.

Verse 11. The house of the wicked shall be destroyed, but the tent of the upright shall flourish.

In בית (*house*) and אהל (*dwelling*) there is a synecdoche for the whole equipment of the house of the wicked and for their posterity, and for the scanty and narrow furnishings of the righteous and for their descendants. In יפריח (*they shall flourish*) there is a metaphor from plants, especially those which possess so much richness that they do not lose their greenness even in winter, in the most turbulent storms, or even in a flood, such as the olive or the laurel.

The aim is that he should establish the distinction between the good and the wicked by means of a right and just valuation of outward things.

When the wicked abound in children amid buildings magnificently constructed and splendidly adorned, they are accustomed to insult God and all the godly, place their confidence in their wealth, and sometimes persuade themselves that they are

[1632 witness | PDF p. 242 | printed columns 458–459]

approved by God; for that reason they take courage and raise their crests. See Job 8:15. The Holy Spirit tears away their vain confidence when he teaches that all these things are not merely to be shaken but utterly laid waste, even if they erect buildings like fortresses, much less their tents and tabernacles, as is expressly stated in John 20:21. For God is no respecter of persons, but overthrows poor and rich alike if they are wicked.

And just as this proverb beats down the pride and hypocrisy of the wicked, so it comes to the aid of the temptations of the godly, who are wonderfully tormented by this felicity of the wicked and are almost solicited to fall away. Psalm 73.

When the wicked behold the abject condition of the godly, they judge them most miserable and proudly insult them; the godly themselves are also cast down. Solomon meets both evils when he teaches that they too will blossom and grow green day by day, and therefore are not to be judged from their presently abject condition. For their cottage is to be raised into a house or palace, provided they patiently await and expect God. See 2 Samuel 23:4.

It must nevertheless be noted that, since these promises and threats concern the things of the present life, they are true not universally but ἐπὶ τὸ πολύ (*for the most part*). For in Job 21:28 it appears that Job's friends used this argument to accuse Job of impiety. Job responds to them in verse 30 that the wicked are sometimes rescued and carried out of the midst of calamities in which the godly are crushed.

Verse 12. There is a way [which] seems right in the sight of men, whose end [is] a manifold way to death.

Some things are of such a kind that the light of nature, which flashes and, as it were, sparkles out of the darkness, immediately comprehends them in thought and judges them vicious and worthy of hatred: that God does not exist or is not to be worshiped; that parents are not to be pursued with honor; that there is to be promiscuous use of women and possessions; that one may go about naked; or that pledged faith may be broken. Yet there are other things of this kind which, although evil, have an appearance and image of truth or honor. Indeed, they have a greater semblance of truth and honor than that which is in fact true, honorable, and holy: for example, making the greatest possible expenditures in divine worship even though they have not at all been commanded by God's word, and afflicting the body as much as possible, and so forth. In duties toward neighbors, examples are being unserviceable toward magistrates who disobey God and oppose his religion; repaying evil with evil; dismissing a disobedient wife; and administering one's own people and the things acquired by oneself according to one's own judgment. This is confirmed by a parallel. For just as a certain road seems straight in making journeys although it is crooked, so it is also in morals and the manner of life.

Therefore it does not belong to our understanding, although sharpened by nature and polished by art, to judge; instead, every single thing must be brought to the standard of divine truth so that judgment may be rendered from it. See John 7:24. Christ, whose senses were most perfect and whose powers of mind were most vigorous, refused to pronounce judgment from sight or hearing. Isaiah 11:3. Here belongs Christ's saying: *Whoever wishes to become my disciple, let him deny [himself]*, and so forth.

From this it is gathered how frivolous and empty is that defense made by the Papists and others from good zeal and what they call intention. See the example in the Levite who put forth his hand to steady the ark, and John 16.

By saying that its furthest point and end is not a way but ways of death, he teaches that, when one error is admitted, multiple errors spring up: sins are chained together in this manner.

But in saying that its last end is death, although even its beginnings when badly undertaken

[1632 witness | PDF p. 243 | printed columns 460–461]

lead to death, he speaks in this way because it appears especially then. Next, a life badly led at its beginning condemns no one unless he continues in evil.

Verse 13. Even from laughter the mind aches, and at last gladness [becomes] grief.

The sense is that people are often inwardly burned by grief while they display laughter and joy; and that even if their joy is true and by no means feigned, it nevertheless ends in grief. In the former part the pride of men seems to be marked, which does not acknowledge God's hand and stroke upon itself: seeking to commend themselves to men, they endeavor to exempt themselves, as it were, from the common lot of men; and, so that they may seem as excellent as possible to men and as free as possible from sins, they desire to appear immune from every punishment and curse. Yet we ought not merely to refuse occasions of grief when offered but even to summon the grief which may produce repentance. It is indeed true that there is a time when restraining sorrow is advantageous, as in Leviticus 10 and Nehemiah 8. But, for manifold occasions, the time when we are called to sackcloth and haircloth occurs much, much more frequently. Furthermore, it is also taught that grief is not far removed even from true joys, that sorrow is before the doors, and that joy presses so closely upon sorrow with its heels and, as it were, treads upon gladness's footsteps, that it somehow desires to enter the mind before joy has hurried outside. The sum and chief point of this part of the Proverb, therefore, is that the world's joys are fleeting and perishable: they vanish in a brief moment and are exchanged for the opposite grief. See John 16:20 and 14:27, where the distinction is drawn between the world's joy and the joy of God's children. See Job 21:10, 11, 12, 13 and Psalm 78; see 1 Thessalonians 5:3. This indeed happens not only

to the wicked but to the godly and those who fear God, as in Job 1:18, 19 and Psalm 30:7, 8, not only in unlawful joys but even in lawful and permitted ones. Hence Solomon said of laughter that it was an insane thing which brought no felicity, Ecclesiastes 2:1, 2, 3, and so forth, through almost the whole chapter, where he also speaks about lawful and liberal pleasures.

Therefore that teaching of the Apostle must be held fast, 1 Corinthians 7:29, 30, 31: namely, that we apply moderation in these recreations of the present life and neither devote our minds to pursuing them nor keep our minds fixed upon them; finally, that we are not retarded in the course of the Christian life by the enjoyment of these things or held entangled in them. This is for the following reasons. First, the shortened time ought to admonish us not to spend an excessive amount of time enjoying these things. Second, even if we would prefer to spend a longer time in them, they are nevertheless of such a kind that they swiftly pass away; although we may bear parting from them less equably, they will themselves nevertheless take flight of their own accord and withdraw from us.

Next, since grief so suddenly succeeds joy and the greatest sorrows very often take the place of the greatest joys, we should imitate the example of Job, who did not rest amid supreme and profound peace. Rather, even when events flowed according to his soul's desire, he was not free of fear lest some calamity arising from sin interrupt his present felicity, Job 3:25, 26. The latter part of verse 26 teaches that this fear is the means of driving away panic terror, for it indicates that it happened wholly beyond the accustomed and ordinary course that such a great calamity befell a man earnestly concerned to depart from sin.

Finally, it must be observed that we should pursue a constant and solid joy which no one can take away, since the joys of this life are so fleeting and momentary. John 14:27 and 16:22; 1 Peter 1:8.

Verse 14. A man of backsliding heart shall be satisfied with his own ways, but a good man [withdraws] from fellowship with him.

In ישבע, *shall be satisfied*, there is a metaphor drawn from foods. For just as those who consume foods acquire strength or weakness according as the foods are wholesome or unwholesome—and in proportion as they have eaten them more sparingly or fully—so each person receives from either good or evil works a recompense suited to the works he has done. In סוג לב, *backsliding heart*, there is a metaphor drawn from the body to the mind. For just as backward motion in making a journey is blameworthy, so it is when the mind turns toward something worse and, in a manner, goes backward.

The wicked are so disposed that they commonly persuade themselves that they will be dealt with according to the merit of their own works if they possess at least the appearance of good works, although these are not good works. Or if their works are flagitious and of a kind condemned both by the testimony of their own conscience and by the common judgment of all, they then fashion and invent the notion that they will not be recompensed according to the merit of their works, but that God's mercy will intervene and turn away punishment. Or if they cannot utterly cast off the sense of God's just judgment and vengeance and drive it away from themselves, they nevertheless attempt to bring at least this thought into their mind: that they will by no means be dealt with strictly according to the merit of their works, but that God will moderate the severity of justice toward them. It often occurs to the godly, however, when they contemplate the frequent misery of their present condition, that there is no reward for good works. We are taught by this proverb to diminish the opinions of these men. For God

who among men—and that in the commerce and trading of this life—requires just weights and just balances will much more himself observe this justice in matters which pertain to eternal life. The godly, therefore, cannot advance and make such progress in doing good that God's justice does not reach so far in punishing these and rewarding those. For to admit this thought concerning God, that he is not supremely just in each respect, both in punishing and in doing good, is to set an idol in the place of God.

Next, it must be noted that the wickedness of works is judged not from their outward appearance but from the heart's defection and, as it were, backward motion, so that even if they were most highly approved in the eyes and ears of all men, they are nevertheless hateful and detestable to God unless they proceed from a rightly ordered and trained mind.

The wicked, feeding themselves upon their own crimes as upon food and drink, are nevertheless never filled by acting criminally and wickedly. Hence it also happens that old men advanced in age, when their strength and health no longer permit them to devote themselves to lusts, nevertheless take pleasure in foul and obscene discourses; and those who have been assassins and gladiators, when bodily weakness no longer permits them to lie in wait for slaughter and blood, nevertheless desire to converse about fights and combats. But the time will come—sometimes in this life, always in the life to come—when they will be sated with their own crimes to the point of utter loathing and nausea. Next, since it often happens that God's vengeance proceeds at a slow and suspended step, they imagine in their mind that no recompense for their sins will ever come. But so far is this conclusion from being drawn from God's forbearance that, on the contrary, the final judgment must most certainly be expected because individuals do not receive according to their merit in this life.

Finally, although the godly, while applying themselves to good works, never

[1632 witness | PDF p. 245 | printed columns 464–465]

satisfy themselves in doing good but always hunger and thirst for righteousness, nevertheless after this life they will at last be filled by living justly and piously to such a degree that there will be nothing further for them to require. And although in this life they often do not receive the reward due to works by promise, nevertheless in the future age they will be rewarded with full recompense for all the good works they have performed.

Verse 15. A fool believes every thing, but a shrewd man attends to his step.

In לאשורו, *his step*, there is a metaphor from animate to inanimate things. For just as animate things walk with their feet from one place to another, so affairs which are conducted have their beginnings and then their progress until, as it were, they come to their ends and outcomes as to seats and stations.

Wondrous is the perversity of our nature, by which we gape after false and empty things and are utterly delighted in them, while turning away from true and sincere things and acquiescing in them only with the greatest difficulty. For just as the Apostle institutes from the Prophet a grave complaint concerning the perversity of the mouth, portraying it as overflowing with lies and deceits, so we must judge no differently concerning all those things which are suited and inclined to drink in empty and false things.

A grave and illustrious testimony to this matter appears in the Queen of Sheba, who, although otherwise most wise and experienced in many things, nevertheless did not believe the things she had heard about Solomon's Wisdom. To this belongs the example of Hanun, king of the Ammonites, who, because he gave ear to certain false suggestions from his senate, brought destruction upon both his kingdom and his nation. Although this corruption of credulity is somewhat subdued in the godly and those regenerated by the Spirit of God, nevertheless no obscure traces of that perversity remain. A witness is

the history of the slaughter of Ammon, from which the rumor spread that all the king's sons had been killed together and at the same time. David immediately seized upon this rumor, although it was empty and false. But the clearest example of all is what Scripture relates concerning Jacob, who eagerly and immediately embraced the little rumor delivered to him about Joseph's death, but admitted with the greatest difficulty the report that the same man was alive, although it was certain and confirmed by the testimony of many. From these things it is readily evident how timely Solomon's admonition is: that the simple and incautious person gives credence to every thing. Thus, although men who indulge themselves do not regard lightness in believing as a vice, and even attribute it to a certain goodness of nature, God nevertheless condemns it as sin through his Spirit. How many evils this lightness in believing can be the seed and cause of is left attested in the single example of Saul. For it is indicated that his immortal and, as it were, Vatinian hatred against David, which begot the most severe persecution, flowed from the fact that he gave credence to the uncertain rumors, whisperings, and suspicions of David's ill-wishers. Just as this brought the greatest troubles upon David, so it cast Saul into the chasm and precipice of utter destruction. To this refer that horrible defection of the Jews from Christ because they gave credence to the rumor that Christ's body had been stolen from the tomb and carried away.

This vice must be avoided all the more diligently because it found a place in the best and likewise most prudent men. Let David be the example: he gave ear to Ziba's slander against Mephibosheth and thereby bound himself with a grave crime and enormous injustice toward Mephibosheth.

Before all things, however, let us act cautiously, lest in matters pertaining to God's glory and our salvation we give credence rashly.
[1632 witness | PDF p. 246 | printed columns 466–467]

give credence to those things by which we are either imbued with a false opinion or lose an accepted truth. Here the Papists display astonishing credulity, for they put faith in those prodigious tales collected in the *Golden Legend*, as they call it. Therefore we must hold to what Christ admonishes: that we should take heed to what we hear; that not every spirit is to be heeded, but the spirits are to be tested, whether they are from God; and that in this matter we ought not to be like infants or children, who are carried here and there by every wind of doctrine. This is to be done all the more circumspectly because in these matters there is a remarkable levity and propensity in our nature either to imbibe new errors or to depart from a true opinion, however ancient. See 2 Thessalonians 2 near the beginning; John 21:23; Galatians 1:6, 7, and so forth. To this refer the example of the prophet sent to Jeroboam and deceived by the old prophet, 1 Kings 13.

What it is that he censures in this passage, however, is understood from the latter half-verse; from it judge what he approves. Here first he opposes to that simple and inexperienced person one who is shrewd, knows how to deal with people, and accommodates himself as circumstances require. Yet he does not affirm that this person distrusts everyone and grants assent to no one's speech, which is just as much a fault as prostituting one's trust indiscriminately to everyone. Rather, concerning every matter that admits of doubt, he inquires diligently and weighs all the force of the reasons which can rightly move him either to add credibility to the matter narrated or withdraw it. For he does not receive rumors as certain merchants receive wares, buying them neither counted to a definite number nor weighed. Instead, such men investigate the causes of the things reported and observe the circumstances of the place in which something is said to have been done, the time when it was transacted, and the person through whom it was accomplished, so that from these indications they may rightly determine whether the matter is true, false, or merely probable. If Saul had done this, he would easily have refuted the slanderers. For if he had considered with himself how faithfully and wisely David had fought the Lord's battles; how dear he had been to his son Jonathan and his daughter Michal; if he had allowed it to settle in his mind how dear David had been to God, who had so wonderfully and unexpectedly snatched him so often from the jaws of death; and finally, if he had duly meditated upon how David, when shut up in his hands, had let him go whole and untouched, he would never have allowed himself to be persuaded that David had laid snares for his life. The same is true of David: if he had duly weighed Ziba's character as one seeking his own advantages and bringing a gift; Mephibosheth's incapacity; the unsuitableness of the time for seeking the kingdom, when David's son was himself seeking it; and the testimony of Shimei, who arose from Mephibosheth's family, by which it was foretold that the kingdom would pass into the hands of David's own son Absalom, he would never have admitted that slander, or at least would not have pronounced so unjust a judgment as though the matter were wholly true and certain. In discerning and judging rumors and doctrines concerning God's kingdom, however, recourse must be had to nothing other than the sacred Scriptures, and especially to that maxim by which Christ teaches us to distinguish foreign doctrines from genuine ones: namely, whether the spirit who speaks seeks Christ's glory. For the Spirit of God always glorifies Christ and his merit.

In short, this is how the matter must be settled. Once the things reported have been inspected and thoroughly investigated, assent and belief are to be given to those which possess undoubted truth; belief and assent are to be denied to those which are certainly false; but in those which cannot be thoroughly investigated, belief is to be withheld until their ascertained truth has been grasped and discovered.

Verse 16. The wise man fears and withdraws from evil, but the fool thrusts himself into it, so presumptuous is he.

There is a certain fear which love casts out, 1 John 4. There are also the fearful whom God, after casting them out of the kingdom of heaven, thrusts into the lake burning with fire and brimstone. But there is a lawful and praiseworthy fear, one that is utterly necessary for passing life piously and happily: by it, as by a bridle, people are held back from sin, and by it, as by a spur, they are urged toward every kind of duty. The common people proclaim those to be high-spirited and well-born who are ready and bold to undertake all kinds of affairs and to complete them once undertaken. By contrast, they judge those who hesitate and set dangers before their eyes to be drones, cowards, and devoid of high spirit. The Holy Spirit therefore meets this popular error and establishes fear as a necessary safeguard for avoiding the troubles of the present life. In order that this may be clearer, we must first see what fear the prophet designates here.

Fear in this passage is an affection of the mind by which someone, conscious of his own depravity and of the divine vengeance that deservedly hangs over him, walks cautiously and anxiously with respect to the evil both of guilt and of punishment, yet not without an awareness of divine mercy. Therefore this proverb excludes those who, in order to avoid the evil of punishment, make themselves guilty of the evil of fault. It likewise excludes those who flee sins only because they dread God's judgment. For true wisdom chiefly engenders fear of sin. That the godly person is marked by this sign and token, that he shrinks from an oath, Ecclesiastes 9—of which there is an excellent example in Abraham's servant Eleazar and in the scouts—extends more widely and spreads to every kind of sin. For the one who shrinks only from punishment and does not bear a mind alienated from guilt represents a person who, wishing to remove a tree whose poison harms his beasts, plucks its leaves and fruit, or breaks off its branches and shoots, from which it afterwards sprouts again more abundantly and in greater profusion, when the axe ought instead to have been laid to the tree's trunk, or rather its root.

Moreover, even if a person is conscious of no singular or unusual sin, the guilt common to all mortals supplies us sufficient reason why we should fear evils for ourselves—not only those which flash before us and offer a likelihood of their approach, but even hidden evils which display no appearance of themselves. And even if the fear of divine correction did not keep us anxious, nevertheless, since God sometimes sends a cross upon his own people to make trial of their faith and to present an example before others, it is not fitting for us to be secure against evils. The Holy Spirit relates in Job 1 that Job possessed this fear, because he worshiped God with sacrifices and prayers on behalf of his sons and daughters, lest perhaps, while they were holding a feast, something should escape them through imprudence and thoughtlessness that would kindle God's wrath against them. In chapter 3 he declares this expressly. The fact that this fear profited him little in averting the evil that followed was brought about by an extraordinary reason, as was said above. Yet it profited the whole Church, which obtained from his sufferings an extraordinary example of patience; and it profited Job, who was ennobled before all posterity by his incomparable endurance. An example of this matter is set forth in Jacob, Genesis 32. When he received a report that his brother Esau was approaching with four hundred men, although the message had said not a word of hostility, he nevertheless feared him. He therefore fortified himself against Esau's attack, if there should be one, with prayers to God, with a gift prepared and furnished for his brother, and with

[1632 witness | PDF p. 248 | printed columns 470–471]

an orderly and suitable disposition of his companies. Here it is also indicated by what arts and instruments people may avoid evil.

And the weightier and more important each matter we handle is, the fuller of fear we ought to be, because many things commonly befall matters of this kind which either overturn their hoped-for success or certainly impede it. Hence the Apostle numbers his fears among the crosses that pressed upon him, 2 Corinthians 6:5 and 11:28. See an example of this in 1 Thessalonians 5:5 and Acts 15:36.

Yet care and anxiety about lesser matters are not to be cast aside by a wise or godly man—for example, about covering springs and wells, banking a fire with ashes, attaching battlements or barriers to houses, and confining a goring ox at home.

*But the fool thrusts himself in and trusts.]* Such a person generally regards it as a kind of foolish timidity to set dangers and hazards before his own eyes, and especially to tremble at God's wrath because of offenses and lesser sins. Hence words such as these commonly fall from his lips: that we would tremble at the stirring of a single straw if we wished to test every detail by the standard of the divine word and undertake nothing beyond the word's prescription. As for the fact that confidence is censured here, though it is highly commended elsewhere, it must be understood that the confidence designated here is that by which someone rests in himself and his own strength, or trusts in God without his word and promise, and therefore often contrary to God's word. See an example of this in Goliath, who, trusting in himself and his weapons, hurled himself into destruction. See likewise the history of Asahel, 2 Samuel 2:20–23. See the history of Balaam, who was deterred by many tokens from his journey to the king of Moab, yet hardened his mind and wished to persist in his undertaken plan. See also the history of King Ahaziah and his captains of fifty, 2 Kings 1.

In all these the opposite occurs from that which befalls the wise. For while the wise avoid evil by their fear, it happens to these people that they rush heedlessly into evils and destruction. The cause of them all seems to be assigned by the Psalmist: that the wicked man, through the pride of his mind, is unwilling to inquire of God. To this may be referred very many in our own age who, presuming upon divine mercy, dare to perpetrate almost any evil.

Verse 17. Headlong anger produces folly, but it is hateful to the most circumspect man.

In אפים (*nostrils*) there is a metonymy of the subject, because anger is detected in the nostrils, whether from their pallor or from their snorting. In אולת (*folly*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct, by which signal wickedness is denoted. The same trope occurs in מזמות (*crimes*) for a calculating man.

In this proverb virtue is not opposed to vice, as it generally is, but sin to sin, as sometimes happens, and indeed the graver sin to the lighter.

The affections and disturbances of the mind have been usefully given to people by God, and they provide helps and supports for accomplishing excellent things, provided that they are kept within a mean by the fear of God as by a bridle, and are not allowed to pass beyond measure in accordance with the viciousness of our nature. Therefore anger itself is not censured, but rather if someone is prone to anger or suffers himself to be kindled on the slightest occasion, so that like tinder he easily catches fire and grows hot like an earthen pot—rather than a bronze cauldron—when a little fire is placed beneath it. This is what Christ condemns in Matthew 5. And these are generally the people of whom it is said in the proverb that they repay a blow for a word. See an outstanding example of this in Lamech, Genesis 4.

This anger both is itself a sin and gives birth to other, very great sins. This is confirmed by the testimony of the apostle James: for anger

[1632 witness | PDF p. 249 | printed columns 472–473]

of man does not work God's righteousness. See Genesis 49 concerning the anger of Levi and Simeon. How great the evils that David's anger against Nabal would have caused, had it not been calmed by Abigail's prudent and suitable speech, can easily be understood from that very speech. From this it can readily be understood what kind of monster unbridled anger is, unless it is bound as with chains by the fear of God and restrained with great caution, since it yields such dreadful fruits even in the godly and regenerate. Therefore let no one, trusting in his godliness and in the Spirit of God with whom he is imbued, loosen the reins for this insane beast, since those who had drunk so abundantly from the water of life have allowed this fire to break out through carelessness and, by its conflagration, to ravage their own dwellings and those of their neighbors, as it were.

The atrocity of this sin is also evident from the fact that it is utterly and completely opposed to the attribute which is in God, who is called ארך אפים (*long of nostrils*); we ought to conform ourselves to his pattern, just as we are invited to imitate his holiness and mercy.

In the latter half-verse Solomon censures the person who, after receiving either a real or an imagined injury, so conceals it and passes it over in silence that meanwhile he stores it deep in his mind, waiting for an opportunity to avenge himself thoroughly—when, where, and by what means.

This sin is more atrocious than the former because it is more difficult to cure, since it is pressed down and cooked within, whereas the former person is pacified more quickly after pouring out his bile. Second, boiling anger takes away a person's sense and judgment, so that, scarcely master of his mind, he rushes upon the one by whom he feels himself injured. Third, it is easier to guard against the person who displays his anger in face and voice, and to prepare instruments for repelling his violence, whereas against this man we are fortified by no weapons; and the former generally harms himself more than others, because he often attacks an enemy while unprepared. Finally, this man brings about a more certain and unquestionable killing, since he almost never attacks his enemy except with the certain hope of destroying him. A clear example of this is found in Cain, Genesis 4, and in Absalom, who concealed for some years the signal injury he had received through the violation of his sister. Hence Solomon says that this latter man is to be hated, as one who takes vengeance with deliberate malice, while the former, who inflicts evil when kindled by sudden anger, is rather worthy of compassion.

Verse 18. Fools shall possess folly, but the shrewd weave a crown of knowledge.

In נחלו (*shall possess*) there is a metaphor: as an inheritance belongs to an heir, so folly belongs to a fool. Likewise in יכתרו (*shall be crowned*) there is a metaphor: as a crown is an ornament to the head, so knowledge is an ornament to the prudent person. In אולת (*folly*) and דעת (*knowledge*) there are metaphors of the subject for the adjunct fruit.

פתאים (*the simple*) are, as is established from chapter 9, all those who retain ignorance drawn and derived from Adam's loins and have not been regenerated and reborn by the Spirit of God. From this it is clearly established what a miserable and unhappy condition we have obtained. We are all not merely Adam's children but also his heirs. But of what a mournful and calamitous inheritance! It is an inheritance more ruinous than extreme poverty. For that which the Apostle recalls from Job in 1 Timothy 7:6—that we are brought naked into this world and will depart naked and migrate from the world—is the smallest part of our unhappiness. For by this inheritance of folly we are first disinherited from righteousness and the heavenly kingdom, and therefore from every right and lawful possession of the earth, the sea, and all the other creatures. Then by this inheritance of folly we become children and heirs of divine wrath and, by the same act, heirs of hell. How much more wretched is our condition, since we pursue that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 250 | printed columns 474–475]

folly as an inheritance and cling to it.

Furthermore, this inheritance of folly is exceedingly fruitful and fertile. For it bears not merely yearly or monthly fruits, as certain trees do, but daily, hourly, and even moment-by-moment fruits—and deadly ones at that. For although its abundant harvest or vintage is deferred and reserved until the day of death, or rather of the Last Judgment, nevertheless, while we are in this life, it continually produces hardness of heart and greater alienation from God, and from time to time sprinkles some part of our life with diverse and various scourges. To be sure, not all its fruits are sharp and bitter to our corrupt taste, nor are all its bites felt at once, nor do its stings prick immediately.

For just as in bodily diseases the seed and foundation of a disease has been laid long before it causes pain, so it also happens in the corruptions of the soul that we are not immediately assailed by pain when something contrary to God's glory and our salvation is committed. Furthermore, the fact that this inheritance is of the greatest duration, and that we cannot cast it away when we wish, adds to its bitterness and misery. We can readily eat up and squander an estate received from our parents, or transfer it to another. And even if we preserve and retain it during life, it is nevertheless lost at death. This unhappy inheritance, however, accompanies us in death itself and is not barred from the grave.

Let us therefore learn how biting and bitter sin, or folly, is. If we do not perceive its bitterness in the root, let us at least grasp its bitterness and sharpness in its fruit; if we do not recognize it in its cause, which belongs to the wise, let us nevertheless know it in its effect, which even fools attain. For just as those skilled in herbs and plants know the unwholesomeness and bitterness of roots sunk deep in the bowels of the earth, which the unskilled understand only from the bitterness of the fruits, so also those instructed for God's kingdom, Matthew 13:52, and those who through habit have faculties exercised for discerning good and evil, Hebrews 5:14, possess within themselves a thorough knowledge of the root of bitterness in the human heart. Since, then, we have perceived its harmful and ruinous nature, let us take care, trusting in divine power, to shake it off. It is the parent and begetter of so many evils and is indeed chastised severely and harshly even in God's own children.

In the other half-verse it is taught that true and uncorrupted knowledge of God weaves a crown and places it upon the head of the one imbued with that knowledge. That is, it renders him no less acceptable and glorious before God, angels, and godly people than a person's head, when wreathed with a crown, renders him pleasing and worthy of notice to others. Thus it first drives away the ignominy which is thoroughly inborn and implanted in us, and then adds honor and dignity. To this are to be referred the things said earlier at the beginning of Proverbs, where wisdom is said to be like a necklace, bracelets, and rings for adorning those endowed with it. To this also pertains what is said in Ecclesiastes 8, that wisdom makes a person's face shine and causes it to be radiant. The metaphor of the crown, moreover, implies that all the godly are kings: in this life they now wear the crown of knowledge and the apprehension of truth, and they are destined to wear the crown of glory and highest dignity. Hence they are called the glorious in Psalm 16. From this it is clear that the fruit—the good portion—which a fool receives from his folly is ignominy and contempt. For all the wicked are plainly the Devil's bond-slaves and the servants of sin.

The fruits of this crown, however, are tasted in this life and received in the future—not for years and months, and so forth, but unto ages
[1632 witness | PDF p. 251 | printed columns 476–477]

of ages. But neither is it a flowery crown, which withers, but one of the purest gold, which is of perpetual and eternal duration and does not forsake us in death. Just as the root of folly is pleasant and agreeable to people of corrupted taste, while its fruit is most bitter, so the root of knowledge is indeed bitter, but its fruit most sweet. Therefore, from the sweetness of its fruits let us learn to love knowledge and delight ourselves in it as in a crown.

Verse 19. The evil bow themselves before the good, and the wicked at the gates of the righteous.

In שחו (*they bow themselves*) there is a synecdoche of the species, bowing, for every kind of reverence and honor; there is also a synecdoche in שערי for every abject and humble submission or surrender.

In Psalm 10 the Prophet teaches that the wicked bow and prostrate themselves before the godly with this design alone: to seize the godly when they are unwary. They are just like a lion which, crouching, hides itself in a thicket or bush until the prey passes by, whereupon it seizes and tears it apart with its claws. But the Prophet is not speaking in this passage about that kind of bowing, but about the submission by which the godly are treated with honor and dignity.

Here we must first inquire into the causes by which the wicked, who are proud by nature and at the same time the most loathsome haters of good people, are driven to humble themselves before the godly. The causes are of two kinds: God's excellent gifts in the godly, and God himself, who also procures favor for them apart from the excellence of gifts.

There is some diversity among gifts.

For they are { internal } and the same are { simple,
             { external }                  { composite.

Among internal gifts, and those by no means composite or double, the chief and first is piety and innocence of life. It sends out rays of light that strike the eyes of the wicked in such a way that they are compelled by the name of piety to esteem and revere the godly. Hence comes that saying in Ecclesiastes chapter 8, verse 1, that wisdom makes a man's face shine. To this pertains what was brought forward earlier from Proverbs 3:16, that riches and honor stand at wisdom's left hand. In this manner Herod was compelled to revere John the Baptist, though otherwise abject and despicable both in clothing and manner of food. In this manner Balaam honored the Israelites when he wished for a death like theirs. And Saul, though a king, honored David his subject when he confessed that David was better than himself and asked him to be gracious to his sons and grandsons.

Here, therefore, let us learn to devote ourselves to piety and to correct our error, since we place no power in piety for acquiring favor and honor. Indeed, what is more detestable, we regard piety as opposed to dignity and honor, to such a degree that we suppose the man who embraces virtue to bid honor a long farewell. It is enough, and more than enough, to confirm us in the pursuit of piety that God treats the godly with honor, and that angels and elect men pursue them with honor, even if all the wicked were to spit in our faces and trample us like the mud of the streets. But now, since not only do these honor us of their own accord and load us with praises, but even the wicked, who are separated from the godly by so great a distance, often do this, woe to our unbelief! For we think so wrongly about piety and integrity of life that we persuade our minds that they wage perpetual and irreconcilable enmities against honor and dignity.

But if the wicked are often captivated by love of piety, how shall we persuade ourselves and our souls that we are

[1632 witness | PDF p. 252 | printed columns 478–479]

to be reckoned among the truly godly, when we hate the godly bitterly because, namely, they are rather offensive to us and have injured us with some slight wrong? How much less can we do so if no other cause makes them hateful and unwelcome to us than that they cultivate piety and devote themselves to righteousness?

Thus far the discussion has concerned the honor which flows from pure, bare, or simple piety. There follows the honor which issues from piety heaped up and joined with other gifts of God as well.

That piety is composite which has other excellent gifts of mind attached to it, as when God bestows the gift of languages, of healing, or the ability to perform other miracles; or when he attaches to piety the honor of civil office or ecclesiastical ministry. Joseph belonged to the former kind: his older brothers were compelled to supplicate him and bend the knee before him. Though they were not altogether wicked, they had nevertheless acted wickedly toward their brother Joseph, Genesis 50 and elsewhere. To this pertains the honor shown to Moses by all the Egyptians, whom he had nevertheless afflicted with many signs and portents. So too the honor shown to Daniel and his companions above the other native subjects of the kingdom and the Chaldean nobles. By this we are taught that God often bestows upon his own people and those who fear him gifts which the wicked desire so greatly that they are compelled to court and seek their favor and goodwill. At the same time, let it also confirm us concerning the honors to be obtained among our countrymen and the people of God, if it is expedient for us, since the godly have been honored among barbarians and the impious. Indeed, it sometimes happens that the wicked not only bow themselves before the good, but, in deepest submission and as though keeping watch at their gate, seek their aid and assistance. Of this kind is the example of Naaman when he was ungodly: with a great clatter of horses and pomp of chariots he approached the gate of Elisha, who in outward appearance was abject and poor and had been driven by poverty to barley bread. See 2 Kings 5. Likewise, the impious kings of Edom and Israel, 2 Kings 3:12, and Joash, 2 Kings 13.

Let this relieve our grief and strengthen our patience if at any time we are compelled to stand at the gate of the wicked: the wicked also rather often prostrate themselves before the godly, and a reversal will one day come.

Finally, piety and the gifts of God arouse wonderful loves of themselves, so that the wicked roll themselves in adoration at the feet of the godly and worship them like gods. See the example of Nebuchadnezzar, Daniel 4, and Acts 14. For although they do this wickedly and against the will of the godly, nothing confirms the truth of this proverb more. This blessing begins in this life, and that not among all the wicked but only some, whose minds God bends to favor piety and integrity. But at the last judgment and on that blessed morning it will be done by all the wicked, over whom the righteous will rule, Psalm 49, and whom they will judge—even the reprobate angels, 1 Corinthians 6.

The other member among the causes is God. Even if there is no piety, or the gifts are slight, he nevertheless bends the minds of the wicked so that they honor his people, as the Egyptians honored the Israelites, although hostile to their idolatry. But the godly despise the wicked, Psalm 15:4. For although they honor them because they have been placed in a position of eminence, nevertheless they never praise or honor them on account of their wickedness.

Verse 20. Even to his neighbor the poor man is hateful, but the rich man's friends are many.

In this passage רעהו is taken for *friend*, not for *neighbor*, as is evident from the following אהבי (*those who love*).

For anyone to hate his own flesh, or

[1632 witness | PDF p. 253 | printed columns 480–481]

any man who is anywhere, is contrary to duty, Isaiah 58:7. But what is more alien to humanity than that a man should pursue with hatred a friend joined to himself by love? Yet it approaches still nearer to savagery if, on the very ground by which it is more fitting to exercise the duties of charity, he proves himself less benevolent and loving. What is still more fierce and barbarous is that men are more unjust toward the one who has been wounded and pierced by an arrow sent from God's hand. For if someone is wounded by a mortal, people of a good disposition generally treat him with compassion. How much more ought this to be done when someone has been struck by God's rod? From this it is clear that people often deceive themselves: those who love suppose themselves to be lovers of men, and those who are loved decide that they themselves are dear to others. That it is otherwise is proved most clearly when a change of fortune declares that riches, not people, are the objects of love. Consequently the hidden hypocrisy of the lovers and the foolish error of the beloved are convicted. The former persuade themselves that they love people, or godly people; the latter settle it within themselves that they are loved and held in delight because they are human, or because they are godly, learned, and so forth.

This hypocrisy is all the more detestable because people so love themselves and are φίλαυτοι (*lovers of self*) that they prefer themselves and their own persons to riches and the other gifts of God, choosing rather to suffer the loss of everything than the loss of their own life. But in their love toward others, they so prefer riches and the other gifts which can be of advantage to those people over the people themselves that, when those things are removed and taken away, love does not merely vanish but is changed into hatred.

Therefore we who love are admonished to search our souls and inmost parts, to learn whether our love is founded rightly and sincerely: whether we embrace a man because of God or because of ourselves; whether we pursue him or his possessions rather with charity; whether we love the image of God faintly portrayed in him, or rather the world's deceptive appearance and pomp; and finally, whether we hold dear the soul, by far the better part though invisible, or the body and the things exposed to the senses. But we who are loved are taught not to trust excessively in people who have not yet been examined and thoroughly known, nor to rest content in human benevolence toward us, but even amid the greatest throng of friends to repose in the protection and defense of God alone. See the example of Job, who was deserted by his own servants and wife. See the overthrown commonwealth of the Israelites, Lamentations of Jeremiah 1. This was done above all to Christ, Matthew 26.

The poor are here suitably admonished to approach God and cling to him all the more firmly insofar as they have been deserted by men.

The first half-verse is confirmed by the latter, just as the latter is inferred from the first. Here is confirmed not only what Christ teaches in the proverb, *to him who has, it will be given*, namely, with this purpose, that he may receive more; at the same time the depravity of the human disposition is also exposed, because it values riches, which Christ values least in Luke 16, more highly than the very person who is laden with riches, or any of his gifts from God, however excellent, with which he is adorned. For if we are willing to judge truly, we shall discover that more friends are procured by riches than by any gift of God, even the choicest and by far the most precious.

Verse 21. He who despises his neighbor will be a sinner; but he who shows favor to the poor—oh, blessed is he!

In חוטא there is a synecdoche of the genus for *sins grievously*. For otherwise the one who departs even in the smallest degree from the love and charity commanded in the law sins.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 254 | printed columns 482–483]

In the preceding proverb hatred of another is condemned; but in this one even contempt is condemned, although it does not arrive at hatred as though fully formed and complete. Next, in the preceding proverb the person condemned is one who proves undutiful to a poor friend; in this passage it is one who is undutiful to his neighbor, although the man is not his friend. Finally, in the preceding proverb the person condemned is one who is unjust and unmerciful toward the poor; but in this proverb it is one who is undutiful to the poor, sick, ignoble, or unlearned man, provided—as is true of anyone—the name *neighbor* falls to him. For whatever miseries and calamities surround him, he does not cease to be your neighbor, that is, one joined to you by close bonds. First, he bears the same remnants of the divine image which you bear, and on that ground alone he must be embraced with love, even if everything else were absent. If someone met you whose face and bodily stature greatly resembled your own, you would certainly incline toward him above others with a certain benevolent goodwill. How much more, then, ought you to embrace one who resembles you in his soul, your nobler part, especially since in that respect in which he is like you he reflects the image of God our Father, and then by his deformity and the ugly likeness of Adam represents you to the life. Although this fact may not procure love, it certainly ought to restrain and drive away contempt and bend you toward compassion. But if, engrafted into Christ by faith, he has also put on Christ's image, you ought to love him more inwardly and ardently as one joined to you by a new and most powerful bond of kinship—unless you have chosen to hold Christ in contempt in his likeness just as you hold God in contempt in his image. But if, while you have been conformed to Christ's image, he is alien to it, who, I ask, has nevertheless distinguished you? Did you do this, or God? And why did he distinguish you and confer this difference? Was it by your merit and the worthiness of your works, or by mere and free grace? By grace certainly, and by his own entirely free will he did this, 1 Corinthians 4.

The sum comes to this: no one can sink so far into miseries and sins that he does not bear the impressed traces of that divine glory which deservedly vindicate him from contempt among men. See Isaiah 58:7.

He חוטא (*sins*) because he considers neither his neighbor's nobility and excellence, nor his own advantage and deformity; because he has forgotten the common misery to which he himself is liable, and into which he could easily be plunged and driven in his neighbor's case, as though in a single moment; just as his neighbor can also suddenly and abruptly be raised to his fortunate condition, if he enjoys any such condition. Nor is he merely proud and insolent toward his neighbor, but he also proves insulting toward God. For he who despises his neighbor because he is miserable and afflicted holds in contempt God, who has inflicted the misery, Proverbs 17:5.

In the latter half-verse he establishes the contrary of that departure from duty which he indicated earlier, and at the same time clearly illuminates the sense of the first part. First, therefore, he opposes the showing of favor to contempt of one's neighbor. From this it becomes still clearer who is a despiser of his neighbor. For the only man to be regarded as a despiser of his neighbor is not the one who tramples him underfoot, treads him down with his heels, spits in his face, or afflicts him with some more notable insult, but also the one who does not show him favor and embrace him with goodwill. Thus the Levite, and afterward the priest, who passed by and continued their journey past the half-dead man, despised him, although they brought no evil upon him, not even by word or expression. The one Samaritan who supplied aid was free from contempt.

When he adds *to the afflicted*, he openly indicates what kind of neighbor he understood in the first part of this proverb: namely, one overwhelmed by miseries and calamities. At the same time we are taught what it is that engenders contempt and disdain in our minds toward brothers and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 255 | printed columns 484–485]

neighbors: not sins or crimes, which truly are evils simply and purely, but hardships, which in their own nature are not evil and hateful, as the preceding proverb affirmed; not sins by which they bind themselves, but calamities which God's hand lays upon them; not evils which issue from hell and the devil, but those which are sent from heaven and of which God is the author.

Of evils of this kind it is truly said: [there is no evil in the city which the Lord has not done.] Here, therefore, let us learn to correct the perversity of our judgment. We hate the fruits bitterly, but do not hate the tree; its shoots and branches are hateful to us, while the root from which all bitterness arises is sufficiently friendly and pleasing to us.

When he says that the one who shows favor to the afflicted is blessed, he implies that those who hold their neighbors in contempt are unhappy. For however much they judge themselves blessed, and on account of their own happiness despise others burdened with miseries as though they were unhappy, they themselves are in reality the most wretched and unhappy little men.

But here it may be asked how he attributes happiness to works, and indeed to almsgiving alone. Answer: happiness is not attributed to works as causes of happiness, but as fruits of the blessed man. He attributes this to a single good work because the theological virtues are connected and bound together. But it is asked further how one who shows favor to the afflicted is truly called blessed, since the Pontificians, Turks, and other unbelievers do this, though they are nevertheless accursed by God. Answer: they give and expend something for the relief of the afflicted, but do not do it in obedience to God, but rather either to hunt after reputation or from fear of hell. Nor do they do this out of charity toward their neighbor, but because they are lovers of themselves, while persuading themselves that by these things they will merit salvation.

Verse 22. Do not those who devise evil go astray? But kindness and faithfulness belong to those who devise good.

In חרשי (*plowmen*) there is a metaphor transferred from plowmen and bodily things to the mind, which seems to be continued in the verb יתעו (*they go astray*). For just as those who plow a field often draw crooked and wandering ridges, beds, and furrows, so those who fashion and devise evil are frustrated in their hope.

There is scarcely anything which wicked people do not expect to be able to attain by their arts and devices. Thus those who attempted to build the tower of Babylon had no doubt about the outcome, Genesis 11; and in Psalm 12 they promise themselves that they will accomplish everything with their tongues; and Proverbs 1:11, 12, 13. Thus in Isaiah 7 the wicked held it certain among themselves that Jerusalem would be conquered, and even designated by name the king whom they were going to set over the Jewish kingdom while it was still intact. And in Psalm 2 they planned to dispossess Christ himself of his kingdom.

But the Prophet declares that they will be disappointed in their hope, and against their vain confidence, which they conceive about bringing their own goals to completion, he sets God's most certain judgment, which is free from all hesitation. For this reason he expresses the sentence by an interrogation. He also seems to confirm it with a comparison. For just as a man who in plowing draws crooked beds, ridges, and furrows does not receive the crop for which he hoped, but is often deprived of the harvest, so those who devise evils lack the outcome which they expect. Isaiah 8: [Take counsel, decree a sentence; it shall not stand, but the counsel of Immanuel.] Bring here also Proverbs 1:28: *The expectation of the wicked shall perish.* For although it sometimes happens that the wicked obtain their wishes and desires, nevertheless the things which
[1632 witness | PDF p. 256 | printed columns 486–487]

the end, but do not attain it. For what they attain is neither advantageous nor useful to them, but issues in their greater and more grievous destruction; nor do they inflict loss upon the godly (as they desire), much less extinguish them, since the calamities of the godly redound to their greater holiness in this life and to glory in the future, and from the ashes of the godly a more numerous offspring of the Church is sown. Furthermore, whatever the wicked attain according to the lust of their own mind, nevertheless all their joy or expectation is terminated by death. Proverbs 11:7.

In the other half-verse it is taught that those who give diligent attention to good things and to things commanded by God are never without a pleasant and desirable fruit. *Say to the righteous that it shall be well with him*, Isaiah 3; Hebrews 6:10; Acts 10:4; Matthew 10:42.

But when he says that kindness or beneficence awaits them, he thereby indicates that recompense is not owed by the merit of the work, but comes from the gratuitous will and good pleasure of God. And when he joins truth to kindness, he declares the certainty of the matter, which cannot deceive; and he expressly opposes it to what was said about the craftsmen of wickedness, whom he previously affirmed most certainly to err and to fall short of their hope. It happens not rarely that many hindrances are placed before the godly endeavors of righteous men; it also often happens that bodily sorrows, together with the cross of the soul, overtake the undertakings of the godly. Finally, even if nothing adverse should intervene, nevertheless the good which they pursue and to which they apply themselves vigorously is deferred for a long time and delayed by the sure counsel of God; nevertheless the endings and denouements are comic and pleasant. See how many years Abraham awaited the blessed seed; how many Isaac awaited his Jacob; how many Zechariah awaited John; how many years David awaited the kingdom, and so forth. But at last God will surely come, Habakkuk 3, and he bears his reward with him in his hands. Isaiah. Those who serve for wages have an appointed day on which they receive the due and just reward for their labors—namely, as soon as they have completed the assigned work about which agreement was made between them. But those who bestow a gratuitous reward do not wish it to be confined within the narrow bounds of days or years, but to be left most freely to their own discretion and will. Therefore God, who bestows all things kindly and confers his gifts and rewards upon us who do not merit them, does not wish to be enclosed within fixed periods and limits of time. Servants also receive a salary by the year or half-year; day laborers receive their wages day by day.

Therefore, if you have fulfilled the law exactly and completely, you indeed can boldly and precisely demand the due reward at once; sons, however, cannot do the same, since they have no prescribed time for receiving their father's liberality and for the most part do not experience their father's beneficence before their parents' death. Yet it does not therefore follow that the lot of sons is worse than that of servants or day laborers. And for this reason a modest petition befits sons, so that they frame their requests in such a way as to entrust the whole matter to their father's will. Whatever happens, this must certainly be established: [God is good and kind to Israel, and to those who are of an upright heart.] And just as all hopes of the wicked perish at least in death, so, on the contrary, the works of the godly follow them at least in death. For God is accustomed to reserve many works of the godly to be recompensed on the day of death.

Verse 23. In every labor [there is] gain; but in the word of the lips there is only want.

The word מותר sometimes signifies a remainder, and sometimes abundance or excellence. That it is taken here for abundance is demonstrated by the opposite word מחסור (*want*).

[1632 witness | PDF p. 257 | printed columns 488–489]

The sense is that by diligent labor those things which pertain to the maintenance of this life are abundantly procured, but by vain, futile, and ineffectual talk about seeking a livelihood nothing but want is attained.

And first, as regards the former part of this Proverb, its truth appears to be derived from the promise made to Adam. For although those words—(*in the sweat of your face you shall eat your bread*)—contain a threat bound up in them, nevertheless they also have an implicit promise: namely, that it would sometime be the case that, if people labor actively and vigorously, all things necessary for sustaining life would be supplied in abundance. There too, just as burdensome labor is commanded in this passage, hard labor full of weariness is commanded by the Lord, whether that labor is undergone by bodily strength, by the faculties of the mind, or by both joined together. To this is added the Apostle's testimony, 2 Thessalonians 3:10.

Hence we learn that all the gain which is sought from playing cards, gambling, the stage or acting, and that kind of illiberal game goes to the wind, and that those who seek profit from it attain no solid fruit. This applies not only to these, but also to those who devote all their effort and zeal to honorable and liberal games (such as archery, games with balls, and so forth) and seek their livelihood from them or support their life by them. I will not involve myself in the question whether it is lawful to gamble for money and to contend in play for a fixed prize. But this is beyond controversy: it is lawful for no one to support himself or his own by any kind of play, however liberal it may be. Of this Solomon is the weightiest witness, Proverbs 13:11, where he declares in this manner: *riches from vanity (that is, riches so acquired) shall be diminished; but he who gathers by hand shall increase* (that is, his riches).

What is written in Psalm 127 appears to undermine the truth of this proposition in appearance: rising early, going late to bed, and feeding oneself scantily are said to be vain labor unless the Lord should favor the endeavors and labors of men. Then, 1:6. A hired laborer who leases out his work and receives the price of his labor is said to cast it into the same pierced and perforated purse. Yet by this Proverb the Holy Spirit appears to place all things within the laborer's power, so that no one who is assiduous in doing his work should be deprived of sustenance. But we must remember that these propositions and promises which relate to the present life are true ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ (*for the most part*), and always have this exception implicit in them: *if it has seemed good to the all-wise God*. For the truth of this proposition shines so clearly that God's blessing follows almost all who labor diligently, even the wicked and those alienated from God. But if—as does happen—God withholds his blessing, then although things do not flow according to their wish or abound in plenty, men moved with compassion are nevertheless generally accustomed to assist those whom they have found laborious and diligent in their work.

In the latter half-verse the Holy Spirit appears to lash the foolish vanity of many men, who customarily spin out many discourses about labors and occupations, yet meanwhile do not put their hand to the work. For you may often find those who speak many words about their plans and endeavors which they have determined among themselves to undertake, although they do not move so much as a foot toward the appointed task. Not unlike this proposition of Solomon is what is said in Isaiah 36:5, where Rabshakeh says that war is carried through not by words or by discourse of the lips, but by strength and power. This seems capable of illustration from the example of Jacob's sons, who are reproved because they argued back and forth at great length in words about setting out for Egypt, and consumed as much time in their discussion as could have sufficed to complete the journey, Genesis 42:1 and 43:10.

Furthermore, from the comparison of the two oppositions in this Proverb we are taught that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 258 | printed columns 490–491]

talkative and verbose men always delay in doing the work and are slothful. For Solomon opposes strenuous labor and laborious industry to verbosity and many words. This is proved not only by common experience but by the clearest testimony of Paul, 1 Timothy 5:13.

Yet this must not be understood as though it were not lawful, or even truly expedient, first to confer in speech about those things which we are about to undertake in deed and work. Here instead what is prudently proposed by the Levite in Judges 19:30 ought to be set forth for imitation.

This Proverb can suitably be transferred to matters of the life to come. For it is not he who professes faith in words, but he who expresses faith in deeds, who will be saved and will be filled with the delights of God's kingdom, Matthew 7:20: *not everyone who says, Lord, Lord*, and so forth.

Verse 24. The crown of the wise [is] their riches; the folly of the dull remains folly.

In עטרת (*crown*) there is a metaphor transferred from corporeal to incorporeal things. For just as a crown is the glory and ornament of the head, so the riches of the wise commend their wisdom and make it more conspicuous. In the second half-verse אולת (*folly*) is a synecdoche of the genus—namely, folly—for notable and conspicuous folly.

The sum of this Proverb is that riches are according as a person uses them. If they fall to a godly and wise man, they are suitable and serviceable to him for all things; the contrary happens if they come to a fool and an ungodly man, for the accession of riches makes a fool become some rabid and raging creature. This must not be understood as though riches, which are God's gift, lost their good nature through their abuse or were changed into an evil, but rather that their abuse is hostile and harmful to those who abuse them.

Therefore, although riches make a man neither good nor evil, neither wise nor unwise, nevertheless they have this power: they disclose goodness or malice, wisdom or folly, and bring into the sun and open air what was lying hidden in the shade. For just as a sword placed in a man's hand makes him neither sober nor raging, but its use reveals which he is, so riches, since they are suitable instruments by which the inward affection of the mind may exercise itself in outward deeds, make plain through their application to outward work what the inward affection of the mind is. And just as it is said of a magistracy that the man is revealed by it, so the same can be declared of riches. Hence that saying in Ecclesiastes 7:11: *Wisdom is good with a possession*, and so forth. It is indeed good without a possession, but he means that it shines more when it has a possession joined to it. What has been said will become clear by adducing examples. First, riches reveal beneficence and kindness of mind enclosed within when the rich receive strangers and guests liberally. By this distinction Abraham and Lot, as though crowned with a wreath, are celebrated and renowned in Scripture. They do so especially when they employ their liberality for God's worship, like those who brought most precious gifts for the construction of the tabernacle in Exodus 35 and, later, of the temple, 1 Chronicles 29. But of all examples that can be sought anywhere, by far the most magnificent is found in Solomon. For how could that incomparable wisdom ever have shone forth even in the smallest part of itself—whether in the investigation of natural things, in the building of the royal palace or the temple, in the suitable and seemly ordering of servants, or finally in the household management of provisions and the governance of the whole kingdom—unless God had endowed him with

[1632 witness | PDF p. 259 | printed columns 492–493]

an abundance of riches? How his riches added a greater ornament to his head than that diadem which his mother Bathsheba had caused to be skillfully prepared and fashioned for the day of his wedding.

Just as the wisdom of wise men shines in the possession of riches, so too in their loss the virtue of those men emits wonderful rays. This is excellently confirmed by Solomon's testimony in Ecclesiastes 7:12. For Abraham's gentle and mild spirit (when he yielded to Lot) would never have been so ennobled if he had not been laden with riches and resources of every kind. Otherwise he might have appeared to have withdrawn from contending with Lot for his own right because of a certain inequality. But this is seen especially in Job, whose equanimity and patience became most celebrated and renowned through the loss and forfeiture of abundant resources; yet this would never have happened unless the possession of the greatest resources had preceded it. Chapter 1.

If this does not shine out in itself, it can nevertheless be discerned from the contrary perversity of fools in using riches. For besides the fact that the proverb vaunted elsewhere has a place here—that an ape is an ape even if it wears golden insignia—the fact that their foolishness is increased, or at least more clearly manifested, appears first from this: riches and resources are like a fortress for fools, in which they trust and on which they lean, as Solomon taught above. Then they are proud through confidence in them and grow insolent against God, as Scripture commits to memory concerning Nebuchadnezzar and Alexander, Daniel 4:7, 10. Furthermore, through presumption founded on riches they prove insulting even toward the most eminent men, as Nabal was toward David, 1 Samuel 25. Finally, riches are attendants upon every kind of crime and serve to fill up and accomplish them all. Thus, if someone is lustful and addicted to shameful conduct, if some are inclined in mind toward gluttony and drunkenness, if they pursue luxury in clothing or buildings, they rush by the help of riches down the precipices of crimes and plunge themselves most deeply into the sins toward which they are inclined in mind.

Hence we learn that riches are not merely not to be sought, but are to be avoided by every means unless, together with the enjoyment of riches, we beg from God by prayers the grace by which we may attain their just and lawful distribution and placement. From this we understand how grossly those err who locate a great part of happiness in this one thing—that someone abounds in riches—however little he labors or is solicitous about their lawful use. Not only the rich themselves are seized by this madness—which would be less astonishing—but also even those who are destitute of riches and are merely idle spectators of other men's wealth.

Then this also must be observed: there is great perversity in those men who load their own sons or grandsons, however foolish and ungodly, with ample patrimonies, whether by inheritances or by gifts, by means of which they cast both themselves and others headlong into destruction.

Verse 25. A truthful witness delivers lives, but a deceitful one blows together lies.

In נפח (*blows out*) there is a metaphor by which an inflamed zeal for lying is designated, since one who blows out his breath does this with some force and exertion. In נפשים (*lives*) there is a synecdoche of a member for the whole man.

The sum and chief point of this Proverb is to teach us how great the powers of the tongue are, whether to save or to destroy, and especially in truth-speaking and the lie opposed to it.

The word עד (*witness*) is sometimes taken strictly for him who publicly, whether in a civil or ecclesiastical setting, declares his judgment concerning

[1632 witness | PDF p. 260 | printed columns 494–495]

some matter. In this kind of setting the truth of this Proverb is discerned in a special way. For whether a judge sitting on the tribunal, a witness brought forward to confirm or refute some disputed matter, or finally a minister speaking before the congregation is in view, each one carries great weight either for delivering men from every evil, and even from present or eternal death itself, if they have conformed their judgment to the rule of truth, or for enclosing those same men within the jaws of death and evils. Sometimes it is also taken more broadly for one who privately tells his neighbor something either true or false. In this sense what is affirmed in this Proverb is true. For besides the fact that one who lies kills his own soul, he also destroys the souls of others who corrupt themselves by wicked imitation; just as, on the contrary, one who speaks truth from a good conscience saves both himself and others.

Hence we learn that the power of life and death lies in the use of the tongue, and that not only the life of the man who uses his tongue truly or otherwise is at stake, but also the lives of others. Hence a rightly instructed tongue is also compared to a tree of life and sweet waters, just as a deceitful tongue can rightly be reckoned a tree of death and bitter water. But because this matter has been discussed often and at greater length, we will refrain from accumulating more here.

Verse 26. In reverence for Jehovah [there is] strong confidence; he is accustomed to be a refuge for his sons.

Verse 27. Reverence for Jehovah [is] a spring of life, for departing from the snares of death.

In יראת (*fear*) in both verses there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole of the true worship of God. In בנים (*sons*) there is a double synecdoche, of part and species: sons for children, grandchildren for descendants, and so forth, and males for males and females together. In מחסה (*refuge*) there is a metaphor transferred from ordinary bodily warfare to spiritual and heavenly warfare.

In מקור (*spring*) there is likewise a metaphor from corporeal to incorporeal things. The same is understood in מוקשי (*snares*), where sins and grievous temptations are likened to nets and fish-traps in which little beasts are entangled and held fast, so that they cannot extricate themselves from them.

First, in the words *fear of the Lord* there is an ambiguity which must be distinguished. For there is servile fear, such as there was in Adam, which drove him to flee from God's presence, Genesis 3:11. It also exists in the wicked and even in the demons themselves, James 2. There is also in the beasts themselves a certain fear instilled by God, by which, as by a bridle, God restrains them for the most part, so that they do not dare attempt anything against man, Genesis 9:2. But there is a certain filial fear which does not exist in the wicked, Genesis 20:10, but only in the godly and faithful, by which, out of love for God and a grateful affection of mind, they fear incurring God's displeasure.

Then the fear of Jehovah is required here in order to exclude fear of men, which is contrary to the fear of God or in any way obstructs it, Matthew 10, and also fear of idols, 2 Kings 17:35–36.

Hence in Deuteronomy 10:16 God is called greatest, most mighty, and to be revered, or formidable; and this is expressed more forcefully in Psalm 76:8.

Its fruit or effect is that one withdraws from evil, Job 1:1, and in the same action does good. Although these are distinct, they are nevertheless never severed. Therefore it must be understood that we attempt no good and contemplate no departure from evil without some obstacle immediately springing up, either within our souls, or without in the world
[1632 witness | PDF p. 261 | printed columns 496–497]

or whenever there is some obstacle by which we are frightened away from carrying out good or fleeing evil; nor will the fear of God ever lack an antagonist ready to grapple with it and descend into battle. True fear of God, therefore, does not consist in there being no struggle or hand-to-hand clash, as it were, against the motions and endeavors of the godly (for in this fleeting life we must not even expect that); rather, it is exercised and proved genuine in this: if, as the stronger in the struggle, it lays low the adversaries that oppose it or puts them to flight; if, in the dwelling and, as it were, citadel of our body and soul, as commander and prefect of the praetorium (among us, *controllor*), it restrains refractory affections and thoughts that rise against obedience to God and, as it were, compels them into order; and if what the Apostle pronounces concerning the peace of God in Colossians 3:15—that it should βραβευέτω (*brabeuetō*, act as arbiter) in our hearts—also applies to the fear of God fixed in our hearts. Yet this must not be understood as though the fear of God always triumphed and subdued the rebellious motions of the soul, and never yielded ground to corrupt motions rising against it; but rather that, when put to flight and abandoning its station, it immediately returns to its former station, its enemies subdued with greater force and strength than before and driven outside.

Since we have understood what the fear of God is and what sort of thing it is, let us consider what its reward is and what recompense comes from God.

In the former, the fear of God is said to be a strong fortress in which one may safely trust. [For godliness is useful for all things and has promises for the present and future life, 1 Timothy 4; and in Psalm 34 those who fear God will lack no good thing—lions, and so forth. A fortress fortified by the invocation of the divine name alone is also one to which a man may retreat and be safe, whatever calamities press him, Acts 2 and Proverbs 18:10, where it is expressly opposed to the confidence that men falsely place in riches. And this Proverb seems to be attached to the preceding one lest he appear to have attributed too much to riches by adorning them with a distinguished crown. It is as though he said: riches can often serve as an aid to the godly in their troubles, but there is no firm and solid protection against calamities that approach and assail except in the true fear of God and lawful invocation—not in riches, knowledge, strength, friends, and so forth. The second promise concerns posterity, just as it is also contained in the promise of the Decalogue. See Psalm 86:16. But its most notable example is extant in Jehu, who, because he overthrew the worship of Baal at God's command, although he did not establish the true worship, was nevertheless rescued by God—both he himself and those sprung from him to the fourth generation—from many dangers that came upon them. By nature's guidance men undertake care for their posterity, and they are intensely anxious that, when they have departed this life, they may transmit their patrimony to them; meanwhile, they do not think about godliness even in a dream. I would ask these men whether they do not give credence to God's promises, which they do not dare openly to contradict. I ask next whether they think that what they keep shut up and heaped together in their possession is held with surer ownership and will last longer than what is preserved for us by God's promise in his coffer, as it were.

Verse 27. By this Proverb God's munificence toward those who fear him seems to be enlarged still further beyond what was expressed in the preceding Proverb. For there he likened the fear of God to a most secure support, which nevertheless is outward and external; here he compares it to a spring of life, from which whoever drinks introduces the water of life into his very inward parts and the deepest parts of his soul. And whereas in the preceding Proverb Solomon presented the fear of God as the vindicator and repeller of the evils that befall us in this life, in this Proverb he attributes this force and efficacy to it: that it rescues us lest we fall into danger and be ensnared by the deceits and ambushes of evils. From the two Proverbs considered together, therefore, it is perfectly clear how highly godliness and the fear of God ought to be valued by everyone. By its protection evils are driven away and goods are summoned; peace and tranquility are procured; an aroused war is quieted; life is brought in and death cast outside; finally, heaven is opened and the gates of hell are closed and barred.

How great and excellent this benefit is, in that it is compared to a spring of life, appears first from the fact that we are all thirsty and will perish of spiritual thirst unless provision is made for us. Isaiah 55:1 and John 7:37. Neither thirsty earth nor fiery dragons need more water than we need the waters of life offered here. Next, we must reflect that we dwell in a southern region, where water is difficult to obtain. See Genesis 21 and from verse 18 onward, and so forth, and Psalm 126:4. What of the fact that we dwell in the world as in a desert, where water is difficult to find? For no desert is more desolate or more destitute of water than men are destitute of living water. From this it is not difficult to gather how great a benefit is offered to us in this passage.

Next, in comparing fear to a fountain or spring, he seems to signify an abundance and wealth of life and a plentiful salvation. John 10:10; 2 Peter 1:11. This ought to be all the more precious to us because we resemble that woman in the Gospel who suffered from a flow and spring of blood. To wash away and cleanse that blood, a great abundance of water is needed, by which we may both be justified from all sins and sanctified from the plague of sins adhering to us. The effect of this plentiful redemption in Christ is indicated in the latter half-verse, when it teaches that by the benefit of this fountain men escape not from one or another snare, but from all the snares and nets of death.

Next, the fact that he teaches it to be not only a fountain but also one of perennial water is full of consolation, for thereby we are made more certain that this life, once begun in us, will last forever, John 4. In this respect it differs very greatly from all drinks for the body and the present life. For there is no drink so health-giving that it can fortify us against poisoned drinks so that we will not die if we partake of them. But this water is of such a kind that whoever has once drunk it will never afterward die from poison he has swallowed.

In both Proverbs it might seem incongruous that what belongs only to Christ, or to justification through faith, is attributed to godliness and divine worship, that is, to works of the first table. For Christ is our hope and confidence; he is the refuge or shelter to which we may safely retreat, Isaiah 32:2, 3; finally, he is the fountain of the water of life, Jeremiah 17 and Psalm 36:10. I answer that faith in Christ is not excluded from the fear of God, and therefore that these promises rest upon his merit as the cause of salvation and rest upon Christ as their foundation and base; but that they belong to works and to godliness toward God not

[1632 witness | PDF p. 263 | printed columns 500–501]

otherwise than insofar as those works are fruits proceeding from God's pure and sheer grace, meriting nothing from God in themselves but receiving everything first from God's gratuitous will. But if good things are conferred for no merit of our own, much less are they conferred upon posterity for the merit of their forebears, as the Papists infer from the preceding verse.

Verse 28. In the multitude of the people [is] the honor of a king, but in the lack of a nation, the crushing of a ruler [comes.]

In מחתת (*consternatio*, consternation) there is a metaphor from corporeal things, which fly apart into pieces and fragments, to incorporeal things, which likewise flow apart as bodies do unless upheld by due and just supports.

This Proverb teaches what the duty of kings and princes is; it then adds arguments by which they may be induced to discharge their office.

The magistrate's duty is to see that those subject to him are numerous, and for that reason to adopt such a manner of governing that the nation may be populous and numerous. For this purpose they must labor to ensure that peace is cultivated in their dominions, since it is especially suited to the multiplication of a people. Their first care, therefore, ought to be to keep the people safe and sheltered from external incursions of enemies, and next to prevent them from being oppressed by domestic injuries. Accordingly, they themselves must exercise restraint and not rage against their subjects' bodies. Those judges of the kingdom are reproved here who take it amiss if someone sets forth the innocence of a prisoner brought to a capital trial, as though he were pleading against the prince, when the man who justly rescues a subject whose life is in danger is especially pleading the Prince's own cause. They must also see that they do not invade their subjects' possessions or permit the subjects to assault one another, whether by thrusting their hands into another's possessions or by the harmful and hostile use of their own goods. To this head belong the cases in which things that are common either by their nature or by use and occupation are not held in common; when fallow fields intended for sowing are turned into pastures; and when field is accumulated upon field and house upon house. Isaiah 5, where the Prophet notes the hideous effect of this. Thus the pursuits of kings and princes are censured here when, devoting themselves to extending the borders of their kingdom, they lose their subjects by waging war; when, in order to heap up treasures and gape after riches, or to erect splendid buildings and feed horses and dogs, they apply their minds to these things and neglect the increase of their people. Moreover, they must see not only that their subjects live in peace but that they live religiously and honorably. If they do otherwise, even if men bring them no trouble, God will nevertheless bring famine and plague, by which the people will be consumed. See the excellent examples of Moses in Deuteronomy 1 and of Solomon in 1 Kings. For under their governance the people increased beyond measure.

The first argument is that the multiplication of the people through the king's provident and solicitous administration will redound to royal dignity and will add more honor to him than wearing any crown, however glorious. Thus he will never lack a crown as long as the memory of his administration remains. For as the number of children is the glory of parents, and the multitude of the flock is the praise of the shepherd, so the multitude of subjects is the glory of the king. Here it is asked why God was so greatly inflamed against David because he gloried in a people that had increased so greatly, 2 Samuel, since God himself wills that this should redound to the honor of kings. Answer: honor and dignity are so attached to this administration that men, meanwhile, ought not to seek that honor or gape after it.

The other argument is drawn from the attendant crushing, which arises from an administration contrary to that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 264 | printed columns 502–503]

which was described above. That crushing will arise in two ways. In dangerous times he will miss subjects to help him against the assaults of enemies; and the few who survive his tyranny and perverse governance will be little disposed to remain loyal to him.

Next, from this latter half-verse it is clear that an increased and numerous people will contribute not only dignity but strength to a king, as is gathered from the first half-verse; and that a tyrant's tyranny will bring him not only crushing but disgrace.

From these things it is clear what sort of people and subjects Solomon means: namely, faithful and obedient ones. For if they are rebellious and refractory, the more they have multiplied, the greater disgrace they would be; and the fewer the subjects are in number, the less crushing would threaten the king from them.

From this it is also deduced that the godly must labor to make the subjects of the kingdom of God and Christ grow more numerous day by day, if indeed we desire to promote God's glory and honor. For the same reason, those men are still more intolerable who convert to private use in places where the Gospel flourishes those things which custom has made common. For by this deed they confine within narrower borders not only the kingdom of an earthly king but the kingdom of Christ the Lord.

Verse 29. One slow to anger abounds in understanding, but a hasty man raises up folly.

In מרים (*elevat*, raises up) there is a metaphor transferred from corporeal things to signify incorporeal things. For as a thing raised on high is seen by everyone, so the ignorance of a man who cannot restrain his spirit is set out for many to behold. There also seems to be a synecdoche in אפים (*anger*), where anger is put for every disturbance of the soul, as may appear from the opposing member of the antithesis.

One “long of angers” is not a man who remains angry a long time and does not easily relinquish the anger he has conceived. For this is reproached in Proverbs 27:3, just as, on the contrary, it is counted praiseworthy if someone puts away his conceived anger before sunset. He, therefore, is called “long to anger” who is slow to take offense and who—according to the figure of speech just mentioned—so rules and governs his affections that they do not burst out untimely and, as it were, break down the doors of the soul. For the affections of the soul, which by nature and at the first creation were moderate and orderly, have been infected by the stain of sin and become violent and turbulent, driving themselves and their masters toward the precipices of death and hell until, through the regeneration of the spirit, as through a kind of second creation, they are refashioned and reformed. But of all disturbances, none is more ungovernable than anger, which, like Job's rhinoceros, can be neither tamed nor made gentle by any human strength or bonds. The Spirit of God alone knows how to put a bridle in its mouth and restrain and check it with a halter. Since the most wise God, who thoroughly knows every heart, perceived this, he devoted particular work and effort to subduing this disturbance. This is perfectly clear from the multitude of Proverbs directed and adapted to this matter, by which he holds anger confined as with many bridles and halters—not merely so that it will not run out rashly when no occasion is presented, but not even when a weighty provocation to anger is offered to men, as when Shechem's son violated Jacob's daughter and Nabal heaped most unworthy insults upon David. He traces the knowledge and skill of this self-government to singular understanding as its fountain, from which it flows and streams. Since indeed everyone

[1632 witness | PDF p. 265 | printed columns 504–505]

so greatly desires the fame and praise of this understanding, yet how few are found who labor to bridle anger, by which they might obtain this praise? On the contrary, how many are there who strive to keep it as far as possible from themselves and their borders, and count it praiseworthy if they allow themselves to be stirred by the slightest offense as by a breeze—seeking glory from the very thing for which it would be proper for them to be covered and overwhelmed with shame.

It is also most worthy of observation that in this passage the Holy Spirit opposes his most correct judgment to men's perverse opinion and refutes their darkened perception by the light of his sentence. For almost every man attributes it to folly if someone who has suffered an injury does not immediately blaze up and pursue it with the greatest vengeance; by contrast, God ennobles a placid and gentle spirit by this Proverb. Thus in Matthew 5 Christ pronounces the gentle blessed. Hence also Moses, with this title of gentleness placed upon his head like a crown, is exalted above all mortals in the world. There are indeed some who, struck by a certain stupor or unaware of an injury done to them, are not affected by even the slightest sensation of the evil inflicted upon them. The praise bestowed here does not apply to them at all; their ἀναισθησία (*anaisthēsia*, insensibility) ought to be regarded as blameworthy. Rather, he is at last to be praised who, touched by a proper sense of the injury done to him, nevertheless bears it calmly and equably.

This understanding, however, is not human but descends from heaven, from the Father of lights, concerning which see James 1 and Proverbs 3. From this it is also clear how highly godliness is to be valued, since it knows how to tame so wild, savage, and truculent a beast and render it as placid as a lamb.

He calls that man “short of spirit” whose mental disturbances burst out from his soul after a brief interval, though they ought to be suppressed and held within; who, as soon as he has conceived something in his mind, immediately brings it forth; whose affections sit upon the very face and surface of his soul and are not pressed down into its depth, so that they quickly leap out; who is like a house with no doors or bars, like a city encircled by no walls and guarded by no watch or sentries, like a meadow surrounded by neither hedge nor stone wall, into which beasts of every kind rush and break forth, and like a ship deprived of helmsman and rudder, which is driven at every impulse of wind and wave. His mind readily admits provocations to disturbance and, once admitted, immediately drives them out.

Concerning such a man, the Prophet pronounces that he raises up folly and sets it forth for every eye to see, though it would otherwise have lain hidden within, had it not emerged and been published by this impotence of the affections, which, like a herald, proclaims his folly. Thus, as it is said elsewhere that a fool is regarded as wise as long as he keeps silent, so it might be affirmed here that a fool could pass for a wise man if he were not irascible or subject to other disturbances. For just as the longsuffering man openly manifests by his patience and endurance the understanding otherwise hidden and concealed in his soul, so one who succumbs to disturbances openly betrays his folly.

The statement that he raises up folly does not refer only to one who provokes another, but also embraces in its scope the man himself who is provoked by another without cause. Nor does he betray his folly merely in that he is easily stirred to anger, but also in that by the same deed, while he himself rashly and inconsiderately resorts to foolish remedies, he incites others—whom he has provoked by his ignorance—to do the same.

Verse 30. The life of the flesh [is] a healing spirit; but the rottenness of the bones, envy.
[1632 witness | PDF p. 266 | printed columns 506–507]

In בשרים (*flesh*) there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole body; this must also be understood in the word עצמות, *bones*.

A healing, or beneficent, spirit—one which, moved by pity for the miseries of others, applies medicine to them so far as it is given and according to its ability, while bringing them aid from those things which God has bestowed upon it—see Proverbs 17:22—both prolongs its own life and also preserves its health.

A number of causes of this are assigned. First, by this deed joy and gladness of spirit arise, which preserve life and health no less than grief and sadness in some measure undermine them, Proverbs 17:22. This joy, however, arises in two ways. First, indeed, it arises from a mind conscious of good within itself, because it desires to benefit everyone, and this is deemed a continual feast. Next, because the person who, with readiness of spirit, runs cheerfully to help others will not fail himself in the lawful supports of this life, but will use them in moderation for his own refreshment; for it often happens that one who is restrictive toward others is also sparing and miserly toward himself.

Furthermore, one who is ready to succor others is fortified by God's promises that aid will be given him in his own times of need. For just as bloodthirsty people in middle age are snatched away by the rapacious hands of death, so, by contrast, those who preserve the lives of others prolong their own by this work. This occurs either immediately from God or through the mediation of people, whose spirits God inclines so that, when we have shown kindness to others, others in turn render compensation to us. See the example of the Shunammite woman and of the poor widow, the former of whom showed kindness to Elisha and the latter to Elijah.

It must be noted that this promise is made to a healing spirit, not to a mouth which displays benevolence in words; nor yet to a foot which runs to bring healing, or to a hand which applies medicine, but only to the spirit. For as soon as one of these imitates clouds that are dry and devoid of water, the latter two are deprived of the promise, unless a beneficent spirit is present—a spirit which benefits us even then in preserving life and health, when we lack the strength by which we are denied the execution of what we desire.

Finally, it is worthy of notice that a generous spirit, beneficent toward those who are afflicted and calamitous, not only preserves health within but brings it about. Further, that a person is vigorous and lively and does not have a deformed appearance. For this seems to be indicated by the statement that a healing spirit renders the flesh vigorous and lively; this will become more fully apparent from the opposite member of the latter half-verse. It is also confirmed by Proverbs 15:13.

*Envy is rottenness of the bones.*] For since the bones are dried up by grief and sadness (as Solomon teaches elsewhere, Proverbs 17:22), and since grief and continual anguish of spirit are the constant companions of envy and inseparable from it, it cannot happen but that the bones are dried up and wither through envy. Dryness, moreover, is necessarily followed not very long afterward by rottenness. This anguish of spirit, by which the envious person is tortured, arises from a spirit conscious of evil within itself: first, because it dispenses nothing toward establishing the health of others; next, because it is indignant that it is well with another person and that he prospers. By these two things, as though by bloodsuckers and leeches, the body's moisture is so drained away that not only is the face pale and leanness consumes and eats away the flesh, but further the very bones, as the body's foundations, are consumed. Hence no hope remains of recovering the health that has been lost, though it can be restored when the disorder is only in the flesh.

For there is no morsel that another person—the one whom he envies—eats which does not gnaw at the envious man; no increase of riches, when

[1632 witness | PDF p. 267 | printed columns 508–509]

he either sees it or has received a report of it, which does not make him poorer; no accumulation of honor which does not in some measure depress him and make him less honored; finally, no happiness which does not make him unhappy.

For envy is pain arising from another person's advantage. As clothes are corrupted by the moth and iron by rust, so the spirit is corrupted by envy, to such a degree that the envious person becomes his own executioner, both αὐτοφονεύς (*his own murderer*) and αὐτοκτόνος (*his own slayer*). Indeed, he willingly procures harm for himself in order to bring loss upon the other person against whom he is suffused with envy; and he will even bear without resentment to have both his own eyes torn out if he understands that his neighbor is thereby deprived of just one. And since this disease greatly assails even the godly, who are imbued with the Holy Spirit—as we see that it did among Joseph's brothers and Christ's disciples—it is no wonder if this crime cruelly tortures the ungodly and renders them wasted. See also the danger in which David was placed by rivalry toward the wicked, so that it may be discerned more easily how great an evil envy is.

In order to meet this disease, let us reflect within ourselves that God in his infinite wisdom has distributed gifts to people in that measure and variety which is best and which is advantageous and useful for us ourselves, provided that we look to the body of the Church.

Verse 31. He who oppresses the poor reproaches his Maker; but he who shows favor to the needy honors him.

In דל (*poor*) and אביון (*needy*) there is a synecdoche of species for all the afflicted, such as widows, orphans, the blind, the mute, the deformed, and so forth.

The sum and principal point is that clemency and humanity must be employed toward the poor.

God hates every oppression and injury, upon whomever it is inflicted and by whomever; but especially that by which those are violated who, because of their own powerlessness and weakness, are exposed and subject to the injuries of others.

For no one attacks a rich man because he is rich, but nearly everyone generally falls upon the poor and those who lack the strength to resist. Therefore, by this Proverb the Holy Spirit constructs a bulwark by which people may be restrained from inflicting injuries upon the poor. This argument is treated lucidly in James 2 and fortified by various reasons. The first is drawn from the accompanying respect of persons, to which God is a stranger; he commands that his own people should also be conformed to him and consequently far removed from it. The second reason is drawn from the efficient cause, namely, from God's love in the election of the poor, which ought to lead his people to love and esteem them likewise. The third reason is drawn from the effects of the poor, who do them no injury; this is argued from the opposites, because the rich burden them with injuries. Finally, God's name is invoked by the poor, and this is amplified by the contrary blasphemy of the rich. But there is this difference between the passage of James and that of Solomon: James speaks strictly of the godly poor, and does so in a contrast made with rich people who are strangers to faith in Christ; our Prophet here, however, institutes a discourse about the poor universally. The reason by which he attempts to restrain this contempt for the poor is drawn from its effects: contempt against a poor person rebounds against God, whose creation and workmanship the poor person is.

This workmanship, indeed, is understood in two ways. Generally, it means that he was created in the image of God. Then, in particular, it means that God wisely, by his most watchful administration of all things, imposed the burden of poverty upon those who undergo this condition of poverty. This latter meaning of workmanship seems most properly to fit this passage.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 268 | printed columns 510–511]

That poverty is sent by the Lord is declared first by a general maxim: [there is no evil in the city which the Lord does not do, Amos 3; and by the particular statement, 1 Samuel 2:7: the Lord makes poor and makes rich;] he casts this person down, but raises that person up. An example of this is found in Job 1: “The Lord gave, the Lord took away,” and so forth. And although poverty and riches alike issue and flow from the Lord as their author, poverty nevertheless seems for this single reason to be referred to God more particularly: in it we are merely acted upon, without even our will joining in, since no one willingly becomes poor, just as there is no one who does not seek riches. Therefore, just as no one without a heinous crime accuses of depravity or imperfection God's creatures, commended by their Creator's judgment and most holy mouth, so no one with a lesser crime draws this work of divine providence into reproach. And just as no one accuses the creature without by the same work rising up against the Creator himself, so also no one persecutes a poor person, as poor, or a blind person, as blind, without likewise becoming contemptuous toward God, who by his providence brought this about. If, in a mortal household, the father of the family assigns to someone a lowly and abject station—for example, the cleaning of a privy or latrine—who is there who heaps this reproach upon him without immediately incurring the displeasure of his master? How much more will this occur when God, most just and most wise, thrusts someone down to the lowest place? How unworthily this is done cannot easily be conceived, much less spoken. For since there are various causes why God makes someone poor, this is not the least of them: that he who established this distinction between you and him might arouse and sharpen you, the rich person, to give him thanks. It is a mark of a supremely ungrateful spirit for you to seize from this an occasion to treat God with contempt, where he supplies abundant material and a full harvest for praising him. To this is added that a person makes himself guilty of a most grave crime when he afflicts with further and repeated blows a man struck by God's hand.

But if he has brought that poverty upon himself by the dissipation of his goods or by idleness, nothing prevents him from paying some penalties for his wickedness through the reproach and contempt heaped upon him. For poverty of this kind is contracted not so much from God as from himself; and accordingly in Proverbs 6:11 it is called poverty and want. This, however, must not be understood as though it were lawful for everyone to rage against him and loose his mouth, but only for those whose concern it is and who are endowed with some authority over him or are connected to him by necessity. For since burdening him with reproach is a certain kind of vengeance or punishment, for anyone at all to exercise it is contrary to duty.

Just as others are instructed by this Proverb toward modest conduct with the poor, so the poor person also is prepared for patience when he considers that this proceeds from no other source than God's wise, just (I add also merciful) providence and administration, and for that reason that he ought also to show himself grateful to God. But if this duty of praising God because of his poverty rests upon the afflicted poor person himself, how unworthy it would be for others, who do not feel his poverty, to seize from it an opportunity for blaspheming God and burdening him with reproaches, when from it a reason comes to that poor person himself for blessing God!

The former half-verse is confirmed by the latter from opposites. For if one who shows favor to a poor person honors God, it follows that one who oppresses him treats God with contempt. In this the Holy Spirit supplies us with a singular remedy against contempt for the poor. For if pride has so far beset our spirit that we judge a poor person unworthy to be treated with any honor by us, nevertheless, unless we wish to be ungrateful and impious toward God, it is necessary that of the poor person

[1632 witness | PDF p. 269 | printed columns 512–513]

we take account. And among mortals we see that care is taken for the very lowest things, even—as the Proverb says—for a most worthless dog, if only, because they belong to someone, we embrace them with honor and affection.

Further, it must be noted that the person said to honor God is not one who merely does not oppress the poor person, but one who shows favor to him. For it is not enough that we abstain from injury and contempt unless, with a certain συμπαθείᾳ (*fellow-feeling*) and compassion, we help and succor him in his distress.

Verse 32. The wicked man thrusts himself into his own evil; the righteous man takes refuge even in his death.

There seems to be an ellipsis in the former part of the Proverb; במותו (*in his death*) must be supplied from the latter half-verse. In במותו there is a synecdoche for grievous affliction.

The chief point and sum is that the wicked, especially when afflicted (and the teaching that follows must be adapted to this sense), procure unnecessary evils for themselves, while the good suffer no loss in the evils which necessarily befall them.

Nothing is more important and more ancient to the wicked than that no evil of punishment should touch them, though meanwhile they are not concerned even one whit about the evil of guilt. But by God's most just judgment it occurs that the evils of punishment which they most abhor befall them, and not only befall them, but that by their sins, as though by their own hands, they draw evils upon themselves and hasten them. See Proverbs 1:31 and very often in Proverbs and the Psalms. For you could sooner separate a shadow from a body or a lash from an ass than God's vengeance from the tireless pursuit of sin. But this is not their greatest misery. That consists in this: struck by God's hand, through impatience they procure for themselves other punishments of God that are more grievous and become their own executioners. See the example of Nabal, dying from uncertain fear; of Saul, who fell upon his own sword; and of Ahithophel and Judas, who contrived death for themselves. For since afflictions are curses sent because of sin, in themselves and by their own nature they are deadly and lethal, and with their sting they prick and stab people all the way down to hell. And just as dogs constrained by fetters and chains, when they try to free themselves, bind themselves more tightly and double the knots, so the wicked, shackled by God's fetters, when they try to shake them off, tighten them and draw them closer. Who, then, should greatly fear the machinations of the wicked against himself, when by those very artfully fashioned evils of their own they procure destruction and ruin for themselves? Or who should cast down his spirit because he perceives the armed hand of no Magistrate against the wicked, when they prepare destruction for themselves and become their own executioners? Therefore, the shortest way to avoid punishment is to flee from sin as from the plague.

Furthermore, when the wicked are situated in the midst of evils, they attribute their evils now to other people, now to fortune, and now to God himself. But the Holy Spirit locates the cause and seed of their evils in the people themselves and declares that they are αὐτοκτόνας (*self-slayers*). For sometimes the sins they commit are of such a kind that, like an edge and a point by which sinners are slaughtered, they cling wholly to them, as in gluttony, drunkenness, monstrous lusts, and so forth. Yet never, however far they may seem to be removed from danger, and even though they may present some appearance of furnishing a certain refuge and support for life, does the ἔκδικον Dei ὄμμα (*avenging eye of God*), which continually keeps watch, permit that crime to go away unpunished; rather, [the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all the impiety and injustice of men.]

By carefully avoiding sins, the righteous man by the same work escapes the penalties of sin in such a manner that the evils which necessarily befall him from

[1632 witness | PDF p. 270 | printed columns 514–515]

the frail condition of man (such as disease and death) bring him no loss but are instead counted as gain, because in those afflictions he takes refuge in God. All this occurs from a careful and vigilant zeal to avoid the sin around which those things lie and stand, while through patience and the consolation of the Scriptures he blunts every sting in diseases and in death itself. [For all things work together for good for those who love God.] Romans 8. From this it is gathered when we must especially keep watch against the snares of sin: namely, in a time of the cross and affliction. Hence also at this time the duty of praying is laid upon us. Psalm 50 and 1 Peter 5:8–9. Then especially our adversary the devil is said to roar like a lion. For this reason sobriety of mind in particular, and watchfulness, are commanded. For just as small beasts are then in the greatest danger when a lion roars, consumed by hunger, so we are in danger when God permits our adversary, who never ceases to thirst for blood, to put forth his hand and touch us or our possessions.

Verse 33. Wisdom rests in the spirit of the prudent; but [that which is] in the foolish displays itself so as to be known.

In לב (*heart*) there is a metonymy of the subject, because the seat of the affections is in the heart. In תנוח (*rests*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things, by which it is signified that prudent people, with knowledge held firmly in their spirits, bring it forth only opportunely and in due season. In קרב (*midst*) there is a metaphor. For just as one who teaches while standing in the midst of a circle and gathering is easily heard and understood, so the prudent person presents his wisdom in such a way that it may be perceived as conveniently as possible by the ignorant and unskilled.

The sum is that the prudent man, while firmly retaining knowledge acquired by labor, brings forth his wisdom at suitable time and place for the instruction of simpler people.

First, it must be observed that the wisdom of the prudent does not consist in his understanding it, retaining it in memory, or even speaking about it and holding discourse about it, but in his loving and embracing it. For the heart is the seat of the affections. Hence in Psalm 119 the word of God is said to be stored and laid up in the innermost recesses of his spirit, like good seed that bears fruit in due season, or like treasure surrounded and hidden on every side, so that it may not be taken away by the fraud or violence of thieves and robbers. See Proverbs 10:14 and 12:23. Hence also in 2 Thessalonians 2 the Apostle places the cause of treachery and apostasy from the Gospel in this: that they did not embrace the truth with love. For if anyone loves wisdom, it is necessary that he understand it and retain it in memory; the converse is not true—that loving wisdom follows in a person who has it comprehended by understanding and memory.

Yet he does not keep wisdom hidden in such a way that he fails to bring it into the midst at an opportune time. For it is expressly said in Proverbs 10:13 that wisdom is found on the lips of the prudent. He designates the opportunity for making it known, namely, when the prudent person encounters fools and those destitute of wisdom, toward whom by God's command he ought to take the place of a parent. See Romans 2:17–20. He will not prostitute his pearls to swine, who would tread and trample them underfoot; he will not cast them to dogs, which would rise up against him; but neither will he set them before those who, being more prudent than he, know how to instruct him more accurately. Instead, he will adapt his ear for learning from them rather than his lips for teaching them.

Verse 34. Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is a reproach to nations.

In תרומם, *exalts*, there is a metaphor from corporeal things which, although heavy in weight, are lifted on high by force and strength applied to them. Thus a nation, born by its own nature to be cast down in ignominy, nevertheless seems to be honored by the administration and exercise of righteousness and to be set, as it were, in the highest place.

Deuteronomy 28:13: *If you obey*, and so forth, *Jehovah will set you as the head and not as the tail, so that you may be only above and not below*; then verse 12: *you shall lend and shall not borrow*; and Deuteronomy 4:6: even to the profane nations the people will appear understanding, prudent, and very great.

The form of speech is emphatic, since by personification hands, as it were, are ascribed to righteousness, by which a nation is held in the highest esteem and raised aloft. It is as if he said: although no human being should favor a nation of this kind, the righteousness which it exercises nevertheless will not allow it to lie neglected and dishonored. For although it is God alone whose hand raises this person and casts that person down, and although mortals are inclined to deprive God of his due honor, nevertheless, so that it may become better known to everyone that God favors no mortal with honor except through the intervention and aid of righteousness, he transfers to righteousness—which is merely his handmaid—what is proper to himself, in order by this device to draw people who are averse and alien to the love and pursuit of righteousness toward its kisses and embraces. Thus, in order to vindicate the ministry of the Church from the contempt to which it is exceedingly liable, God attributes to it the power and title of saving people, although that is proper to God alone. For just as a King, in order to gain for his son favor and authority among the people who hate him, confers his gifts and every kind of benefit only through his son as mediator and advocate, so God willed that we, who are far too averse to righteousness, should aspire to that exaltation which we desire eagerly enough only through the observance of righteousness.

The truth of this Proverb is confirmed by the histories of Kings and Chronicles, where one may see each commonwealth of the Jews flourishing in proportion as it was most tenacious of righteousness and equity: under David, under Solomon, under Hezekiah, and so forth. Indeed, even in commonwealths utterly alien to the commonwealth of Christ, where the shadow of righteousness and equity rather than true righteousness was cultivated, God nevertheless willed to make righteousness so highly commended among human beings that he allowed even that painted and shadowy righteousness to depart rewarded with no small prize of peace and outward prosperity. For just as God compensated Ahab's fast with a great reward, although it was ungodly and superstitious, in order to invite people to cultivate true fasting, so, in order to show with how great and solid an honor he will crown the pursuit of true righteousness proceeding from a pure heart, God is accustomed to reward with prizes not to be despised even the mask of that same righteousness when it is placed in some outward practice.

How necessary the use of this doctrine is is sufficiently declared by the minds of mortals, so thoroughly entangled and ensnared in this error that they suppose there is no path to honors unless they lie, pretend, dissemble, flatter, and are, as it were, made and compounded from frauds themselves. What else is this than to appoint the devil as the distributor of offices, dignities, and honors? Since he is the author of these vices, he must then also be their rewarder.

But if righteousness carries a nation upward and raises it aloft, much more will it heap honor upon a single person. For if righteousness raises on high a nation which is never

[1632 witness | PDF p. 272 | printed columns 518–519]

so thoroughly purified and cleansed that it does not have many wicked people joined to it who keep the poison of their injustice enclosed within them, how much more will it raise the person who is righteous and a cultivator of equity? And if God does not permit a righteousness that appears in a certain outward guise to go without a crown, much less will he fail to crown one that is sincere and unpainted.

He confirms the preceding part of the Proverb from its opposites: the pursuit of sin exposes a nation to reproach, however extensive and populous it may be and however widely it may rule, so that they either pass into a proverb and are received with hissing by passersby, or are even established as an example of divine vengeance. See Deuteronomy 28: *they shall be at the tail, not at the head; they shall be in the lowest places, not in the highest; they shall borrow and shall not lend*. See an example of this in the five cities, which were without doubt exceedingly populous because of the unusual fertility of their land. See the notable history of the tribe of Benjamin, nearly destroyed because the Levite's wife was violated, Judges 19–20. Likewise, see chapter 1 of Jeremiah's Lamentations, where the mournful destruction of the Jewish kingdom, which formerly ruled far and wide, is described. See in Titus how the Cretans are branded with the reproach of lying, and in Acts 17 how the Athenians are blackened by the mark of curiosity. But if God exterminates extensive nations because of their sins, much more will he exterminate families; and if he does so to families among whom some good people are nevertheless intermingled, as is clear in the book of Daniel, how much less will he spare a wicked person in whom there is nothing good to hinder and delay God's just vengeance?

Verse 35. The king's favor is toward an understanding servant, but his fury shall be against one who causes shame.

There is an ellipsis in the former part of the verse, which must be supplied from the contrary of מביש, *one who causes shame*, namely מכבד, *one who honors*. There is likewise another ellipsis in the latter half-verse, supplied from the contrary of משכיל, *understanding*, namely כסיל, *foolish*. There is another ellipsis in the substantive verb, which is expressed in the latter part and understood in the former. Finally, the ל, the sign of the dative case, prefixed to עבד, *servant*, is understood in the latter part.

The sum is that the condition of a servant is not so abject or despised that by doing good he cannot bring a great reward upon himself and by doing evil summon and draw grave loss upon himself.

Since Kings, Masters, and so forth are included within the scope and embrace of *parent* in the fifth commandment, it follows that subjects, servants, and so forth are also contained within the meaning and comprehension of *children*. Therefore, just as it is the duty of children to procure honor for their parents, so it is also the duty of servants to take care that they so conduct themselves that honor thereby returns to their masters. Conscience toward God ought especially to move them to this, for he both commands it and proves the generous rewarder of the obedience he has commanded, nor does he allow those who remain in their duty toward their masters to depart without a just reward. This also ought to sharpen their eagerness and correct their sluggishness: it is promised here that they will be a delight to their master. For God, who makes this promise, rules human hearts and, according to his pleasure, bends and bends them back, leads and leads them back. If Kings, who have so great a supply and abundance of delights at hand, are so delighted in a bondservant that they almost rest content in him, why should we not expect the same much more from those who do not have so great an abundance of delicacies and delights at hand?

Kings and Masters are also admonished of their own duty: they must have an entirely paternal spirit toward their servants and must not embrace their children so closely that meanwhile they spurn their servants and hold them in contempt. Nor are they to have any greater regard for their servants than for an ox or horse, which they feed so that, when well fed, it may serve them more conveniently. For what is said universally—that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 273 | printed columns 520–521]

God chooses base and despised things by whose ministry he accomplishes great things—is found true in this matter. See the example of Naaman's maid and of his servant in 2 Kings. See also Joseph, both in Potiphar's household and in Pharaoh's court; likewise Daniel and his companions, who accomplished for the King what no one from his native nation could accomplish. In these same persons, if you except Naaman, one may see how servants were of the greatest profit and delight to Kings and masters. Since this was done by masters who were foreigners and—what is more—alien to God, why should servants not expect it from masters who are fellow citizens, sprung from the same people, and fear God?

Here the example of Saul is memorable. Although he proved ungrateful and malicious toward David, a servant who had deserved well, God nevertheless ordered all things so wonderfully that by those very counsels through which Saul plotted David's death he rendered David honored: he chose him as a son-in-law and set him over the entire army. To this must be referred the command given to masters in the law concerning the manumission of their bondservants, that they should not send them away empty but should load them with the blessings with which God had enriched their masters.

If it is asked here who is an understanding servant, I answer: a servant who fears God. First, one who is imbued with the fear of God is not left without God's other gifts, especially some prudence in conducting affairs. See Psalm 119, section 13, where David affirms himself more prudent than his enemies, elders, and teachers on this one account: that, captivated by love of God's doctrine, he had meditated upon it continually. Next, the fear of God is like a spur by which he is driven to discharge his duty. Finally, God's blessing, which grants the desired success to our undertakings, advances and prospers the efforts of godly servants. Hence it is that David, in Psalm 101, professes that those who fear God are the servants he will choose for himself.

But just as a prudent servant gains the King's favor by honoring the King, so a fool, by bringing shame both upon himself and upon the King, kindles the King's fervent and overflowing wrath. Here it is perceived how great a loss one sustains from a foolish or wicked servant. For his perversity and ignorance are of such consequence that he can cover with shame not only himself but even the King, who is full of dignity and majesty. See the examples of Gehazi, Haman, and Ziba.
