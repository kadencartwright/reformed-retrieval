## QUESTION VII

### How are the evangelists Matthew 1:12–13 and Luke 3:27 to be reconciled with 1 Chronicles 3:17–19?

For brevity’s sake we shall combine three fairly serious difficulties in one chapter. First, how is Zerubbabel made the son of Shealtiel by the evangelists when the author of Chronicles calls him the son of Pedaiah? Second, how is Abiud established as Zerubbabel’s son by Matthew, Rhesa by Luke, but Meshullam and others by the author of Chronicles, who is completely silent about both Abiud and Rhesa? Third, should one Shealtiel and one Zerubbabel be accepted, or must two Shealtiels as well as two Zerubbabels necessarily be established?

As to the first, some pass it over entirely, whether from thoughtlessness or because, as often happens, they judged the difficulty either too slight or too serious. Calvin, Beza, Hammond, Maldonatus, and Estius certainly read the anxious disputes of many men on this matter, yet in their commentaries on the passages before us they judged that nothing at all should be raised about this difficulty. Those who do propose it follow three different paths in resolving it. Those in the first group—

[Logical printed p. 56 | IA leaf 394 | access PDF 394 | OCR object 393]

—require at least two distinct Zerubbabels, one of whom was Shealtiel’s son and the other Pedaiah’s. Why we do not agree with them will become clear under the third difficulty.

The second group, like the third, posits only one Zerubbabel and makes him the son of both Pedaiah and Shealtiel. The second group favors a distinction between immediate and mediate descent, as though Zerubbabel were the immediate son of Pedaiah and the mediate son of his true grandfather Shealtiel through his son Pedaiah. The third group prefers the distinction between natural and legal descent, as though Zerubbabel were Pedaiah’s natural son and Shealtiel’s legal son: either because Zerubbabel was Shealtiel’s successor, Pedaiah having died before his father Shealtiel; or because after Shealtiel died, his younger brother Pedaiah begot Zerubbabel from Shealtiel’s wife by levirate right, and Zerubbabel is therefore generally called not Pedaiah’s son but Shealtiel’s.

Junius, Diodati, Vatablus, and many others stand among the second group. Cajetan, Arias Montanus, Gualter, Baldwin, Walaeus, and especially Spanhemius stand among the third. Piscator combines both groups, though he inclines more toward the third. The middle and later English annotators—who on Matthew 1 are precisely the same—propose all three solutions but do not determine toward which of the three they themselves incline.

The entire question between the second and third groups comes down to whether Pedaiah was Shealtiel’s son or his brother. There is no way to decide this dispute except from the text of 1 Chronicles 3:17. Here we read expressly, “Shealtiel his son,” that is, the son not of Asir immediately preceding but of Jeconiah. It is true that the Septuagint translators, the Old Latin version, the old and new English versions, and other interpreters make *Asir* a proper name. Junius, however, and after him Diodati and Piscator, much more correctly make it a common noun denoting Jeconiah’s condition in bonds and prison when he begot Shealtiel. Otherwise, if *Asir* were the proper name of Jeconiah’s firstborn, why would the designation בן, “his son,” be attached to Shealtiel rather than to Asir? Why would the second of Jeconiah’s sons be called his son rather than the first, Asir?

In any event, the six following names—Malchiram, Pedaiah, and the rest—stand in the same order with Shealtiel; that is, they are his brothers and Jeconiah’s sons, not Shealtiel’s. The most learned Junius certainly appears rash here. In order to make Pedaiah and his six brothers, the sons of Jeconiah, not Shealtiel’s brothers but his sons, he corrupts the text—not only by rendering the copulative ו with the adversative “but,” but also by adding the pronoun “his,” contrary not only to the words but also to the tenor and purpose of the text, as Piscator rightly notes. Junius does indeed have Vatablus as the author of this corruption, but I do not know what followers he has unless Diodati be excepted. Diodati adds more to Junius’s changes by reading verse 18 thus: “Shealtiel’s sons were Malchiram, etc.,” contrary to the original and all the ancient interpreters.

There was never any Chaldean paraphrase of the books of Chronicles; and the Greek, Latin, Syriac, and Arabic versions reject these additions. I acknowledge that in this passage the Syriac and Arabic interpretations, and even the Greek, do not possess great authority, for all of them plainly disagree among themselves, with the original, with the Old Latin, and with all more recent versions. First, the Syriac and Arabic entirely delete the name Asir. Second, for Shealtiel the Syriac reads *Asrashtiel* and the Arabic *Esebol*, which bear no resemblance to Shealtiel. Third, for Pedaiah both read *Peraja*, misled by the similarity of the letters *daleth* and *resh*. Fourth, in verse 19 both make Nedabiah Zerubbabel’s father instead of Pedaiah. These four errors in two verses cannot be defended.

The Alexandrian Greek also plainly errs when, unlike the Vatican text and all other ancient and more recent interpreters of the Hebrew text, it substitutes Shealtiel for Pedaiah as Zerubbabel’s father. Shealtiel was indeed Zerubbabel’s father, and is so called in various passages of Scripture, but in the passage before us Zerubbabel’s father is given as Pedaiah, not Shealtiel. An error should be acknowledged much more readily here in the Alexandrian exemplar, though otherwise most excellent, than in the Hebrew original, the Vatican Greek, and all the other versions from which the Alexandrian differs here.

It is clear from what has been said why we judge Shealtiel to be Pedaiah’s brother, not his father, and therefore agree with Spanhemius and the third group against Junius and the second. Spanhemius does offer an argument for our opinion here, but it does not altogether please us. He proves that Shealtiel was Pedaiah’s brother because otherwise Jeconiah would not have had several sons, whereas the text itself signifies a plurality of Jeconiah’s sons with the plural noun בנים. This is not a firm foundation, for an interchange of the plural number for the singular is frequent, especially in the books of Chronicles. In the immediately preceding verse 16 we read of Jehoiakim’s “sons” in the plural, although only his one son Jeconiah is then named. As to Zedekiah, who follows Jeconiah there, we need not make him a person distinct from King Zedekiah. He is called Jeconiah’s son there because he succeeded him in the kingdom, although he was Josiah’s natural son and Jeconiah’s uncle, in no way Jehoiakim’s son: if one regards nature, he was his brother; if law, his grandson, since he was only a mediate successor after Jeconiah. In verse 22 likewise the “sons” of Shecaniah occur in the plural, although only his one son Shemaiah is mentioned. Hence both the Syriac and the Greek and Latin interpreters render the Hebrew plural “sons” with the singular “son.”

On the first difficulty I add only—

—that what Spanhemius adduces here concerning the right of levirate marriage appears precarious and to rest on no solid foundation. Let Zerubbabel be Pedaiah’s natural son and Shealtiel’s legal son; why, however, should we explain “legal” here of a son begotten from Shealtiel’s wife through levirate right by his brother Pedaiah, rather than of an adopted son or a mere successor? Spanhemius has nothing by which to prove any such thing. As for Gomarus’s argument from the word “begot,” repeated altogether too often by Spanhemius, as though successors and adopted sons could not be said to be begotten, we have answered it elsewhere. In any event, if there were nothing else, the very passage before us appears to rout that argument. They are compelled to admit a metaphorical generation here just as they admit a metaphorical filiation. For Shealtiel is said to beget Zerubbabel, yet not properly, since they acknowledge that Zerubbabel was not Shealtiel’s natural son but only his legal son—that is, his grandson by his brother Pedaiah. Who can rightly determine whether he was born from Shealtiel’s widow or from Pedaiah’s own wife, since not only the sacred page but all other authors are silent on the matter?

As for the second difficulty, I likewise find three answers to it. The first is that of those who multiply Zerubbabels. It is easy for them to make two lines from two men: they make Abiud the son of one Zerubbabel and Rhesa the son of the other. More on this answer under the following difficulty. Here I observe only that doubling Zerubbabel does not solve the knot unless the man is tripled, for not two but three different lines of descendants flow from him: Matthew’s through Abiud, Luke’s through Rhesa, and the author of Chronicles’ through Meshullam.

The second answer is that of Annius of Viterbo, who preserves the identity of the persons but multiplies their names. According to him, the one Zerubbabel had a son with three names, since he was called Meshullam, Rhesa, and Abiud; indeed, this man’s son and grandson and all the other descendants down to Mary likewise each had three names. Thus all three lines in Luke, Matthew, and Chronicles are one and the same, agreeing in the same persons and differing only in names. I marvel not so much that Italy could produce so shameless an impostor, who under the fictitious name of Philo dared to foist innumerable inventions upon the world, as that most learned men were found who drank in ravings of this kind with ears too wide open and credulous minds. It is painful to see Erasmus and Salmerón expressly follow Annius’s follies. Besides the manifest vanity of a long series of men in which not one or two but all have three entirely different names—an example of which appears in no history, sacred or profane—the evangelists’ catalogues themselves reject this madness. Between Zerubbabel and Joseph Luke numbers eighteen persons, Matthew only nine, and 1 Chronicles fewer still. What then is to be done with Luke’s eleven supernumeraries? From where will Annius mint two new names for each of them?

Hugo Grotius, a singular inventor of things at once new and old, comes running here and, in his catalogue of Matthew, adds opposite every person named by Luke an equal number of anonymous men for Matthew. Splendid reinforcements for Annius! But let us cease trifling.

We rest in the third answer, that of Gualter, Paraeus, Baldwin, Walaeus, and others. They hold that three different lines of one and the same Zerubbabel are described: one in the Old Testament, through his eldest son Meshullam and also his other sons, whose offspring were distinguished and from whom governors and leaders were appointed over the nation of the Jews for some centuries after its return from Babylon. In the New Testament two other new lines of the same Zerubbabel are set forth, one through Abiud and the other through Rhesa. They were Zerubbabel’s sons but private men whose life among the people was obscure and poor and was therefore omitted from Chronicles. No one who ascribes divine authority to Matthew and Luke doubts that besides those named in Chronicles, Zerubbabel had sons Rhesa and Abiud, whose lines were carried down to Joseph and Mary. That these men were obscure is demonstrated by the profound silence concerning them among all sacred and secular authors; and that they were poor is proved by the very slight fortunes of Joseph and Mary, who descended from them in a straight and legitimate line. It cannot be known with certainty that this obscurity and poverty was the true reason they were passed over by the writer of Chronicles. It can nevertheless be accepted as a very probable conjecture until a more probable one is brought from somewhere else.

The third difficulty remains, more serious than those above: were there several Zerubbabels and Shealtiels? I had thought that the affirmative was Spanhemius’s alone after Gomarus. On further inquiry, however, I find that the opinion is ancient and that many of the most learned men among both papists and Protestants stand for it. The *Glossa ordinaria*, Tostado (Abulensis), Cajetan, Barradas, Jansenius, Galatinus, Cornelius a Lapide, Chemnitz, Baldwin, Walaeus, Grotius, and others expressly defend it. Paraeus, however, and, as I think, the others generally who do not raise this dispute, take the negative. I shall state why this latter opinion pleases me more when I have answered the arguments of the former. Galatinus offers the following arguments in book 7, chapter 12.

First, things are said of Zerubbabel that cannot apply to one man. Haggai, namely, affirms that he saw the former temple and yet lived long after the later temple. So long a life could not belong to one man in that age. Reply. In the same place Galatinus, from his spurious author “our holy Rabbi”—

—Rabbenu Hacadosh, proposes many foul fables. In his replies to Antoninus, consul of Rome—who never existed—that fabulist teaches that the prophet Elijah descended to him from heaven while he prayed in the cave of Hebron where the patriarchs are buried, and from the prophet Isaiah demonstrated to him by cabalistic art that the Virgin Mary, for whose sake God had chosen the Israelite people, the tribe of Judah, and the house of David from which the Messiah would be born, had been made supreme mistress of the whole world and true queen of heaven. Very many utterly false fables of this kind, with which Galatinus endeavors to please the papists, prove him a frivolous man of very little authority. Spanhemius certainly declares that the argument before us carries no weight, and rightly so. The prophet Haggai says nothing about Zerubbabel, but speaks only of those among the people who stood by Zerubbabel when he founded the second temple and who had seen the first. He by no means asserts that Zerubbabel was among their number.

Nevertheless, although Spanhemius rejects this argument of Galatinus, he himself offers another very similar one, if not truly the same, in the same place, part 1, page 109 of his *Doubts*. He professes that it especially drew him into the opinion we are refuting. “Matthew’s Zerubbabel,” he says, “was born long after the Babylonian captivity, because his father Shealtiel was born in that captivity. But Luke’s Zerubbabel was born either before the captivity or in its first years, since at its end he was a man mature enough to act as the people’s leader on their return.” Reply. This correction of Spanhemius appears to add no strength to Galatinus’s argument. Suppose that Shealtiel was born in the fifth year after the captivity of his father Jeconiah and begot Zerubbabel when he was twenty-five. In this way, in the seventieth year of the captivity Zerubbabel will be a full forty years old, an age mature enough to lead the people.

Galatinus adds a second argument, which Spanhemius also borrows, though he leaves its author unnamed. “From Matthew’s Zerubbabel to Joseph,” he says, “there were only nine generations, but from Luke’s Zerubbabel there were seventeen. Therefore each Zerubbabel was not one man, but two.” Reply. From Spanhemius himself at the passage cited, page 113: Zerubbabel’s descendants from his son Abiud in Matthew were fewer, and from his son Rhesa in Luke more. Nothing is more frequent than for some in two families to be longer-lived and others shorter-lived, so that in the same span of time one family numbers more persons and the other fewer.

Gomarus has a third argument from Augustine, *Questions on Deuteronomy*, chapter 46. Spanhemius presents it as his first though not his principal argument: there must have been two Shealtiels because all their ancestors back to David differ. The father of one is Neri, whose line runs to Nathan; the father of the other is Jeconiah, whose entirely different line extends to Solomon. Reply. Augustine’s authority does not press us here, since both Jansenius and Chemnitz acknowledge in their commentaries on the passage before us that all the fathers are silent on this matter. Each of them draws an argument from their silence for his own opinion here against us, though, as it seems to us, wrongly. Augustine’s testimony certainly implies that our opinion was the common one in his day. As for the argument he advances for multiplying Shealtiel, he professes it to be only his own conjecture; anyone may see how slender a foundation supports that conjecture. Its whole force rests upon this hypothesis: that one man cannot have two fathers, one legal and the other natural, whose different lines meet in the same root. That is, Shealtiel’s natural father Neri could not descend from David through Nathan while his legal father Jeconiah descended from the same David through Solomon. It assumes, moreover, that one man cannot through different sons send out two lines of descendants which, by right of marriage, adoption, or succession, finally converge in one and the same person. In other words, the distinct lines of one and the same Zerubbabel through his two sons Rhesa and Abiud could not have converged in Joseph or Mary. These certainly are the very *assumptions* in the argument before us. They ought not, as it seems, to be assumed without proof, since Spanhemius himself on page 110 professes that convergences of families of this kind are quite frequent among the Jews and among every nation.

Having therefore rejected the former opinion concerning a plurality of Zerubbabels and Shealtiels, we embrace the latter, concerning their unity, for the following reasons. First, persons are not to be multiplied in histories without great necessity, as Spanhemius himself rightly warns on page 58 in this very argument. He denies against Tostado that Shealtiels should be multiplied if any more convenient solution to the difficulties can be found without multiplication. But the ordinary distinction between legal and natural fathers and sons, commonly employed by both ancients and moderns, quite suitably solves all the arguments for which the present multiplication was invented.

Second, this multiplication supposes various histories about which neither Scripture nor any other writers say a single word. Namely, it supposes that Shealtiel, the son of King Jeconiah—who had been exalted by Evil-merodach above all the kings subject to the Babylonian Empire—possessed no authority among the Jews in Babylon, but that he and his son Zerubbabel, Jeconiah’s grandson, spent their entire lives in obscure and impoverished circumstances; while Neri—

—a private man obscure along with his remote ancestors, had a son Shealtiel who became leader and prince of the people and Jeconiah’s successor, with Jeconiah’s own son Shealtiel passed over; and that Neri’s grandson Zerubbabel, God miraculously touching the hearts of the people, was chosen as prince to bring the captives back and restore the temple, while Zerubbabel, Jeconiah’s grandson through Shealtiel and the lawful successor of Solomon and all the Jewish kings, sank obscure and despised among the dregs of the people. Not everyone, I think, has so strong a stomach that he can digest these things.

Third, those who multiply the persons do not agree among themselves. Some double Shealtiel alone, some Zerubbabel alone, and most double both.

Fourth, the most learned Tostado thinks Zerubbabel should be not merely doubled but tripled, on this argument. If he is doubled on account of the two lines of descendants recorded by Luke and Matthew, then he must be tripled on account of the third line of descendants described by the author of Chronicles. Since Spanhemius admits the minor premise, whatever he replies will by no means weaken the major. The argument will therefore stand unless, by abolishing the duplication, he removes the triplication.

Fifth, this doubling entails that Mary, and therefore Christ, has neither affinity nor blood relationship with any king of the Jews except David alone. Yet according to Matthew, Joseph descended in a straight line from all the kings of Judah, a line that never failed or was interrupted, and thus he had a clear right to David’s throne. Mary and Christ, descended from Neri, could claim no right to it as long as any one of Joseph’s relatives remained who descended in a straight and continuous line from Solomon and all the kings. Few Christians, I think, will admit these things when they are seriously considered.

Finally, this doubling will place by far the greatest distance between Joseph and Mary. For although each was from the tribe of Judah and the family of David, unless the lines of Solomon and Nathan converged in one Zerubbabel, Joseph will be more than thirty degrees of kinship distant from Mary. That distance is so great that it is astonishing that within the tribe of Judah and the family of David Mary had no nearer kinsman to marry than Joseph. We shall not continue to burden the former opinion with further absurdities.
