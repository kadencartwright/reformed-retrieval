QUESTION XII

ON THE USE OF CONSEQUENCES

Must the Doctrines of Faith and Morals Be Demonstrated Only by the Express
Word of God, or Can They Also Be Legitimately Proved through Consequences
Drawn from Scripture? The Latter Is Affirmed.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. The question arose from a new method of disputing among the Roman
Catholics devised by quacks and mountebanks. In order to free themselves more
easily from our arguments—by which we invincibly establish our position from
Scripture and pierce through their errors—they thought they would have no
more convenient way to escape than to force us to demonstrate that all our
doctrines are contained in Scripture in so many words, rejecting every use of
consequences. Cardinal du Perron appears to have been the first among the
Roman Catholics to devise this art, in his *Reply* against the king of Great
Britain. He was followed by several others of the same ilk: Gunterius,
Cottonus, Arnoldus, and especially Veronus, who even composed a distinctive
method of disputing that was consequently called the Veronian method. The
Wallenburg brothers in Germany and other missionaries who shun the light
joined them. But the ancient heretics had already lit the way for them. The
Arians used this argument more than once in order to overthrow *homoousion*;
likewise, the Macedonians denied the deity of the Spirit because Scripture
nowhere expressly says that the Holy Spirit is God; see Gregory Nazianzen,
Oration 5, *On Theology*. Maximus the Monk testifies that the Apollinarians
and Monophysites used the same weapons in the twenty orations that are
commonly but wrongly attributed to Athanasius.

II. The fifth article of the French Confession—from which they wish it to
appear that we have drawn the opinion they fasten upon us—does indeed assert
the perfection of Scripture when it says that Scripture is the rule of all
truth and embraces whatever is required for God's glory and our salvation,
so that neither human beings nor even angels themselves may add anything to
it or take anything from it. Yet the article does not establish that nothing
is to be admitted unless it is read in Scripture in so many words. Indeed,
near its end,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 35 | IA leaf 89 | PDF p. 89]

when it is added that we receive the three creeds—the Apostles', Nicene, and
Athanasian—it is sufficiently indicated that we do not pursue the fine points
of words but the truth of doctrine and worship. It therefore wishes the Word
of God alone to be retained to the exclusion of all traditions, but it does
not thereby restrict us to the express Word to the exclusion of consequences.

III. To understand the question, it must be observed that something can be
said to be in Scripture in two ways: according to its meaning or according to
its wording; that is, expressly and in so many words, or implicitly and with
respect to the sense. We say that all things are contained in Scripture not
in the first way but in the second.

IV. Consequences can be considered either materially, insofar as they denote
the doctrines themselves drawn out through consequences, or formally, for
the connection of the terms itself. In this manner Augustine, *On Christian
Doctrine* 2.31, distinguishes between the truth of propositions and the truth
of connections. Again, some consequences are inherent in and drawn from
Scripture, being contained in it virtually; others are inferred into
Scripture and brought into it. Some are proximate, necessary, and evident;
others remote, probable, and obscure. The former, not the latter, are under
discussion here.

V. A sound proof of a thing is such either in itself or in relation to this
or that person. The former consists in the demonstration of the thing; the
latter, in the conviction of the person. The former is always present in
correct argumentation through consequences, but the latter is not.

VI. The articles of faith that must be proved from Scripture are either
positive and affirmative, containing some doctrine that must be believed, or
negative and exclusive, rejecting errors brought in by heretics. The former
must be proved clearly and certainly from the Scriptures because they are the
proper object of faith. As for the latter, it is enough that no mention of
them is made in the Scriptures and that those things are taught from which
they are easily convicted of falsehood. Hence our adversaries make themselves
ridiculous when they demand that we prove in so many words from Scripture
that there is no purgatory, that the pope is not the head of the church, that
the mass is not a sacrifice, and so forth. 1. The one who affirms must prove,
not the one who denies. 2. These doctrines are not positive but negative for
us. It is therefore enough for us to teach that they are nowhere contained in
the Scriptures and that other things are found there from which they are
easily refuted.

VII. The sufficiency and perfection of Scripture do not consist in every
error and heresy being condemned in it by name, but only in every positive
doctrine being clearly delivered. For just as the straight is the standard
of itself and of the crooked, once all necessary truth has been set down, the
errors opposed to it are easily rejected.

VIII. The question, therefore, is reduced to these terms: in addition to the
express Word of God, must evident and necessary consequences be admitted in
theology; or can the doctrines of faith and morals be legitimately proved
from them? We affirm this, while the adversaries deny it—though not all of
them. For Bellarmine agrees with us here, *On the Word of God* 4.9 and *On
Justification* 3.8. “Nothing,” he says, “belongs to faith except what God has
revealed through the apostles and prophets, or what is evidently deduced from
it.” Canus, *Theological Topics* 12.6; Salmero, volume 1, prologue 9, chapter
7; Turrecremata, *Summary of the Church* 4, part 2, chapter 8; and several
others agree with him, establishing that what is deduced from the Scriptures
by a necessary and legitimate consequence belongs to faith.

[Marginal heading: The use of consequences is proved from the purpose of
Scripture.]

IX. Many things prove the use of consequences in matters of faith: 1. The
purpose of Scripture, which is teaching, refutation, correction, training,
and consolation, 2 Timothy 3:16; Romans 15:4. This purpose cannot be attained
without them, because neither can a thesis be transferred to a hypothesis,
nor can Scripture ever be applied to either theoretical or practical uses.

[Marginal heading: The nature of man.]

2. The nature of man, to whom the mysteries of religion are entrusted. He is
not a block of wood or

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 36 | IA leaf 90 | PDF p. 90]

a brute, but a rational creature. Therefore, since he is capable of
reasoning, he ought to search the Scriptures, John 5:39, and not cling to the
bark of the words, but penetrate to their very marrow and meaning, so that
from the things he reads he may also understand some things he does not read,
as Augustine well observes in *Against Maximinus* 3.3.

[Marginal heading: The wisdom of God.]

3. The wisdom of God. Just as a wise person, when he speaks, wishes
everything that can be legitimately elicited from his words to be understood
as spoken by him, so the supremely wise God, who foresaw whatever could be
deduced from the Word, spoke in such a way that he wished whatever is
legitimately gathered from what was said to be regarded as his Word.

[Marginal heading: From the practice of heretics.]

4. The practice of heretics. In order to defend their errors better against
the orthodox, they entirely rejected the use of consequences. Thus the
Arians denied *homoousion* because it was not contained word for word in the
Scriptures, for which reason Basil called them hunters of syllables. And in
Oration 37 Nazianzen calls a Pneumatomachian who uses the same fraud an
alphabetical sophist and a slanderer of words.

[Marginal heading: From the Roman Catholics' use.]

5. The Roman Catholics' use. They are accustomed to prove through
consequences most of their doctrines concerning the primacy and infallibility
of the pope, transubstantiation, purgatory, and the like.

[Marginal heading: From the example of Christ and the apostles.]

6. The example of Christ and the apostles, who are frequently read to have
used consequences. Thus Christ proves the resurrection of the dead against
the Sadducees from the formula of the covenant, Matthew 22, although nothing
about the resurrection is stated there expressly. And the apostles prove
that Jesus of Nazareth is the true Messiah promised in the Old Testament,
although nothing about him is read there word for word. It is no help to
object that the authority of Christ and the apostles is infallible and that
their consequences are therefore also of undoubted truth, while ours are not
such. For although Christ's consequences are infallible in themselves by the
authority of the speaker, their force among the Sadducees did not arise from
the authority of the speaker, which they did not acknowledge, but from the
nature of the things said. Otherwise, how would Christ have stopped the
mouths of his enemies by that very proof when they did not acknowledge his
authority?

X. Although Christ's inferences pass for us into the Word of God by that very
fact, because they proceed from Christ, and by right become an object of
faith, it is nevertheless false that the Sadducees, Christ's enemies, in fact
regarded them as such in that capacity. Rather, they were admitted only
because they had a foundation in the saying of Moses cited by Christ. Thus
they were recognized by the Sadducees as such insofar as regard was had to
what was said, not to the one saying it.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

XI. The “reasonings” against which the weapons of our warfare are directed,
mentioned in 2 Corinthians 10:4, are not every kind of reasoning. Rather, as
the passage says, they are “reasonings raised against the knowledge of God”:
reasonings that attack rather than serve, that oppose the gospel rather than
minister to it. These rightly must be destroyed because they are incompatible
with faith. But legitimate reasonings, which are subjected to revelation and
properly employed to explain and apply it, must not likewise be destroyed.

XII. The foundation on which something rests is one thing; the instrument we
use to know that thing is another. What has a fallible foundation cannot be
infallible, because an effect cannot be nobler in its whole kind than its
cause. But reason here is not the foundation; it is the instrument.

XIII. Although the intellect that elicits consequences is fallible, it does
not follow that the consequences are false and uncertain. 1. It is one thing
to be able to err and another actually to err; one thing to err sometimes and
another to err always. A faculty that is of itself and always fallible in all
its operations cannot ground infallible certainty. But the intellect is not
such, because it is fallible not in itself but accidentally, and not in all
things but only in some.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 37 | IA leaf 91 | PDF p. 91]

Otherwise, if the argument held universally, and if no trust were to be
placed in reason because it sometimes errs, it would follow that all
knowledge and certainty would truly be removed from the world and Pyrrhonism
and incomprehensibility introduced. In the same way, all certainty of sense
would be removed because it sometimes errs, something anyone readily
understands to be utterly absurd. For the capacity for fallibility is not
necessarily joined with the act; indeed, its passing into act can be
prevented by applying legitimate means. Therefore, just as the senses must be
freed from the impediments that hinder their certainty, whether found in the
object, medium, or organ, so the intellect must be freed from prejudices that
hinder right reason, and then it will not err. Here, however, we are speaking
of a sound and properly constituted intellect.

XIV. It is one thing to apprehend the rationale of a consequence and another
to apprehend the consequent itself. Faith apprehends the consequent; reason,
the consequence. Yet it does not therefore follow that the faith by which the
consequent is believed rests on reason, because here reason is not the
argument on account of which I believe, but the instrument by which I
believe. An instrument does not introduce into the text what was not there,
but through legitimate consequence draws out what previously lay hidden
there. Hence the conclusion emerges as theological from the inferential
middle term, but as logical from reason, or the instrument that elicits the
consequences. The consequence with respect to its material aspect is founded
on the Word, but with respect to its formal aspect on reason.

XV. Although reason concurs in eliciting consequences, it does not follow
that faith is established by reason. Likewise, although faith comes from
hearing, it does not follow that sense is the foundation of faith. Faith uses
reason but does not rest on it; it uses reason as an instrument of application
and a mode of knowing but does not rest on it as the foundation and rule of
things to be believed.

XVI. Mixed syllogisms, in which one premise belongs to natural light and the
other to revealed light, do not cease to belong to faith: 1. Every proposition
receives its designation from the subject, not from the predicate. 2. The
proposition that belongs to revealed light virtually contains what is sought
from natural light for its proof, and so communicates its force to it. 3. To
prove a conclusion of faith, the middle term is taken not from nature but
from Scripture. But when an adversary denies the connection of the middle
term with the major extreme, aid is supplied through the principles of
reason—not to prove the truth of the middle term, but its connection. For
example, I deny that the bread in the Supper becomes the body of Christ,
employing this middle term: because it has the accidents of bread. But if the
connection of the middle term with the major extreme is denied—namely, that
what has the accidents of bread is true bread—it must be proved from reason,
because it does not formally appear in Scripture but only virtually.

XVII. So far is the ministry of reason from making faith doubtful that it
rather greatly assists and establishes its knowledge and certainty. Only
distinguish reason in the abstract and in the concrete: reason illuminated in
a believer, or blind in an unregenerate person. It is true that blind and
false reason makes faith doubtful, but this is denied of sound and enlightened
reason.

XVIII. Although the theologian has a more perfect knowledge of consequences,
this does not prevent an uneducated person from doing the same according to
his measure, even though he is ignorant of logic and metaphysics. The light
of reason and natural logic are sufficient for him to discern natural
consequences.

XIX. In this kind of argumentation, where something is elicited through a
consequence, the following are required: 1. One of the premises, or both, must
be contained in Scripture immediately or mediately, in proper or figurative
words. 2. The consequence or inference must be necessary and evident:
necessary not only by necessity of form but also of matter, or of the
consequent, as when

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 38 | IA leaf 92 | PDF p. 92]

the consequent is deduced from the antecedent, the species from the genus, or
the effect from the cause. 3. It must be evident for the sake of assent, so
that because of the inference's evidentness we assent no less to the
conclusions than to the premises.

XX. It is one thing to speak of reasonings extracted from and according to
Scripture, which are subordinated to revelation and resolved into it; another
to speak of reasonings that oppose it. Although heretics use the latter to
confirm their errors, it does not follow that the former are not to be
admitted. The legitimate use of a thing must not be taken away because of its
abuse.

XXI. The use of consequences is not foreign to the simplicity of Christ's
disciples. For although Christ's sheep ought to be simple, they ought not to
be brute beasts but rational. Nor, because we ought to hear Christ's voice
alone, is the use of reasonings therefore hindered, provided it is employed
with legitimate restraint. Indeed, because Christ's voice must be heard, the
Scriptures must be searched and a stranger's voice distinguished from
Christ's voice, something that cannot be done without the use of reason. Thus
reasonings drawn by consequences from Scripture are so far from departing
from Scripture or adding anything to it that they merely draw out the things
that are in Scripture and put them to use.

XXII. Although Scripture is said to be perfect as the foundation of things
to be believed and done, insofar as it contains all the doctrines and moral
precepts necessary for salvation, this does not prevent it from needing both
explanation and application. As a rule it is perfect, yet its application is
needed. This application does not interfere with the perfection of the rule,
but rather proves and declares it.

XXIII. The “vain talk” that the apostle proscribes in 1 Timothy 1:6 is not
sound reasoning through consequences from Scripture. Rather, it is curious
and anxious discussion about vain things, which in verse 4 he calls “endless
myths and genealogies.” Such were not only the Gentiles' fables about their
gods and their theogony, but also the Jewish ravings about Lilith, Behemoth,
Leviathan, and other feverish Talmudic trifles of that kind.

XXIV. The abuse of reasonings must not be confused with their use, nor must a
thing considered abstractly be condemned in itself because of its perverse
application concretely. Thus many people are mistaken when distinguishing
various objects by hearing or sight, but it must not therefore be said that
everything apprehended by those senses is uncertain. Likewise, if those who
use consequences to establish the false doctrines of transubstantiation or
consubstantiation err because their consequences are forced and illegitimate,
those who use them legitimately must not therefore be condemned. Otherwise,
it would follow that even the use of Scripture itself must be condemned
because many heretics abuse it.

XXV. It is one thing to hear someone as a framer of doctrines. In this way
Moses, the prophets, and Christ alone must be heard, both with respect to the
things delivered and the manner of delivering them. It is another thing to
hear someone as a maker of instruments suitable for explaining and applying
doctrines. In this way people who use consequences can be heard.

XXVI. The principles of doctrines and of the truth of propositions are one
thing; the principles of the truth of deductions are another. The former are
drawn from Scripture, the latter from reason. And because the truth of
propositions is the chief consideration, it is considered chiefly in
arguments, and from it judgment must be made about the conclusion. Hence an
argument's conclusion will be theological because the principle of its
doctrines is such. Reason, however, is not the principle of the thing but of
the knowledge of the thing—and not so much the principle as the instrument by
which what is proved is known.

XXVII. A consequence is a work of reason insofar as it is considered formally
and instrumentally, because it is elicited through reason, but not insofar as
it is considered originally and materially, as the doctrine elicited through
consequence. Thus

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 39 | IA leaf 93 | PDF p. 93]

consequences do not belong to faith on the part of the instrument by which
they are elicited. For I do not believe because I reason in this way, but
because the Word of God says this. Nevertheless, they belong to faith on the
part of the principle from which they are elicited; for as the premises are,
so are the conclusions deduced from them.

XXVIII. Something is regarded as proceeding from the Holy Spirit either
mediately and in the branches—namely, what is elicited from Scripture by the
ministry of reasoning—or immediately and in the root, what is contained in it
in so many words. Consequences are from the Holy Spirit in the former way,
not the latter.

XXIX. Although whatever is elicited in any fashion from the Reformed
confessions and imputed to the Reformed must not be held to be their doctrine,
nor must whatever is gathered from the Lutherans' doctrine immediately be
held to be their position, it does not follow that what is legitimately and
evidently elicited from God's Word cannot be held to be God's Word, because
the reasoning is not the same. For the Holy Spirit, who searches “the depths
of God,” 1 Corinthians 2:10, is omniscient and could foresee and intend
whatever could rightly be drawn from what he said. But human beings, who are
neither omniscient nor infallible, could not know what might later be drawn
from what they said.

[Marginal reference: 2 Peter 1:20.]

XXX. When Peter denies in chapter 1 of his second epistle that Scripture is
of private interpretation, he does not mean private subjectively, an
interpretation that can be legitimately drawn by a private person from a
comparison of Scripture. Otherwise, the Holy Spirit would not command people
to read and search the Scriptures, to compare spiritual things with spiritual
things, 1 Corinthians 2:13, to prophesy according to the analogy of faith,
Romans 12:6, and to apply the Scriptures for conviction and correction, and
so forth, 2 Timothy 3:15. Rather, interpretation is understood to be private
originally, as the text has it, “one's own interpretation”; that is, a
personal and peculiar interpretation that arises from each person's own mind
and judgment and that the words of Scripture and their comparison do not
supply. Such is the right the pope assigns to himself of interpreting
Scripture as he pleases according to that infallible spirit which he makes his
own and private possession.

XXXI. For Scripture to be expounded through Scripture, it is enough that
Scripture supply the interpreter with foundations and reasons in the
preceding and following circumstances, parallel passages, purpose, and so
forth, by which he can elicit Scripture's genuine sense and demonstrate it to
others so as to reach their conscience. But the Scriptures need not say
anywhere that this passage must be explained through another, nor must the
consequence and interpretation I propose be read expressly in Scripture.
Similarly, when a jurist compares law with law, explains one from another,
and says that the emperor and lawgiver interprets himself, the emperor need
not somewhere say expressly that this law is to be explained through that
one, and these words through those.

XXXII. Although Scripture does not judge the soundness of a consequence with
respect to the truth of the connection, because this depends on the ministry
of reason, it does not cease to be the sole judge of controversies of faith
with respect to the truth of propositions. Likewise, the law does not cease
to be the judge of disputes that arise in a commonwealth even though it does
not say that the consequences deduced from it to acquit one person and
condemn another are sound and well formed. Nor has it ever been heard that
the principles of disciplines concerning real things judge the soundness of
the consequences formed from them, even though controversies are decided
from those principles—physical controversies from physical principles,
ethical controversies from ethical principles.

XXXIII. Just as hearing does not make the sound, and sight does not make the
light that it perceives by seeing, so the intellect likewise does not make
its object—the truth and Word of God that it understands by reasoning—but
equally presupposes it. But just as the intellect by reasoning causes the
truth or Word of God to be concluded, so

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 40 | IA leaf 94 | PDF p. 94]

sight and hearing by seeing and hearing cause the light to be seen and the
sound to be heard.

XXXIV. Although what no one denies and everyone immediately comprehends is
clear and evident, it does not follow that what everyone does not immediately
grasp is not clear. For many things are often obscure to many people which
could and should have been perfectly clear to those same people, either
because they do not apply due attention or because they are blinded by
prejudices. When we say that our consequences are evident, we do not mean
that no one denies them or that anyone easily grasps them without any
attention and examination. Rather, we mean that they are evident to those who
attend diligently and consider them in the proper manner, or that they are
such that anyone who does not see them cannot have a good conscience.

XXXV. If by “formally revealed” is meant what is expressly read in the Word
of God, it is certain that consequences cannot be called formally revealed.
But if it means what is elicited either in so many words, in equivalent words,
or through an evident and necessary consequence, it cannot be denied that
consequences are formally revealed and therefore rightly called the formal
object of faith.
