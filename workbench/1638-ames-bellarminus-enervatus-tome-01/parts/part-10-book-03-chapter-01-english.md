### Book Three

### On the Roman Pontiff

### Chapter I

##### On the government of the Church, controversy 3, book 1, from chapter 1 to 10

The hinge of the whole question turns upon this: Must the visible government
of the whole militant Church be monarchical? The Papists affirm it; we deny
it.

##### Bellarmine's arguments

##### Thesis I

Bell.: *The best and most useful government of all is the government of the
Church; but monarchical government is the best and most useful of all.* Prot.
1. This whole argument—in which Bellarmine consumes eight entire chapters and
part of chapter 9—is an idle and inept speculation. For no government, or any
other institution, can be called simply the best or most useful unless account
is taken of the subject which it serves by directing it toward its end.
Therefore, since there is such variety among the people to be governed, no one
government, humanly speaking, can be called absolutely the best and most useful
for all. 2. Something that appears best and most useful in its own nature can
be absent from the government of the Church—for example, that the rulers be
such as cannot err, whether the question concerns right or fact. Bellarmine
does not dare claim this for his monarch, the Pontiff. 3.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 137 | printed p. 127 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy cannot enter into a contest over which is
to be preferred to which, except in those societies that admit *archē* and
*kratos*, or ἀρχὴν καὶ κρατὸς—that is, dominion and command—in their rule. But
the visible ecclesiastical government is not command but ministry, 1
Corinthians 4:1 and 2 Corinthians 1:24. 4. The universal Church is not one
society visibly. Therefore, the one particular government of one monarch
cannot be the best for the whole Church, even if it were the best in every
particular society. For Bellarmine himself says thus in chapter 8: *If the
particular churches were not bound together with one another so as to form one
body—understand a visible and political body—each one's own ruler would
suffice for it; that is, there would be no need of one monarch.* But we assume
that they are not bound together in such a way that they form among themselves
one visible and political body. Therefore, there is no need of one general
monarch. The assumption is confirmed even from the fact that the particular
churches are themselves complete bodies, exercising within themselves all the
worship instituted by Christ and enjoying within themselves every ordinary
ministry instituted by Christ. For there is neither any worship proper to the
ecumenical Church as such, nor can any ecclesiastical minister discharge this
office toward that universal Church which Bellarmine wishes to be composed of
all the particular churches. 5. Among the Papists themselves there are not
only various doctors of great authority, but also entire academies and
national churches which deny that the government of the Church is
monarchical. For the doctors of the Sorbonne, or the theological faculty of
Paris, distinguish the state of the Church from its government and determine
that its government is *aristocratic*. They also do not hesitate to affirm
that this is *the best of all and the most agreeable to nature*. *On
Ecclesiastical and Political Power*, 1611. Moreover, in these men it is the
French Papist Church that speaks. 6. It is not ours to pronounce what
government is best and infer from this that God ought to have established it
in the Church. Rather, we must inquire from the Word what sort of government
God has established in the Church, and from that certainly conclude that such
a government is best and most useful for the Church. In matters of this sort,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 138 | printed p. 128 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

what our Savior pronounces concerning things outwardly splendid often has a
place, Luke 16:15: *What is exalted among men is an abomination in the sight of
God.*

2. Bell., chapter 9: *It is certain that the Church of immortal angels is the
pattern of the Church of mortal men,* Hebrews 8:5. *It is also certain that
among the angels there is one who presides over all the others. He who is now
called the Devil was first endowed with this dignity; in Job 40 he is called
Behemoth, and Lucifer in Isaiah 14, and in Ezekiel 28 he is said to be
surrounded by precious stones, that is, by choirs of angels. But after the
Devil's fall, St. Michael occupies his place.* Revelation 12. Prot. Certainties
of this sort are mere dreams and sure signs of a man laboring under a
monarchical fever. For: 1. Hebrews 8:5 treats of the ceremonies of the law, not
of the Christian Church as such. Those ceremonies also had Christ, not the
angels, for their pattern. Nor could it in any way happen that sacrifices and
similar observances, which regarded sin and its abolition, contained any
representation of the state of the angels. 2. The Scholastics themselves
derive the hierarchical order they posit among the angels not from
monarchical command, but from the various degrees of knowledge, office, and
actions. Thomas, part 1, question 108; Bañez on that passage. 3. Even among the
Scholastics themselves, as Bañez teaches on Thomas, part 1, question 63,
article 7, *it is not an error or any rashness to deny that the highest of all
the angels fell, because the testimonies of Scripture which can be brought
forward to the contrary can be sufficiently explained; and Augustine, On
Genesis, book 3, chapter 10, and book 11, chapter 19, judges that opinion to be
probable.* 4. Behemoth in Job 40 is a beast *eating hay like an ox*, verse 10.
Lucifer in Isaiah 14 is the king of Babylon, *the man who shakes kingdoms*,
and afterward *descends into the grave*, verses 15 and 16. Ezekiel 28 expressly
and by name treats of the *king of Tyre*, verse 12. He is also called only a
cherub, verse 19. But the Papists place seraphim, not cherubim, in the highest
order of angels. If Bellarmine takes refuge in playful allegories, he violates
the law he himself approved in *On the Word of God*, book 3, chapter 3:
*effective arguments must be sought from the literal sense alone.* 5. By
Michael in Revelation 12:7 Christ is understood, as is manifestly apparent
from

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 139 | printed p. 129 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

verse 11: *They conquered him by the blood of the Lamb.* Nor are any others
understood there by Michael's angels than the faithful, *lavish of their lives
even unto death*, in the same place. 6. The angels have no need of a vicarious,
ministerial monarch, because the principal Monarch is present with them in
heaven.

3. Bell.: *The Old Testament was a figure of the New, but in the Old there was
monarchical government. Therefore—* Prot. 1. The Old Testament, with respect
to its government, was not a figure of the New with respect to its visible
state distinct from Christ. For Christ is the body whose shadow was in that
Testament. 2. By similar reasoning someone could conclude that one temple and
place of solemn worship ought to be established in the New Testament. 3. The
high priest among the Jews was not a monarch, for he neither made new laws nor
by himself put the laws that had been made into execution. Memorable, and also
to be applied to the New Testament, is this observation of Josephus in
Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book 8: “Some entrusted the authority
of their governments to monarchies, others to the rule of a few, and still
others to the multitudes. But our legislator paid no regard to any of these;
instead, forcing the expression, as one might say, he showed the government to
be a theocracy, assigning rule and power to God.” *The Israelite Church—like
the Christian churches—was constituted neither a monarchy, nor an
aristocracy, nor a democracy, but a theocracy.*

4. Bell.: *The Church is compared to an army, a betrothed woman, a sheepfold,
a house, and a ship. But an army has one commander, a woman one head, a kingdom
one king, a sheepfold one shepherd, a house one steward, and a ship one
helmsman.* Prot. 1. Comparisons neither prove nor illustrate if they are
extended beyond that quality in which the comparison is instituted. But none
of these comparisons is anywhere employed in Scripture to declare the
external government of the whole Church. Therefore, they neither prove nor
illustrate that government. 2. It is manifestly false that there is always
one vicarious and ministerial commander of an army,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 140 | printed p. 130 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

head of a body, shepherd of a sheepfold, steward of a house, or helmsman of a
ship. Principally, however, all these belong to Christ alone. 3. The whole
Church is nowhere in Scripture called—and cannot piously be called—the Pope's
army, the Pope's body, the Pope's sheepfold, the Pope's house, or even the
Pope's ship. Therefore these comparisons do not belong to this fictitious
monarch. 4. Bellarmine cannot be excused of blasphemy for noting here in
passing *that by the one Shepherd in John 10:16 Peter and his successors can
be understood.* Moreover, it is ridiculous that he wishes only one steward to
be understood in Luke 12:24, and that *this steward is Peter himself*.
Arguments of this sort are not worthy of any serious refutation; the
consciences of their authors and the common sense of their readers sufficiently
refute them.

5. Bell.: *When Christ dwelt on earth, he administered the visible Church as
its highest Pastor and Ruler. Therefore the Church ought now also to have an
external and visible monarchical government; otherwise it would not be the
same Church.* Prot. 1. With respect to the external person he sustained on
earth, Christ did not conduct himself as a visible monarch but, for the
greatest part, as a minister, Luke 22:27; Matthew 20:28. 2. Properly speaking,
he was the external minister of the Jews alone, Romans 15:8. *Personally*,
says Cajetan on that passage, *he discharged his office with respect to the
Jewish nation and ministered to that nation alone.* 3. At that time, in
Bellarmine's judgment, the Jewish priest was the visible monarch of the
Church. Were there two visible monarchs at once, and were they so much at odds
with each other? 4. The Church can be the same with respect to its essence,
although its external government—which is an adjunct acceding beyond the
essence—is for the greater part changed.

6. Bell.: *By the same reasons with which it is proved that one bishop ought
to preside over parish priests, an archbishop over bishops, and a patriarch
over archbishops, it can be proved that one supreme Pontiff ought to preside
over patriarchs.* Prot. 1. But we subsume that the monarchical superiority of
patriarchs, archbishops, or bishops cannot be proved from Scripture by any
arguments, and thus that even every proof is lacking for the Pope's monarchy.
3. Bishops,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 141 | printed p. 131 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

archbishops, and patriarchs did not exercise that monarchical power before
they became papal—that is, either forerunners or servants of pontifical
tyranny. Wherever they do exercise it, they bear a certain image of the Pope.
Formerly they were content with a primacy of order granted by men.

7. Bell.: *The Church cannot be propagated without a universal monarch, for
no one can preach unless he is sent,* Romans 10:15, *and no one except the
highest Prelate of the Church can send those who are to preach in other
provinces.* Prot. 1. When Romans 10:15 says that no one can preach unless he is
sent, this is understood of God's sending. Cajetan says, *Supply: by God; for
no one rightly attempts to preach the things of God and of the divine counsel
unless he has been sent by God.* 2. God sends preachers into every part of the
world without a vicar, Matthew 9:38; Luke 9:49–50. Men also send them who,
moved by love and zeal for God—whether they are ecclesiastical or civil
persons—prudently bring it about that others, furnished and approved with
suitable gifts, undertake that work, with just order observed as far as
possible. Love itself sends when a just occasion coincides with a suitable
capacity for procuring the salvation of others. 3. Heretics can propagate
their errors without the Pontiff; why cannot the orthodox propagate the
truth?

8. Bellarmine: *All the faithful must think exactly the same in matters of
faith, but this cannot happen unless there is one supreme Judge.* Prot. 1. All
the truly faithful think the same in necessary matters without an external
supreme judge. In other matters it is not absolutely necessary that they think
the same. 2. The Pontiff has not yet brought it about that all Papists think
the same, not even concerning the Roman Pontiff himself and his power in
judging—any more than the ancient pontiff brought the Pharisees and Sadducees
to think the same. 3. Faith is not engendered by the command of a man who
judges, nor is its unity preserved by such command, but by the Word of God. If
unity does not follow from its use, this occurs through the perversity of
those who use or abuse it. 4. While Peter himself was still alive with the
other Apostles, various dissensions and heresies arose. The Church is
preserved, not by the power

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 142 | printed p. 132 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

or force of some visible monarch, but by the Spirit of God, lest it be
scattered by those dissensions, Zechariah 4:6.

##### Our arguments

9. Prot. Matthew 22:25–26; Luke 22:26: The kings of the Gentiles exercise
dominion over them, and so forth; but it shall not be so among you—you,
however, shall not be so. Bell. 1. *By these words the Lord does not remove
monarchy from the Church, but rather institutes it and advises that it differs
from the civil monarchy of the Gentiles. For he does not say, “You shall not
preside in any manner,” but, “Not as the kings of the Gentiles.”* Prot. 1. In
these words a comparison of dissimilar things is instituted between the kings
of the Gentiles and the ministers of the Church. Therefore, what is attributed
to kings is removed from the ministers of the Church. But nothing is
attributed here to kings except dominion and command; therefore this is what
is removed from the ministers of the Church. 2. What the sons of Zebedee
sought, and what the disciples disputed among themselves, is removed from the
ministers of the Church. But this was dominion, Matthew 20:21; Luke 22:24.
Therefore dominion is removed from them. 3. That which is directly opposed to
ministry is removed from the ministers of the Church, just as *being a
minister* is opposed to *having those who minister to oneself*, Matthew 20:28.
But this is dominion; therefore dominion is removed from the ministers. 4. In
collecting the institution of dominion and monarchy from these very words—on
the ground that a manner of presiding is prescribed to the ministers of the
Church different from the manner in which kings preside over the
Gentiles—Bellarmine surpasses his very self in sophistical shamelessness. For:
1. They can preside in another manner without any dominion, and they can also
exercise dominion in another manner—if this passage concerned dominion—without
monarchy. 2. By similar reasoning he could conclude from the first Psalm that
wicked men are and shall be happy in their own manner, because this is
affirmed there of the godly and then, in verse 4, it is added that *the wicked
are not so*. He could also conclude that divorces were made from the beginning
for any slight cause, though not in the same manner as later, because in
Matthew 19:8 Christ says, *It was not so from the beginning*; and that the
faithful perpetrate every impurity, though in a different manner from the
Gentiles, because

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 143 | printed p. 133 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

in Ephesians 4:20 the Apostle says that the faithful *did not so learn Christ*.
3. *So* marks the quality in which the comparison was instituted. But that
quality was not the manner of exercising dominion, but dominion itself. For it
is not attributed to kings that they exercised dominion in this or that
manner, but simply that they exercised dominion. Therefore the ministers of
the Church ought to be unlike kings, not in the manner of dominion, but in the
thing itself.

Bellarmine 2: *It is clearly added, “Let him who is greater among you become
the lesser, and let him who goes before”—in Greek, ἡγούμενος, a leader, a
prince—“become as one who ministers.” Therefore one leader had been designated
by the Lord.* Prot. 1. It is also clearly added in Luke 9: *He who is least
among you.* Had one man been designated as the least? 2. In Matthew 20:26–27
the meaning of this phrase is explained twice: *Whoever wishes to be great, or
first, among you.* 3. If one man had been designated as leader, how did it
happen that they so often asked and disputed who was greatest? 4.
Bellarmine's argument from the word ἡγούμενος, which signifies one who leads,
to the establishing of a leader is as though someone wished jokingly to prove
that the rector of an academy or the regent of a college is, properly
speaking, a king. But on the contrary, we collect as certain that by the very
fact that the Holy Spirit everywhere calls kings and princes ἄρχοντες, with a
title of dominion, but always designates the ministers of the Church by titles
of office and ministry—such as ἡγούμενος, which denotes the office of going
before others—by this very fact, I say, the Holy Spirit wished to indicate
that dominion belongs to the princes of the world but not to the ministers of
the Church. At least this is most plainly apparent: one monarch is not
designated by this word, because there were various ἡγούμενοι not only in
various churches, but even in one particular church, Acts 15:22; Hebrews
13:16–17. Scripture also distinguishes between ἡγεμόνα and ἡγούμενον. By the
former word, for the most part at least, it denotes a ruler, Luke 21:12, and so
forth; that word is never employed to designate a minister of the Church.

Bell. 3: *Christ truly presided and was Lord, although not as the kings of the
Gentiles. Therefore, declaring the matter by his own example, he wishes one of
his own truly to preside, but without the lust for domination found in kings,
who are tyrants for the most part.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 144 | printed p. 134 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Prot. 1. It is certain that both were in Christ: the right of dominion over the
Church, and the state of one ministering. But here he expressly sets only his
ministration before his disciples as an example, Luke 22:27; Matthew 20:28.
Let violence therefore be done to his words when the other part is drawn into
the example. 2. Christ never exercised external command, but was *as one who
ministers, in the form of a servant*. Therefore, in himself he exhibited no
dominion for his disciples to imitate. 3. What he set forth for imitation he
did not commend to some one man, but to all: *But you, whoever among you—* 4.
What Bellarmine adds concerning tyrants is in no way suitable, for what Christ
attributes to kings belongs to all kings; but Bellarmine himself does not dare
affirm that all kings are tyrants.

Bellarmine 4: *Kings leave their kingdoms to their sons by hereditary right;
the prelates of the Church do not do so.* Prot. 1. Therefore, at least in this
passage, Christ—on Bellarmine's interpretation—forbade the ministers of the
Church nothing else than tyrannically exercising their command while they
lived, or, when about to die, appointing their own sons as successors to the
same command. It is wonderful that Christ was so solicitous to forbid these
common things, yet explained them so obscurely that neither the slightest
trace nor any vestige of either tyranny or succession appears in his words. 2.
By this reasoning there will be no difference between those kings who are
elected by the votes of the people and the ministers of the Church.

10. Prot. When the Apostle represents the whole sacred ministry and government
of the Church instituted by Christ, he makes no mention of one visible monarch,
Ephesians 4:11. And when he commends unity on account of one body, one Spirit,
one hope, one faith, and one baptism, he makes no mention of one Pontiff and
monarch. Bell. 1: *The supreme pontificate is expressly set down by the Apostle
in those words, “And he gave some as Apostles,” and more clearly in 1
Corinthians 12, “First, Apostles.” For the highest power was given to all the
Apostles, but to Peter alone as an ordinary pastor who would have a perpetual
successor; to the others it was given as to delegates.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 145 | printed p. 135 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Prot. 1. If all the Apostles had the highest power, then no one of them was
monarch. 2. If Peter as Apostle was an ordinary pastor, then extraordinary and
ordinary pastor mean one and the same thing; and then all the Apostles were
also ordinary pastors. 3. If the ordinary and perpetual succession of Peter is
expressly set down in this passage, then it is contained in the very title
*Apostle*, and thus all the Apostles also have a similar succession. 4.
*Apostle* and *delegate* are the same; therefore Peter was no less a delegate
than the rest, nor, by reason of his apostleship, did he have an ordinary
succession any more than the other delegates.

Bellarmine 2: *It can also be answered that in this passage the Apostle only
enumerated the various gifts which are in the Church, not the hierarchy.*
Prot. 1. In truth, he did not delineate a hierarchy, but a *hierodoulia*—sacred
service. 2. Nevertheless, he enumerated all the grades of ministry, both
extraordinary and ordinary. 3. He enumerated only those gifts that pertain *to
the work of ministry*, verses 12–14. The ministers themselves are gifts.

Bellarmine 3: *The Pope is included in those words,* Ephesians 4:4, *one body,
and one Spirit.* Prot. 1. The question in the argument is not what is included,
but what is expressed. 2. If the Pope is included in those words as the cause
of the body's unity, then either he makes the one body without the Spirit or
through the Spirit; but both are absurd. 3. The Pope is not the cause of
spiritual and mystical unity, but such is the unity of the body through the
Spirit. 4. Christ himself is not properly designated in those words as the
cause of the Church's unity, but in verse 5: *One Lord.* Therefore neither is
Christ's vicar designated there.

11. Prot. Christ alone is the head of the Church, Ephesians 1:22 and 4:14;
Colossians 1:11. Therefore, those who add another head do injury to Christ.
Bell. 1: *The Pope is head not together with Christ, but under Christ.
Therefore no injury is done to Christ, but his glory is increased, because
Christ is head in a nobler manner, just as a king is above a viceroy.* Prot. 1.
A head under a head is not like a viceroy under a king, but like a king under a
king. 2. No subject dares to arrogate to himself the title and place of viceroy,
or to bestow it on another,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 146 | printed p. 136 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

without the king's express mandate going before. But no documents are produced
concerning Christ's express mandate for a head substituted beneath him. 3. A
king dispenses all royal things through a viceroy, but Christ as head of the
Church dispenses the influence of life, sensation, and motion in the Church by
himself, with neither the Pope nor any other head intervening, as the Papists
themselves concede. Thomas, part 3, question 8, article 6. 4. Christ is head by
reason of faith. Alexander of Hales, part 3, question 12, member 1, article 2,
section 2: *He is head by the very fact that he is believed.* Therefore, if
the Pope is head under Christ, the faith of the Church is directed toward the
Pope under Christ. 5. If the Pope is a head under Christ the head, then he
represents Christ in the grace of union, for *the grace of union in Christ is
the same as the grace according to which he is head of the Church*. Alexander
of Hales, part 3, question 12, member 1, article 2, section 1. By the same
reason, under Christ he is also that firstborn in whom all fullness dwells,
filling all things and having all things subjected under his feet, Ephesians
1:22; Colossians 1:18–19.

Bell. 2: *Many of the things that belong to Christ are also attributed to men
in Scripture itself without injury to Christ, such as light, foundation,
Pastor, Bishop, Apostle, and Doctor.* Prot. 1. They are not attributed without
Christ's express consent. They are not attributed singularly to one man. 3.
Since many other things are attributed to men in the Scriptures, while the
title *head of the Church* is nowhere throughout all Scripture communicated
to any mere man, this very fact is a certain argument that this prerogative is
singular and reserved for Christ alone. 4. Although those names that properly
refer to ministry can in their own manner be attributed to men, those that
signify the closest union of Christ and the Church—such as the names *Head*
and *Bridegroom*—neither can nor ought to be transferred to any men. 5. The
correlates of those relative names which are attributed to men are also
referred to those same men. Therefore, if the Pope is head of the Church, the
Church will also rightly be called the body of the Pope. But this is unheard
of.

Bell. 1: *The cause of this is that Christ alone is the principal and perpetual
head of the whole Church.* Prot. 1.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 147 | printed p. 137 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Principality and perpetuity are not the cause—not even an indispensable
condition—why relatives and correlatives are referred to each other.
Therefore, their absence cannot be the cause why they should not be so
referred. A king in his kingdom, as inferior to God, is not absolutely
principal; as mortal, he is not a perpetual king; and yet the kingdom is said
to be the king's. 3. If the Pope is not a perpetual king, what will become of
the Church and its unity when that head is cut off, especially if there is an
interregnum of several years, as it is agreed has sometimes happened? 2. Bell.
2: *When the Church is called the body of Christ, the word “Christ” can
conveniently be referred not so much to Christ as head, but to him as the
hypostasis of that body. In the same way, when we say, “The body of Peter or
Paul lies there,” we do not signify that Peter or Paul are bodies, but that
they are the persons whose bodies they are.* 1 Corinthians 12. Prot. 1. Here
Bellarmine flatly contradicts Paul, Ephesians 1:22–23; 4:15–16; 5:23;
Colossians 1:18; 2:19. Paul expressly teaches that the Church is called the
body of Christ insofar as Christ is its head. 2. This meaning of *hypostasis*
is unheard of, and its application is absurd. For Peter cannot be called the
head of that body which lies here, but Christ is rightly called the head of
that body of which Bellarmine wishes him to be the hypostasis. Men are the
body of the Church just as a body lying here or there is the body of Peter.
The Church is not assumed into the unity of Christ's person. In 1 Corinthians
12:12 Christ is synecdochically called the Church, which is mystically united
to Christ as to its head. Thus Cardinal Contarini on that passage: *The whole
Church and this mystery of the body are called by the name of Christ; for the
head together with the members is one and the same body.*

12. Prot. One man cannot suffice for governing all the churches. Therefore,
monarchy is unsuited to governing the universal Church. Bellarmine 1: *That
one man should govern the whole Church through many ministers and pastors
subject to him is not only possible, but also useful and convenient. Paul did
this,* 2 Corinthians 11:28, *and so did each of the Apostles.* Prot. 1. God
never called any ecclesiastical ruler or pastor to feed or govern through
others, because this is to call someone not to ministry but to dominion. For

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 148 | printed p. 138 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the power to delegate power to others belongs to dominion. But the highest
rulers and Pastors of the Church are called only to ministry, 1 Timothy 1:12.
2. If the Pope governs through others, then they do not govern and feed their
churches by their own right, but as the Pope's delegates, and thus they are
not immediately and formally God's ministers, but the Pope's. It is in no way
apparent that Peter governed all the churches through the other Apostles and
the remaining ministers. Paul had been laboring a long time before he had any
dealings with Peter, Galatians 1:17–18, and afterward he discharged his
ministry as Peter's associate, not his delegate, chapter 2, verse 9, employing
an equal right and efficacy of calling with Peter himself, verses 7–8. 4. When
Bellarmine affirms that the monarchical government of the Pope ruling through
others is *useful and convenient*, it cannot be denied if he means the
advantage of sordid gain accruing to the Pope by this arrangement; but the
benefit, fruit, and edification of the Church require that all Pastors attend
to their flocks themselves. 5. Paul had solicitous care for all the churches,
but he did not govern each and every one either by himself or through others.
For then it will follow that Paul was bishop of the Roman Church and governed
it through Peter and his successors. If having solicitude is also governing,
then Paul was ruler and Pastor of Peter himself. For he had great care and
solicitude that Peter walk with a right foot and without offense, Galatians
2:14. And such was the solicitude of which Paul speaks in 2 Corinthians 11:28,
as is apparent from what follows: *Who is caused to stumble, and I do not
burn?* 6. If each of the Apostles did the same, then all were universal
monarchs and at the same time obeyed various monarchs. To what end is there so
much confusion in the best government of all?

Bell. 2: *God gave one man empires greater than the present Christian world;
at one time he willed that nearly the whole world obey one man. Moreover,
ecclesiastical government is easier than political government.* Prot. 1.
Bellarmine never seriously reflected with the Apostle in 2 Corinthians 2:16:
*But who is sufficient for these things?* 2. In anger God often gives one king
to many peoples. 3. He gives kings the command by which they can create

13. Prot. 1 Corinthians 3:22: Paul, Apollos, and Cephas are yours. Bell.: *I do
not perceive the force of the argument.* Prot. With respect to the present
case, the force of the argument consists in this: the Corinthians are warned
by this argument not to depend upon any man as their lord, because all are
equally their ministers, Cephas no less than Apollos. Therefore, no man is lord
or monarch of the Church. Bellarmine: *It does not follow that because the
ministers were instituted for the benefit of the Church, the Church is
therefore in authority above the ministers.* Prot. Nor do we conclude this
from it. But if, as ministers, they so serve the Church that one must not
depend upon them, then none of them is the Church's monarch.

14. Prot. Christ sent all the Apostles equally, John 20:21. Bell.: *In those
words one is not placed above another. But in John 21:16 it is said, “Feed my
sheep.”* Prot. If in the sending one was not placed above another—*I send
you*—nor in the communication of the Holy Spirit—*Receive the Holy
Spirit*—nor in the power of remitting and retaining sins—*Whose sins you remit
or retain*—if in none of these things treated in John 20 was one placed above
another, certainly *feeding the sheep* is not the privilege of one above the
others. For all were sent to feed the sheep; they were given the Spirit for
this end; and to remit and retain sins is itself to feed.

15. Prot. Besides these arguments which Bellarmine collects from our men, many
others can and are customarily brought forward by which this monarchy is
overthrown. We shall add only one, which will have the force of the most
effective demonstration among all people of sound judgment. If God had
established in the Church such a visible monarchy as the Papists depict and
thrust upon us, then without doubt he would have accurately and clearly
described in the New Testament all those things necessarily required for such
a government and state. The reason for the consequence is

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 150 | printed p. 140 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

manifest: 1. From the wisdom of God, which is exercised in revealing and
explaining things according to their importance, so that he diligently takes
care that the gravest and most useful things become especially well known to
the Church. But if we believe the Papists, that monarchy is of such importance
that without it the Church cannot be safe, the Word of God cannot be certain
and useful to men, and Christ himself cannot obtain a kingdom on earth. 2.
From the examples in which this proportion has been diligently observed. For
Christ is described most accurately in both Testaments, so that almost nothing
is omitted that some inquisitive person could desire. Likewise the high priest
in the Old Testament is presented, together with everything pertaining to
him—even those minute details of garments and similar circumstances—in such a
way that he must necessarily become known to all; nor was there ever anyone
among the Jews so stupid that, if he read the books of Moses, he did not at the
same time understand that the high priest was established by God and what he
was like. But the pontifical monarch far surpasses the ancient Jew, and with
respect to the power he has in the Church of God he is of the same order as
Christ himself acting on earth. Yet: 1. In the whole New Testament neither the
name nor title of a monarch or of any ecclesiastical and visible monarchy is
found. 2. The seat of this command is nowhere designated, unless perhaps under
the title of the seat of that great Whore and of the Antichristian Beast in
Revelation. 3. Nothing singular is noted either to be performed by this
monarch alone, or to be rendered by others to him alone. 4. No insignia of
this majesty are handed down by which it can be recognized. 5. No certain
order of succession in this command is designated. 6. No grades of
subordination to be derived from this monarch are declared. 7. No exercise or
practice of such command is narrated in the whole New Testament. Therefore,
it can be concluded with the greatest certainty that a monarchy of this sort
was not instituted by God.
