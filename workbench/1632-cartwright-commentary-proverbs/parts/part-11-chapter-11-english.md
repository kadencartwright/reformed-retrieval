CHAPTER XI.

Verse 1. False balances are an abomination to Jehovah, but a perfect weight is acceptable to him.

Balances of deceit (or deceitful balances) are an abomination (or are supremely to be abominated) to the Lord; but a perfect stone (is) his pleasure (or that which he supremely wills and desires).

The sense is that God is supremely displeased if instruments established by God through human industry for the convenient exercise of right and justice among people are perverted through fraud and result in harm to those same people. By contrast, those who use them uprightly and lawfully do a thing most pleasing to God.

Here, by the word *balances* and *stone*, by synecdoche, he understands not only weights but measures of every kind, whether of liquids or

[1632 witness | PDF p. 126 | printed columns 226–227]

dry goods, whether the quantity of the things is continuous or discrete. Nor indeed are weights alone understood, but also the very things that are weighed, so that they may be of that price and quality at which they are valued by both sides in conducting commerce. This rule is violated not only when people are deceived by unequal weights and measures, but also when wares are defective, spoiled, or in some way worse than they are simultaneously valued and advertised by the contracting parties. Nor does it include only those things transacted among people solely by measures or weights, but also those that pass not by weight but by stamps and impressions, such as money, which was formerly weighed but is now struck with a fixed image for the sake of greater expedition. Moreover, what is affirmed of balances in the former half-verse must likewise be understood of the stone in the latter half-verse. Conversely, what is predicated of a perfect stone in the latter half-verse must be understood of a perfect balance.

This invention, so useful and so necessary to human beings, is a singular benefit of God. By it people conduct commerce among themselves for their mutual and reciprocal advantage, so that each contracting party may supply what it lacks and neither may be exposed to deception or the danger of fraud. For since the exchange of wares for wares and of services for services would be very inconvenient because of the inequality of the things themselves, money was invented for this purpose: by removing the inequality arising from the disparate price of things, it would establish equality together with equity. From this came weights and measures by which as much of the sought and desired commodity would be received as the money for which the commodity was exchanged was customarily reckoned to be worth among those who engaged in commerce. The greater and more divine this benefit of God, the giver of all good things, is, the more grave and atrocious is judged the crime that deprives people, in the common use of life, of so notable a beneficence of God—and not merely deprives them, but further afflicts them with serious harm.

When he says that balances are תועבת (*abominations*), there is a metonymy of the adjunct for the subject. For neither balances nor a stone are an abomination to Jehovah, since they are good creatures of God. Rather, he means people and all human actions that drive a neighbor into fraud. For if among human beings God punishes only those who sin against him—as he answers Moses, who wished the punishment for his people’s sin to be transferred to himself—much less does God transfer his wrath from offending human beings to inanimate things. But he uses this form of speech both to declare the greatest possible detestation of this fraud and to intimate that weights and measures of this kind must be broken and removed from their midst. It must be noted that people, and so forth, are not only hated by God on this account, but are also to be abominated. Nor was this sufficient for the Prophet unless he affirmed that people of this kind were abomination itself, that is, supremely to be abominated. For since by these frauds they dissolve the common fellowship of life, and do so under the appearance of justice, the result is that they sin more gravely against God and against the fellowship of human life than those who seize people’s goods either by secret arts or by force. For just as injustice or avarice committed under the appearance of religion is judged more execrable—as when God complains that the temple has been made a den of robbers—so it also happens when one’s neighbor is violated under the name and pretext of right and justice, so that the place of right and justice becomes a hiding place in which plunderers and predators conceal themselves. To this is added that this injustice is hardly committed without falsehood and lying. Add that whereas other thieves rob one or another person, these strip innumerable people; and whereas

[1632 witness | PDF p. 127 | printed columns 228–229]

those who understand the calamity of being robbed by others attempt to repair it, these people remain secure while robbing. To this is added that the poor, whose interests ought especially to have been considered, are plundered most of all, since by their poverty they are compelled to buy everything in small portions. The rich, by contrast, buy everything together and at once, at the most convenient place and time and in the most convenient frame of mind.

Furthermore, it must be noted that in this passage a tacit antithesis between God and human beings is intimated. It is as though the Prophet were saying: this sin is indeed regarded as light among people—or rather as a joke—since they account frauds employed in commerce of this kind a certain cleverness and shrewdness, and they do not take action against those who offend in this way. Indeed, so great an impunity generally prevails everywhere here that people do not hesitate to boast of their tricks. For what Solomon declares concerning the buyer—that while buying he belittles the wares and disparages them into contempt, but after returning home with the thing bought he praises his good fortune and his cleverness in deceiving the seller, Proverbs 20:6—the same must be understood of the seller. While selling his wares, he extols them with the highest praises; afterward, however, before his servants or wife he will not hesitate to confess his falsehood in lying. But let us remember that we must not stand here upon human judgment but upon God’s judgment. These frauds ought not to be regarded with less hatred merely because people are accustomed to be only slightly inflamed against these deceptions.

In proportion to the abomination with which God is moved against people of this kind, punishment will be inflicted by the just judge. For just as people punish with bitter penalty those things that are exceedingly hateful and hostile to them, so God [punishes] the things that are hateful to him. Hence the Apostle says, “Because of these things the wrath of God comes upon the children of disobedience,” Colossians 3, and especially 1 Thessalonians 4:6. God punishes this depravity with poverty, Proverbs 10:2, and does so not merely by cursing the things acquired, but further by bending the minds of others, in his just judgment, to practice a similar fraud against the offender. Thus Christ confirms in Matthew 7, “With what measure,” and so forth. But he also punishes deceivers of this kind with eternal destruction. This happens all the more insofar as people are found to be more remiss or lax in punishing these sins.

One sins more gravely, however, when judgment in the gates is corrupted by gifts or other depravities, when the scales of judgment incline more heavily toward one side. For since right and judgment are not a human invention but something God has engraved upon human souls, departure from it is thereby all the more foul. Most foul of all is when ministers of the word do not employ just weights but follow respect of persons in teaching or exhorting, and do not pursue the duties—or departures from duty—of a prince just as they do those of a subject, or of a master just as those of a servant, and so forth. If there are some who, led by zeal for justice, hate these frauds, let their hatred drive us toward hatred of this injustice. How much more must we do so when we understand that it displeases the almighty God? For there is certainly not enough power in the hatred of mortals to confirm us in detestation of this injustice unless in our soul we conceive God as a most hostile enemy to this kind of injustice.

In very many proverbs, when opposites are comprehended in one and the same verse, it happens that both parts of the verse furnish one another with mutual and reciprocal proof. This is done by the Holy Spirit so that we may more easily comprehend a truth that we grasp with difficulty in one of the opposites when we behold the truth as more obvious and exposed in the other opposite. For example, the saying that a wicked person is rescued from death does not seem so atrocious and grave a crime to us; but

[1632 witness | PDF p. 128 | printed columns 230–231]

to punish an innocent or deserving person with death is hateful and odious to everyone. From this it is proved that the other opposite must likewise be regarded with hatred.

Verse 2. When pride arrives, disgrace comes; but wisdom is among the modest.

As honor and glory follow submission of soul, so foolishness preceded pride. For just as in the latter part of the verse honor, the opposite of disgrace, is understood as the effect of submission, so from the former part of the verse foolishness, the opposite of wisdom, is understood as the seed and cause of pride.

Pride is a vice of the soul by which we think something greater about ourselves than truth allows. In the former part of the verse a personification seems to be employed, which is further adorned by an interrogation. For he attributes to pride and disgrace the character of travelers—or rather runners—who contend in a race over which will prevail in speed. Here, although pride sets out on the road first, the disgrace following it so accelerates its own course that it catches and overtakes it. See Joshua 22:21: “Today you will sin; tomorrow you will be punished.” For however much pride, stirred up as though from hell out of our depraved nature, proceeds at the most strenuous and swiftest pace, disgrace sent from heaven, Romans 1:18, does not yield to it in speed. Nor is it any wonder, since Scripture affirms that God resists the proud, 1 Peter 5:5, which is said καθ’ ὑπεροχὴν ἢ καθ’ ἐξοχήν (*by preeminence or by excellence*). For God resists all sinners, but especially the proud. See Isaiah 2:11–17, where the end of this abasement is also noted. There is indeed a disgrace that is born together with pride itself, like its twin, and is conspicuous to the eyes of the godly and the angels, since the very sin in which they boast, Philippians 3:9, cannot be free from disgrace and dishonor. But in this passage the Prophet speaks of that disgrace which the wicked recognize and which the person puffed up with pride himself perceives. The Savior speaks of it in Luke 14: “He who exalts himself will be brought low.” This abasement, taking its beginning here on earth, will afterward be completed in the very lowest gulf of hell. Although this course of disgrace is always swift and unimpeded, it nevertheless admits degrees of speed. For God, according to his inscrutable judgment and incomparable wisdom, inflicts punishment and vengeance now more slowly, now more swiftly. He endured Haman for some time; but Nebuchadnezzar, at the very moment when he was magnificently exalting himself, he drove out into the field with wild beasts and brute animals, Daniel 4:27. The same must be understood of Herod, Acts 12.

No pride, however, is more deadly or more hateful to God than when people rise up against God himself by justifying themselves. Of this kind is the Pharisee’s conduct written in Luke 18. The same is practiced by our Pontificians, whose self-exaltation is more wicked than the Pharisee’s boasting, since they attribute to themselves the glory of free will that the Pharisee refers to God. Likewise, they ascribe false worship to him, abandoning the genuine; it is clear that disgrace hastens with the greatest speed against them. Joshua 22. Solomon identifies foolishness as the source and fountainhead of this pride, as is gathered from the opposite, just as was said above. For from ignorance of himself and ignorance of God such a person takes courage and spirit. Here it must be noted that Solomon especially charges with foolishness the person who regards himself as wisest of all. What is commonly said in our language harmonizes with this saying of Solomon: “He is a proud foole” (“He is a proud fool”). The remedy for this sin or foolishness must be sought in Romans 12, where the Apostle admonishes μὴ ὑπερφρονεῖν παρ’ ὃ δεῖ φρονεῖν· ἀλλὰ φρονεῖν εἰς τὸ σωφρονεῖν (*not to think more highly than one ought to think, but to think with sober judgment*),

[1632 witness | PDF p. 129 | printed columns 232–233]

namely, that no one should arrogate more to himself than the matter itself allows or than he himself has received. Next is that saying in 1 Corinthians 4:7, that one should not boast or exalt himself in things received as though they were his own. Furthermore, having clearly perceived the gifts bestowed upon himself, he is blind to both his own defects and his vices. Finally, passing over the virtues of others, he considers their blemishes or tumors more attentively.

The humble person is one who thinks moderately about himself in proportion to the gifts received from God, and so forth—the things contrary to those above. And this is a certain principal wisdom given from heaven, however much people place so notable a virtue among things to be reproached. Nor is it merely a virtue, but it is as it were the basis of virtues and the mother, as it were, of the other virtues, Ephesians 4:1; 2 Peter 1:5; James 4 and 3:13, 17. Honor and glory accompany it. Thus Christ celebrated it in a proverb: “He who humbles himself will be exalted.” Furthermore, since wisdom is joined with humility, and wisdom makes a person’s face shine, as Solomon relates, it follows that we ought to judge that the person submissive in soul will be clothed with honor and glory. See the parable in Luke 14. An example is proposed in David, Psalm 131, and in Abigail, who, considering herself unworthy to wash David’s feet, was raised to fellowship with the king upon his throne. But the clearest example of all appears in Christ, Philippians 2. Because it is also proposed to us in the same place and in Matthew 11, it takes away our excuse.

Verse 3. The integrity of the upright leads them, but the perversity of the treacherous devastates them.

This opinion has prevailed among everyone and indeed remains deeply fixed in us and in our depraved [nature]: that the person who conducts himself uprightly—who is the same within and without, in soul and mouth, who speaks what he feels in his soul, and who neither simulates nor dissimulates—casts himself into the midst of the danger of every evil; and that one lives nowhere more safely than if we do not conform the course of our life to the prescription of the divine command but instead conform our life and conduct to the character of customs, times, and places. By this proverb Solomon removes and uproots from us this error, the parent of all impiety and depravity, when he teaches us thoroughly that by a plain and simple manner of acting we attain the desired condition of life, but by a crooked, winding, and treacherous manner of life we are hurled headlong into destruction.

This opinion, which has entered everyone’s mind and driven deep roots in their souls, may seem also to acquire strength from the testimony of Scripture. Isaiah 59:15 confirms this very thing when it says, “[He who departs from evil exposes himself to plunder and robbery.]” But it must be understood that a people of monstrous depravity is described there, one restrained by no barriers or bounds of either humanity or godliness, but which, with a flood of evils as with surging waters, overflowed every bank of right and honor. Consequently, such monstrous depravity does not shake this doctrine, so that it remains safe and intact. It must especially be observed that there it is indicated what the zeal of flagitious people attempts to bring about, but here what is accomplished by God’s singular care and love toward the godly. These two things agree perfectly with one another.

From this another difficulty also springs up. One may rightly ask why the Prophet does not instead say that God preserves the godly by their integrity and likewise overwhelms the wicked by their perversity. For in the form he uses he appears to attribute rather too much to human uprightness for preservation, or to depravity for destruction. The answer is that this is done with a definite purpose: namely, so that solely from a person’s integrity—even though no safeguards for preservation are present, while a thousand devices for destruction are—we may understand this much concerning God and the godly—

[1632 witness | PDF p. 130 | printed columns 234–235]

that he preserves them safe and unharmed, but breaks the wicked, even though their life is fortified by many defenses and not even the smallest cloud of harm appears to threaten them. This way of acting is compared by James to a royal road.

This proverb confirms generally what is said in Matthew 7: “With what measure you measure,” and so forth. The Apostle Peter, in 1 Peter 3:10, notably both confirms the first part of this passage and illustrates it from Psalm 34:10, 11, and 12, but especially in verse 13, where he presents it as something strange and incredible if anyone should be so alienated from every sense of humanity that he would wish to harm a person of peaceful and gentle disposition. This is contrary to what people maintain when they distrust that they can be safe among wolves—something not unlike what is tossed about in our proverb, “it is better to be a shrew then a sheepe” (“it is better to be a shrew than a sheep”)—unless they howl with them. See how Jacob’s simplicity dissolved Esau into tears, and David’s integrity did the same to Saul. But if human depravity should exalt itself so far that people harm the undeserving, or even the well-deserving, nevertheless “blessed are you if for righteousness,” and so forth. For this to happen, one must walk before God always and continually, Genesis 17.

That the wicked person advances by his winding methods of action only toward his own destruction is confirmed by what is found in Job: “Who has provoked God and succeeded afterward, or who was thereafter permitted to be at peace?” Since, therefore, God’s wrath is summoned by living wickedly and craftily, even if fraud turns out well for such people, it is vainly regarded. This is worn smooth by the common proverb, κακὴ βουλὴ τῷ βουλεύσαντι κακίστη (*evil counsel is worst for the counselor*). God catches the crafty in their own counsels as in snares, Job and 1 Corinthians. The truth of this is confirmed not only by countless examples of cunning people who perished by their own devices. Of this kind is Saul, upon whom the crafty counsels undertaken against David recoiled when they served either to preserve or to ennoble David, and so forth. It is also confirmed by examples of godly people who feared God and who, by indulging themselves in their crooked counsels, would have contrived their own destruction had God not removed it. This may be seen in Abraham’s lie, by which he would have prostituted his wife’s chastity, and in David’s dissimulation before Achish the Philistine, by which he pretended that he could scarcely bear not to have been admitted to battle against the Israelites, had God not wondrously freed each from the difficulties in which he had entangled himself. This must not be understood as though wisdom and prudence in conducting affairs are to be cast away. Therefore, to conclude this whole passage, let us always value the Savior’s command, “Be simple as doves.” By contrast, let us trample underfoot what is celebrated in Machiavelli’s proverb, that a fox’s skin must be sewn onto a lion’s hide.

Verse 4. Wealth does not profit in the day of fury, but righteousness delivers from death.

A proverb similar to this is found in chapter 10, verse 2. Therefore, passing over the respects in which they agree, we shall touch only upon the things peculiar to this passage. This is its distinctive point: whereas he had earlier said that treasures of wickedness profit nothing at all, here he confirms universally that riches—whether acquired rightly or otherwise—do not profit in the time of overflowing fury. Therefore, lest anyone seize from the preceding proverb the hope that, although riches badly acquired vanish into smoke, those honestly acquired will nevertheless be like a certain citadel in which one may safely take refuge when affairs are afflicted, the answer is that they will be of no profit at all in the time of overflowing wrath. It is as though he said that they can profit when affairs are peaceful or only lightly disturbed, but that when a tempest has arisen help and support are awaited in vain. Let us consider what kind of situation this is. If someone, having violated another man’s wife, should offer

[1632 witness | PDF p. 131 | printed columns 236–237]

to the husband, who rightly loves her, an immense or infinite quantity of money, so that by reconciling the husband he may escape the just and due punishment for adultery, the husband will nevertheless reject it, Proverbs 6:35. The same applies to the avenger in a murder. If a king has become enraged against someone, the prince’s wrath is redeemed by no price. It is said of the Chaldean soldiers in Isaiah 13:7 that they will hold gold or silver in contempt as a ransom for life. See an extraordinary example in 2 Kings 24, where it appears that riches served as bait by which the Jerusalemites were drawn into captivity, while the poor were permitted to remain at home in their native soil. Since this is true, what will happen when almighty God boils over with wrath? Riches can be of use to a person such as yourself—even to a king who is otherwise exceedingly wealthy—but they cannot be of use to God, to whom belongs all the gold and silver there is. The day of overflowing divine wrath is manifold. One such day occurs when God has set before a person’s eyes, as in the mirror of conscience, all his sins in order and divided, as it were, into fixed classes, and has at the same time subjected to his contemplation the punishment due to them depicted in living colors. But the universal and equally dreadful day of overflowing divine wrath is especially the day of death, which Solomon appended by way of example, for no day in this age is more terrifying to the wicked. See Psalm 49:8, 9, 10. From this follows the Psalmist’s exhortation that, if riches abound, we should not set our heart upon them, and the Apostle’s in 1 Timothy 9, that we should not place hope in uncertain riches—a matter in which it is easy to sin. We must likewise beware, on Christ’s authority, that while greedily pursuing the world’s riches we do not meanwhile cast aside the care to grow rich in God.

Verse 5. The righteousness of the upright will direct his way, but the wicked person falls by his own wickedness.

This proverb is not dissimilar to the one premised in the third verse of this chapter. Though it is varied with different words, it seems to have precisely the same sense. If it should perhaps be asked in this connection how it happens that the Holy Spirit, who everywhere—and especially in the Proverbs—strives after brevity and concision, repeats the selfsame thing rather frequently, the answer is that here, if anywhere, what the poet sang is true: “Repetitions will please.” For just as doctrines that possess singular usefulness and importance for salvation above others are frequently reiterated and set forth again throughout the whole body of Scripture, so the same counsel of God shines in each of the prophetic books, and for this very reason in the Proverbs. For just as a stylus is applied repeatedly to hard and resistant metal so that an impression not lightly fading away may be engraved, and as a plow is drawn repeatedly through harder earth so that a deeper furrow may be prepared to receive the seed, so the Holy Spirit, having discovered our perversity in this matter and our hardness of soul, repeats this doctrine frequently, so that by doubling the doctrine this evil of hypocrisy may at last be subdued. Yet this repetition is not without interest and profit if anyone looks deeply into these things. First, it teaches that not every kind of righteousness delivers, but that which flows from a sincere soul. Next, because he says that the righteousness of the upright makes his way straight, whereas he earlier appointed integrity as the guide, as it were, of the way, it is gathered that so far is anything from being taken away from or lost to a person by a sincere manner of acting that, even if the way was previously crooked through human injustice and ignorance, nevertheless by simple and sincere conduct the way that was previously oblique and winding is made straight. From this it is also gathered that the way of sincere conduct is made easy and unencumbered: just as a straight way, drawn to a straight line, is completed without any trouble or hesitation, no

[1632 witness | PDF p. 132 | printed columns 238–239]

differently does it happen with the person who walks uprightly, to whom God’s secret counsel lies open. The person who orders his life contrariwise partly puts out his own eyes by continual dissimulation, when what he earlier did while conscience resisted him from its knowledge of the truth, he afterward admits as lawful and permitted by God’s word; and partly he is blinded by God’s most just judgment, “[which] takes away even what the one who does not have possesses.”

Verse 6. The righteousness of the righteous will deliver them, but the treacherous are caught by [their own] misery.

This is almost of the same kind as the third and fifth verses, although a distinction seems to stand out in it: the preceding proverbs contained praise only of sincerity, that is, of a particular virtue, whereas this one commends righteousness as the whole aggregate of all the virtues. Further, the preceding proverbs designate the advantages that come to the possessors of sincerity, but this one the disadvantages from which righteousness rescues them. Since *righteousness* is taken in several senses, one is the cause and, as it were, the tree—namely, the righteousness of Christ imputed to us through faith; the other is the effect and, as it were, the fruit. The latter is generally spoken of here in the Proverbs, and it is introduced as an avenger and champion rescuing us from the evils to which we are liable in this age. For since human life, and especially the life of the righteous, is exposed to many miseries, it is worthwhile always to have a remedy and cure at hand. Most people seek this remedy in riches, others in honors and authority, others in friends, and others in all these defenses of our life; very few seek it in a blamelessly lived life. Yet this alone, just as it casts us into many dangers, delivers and rescues us from them all. By contrast, riches, and so forth,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 133 | printed columns 240–241]

drive us into many evils of both soul and body, but cannot rescue us from them. Therefore, let us not fear that righteousness may be οἰκοφθόρος (*a destroyer of our household*) to us. For it does not so certainly summon misery as it will deliver us from the miseries that are brought upon us, whether for righteousness’ sake or for the sake of something else.

It must further be noted that each person’s righteousness frees that person himself, and sometimes others as well, as in the cases of Noah, Samuel, Job 22:30, and Daniel. This, however, must be restricted to deliverances in this age, not to those of the future age as the Pontificians wish. For just as no one is righteous except by his own faith, so no one will escape by anything but his own righteousness the judgment of God that will be exercised in the next age.

Verse 7. When a wicked person dies, expectation perishes; even the strongest hope perishes.

By the words *hope* and *expectation*, that which is hoped for and expected is understood metonymically. Otherwise this saying would make no distinction between the upright and the wicked, since hope is bounded by life and since faith and hope are contained within the narrow limits of this present life alone, whereas love is a companion also of the future life. “[For hope that is seen is not hope; for what someone sees, why does he hope for it?]” Romans 8.

The truth of this proverb will then appear clearly if we consider what is the object of the wicked person’s hope. It generally lies in things that are fleeting and subject to corruption. Of this kind are riches, honor, length of life, pleasures, and so forth. Since all these things are bounded by the span of this life alone, those who have departed from life must immediately fall from their hope. For if the godly are deprived by the intervention of death of the goods of this age, Job 7:7, the wicked cannot enjoy them after death.

The wicked frequently do not obtain the things they hope for, but their eyes are worn out by expectation and are pierced, as it were. If hope drawn out and deferred is sickness to the soul, Proverbs 13:12, how much more is it so to be frustrated altogether, Job 11:20. Thus the Jews expect the kingdom with great pomp and display. Sometimes they not only fall from their hope, but further fall into evils contrary to the things they hope for, as happened to Absalom and Adonijah, and likewise to Sisera’s wife, Judges. But if they obtain the things hoped for, nevertheless their hope perishes with the arrival of death, at the expiration of their soul or breath, Job 11; see Luke 12:10. Even their strongest hope is sometimes intercepted by death, as happened to the Israelites who, when they had already been brought to the borders of the land of Canaan and were even on the point of touching them, were sentenced to forty years’ wandering in the wilderness, whereby they were also deprived of its possession and utterly rooted out. What is more, the quail that they were already chewing between their teeth, and that in their strongest hope they had devoured, were snatched from them by death. By contrast, because the godly do not so readily cherish hope concerning these fleeting goods but are prepared for either fortune according as God’s will ordains, they are not frustrated in their hope and so do not fret in their souls. But among the wicked are found very many who not only promise themselves in this life that all things will be prosperous and desired, but further refresh themselves with the hope and expectation of future happiness in heaven. Indeed, you may see many who will repeatedly declare and profess themselves to be fortified with an apparently greater confidence in the kingdom of heaven than those who are truly faithful, and who consequently proclaim that it is a very slight matter to cherish a hope of this kind in one’s breast. But this hope, supported by none of God’s promises, vanishes at least with death. For if you ask whence they conceive this hope, they will answer that God is merciful, paying little attention to the fact that his mercy is toward those who fear him. Just as the rich deceive themselves in this way, so the poor have confidence and hope that because they are exiles on earth, they will be citizens in heaven, however unchaste and incontinent a life they lead. Others conceive hope from the fact that God heaps earthly advantages upon them, as though the heavenly inheritance were equivalent to the earthly. They likewise place great hope in their repentance at the very moment of death, forgetful that repentance is God’s gift and does not lie within our power or choice. Some also persuade themselves that they will merit life by their own works. Others think that by the works of other people, undertaken and performed after their death for their benefit, they can be exempted from eternal death. All of these not only fall from their hope at the advent of death, but will receive things utterly contrary to those they expect. From this the Psalmist gathers in Psalm 49:17, 18 that we ought not to be troubled or moved when all things flow according to the wicked person’s wish.

The godly, therefore, cast the anchor of hope not upon the earth but upward into heaven, Hebrews 6:19, 19. Nor do they do so rashly, by chance, or according as human reason directs, but by the sure indication of the Spirit in the word, while permitting ourselves to hope for those things that God has confirmed by the sacred testimonies of Scripture that he will perform for us. In this way it will come to pass that our hope never puts us to shame.

Verse 8. The righteous person is delivered from distress, and the wicked person comes into his place.

By the name *righteous* is understood, as was said above, the person who, having been made righteous by imputation, produces fruits worthy of repentance. By contrast, the designation *wicked* identifies the person who, abandoned by God to the stubbornness innate in us, also produces its bitter and rotten fruits. And just as the righteous person is rescued from distress by both God’s goodness and patience, so by God’s just judgment and admirable power the wicked person is substituted into the righteous person’s place. He does not, however, enter it willingly, but is, as it were,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 134 | printed columns 242–243]

dragged away by the twisted neck. When it is said that the wicked person has been substituted and put in the righteous person’s place, this must not be received as though the wicked person paid the punishments due to the crime of the godly, but as meaning that the wicked person deservedly bears the punishment that the godly person would have borne undeservedly, while the godly person will be free from evils. For it would be utterly contrary to human right for one person to pay the punishments due to another’s wickedness; much more must it necessarily be contrary to divine justice. This proverb teaches how greatly God cares for the life of the godly and how precious it is to him. Psalm 116:15 declares how precious their death is; but this passage declares how dear their life is, though to the wicked it is so vile and abject that they will generally have greater regard for a dog than for the life of the godly. See how Ahasuerus, at Haman’s single petition, condemned the whole nation of the Jews, without the cause being heard, to death and destruction, and did so without accepting any payment or being induced by hope of a payment. Even the godly themselves are restrained by the fear of God from valuing their own life too highly, lest they judge that it should be preferred either to God’s glory or even to their neighbors’ salvation. But God embraces with singular care and concern those who are despised by the world, Psalm 79:4, and not loved too greatly by themselves. Therefore, the more the life of the godly is vile to the wicked and not too dear to themselves, the more God cares for it. By contrast, “he who loves his life,” as Christ reports, “will lose it.” And since he gathers the tears of his own and stores them in a bottle, and since even the hairs of their heads are numbered so that he may avenge those tears, much more does he care for their life.

What is taught in this passage is confirmed by many examples, both from the experience of common life and from the testimonies of Scripture. Thus we read that the cross prepared for Mordecai was destined for Haman’s hanging, and that the executioners appointed for the Jews throughout all the provinces perished at the same time and in the same manner in which they had planned to kill the Jews. Thus God redeemed the life of Hezekiah and the Jews by the slaughter of the Ethiopians, Egyptians, and Sabeans. To this pertains what is celebrated by the Prophet in Isaiah 43:3, where it is also clear that God prefers his own not merely to one nation but to many, and indeed to the whole world. What is more, to rescue the one man David from death and danger, he substituted many, both Philistines and Israelites, 1 Samuel 23:27, 28. Thus Haman and all his sons were also substituted in the place of one Mordecai. Thus, in place of Daniel alone, were many nobles together with their families, Daniel 6. See Acts 12 concerning the guards and King Herod, who succeeded into Peter’s place.

The cause of so great a distinction is not placed in the godly or righteous, or in their works, but in God’s pure love, as in the fountain from which this benefit flows, Isaiah 43:4. That love of God, in turn, is derived to us from the fact that Christ the righteous, having once died for us the wicked, took our place. We must nevertheless beware of expecting this universally in the present life, but only as often as God has seen that it would serve his glory and the Church’s use. Certainly, one may hold that this happens rather frequently; but, as in all the other promises of this life, no one should persuade himself that the same perpetual and constant order of divine goodness in delivering the godly will be observed. Furthermore, this care of God especially flourishes and is declared when the righteous steadfastly and diligently persist in the ways of the Lord. Sometimes God, intending to make trial of his people’s faith—or rather to furnish others with an example of it—is said to sell them without accepting a price, Psalm 44 and Romans 8:36.

Verse 9. A hypocrite corrupts his neighbor with his mouth, but by knowledge the righteous are delivered.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 135 | printed columns 244–245]

In *mouth* there is a metonymy of the subject for its adjuncts, namely, words or speech.

In Psalm 69 the words of the wicked are compared to a sword and arrows, and elsewhere to a razor. In Psalm 120 they are compared to the arrows of a mighty man and to coals of juniper, which burn more fiercely and retain their fire longer. That they greatly harm the soul, the body, or both together is confirmed by very many statements and examples of Scripture, as in Ziba, who accused Mephibosheth before David. Next, as Paul testifies, “[evil] conversations corrupt good morals.” But of all the plagues of the tongue or mouth, none is more deadly than when people harm others under an appearance of piety or love. Solomon chiefly designates such people in this proverb, for danger does not threaten only from an open enemy or from those who are manifestly wicked. Among those who seek this present life and lie in wait for it, let Doeg be an example: he overthrew the whole family of priests by his slander while endeavoring to prove himself a faithful subject to Saul, 1 Samuel 22. Indeed, Haman would have destroyed nearly the whole nation, and that a holy one, by his slanders, while making a show of the king’s security and the kingdom’s peace in order to avenge himself, Esther 3. As for the tongue’s poisoned missiles, by which a person’s eternal salvation is attacked, although they cannot be launched without extraordinary impiety, Satan nevertheless contrives that they may seem to have been undertaken or wielded, if not from piety, at least not without piety. See in Proverbs . the harlot’s most shameless speech, in which she nevertheless mentions the vows she has paid for this reason: that she may not seem alien or averse to piety. The same must be understood concerning heresy, 1 Timothy 4:2; 2 Peter 2:2, 3. A living and express representation of this is presented in monks and priestlings, and indeed in all the Pontificians, who put forward Scripture, councils, and the Fathers as pretexts for the most shameful things.

*But the righteous are delivered by knowledge.* From this it is clear that arrows of this kind are lethal and deadly to the wicked, and that only the righteous are rescued. If you ask what, then, is that knowledge by whose skill the righteous emerge from danger, Solomon answers in Proverbs 2:12 and Proverbs 5. From both passages taken together it is established that the true fear of God—which Solomon calls wisdom and knowledge—delivers a person from the corrupt tongue both of the man and of the woman. Thus Mordecai and Esther, by knowledge of the truth and true piety joined with prudence and a circumspect manner of acting, rescued both themselves and their nation from the destruction prepared for them and their people. Thus Joseph’s piety at last shook off the accusations laid upon him by his mistress. From this it is clear that the righteous must acquire knowledge, and hence 2 Peter 1:5 counsels that knowledge be added to virtue. For people who are least malicious are led astray through ignorance and inexperience of the divine word, Romans 16:18. Therefore he sets a deliberate and understanding heart as medicine for a crafty and deceitful mouth. Although it sometimes happens that the godly are overthrown in life or possessions by false accusations of this kind, this proverb nevertheless retains its truth. First, for the most part it happens otherwise. Next, God will at last make their obscured innocence shine like the noonday. Finally, when they exchange this life for a better one, they are delivered from those evils in which Satan wished to entangle them.

Verse 10. At the good of the righteous the city exults, and when the wicked perish, singing [is practiced].

In the word *city* there is a metonymy of the subject; in the word *singing* there is a synecdoche of species.

In this proverb, as in very many others, its members furnish mutual confirmation to one another. For if the city exults at the good of the righteous, it will certainly sing over the destruction

[1632 witness | PDF p. 136 | printed columns 246–247]

of the wicked. When the righteous are adorned with the good of riches, πολυπαιδίας (*many children*), a numerous family, honor, and authority, the city will receive just material and occasion not only for inward gladness but also for exultation—that is, for declaring by outward signs and bodily gesture the joy enclosed within the soul. First, having received a good, they do not abuse it, as the wicked do, to the disadvantage of others. Second, they contribute that same good, whatever it is, to the city’s benefit and honor as well. For they do not regard only themselves and their own, but the Church first of all and the commonwealth; and not only do they earnestly desire what is best for them, but by their own effort and endeavor they labor to bring it about. Third—and this is perhaps the greatest point—by the covenant God is benevolent not only to them but to all who are joined to them in communion and the fellowship of life, both by turning aside evils that would otherwise oppress the city and by heaping up good things. An example of this is Joseph’s promotion to rule, by whose wisdom a great famine was driven away from many Egyptians and other neighboring nations. A most illustrious example of this doctrine appears in Esther and Mordecai. It appears first in Esther, at whose ennobling marriage with the king a remission and relaxation of the levy or tribute was granted to all the provinces. It appears next in Esther and Mordecai, who, after the people’s liberation had been accomplished by their work, established for the people two annual feast days on which they were to display every sign of gladness and exultation. Above all, however, God, the author of this benefit, must be praised, as he must also be in the destruction of the wicked. For the firstfruits of this exultation or singing must be paid to God in praises and thanksgiving. To this is added what is related of the city of Sodom in Genesis 18, which was to have been delivered from burning for the sake of ten righteous persons, and what is found in Jeremiah 5:1, where the Lord would have spared it if he had found only one good man. For Josiah’s sake too, the evil to be brought upon the Jews was suspended and restrained while he was alive. If one righteous man brings so much benefit to a whole city or kingdom, what will he do for one family alone, with which he is more closely joined than with an entire city or kingdom? Let this teach us how ardently we must pray that righteous persons be set over us in the family, the Church, and the commonwealth, and likewise how earnestly we must contend before God to retain the righteous whom God has given us.

The other part of the saying is drawn from the contrary effects of the wicked and from God’s curse upon them and everything they undertake. A distinguished example of this is found in Haman and the wicked enemies of God’s people, who conspired against the life of God’s people for the third day of the twelfth month. What, then, shall we say here of David, who celebrated Saul’s death in a special song? Answer: he mourned not the death of a tyrant but the manner of his death, because Saul was killed by his own soldier. For just as we ought to desire the death of tyrants, so we ought to abhor their unlawful killing by treachery or civil tumult. Next, the answer is that David performed the domestic duty of kinship, because he was Saul’s son-in-law; otherwise he does not seem likely to have done this. Nevertheless, one must carefully beware here lest our song regard ourselves, private advantage, or vengeance. It ought chiefly to be referred to the glory of God, and next to the benefit of the Church and the commonwealth.

Verse 11. By the blessing of the upright the city is raised up, but by the mouth of the wicked it is destroyed.

In the words *city* and *mouth* there is a metonymy of the subject—or rather, in the word *city* there is a synecdoche of the part, as appears from the latter part of the verse, where it is affirmed that the city itself will be demolished. In the word *blessing* there is also a synecdoche of species for

[1632 witness | PDF p. 137 | printed columns 248–249]

every kind of godly and upright speech, as is evident from the latter part of the verse, where corrupt words are opposed to blessing. By the word *blessing*, therefore, are chiefly understood προσευχαὶ (*prayers*), whether they are petitions or δοξαστικαὶ (*doxologies*). Next comes the sparing and lawful use of oaths, likewise vows, and finally familiar and customary conversation with other people, provided it is true, honorable, and seasonable.

This proverb of Solomon refutes an inveterate error deeply rooted in human minds: people imagine that words possess little weight for themselves and their souls, whether for blessing the commonwealth and Church or for rendering them miserable and unhappy. Consequently, by their laws they provide neither a reward for words well spoken nor a penalty for wicked or impious speech. They judge words, whether praiseworthy or blameworthy, no differently from a breath or light breeze that vanishes without great benefit or harm, or from smoke that a gentle wind entirely disperses. Scripture, however, values words or speech so highly that it treats them either as a cleansing wind fit for winnowing or as a whirlwind that devastates and breaks down everything. Nor does it treat speech as smoke, but rather as a fire that either refines and purifies or consumes and destroys all things. Accordingly, the Church and the commonwealth together must consider the reward or punishment of words badly and well spoken. How great the power of a godly man’s prayers is appears from Abraham’s petition for Sodom, Genesis 18, and likewise from James 3:16, 17, 18. The same is also established in the lawful use of an oath, Jeremiah 4:2.

How a city is demolished by the mouth is gathered from the contraries of blessing. Further proof of this may be sought from the custom of God’s people, who, to expiate blasphemy against God and the king, were accustomed to summon the entire city in which that crime had been committed to the sanctification of a fast, 1 Kings 21. This is especially to be considered in false teachers; see Deuteronomy 13.

Let this doctrine add spurs for us to pursue godly and honorable speech, since such words profit not only us but redound to the benefit of the entire city or town in which we dwell. The fact that by impious or wicked speech we destroy our own souls ought to restrain us and, as it were, put a bridle upon us or confine our mouth with a curb-bit. Much more, then, must we refrain from evil words when we understand that the corruption of speech creeps and spreads to the destruction of many. Indeed, a venomous tongue appears to possess a certain greater power to harm than a bloody hand, for with the hand you might kill only one man in a single moment, while with one corrupt statement you may slaughter very many. Next, when we see the wicked in such great number assaulting the city of God with corrupt words, let us also strive by godly and upright words to buttress the city of God, as it were, lest it fall or collapse. If a city is overthrown by a wanton and blasphemous tongue, what will happen to a family? Therefore David casts slanderers and detractors out of his house and drives them away, Psalm 101. The principal cause of both this raising up and this demolition is the blessing of God following the blessing of the godly, or his curse following the curse of the wicked.

Verse 12. When a madman despises his neighbor, a most prudent man plays deaf.

In the word *contempt* there is a metonymy of cause, for it denotes insulting words or deeds with which someone burdens his neighbor. This appears from the verb *to be silent*, which he opposes to this contempt; for if the contempt were kept enclosed within the mind, there would have been no praise for the person who suffers it. By the name *neighbor* is understood anyone who shares the same human nature of which he is a part,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 138 | printed columns 250–251]

who holds another in contempt. By the name *heart* is understood, by synecdoche, intelligence, as appears from the opposed member concerning prudence. Finally, in the word *being silent* there is a synecdoche of genus, for he does not mean silence universally, but only that there is no answering or repayment of slanders or insults. Someone may, consistently with the truth of this proverb, answer the insults hurled and directed at him, provided he does not repay curse with curse.

Two things are taught by this proverb. First, no one, mindful of his own frailty and of his equal condition with his neighbor, should spurn another. Second, we should avoid retaliation for insults inflicted upon us. As for the first prohibition, it has a reason attached in the word *neighbor*. By this word Solomon declares that there is so great a likeness of one person with another, so great a connection and community, that whoever spurns another, however despised and abject, must necessarily have forgotten himself. What if this man is poor and that one rich; this one heaped with honor and that one lowly; this one endowed with authority and that one a private person; this one learned and that one uneducated? Yet their connection is so great: first in creation from one and the same matter, in birth, in death, and in eternal reward or punishment—the sum of which is that he is our flesh, Isaiah 58:7—that it rightly vindicates our neighbor from contempt. First, there are more respects in which they agree than in which they differ. Next, the former things are older than the latter. Third, the things by which we are joined as though by bonds are stronger than those by which we are separated. To this is added that in whatever respect some are superior and more eminent than others, they received it not by their own merit but by God’s free gift, 1 Corinthians 4:7. Next, what Proverbs 22:2 says concerning the rich and the poor—that by the discerning disposition of God’s providence they meet for a particular good of the Church and commonwealth—must also be understood of the other distinctions mentioned above. But if they are joined not only by the bond and fellowship of human nature, but are further united in Christ by the bond of the Holy Spirit, then they are bound by the closer and more numerous ties enumerated in Ephesians 4.

It must next be noted that the Holy Spirit takes away from the contemptuous person the very quality in confidence of which he especially despises his neighbor. Since this contempt springs from an opinion of his own prudence, the Spirit denies him even its smallest tinge or faintest trace when he calls him exceedingly senseless. What is said in Psalm 15:4 admits an exception; an example appears in Mordecai, who refused to honor Haman. The other part of the proverb sets patience before us: we are to bear contempt calmly, as David did, Psalm 38:13, 14, 15. Christ the Savior enlarged this when he not only forbade repaying abuse with abuse, but further commanded us to pray well for those who curse us and rage against us. The reason for this silence is sought from the consideration of our sins, of which we are guilty before God, however little we have deserved these insults before men. Next, an insulting person is more quickly and easily pacified by silence, but provoked by retaliation. Thus a barking dog ends its barking sooner through silence than through shouting back; and mud spattered on a garment is removed sooner if you do not touch it but allow it to dry, so that it vanishes of its own accord.

Verse 13. One who goes about [with] detraction reveals a secret, but one faithful in spirit conceals the matter.

In the phrase *faithful Spirit* there appears to be a synecdoche of genus, for it is taken not for faithfulness

[1632 witness | PDF p. 139 | printed columns 252–253]

in every matter (for then the point of the proverb would be blunted), but for faithfulness in words, which is opposed to detraction. In the word *word* there is also a synecdoche of the part: a word, namely, stands for an entire statement, or the adjunct *word* stands for the underlying thing to be signified, to which the word is attached.

Since it is a great support in this life to attach to oneself a faithful friend with whom one may share counsels and the thoughts of one’s mind, this proverb teaches us what caution must be used in choosing a friend. For since it is a virtue proper to friendship to keep entrusted secrets silent, while a detractor or slanderer is a babbler who, as though full of cracks, leaks here and there, this proverb teaches that one must beware of entrusting to a slanderer anything one would wish to remain unspoken. For a person who prematurely betrays and publishes his neighbor’s failings, or who even invents and composes falsehoods to scatter among the people, cannot with any likelihood be thought capable of concealing secrets. Whoever eagerly divulges another person’s failing must be thought incapable of keeping silent even about a friend’s error or fault. Above all, if he devises lies and fictions to please others, he will much more readily blurt out true things and actual events.

Next, wherever a detractor is mentioned in the Scriptures—Leviticus 19, Jeremiah 6 and 9, and Ezekiel 22—he is there, as also in this passage, branded and scorched with the mark of an itinerant peddler. For it happens that whoever does not devote himself to the conduct of affairs wanders here and there. To this pertains the etymology of the word, which, being derived from merchants or dealers, teaches that detractors run about here and there. As the merchants buy wares to sell to others, so these people gather rumors to scatter this way and that. As the merchants mix their wares, so these people mix falsehoods with truths and make what they have heard from others worse by their own inventions. Since many sins accompany an idle person, as is stated in Proverbs 6, it is clear that detraction belongs to that troop of vices. This is shown most fully and plainly in 1 Timothy 5:13, where it is affirmed that idle women are at once wanderers or rovers and afflicted with an unruly tongue. Indeed, the matter is so ordered that those who do not occupy their hands in performing some useful work exercise their tongues in devising false and harmful things. Here another reason emerges why detractors uncover secrets. Since through idleness they are necessarily destitute of the maintenance, food, and clothing of life, in which those who seek it by the labor of their hands abound, they are compelled to supply this want of life’s maintenance from elsewhere. For this reason, if they know or even suspect someone to be ill-affected and hostile toward another, and therefore eager to hear evil about him, they either rashly blurt out something about him or devise a falsehood to relate, so that by their slanders and malicious statements they may earn a table for themselves. See the example of Delilah, Judges 16. Therefore, in order to fortify this life with firm and pleasant friendship, we must beware of detractors. And that we may be safe from them, we must shun those who are either altogether idle or expend their labor upon useless things. Meanwhile, let us remember that this is to be understood of a secret that ought to remain unspoken, for Jonathan related his father’s secrets to David. Thus some members of the royal council, unless I am mistaken, disclosed the counsel undertaken against Jeremiah and Baruch. By contrast, whoever keeps faith in words and promises will keep the pledged faith to conceal a secret, especially when he has pledged that faith to a friend. For if in all matters he has accustomed his tongue to relate the truth, much more will he do so when it is legitimately required by a friend.

Verse 14. Where prudent counsels are not [present], the people falls, but safety [is] in the abundance of a counselor.

In the association and conjunction of many,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 140 | printed columns 254–255]

who have gathered themselves into one body of a commonwealth to preserve their mutual advantage as well as benefit, Satan, the enemy of human society, devises many things by which he may weaken their communion with one another and its bond. Hence it is that in almost every age there have arisen Nimrods, Nebuchadnezzars, and Alexanders, and in our own age Turks and Spaniards, who, itching with the desire to rule, have endeavored to force others into servitude. Next, although under nature’s guidance people willingly bind themselves to one another in society for their own preservation, nevertheless individuals for the most part transfer the care of the common interest from themselves to others and devote themselves to their own private advantages and those of their families. They forget the advantages of the commonwealth to such a degree that they bring destruction upon themselves, even though they are harassed by neither the snares nor the incursions of any foreign enemies. Seeds and sparks of dissension often arise and sprout among a few citizens; unless they are extinguished in time, they gather strength and spread to the division and sundering of the whole people. Hence citizens, engaged against one another, fall by their own blows, like earthenware flasks rubbed against one another, dashed together, and shattered.

But if there are any—and surely there are few—who direct their thoughts and counsels toward the common benefit, yet, being stronger in will than in ability, they do not bring about the benefit they set before themselves; there are also occasions when want of foresight is exceedingly harmful. Brute animals preserve and sustain their community without a leader, Proverbs 30, but human society is assailed by so many snares and attacks that it cannot be safe and unharmed except under a leader, and one well instructed in counsels. From all these things it is clear what is taught in this passage: a people not governed by sound counsels falls. The surest and clearest proof of this appears in Isaiah 3, which, in announcing the ruin and destruction of the Church and commonwealth, threatens the removal of the prudent senator. The same is confirmed in Ecclesiastes 10:16: *Woe to you, land, whose king is a child.* It is likewise confirmed by the example of Rehoboam, who through the rash counsels of his young counselors lost the greater part of his kingdom and would have lost the whole, had not God by extraordinary providence reserved two tribes for him, so that the pledge given to David and the word spoken through the prophet Ahijah might stand. See further the youthful counsel given to Ahasuerus, by which he would have utterly ruined his kingdom, Esther 3.

For since scattered domestic affairs perish unless they are administered by counsel and some settled method, much less will a commonwealth be able to stand without the aid of counselors. There are some who, because they are very numerous, suppose themselves fortified against every kind of storm; although they have prepared arms, they accomplish nothing without counsel. Others devise frauds to meet dangers, trusting that by inflicting evils they can forestall them. Whereas Solomon confirms that a matter can be successfully conducted only by counsel and solid wisdom. We must therefore see that our counsels are drawn from God’s word, or brought to it as to a rule; and let what David professes concerning himself in Psalm 119 prevail: [God’s statutes were his counselors, by whose protection he affirms that he became wiser than his teachers and enemies, and also than old men taught by long experience.] See how Jeroboam perished with his whole house through his own devices and counsels, which the flesh dictated, 1 Kings 15:29.

It must further be observed that commonwealths destitute of counsel are not only weakened but torn up and rooted out; they are not only diminished but crushed to pieces; they do not merely decline and wither, but perish utterly. Therefore, since we see how momentous it is for a commonwealth to be destitute of counsel and faithful counselors, it is worthwhile to apply ourselves to prayers to this end, that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 141 | printed columns 256–257]

God may grant us wise counselors; that he may preserve them for us as long as possible, imbued with sound and salutary counsels; and that he may turn them away from the crafty and cunning inventions and fabrications of our corrupt nature, however ingenious they may appear. Finally, we must pray that princes and people together may rest in sound and salutary counsels and obey them; otherwise the commonwealth, as one destitute of counsel, will perish.

[ *But safety is in many counselors.* ] This half-verse is proved from the contrary. For if commonwealths perish through want of counsel, they must necessarily be preserved by its abundance and breadth. An eminent example of this appears in Solomon, who, although he was most wise of himself and far surpassed in wisdom all who ever were or will be, nevertheless for the happy constitution of the commonwealth chose for himself a senate selected from the wisest men. Hence it came about that the people under him enjoyed the highest peace and happiness. The excellence of his senate is evident from the history of his son Rehoboam.

Verse 15. He who becomes surety for a stranger is utterly broken, but he who hates those who clasp hands is safe.

In the word רע (*by breaking*) there is a metaphor taken from a potter’s vessel, or from some other thing which, once crushed into fragments and particles, cannot be restored. It is as though you should say: you will perish just as a broken vessel perishes, and that not only through the dissipation of wealth and resources, but also at the cost of liberty, health, and even life itself.

In the word זר (*stranger*) there is a synecdoche of species. For it does not signify only one who is unknown to me, but one whose condition is unknown, or rather anyone for whom it is neither safe nor prudent to become surety. For just as one who admits another woman besides his wife is said to join a stranger to himself, so one becomes surety for a stranger when he gives a pledge for someone to whom he is not bound by any duty of office. Further, in the word שונא (*hates*) there is a metonymy of cause; that is, by fleeing the clasping of hands he declares that he hates it. In תקעים (*pledge*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for sureties, who customarily become surety with an accompanying clasp of hands.

Many strike against suretyship and pledges as against a rock of the commonwealth and, making shipwreck of their household estate, weaken the commonwealth itself. Hence Solomon here and elsewhere raises a signal, as it were, and warns us that by sailing past this rock we may be carried safe and unharmed into harbor.

He does this all the more because many are found who, though by no means evil, are ensnared by these misfortunes. For it seems an act of humanity to present myself as surety for one who desires to employ me as his surety. When he approaches and appeals to me as surety, he seems to have done this from a persuasion of my humanity and a certain obligingness; to show yourself difficult and inexorable to him seems ill-tempered and excessively hard. Hence there are those who say they cannot refuse their petition without shamelessness, especially if the petitioners press importunately. What is more, they color this departure from duty with a paint of Christian love, as though one who refuses to become surety for another were estranged from love. But what kind of humanity is it to violate human society, and by this obligingness to exercise cruelty against yourself and those whom you ought especially to care for, when, bound by these chains, you are compelled to desert those whom God entrusted to your faith? Next, what kind of love is it that so cares for another’s advantages that it neglects oneself and one’s own? Or what sort of love is shown in helping bankrupts and prodigals, who exhaust everything, and which, in supporting the slothful and those torpid with idleness,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 142 | printed columns 258–259]

is consumed? For such are generally those who seek sureties of this kind, as is clear from chapter 6 of this book. Finally, what kind of love is it to despoil yourself and strip yourself of every good, so that you may prepare medicine for another who is incurable, or who is in debt to such an extent that he can emerge from it with neither your resources nor his own, or who, like a leech, sucks up everything he can scrape together from every quarter? Next, the Apostle in Romans 13:8 teaches that we ought to owe no one anything, as far as that is possible, except to love one another. The righteous person indeed is generous and gives much, but, as it is in Psalm 112, *he prudently orders and dispenses his estate with judgment*; for very many are found who make it their concern to put other people’s goods up at auction so that they may attach companions in misery to themselves. Others cast their friends into danger, if only by this means they may draw out the time and evade the due date, even though they wholly despair of extricating themselves from debt.

Those who sin in this direction are more easily led into this fraud because, enticed and soothed by flatteries, they suppose that they will accomplish something in this way, while acquiring friends whose assistance they may use to complete their own business. Others, somewhat more cautious than these and judging the matter not to be wholly free from danger, nevertheless decide to try their fortune because it may turn out otherwise. The wise man meets these people when he says they are to be utterly broken and dashed to pieces. It is as though he should say: you indeed reckon it a light thing to clasp hands, a light thing to subscribe a bond and affix a seal; or if perhaps you suspect that danger lies beneath, you nevertheless think that it may turn out otherwise, or, if it happens, that it will still be rather slight. For you suppose that the person for whom you interpose yourself as surety is not so estranged from all humanity that he would wish to repay your goodwill toward him by betraying you. Moreover, you imagine that the man to whom you have made yourself debtor will not be so hard an exactor as to demand another’s debt so severely from you, to whom this obligation and debt do not belong, and who have never received from him any compensation, however small, or even a cup of cold water. But the matter is far otherwise. For not only will you gain nothing from it or enter a doubtful or two-sided danger; you will cast yourself into certain peril. Therefore not only does the saying once celebrated as Apollo’s oracle hold true here, *Give a pledge; loss is near*; but this is also worthy of memory and attention, which some wisely advise: that you take from your chest the entire sum for which you become surety, set it aside, and always keep it in ready money, no less than if the obligation were your own. But the contrary seems to appear in Luke 6: *Give to everyone who asks, and do not turn your face away from one who wishes to borrow.*

These things, therefore, were not said as though every suretyship must be judged unlawful. For when he commands that one must not become surety for a stranger, he indicates that there is no obstacle where it is just and permitted—as when someone has incurred debt while suffering loss for religion’s sake, or when someone diligently and circumspectly devoted to an honorable calling is overwhelmed by some sudden storm that either could not be avoided or could not easily be turned aside. Yet caution must be used here, lest we attempt anything beyond our strength or beyond what can conveniently be furnished without weakening our own condition. We are therefore neither absolutely forbidden here nor absolutely commanded in Luke 6; we are only warned to give cautiously to others—not to strangers, but to members of the household of faith, and to those who are oppressed; and to do so rather to rescue them from extremity than to raise them up, and to do it freely.

In the latter part of the proverb he teaches from the adjunct, security, how deservedly rash suretyship must be avoided. From this it is further clear that one who has rashly become surety for another loses his liberty along with his household estate, and into hiding places and darkness

[1632 witness | PDF p. 143 | printed columns 260–261]

is thrust. He is not only banished from human society, but is also driven and expelled from God’s presence and serene countenance in the Church and assembly of the saints. The fear of his creditors continually hangs over him like a lictor or court officer, so that wherever he sees a person, he is immediately struck with fear lest it be the very creditor seeking to take him to prison. Common experience sufficiently teaches this matter.

From all these things it is evident that God has laid down for us a law for disposing of our wealth and resources: it is no more lawful for us rashly to squander them than greedily to hold them fast; rather, we must administer them prudently for God’s glory and our neighbors’ advantage, especially the advantage of those committed to our care. But if it is not right to squander our resources in relieving the miseries of others, far more must those be condemned who spend everything on tending their own skin; who make their wealth a servant to gluttony and drunkenness; and who thereby not only fail to refresh and revive others, but even destroy themselves or at least weaken their health and strength.

Verse 16. A gracious woman holds fast to honor, as the powerful hold fast to riches.

In this passage one of two kinds of grace is understood by the word *grace*: either grace of body or grace of soul. The former is not meant—namely, comeliness or beauty that comes to human beings from nature—and therefore beauty and adornment of soul are meant, which are gifts sent down from heaven by the Holy Spirit. For you may see very many women who are infamous and filled with ignorance, yet excel in bodily beauty and comeliness. Next, there are some who at first were valued for beauty and comeliness, but afterward, when that same beauty was either prostituted or lost through age or disease, remained in no honor. For *comeliness is deceitful and beauty vain*, Proverbs 31:30. Yet in this passage the gracious woman is said to hold fast to honor and not to allow it, once acquired—or rather given from heaven—to slip from her hands.

By a gracious woman, therefore, understand one who, together with the true worship of God, preserves and fosters those virtues that are especially proper to a woman—namely chastity, modesty, and gentleness—so that she is pleasing and esteemed both by God and by human beings. She cultivates these virtues, fosters their seeds infused from heaven, and takes care that they are neither extinguished nor crushed. She encircles herself with these virtues as with a maidenly girdle or belt, and adorns herself with them as with a necklace, jewels, rings, earrings, and amulets. She devotes her labor to cultivating them, so that she may strengthen these virtues and make them increase daily. Finally, she sets their retention before her eyes as an appointed goal and mark, and for this reason enters upon the ways and methods most conducive to preserving them. She adopts bodily adornment that, because of its decency and modesty, is most suitable for her in doing these things; concerning this, see 1 Timothy 2 and 1 Peter 3. She chooses companions who help her in this pursuit. She keeps watch over her labors and work so that she may strengthen herself in the cultivation of these virtues. Neither in food, nor in relaxation from her occupations, nor even in sleep does she set aside care or concern for these virtues. For either excessively elaborate or insufficiently modest bodily adornment shakes off humility, gentleness, and chastity of soul. Idleness, moreover, both produces pride and adds spurs to lust, so that women who otherwise, because of both nature’s deformity and weakness, would be unfit for intercourse, burn with desires when released from performing work. The same judgment must be made concerning the rest. In celibacy she takes pains

[1632 witness | PDF p. 144 | printed columns 262–263]

to present herself chaste to God in body and soul; in marriage, first that the marriage bed may remain undefiled, and next that she may show herself compliant, gentle, and peaceable toward her husband in all things. This woman’s virtue, however, will shine more brightly if we examine what the pursuits of women generally are. Very many spend days and nights either preserving by every possible means the beauty and comeliness granted them by God or putting on an artificial and false beauty. It is scarcely credible what great discomforts they endure in this matter: partly by abstaining from the things in which they take the greatest pleasure, and partly by undergoing those things that cause them the greatest trouble and discomfort. Yet they do not attain what they desire, nor can they attain it, whether to preserve their present beauty from corruption or to acquire by art what they do not possess by nature. How much better it would be for them to devote their labor and effort to those virtues which, although not in them, can be acquired, and which, once acquired, can be kept inviolate and incorruptible for all eternity. And if they are asked why they labor so greatly over these perishable things, they will give one of two answers: either to please others, which would be shameful, or to gain their husbands’ approval, which alone is tinged with a certain semblance of honor. And although some account must be taken of this latter consideration, so that they and their things may please their husbands, nevertheless to place either all or the principal part of their concern in these matters is not characteristic of women who *hope in God*, 1 Peter 3:5, and do not doubt that he himself will render them sufficiently pleasing and acceptable to their husbands, provided they have not been utterly careless in these things.

There are others who pursue honors and riches and turn over in their minds night and day how they may become wealthy, and so forth. Of all these it can truly be said that they are very far removed from the woman praised here. For it must certainly be maintained that it is impossible for them to hold fast both this honor and those momentary things.

By this word *holding*, or *holding fast*, the Holy Spirit indicates that there will be no lack of those who endeavor and exert themselves to snatch this honor from her; hence she too grips it all the more firmly. For it is well known that Satan is ready to lay snares for women as the weaker vessels. Since he is and is called an unclean spirit, surely he will leave no stone unturned to extort chastity and modesty from modest women. Therefore he sets snares for them and, as though they were a besieged city, surrounds them with various kinds of temptations: on this side with entreaties, on that with reward, often with enchantments of speech, sometimes with an appearance of virtue and piety or religion—as though men loved them not so much from love of their beauty as because they were adorned with piety. Hence Solomon affirms that honor is held firmly by her. He also illustrates this by the likeness of housebreakers and violent men, who are inflamed by so great a desire to increase their property that, not content with their own resources and possessions, they lay violent and rapacious hands upon the goods of others. Thus, although she is a woman and of the weaker sex, in preserving chastity, modesty, and decorum she is to show herself no less vigorous than a man, and to devote no less vigorous effort to preserving her honor than housebreakers devote violence either to guarding their own riches or to seizing the wealth of others.

From this it is further clear that it belongs to the godly to sharpen themselves all the more indefatigably for the exercises of piety, with the industry of the wicked in accomplishing crime set before them, just as in Luke 16 the foresight and deliberate manner of action of the unjust steward is praised.

Verse 17. A kind man is beneficial to himself, but a cruel man troubles his own flesh,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 145 | printed columns 264–265]

Here there is a synecdoche in the words *soul* and *flesh*, so that in both half-verses both words, *soul* and *flesh*, are to be understood.

It is deeply implanted in human nature to look after oneself and therefore to take care that some benefit from everything one does should certainly return to oneself and flow back, like streams into the sea. The Holy Spirit, well aware of this fact, confirms that this matter will turn to our advantage, in order to train us to exercise kindness.

When people deliberate, either with themselves or with others, about giving something to the poor and afflicted, the thought immediately enters their minds that whatever they bestow upon others is lost and falls away from themselves. Therefore, to blunt the edge of that temptation, it will be exceedingly useful to turn over in our minds this proverb of Solomon, by which God through the Prophet assures us not only that nothing is taken away from us by giving to and relieving the needy, but that very much is also added. Therefore, if neither God’s glory nor our neighbors’ salvation moves us to open the bowels of mercy to needy brothers, nevertheless let what is taught in this passage prevail: by beneficence toward others we will benefit ourselves. To this pertains what is said elsewhere, that those who have shown kindness and liberality to the poor lend at interest to God.

If we investigate the reasoning of this saying, what first presents itself is a soul conscious of a good work, by which we are wonderfully refreshed and restored. Next, we open the fountain of divine bounty toward us, which will wash us with the good things of both the present and the future life. For the timely distribution of alms sends a cry to heaven and to God’s ears, which God must hear, even if those who receive the alms should through ingratitude be no less mute than fish. Finally, if it should ever happen that we are left destitute, or indeed that those born and propagated from us are, God is just and faithful to stir up the minds of others so that they measure back to us with the same measure—one packed and overflowing—with which we measured out to others.

In the antithesis of the former half-verse all the contrary things are to be understood. Here it must further be noted that not only one who oppresses others and strips them of their goods is to be regarded as cruel, but also one who contributes nothing from his own possessions. Such a person, as the Apostle John testifies, closes his bowels, and so forth, and from this is convicted also of hating his brother. 1 John 3:17; Matthew 25:41, 42, and so forth.

Verse 18. The wicked man procures a false reward, but the wages of one who sows righteousness are sure.

This proverb confirms what was set forth in the preceding one. At the same time it meets a tacit objection of the godly, whose eyes are dazzled by the splendor and abundance of the wicked, just as, by contrast, the generally meager and slender condition of the godly offends them. The sense, therefore, is that the wicked advance so far that they generally attain the wealth and resources they seek, although they heap and amass them through injustice—whether they acquire them by force or fraud, by barren parsimony and excessive restraint in giving, or finally by both methods—doing so either by plundering others or by withholding from those who lack the things in which they themselves abound. They do indeed receive the reward of their labor and weariness, but because it is false and counterfeit it contains nothing firm or solid in itself. For they do not attain the goal and end they set before themselves in scraping and heaping riches together. First, they seek them for length of life, although, like cruel and bloodthirsty people, they are carried off in the midst of their lifetime by an untimely death; see Psalms 37:2, 10 and 55:24. Next, they seek them for the comfort and pleasures of life, which

[1632 witness | PDF p. 146 | printed columns 266–267]

the worm of conscience either takes away or greatly weakens, as they are repeatedly stung over things either ill-gotten or ill-kept. Thus no seasoning can be so agreeable to the tongue’s taste as the bitter thought of the injustice by which their feasts were obtained is disagreeable. Third, they resolve above all to transmit them to all their posterity, although a third heir of ill-gotten wealth can scarcely ever be found, even by searching with a lighted lamp. Finally, by accumulating riches they resolve to obtain a name for themselves for all time, but that name is either nothing or rotten, just as we learned before. From this it follows not only that the wicked person falls short of the end and goal he appointed for himself, but that everything turns out for him in a way contrary to what he proposed. So far is he from attaining the happiness he fashioned for himself in his imagination that, as the Prophet relates in Habakkuk 2, his riches become mud by which he is utterly overwhelmed. Therefore, when the godly are moved with indignation against the wicked and envy their prosperity, this happens because they look to the accumulation and possession of riches. But if they fixed their eyes on the meagerness of the fruit the wicked receive from riches, or on its momentary duration, they would never be so greatly moved. Who envies people condemned to the mines, even if they are occupied in extracting, gathering, or carrying gold from golden mines? Or who admires the happiness of those continually occupied in minting gold or silver that is soon taken from them and transferred to the use of others? Behold the matter itself: the condition of these people, who not without great torment gather, preserve, and lose their riches. For, as will be said afterward and is proved by experience, the wicked scrape and rake together riches that afterward, by God’s singular providence, devolve upon those who fear God.

In the other half-verse he teaches that the godly person, by giving here and there, gathers a reward that, although not of such splendor and outward show as the reward set before the labors of the wicked, is nevertheless firmer and of longer duration. It neither melts like wax, dissipates like smoke, nor vanishes like the fat of lambs, as happens with the reward of the wicked. He intimates the reason for this when he says that by his liberality the godly person commits seed to the earth, from which a sure harvest is to be expected, since it is seed cast into watered and well-cultivated fields. Ecclesiastes 11:1. See chapters 8 and 9 of the Second Epistle to the Corinthians. Let the woman of Zarephath, 1 Kings 17, serve as an example; likewise the Shunammite woman. Here consider the incredible blessing of God, by which it happens that a person makes more progress toward riches by giving and pouring out than one who scrapes together whatever he can from every quarter, by fair means or foul, and at the same time keeps and withholds more than is right.

Verse 19. One who rightly adheres to righteousness [sows] to life; but one who pursues evil, to his own death.

There are many difficulties that deter a person from righteousness and its pursuit. Among the greatest of them is the belief that people cannot preserve themselves in life by cultivating righteousness, since by frauds and lies the way lies open to make abundant profit by which we may maintain the necessities of life. Besides this, so great is the tyranny of injustice in the world that one who is a friend or patron of righteousness repeatedly exposes himself, as it were, to death. For since the more powerful often become unjust—people who possess power over life and death—and cannot endure in others the splendor of the righteousness that they themselves hate, it happens that worshipers of righteousness expose their lives to danger by their own righteousness. Thus Abel, 1 John 3:12. See the history of Naboth. Solomon, however, repels this temptation when he introduces the Author of life promising life if we devote ourselves to righteousness. Hence long life is promised not only to obedience to the fifth commandment, but generally to the observance of all the commandments.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 147 | printed columns 268–269]

And 1 Timothy 4 says, *Godliness has promises of the present life and of the future life.* Therefore, if it should ever happen—as it does less often—that fear of death deters us from duty, let what is celebrated in the proverb enter our mind: we tread a road that leads to life, and indeed one that leads in great part to the present life. But if that life is cut short, it is nevertheless recompensed to us with an increase of future life. Therefore even in death itself, by faith in God’s promises, life must be expected by us; and for that reason, to depart from duty through fear of death is to proceed directly toward death.

But it is also required that we persist constantly in the way of righteousness that we take up and enter. See Proverbs 3:18. Otherwise that saying in Ezekiel 18 has its place, that God will forget all the righteousness someone has done. Indeed, it would have been better never to have approached the borders of piety or greeted it. For then that saying in 2 Peter 2 has its place, as does Jude’s saying concerning those twice dead.

The latter half-verse proves and illustrates the former part, just as it is proved and illustrated by the former. For very many, eagerly gaping after riches and honors, think that through the enjoyment of them they have obtained a great aid to a longer and more comfortable life. Nor are there lacking those who, in the hasty and accelerated acquisition of the same things, promise themselves a way of life more open and shorter toward eternal life. They thereby strengthen themselves in the pursuit of acquiring those things: once an abundance of the things necessary to sustain this life has first been obtained, they suppose that afterward, without distraction or interruption, they may devote their whole selves, all that they are, to seeking the kingdom of heaven. This proverb teaches that all such people err and are deceived, since they enter a death-bearing road and cling in the very jaws of death.

When he says that the person who pursues evil tends toward death—not the person who commits evil—he establishes a distinction between the upright and the wicked. For although the upright commit sins, they do not pursue them; but the wicked are borne toward evil with as great an impetus of desire as dogs toward the wild beasts they hunt. They are driven by the highest appetite. To this pertains what was said above, that they do not sleep unless they have committed sin, and that sleep flees from their eyes; likewise what the Apostle notes concerning sin’s reign in a mortal body.

It may seem remarkable how, although all people by an instinct of nature and deliberate purpose seek life exceedingly and especially recoil from death, nevertheless so few are found who enter or keep to the road by which they may either avoid the latter or obtain the former. Thus it may rightly be said of them that they hate life and embrace death. They desire life, of course, and flee death; but because the things that pertain to life involve some trouble, they avoid them, while they eagerly pursue the things that lead to death because these titillate the senses and drench them with a certain momentary sweetness. It is otherwise in matters that pertain to this life. For just as people desire harvest and vintage, so they do not flee the labors by which they attain them, though those labors are heavy and troublesome. And those who run in a stadium not only pass through the toil and weariness of the race, but further afflict themselves, deprive themselves of food, and eat very hard bread, lest they grow weary in the race, panting and short of breath. People plainly laugh at and mock those who, while desiring the good things of this life, spurn the means by which the desired object is reached. By contrast, although they reproach no one for seeking eternal life, they nevertheless hold up to ridicule those who devote themselves to the means by which they attain it. They judge us worthy to be deprived of this life’s advantages unless we devote diligent effort to acquiring them, and so forth; but in heavenly matters it is otherwise. This demonstrates the astonishing perversity of the human being.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 148 | printed columns 270–271]

Verse 20. Those perverse in soul are an abomination to Jehovah, but those perfect in their way are his delight.

In the words *abomination* and *delight* there is a metonymy of the adjunct, familiar to the Hebrews, by which they signify the highest degree of good or evil in some matter.

In this proverb there is a latent and hidden opposition between the judgment of God and that of human beings. People are held in great esteem among human beings if they so order their life that they are blameless and innocent before others, while they suppress the poison of their soul within and possess a soul filled with malice and impiety. So far are they from being arraigned before judges that they are even treated with the highest honor. Next, they think magnificently of themselves. For when they measure the course of their life by the example of those who live ruinously and dissolutely and plunge themselves into every kind of impiety and depravity, they take courage and come to appear exceedingly splendid. But before God they are abominable. This is also excellently confirmed in Luke 16:15, where all these things are comprehended: both justifying themselves and being highly esteemed by others, although they are execrable to God. For they are like boiling pots that retain their foam within and in the bottom of the pot, and do not send it to the pot’s mouth, from which it might more easily be removed. Or rather, people of this kind are like the cups or dishes described by Christ in Matthew 23: washed outside but full of stench and filth within; tombs whitewashed outside but filled within with foul-smelling corpses.

The reason for this is that God, because he is Spirit, requires before all things the worship of the heart and of the inner person. Hence the saying, *Give me your heart*, and Proverbs 4:23; 1 Chronicles 29:19.

Therefore it is incumbent upon us not only to approve ourselves in the judgment of human beings and angels, but to please God, without whom all things, however splendid in appearance, are hateful to God. See 2 Corinthians 10:18. Let us also remember that the human heart, however winding and full of hiding places and secret recesses, is nevertheless searched through by God to its deepest base and center. See Jeremiah 17:9, 10. Therefore it is necessary for us ourselves to know and search it thoroughly. For it often happens that we are ignorant of ourselves and of our thoughts, 1 Corinthians 4:4. The reason given ought also to move us to level our souls, as it were, because he abominates those perverse in heart. Who would not strive in every way to avoid the indignation of a prince who could not bear even the sight of him? See the example of Haman, whose face Ahasuerus ordered to be covered.

By contrast, those who are perfect in their way are supremely approved by God. It appears that, for the antithesis to be exact, it ought to have said that those perfect in heart and upright in soul supremely please God and are by far most acceptable to him. But Solomon preferred to say those perfect in their way and habitual manner of life. For although it is true that those of a good and upright soul are approved by God, nevertheless, because there is a class of people that endeavors to cloak its perversity in its habitual manner of life by claiming that its souls are rightly disposed, the Prophet therefore requires both: that they be sincere in soul and express that roundness of soul in deeds and their habitual manner of life. Thus James 2: “[Show me your faith by your works.]” Of this kind are the pseudo-Nicodemites, who, presenting their bodies before idols, pretend that they preserve their heart for the true God. Likewise there are those who, belching forth foul words, will nevertheless affirm that they possess an uncorrupted mind. From this we learn that we ought not to be so concerned about a human day, or judgment, 1 Corinthians 4:5, provided that we approve our obedience to God.

From this antithesis it is clear that תמימי דרך are not those perfect or immaculate in their way, but those who are sincere and candid and by no means

[1632 witness | PDF p. 149 | printed columns 272–273]

perverse turncoats, changing themselves, as they say, into every form.

Here it may be asked whether evil and perverse actions do not flow from a perverse heart just as the good fruit of works flows from a good and sincere soul, since Solomon looks to the soul and the inward faculties of the soul in the wicked, but to outward fruits in the godly. The answer is that perverse actions flow from a perverse heart, just as zealous actions flow from an upright one. But the difference is that the actions flowing from a perverse heart are often so tinged with a certain appearance of virtue that it is difficult to see through their depravity and foulness. For in hypocrites vices sometimes possess an appearance and splendor more brilliant than the virtues themselves. From this also arises the abomination with which God pursues hypocrites. We are urged toward this incorruption of life by the contrary reasoning found in the former half-verse; concerning that reasoning, see Romans 12 and often elsewhere.

Verse 21. With efforts joined, the evil man does not obtain impunity, but the seed of the righteous delivers itself.

In the word *hand* there is a metonymy of the subject for the strength that is in the hand, and in addition a synecdoche of the part for the whole person. In the words *he will also be innocent* there is a metonymy of the subject for the adjunct: obtaining impunity. In the word *seed* there is a metonymy of the cause for the effect, the prosperity that is generated from seed. Likewise, in the word *righteous* there is a synecdoche of the plural for the singular. For although only one parent is righteous, nevertheless the seed is holy by covenant and for this reason shares in the promise.

It is no slight spur to sin that people hope they will go unpunished after committing a crime. For they either hope they will remain concealed, or, if they are betrayed, nevertheless suppose that an escape is prepared. The Prophet removes this enticement to sin when he plainly affirms that the wicked person, once apprehended, will pay the penalties his crime deserves. If we considered only human beings, who bear the sword of vengeance yet pass over crimes committed, and who often wink at crimes sufficiently known and perceived, those who promise themselves immunity would not always be deceived in their opinion. For human beings are sometimes deprived of power to punish the wicked, as David once did not dare deservedly to punish Joab, who enjoyed favor among the soldiers. Sometimes those who possess power lack courage of soul. There are also occasions when, although these are present, the will is lacking because of friendship, affinity, or corruption by gifts. Yet, although the wicked escape the vengeance of one person, God raises up another to take punishment from them, as was seen in Joab, from whom Solomon exacted punishment. Sometimes God exacts punishment from them through other evildoers, as is clear in Abner and Amasa, who were killed by Joab; and in our own memory we have often seen one assassin slain by another. But since God is the judge of the world, who knows and hates sin and on account of neither his faithfulness nor his justice can permit a crime committed to go unavenged, let those not flatter themselves with vain hope who have persuaded themselves that they can escape either the just and due penalty for their sins or at least chastisement. For they have fashioned in their soul not God but rather an idol—to say nothing of the accusation first made by their own conscience, and afterward its judgment, by which God executes vengeance as though from a heavenly throne. See Genesis 4:7. Likewise consider the example of Cain, whom conscience tormented more than if a thousand lictors had been employed. And people whose duty it is to take vengeance upon evildoers, yet do not do so, are not only unjust but cruel toward the evildoers themselves, and indeed toward the whole commonwealth, because by this means they give place to God’s more severe vengeance.

But still greater spurs

[1632 witness | PDF p. 150 | printed columns 274–275]

are applied to sinning when people undertake a wicked deed through confidence in accomplices in crime or in other friends, with this hope: that through their protection they will be exempted from the penalty and punishment due them. God shakes off this empty confidence by this proverb when he says, *hand to hand*, and so forth. A threefold cord certainly has very great force in an honorable and lawful matter, and is not easily broken, Ecclesiastes 4. But a cord threefold or even a hundredfold, when opposed to God in the patronage or defense of sin, feels like a thread of tow when it encounters fire. For before God’s vengeance every shield and every boss is made of papyrus. See the history of the flood and the overthrow of Sodom, where the ungodly were bound together in the highest conspiracy in wrongdoing. Therefore let us remember what is written in Nahum 1:23, where various epithets are attributed to God, all of which contain the undoubted certainty of vengeance upon the wicked: that he is an avenger, that he is lord or possessor of wrath, that he is a keeper of sins lest any escape, and that he will by no means hold a guilty person innocent. Habakkuk 2:3 also confirms the truth of this proverb excellently, as do the things said in Amos 5:19; Jeremiah 48:43, 44; Isaiah 24:17; and Job 20:29. From these let us readily draw the truth of this proverb: God leaves neither kings themselves, nor those especially dear to him, nor finally those who are at once beloved by God and possess a kingdom, free and untouched by his penalties and chastisements. Let the most powerful kings of Babylon and Assyria serve as examples, and likewise David and Solomon. Therefore, if there were no hand on earth able to reach us, nevertheless let us think upon the long-armed God and flee sin, if we think his vengeance ought to be fled.

*But the seed*, and so forth. Just as hope of impunity adds a spur to committing sin, so people are greatly deterred from the pursuit of virtue and piety because they fear that they will be ruined by the cultivation of virtue and piety. Solomon meets them in the latter part of this proverb. He does not deny that those who devote themselves to doing good works may bring upon themselves afflictions by which they are exercised. But so far will they be from being buried or crushed in them that not only they themselves, but also their children and descendants, on account of the covenant made with them, will be rescued and delivered from evils.

From this it is clear that we are fortified by no certain promise that affliction will not arise for us from things well done; rather, it is confirmed that we will not be overwhelmed in evils. See Psalm 34: *Many are the evils of the righteous, but*, and so forth. Not only the righteous person himself, but those sprung from him will also be rescued even from evils of which they are worthy and which they have deserved. This is declared abundantly in the books of Kings, where it is repeatedly related that for David’s sake God spared the kings of Judah.

Verse 22. Like a golden necklace at the snout of a pig [is] a beautiful woman who turns away from reason.

In the word טעם (*taste*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things to the soul. For it teaches that reason is in the administration of affairs what agreeable flavor is in foods.

By this proverb Solomon reproves those who, resting content in bodily gifts however excellent, pass negligently over the virtues of the soul. He teaches that so far are they from being rendered more adorned by those gifts that they are, on the contrary, rendered deformed and more ugly and ignominious. For people, enticed by excellent bodily gifts to look at them and inquire about them, are by the same labor and at the same moment compelled to behold the corruption and foulness in their life and manners, just as a golden necklace hung at a pig’s snout turns everyone’s eyes toward the pig.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 151 | printed columns 276–277]

For outward gifts of the body—especially when they are more remarkable—are like a signal raised up, at which people flock together to look. This is seen especially in Absalom, whose depravity was made more renowned by the admirable beauty of his body. If someone were furnished with numerous offspring, among whom one were of outstanding beauty while the rest were extremely deformed, would he have matter for glory, or think it prudent to admit many people to view them? Or if he had a daughter very beautiful in her teeth but Ethiopian or monstrous in the rest of her body, would he rejoice or exult in this daughter of his? Such is elegance of form when joined with evil manners. What did bodily beauty and soft, flowing hair profit Magdalene, when through the abuse of them she was made the dwelling place of seven most foul spirits? What is said here about a woman’s beauty must be understood also of other gifts of body and soul—strength, swiftness, eloquence, learning, and so forth—both in men and in women. Unless they are seasoned with virtue and piety as with salt, they are both aids and incitements to depravity and also vehicles and chariots upon which crimes are placed and exposed more openly to everyone’s eyes, to the greatest disgrace of those who commit them.

It must be noted that beautiful and at the same time immodest women, who in their own judgment and that of their lovers are to be compared with peacocks and the Phoenix, are compared by the Prophet with sows. This is certainly not because the Prophet condemns beauty, which he compares to a necklace, and indeed a golden one. Nor does it then cease to be a gift of God when it is transferred to foulness. For just as a golden necklace hung at the snout of a pig does not cease to be golden, not even when it is brought and thrust into mud and other filthy things, so bodily beauty does not cease to be such when it is diverted to a use other than that for which it was given. But he teaches how destructive it is to people when, through love and pursuit of it, better things are spurned, and when what ought to have been a spur toward virtue restrains people from vice no more than a golden necklace hanging from a sow’s snout keeps her from wallowing in mud; indeed, it becomes an enticement to committing crime. Therefore it is fitting that we be held to the pursuit of reason or wisdom, by which a form beautiful in itself may shine more brightly. For *wisdom makes a person’s countenance shine*, Ecclesiastes 8:1, even the countenance of one outwardly deformed; how much more will it illustrate and illuminate one beautiful and comely.

*Virtue coming from a beautiful body is more pleasing.* Ovid.

Verse 23. Only good comes to the desire of the righteous; fury [befalls] the expectation of the wicked.

In the word *hope* there is a metonymy of the adjunct for the thing hoped for. In the word *fury* there is a metonymy of the efficient cause for the penalty proceeding from fury. This proverb teaches the happiness of the godly even in this present life, much more in the future life, and the supreme unhappiness of the ungodly after this life. It does so to blunt that temptation by which the condition of the godly in this life is regarded as miserable and wretched. Is not the condition of those who obtain what they desire happy and prosperous? For it is clear from verse 24 of chapter 10 that God fulfills the desire of the godly; likewise Psalm 10:17. Yet although this happiness is great, it is proclaimed to be greater and broader still when Solomon teaches that they obtain only good. By this he intimates that desires and appetites conceived by them which are less seemly, or which would harm them if granted, are not granted. For it sometimes happens that through ignorance or an intemperate affection they desire something that would be harmful to them. God denies it to them, since they obtain only good

[1632 witness | PDF p. 152 | printed columns 278–279]

things.
Hence Psalm 10:17 also affirms that God hears those desires of the godly which are stirred up under the guidance of the Spirit directing their heart. To this pertains that saying in John 5:14: *Whatever we ask according to his will, we know that we receive it*; and that saying in James 3. Sometimes it also happens that the things which the godly seek less suitably turn out for their good; see 2 Samuel 7.

From this it is clear that the desires of the godly are usually good and lawful. Further, these desires are not scattered by them into the air and tossed out in certain chance, rash wishes, but are deposited in God’s bosom, wrapped in faithful prayer. Hence Psalm 10:17 and 20 confirm that the desires of the godly are granted by God through the intervention of prayers. This causes us not to overflow or luxuriate so greatly in our desires and wishes. For people imbued with at least some drop of piety will be ashamed to place before God things whose pursuit they would otherwise be ashamed to profess either privately among themselves or publicly among people. It does sometimes befall the godly to desire unlawful and absurd things, as David did in 1 Chronicles 11:17 and 2 Samuel 23, but they do not dare ask them from God in prayer—unless perhaps they do something of this sort through ignorance or ignorance of the divine will, or through the heat of a severe temptation. The former was done by the Apostles when they called down fire from heaven to burn the Samaritans; the latter by Jonah when he wished for present death. Therefore, in order to restrain and bridle the foolish and harmful desires of our souls, it is worthwhile, as soon as desires are born and spring forth, immediately to set them before the tribunal of the divine word and seek from it an examination whether they are of the sort that can be brought and admitted by prayers into God’s sight. If they cannot bear his sight, let us judge them at once unlawful and harmful to us. For in matters pertaining either to the present or the future life, there is nothing so small or so great that we may not offer it to God in prayer with the highest confidence and freedom.

In the second member of this proverb it seems it ought to be said ἀντιθετικῶς that God denies the wicked their desires, which indeed would be for their great good. But on the contrary, God grants their blind desires and tumultuous wishes; indeed, he often exceeds their vows. Psalm 37:7. See Psalm 78:29, 30. Likewise, they desired death in Egypt, and this wish was fulfilled for them in the wilderness. But of all these it can rightly be said what Hosea 13:11 affirms of Saul: he was given as king to the Israelites when God was angry and was taken from them when God was angry beyond measure.

But their hope is fury, or exceeding wrath. That is, not only do the good things they hope for fail to befall them, but penalties and punishments proceeding from God’s fury are substituted in their place. Chapter 10:24 says that the evils feared by the wicked befall them, and verse 28 says that the hope of the wicked will perish. Here he pronounces something greater and more grievous. For not only do they fail to obtain the good things they hope for, but they experience the evils they abhor. From this it is clear how crowded and complete in every respect the unhappiness of the wicked is. From it also emerges that the wicked cherish hopes in their souls by which they endeavor to strengthen and sustain themselves against the goads of their conscience. But these hopes will shame them in two ways. First, they fall away from the good things they hoped for, as is said of those in Jeremiah 2 and Job 6 who approach a cistern to seek water but, finding none, return ashamed. Next, they are burdened with unexpected evils, as though instead of water suitable for quenching thirst they brought back poison by which, their inward parts inflamed, they thirsted much more than before.

Verse 24. There is one who scatters, yet is increased still more; but one who withholds himself from

[1632 witness | PDF p. 153 | printed columns 280–281]

what is right [withholds] only unto want.

The word *scattering* is a metaphor taken from those who commit seed to a field and reap it multiplied. When he says there is one who scatters and becomes richer, he indicates sufficiently that not all who lavishly bestow the resources granted by God or otherwise acquired are increased in riches. For there are bankrupt spendthrifts, gluttons, drunkards, and followers of prostitutes who squander their goods ruinously and prodigally and afterward are reduced to the greatest misery and want of all things. See Proverbs 21:17 and Luke 15. For these cast their seed, as it were, among cliffs or rocks, or into a desert or an enemy’s land, where it will never grow.

But one who scatters the seed of his resources for the propagation of religion, the support of the commonwealth, the preservation of his household, and the refreshment and restoration of the poor and strangers will without doubt increase his property and render his possession stable and long-lasting.

Two things, therefore, are considered here: first, instruction concerning duty; then, a promise by which we may be better kept in that duty. In the former there are two things: one, that we give neither sparingly nor illiberally; the other, that we do it promptly and with a cheerful spirit. Both are clearly drawn from the metaphor of casting seed. As regards the magnitude of our giving, it is fair that, since God has been liberal and beneficent—or rather, munificent—toward us, we should not be dry and tightfisted toward our brothers. For just as Christ exhorts his disciples that, having received freely, they should give freely, so it is no less fitting that with the same measure by which God bestowed things upon us with so full a hand, we should pour back the same beneficence for the uses already described. Let us imitate clouds full and pregnant with water, which send down abundant rains upon the earth, remembering what the Apostle cites to the Galatians: *Those who sow sparingly will reap sparingly.* To this the present metaphor looks, for if the seed is scant and sparse, the harvest will correspond to the sowing.

Next, this must also be done promptly and with a cheerful spirit. For *the Lord loves a cheerful giver*, 2 Corinthians 9, to which the present comparison looks. For the farmer or husbandman casts seed into the earth not without singular zeal and delight, and does not commit the seed to the ridges of earth with a reluctant and grieving spirit. To this it may be beautifully referred that it is fitting to anticipate the request of the poor and of others for whom we have care.

The promise is that the seed of liberality will be increased, since whatever is always distributed to the uses I have described is cast into a very fertile and fruitful field, and one that is also faithful. See Ecclesiastes 11. For the bosom of the poor is watered soil. Most especially, however, whatever is contributed to the cultivation of religion is sown as though in God’s paradise and beside streams of waters. See Haggai 2. To this is added that people are favorably disposed toward a man of this sort and willingly help him.

The latter part of the proverb seems to be general, while the former part is more particular. It is as though he said that withholding money or other resources more tightly than is right conduces to bringing about want and poverty. See Haggai. Moreover, besides the fact that God’s curse scatters his goods, the miser himself, through avarice and because he does not lay out money in the proper place at the proper time, loses many opportunities either to become richer or to preserve what he has acquired.

This whole proverb, when transferred to gifts of the mind, contains a most illustrious and enduring truth. For the gifts with which God adorns us, when expended for the use and edification of others, receive a great and certain increase. By contrast, they are quenched and contract rust if they are allowed to lie idle.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 154 | printed columns 282–283]

Verse 25. A beneficent soul is made fat; and one who communicates abundantly will himself pour forth more.

In נפש (*soul*) there is a synecdoche of the part; when construed with the noun, however, it signifies, after the Hebrew manner, a remarkable will and zeal for blessing. In ידשן (*will be made fat*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodies of good condition to a person with respect to his chief part, to denote prosperity of soul; with respect to a person’s body, however, it is a synecdoche of the part for complete health. In מרוה (*one who waters*) there is a metaphor taken from gardeners or vegetable-growers who, when rain is withheld, irrigate beds planted with seeds by foot, as the saying goes. In יורה (*the morning rain*) there is a metaphor indicating that one who formerly gave more sparingly and with some inconvenience to himself will afterward, because his resources have been increased by God’s blessing, give both more abundantly and much more conveniently.

The preceding proverb and others, both those that came before and those that follow, tend and are directed almost entirely to this: that they may stir people to beneficence and generosity toward others. These things are repeated and doubled so often because the Holy Spirit, who knows and has thoroughly examined hearts and reins, well observes people’s slowness to give and do good—or rather, the aversion and alienation of their souls. But just as skilled and provident physicians, when they administer medicines hateful to their patients, take care that the same medicines are repeatedly placed in foods and drinks, but in one very different form after another, so that what they cannot effect once and all at once in a single drink they may accomplish through repeated powers and a great variety of medicines and foods; so the Holy Spirit, conscious of nature’s nausea in spiritual matters, has so adjusted his style that by repeating the same things frequently for our good, he has provided both for nature’s delight and for a greater increase in knowledge of his will, while he repeatedly presents what was said before in a varied form of speech and not without some addition.

Consider whether by the word *blessing* in this place both the beneficence that consists in words and that which consists in deeds may rightly be understood—especially since blessing properly belongs chiefly to speech—so that nothing may impede this sense in the latter half-verse.

First, then, by describing the liberal person as one who has a soul of blessing, he teaches that the fountain of liberality is situated in the soul. Unless the soul is inclined and, as it were, laid open toward the person we help, whatever is disbursed is lost to us. This is confirmed by 1 Corinthians 13:3. By contrast, *provided there is a ready will, whatever little you disburse is accepted*—even if you give nothing, but are merely moved by compassion for another’s misery. Thus, once the soul has first been unlocked by the key of love, the soul is like the key by which the hand is unlocked and opened. For a soul kindled by love makes alms, as it were, fiery and airy, so that it seeks heaven and flies upward. Otherwise it would be leaden and heavy, borne downward and pressing us together with itself toward the earth—or rather, toward hell.

Next, this manner of speaking teaches that progress must also be made in liberality: namely, until our soul is as though poured forth in blessings. Psalm 112. Its effects are to give here and there, even to seven and even to eight, Ecclesiastes 11:2; and indeed we must advance to such a point that, if necessary, we are prepared to pour out not only our resources but even our very soul, and to expose it for the good of our neighbors, especially our brothers.

The promise that seeks to keep and sustain us in the duty of beneficence is that it will be well with the beneficent person both in soul and in body. In the sixth preceding proverb God promised that our estate and resources would be increased—the very things we suspect will be diminished by giving.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 155 | printed columns 284–285]

This proverb, however, promises something more excellent: namely, that it will be well with us ourselves in soul and body; that the soul will be increased and adorned with God’s spiritual riches, and the body with health and strength. By this promise Solomon meets a tacit objection. For when we apply ourselves wholly to this care, that provision of every kind of maintenance be made for the body, both in food and clothing, as in Matthew 6, this thought easily steals upon us: that as much as we expend upon others will be taken away from our living well and long. God therefore meets this objection with so munificent a promise. To confirm this matter, let there first enter our mind what Solomon writes generally concerning the whole pursuit of piety and wisdom, Proverbs 3:2, 8. More particularly, let the words of Matthew 10:40, 41, 42 be weighed. For to receive a prophet’s reward is to receive knowledge of heavenly things and to be commended by most effectual prayers; just as to receive a righteous person’s reward is to be strengthened by the righteous person’s prayers, which are ἐνεργούμεναι (*effectual*). See how variously and manifoldly the Shunammite woman was helped because of the hospitality she furnished to Elisha, and how provision was made for her and her household when famine raged. See also Acts 10 concerning Cornelius’s alms.

In the latter half-verse two other tacit objections are met. First, liberality is hindered because our household estate is rather narrow. If we share any of it with others, we must both provide more sparingly and austerely for ourselves, and access to future liberality will also be closed off when a greater increase in our wealth and resources would otherwise have occurred. The answer is that one must give even if one’s riches are not so abundant, and although one maintains oneself somewhat more austerely. For it will come to pass that one who now does this more sparingly and with greater difficulty—just as when a vegetable-grower waters a garden more sparingly and with greater weariness—will afterward be like rain, by which the garden is watered both abundantly and without any labor, much less fatigue.

Next, although we abound in a supply of all things, it nevertheless often happens that the godly are deterred from giving because they perceive themselves to be more constricted in soul and more reluctant than is right. For this reason they suppose that in giving they lose both labor and oil, since their souls are not enlarged any more and they feel no greater cheerfulness in giving. Such people must first be taught and instructed to exercise liberality even though they do not possess so cheerful a soul and open a countenance. For by use and exercise in giving it will come to pass that those who now distribute their riches to the poor more sadly than is fitting and with greater difficulty will afterward do so more easily and not without delight.

Verse 26. The people will curse one who withholds grain, but blessing [will rest] upon the head of one who sells it.

In the word יקבהו (*they will pierce*) there is a metaphor transferred from those who pierce someone through with a sword or dagger, as though someone were transfixed by the people’s curses and utterly stabbed through. In ראש (*head*) there is a synecdoche. In משביר (*one who breaks apart*) there is a metaphor taken from broken things: grain distributed this way and that from a heap, in which it had been one continuous thing, is as it were broken and torn asunder.

Although the retention and withholding of all things that are useful and advantageous to others, especially to brothers, is dangerous, the withholding of no thing that pertains to preserving human life is so hateful to God and human beings as the withholding of grain. Without it one not only fails to live comfortably, but is not even permitted to live long. To this pertains the attribution of a staff to bread in Isaiah 3:1: without it people fall to the earth unless they are sustained by bread as by a staff. An illustrious testimony to this matter is Ecclesiastes 5:9, where fields are preferred to all the other goods of this life; without them no one, not even a king,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 156 | printed columns 286–287]

can live not only neither comfortably nor honorably, but cannot even live at all. For if someone should heap up money without measure and keep it shut in a chest, where it is useful to no one but is eaten away by rust, he is guilty of a grave and atrocious sin, since he will have the rust itself as witness and accuser against him. Clothing has an even greater use, since without it one cannot live honorably, healthfully, or comfortably; whoever retains clothes beyond what is right will have moths speaking testimony against him. The same applies to merchants who preserve their wares so that they may afterward sell them at the dearest price. But one who withholds grain summons a graver judgment upon himself. This must not, however, be understood of every accumulation and withholding. For Joseph had preserved an immense quantity of grain for the years of dearth, and cities likewise store a great supply by which they may more easily endure a siege, should one occur. Rather, those are condemned who, when grain is lacking, withhold it so that they may afterward sell it at a higher price.

Here, therefore, it must be noted that everyone must beware lest, in exercising liberality toward certain people, they injure an entire nation or province. For that liberality will never be pleasing to God, although it is practiced by very many.

How great an evil this is is proved by the punishment imposed: the whole company of the people launches curses and maledictions against people of this kind. Although they generally do this through impatience, nevertheless God hears and avenges their confused outcries and their complaints cast forth in blind fury. For if it is true—as it is most true—that God hears the cry of one person who is oppressed, Exodus 22:23, and if he hears the cries of reapers, James 5, how much more will he hear the cry of the whole people? Hence it is that some repeatedly lay violent hands upon themselves and end their life with a noose when either their grain rots under God’s curse or God suddenly, beyond their expectation, provides his people with grain from elsewhere, so that they fall from the hope of profit they had conceived. Although this sin is grave everywhere, it is graver and more atrocious among us, for whom grain serves not only as bread but also as drink. In very many regions one lives conveniently upon water or wine, because the heat of the climate renders the waters purer and more refined.

He proves the bitterness of this punishment for withholding grain from the contrary reward of those who distribute it. Here it must first be observed that the antithesis is arranged by the Prophet in a manner very different from what seemed likely. For the nature of the antithesis required Solomon to say that the blessings of the people would be heaped upon the distributor of grain. Yet from his manner of speaking he maintains that God is the author and fountain of this blessing, and is so immediately—not that the blessing belongs to the people, as their curses did in the former half. By doing this he wished to forestall the objection that the people might be ungrateful once they had received the grain they had desired and awaited, especially when they considered that this commerce was a benefit common to both parties, since one person bought grain with his own money. Therefore the Holy Spirit affirms that God is the fountain of the blessings when he confirms that they will be poured upon the head. For when he teaches that these blessings will fall back upon the distributor’s head, he intimates that they descend from heaven. He does this all the more because, if someone receives a just price for grain, the deed might seem to be excluded from the number of good works that God rewards.

Just as in this matter lawful commerce in grain is especially commended to us by the Holy Spirit, so every other business that is both lawful and lawfully practiced is taught not to lack its own reward and recompense before God. From this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 157 | printed columns 288–289]

the pretext that a seller advances for withholding grain is also abundantly refuted. From this it is clear how little people desire heavenly things. If grain for nourishing bodies is withdrawn, they immediately cry out, shout, and pursue with curses those who withhold it; but when the grain of the divine word is withdrawn, they are not affected in the same way. Further, if one who holds back food suitable for feeding bodies is accursed, how much more will one who steals away the nourishment of the soul be liable to execration? And if one who offers grain for sale at a price to nourish the body is heaped with blessings from God, how much more will one who [offers] the grain of the soul be burdened with benefits from God? Finally, if it is so pleasing to God to sell grain and the other conveniences of this life at a fair price, how much more pleasing is it to bestow something freely?

Verse 27. One who diligently seeks good [for others] obtains goodwill; but evil will come to the one who seeks evil.

In the word שחר there is a metaphor taken from those who, if they vehemently desire something, rise in the morning so that time may be available for the accomplishment of the thing they desire. If one way does not succeed, another way may be available for them to undertake it. Further, they apply the dawn especially to those affairs because, newly refreshed by sleep, they possess unimpaired powers of both soul and body, so that they may more conveniently complete the business incumbent upon them.

Two things are proposed in this proverb: one concerns the duty to be performed by everyone; the other concerns those things by which we may be kept in duty or even be stirred more cheerfully to perform it. There are two things in the duty: one, that we seek what is good; the other, that we do it as soon and as early as possible. In the former we are admonished of our innate perversity, by which we bear a soul wholly alien and averse from doing good. Hence we are so often commanded in the law to *do good and depart from evil*, Psalms 34 and 14, Isaiah 1, and so forth. For although each person is led by a certain appearance of good in all things undertaken or to be undertaken, it is nevertheless wholly established that everything we do is nothing but evil. Therefore, whenever these precepts concerning seeking good occur to us, let the readiness adhering and fixed within us to perpetrate every evil immediately enter our minds.

In the latter part of this command we are instructed concerning our sloth and delay in doing good, and this even after the reformation of the Holy Spirit in us has been begun and commenced. Thus, just as the former member of this command indicates to us, not obscurely, our aversion from good, wholly inborn and innate, so the latter does not permit us to forget our drowsiness and backwardness in good works. Just as under nature’s guidance we not only do evil but perpetrate it eagerly and φιλονεικτικῶς (*contentiously*), so God requires of his own not merely that they do good but that they pursue it diligently and at a swift pace.

By this proverb, therefore, we are admonished to do what is good in due season, so that we do not allow offered opportunities for acting well to slip from our hands. Rather, *while we have opportunity, let us do what is good*, Galatians 6:10. Likewise, *while it is day, let us work, because night comes when no one can work*, John 9:4. Let us not sleep through summer or harvest. To this refer the warning that one must not boast of tomorrow, Proverbs 27:1. Very many sin in this way: they draw out the day and cast everything back to tomorrow. Next are those who depart from duty because of the smallest obstacles and who, as Solomon says, observe the wind; they watch when the clouds send down rain, when they are pregnant with waters. Here those are reproved who do not seek good in tender youth, who while possessed of unimpaired

[1632 witness | PDF p. 158 | printed columns 290–291]

strength do not devote themselves to piety, but only then at last when, exhausted in strength and unable because of the frequent illnesses of old age, days arrive in which it is customary to say, “I have no pleasure,” not even in those things that are pleasing to an animal man, much less in those that belong to the Spirit, and when cloud follows rain, Ecclesiastes 12. Above all it must be held fast what the Apostle admonishes in 2 Corinthians 6:2: that while it is the accepted day—that is, while the Gospel flourishes and is proclaimed—we should approve ourselves to God in all things.

The reasons upon which this command rests follow. The first is drawn from the attached promise of the good that will ensue; the other from the contrary adjunct that will come upon contrary subjects.

The promise by which those who persist in this way are sustained is that they seek favor or goodwill. Since there is no word here that restricts goodwill to God or confines it to human beings, and since neither the circumstance of the passage nor the περίστασις (*circumstance*) prevents us from referring these things to both God and the human being, we embrace that sense as the more august one, since it includes both God and human beings. Everyone is held to the pursuit of obtaining human goodwill, and no one is so savage or wild that he does not profess himself bound by the pursuit of conciliating God. But very many, because they do not know the way by which these things are attained, weary themselves in vain with their own counsels and inventions. For when they seek God’s goodwill from their own fabrications or the determinations of others, they render more hostile and averse the God whom they endeavor to regain for themselves.

Many also devote themselves to obtaining human favor and goodwill and strain every nerve toward that end. Yet they do not obtain what they pursue; or, if they obtain the goodwill of certain people, they gather the offense of others, by whose offenses they are harmed as much as they are helped by the zeal of those whose goodwill they court. Thus Absalom, after gathering his subjects’ souls by evil arts, had exasperated others, and indeed those whom it most concerned him to have well-disposed. And although people enter upon the goodwill of all human beings, it often happens that by this very thing they fall away from God’s will and goodwill. Hence in Matthew 6 Christ pronounces people accursed when everyone speaks well of them.

This one thing is the summary of conciliating both God and the human being: that they diligently seek good. This is especially so if they first seek that good which reconciles God to them, that is, God’s kingdom and his righteousness. For Solomon derides human foolishness because nearly everyone seeks the face and goodwill of a ruler while neglecting God, Proverbs 29:26, although the conciliation of the king depends upon God alone. Next, Proverbs 16:7 teaches that the hatred of enemies toward us will be extinguished only when our ways are acceptable to God. By beneficence even enemies otherwise hostile are appeased, Romans 12:22, as is established by Jacob’s example. Therefore the greatest power must reside in beneficence, since even the most hostile people lay aside their enmities and become well-disposed toward us. Illustrious examples of this appear in David, who, being pleasing to God, was also pleasing to courtiers and to people otherwise alien from piety. But the chief example is Christ, Luke 2:52.

Here, however, someone may ask how this agrees with what is said: [*If anyone wishes to live godly in Christ, he will suffer persecution*], and with nearly infinite other statements concerning the world’s hatred toward us because of righteousness. The answer is, first, that 1 Peter 3:13 presents it as a monstrosity of a person if anyone should be found who harms us when we have been zealous followers of good things. Next, the promises of this life are wrapped in a condition and hypothesis: insofar as this shall seem expedient for us to God,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 159 | printed columns 292–293]

Therefore human goodwill arising from beneficence is not so certain and firm; but this disadvantage is abundantly compensated by God’s goodwill toward us, which is faithful and firm.

The other reason—or rather, a confirmation of the former—is that the evil a person pursues will come upon him. From the contraries it is therefore plainly concluded that the person who seeks goodwill perhaps seeks good or beneficence; what was lacking in the former half-verse must be supplied in this one.

Here, however, someone may object that no one loves himself so little that he would prefer evil for himself, much less pursue it. Solomon answers him that the one who does not perform good seeks the evil that will oppress him. For when he opposes one who diligently seeks good to one who seeks evil, it is clear that no middle intervenes between these two extremes. Therefore vain is the boasting of those who, to flatter themselves in their sins, will say that they harm no one or do injury to anyone; that they do not live from plunder but from resources already at hand or from an inheritance. By this very fact they are convicted of seeking evil because they do not seek good. But if they also endeavor to harm others in order to profit themselves, it is no wonder if the harm they contrived for others falls back upon their own head. See near the end of Psalm 7.

It must not be passed over that this proverb does not condemn the person who has perpetrated some evil, but the one who pursues evil, is assiduous in it, and occupies himself in it as though in an art.

Verse 28. He trusts in his riches; he himself will fall, but the righteous will flourish like a branch.

To close up the fountains of beneficence, no more deadly enemy exists than confidence in riches, which produces avarice, while people lean upon their wealth as upon a bulwark, Proverbs 10:15. Hence riches are said to be like a fortress to the rich person; by contrast, the poor person casts down his soul because he is poor. Thus this sin, taken broadly, embraces within its capacity both rich and poor. Hence the frequent exhortations: Psalm 62:11, *If riches abound, do not set your heart upon them*; 1 Timothy 6 admonishes the rich not to trust in uncertain riches. See also Psalm 49, because the wicked are described from the adjunct of confidence in wealth, and Psalm 52, from which the Apostle calls avarice idolatry in order to engender hatred of it. Psalm 62:12, however, after discouraging confidence in riches, teaches how necessary that exhortation is: *God has spoken once, and I heard this twice, that strength belongs to God.* Psalm 49 also designates the vanity of this confidence when it teaches that no amount of money and no price can redeem anyone’s life, although riches are accumulated chiefly for this end: that they may preserve this life and conserve it longer. Therefore, one who leans upon riches trusts in an Egyptian reed or a spider’s web, or is like a withered leaf that is cast down by the wind or falls of itself and does not recover greenness or life again. See 1 Timothy 6. An excellent testimony concerning this matter is James 1:10, 11. From it is shown the vanity of the person who trusts in riches and believes that he can be supported by so vain a thing. Sometimes a flower is struck off while the grass or its stalk is vigorous and green; and although that does not happen, nevertheless, when the grass withers, the flower immediately falls. Further, the flower cannot preserve the grass from destruction and dryness. How, then, do riches exempt their possessor from destruction and calamities?

In the latter half-verse he opposes the confidence of the righteous to riches. Here it must be noted that the righteous person is not one who merely refrains from overturning others by means of their wealth while contributing nothing himself to the relief of the poor, as misers do. Rather, something far greater is required of the righteous person, and chiefly this: that he trust in God,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 160 | printed columns 294–295]

which is especially the duty and work of the righteous person.

Next, he establishes by far the greatest difference in the outcome of this person and that one, when he compares the righteous person to a branch, but the person who trusts in riches to a leaf. For a branch deeply fixed in the tree does not fall. And although in winter it loses its greenness, the tree’s sap having been transmitted to the root, nevertheless at the beginning of spring it grows green again and recovers its former beauty. The righteous person is no different: although for a time, when the winter of affliction approaches, he loses his outward splendor, afterward he nevertheless returns to his former beauty, or even more splendidly than before. See Psalm 92.

Verse 29. One who troubles his house will possess wind, and the fool will [be made] servant to the wise in soul.

In the word בית (*house*) there is a metonymy of the subject for the members of the household. Likewise, in רוח there is a metaphor by which he indicates that a person profits no more by troubling his house than one who desires to possess the wind by inheritance. The wind has no use, and no one can press or constrain it.

The Apostle teaches in 1 Timothy 5:8 that *those who do not provide for their households are worse than heathens*. Therefore, one who not only fails to provide for them but further troubles them either by cruelty or by squandering his goods must be twice worse than a heathen. For by brawls, quarrels, and secret conflicts, zeal for reflecting upon useful matters grows cold, labors are interrupted, and the affections and inclinations of souls are pulled apart. Thus not only do the zeal and endeavors for making an estate falter, but there further arises such bitterness and alienation of souls, such cruelty and harshness, that they prefer disadvantages for the head of the household. So far are they from wishing to benefit him when they are able. To this is added that by the word *peace* in both Testaments (by a metonymy of the cause) prosperity and an abundance of things agreeable to the soul are described. Hence also the illustrious Psalm 133 celebrates peace among brothers from the adjunct of an abundance of all things, when it compares that peace to dew by which the surrounding fields, bathed on every side, grow wet and are watered to fruitfulness. By contrast, if every kingdom and city divided against itself will be desolated, how much more must a household be dissipated, since it is not so strong and robust as a city and kingdom to sustain the irruption of contention and assault. To the same effect is the Apostle’s warning that those who bite one another will consume one another, Galatians 5:15. Experience sufficiently indicates that a household estate is dissipated by division and hostilities between husband and wife. How much more will that happen when the head of the household, like a lion, has exasperated the whole family by his passion for quarrels?

A person also troubles his house who, at home or abroad—in which latter class people sin more often—consumes the resources or patrimony granted him by the liberality of God and his parents and drains it like a leech, so that he lacks the means to maintain his family honorably. Or it happens when, although wealth is abundantly available, he is nevertheless prevented by avarice and narrowness of soul from expending upon the household what is just and apportioned, Proverbs 15:17. Further, he troubles his house when he wears out the members of his household with continual, unresting, and scarcely ever interrupted labors, and grants them no relaxation from more burdensome occupations and no recreation of soul. Or again, when he withholds just chastisement for sins, as Eli did in 1 Samuel 2. To all these things is opposed the management of a household described by Job and applied in practice: that he denied neither a servant nor the lowest bondman in his household—much less his wife or children—the right to state his own case freely and to dispute it with him.

Finally, a person troubles his household who does not instruct and form it in the fear of God,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 161 | printed columns 296–297]

and who neither does this himself nor compels them to observe the Lord’s Day duly and religiously. For when Achan is truly said to have troubled Israel because he had perpetrated the deed from which God’s blazing anger flared up against Israel, why should one not also rightly be regarded as the troubler of his own house who, by neglect or contempt of divine worship in his household, opens a way and approach for the irruption of divine wrath? Thus Ahab is said to trouble Israel because he had defended idolatry and the false worship of God, 1 Kings 18:18.

*To possess wind by inheritance.* The penalty for this sin is proposed: that he will profit nothing from it and will be reduced to nothing. For although very many things of this sort are such as to produce a very great hope of profit, nevertheless they will no more obtain the profit they pursue than they can hold the wind. Or if they obtain anything by wicked labor, nevertheless whatever it is turns out like a swollen womb that, although it produces in those who look upon it a hope of offspring, nevertheless passes away into wind without a fetus. Not only does it fail to benefit its possessor, but like flatulence in the belly it produces pains. The Greek proverb ἀνέμους γεωργεῖν (*to cultivate the winds*) is akin to this.

The other penalty is that he will be reduced to servitude and made the property of a wise person, especially one who knows how to govern his household peacefully and quietly. Here it first presents itself for notice that he calls the troubler of his own house a fool. Thus Proverbs 18:6: [*The lips of a fool enter into strife, and his mouth summons confusion.*] Next, he teaches that the fool will come to such a pass that he is compelled by his own labor to surrender himself into servitude to one skilled in governing a household, driven to do this by his own ignorance. See what God threatens to Eli’s descendants; see the parable in Luke 15.

Verse 30. The fruit of the righteous [is] the fruit of the tree of life, when the wise person imbues souls with instruction.

This entire verse, nearly as extensive as it is, is an allegory, but one composed of metaphors that are very familiar and plain to everyone. The former is drawn from the likeness of a tree, especially the one that received the name of the tree of life in Paradise. The other appears to be taken from hunters, fishers, or fowlers. The sum of this proverb comes down to this: that people may learn at what price it is fitting to value a righteous man, who is regarded as most worthless among people and valued like dung and refuse.

In the former part the righteous person is compared and likened to the tree of life planted by God in the garden of Paradise; his works, words and deeds, discourse and manner of life are compared to the fruit of that tree. Here the cause of the righteous person must first be considered, namely, God’s planting. For although the other parts of the earth spontaneously brought forth plants, trees, and so forth, concerning the trees of Paradise Scripture precisely and expressly adds that they were sown and planted by God, Genesis 2:8. By this form of speaking God seems to intimate that a certain special labor and care were applied to its planting, and that it did not spring up like the other trees. From this is gathered, first, the excellence of the righteous person: he is compared not merely with the trees of Paradise but with the most excellent among them. Next, the righteous are not born but created by a certain second creation. We were not sown righteous by our parents, but were planted and grafted by God’s hand. Hence the statement in Psalm 1 that the righteous are planted beside streams of waters; Matthew 15, the Father’s planting; and John 15:15, God’s agriculture. For just as the trees of Paradise did not grow by the common and general manner and principle of the other trees, so the righteous are not generated by the ordinary course and tenor of nature; instead, they experience God’s healing hand in their restoration. This deservedly admonishes us not to rest content in that first light into which we were brought forth by our parents, until in us, in the

[1632 witness | PDF p. 162 | printed columns 298–299]

Gospel, the splendor of the sun of righteousness that is in Christ should shine.

Next we must look into the fruits or products of the righteous person, which are illustrated by the comparison from the fact that they are weighed with equal weights against the fruit of the tree of life. Nevertheless, if we probe into the very entrails of the things compared, we shall discover that the fruits of the tree of life are lesser and inferior to the fruits of the righteous person. For the more excellent the life of the second Adam is than the life of the first was, the more excellent is the fruit that nourishes this life than that which preserved the former. To this is added that the former tree could preserve from death one persisting in righteousness and piety, but this one makes a person dead in sins live again. Fruits taken and plucked from the former tree caused so much less fruit to remain as the greater quantity was taken from it and put to human use. This tree, by contrast, is laden all the more with fruits of piety and righteousness as it is stripped for the use of more people, in such a manner that it still retains within itself what it communicates to others, with its goodness and power even increased.

Hence the plainly inestimable value of a righteous person may to some extent be estimated, as may the great care and solicitude with which one must apply oneself to preserving for a long time among the living a person who produces such excellent and salutary fruits. Deuteronomy 20:19 commands that even for the sake of capturing a city in war, a tree that produces fruits for human nourishment—though fruits that will perish—is not to be cut down. With what severity of divine judgment, therefore, do those bind themselves who remove from the midst of humanity the tree of righteousness that confirms eternal life in human beings, especially since the former trees for the most part produce fruit only in the year, but this one in every month, indeed on every day and hour; the former often out of season, but this one opportunely and in due season. If in the whole world there were even a single tree that produced fruits by which this frail life might be prolonged and extended to one thousand years—good God!—what a multitude of people, abandoning all their business, would stream and fly to it, wherever it was situated in the remotest regions of the earth. Yet these same people contemptuously and disdainfully pass by this tree that supplies eternal life. Of what value, then, is it if anyone has this tree planted near him in the city, in his household?

In the latter half-verse he chooses one species of the righteous person’s fruit from the whole genus, and indeed the chief and primary one, as though in this one thing he determined to present an excellent specimen of the incomparable value of this tree. It is as if he were to say: this tree of righteousness bears manifold and various fruits in relieving the needy, rendering to each person what is his own, cultivating chastity, and also calling upon God in piety, and so forth. But this one is the most excellent and choice fruit: if he should win souls, either by the example of an uncorrupted life, Matthew 5 and 1 Peter 3, or by admonition and instruction in the doctrine of truth, as each person’s calling allows. This excellent kind of hunting, fishing, or fowling—when a person enclosed in Gospel nets is led from the vast and deep sea to the shore; is confined from the open and broad air in the cage of the Church; and is gathered from the wilderness of the world and of hell into God’s enclosures and the folds of the Church—can never be praised worthily enough. Hence the ministry of the Church, especially the apostolic ministry, is called fishing by Ezekiel 47 and by Christ in Matthew 4.

Finally, he adorns the righteous person with the title of *wise*. By this appellation he meets the most wicked judgment that perverse people make concerning the godly and righteous. For the wicked defame the godly and righteous with the reproach of folly, as though they had been deprived of the common sense and reason of human beings. By contrast, the Holy Spirit judges them alone to be truly and solidly wise. It is as if he were to say that no common skill is required in hunters and fishers so that they may catch little beasts by their snares: it is fitting that they observe the time and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 163 | printed columns 300–301]

wind, darkness, and very many other things. But that alone is skill and wisdom, and that person ought rightly to be held skillful and wise, who knows how to catch in Gospel nets the souls of human beings instructed in a thousand arts for casting themselves headlong into destruction. Hence the righteous and godly are called *the salt of the earth* in Matthew 5. See Psalms 10 and 43; Hosea 14:10; James 3:14, 15, 17.

From these things it is further gathered what the highest praise of ministers is: namely, not whether they can raise up a reputation for themselves, cause many to applaud them, and have people extol their inward knowledge, but that the wisdom of a true minister consists in this—whether he knows the art of converting souls to God.

Verse 31. Behold, the righteous—in this earth it is recompensed [to him]; how much more the wicked and the sinner?

What seems opposed to the preceding proverb is that the wicked person, who sins continually, prospers and possesses a happiness interrupted and impeded by no calamities. Just as this stimulates people to sin, so it distresses and torments the godly, cools them in the pursuit of piety, makes their hands more slack in working, or even solicits them to fall away from piety. This proverb therefore meets this evil. By an argument from the lesser it firmly concludes that the sin the wicked commit will not pass unpunished.

First, in the antecedent member of this conclusion the particle הן (*this*) raises us to diligent and assiduous attention to this proposition, as one most worthy of notice. Or rather, it designates a thing we hold as ascertained and perceived by the testimony and experience of our own eyes. This is also sufficiently declared by the words ישלם בארץ (*it will be recompensed in the earth*). What it teaches is of this sort: it is just that even in this life the righteous person, while dwelling on the earth, both receives some reward for good works and pays a sufficiently sharp chastisement for his failings. Since, therefore, one whose happiness is not contained within the narrow bounds of this life is heaped with good things here, and one whose failings have been expiated and most fully compensated for in Christ’s blood is afflicted with evils, how much more will the wicked person pay penalties either in this life, in the future life, or in both, since his sins are retained? And if one who once or twice perpetrates some crime through ignorance, imprudence, or the weakness of the flesh, and afterward deeply repents of it, does not escape God’s hand, what will happen to the sinner—that is, to one continually devoted to sin—especially since he is borne toward sin with his whole impetus, like an armored horse, with the greatest exertion and eagerness of soul? See Psalms 3, 6, 7. The first part of this argument is confirmed not only by daily experience but by testimonies of sacred Scripture: *Piety is profitable for all things and has promises of the present and future life*, 1 Timothy 4. *Seek first God’s kingdom, and these things will be added*, Matthew. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob serve as examples. The other part is likewise confirmed from Psalm 38 and 2 Samuel 7:14, 15: *If his sons sin against me, I will visit them with the hand of a man, but I will not take my mercy away from them.* An illustrious example of this appears in David; likewise in Moses and Aaron, who because of sin were excluded from possession of the land of Canaan; likewise in the example of Eli. What of the fact that God sometimes chastises the righteous most bitterly when they have provoked him by no graver crime, so that he may prove their faith and establish in them an example of patience for all posterity? This is evident in Job. How, then, will the wicked person escape?

This conclusion is further confirmed from Jeremiah 25 and 1 Peter 4, which also appears to allude to this passage in verse 18. Therefore, since these green pieces of wood admit the fire of divine wrath,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 164 | printed columns 302–303 | Chapter XI ends before CAPUT XII in the left column]

what will happen to dry and smokeless pieces of wood, and what to vine shoots whose only use is for burning? By this halter and bridle, therefore, let us be constrained from the snares of sin. For although God’s hand should touch us very little in this life, nevertheless it will strike so much more grievously the farther and longer it has swung its arm.
