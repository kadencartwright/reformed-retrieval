CHAPTER XIII

Dr. Burmann’s View of the Moral Law Is Examined

From what has been set out above, the nature of the *Moral Law* is sufficiently
clear. But before we continue with our proposed subject, it will be useful to
bring the adversaries’ doctrine concerning the nature of the *Moral Law* to the
anvil for testing.

Of all the adversaries, one man, Dr. *Burmann*, treats this subject most
clearly, namely in his *Apology for the Vindication of the Disquisition on the
Morality of the Weekly Sabbath*, chapter 1, page 4 and following. Let us
therefore examine more closely what he says, since, so far as I know, no one has
yet answered him on this point.

He says that “what is moral, properly speaking, is that which flows from the
image of God,” and so forth. But we considered this statement and the
explanation attached to it above in Chapter XI.

A little later, in section 2, he says that “what is moral is in reality the
same as what is otherwise customarily called natural right.” Reply: Since
natural right is twofold—one kind natural to God and another natural to us—a
distinction which he himself seems to admit later, he ought to have said
whether he means one of them here or embraces both. If by *natural right* he
here means it as embracing both, this statement can be admitted. But if he
intends *natural right* to be only that which is called *right natural to God*,
it is easy to see how greatly he has erred, since it is beyond doubt that very
many other things are and are called moral besides those which are said to be
natural to God, as is clear from what was said above.

He adds further, “and which from its very own nature, before a precept, is good
or evil.” Reply: We readily grant that something can of its own nature, before
a precept, agree or disagree with nature; and we concede that on this account
it can in some sense be called good or evil. But we deny that before a precept
anything is of its own nature morally good or evil, for the foundation of this
moral goodness or wickedness is the law of God, that is, a command or a
prohibition. Among brute animals there are actions which agree or disagree
with nature, and in this respect are good or bad; yet there is no moral goodness
or wickedness in those actions, because they conform or fail to conform to no
law. Consult what was said above in Chapter XII. Let us now see how he proves
this.

[1674 logical printed p. 160 | IA leaf 178 | PDF p. 178]

“For although,” he says, “almost all a person’s voluntary actions, considered
individually, are good or evil insofar as they are subjected to some precept,
nevertheless they are not all good or evil from their own nature, and hence not
all morally good or evil. Rather, many are neither good nor evil in themselves,
but become such from a precept and from the particular circumstances which
clothe them, since in themselves they are indifferent and always remain such
in their own nature, even after the precept.” Reply: 1. From this it is clear
that the adversary wants something to be morally good or evil before a law has
been enacted or a precept given. This must not be admitted, since it is utterly
false. 2. I do not approve of his saying that only *almost* all a person’s
voluntary actions, considered individually, are either good or evil insofar as
they are subjected to some precept. For in saying this he implies that at least
some individual voluntary human actions are neither good nor evil even insofar
as they are subjected to some precept. But what or what kind are those human
actions which are subjected to a precept and yet, insofar as they are subjected
to a precept, are neither good nor evil? I do not mean in themselves or in
their own nature, but in relation to the command. If there are some human
actions subject to a command which nevertheless are neither good nor evil
insofar as they are subject to the precept, then no human action at all is good
or evil insofar as it is subject to a precept. For what is not said of every
such thing is said of none insofar as it is such. 3. I concede that many human
actions, considered in themselves and in their own nature, or specifically,
are indifferent—that is, neither morally good nor morally evil—while, by reason
of the particular circumstances which clothe them, or considered individually,
they are either good or evil. But will the adversary say that all those human
actions which in themselves and in their own nature, or considered
specifically, are indifferent are therefore not moral? Killing a person is an
action indifferent in its own nature, sometimes good and sometimes evil by
reason of the circumstances which clothe it. Therefore, as he puts it, it
always remains such in its own nature even after the precept. Consequently,
the command not to kill an innocent person will not be a moral precept, because
the action of killing a person always remains indifferent in its own nature,
although by reason of the circumstances and God’s command that this or that
person—an innocent person, for example—not be killed, it is evil. The same can
be said of certain actions under the fifth, *seventh*, and *eighth* precepts.
For this or that particular action which here and now would not be a display of
the honor owed to parents, and would therefore be unlawful, may, when the
circumstances are changed and a command of God is posited, be lawful; Matthew
19:29, Mark 10:29, and Deuteronomy 33:9. He himself later concedes this, as we
shall hear. So too intercourse with a sister, or within the prohibited degrees,
is unlawful; yet with the circumstances altered and a command of God posited,
it was lawful among the sons of *Adam*. Likewise, taking the property of others
is unlawful; nevertheless, when the circumstances were altered and a command
of God was posited, it was lawful for the Israelites. Therefore all these and
similar actions will be indifferent in themselves and will always remain such
in their own nature, even after the precept; consequently, they will not be
moral actions. Nor, therefore, will the precepts which command or prohibit such
things be Moral—which is nevertheless false.

But let us hear what follows. “To worship God,” he says, “and to honor parents
is naturally good and therefore moral, since the law of nature commands it,
even before an external precept. But to worship God or honor parents at a
certain hour or on a certain day is not a matter of natural right, since nature
does not teach it.” Reply: 1. Worshiping God and honoring Parents is indeed
naturally good and moral because it is commanded by the law of nature. But does
he think

[1674 logical printed p. 161 | IA leaf 179 | PDF p. 179]

that nothing is moral unless it is natural in this way? We deny this for the
reasons given above. 2. When he adds “even before an external precept,” he seems
to imply that we think or affirm that the entire morality of actions depends
upon the external precept alone and not also upon the law of nature. But in
this he is mistaken. 3. Does nothing belong to Natural Right except what nature
in its present corrupt state teaches us? We proved above that this also is
false. 4. Although nature does not teach on which day or at which hour God is
to be worshiped, it nevertheless teaches, as we will show later—and he himself
perhaps will not deny—that God is to be solemnly worshiped on some day. Once
that day has been determined by God himself, it is moral to worship him on that
same day. In the same way, because nature teaches that God is to be worshiped
in a manner devised not by human beings but prescribed by God himself, it is
therefore moral to worship him in that prescribed manner and no other.
Otherwise the second precept of the Decalogue, which, as we will show in its
place, particularly requires this, will not be moral. Whether a determination
removes morality, however, we will consider in Chapter XV.

“Some things, therefore,” he continues, “are good or evil from their own nature
before a precept; but other things are indifferent from their own nature and
become good or evil only from a precept. What is morally good, properly
speaking, is that which is such from its own nature, which has been engraved
upon nature itself, and which can be drawn and learned from it. Thus the duty
of a son toward his father is natural when nature itself teaches it to the son,
and when, by examining his own nature and considering himself to be a son, he
can know from himself that his father is to be loved and honored and obeyed in
everything. Nature alone, however, does not suggest to the son that his father
must be honored at a certain place, time, or in a certain manner; that must be
learned from the Father’s positive will. In the same way, once a King is
posited, nature teaches a Subject to obey him in all things with lawful
submission. But if the King wishes to be honored in some particular manner or
at some particular time, or gives a positive command—for example, that no one
is to ascend the rampart at a certain hour—this depends upon the sovereign’s
command, which the Subject cannot know from himself.” Reply: 1. We readily grant
that some things are by their own nature morally good or evil even before an
external precept—about which he seems to be speaking, insofar as it is
distinguished from and presupposes the law of nature. We also concede that some
things are indifferent, that is, neither good nor evil before a precept, but
become good or evil only from a precept. Yet we consistently deny that there is
any moral good or evil before every precept. We also deny that it follows from
this that all things which can be called indifferent before every precept do
not become moral once a determination by precept has been posited. 2. He says
that a thing is morally good when it is such from its own nature. But what is
it to be moral from one’s own nature? Does it mean what is agreeable to nature,
or what is necessarily so, in such a way that it cannot be otherwise? If the
former is meant, we do not refuse it; but if he means the latter, he is greatly
mistaken, as is clear from what has been said. 3. He is also greatly mistaken
if he says that nothing which depends in some way upon God’s command should be
called moral. For God’s command does not remove the morality of an action;
rather, it establishes and confirms it—indeed, it brings it about—as we showed
more fully above in Chapter III. 4. When he says that what is morally good,
properly speaking, is that which “has been engraved upon nature itself,” either
he understands everything which the nature of things originally created by God
teaches and prescribes—whether in the human being himself or outside him, in
the nature, order, sequence, and arrangement of created things, and so
forth—or he means only what human reason, now corrupted, can readily perceive.
If by this expression he intends the *latter*, we cannot assent for the reasons
given above. But if he means the *former*, all will be correct.

[1674 logical printed p. 162 | IA leaf 180 | PDF p. 180]

5. The words which follow are also ambiguous: “and can be drawn and learned
from it.” For if he means all the things which could originally be drawn and
learned from the nature of the human being and of every other creature, as
also from the manner and sequence of creation and similar things, or which can
even now be drawn and learned with the aid of the divine word, which illuminates
our darkened and blinded minds and teaches them to infer conclusions from the
premises correctly and skillfully, this too can be admitted. But if he wishes
nothing else to be regarded as moral except what can now be drawn and learned
from the principles innate in us—principles, however, which are very greatly
weakened and all but extinguished—we cannot admit it, for the reasons explained
more fully above. 6. We know that Nature can teach sons that a father is to be
loved, honored, and obeyed in all things, and can teach Subjects to submit to a
lawful King with due obedience. But what will follow from this? Does Nature
teach nothing further? We know that Nature teaches that God too is to be obeyed
in all things. Will it then follow that no particular precept is taught by
Nature? In that case no precept of the Decalogue will be natural or moral,
which is absurd. Nature indeed teaches that God is to be worshiped and adored.
Does it not also teach some manner of worshiping him? If not, there will be no
natural worship. 7. Although Nature cannot teach a son or a subject those
particular duties which must be learned entirely from the positive will of a
father or King, there are nevertheless some particular duties in which a
positive command has or can have a place and which can nevertheless be called
natural or moral. For example, defending Parents, King, or Country against the
injuries of adversaries is moral, although here a positive determination or a
positive command from Parents or King is sometimes necessary. More examples
can be given.

What further does he say? “In like manner, natural right, which constitutes
moral good or evil, has been inscribed by God Most Good and Most Great upon
human nature, and is such before an external precept. To this belongs τὸ γνωστὸν
τὸ θεῖον, ‘what is known of the divine,’ and knowledge of the things which are
θεοπρεπῆ ἢ θεοειδῆ, ‘God-befitting or Godlike’—the things customarily
called the remnants of the divine image, whose guardian is conscience. On
these matters see the Apostle treating explicitly in Romans 1:19, 32 and 2:14,
15. All these things are restored through grace.” Reply: 1. That Natural Right
which constitutes moral good or evil is twofold. One is *Right Natural to God*,
the other *Natural to Us*, as explained above. 2. We concede that this Right
was inscribed upon human nature, that is, was revealed in such a way that the
human being as originally created could readily understand it through innate
knowledge. But that all the Natural Right which constitutes Moral good or evil
can now be perceived by us with equal ease is something I scarcely think the
adversary himself will assert, since he here acknowledges that only certain
remnants of the divine image now survive and that grace is needed by which the
things lost can be restored. 3. It is true that everything which belongs to
Natural Right is moral before an external precept, if “external precept” is
here taken—as it ought to be—for a superadded precept distinct from the natural
precept. From this it is clear that an external, or positive, precept does not
overturn the nature of a moral action, for the moral action remains such
notwithstanding this superadded precept. 4. We concede that τὸ γνωστὸν τὸ θεῖον,
“what is known of the divine,” belongs to this Natural Right, as do those things
which are θεοπρεπῆ ἢ θεοειδῆ, “God-befitting or Godlike.” But we further
add that certain other things belong to Natural Right, and therefore are
moral, beyond those which a human being, through such slender remnants of the
divine image,

[1674 logical printed p. 163 | IA leaf 181 | PDF p. 181]

can now perceive and recognize. And this we have proved more than sufficiently
in what was said above.
