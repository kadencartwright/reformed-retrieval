VERSE 12. For who knows what is good for a person in this life, during the
number of the days of his utterly vain life, which he passes like a shadow?
Who is there to tell a person what will happen after him under the Sun?

The argument is taken from the accompanying ignorance of a person who does
not know what is useful and advantageous to him. Consequently, by the very
ways and methods through which he persuades himself that he is providing for
himself and his own good in spending and distributing his resources, he harms
himself. In the management of food, because he does not know what benefits his
health, he often sins against his health. The same error is sometimes
committed in clothing. When he courts the favor of powerful people with
gifts, it often happens either that he provokes others whom he has not pleased
with gifts, or that, by raising someone whom he has rewarded to the hope and
expectation of further gifts and then disappointing that hope, he makes the
same person an enemy. Finally, because of this ignorance, he commonly does not
know when he ought to have an open hand in giving or a closed hand in
retaining.

This ignorance is confirmed by the accompanying brevity of this life, which
is illustrated by the comparison with a passing shadow. The brevity of our
age causes us to remain inexperienced and utterly ignorant of many things
that the practice and experience of a long life would teach us. Here piety
and the fear of God have greatly supported us; on this matter see Psalm 112.

Another argument is taken from the accompanying ignorance of the things that
will happen afterward and loom behind him. For when he goes astray in things
that are present and lie before his feet, he abuses riches to his own harm.
How much more will he do so in matters that do not yet exist and that commonly
happen after his death?
