CHAPTER III

Whether contingencies fall under God's absolute decree, and whether
conditional decrees are to be admitted in God. James Arminius, the Jesuits,
Suárez, Molina, Lessius, and others are called to examination. The
Jesuit-Arminian fate is proved.

The older Scholastics do not deny an absolute decree concerning future
contingents. The more recent ones, however—especially that detestable tribe of
Jesuits, Luis de Molina, Leonard Lessius, Pedro da Fonseca, Gregorio de
Valencia, Gabriel Vásquez, and Francisco Suárez—do so. Nor do James Arminius,
Corvinus, and Nicolaas Grevinckhoven wish to fall short of their masters; among
the pseudo-Lutherans, the source names “Balscha. Meisnerus.” James Arminius
acknowledges a certain determination which, once posited, leaves the free cause
absolutely indifferent

[Marginalia: Molina, *On Concord*, bk. 1, q. 22, art. 4, disp. 2; Lessius, *On
Efficacious Grace*, ch. 3; Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, bk. 6, ch. 2, q. 5, sec. 3;
Valencia, vol. 1, disp. 1, q. 22, pt. 3; Vásquez, pt. 1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 69 | IA leaf 87 | PDF p. 87]

to acting or not acting; but he denied a determination precisely to the one
side rather than the other. Thus he plays with words: his determination is not
a determination, but a release of the contingent cause from the direction of
providence, as Cicero, Epicurus, and all who denied God's providence taught.
Francisco Suárez, in his little work *On Aids*, book 1, *On Concurrence*,
chapter 16, sections 6 and 7, acknowledges in God an immanent predefinition by
which the end infallibly follows from a fitting application of means according
to God's foreknowledge. Yet this decree presupposes knowledge of contingents
which are future by nature before they are decreed. This is to decree—or
rather, to post-decree—future contingents after they are already future, which
is no decree at all.

Vásquez and Valencia are more candid in this matter, for they call it a
conditional decree. Yet Fonseca surpasses everyone's candor. In *Metaphysics*,
book 6, chapter 2, question 5, section 7, conclusion 2, he speaks thus: “A
possible and true predefinition of future free acts is nothing other than an
approving decree of the divine will, by which God wills absolutely that they
be future.” Elsewhere he calls it, without circumlocution, a
“post-definition.” But good angels and holy people approve a future good after
it is future, while reprobate angels and people approve every future evil after
it is future. Thus, if we accept bare knowledge, God's providence concerning
future contingents is no greater than that of intelligent creatures. Gabriel
Vásquez posits a concomitant decree, but with the same injury to

Corvinus, against Boger, pt. 2, ch. 2; Grevinckhoven, p. 363; Messin., *Sacred
Anthropology*, decade 1, q. 4.]

God's providence.

2. But I prove God's antecedent and absolute decree: first, by Scripture;
second, by reason; and third, by testimonies. Thus Scripture says in Ephesians
1, “He chose us that we should be holy,” and in 2 Thessalonians 1:13, “He chose
us for salvation through sanctification of the Spirit and faith held toward
the truth.” Therefore God chose us absolutely for free acts of holiness and
faith.

Suárez replies that this decree presupposes future acts performed freely in
accordance with the fitting motion of the will. But I answer as follows: either
God foreknows that future thing before he decrees it, or he does not. If the
former, Suárez deceives his reader, for what already is can no more be decreed
than yesterday's sunset can be decreed today. A future thing is not decreed
after it is future; a decree gives the thing being so that it may be. But what
is already foreknown as future already possesses being antecedently to the
decree. If, however, God foreknows it as not yet possessing being, then the
decree precedes the future contingent effect, which is precisely what we set
out to prove.

Second, the will can either resist the congruous decree or it cannot. If it
can, the decree is not congruous but incongruous. If it cannot, liberty's
indifference is as tightly circumscribed by this decree as by an absolute one;
and thus indifference is not of the essence of liberty, as the Jesuit teaches
elsewhere.

Third, Isaiah 14:27 says, “Jehovah has formed a counsel, and who shall make it
void?” Verse 24 says, “As I live, just as I have purposed, so shall it come to
pass.” Chapter 46:10 says, “My counsel shall stand; I will perform my delight.”
Verse 11 says, “I call from the east, out of Persia, a bird”—Cyrus, a most
expeditious warrior—“the executor of my counsel; I have spoken, and I will
bring”

[1651 logical printed p. 71 | IA leaf 89 | PDF p. 89]

“it to pass.” Hence the argument: what God has decreed comes to pass in such a
way that it cannot fail to come to pass. But God decreed the free overthrow of
the Chaldeans by the Medes and Persians. Therefore that free overthrow will
occur, and consequently a free effect, indeed one whose futurity is
unavoidable, falls under the absolute decree. The reasoning is the same for
all contingent things.

Now hear the Jesuit's distinction. “Who,” he says, “does not see that the
decree precedes the foreknowledge of the effect so decreed?” But who then does
not see, I say, that the Jesuit has been compelled to believe the truth? The
Jesuits and Arminians reply: Cyrus devastated the Chaldeans with consequent
necessity, but with no antecedent necessity from an absolute decree; rather,
it occurred from the foreknowledge of Cyrus's free choice. But to what do
Jehovah's words tend? Were the secure Chaldeans calling into question whether
they would be devastated by Cyrus, provided only that they were going to be
devastated?

Second, this new logic makes God's proof a sophism in which he would prove a
thing by itself. This is the conclusion to be proved: “Cyrus will destroy the
Chaldeans.” Proof: “God decreed by an invincible decree that Cyrus would
destroy the Chaldeans. Therefore Cyrus will destroy the Chaldeans.” The
inference belongs to God himself. The Arminians and Jesuits will prove the
antecedent this way: God's decree is certain and fixed from his foreknowledge
of Cyrus's free choice, by which Cyrus would freely determine himself to war;
and the certainty of the foreknowledge rests upon the certainty of the
foreknown thing—namely, that Cyrus will freely destroy the Chaldeans. For the
thing is not certain because God foreknew that it would occur; rather, the
foreknowledge is certain because the thing is certain. Therefore, from first
to last,

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Aids*, ch. 16, bk. 3, sec. 10.]

[1651 logical printed p. 72 | IA leaf 90 | PDF p. 90]

God argues thus: “Cyrus will destroy the Chaldeans because Cyrus will destroy
the Chaldeans.” Does not this fate throw everything into confusion? By the same
reasoning, every proof from God's decree, oath, and promise will be sophistical,
and the truth of Scripture will perish.

Third, if the certainty and necessity of the free devastation do not depend
upon God's decree, I will be permitted to argue according to the Jesuits and
Arminians in this way: God swore and decreed that Cyrus would destroy the
Chaldeans; therefore Cyrus will not destroy the Chaldeans. Thus God's argument
would prove contradictories if the free devastation possessed all its being
antecedently to the decree and had no necessity from the decree. For the causal
or logical connection between God's oath or decree and the nonoccurrence of the
free devastation would be just as close as that between the oath or decree and
the devastation.

Nor does Fonseca's approving decree help them. The thing is just as certainly
future as if God approved its futurition—indeed, the whole certainty of its
futurition precedes the approval no less than it precedes the decree.
Furthermore, an approving decree involves a contradiction in its object. The
object of a decree is what can either be or not be, according to the liberty of
God who decrees; but the object of approval is a being that already exists, and
what already is cannot fail to be. Therefore God can no more withhold approval
from the existence or futurition of future contingents which are future
antecedently to his decree than he can prevent what already is from ever being.
This is simply

[1651 logical printed p. 73 | IA leaf 91 | PDF p. 91]

impossible even for Jupiter, as Hesiod testifies.

4. John 15:16 says, “I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear
fruit.” Therefore Christ decreed that the apostles should freely do good works,
pray, plant churches, and preach. What then? Were they chosen for those works
on the basis of foreknowledge of the apostles' free determination? If so, the
apostles chose Christ, which the text denies. Moreover, the question will
return concerning that free determination: was it decreed conditionally or
absolutely? If conditionally, the question about that condition will recur
infinitely. Therefore one must necessarily stop at God's decree.

5. In Acts 22:14 Ananias says to Paul, “God chose you to know his will, to see
that Just One, and to hear his voice.” Therefore Paul was absolutely
predestined to proclaim the gospel most freely, so that he would be a witness
of the things he had seen, as verse 15 says. Arminius replies that this election
was Paul's temporal designation to an office, not an eternal decree. I answer:
first, this is said gratuitously and is not proved. Second, grant that it was a
temporal designation. Paul had been separated from his mother's womb for the
apostolate, Galatians 1. Thus an antecedent absolute designation will be
consistent with liberty, which overthrows their teachings; and in the same way
an eternal absolute election could be consistent with free acts.

They then say that this election was extraordinary. But what sober person
would listen to assertions dictated without proof? Let it be extraordinary;
nevertheless, it is irresistible and must be absolute, not grounded upon a
free deter-

[1651 logical printed p. 74 | IA leaf 92 | PDF p. 92]

mination. Yet Paul is said to have obeyed the call in Acts 26:19. Therefore he
freely preached the gospel, for, as they themselves teach, there is no
obedience without liberty. Second, Paul would in vain be set forth as an
example for all who would believe, 1 Timothy 1:15–16, if Paul obeyed in such a
way that he could not fail to obey while the rest, possessing indifferent
liberty and grace far less efficacious, could obey or not obey. Third, where a
threat has a place, our opponents loudly insist, no necessity of obeying
exists. But Paul was subject to threats; otherwise why would he have said,
“Woe is me if I do not preach the gospel”? Therefore Paul both obeyed freely
and was absolutely and irresistibly ordained to free acts. Otherwise Paul
would not have sinned if he had evaded the apostolic province by some
subterfuge.

6. Romans 8:29 says, “Those whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be
conformed to the image of God's Son.” But people are freely conformed to the
image of God's Son, both by the freest patience under the cross and by true
holiness. Nor does foreknowledge of future cooperation have any place here.
There will be no consolation beneath the cross from God's absolute
predestination if those who bear the cross would do so conditionally—that is,
antecedently to God's predestination.

7. First Thessalonians 3:3 says, “You yourselves know that we are appointed to
this”—that is, eternally constituted to suffer grievous things in the world.
Ephesians 2:10 says, “Created for good works, which God prepared that we should
walk in”

[1651 logical printed p. 75 | IA leaf 93 | PDF p. 93]

“them.” Therefore we are absolutely ordained to free acts. Arminius replies
that we were not created absolutely for good works, since they could walk in
the vanity of their own heart, chapter 4:17. I answer: first, that does not
follow. Second, it conflicts with the apostle's purpose. He proves that we are
not saved from works because good works, he says, are causally from God's
gracious ordination; but if they precede such an ordination, they are not
causally from it.

Second reply: Suárez says that at most one can infer only the benefit of
predestination by which God prepares supernatural aids for us, through which
we are assisted toward good works. Hence the Arminians contend that God
absolutely decreed the rule of holy living, but did not predetermine liberty to
free acts. I answer: first, they miserably butcher the text. Paul does not say
that we were created for grace or supernatural aids. What would that achieve
against those who made works a condition, since they did not deny those
things? He says instead that we were created for good works from grace.

Second, this does not concern the establishment of the gospel but our
preconstitution for obeying the gospel: “We,” he says, “are his workmanship,”
verse 10. Third, Paul could not infer what is denied in this fashion: “God
prepared the gospel and supernatural aids by which we might act well;
therefore we are not saved from works.” It does not follow. They themselves
deny this consequence, for most of those advocates of works, at least, joined
faith with works. Fourth, Arminius nevertheless contends that the gospel
concerning the salvation of believers was decreed absolutely even if no mortal
person were saved. The Jesuits will rightly not tolerate this,

[Marginalia: Suárez, little work *On the Aids of Grace*, bk. 3, ch. 15, sec.
7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 76 | IA leaf 94 | PDF p. 94]

for thus God's intention would be empty and his decree futile. Consequently,
the reasoning is the same whether God absolutely prescribed the gospel for
free agents or absolutely predefined free agents for the free obedience to be
rendered to the gospel. This answer therefore establishes our position.

8. In Luke 24:44 the Savior says, “The things written concerning me in the law
of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms must be fulfilled.” Verse 26 says,
“Christ had to suffer these things.” Therefore the piercing of his hands and
feet, the perforation of his side, the mockery, the beating of his body,
Judas's betrayal, and all things of this kind, which were done most freely,
occurred according to God's definite counsel, Acts 2:24.

What do the opponents say here? All those things occurred conditionally. But
on what condition? “If God willed it,” perhaps? Away with that! He decreed it,
if he decreed at all. Or did he decree to permit it because he foresaw the
enemies' wicked inclination? That is a frivolous begging of the question. For
that wicked inclination and the malevolent disposition of the Jews against
their own Messiah hold the chief place among those things which Christ—hated
without cause, as it was written in the law, John 15:25—had to suffer. The
question is whether that wicked inclination was decreed by God. If it was
decreed, was it decreed absolutely? Then we have what we sought. Or was it
decreed conditionally? Then the same stone must again be rolled: on what
condition? This, of course, is the mystery of Arminian “wisdom”: after all the
things concerning Christ have been fulfilled, and are future antecedently to
every act of the divine will, God willed those things to occur—

[1651 logical printed p. 77 | IA leaf 95 | PDF p. 95]

that is, if they already are, he wills and decrees them to be as they are. On
this principle I too decree the creation of the world and the redemption of
sinners after they already exist: I will that they should be. Indeed, in this
fashion the Devil decreed Job's torments, so far as acts of the will are
concerned.

9. Jeremiah 1:5 says, “Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you; and before
you came forth from the womb, I sanctified you and appointed you a prophet to
the nations.” Yet Jeremiah prophesied freely, being moved by threats and
promises, chapter 1:17 and chapter 15:19–20.

I further demonstrate that conditional decrees diametrically oppose God's
nature and all his attributes, as well as the whole duty of man and true
contingency; they deliver God over to fate and things to fortune's judgment.

The first argument is from God's providence, which a conditional decree
completely overturns. First, if things are decreed only conditionally, from a
foreknowledge of what they already are, then they possess being antecedently
to God's decree. But in that case they occur apart from God's intention and
therefore by chance. Fonseca replies that it does not follow that they occur by
chance, because after God foresaw that they would be, he intended them. Lessius
says that what we can neither foreknow nor avoid is casual and fortuitous.

I answer: first, Fonseca himself, forgetting his own position, says elsewhere
that those things happen fortuitously which occur apart from the intention of
a free cause. Aristotle says the same in *Physics* 2.5, as do the Coimbra
doctors: “Those things happen by fortune which occur not only apart from the
agent's knowledge but also apart from his intention.” Second, if bare

[Marginalia: First argument, from God's providence; Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, bk.
6, ch. 2, q. 5, sec. 11; Lessius, *On Efficacious Grace*, ch. 4; Fonseca,
*Metaphysics* 7, text 7, in the commentary, ch. 7; Coimbra College, commentary
on *Physics* 2, ch. 5, q. 1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 78 | IA leaf 96 | PDF p. 96]

knowledge of a thing, together with a volition that it be after it already is,
is sufficient for providence, then the person who finds treasure possesses
perfect providence concerning it after he has found it. He knows that the
treasure is there and not only wills and approves that it be there, but is also
filled with no small joy. Yet before he happens upon it by chance, he does not
have foreknowledge of the treasure and therefore, they might say, does not have
providence concerning it. I reply that in this case God's providence will be
more perfect than human providence only through the act of foreknowledge, not
the act of will. But in its formal conception providence includes a volition
of the thing, not bare knowledge.

Third, Fonseca states a contradiction: namely, that God, foreknowing that a
thing will be, intends that it be. For after the thing exists, one can no more
intend that it exist than decree the creation of the world today. Fourth, an
astrologer foreknows that an eclipse will occur and also wills it to occur at
the time he predicts so that he may look after his reputation. According to
Fonseca's reasoning, astrologers therefore have providence concerning eclipses
no less than God himself. Fifth, after a contingent thing is future God cannot
divert its futurition or avert the thing itself. Therefore he has only bare
foreknowledge concerning it.

10. God will also lose the praise of particular providence and all glory in
the coming forth of lots from the lap, in the killing of a person struck by an
axe as if by chance, and in the answer of the tongue, which Scripture says is
from God. For when three or four meditations are in

[Marginalia: Second argument; Exodus 21:13; Proverbs 16:33.]

[1651 logical printed p. 79 | IA leaf 97 | PDF p. 97]

the heart, no other reason can be given why one rather than another leaps to
the tongue, the other three having been set aside, than the determination of
divine providence.

Hear what Corvinus replies concerning the accidental killing of the traveler
in Exodus 21: “God did not expressly will that the axe should fall from the
man's hand onto the head of the person killed. Nor, according to that will—the
absolute will—did he direct the hand so that the axe would fall, and would fall
in precisely that way. Rather, when God foresaw this accident and could have
prevented it or directed the axe elsewhere, he was unwilling to prevent it or
direct it elsewhere.”

I answer: first, according to his custom he fraudulently says, “He did not
expressly will that the axe should fall,” instead of what he should have said:
“He did not will absolutely that the axe should fall.” But I ask whether God
willed conditionally that it fall. I demand the condition. None can be imagined
other than the hypothesis that the axe would fall. Behold the excellent
consolation both for the killer, if perhaps he has killed his own father, and
for the dying victim: the principle of death came from the axe, not from God!

They would say that the axe's falling came from God's conditional will. But
what condition can be imagined? For according to their teachings the axe was
going to fall antecedently to God's decree. O fortune! Must the condition be
sought from the will of the traveler who freely went to that place when the
axe fell? Then the miserable traveler seeks comfort in his own contingent
liberty, not in God. Providence has no place here.

Second, Corvinus acknowledges no act of providence here except some sort of—I
know not what—foresight or foreknowledge. But that

[Marginalia: Corvinus, against Molina, ch. 3, bk. 1, p. 14.]

[1651 logical printed p. 80 | IA leaf 98 | PDF p. 98]

is not the cause of either good or evil; it presupposes the futurition of each.
Third, he says, God foresaw the accident and was unwilling to direct the axe
elsewhere. Here there is no positive act of providence but a negative one,
namely, the nondirection of the axe.

Fourth, I press the point thus: he was unwilling to direct it elsewhere either
by an absolute will or by a conditional one. If the former, I have what I set
out to prove, for then the contingent accident was absolutely decreed by God.
If the latter is granted, I ask on what condition he was unwilling to direct
it elsewhere. Was it, namely, on the hypothesis that the traveler would freely
go there at that moment when his head would receive the axe's blow? But then it
is false to say that God could have directed the axe elsewhere and was
unwilling. Indeed, on the hypothesis that the traveler received the blow, God
could no more direct the axe elsewhere than he can cause what already is not
to be.

Indeed, if the axe determines itself to fall antecedently to God's decree, as
they teach, God could not direct the axe elsewhere, for then he would bring it
about that what already is should not be, which is simply impossible. Thus God,
constrained here by fate, could not forestall or prevent the traveler's death.

Fifth, if they say that God could have prevented the concurrence of these
three contingent acts—namely, the killer's act of striking, second, the
victim's act of journeying there, and third, the axe's falling—I restore the
argument: these three act contingently at the moment when the traveler is
killed. Therefore God either willed that at that

[1651 logical printed p. 81 | IA leaf 99 | PDF p. 99]

time they should act, by an absolute will—and our thesis still stands—or by a
conditional will. In the latter case I demand the condition from the
hypothesis, namely, that they would then act; and thus God could not prevent
the killing unless he opposed consequent necessity, which is simply
impossible. Here you see that despite God's will the killing can occur or not
occur, even against God's will, and that God is constrained by fate. Whom,
then, would the traveler pray to in the morning to escape an unforeseen death,
or to whom would he devoutly give thanks in the evening for having avoided
death? Not to God, since the killing was not effected by his will, for the
killing is antecedent to God's will. The Arminians must therefore establish
altars and sacrifices to fortune, or to contingency as a new goddess, compose
prayers to her, and make our Jehovah fall from his providence.

Sixth, once this conditional will is posited, Matthias is enrolled in the
company of the apostles on the hypothesis that the lot falls upon him. The
good lot comes to him on the hypothesis that it came to him, and Matthias
cannot fail to be elevated to the apostolate. Therefore there is no reason for
him to thank God on the ground that he was chosen by lot.

Seventh, one must also say that the many injuries inflicted upon the innocent
Savior and the many falls of people and angels into torments that will never
end occur by God's conditional will and therefore antecedently to God's
eternal intention and decree. Believe me: Arminianism will be mere atheism.

11. It is certain that an exact number of souls is decreed absolutely by God.
Therefore the free acts of

[1651 logical printed p. 82 | IA leaf 100 | PDF p. 100]

generation by which they are united to bodies are decreed absolutely by God.
Here one must either say that the number of the elect is not certain and
defined by God—which theological ears will not tolerate—or yield to the truth,
as Suárez does when he says that all the moral means of predestination are
probably defined absolutely by God. Consequently, he probably speaks falsely
when he says that free acts cannot be decreed absolutely by God.

Nor is Fonseca's claim worthy of a hearing: he says that souls are predefined
insofar as they are essential parts of the world, but not with respect to
their infusion into bodies. First, who would say that God predefines souls to
exist but does not predefine that they exist, when all David's members are
inscribed in God's book, Psalm 139:16? Second, on Fonseca's principles a
treasury of souls which are never to be united to bodies is possible with God,
if people should never reproduce, as they are free not to do. Who could
tolerate such conjectures?

12. This decree conflicts with God's omnipotence and therefore must not be
tolerated. Proof of the antecedent: if all free acts possess being antecedently
to God's will, they also possess it antecedently to God's omnipotence. Proof of
the connection: God's power, determined through his own free will, is the cause
of all things. Thus not even God can bring about that more acts of willing or
refusing exist than actually exist. Nor does it lie with God that more people
be saved than are saved, or fewer, or that more or fewer people or angels be
damned than

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Aids*, bk. 2, ch. 7, sec. 1; Fonseca,
*Metaphysics*, bk. 6, ch. 2, q. 5, sec. 11; second argument, from omnipotence.]

[1651 logical printed p. 83 | IA leaf 101 | PDF p. 101]

are damned. Certainly, if contingent effects are future antecedently to God's
decree, God cannot by his omnipotence bring it about that those which are
already future should not be future, any more than God can bring it about that
what already is should not be. For what is simply impossible involves a
contradiction and can in no way be the object of omnipotence.

4. It also conflicts with God's wisdom. On this account divine wisdom will
determine absolutely nothing concerning the order of human or angelic
volitions; yet no philosopher would posit an order without a first ordainer.
Proof of the connection: if Peter is going to will this before God has decreed
that he will it, it does not lie with God that Peter should will A before B and
B before C. Nor does it lie with God that Judah's scepter should be taken away
before Christ came, for notwithstanding God's will, the Romans could have
failed to take the scepter from Judah until the twentieth year after Christ's
resurrection. Likewise, the owner of the donkey could have refused the donkey
to the Lord on the particular day when the prophecy of Zechariah 9:9 was to be
fulfilled. A human being could have sinned before the angels despite the
decree. David could have failed to depart from Keilah until Saul arrived. And
infinitely many other things which happen this year could have happened in the
previous year, whatever God's wisdom determined to the contrary.

5. It conflicts with God's absolute liberty and his lordship over crea-

[Marginalia: fourth argument; fifth argument.]

[1651 logical printed p. 84 | IA leaf 102 | PDF p. 102]

tures. For if Peter believes antecedently to God's decree and Judas finally
does not believe, they constitute themselves as the formal objects of election
and reprobation in such a way that either Peter's election or Judas's
non-reprobation conflicts with God's justice and constancy. God's liberty in
predestination is therefore taken away.

13. There are two evasions here. First, the Jesuits say that God, foreknowing
acts of faith and unbelief through middle knowledge, placed some people in
such an order and such circumstances that he foresaw they would believe to the
end; but he placed those who would be damned in the contrary order. Thus, they
say, God's liberty remains intact.

I answer: first, I hear a Pelagian fiction. Second, they will be compelled to
admit here that human salvation and damnation arise from an absolute decree;
and why, then, will they not also establish the exercise of liberty from the
same decree? Indeed, after God has absolutely decreed to save some, according
to their doctrine those people can absolutely fail to believe to the end, and
thus God's decree will be futile. But they would say that God moves those who
are absolutely to be saved in the manner in which, according to his
foreknowledge, he sees that they will believe to the end. I answer in this way:
if antecedently to God's will they are not going to believe finally, they will
never believe even when the most congruous motion is applied.

Third, how does God will by an antecedent will the salvation of all people
without exception, as the Jesuits teach from 1 Timothy 2, and yet will that
some be absolutely damned and for that very reason place them in the order of
things in

[1651 logical printed p. 85 | IA leaf 103 | PDF p. 103]

which he foreknows that they will not finally believe and consequently will be
damned? I believe this is entirely incomprehensible to everyone whose brain is
sound.

Fourth, they will not escape by this fork either. The object of middle
knowledge is prior to that knowledge itself, for all knowledge presupposes its
object. Therefore God's free placement of those who are to be saved in such an
order of things, in which they will believe to the end, was prior by nature;
and the same is true of those who are to be damned. Thus, if they are placed in
the order in which they now stand antecedently to God's knowledge, they are
also placed there antecedently to God's decree, for the Jesuits teach that this
middle knowledge precedes God's decree. Nor can they deny that this free
placement and all God's free acts are the object of middle knowledge.
Therefore God still possesses no liberty in the salvation and damnation of
people, because they are going to be saved and damned before the act of
liberty.

Fifth, those who are to be damned and are placed in such an order either
believe necessarily or freely. If the former, Jesuit theology perishes. If the
latter, they can believe to the end; and consequently, notwithstanding that
placement, they make themselves the objects of election and reprobation
antecedently to God's decree. Those who are not to be saved can be saved, and
those who are damned can fail to be damned, even against God's will.
