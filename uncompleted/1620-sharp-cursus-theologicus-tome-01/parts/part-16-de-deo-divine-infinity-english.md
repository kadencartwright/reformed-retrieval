On the Infinity of God

Infinity is an incommunicable attribute of God by which all things in God are
signified to be without measure, whether of quantity or determination, so
that he is incomprehensible, Psalm 145:3, Job 11:

[1620 Tome I | col. 189 | BSB frame 00111]

7. For what is infinite cannot be comprehended by a finite thing, while God
is infinite with respect to all limits. The limits by which things are
determined are essence, quantity, time, and place. Limits of essence do not
apply to God, because he is infinite and undetermined both with respect to
his essence as it is in itself and with respect to his powers. For if God
could be determined, he would not be the first being.

The remaining limits also do not apply to God. Accordingly, with respect to
the absence of these limits from God, a threefold infinity is attributed to
God, namely eternity, immutability, and immensity.

On the Eternity of God

Eternity is an incommunicable attribute by which God is signified to be free
from beginning and end, prior and posterior, past and future. Accordingly,
the divine essence neither ever began nor will cease, Genesis 23:33,[^1]
Psalm 90:2, Isaiah 44:6 and 57:15, Romans 1:20 and 16:26, and 1 Timothy 1:17.
God, the maker of all things, is therefore also called the maker of the ages,
Hebrews 1:2. Boethius rightly says in book 5 of *The Consolation*: “God is the
whole and perfect possession at once of life without limit.”

It follows from these things that eternity is attributed to created things
improperly and only by communication, because although they will not have an
end, they nevertheless had a beginning.

The uses of the doctrine are:

1. Hence we learn what sort of God we worship.

2. What sort of people we will be because of this God, by participation.

3. This doctrine fortifies us against temptations, for it makes us certain of
salvation and election: of election, because he elects us from eternity; of
salvation, because our recompense will last for eter—

[1620 Tome I | col. 190 | BSB frame 00111]

—nity.

On the Immutability of God

Immutability is an incommunicable attribute by which the essence of God is
understood to be free from every corruption and alteration, and God to be
always like himself, so that he cannot cease to be what he is or begin to be
what he is not, Psalm 102:27, Malachi 3:6, and James 1:17. God is immutable
with respect to his nature, because he is most simply and most perfectly pure
act, free from every composition, and therefore cannot be dissolved,
corrupted, or grow old; with respect to accidents, because they do not apply
to God, and therefore neither does change of place, time, affections, and so
forth; and with respect to his will, because he does not change his will but
firmly keeps his decrees and covenants, Numbers 33:23,[^2] 1 Samuel 15:29, and
Romans 11:29.

Objections

1. God promised many things which he did not fulfill, such as that his seat
would be at Jerusalem forever, and so forth. Therefore.

Response. God’s promise is of two kinds, absolute or conditional. Again, a
conditional promise is of two kinds, either with a tacit or an express
condition. In the promise adduced, however, and in similar promises, the
condition of obedience to be rendered to God was inherent. If God did not
fulfill what was promised, the cause was that the condition previously
expressed was lacking.

2. God became man; therefore, he was changed.

Response. God became man not by conversion or change but by assumption of a
human nature. Therefore, the consequence is denied.

The use of the doctrine. 1. It is that we may know what sort of God we
worship, true and immutable.

2. What sort of people we will be after this life by God’s grace, namely
immutable.

[1620 Tome I | col. 191 | BSB frame 00112]

3. That we may not despair of salvation, and this because of God’s promises.

4. That we may bear adversities with a courageous spirit, because they are
sent by God, our immutable Father.

5. Hence it also follows that in the things which are God’s, namely faith,
hope, and charity, we ought to be immutable.

On the Immensity of God

Immensity is an incommunicable attribute by which the divine essence is
signified to be free from dimension—that is, from place, increase, or
decrease. Accordingly, God is neither increased nor diminished nor
circumscribed by place, but is everywhere, in all things and outside all
things. For what is everywhere is everywhere because it is immense, as in
Psalm 139:7, Isaiah 66:1, Jeremiah 23:23, Acts 7:49 and 17:27. He is in all
places and outside all places, so that he cannot be contained or bounded by
anything, 1 Kings 8:27 and Isaiah 40:12. For what is contained by a limit is
bounded either by the limit of its own essence, as angels are, or by some
limit surrounding it from without. But God can be bounded neither by the
limit of his own essence nor by another, because ubiquity admits neither an
internal nor an external measure. It is therefore proper to God alone, so
that neither angels, souls, nor Christ as man can be everywhere, because
whatever is everywhere is immense through itself and by its essence.

It is asked, however, how God is everywhere.

Response. He is everywhere not only by power but also by essence. Yet he is
not mixed into things or defiled by their filth, nor is his essence
multiplied, extended, or divided, nor does it exist differently in different
places. Rather, he is in the whole world and outside the whole, in each of its
parts and outside each. He is not in things as their form but as their
efficient, conserving, and moving cause. He is said, however, to be in hea—

[1620 Tome I | col. 192 | BSB frame 00112]

—ven and in the saints, and not to be with the impious, Matthew 6:9,
Deuteronomy 7:21, Numbers 14:24, 1 Corinthians 6:19, Psalm 23:4, and John
14:23, not by reason of his essence but of his operation and grace, because
he demonstrates his presence to the godly by more excellent effects. In
Christ, however, he is present insofar as Christ is Mediator, as a part of the
composite.

The use of the doctrine. This doctrine serves:

1. For God’s glory and for confirming his most simple perfection, since
although he is in the most filthy creatures, he is not contaminated by them.

2. It confirms providence, because he sees and governs all things, and so
forth.

3. It ought to be a spur to piety and to fleeing sin.

4. It ought to console us in adversities: God is everywhere; therefore, he
sees and governs our enemies and knows our condition.

5. Therefore, hypocrisy must be fled.

6. Therefore, it is not necessary for us to invoke angels.
