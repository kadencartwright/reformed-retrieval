I say, therefore, II. If this power of the keys was given by Christ to the body
of believers as such, it was given either when the church had not yet been
constituted but was to be constituted, or when the church had already been
constituted. If the former is said, it would follow either that the apostles
had no power at all or that they derived it from the believers, or the body of
believers. The former cannot be said, for Scripture establishes that this is
utterly false. Nor can the latter, because the apostles had their apostolic
office immediately from Christ, Galatians 1:1; Matthew 28:18–20, and all the
necessary qualifications, John 20:22–23. Indeed, they were immediately
designated and appointed by Christ, Luke 6:13, and so forth; Matthew 10:5–7,
and so forth; Mark 16:15–16; John 20:21; Acts 1:24–26. If the latter is said,
then that power had not been given when Christ said to Peter, “I will give you
the keys,” and so forth, because the Christian church had not yet been
constituted. Then let them prove that, in constituted churches, this power was
transferred by the apostles to the body of believers as such, and not to
ministers distinct from that body.

III. This power of the keys was given either to the body of believers alone,
or to believers together with ministers. The former cannot be said, because of
what has already been stated. If the latter is said, then it is asked whether
this power was given to the flock and pastors equally or unequally. If
*equally*, then, 1. this power is given as principally to pastors as to
believers. 2. It is not then transferred from believers to ministers. 3. It
must be supposed that all believers are no less endowed with the required
gifts than pastors; or it must be said that Christ gave power to those to whom
he did not give the gifts required for exercising that pow-

[Volume II; printed p. 169; ONB canvas 177]

er. If *unequally*, then the greater share was given either to pastors or to
believers. If to believers, then believers, not pastors, would more properly be
called *Pastors, Elders, Bishops, Leaders, Governors, Rulers, Stewards*, and so
forth, contrary to Ephesians 4:8, 11; 1 Timothy 5:17; Acts 20:28; Hebrews 13:7,
17, 22; 1 Thessalonians 5:12; Romans 12:8; 1 Corinthians 12:28 and 4:1–2. If to
pastors, there is no reason for pastors to borrow any power from believers. 2.
This greater power was undoubtedly given when Christ said to Peter, “I will
give you the keys.” And let the adversaries prove whence they possess that
power which they claim for themselves.

IV. Those to whom the power of the keys has been given may by perfect right
exercise that power, for power is given so that it may be exercised for the
good of the church and the advancement of edification, 2 Corinthians 8:10.
This is also clear from Matthew 16:19, where it is added, “and whatever you
bind,” and so forth. Authority and the execution of power are similarly joined
in John 20:21, 23. And who does not know that a power which is not brought into
act is vain? But believers cannot exercise this power. For they can exercise
it either when they have ministers—but to what end, then, were ministers given
and ordained?—or when they are destitute of ministers. But, 1. how will they
then prove that this body of believers, destitute of all ministers, is a
ministerial church? 2. By what testimony of Scripture will they prove this? 3.
Let an instance be given. 4. If they could exercise all this power without
rulers, there would be no need to appoint rulers and Christ would have
instituted ministers in vain. 5. Then all would be pastors and could administer
the word and sacraments, the contrary of which we have demonstrated; all would
be *Rulers, Governors, Bishops*, and so forth, and all would preside, which is
absurd. 6. The power of binding and loosing is an official power and is
therefore attributed to princes and governors in Psalm 105:20, 27; Judges
15:10; Psalm 149:8;

[Volume II; printed p. 170; ONB canvas 178]

Matthew 22:13; Mark 3:27; Acts 21:11 and 22:4; Jeremiah 40:4.

V. Apollonius argues as follows in the place cited above: “That position must
not be admitted which overturns the order instituted by Christ in the church.
In accordance with this order Christ established diverse integral parts in
the visible church, as in a heterogeneous and organic body, and gave them
their own particular functions and operations in that ecclesiastical body. For
he established in the church Eyes, Ears, Hands, Feet, those who preside, and
those who lead in the Lord; Overseers, Guardians, Pastors, Ambassadors in the
name of Christ, Heralds, Fathers, Builders, Sowers, and so forth; and the Flock,
People, Household, Field, Children, and those who are governed and are bound to
obey their overseers, and so forth. See Hebrews 13:17; 1 Thessalonians 5:12; 1
Timothy 5:17; 2 Corinthians 5:10; 1 Corinthians 4:1–2, 15 and 3:9, and so
forth. But if all in the church possess the power of the keys, the power of
building and governing the church through ecclesiastical censures, and so
forth, all will be Ambassadors, Overseers, Fathers, Pastors, Guardians, Eyes,
and Ears. For properly ecclesiastical and authoritative oversight and
government belong to those parts of the church as their own function.
Therefore the power of the keys and its exercise are not common to all the
members of the church, but are the proper function of Overseers and Pastors, to
whom the rod and that severe power have been delivered for the edification of
the church,” 1 Corinthians 4:21; 2 Corinthians 13:10.

VI. If the power and exercise of the keys has been given by Christ to
believers, Christ both promised and gave them the Spirit of government and
ministry, and those gifts necessarily required to exercise that power. This is
clear not only from the example of all those sent by God into the church—namely,
the prophets, Jeremiah 1:4, 10; Exodus 4:11–12; Ezekiel 2:1–2; Isaiah 6:6;
Exodus 3:11–12, and others under the Old Testament; and the apostles under the
New Testament, Matthew 28:19–20; John 20:21–23; Acts 9:17, and others—but also

[Volume II; printed p. 171; ONB canvas 179]

because, besides diversity of ecclesiastical *administrations* and
*operations*, there is also a diversity of gifts. For the *gifts* are diverse,
1 Corinthians 12:4–7. It is further clear because Christ, the wisdom of the
Father, Colossians 2:3, and faithful in his house as a son, Hebrews 3:6, was
unwilling to entrust the power of government to just anyone not furnished with
gifts. But I add the minor premise: Christ neither promised nor gave all
believers the Spirit of ministry or the gifts required for carrying out the
ministry. Indeed, Scripture testifies that these gifts were not granted to all,
but these gifts to some and other gifts to others, 1 Corinthians 12:8–11. And
experience confirms it.

VII. Apollonius argues as follows in the place cited above: “The canons and
rules set forth in Holy Scripture by which the just government of the church
and the due use of the keys are directed were not laid down for all believers
in the church, but for pastors and presbyters. Instruction is given by the
apostle to Timothy and Titus, and to other rulers, concerning how they ought to
conduct themselves in the house of God as faithful stewards—in the epistles to
Timothy and Titus, and also in 1 Corinthians chapters 3 and 4. The faults
committed in the illegitimate government of churches and abuse of the keys are
imputed by God as guilt not to all believers in the church, but to certain
persons, namely overseers, 1 Timothy 3:4–6; Titus 1:7; 1 Peter 5:3; Revelation
2:14, 20; 3 John verse 10. God promises the praise and reward for the
legitimate administration of the churches not to all believers in the church,
but to pastors and elders who preside well in the Lord, 1 Thessalonians 5:12;
Hebrews 13:17; 1 Timothy 5:17; 2 Timothy 4:4–5; 1 Peter 5:4–5; Revelation 2:2.
Therefore the government of churches and exercise of the keys were entrusted
by Christ to them alone.”

VIII. Those to whom the keys of the kingdom of heaven have been given, or to
whom the power of governing has been granted by Christ, are governors and are
called such in Scripture and regarded as such. Government and rulers, the
power of governing and governors, are correlatives which posit and remove each

[Volume II; printed p. 172; ONB canvas 180]

other. A person is called a governor because of the power of government, and
no one is called a governor unless the power of government belongs to him. But
the body of believers is never adorned with these titles designating the power
of governing as ecclesiastical officers are adorned with them. Believers are
never called Gods, as rulers are in John 10:33, 36, a title sometimes
attributed to judges, Psalm 82:1, 6; Exodus 21:6. It is not believers but
ministers who are called *leaders* in Hebrews 13:17, a title also attributed to
civil judges by the Septuagint translators in Joshua 13:21; Micah 3:9; Ezekiel
44:3; Daniel 3:2. So also Acts 23:24; Matthew 27:2; 1 Peter 2:19. See also the
other titles in Philippians 1:1; 1 Timothy 3:2; Acts 20:28; 1 Corinthians
12:28; Romans 12:8; 1 Timothy 5:17; 1 Thessalonians 5:12, appropriated not to
believers but to ministers. Indeed, 2. believers are distinguished in
opposition to those to whom these titles belong and are attributed. Ministers
are pastors, while the others are the *Flock*; ministers are those who preside,
while the others are those over whom they preside; and so with the rest. 3.
Believers not only are not governors and so forth, but are expressly commanded
to submit themselves to governors, obey them, and perform other duties, 1
Thessalonians 5:12; 1 Timothy 5:17; Hebrews 13:17.

IX. Then believers—not only men but also women, not only adults but also young
people, whether strong or weak, whether abounding in knowledge and experience
or not—would not only be able but also bound to exercise this power, and hence
to preach the word, administer the sacraments, examine cases, inflict censures,
and so forth. But this is false. The reason for the inference is that Christ
gives no one a talent to be buried in the earth; much less does he give anyone
some duty or office, or the power or authority to discharge an office, in
vain. All to whom Christ grants this ecclesiastical power are bound to exercise
it diligently and seriously and to devote themselves to this one thing, unless
they wish to incur the gravest consequences. For they will have to give an
account to God for bad adminis-

[Volume II; printed p. 173; ONB canvas 181]

tration or neglect of office.

X. Then, in order to exercise this power faithfully, believers are bound to
neglect their own functions and particular callings, contrary to 1 Corinthians
7:20–21; Ephesians 6:5; Colossians 2:22; 1 Thessalonians 4:11. The reason is
that Scripture says in 2 Timothy 2:4, “No one who serves as a soldier entangles
himself in the affairs of this life, so that he may please the one who enlisted
him as a soldier.” The nature of the matter itself will also require this. For
if we speak only of matters pertaining to jurisdiction, so many difficulties
arise in examining and deciding intricate cases, examining witnesses, and
considering other necessary matters that they must either neglect their own
occupation or subordinate it to this one. This is especially so if we consider
that not all the church’s members are equally perceptive or equally able to
discuss obscure and intricate questions with ease, and yet it is necessary
that everyone be satisfied, so that not even the smallest doubt or any scruple
remains for anyone. Whether these and other matters—which I judge it better to
leave unspoken than express—confirm our argument is understood by prudent
persons and by those engaged in the diligent exercise of discipline.

XI. In this way a democratic, purely Popular, and Brownist government is
introduced into the church. For the church and all the members of the church
are the subjects in which the keys, or the power of the keys, reside;
consequently, the church and each of its members possess the whole of
ecclesiastical government and all its parts. All would govern, all would be
eyes, and all would be stewards. This, however, would not be government but
sheer, utter anarchy, whose author God is not, 1 Corinthians 14:33. For
whatever is done by ecclesiastical officers can be done by others; on this
account nothing distinctive is attributed to ministers. Indeed, the
adversaries say that private believers can exercise all jurisdiction, even
excommunication itself, against all their ecclesiastical officers. They,
therefore, are governors.

[Volume II; printed p. 174; ONB canvas 182]

XII. This opinion takes away and destroys the order instituted by Christ in
the church—namely, between pastor and flock, and between those who govern and
those who are governed. It abolishes the particular offices instituted by
Christ, for it makes all persons Pastors, all Teachers, all Governors, and so
forth. Thus, according to this position, no one properly takes honor to
himself, even if he assumes to himself every honor that exists in the church,
and even though God attributes this honor only to certain chosen persons, Acts
26:16; Ephesians 3:7; Colossians 1:25 and 4:7; 1 Thessalonians 3:2; 1 Timothy
4:6. It will follow from this opinion that all the directions for governing the
church contained in Paul’s epistles to Timothy and Titus pertain as much to all
believers as to ecclesiastical officers. These matters are treated more fully
in the writings of Presbyterians against the Independents, and all the
arguments for the contrary position are solidly refuted. It will therefore be
superfluous to add more here, and it would indeed be lengthy to call all the
adversaries’ objections to examination. Here I will only report the position
of certain Independents who wish to appear to depart further from the opinion
of the Brownists or Barrowists concerning the power of the keys and the
division and subjection of that power. For in this way they attempt to blunt
the force of some of our arguments. I will examine it briefly.

They therefore say that there is a key of *faith*, which is the same as the key
of *knowledge*, Luke 11:52; and a key of *order*, Colossians 2:5–6, which they
define as follows: “It is the power by which the individual members of the
church walk in an orderly manner according to the station they have in the
church and assist others to walk in an orderly manner.” They subdivide this
into the key of *Power* or *Interest*, which in Scripture is called *Liberty*,
Galatians 5:13, and the key of *Authority* or *Government*. They call the
latter “a moral power in a superior order or state, binding or loosing an
inferior with respect to subjection.” These are the divisions of the keys, or
of the power of the keys. We must see what subjects they assign to each. They
say that the key

[Volume II; printed p. 175; ONB canvas 183]

of *knowledge* or *faith* belongs “to all believers, whether they are joined to
a particular church or not.” They say that the key of *order* belongs “to all
those who are in ecclesiastical Order, whether presbyters or brethren,” and
that the key of *liberty* belongs to “the brethren of the Church,” through
which, among other things, they can “together with the presbyters inquire into,
hear, and judge public scandals, mark notorious offenders with censures, and
loose the penitent.” They say the key of *authority* or *government* was
entrusted to the *Presbyters* of the church, so that acts of government belong
to their office. Next, they say that “the particular church,” or “body of
saints,” is “the primary subject” of all “ecclesiastical offices,” together
with all their “spiritual gifts” and “power,” but that “the Apostles” were the
“primary subject” of apostolic power. Further, they say that when a particular
church walks in “truth” and “peace,” “the brethren” are the primary subject of
“Ecclesiastical liberty”; “the presbyters,” of ecclesiastical “authority”; and
“both,” of all ecclesiastical “power” which ought to be exercised among them.
This is their position—truly strange, invented, novel, and unheard of until
now. But we briefly reply as follows. (1) By the church to which the keys were
given they understand neither the invisible and mystical church nor the
visible catholic church, but a particular congregation, or body of believers.
How absurd this is, how contrary to Christ’s purpose, how opposed to the text
itself and its context, and how opposed to the commentaries of the doctors,
Fathers, and theologians, both ancient and recent, I leave to others to judge.
(2) Here they have various divisions of the keys never heard before. But when
they define the keys generally, they say that “they are Christ’s ordinances
which he instituted to be administered in the church, such as the
administration of the preaching of the word, the sacraments, and censures.” By
this definition they testify that they attribute all the power of the keys to
ecclesiastical officers, contrary to what was just said, or else that they
attribute the administration of these ordinances of Christ to believers,
contra-

[Volume II; printed p. 176; ONB canvas 184]

ry to what was stated above. It is therefore evident that they openly
contradict themselves. (3) When they so distribute the keys, as we have seen,
that they assign some to apostles, others to presbyters, and still others to
believers, they must say that Peter represented the Apostles, Presbyters, and
Believers. But how can this be? And how can they say elsewhere that Peter
represented Believers alone, as we heard above? (4) They acknowledge that no
key of the kingdom of heaven is devoid of power; nevertheless, the key they
call the key of *faith*, *apprehension*, or *knowledge* is devoid of every
power, because it is distinguished from the key of *order*, which includes
within itself the key of *power* and of *authority*. (5) We must distinguish
between *knowledge* and the *key of knowledge*. Knowledge is common, but the
key of knowledge which the interpreters of the law took away, Luke 11:52, was
rather the public exposition and interpretation of the word. (6) What is the
force of this *key of apprehension*? “Through it,” they say, “we enter into the
state of grace.” But then the state of grace and a particular church are
convertible terms, for by the kingdom of heaven they understand, as stated
above, a particular church. Further, this key will be given to those outside
the church, because through it entry into the church lies open. How then were
all the keys given to the church? (7) They define the key of *Order* badly. For
“order” is not taken here morally and passively, as they define it and as it is
taken by the apostle in Colossians 2:5. See Calvin and Davenant on the passage.
Rather, it is taken actively and ecclesiastically for an active power which
acts upon others; this is properly the key of order and belongs only to
ministers. (8) They say that the key of *order* is divided into the key of
*power* and the key of *authority*. But what kind of legitimate power is
entirely devoid of authority? (9) They say that *power* is the same as
*interest*, *liberty*, and *privilege*. Yet elsewhere they say that this same
power is the same as

[Volume II; printed p. 177; ONB canvas 185]

*exousia* and *kratos*, terms which denote authority. (10) By what right can
*liberty, privilege*, or *interest* be called *power*? The private people in a
kingdom or republic enjoy their privileges and immunities or liberties, but no
one therefore says that the people possess the civil keys or are endowed with
power. The *liberty* of which the apostle speaks in Galatians 5:13 is something
very different, namely deliverance from the yoke of the ceremonial law, as the
context demonstrates; see and compare verses 1, 11, 15, 16. (11) The pastor
seems to be entirely excluded from the key of *order*. For authority does not
belong to him, and he has a different power from that possessed by women,
infants, and other members of the church. (12) They say that *authority* is a
*moral power*. Therefore whoever has the moral power to set forth the commands
of God will have authority. (13) They say that this authority belongs to
superiors, by which they bind or loose inferiors. Yet according to their
doctrine it belongs to inferiors as well, for they say that the whole church
binds and looses and that the Brethren exercise jurisdiction together with the
Presbyters—even jurisdiction over the presbyters, whom they can depose and even
excommunicate. Therefore authority and office have been given to them also,
for they themselves say that Christ gave *jurisdiction* to no one without also
giving the *office*. (14) When they speak about the subjects of these keys,
they say they are diverse; but against every divine and human law the body of
believers is numbered among these subjects, for all the keys were given to
Peter together. And when they were given to him, he could not be considered
both as a private person and as a public ruler of the church. (15) They say
that the *key of apprehension* was given to all believers. Therefore some keys
were given to women and children. Indeed, all believers, whether women or
children, will be permitted to proclaim the word; for by the key of
*apprehension* we can understand nothing other than the key of *doctrine*, or
else it will not be an ecclesiastical key. (16) They must distinguish the key
of *order* from *office*, or

[Volume II; printed p. 178; ONB canvas 186]

else they must say that private believers also discharge some office, for they
say that the key of *order* pertains to all. (17) They say that through the
*key of liberty* the election of pastors belongs to all believers. But since
they say that the essence of calling consists in election and that ordination
is only its mere completion, do they not say that through this *key of liberty*
and *privilege* believers can appoint pastors? Is this *liberty* then
*authority*? (18) When they say that through this key believers can, together
with ecclesiastical officers, examine, hear, and judge scandals and mark the
guilty with censures, is this not authority and jurisdiction, since they mean
something other than a mere judgment of discretion? Indeed, they say that
believers can inflict censures without ecclesiastical officers, and can even
censure the ecclesiastical officers themselves. This is certainly something
other than privilege or liberty; it is pure jurisdiction and the highest
authority. (19) When they say that the particular church is the primary
subject of all ecclesiastical functions, and so forth, then either all the
members of that body will *formally* be ecclesiastical officers—for they are
the primary subject of these functions and of this authority—or they can
appoint ecclesiastical officers. But how do they prove this? The passages
cited, namely 1 Corinthians 3:22; 14:23; and 12:28, prove that the church is the
object, not the subject, of these functions. Nor are they to be understood of
a particular church, but of the catholic church. Does the statement in 1
Corinthians 3 pertain to no one who is not a member of some particular church?
Further, since this promise pertains to women no less than to men, women also
will be the subject of this power. (20) They say that the apostles were the
primary subject of apostolic power. But then, 1. a particular church is not the
primary subject of all offices and all power, or else there are two primary
subjects. 2. Why, then, will Pastors not also be the primary subject of the
pastoral office and power? (21) When they say that, in a particular church,
the brethren are the primary sub-

[Volume II; printed p. 179; ONB canvas 187]

ject of liberty, the Presbyters of ecclesiastical authority, and both of all
ecclesiastical power, do they not contradict themselves, since above they said
that the particular church is the primary subject of all functions and all
power, and so forth? For here they attribute a certain authority—indeed, all
ecclesiastical authority—to the Rulers. (22) They wish the matter to stand this
way when they “walk together in truth and peace.” But what if heresy or schism
arises? To whom, then, will all this power belong? To the believers? Or to the
pastors? But what if a schism arises between the pastors and believers? Will
it belong to a superior synod? By no means. Synods can only give counsel or
state what they think, but they have no authority authoritatively to decide
controversies. They can only assert and vindicate the truth declaratively, but
they can exercise no jurisdiction, according to the adversaries’ doctrine.
Enough on this argument.

*Third*, to confirm the first part of our assertion, we argue as follows. If
this power to ordain ecclesiastical ministers belonged to the people, then the
apostles, Paul and Barnabas, labored in vain in performing this work. Why, I
ask, did they go to Lystra, Iconium, and Antioch to appoint ministers if the
people could have done it? To what end did Paul leave Titus in Crete to ordain
presbyters if the people could have done it by right? It is vain to do through
more persons what can be done with equal convenience through fewer.

*Fourth*, the people did not even possess the right to ordain deacons. In Acts
6:3 the apostles say, “Select from among you seven men—whom we may appoint over
this work.” The people selected, but the apostles appointed. If the body of
believers had been permitted to ordain, why did they set the seven selected
men before the apostles, who prayed and laid their hands on them, verse 6? If
the contrary position had been true, the apostles would have said, Select
those whom you ju-

[Volume II; printed p. 180; ONB canvas 188]

dge suitable and appoint them over this work; ordain them and lay your hands
upon them. For it is
yours to do this, or you can do it by perfect right. But nothing of the kind is
heard. The apostles willingly attribute to the people what pertained to the
people; but what pertained to themselves, they themselves perform.

*Fifth*, if this right pertained to the people, why does Paul impose this
burden upon Timothy and Titus? Why does he prescribe to them what sort of men
those to be ordained should be? Why does he command them to act cautiously and
carefully in this business? It must surely be said either that all these
precepts were given to Timothy and Titus not as public ministers of the church,
which is contrary to the text, but as private believers, which is absurd; or
that by divine right this right pertained only to ecclesiastical ministers and
still pertains to them. For if no trace or vestige of this power granted to the
people appears in any of these epistles, it is probable that no such power was
given to them, since directions and instructions for performing ordination are
set forth in these epistles.

*Sixth*, if by divine right this right to ordain pastors belongs to the private
people, then every individual among the people must also be furnished with the
gifts necessary to ordain, test, and examine those men, lest perhaps some whom
God did not wish to be ordained are ordained. But experience sufficiently
establishes that the people as a whole are not so furnished with gifts that
they can test the candidates in their knowledge of the original languages,
sacred history, chronology, and other branches of learning whose knowledge is
very useful and required in pastors. Nor can they all judge with sufficient
skill the gift of preaching and rightly dividing the word. They will answer in
vain that believers have the anointing and know Christ’s voice. For although
believers are gifted with this anointing, by which they are certainly led to
salvation, and know all things neces-

[Volume II; printed p. 181; ONB canvas 189]

sary for salvation, it was nevertheless not given so that through it they
might be proficient in
all arts and branches of learning of this kind. Further, it would follow from
this that women and young girls possess this power of ordaining, because this
anointing is given to them no less than to others, and they, when endowed with
faith, know the voice of Christ.

*Seventh*, ordination is a part of special ecclesiastical jurisdiction. For
through it ecclesiastical power is transferred to a person. It is an
empowered, or authoritative, sending—the application of a ministry or function
to a person—by which the capacity and power to discharge that function is
instrumentally granted. The office itself and the power of the office are
given through ordination and transferred to the person ordained. But no
ecclesiastical jurisdiction properly belongs to the private people, as we
showed above. And some of the Independents freely confess this, namely that
authority or jurisdiction does not belong to the people. The adversaries,
however, reply that ordination is not an act of jurisdiction but rather of
order, because it does not give a minister his being or constitute him, but
rather confirms him in office. *Reply.* 1. That ordination gives a Minister
his being is clear from the following. (1) Through ordination a person who was
not previously a pastor becomes a pastor or minister. (2) Through ordination
he “is appointed” or placed over others—“that you may appoint presbyters,” Paul
says to Titus. This is the same as appointing magistrates or prefects, that is,
constituting or placing Rulers over others. See Luke 22:42; Acts 7:10. (3)
Through ordination—ministerially, that is—the ministerial office itself is
given, 1 Timothy 4:14; 2 Timothy 1:6. (4) It is an empowered, or authoritative,
sending to proclaim the gospel, administer the sacraments, and perform the
other things that pertain to that office, Romans 10:15. (5) We proved above
that Calling or election does not give the ministry its being. 2. If ordination
is only confirmation in office, then it must suppose that all those ordained
were already

[Volume II; printed p. 182; ONB canvas 190]

constituted officers. Therefore all those whom Timothy or Titus was going to
ordain were constituted pastors and presbyters before Timothy and Titus
ordained them. Why, then, I ask, was Titus left in Crete? Was it to complete
something or add some accidental, unnecessary appendage? 3. If there is any
ecclesiastical jurisdiction, it appears in ordination. For there officers of
Christ’s visible kingdom are constituted, placed over their office,
authoritatively sent to discharge that office, solemnly set apart, and so
forth.

*Eighth*, if by reason of divine institution this right to ordain Ministers
belongs to the body of believers because, as the first and primary subject, it
possesses the power of the keys entrusted to it, then Pastors are not permitted
to ordain other Pastors. For no one is permitted to claim for himself a power
granted by Christ to another or to intrude into an office imposed upon others.
But this is absurd, because it is certain that Ecclesiastical Officers ordained
others, Acts 6:3, 6; 1 Timothy 4:14; Acts 13:2–3; Titus 1:5; 1 Timothy 5:22. And
who will say that women, young women, and other believers can ordain by a
better right than pastors?

*Ninth*, if this right of ordaining pastors belongs to the people, the people
will be superior and the pastor inferior; for as the apostle says in Hebrews
7:7, “without controversy the lesser is blessed by the greater.” The people
then possess the power of judging and preside over the pastors. If they say
that the people do not judge or ordain as Ecclesiastical Officers, they are
ridiculous, for they do what belongs to the office of Ecclesiastical Officers,
and they do it by reason of the power of the keys which they say has been
granted to them. “Believers,” they say, “possess a power divided among
individuals, and they give it united to one person; in this way, although they
are not Rulers, they can appoint Rulers.” *Reply.* They do not possess such a
divided power. This can be established by no testimony of Scripture and

[Volume II; printed p. 183; ONB canvas 191]

no sound argument. 2. Therefore they cannot give it united, because it is a
mere nonentity. “Believers,” they say, “possess the power of judgment, not of
office.” *Reply.* The power of judgment is the power of judging
authoritatively and juridically—for they are not speaking about the judgment
of discretion—and this does not differ from the power of office with respect
to its subject, although it can differ with respect to its formal concept. For
whoever possesses official power also possesses authoritative and official
judgment, and no one else does.

*Tenth*, Apollonius uses this argument in the place cited above, page 69:
“Because the power to suspend or degrade Ministers does not belong to the
multitude. For whoever possesses the authority to take away an ecclesiastical
ministry also possesses the authority to confer it; just as a thing is
contracted, so it is also dissolved. But nowhere in Holy Scripture is it
entrusted to the people to mark false Ministers or Teachers by ecclesiastical
judgment or remove them from the ministry. Rather, this is required of the
Angels of the Churches and their presbyters,” Titus 1:10, 13; Revelation 2:2,
14–16, 20; Acts 20:28–29. The adversaries turn the edge of this argument
against us, saying that it belongs to the same persons to institute and
destitute, for the people, they say, possess the power to mark pastors with
censures and depose them. They attempt to prove this by these two testimonies,
Matthew 7:15; Philippians 3:2. But, 1. if these testimonies prove anything,
they will prove that this power of deposing or degrading pastors belongs also
to women and young women. For they ought to beware of false prophets, dogs,
and evil workers. These and similar precepts pertain to all. 2. If a pastor is
a heretic and there are no other presbyters or pastors who, after examining
the matter, can formally and according to the order prescribed by Christ
remove him from office, the people can indeed reject or drive him away, or
refuse to regard him as their own pastor. But private believers cannot depose
him. The adversaries answer in vain that it follows from this that a spe-

[Volume II; printed p. 184; ONB canvas 192]

cific form can be destroyed while its generic nature is not destroyed. For
this is not a genus and species. A pastor attached to a certain flock is not a
species of pastor, but an individual pastor with this accidental attachment:
that he is attached to a particular place. A Pastor, insofar as he has a
relation to the universal church, is not a genus; for one who is such a pastor
is just as much an individual pastor as when his ministry is applied to a
particular place. Therefore, when this accident is removed, the pastor remains
a pastor, just as a Physician remains a Physician even if he no longer
prescribes medicines for this or that particular person but is rejected by
him. Nor is the saying valid here, “when one of two correlatives is removed,
the other is removed.” For when Peter, to whom a physician was applying
medicine, dies, the relation which had existed between that Physician and
Peter is removed; nevertheless, the Physician remains a Physician. So when a
flock rejects its pastor, the relation between that flock and the pastor is
indeed removed, so that he is no longer that flock’s pastor and that flock is
not this pastor’s flock. Nevertheless, the Pastor remains a pastor. 3. It
would follow from this that the Flock is above the Pastor; for those who
juridically condemn and punish are, to that extent, above those whom they judge
and mark with censures. In vain they say that pastors are above the flock with
respect to office, government, act, and exercise, but that the flock is above
pastors with respect to censures. For this is what we deny, and it cannot be
proved. It would also follow that the imposition or infliction of censures is
not part of government, which is absurd.

The adversaries *object*, I. “Ordination is an act of the church’s power, which
it ought not to transfer to others. Therefore, when it is destitute of
ecclesiastical officers, it is bound by no law to approach other churches in
order to obtain from them pastors ordained by the laying on of hands.” *Reply.*
1. If ordination is an act of the power of the church as distinct from Pastors,
ecclesiastical officers will not be permitted to lay on hands. For the
adversaries do not wish Ecclesiastical Officers as such to be the church or a
part of the church, but mere accidents of the chur-

[Volume II; printed p. 185; ONB canvas 193]

ch. 2. By equal right it would follow that members of the church cannot, when
destitute of pastors, approach another church to receive baptism or partake of
the Lord’s Supper. 3. It would also follow from this that a pastor cannot
discharge a pastor’s office outside his own church.

They *object*, II. “If the people can elect Rulers, they can also ordain,
because election is something greater than ordination, which depends upon
election as its necessary antecedent and is nothing other than the completion
of election, or the admission of a person into possession of the office to
which he previously had a right through election.” *Reply.* It is clear from
Scripture that ordination is something greater. For when election was
performed by the people, ordination was performed by the apostles, Acts 6, and
is an act of the presbytery, 1 Timothy 4:14. 2. Although election precedes
ordination in time or occurs simultaneously with it, ordination nevertheless
precedes in the order of nature, because an election preceding ordination is
not properly the election of a pastor, since the man elected is not yet a
pastor. 3. It can also happen that the man elected is never ordained because
the examiners perhaps find him unsuitable and not furnished with gifts.
Therefore, through that election he had no right to that office except
remotely and conditionally, provided he was found qualified. 4. We said above
that Ordination is something other than the completion of election: namely, a
supernatural act of the presbytery, separating a man to a holy calling and
authoritatively sending him. Election is not anything of that kind, for it
only applies a pastor’s ministry to this flock or place. The people cannot send
a pastor to themselves, but only elect such a pastor or designate a man to be
ordained. Hear Apollonius in the place cited above, page 68: “Nomination or
election does not transfer the power of the ministry to the Person Elected,
but only designates the person to whom, according to the order instituted, it
may legitimately be trans-
