XXVI. On pages 70, 71, and following, he proceeds to explain usage of speech,
of which he speaks in the last place. First he says that *[the usage of speech
is properly that which lays down the law for Interpretation]*; and on page 72
he says that *[interpretation depends chiefly upon that usage]*; and on page
87: *[To discourse*, he says, *I refer usage; to the subject matters, reason;
but I maintain that the Spirit causes us to use both correctly. We nevertheless
wish this to be understood in such a way that, from the nature of
interpretation, we always assign the foremost part to usage.]* The question
instituted here concerns the certain and infallible norm for interpreting the
Scriptures, which is commonly called the infallible Interpreter. The
*Exercitator*, however, ascribes this right and privilege to Philosophy or
Reason; and we have already seen how little *Wolzogen* recoils from this
position. Here, however, he appears to assert something peculiar: namely, that
this right is to be attributed chiefly to usage of speech, and in this he
wishes to appear to dissent from the *Exercitator*. Yet the *Exercitator* would
by no means exclude that usage of speech but would likewise presuppose it. As
for the orthodox, they speak in no such way. [We say, states *Walaeus in the
Synopsis of Disputations, disputation 5, thesis 23*, that Sacred Scripture is
its own Interpreter—or, rather, that God speaks in the Scriptures and through
the Scriptures.] *Wendelin speaks differently in his System of Theology,
Prolegomena, chapter 3, thesis 6*: [Interpretation, he says, is the genuine
explanation of disputed passages according to the norm of the Holy Spirit
speaking in Scripture.] And *in the explanation*: [The supreme right of
Interpreting the Scriptures and deciding Theological controversies rests with
the Holy Spirit

[Page 129 | ONB canvas 183 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000183]

speaking in Scripture, or with Scripture, which is the voice of the Holy
Spirit.] *Polanus, in his Syntagma of Theology, book 1, chapter 45, question
4*, says: [The supreme power or authority of interpreting Scripture and of
judging concerning its Interpretation rests with God—or Christ the Lord—or
the Holy Spirit speaking through Scripture and in Scripture.] And a little
later, in thesis 5: [Sacred Scripture is the minister of the supreme judge,
because it is the voice of God; it is the *Testament* of God. It is the Epistle
of God, through which he addresses us and expounds his judgment to us. Sacred
Scripture speaks and judges in its own manner; it is therefore rightly
established as Interpreter and judge, though properly a ministerial one. Yet
by metonymy the supreme judge may also be established, just as if Christ
speaking in Scripture were said to be the supreme judge. Thus Scripture
interprets itself and judges every Interpretation as the voice of God.] So too
in the same place, question 5: [The norm for interpreting Scripture and for
judging whether the Interpretation of Sacred Scripture is true or false is
Sacred Scripture itself, which is the voice of God.] *Maresius, in the first
locus of his System*, thesis 54: [From these things, he says, it is sufficiently
and more than sufficiently clear that the Scriptures are thus the sole primary
and unbending norm of faith, so that the decision and judgment of controversies
ought not to be taken from elsewhere. Nor do we admit any real distinction
here, as the Papists and Socinians do, between the norm and the Supreme Judge,
except insofar as Scripture itself can be distinguished from God speaking in
it.] The most illustrious *Voetius, in Selected Disputations, part 5, page 426,
in the disputation On the Judge and Norm of Faith*, answers in this way the
question, *What is the norm of the Interpretation of Scripture?* [For inferior
judges or Interpreters, Scripture alone is the principle and the certain,
infallible, inflexible norm of faith or of things to be believed, of
interpretation, and of judgment; from it no appeal can or ought to be made.]
We readily grant that this usage of speech is always presupposed and that, when

[Page 130 | ONB canvas 184 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000184]

the question concerns verbal Interpretation or some translation, the closest
attention must be given to that usage. But in the question concerning the
inflexible or infallible norm for Interpreting the Scriptures, the Reformed
have never hitherto attributed this right to that usage of speech. Let
*Franzius be heard in the preface to his book On the Interpretation of
Scripture, quoting Luther, as cited by Maccovius in Common Places, page 49*:
[Before I report the opinions of others about the meaning, he says, I warn you
that when you read the expositions of the Rabbis, you should in no way give
credence to them. Grammar is indeed necessary and true, but it ought not to
govern the subject matters but to serve them. The blinded Jews have lost all
knowledge of the subject matters and dispute only grammatically about words.
Thus, because they have no knowledge of the subject matters, they dwell only
on explaining words but do not attain the true meaning.] And a little later:
[These things, however, are not to be taken in such a way as though I condemn
the study of grammar, which is altogether necessary; but unless along with
grammar you also learn the subject matters themselves, you will never become a
good teacher.] And again: [For knowledge is of two kinds, one of what the name
means and the other of what the thing is. One who has no knowledge of the
thing will not be aided by knowledge of the name.]
