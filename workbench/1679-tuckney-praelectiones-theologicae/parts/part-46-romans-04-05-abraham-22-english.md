refute this second necessity of imputation with his third argument by

the imperfections of these virtues are not without sin, according to
Augustine's statement, “whatever falls short of what it ought to be is from
fault.” If in the preceding argument we proved that positive sin, which is
the greater matter, dwells in the saints, it will certainly be less
difficult to prove that while they live here they possess some imperfections
and deficiencies in these theological virtues, and consequently in the
whole of inherent grace. We therefore cannot be justified by them, but need
the imputation of Christ's righteousness all the more necessarily in order
to be justified from them.

To answer Bellarmine's proofs to some extent, we first wish the following
general points to be observed:

1. He repeatedly emphasizes that faith, hope, and love, and consequently
the whole of inherent righteousness, *can be* perfect in this life. We say
that the possibility is insufficient. If we are justified by it, it must
actually be perfect. Adam could have persevered in his state of integrity,
but actual perseverance was required. Because he did not render it, he fell
from that state and from the justification of that covenant. Similarly, if
justification in the covenant of grace consisted in the perfection of
inherent grace, possible perfection would not suffice; it would have to be
actual. The following considerations will establish that neither claim is
true.

2. Piscator and Tilenius rightly observed that even if inherent grace
eventually became perfect in this life, it was not always actually so. No
one ascends from childish and imperfect age to maturity except gradually,
step by step, 1 Corinthians 13:12. Forgiveness is necessary to expiate the
whole preceding imperfection, and for that purpose the imputation of the
satisfaction made by Christ. A perfection of inherent grace arriving so
late, if it ever arrived here, could not suffice. It would render only the
present duty then incumbent and would not discharge the former debt under
which we lay.

3. Even if faith were perfect in this life and thereby justified us, it
would be so far from removing the imputation of Christ's righteousness that
it would necessarily require it. Faith justifies only insofar as it lays
hold of Christ and his righteousness, John 1:12. This righteousness, by
which we are righteous, is given and credited on God's part just as it is
received by faith on ours.

4. The same can be said of the whole inherent righteousness which Bellarmine
places in faith, hope, and love.

Even if it became perfect here at some point, as he wishes, it would not
remove the imputation of Christ's righteousness but presuppose it. Whatever
perfection inherent righteousness possesses can be given or possessed only
through his imputation. Whatever it may be, Christ merited that it be given
and infused into us, but his merits cannot be communicated to us except by
imputation.

Moreover, as to the fact itself, we deny that any such perfection of
inherent righteousness, Christ alone excepted, has ever existed or ever
will exist in even the holiest men in this life.

As for Bellarmine's various proofs, we need not pursue them individually. It
will suffice to gather them into a few bundles like weeds destined to be
burned, which we will now touch upon lightly.

[1679 printed p. 259 | IA leaf 267]

First, some of them say nothing at all to this question. To prove that faith
can be perfect here, Bellarmine adduces Peter's statement: “that the testing
of your faith, much more precious than gold tested by fire, may be found to
praise and glory at the revelation of our Lord.” Gold which endures fire,
he says, is perfect; therefore so is the faith likened to it. As though all
gold that endures fire were wholly purified of every particle of dross, even
if refined seven times! Many martyrs endured fiery trial, yet, contrary to
Castellio's charge that they were badly instructed, they felt the
weaknesses of the flesh even amid the flames and most humbly sought pardon
for their sins. Their courage in enduring such great and terrible things
must be attributed to God's almighty support, not the complete perfection
of their faith, since divine power is consummated in human weakness,
especially then as at other times.

Its twin is the passage Bellarmine adduces from Hebrews 6 to establish the
perfection of hope, where our hope is called “a sure and steadfast anchor of
the soul.” What does this prove about its complete perfection? What if the
description refers not to the hope immediately preceding but to security,
as Grotius held? Or if the Apostle indicates what it is and can do in
itself, it must nevertheless be understood, as Grotius adds, on condition
that we use it properly, which we do not always do. Even if we did and the
words were understood of hope, does an anchor's being sure and steadfast
make it deficient in nothing and entirely perfect in every respect? Not at
all. It is sure and steadfast because it is fixed in Christ. Our entire
safety and firmness is due to the tenacity of his hand, not the perfection
of our faith or hope, which sometimes wavers amid mountainous waves, as
Peter's once did. Yet Christ preserved him unharmed with his outstretched
hand while rebuking him: “O you of little faith, why did you doubt?” Peter
Lombard says in book 3, distinction 31, that faith and hope will be abolished
in the future life—which Bellarmine had also said of faith in this very
chapter—because they exist only in part in this life. Therefore, by his own
judgment, they are imperfect.

Refer here also the other passages he adduces: “The saints through faith
conquered kingdoms, worked righteousness, and obtained promises”; “This is
the victory that overcomes the world, our faith”; “Resist him, firm in the
faith”; “In all things taking the shield of faith, with which you can
extinguish all the flaming darts of the evil one.” None of these proves the
perfection of faith in this life. By God's assisting and helping grace,
faith can do all these things—conquer the world and Satan and even purify
the heart—although it is not completely perfect. It does so with Christ as
leader and under Christ's auspices, in whom “God chose the weak things of
the world to shame the strong.” Thus the saints “were made strong out of
weakness,” Hebrews 11:34. See weakness and power together: human weakness
joined to divine power! Though feeble in themselves, they were made strong
in God by his strength, so that the woman Jael might sing the victory song,
“My soul trampled strength.”

These arguments therefore possess neither the force nor even the appearance
of proof.
