QUESTION X

ON THE PURITY OF THE ORIGINAL TEXTS

Have the original texts of the Old and New Testaments come down to us pure and
uncorrupted?—Affirmative, against the Roman Catholics.

I. This question is raised for us by the Roman Catholics, who attack the
purity of the original texts in order that they may more easily secure
authenticity for their Vulgate version and lead us away to the tribunal of the
church.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 97 | IA leaf 151 | PDF p. 151]

II. By the name *original texts* we do not mean the autographs written by the
hand of Moses, the prophets, and the apostles, which are acknowledged to be
missing today. We mean their apographs, which go by that name because they
present to us that Word of God in the very words in which the sacred writers
recorded it under the immediate inspiration of the Holy Spirit.

III. The question is not whether the original texts are so pure that no errors
have crept into many sacred codices through the injury of time, the
carelessness of copyists, or the malice of Jews or heretics. Both sides
acknowledge this, and the variant readings which Beza and Robert Estienne
carefully noted in the Greek, and the Jews in the Hebrew, bear sufficient
witness to it. Rather, the question is whether the original texts—that is, the
Hebrew and Greek codices—have been so corrupted, whether by copyists through
carelessness or by Jews and heretics through malice, that they can no longer
be regarded as the judge of controversies and the standard by which
absolutely all versions must be tested. The Roman Catholics affirm this; we
deny it.

IV. Not all Roman Catholics, however, hold that opinion. There are not a few
whom they call *Hebraists* who have acknowledged the purity of the original
texts and have even expressly defended it, such as Sixtus of Siena,
*Bibliotheca sancta*, book 8; Bañez on 1 Thomas, question 1, article 8;
Andradius, *Defense of the Council of Trent*, book 4; Driedo, book 2, *On
Dogmas*; Arias Montanus, in the preface to the Hebrew Bible; Johannes Isaac,
against Lindanus; Jacques Bonfrère, in the prolegomenon to Sacred Scripture,
chapter 12, §5; Simeon de Muis, *Letter on the Truth of the Hebrew*, page 17;
and many others. But others, on the contrary, strenuously press the corruption
of the original texts, such as Stapleton, Lindanus, Cano, Cotton, Morinus,
Perron, Gordon, and the like. Some follow a middle way: they are unwilling to
assert that the original texts are corrupt, yet they assert that they do not
flow with such purity and integrity that everything in the versions ought to
be examined and corrected by them. This is the opinion of Bellarmine, *On the
Word of God*, book 2, chapter 2, who is found in this matter, as not seldom
elsewhere, to be insufficiently consistent with himself.

[Marginal heading: That the original texts are not corrupt is proved by the
providence of God.]

V. That the original texts are not corrupt is demonstrated by: 1. The
providence of God. It is repugnant to that providence that God should have
willed books to be written for human salvation by “God-inspired” men
(θεοπνεύστοις), and should have willed them to remain until the end of the
world so that saving waters might be drawn from them, yet should have
permitted them to be so falsified that they were unfit for this end. And since
new revelations are not to be expected after God has recorded his whole will
concerning the doctrine of salvation in the books of Scripture, what is more
injurious to God, who promised that he would always be present with the
church, than to assert that he allowed the books which contain this doctrine
to be corrupted to such a degree that they could not be the canon of faith?

[Marginal heading: By the faithfulness of the church.]

2. The faithfulness of the Christian church and its immense labor in
preserving the codices. For since Christians have always devoted themselves
with great care to keeping this sacred deposit undefiled, it is not credible
that they either falsified it or allowed anyone to falsify it.

[Marginal heading: By the religious devotion of the Jews.]

3. The religious devotion of the Jews, who treated the sacred codices with
great fidelity and diligence, even to the point of superstition. Hence
Josephus, in book 2 of *Against Apion*, says that after the course of centuries
no one dared to add, remove, or alter anything in the Jews’ own books, and
that it was so deeply ingrained in them to regard it honorable to die for
Scripture. Philo, in the book *On the Departure of the Children of Israel from
Egypt* cited by Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book 8, chapter 2, goes
further when he asserts that down to his own times, over a span of more than
two thousand years, not even a word in the law of the Hebrews had been
changed, and that any Jew would die a hundred times rather than allow the law
to be changed in anything. Indeed, they are found to be ridiculously
superstitious about the sacred codex to such a degree that, if a corrected
book of the law falls to the ground,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 98 | IA leaf 152 | PDF p. 152]

they prescribe a fast and say it must be feared that the whole universe will
return to its former chaos. So far are they from enduring fraud against the
sacred codex.

[Marginal heading: By the diligence of the Masoretes.]

4. The diligence of the Masoretes concerning not only verses and words but
even the individual letters. Together with all variations in pointing and
writing, they not only kept them numbered but also committed them to writing,
so that no room, or even the least suspicion of room, might be given to
falsifying hands. Arias Montanus presses this argument in his preface to the
Bible.

[Marginal heading: By the multitude of copies.]

5. The multitude of copies. For since the sacred codices had been dispersed
far and wide, by what means could they all have been corrupted, whether by the
injury of copyists or by the malice of falsifiers? Augustine, *The City of
God*, book 15, chapter 12: “Far be it that any prudent person should believe
that the Jews, however great their perversity and malice, could have
accomplished so much in codices so numerous and so widely dispersed.” Vives
says this ought to be opposed to those who allege that the Hebrew codices of
the Old Testament and the Greek codices of the New were falsified and
corrupted, so that the truth of the sacred books might not be sought from
those original texts.

VI. 6. If the original texts had been corrupted, this was done either before
Christ or afterward; but neither can be said. Not before Christ, because
Christ, who reproved various corruptions of doctrine, would not have concealed
this, nor would he have endured using corrupted books. Was the Lord so little
concerned for the salvation of his own that he gave no warning, either by
himself or through the apostles, even by a word, that the books of Moses and
the prophets had been falsified, while meanwhile he refuted the Jews from
those books—to what purpose, however, if they were corrupted and adulterated?—
and invited and urged his disciples to read and search those books? Not after
Christ, both because copies dispersed among Christians would have rendered
such attempts ineffectual and because no trace of such corruption occurs. For
if anything of this kind had happened, why are the passages which Christ and
the apostles cite from Moses and the prophets read in the same way today as
formerly and not at all corrupted? Why do Origen and Jerome, who excelled in
knowledge of the sacred language, so expressly acquit the Jews of this charge?
Therefore, if the corruption was committed neither before nor after Christ,
it follows that it was never committed. Bellarmine pursues this argument in
*On the Word of God*, book 2, chapter 2.

VII. 7. The Jews neither wished nor were able to corrupt the original texts.
Apart from their religious devotion, of which we have spoken, consideration
of the sacred texts proves that they did not wish to do so. For if they had
wished to corrupt anything, they would surely have corrupted the oracles which
speak about Christ and confirm the Christian faith. Who would believe that,
if they did this from hatred of Christians, as is supposed, they falsified
those passages from which they derive no advantage against Christians, but
left untouched those in which Christians locate the foundation of gospel
truth as in its principal proofs? Yet the matter stands entirely thus:
whatever things are said to have been corrupted by the Jews harm Christians
little or not at all, while the chief oracles about Christ remain intact and
are much clearer and “more emphatic” (ἐμφατικώτερα) in the Hebrew than in the
versions, as Jerome, letter 74 to Marcella; Johannes Isaac against Lindanus,
book 2; and Andradius, book 2 of the *Defense of the Council of Trent*, observe.
That the Jews could not have done it, even had they wished it most intensely,
is demonstrated not only by the multitude of copies but also by the vigilance
of Christians, whose copies not even all the Jews together could have
corrupted, and by the provident wisdom of God. If this wisdom does not allow
one jot or tittle to pass from the law without being fulfilled, Matthew 5:18,
much less would it have allowed the body of heavenly doctrine to be undermined
by the Jews and so great a treasure to be taken from us. Rather, as Bellarmine
rightly notes, God “willed for this purpose to disperse the Jews throughout
the whole world and to have them carry around the books of the law and the
prophets, so that our enemies would furnish testimony to Christian truth,”
*On the Word of God*, book 2, chapter 2, argument 5. Hence Augustine calls the
Jews “a record-bearing nation, carrying the law and the prophets, as servants
are accustomed to carry books, so that the former, by carrying them, might

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 99 | IA leaf 153 | PDF p. 153]

grow weary and the latter, by reading them, might make progress. For they
serve us. The Jews are like casket-bearers and librarians who carry the
codices for us who study,” *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, book 22, chapter
23. “Enemies in their hearts, witnesses in their codices,” as he says
elsewhere.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. Although various corruptions could have crept into the Hebrew codices
through the carelessness of copyists or the injury of time, they do not
therefore cease to be the canon of faith and morals. For besides the fact that
the corruptions concern matters of less importance which do not touch faith
and morals, as Bellarmine himself admits in *On the Word of God*, book 2,
chapter 2—and he therefore denies that they impair the integrity of
Scripture—they are not universal in absolutely all codices, nor are they of
such a kind that they cannot easily be corrected by comparison with Scripture
itself and with various codices.

IX. The hatred of the Jews toward Christians could indeed have been a remote
cause of the corruption of the original texts, but one which could be
restrained by another and higher cause, namely, the providence of God. That
providence made provision for Christians, no less than for the Jews, that
there should be a sure rule of faith and that the gospel should be derived
from indubitable foundations. This could not have happened if God had
permitted the original texts to be corrupted.

X. The difference between the Septuagint version and the original text does
not imply that the text is corrupt, but rather that the version is defective.
Jerome already acknowledged this in his time, in the preface to Deuteronomy,
in the preface to Chronicles, and in the letter to Sunnia and Fretela.
Bellarmine, *On the Word of God*, book 2, chapter 6, says that it is so corrupt
and defective that it seems entirely different, so that it is no longer safe
to correct the Hebrew or Latin texts from the Greek codices.

XI. The *Keri* and *Cethib*, which are commonly numbered at 848, are so far
from corrupting the text that they instead display the variant readings of
various copies, by which every corruption by innovators is excluded. The
*Chasir* and *Jother* also belong here; they indicate grammatical deficiencies
or redundancies. From these it is evident that the Masoretes had an almost
superstitious zeal to preserve the text, not the least desire to corrupt it.

XII. The *Tikkun Sopherim*, or Corrections of the Scribes, which number only
18, do not presuppose that anything in the text was corrupted. Otherwise,
Christ, if they had been made before him, or the orthodox fathers, if they had
occurred after Christ, would not have passed them by without censure. Nor are
they necessary corrections, as is clear to the reader, but rather elegant
readings and changes not so much of the sense as of the words. They were made
either by the men of the Great Synagogue, whose head was the “God-inspired”
(θεόπνευστος) Ezra, who after the return from the Babylonian captivity
restored the scattered or damaged copies of the sacred books to integrity and
arranged them in the order we have; or by the authors themselves, who, in the
manner of rhetoricians, correct in the text by an *epanorthosis* what they had
said. But the matter itself declares these corrections to be unnecessary,
because the sense does not cease to be complete even if the words are retained
as they stand. See Glassius, *Sacred Philology*, section 1, book 1, treatise 1,
*On the Purity of the Hebrew Text*.

XIII. The similarity of certain letters can indeed cause some errors to creep
into various codices through the negligence of copyists, but they were not so
universal that they could not be corrected from others, especially after the
diligence of the Masoretes, who counted not only all the words but also the
letters, as often as they occurred in the text.

XIV. The Masoretic work is so far from being an argument for the corruption
of the original texts that, on the contrary, it was instituted to guard
against errors, so that not even one tittle could thereafter be changed or
lost.

XV. Although the apostle in Romans 10:18 renders by φθόγγον (“sound”) what had
been expressed in Psalm 19:5 by קום, it does not follow that the Hebrew text is
corrupt and that קום, “their line,” was substituted for קולם, “their voice” or
“their sound.” For קו does not signify only an extended line or a plumb line,
but also a line

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 100 | IA leaf 154 | PDF p. 154]

written, or a letter by which elementary pupils are instructed. Isaiah 28:10
used this word to designate the crude infancy of the Israelite people when he
says that they must be taught “precept after precept, line after line,” in the
manner of children. Thus the psalmist says, “Day teaches day, and night
reveals knowledge to the next.” The word φθόγγος corresponds well to this
word, since it is used not only for a sound but also for the written form of a
letter, just as we speak of a written diphthong and vowel. Moreover, Paul does
not properly cite this passage, but only accommodates it allusively and
anagogically to the preaching of the gospel done by the apostles, attending
to the sense rather than the words.

XVI. Corruption of the words is one thing; corruption of the interpretation
is another. The Jews could corrupt the interpretation of the passage in
Isaiah 9:6, so that the words ויקרא שמו, “and he will call his name,” are
referred to the Father who calls, not to Christ who is called. But they did
not corrupt the words themselves, because whether they are rendered passively
or actively the matter comes to the same thing. For by an idiom of the Hebrew
language an active future without an expressed subject often has a passive
meaning, as Ribera holds on Micah 2:17, and thus third-person verbs taken
impersonally can be rendered sometimes actively and sometimes passively.
Therefore the Hebrew reading, “and he will call his name,” must not be
changed, but explained by supplying the subject—not *God the Father*, whom the
Jews supply, but *everyone*, namely, everyone among the faithful, “will call
his name,” that is, Christ’s name. To make this clearer, it was rendered
passively, “His name will be called.” So too in Jeremiah 23:6 the corruption
lies in the interpretation, not in the words, which are correctly rendered
either in the singular, “he will call him,” as they read with reference to the
preceding nominative, *Israel and Judah*, as the Septuagint translates; or in
the plural, “they will call,” as Pagninus, Vatablus, and Arias Montanus
translate after the Chaldean, Syriac, Arabic, and Vulgate. Jerome has both
readings, “he will call” and “they will call,” on this passage, and no one
among the ancients said that this passage was corrupted.

XVII. Since at Genesis 49:10 the three Targums—Onkelos, the Jerusalem Targum,
and Jonathan—and Kimchi understand the Messiah by שילה, it is clear that the
Jews did not corrupt this passage to prove that the Messiah had not yet come.
Add that the name שילה, which is attributed to the Messiah in the Talmud,
tractate *Sanhedrin*, chapter 11, opposes the Jews and indicates Christ no less
than the word שילה, “sent,” which they contend was there—whether it is derived
from שיל, which signifies “son,” or from שלה, which suggests “peaceful,” or,
as the Septuagint follows and seems more suitable, stands for אשר לו, ᾧ
ἀπόκειται, “for whom it is reserved,” “whose is the kingdom,” as a similar
phrase is read in Ezekiel 21:32.

XVIII. The passage in Zechariah 9:9 is not corrupt when it says that the
Messiah will be a king who is righteous and נושע. For this word can either be
taken passively to denote that Christ was “saved,” as the apostle speaks in
Hebrews 5:7, from death; or that he saved himself, as is said in Isaiah 63:3;
or it is used actively in a deponent form, as not a few forms are found among
the Hebrews, such as נשבע, “he swore,” and נצל, “he despoiled.” It will then be
a participle signifying the same thing as מושיע, “Deliverer, Savior.”

XIX. Although Exodus 12:40 says that the sojourn of the children of Israel in
Egypt was four hundred and thirty years, which cannot be understood of their
stay in Egypt, since that was only 215 years, but of the stay they made both
in the land of Canaan and in Egypt, as the Samaritan and Greek texts explain,
the Hebrew codex therefore ought not to be regarded as corrupt. Rather, this
is a synecdoche which mentions Egypt alone because it was the principal exile
of the Israelites, the designation being taken from the more important part.

XX. No verses are omitted from Psalm 14, for those which are cited in Romans
3:11, 12,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 101 | IA leaf 155 | PDF p. 155]

and so forth, were not taken from here by the apostle but gathered into one
from several psalms—Psalms 5, 11, 36, and 140—and from Isaiah 59, as Jerome
taught in the preface to book 16 of his commentary on Isaiah.

XXI. In 1 Corinthians 15:47 the corruption is not in the Greek text but in the
Vulgate version, which omitted the name Κύριος (“Lord”), by which Christ is
distinguished here, so that it may be evident that he is the Lord Jehovah, not
a mere man. Thus the antithesis between the first and second Adam is much
stronger: “The first man is of the earth, earthly, but the second man is the
Lord from heaven.”

XXII. Although the δοξολογία (“doxology”) which occurs in the conclusion of
the Lord’s Prayer in Matthew 6:13 does not occur in Luke, chapter 11, or in
various copies, it does not follow that the passage is corrupt. The Lord could
have set forth the same form of prayer twice: first, without the doxology in
the private instruction of the disciples, and then to the mixed multitude,
where he added it. Nor is it unusual for one evangelist to pass over what
another relates, since they did not all consider it necessary to write down
everything. Thus Matthew 6:33 has, “Seek the kingdom of God and his
righteousness,” but Luke 6:31 simply has, “Seek the kingdom of God.”
Therefore, what Luke omitted should not be erased but supplied from Matthew,
since each is “God-inspired” (θεόπνευστος), especially since this passage
occurs in all the Greek codices of Matthew, as Erasmus and Bellarmine admit.

XXIII. Although some codices read in Romans 12:11, καιρῷ δουλεύοντες, “serving
the time,” this does not occur in all of them. Indeed, Franciscus Lucas
testifies that he saw six in which the reading was Κυρίῳ (“the Lord”). Beza
asserts that it is so read in every one of the most approved codices, and
Domingo de Soto observes that this reading is now universally extant in both
Greek and Latin.

XXIV. It is certain that all the Greek codices disagree with the Latin ones at
1 John 4:3. Where the Greek codices have, “every spirit which does not confess
that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh,” the Latin codices read, “every spirit
which dissolves Jesus.” It does not follow that the original texts are
corrupt, because the Greek reading is both more majestic and far stronger
against Nestorius and Eutyches.

XXV. Corruption is one thing; a variant reading is another. We acknowledge
that there are many variant readings in both the Old and New Testaments which
arise from comparison of various codices, but we deny that there is at least a
universal corruption.

XXVI. It is one thing to speak of the attempt of heretics to corrupt certain
codices, which we readily concede. To this pertain the complaints of the
fathers, such as Irenaeus concerning Marcion, book 11, chapter 29; Origen on
Romans 16:13; and Theodoret concerning Tatian, book 1, *Heresies*. It is
another thing to speak of their success, or of a complete and universal
corruption, which we deny, both because of the providence of God, which did not
allow them to accomplish what they intended, and because of the diligence of
the orthodox fathers, who possessed various codices and kept them immune from
corruptions.
