# On Certain Articles

28 April 1556

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin presentation at CO
X/1 264–266. CO reports that Theodore Beza translated the work from French.
The unlocated French wording has not been reconstructed: this English is
accountable to Beza's printed Latin witness and does not present its wording as
Calvin-authored Latin. No wording from Beaty and Farley's modern translation
supplied the English.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 264]

Dearest brother, because in lending money at interest it very often happens
that many things occur contrary to right and equity, it is not sufficiently
safe for me simply to answer your question: namely, what rate of lawful return
on money should be established. For it will sometimes happen that not even the
smallest return can be received from money without offense against God and
injury to one's neighbor. I therefore wish you to hold me excused if I do not
prescribe here the fixed rule you expect.

A certain rate has indeed been established by law in this city. But it does
not follow from this that a creditor may in good conscience exact a return up
to that rate from a poor person when the latter would thereby be oppressed in
some way.

There is another consideration which restrains me from answering you more
fully. I know that you ask me this only in order to reprove a certain other
person who permits himself everything in this matter without taking me into
his counsel, a man who perhaps would not even deign to listen. Therefore, in
my place, let that rule of natural law itself answer him which the Lord Jesus
Christ established as part of the sum of the law and the prophets: we should
not do to another what we would not wish done to us.

As for the widow, if she is able to take her children away with her, nothing
ought to be more precious to her than their souls. Therefore, if the Lord
extends his hand to her so that she may emerge from that mire—or rather, from
that abyss—she will act with little humanity, not to say as a Christian, if
she leaves them behind. But because it is not clear to me what their ages and
wishes are, or what means are suitable for accomplishing this, I leave those
things to her to weigh individually. Whatever the case, she cannot willingly
leave them in this way without knowing that she will obtain for herself a
lasting grief from it.

There is greater difficulty in the case of a married woman. I cannot advise
her to separate from her husband unless she is driven by some greater force
and reduced to an extremity—if he treats her intolerably on account of
religion or for some other reason. I do not define that intolerable degree as
merely occurring whenever she happens to be treated rather harshly at home.
She must display every endurance before she resorts to that remedy.

But if she cannot remain safe from idolatry except at open risk to her life,
and indeed her husband presses upon her so that he persecutes her himself,
then she will be permitted to care for herself by flight. She is not to do so
with an intention of turning away from her husband and deserting him, however
harsh and intolerable he may have shown himself toward her, but only so that
she may avoid the evident danger. In the meantime it may happen,

### [CO X/1, p. 265]

by the Lord's action, that the husband's heart, though so savage, may grow
gentle and be brought into order. But it is unlawful to sin against God for
the favor of any mortal.

Because you are so concerned about others, I hope all the more that you will
consider your own duty toward that great heavenly Father, by whom you have
been given the light of the Gospel of his Son, in which salvation is laid up
for us. And if you have begun well until now, all the more in the future

### [CO X/1, p. 266]

you must strive and advance earnestly every day in every way. Since so many
corruptions flourish there, beware of supposing that you can discharge your
duty in any other way than by devoting yourself single-mindedly to the
glorification of God's name and to the proper flourishing of his worship and
honor among your own people.

May the Lord grant that what I ask of you in his name, he himself, moved by my
prayers for you, may also accomplish in reality. 28 April 1556.

## Translator's notes

1. CO's source note identifies Beza's Latin as translated from French. The
   English translates that derivative witness and does not claim to recover
   Calvin's French diction.
2. *Foenus* and *fructus pecuniae* concern lending at interest and the return
   on money. The answer refuses to infer moral permission from a legally fixed
   rate and makes the debtor's possible oppression decisive; it does not give
   a universally permissible percentage.
3. The object of *quem etiam fortasse ne audire quidem dignaretur* is not
   expressed with complete clarity in context. The English leaves “listen”
   without an object rather than deciding whether the third party would refuse
   to hear Calvin, the addressee, or the proposed correction.
4. The widow and the married woman introduce separate cases. In the latter,
   *non animo divertendi a viro et illius deserendi* presents flight from
   immediate danger as lacking an intention to separate permanently or desert
   the husband. The passage should not be collapsed into a general rule about
   either remaining in danger or dissolving a marriage.
5. CO p. 264 divides *fa-ciente*. The Latin joins *faciente* and moves the p.
   265 marker before the whole word; the English marker correspondingly
   precedes “by the Lord's action.” The p. 265/266 boundary divides no word.
6. The body supplies its own date, 28 April 1556, but no place, recipient's
   name, farewell, or signature. “This city” in the lending discussion is not
   enough by itself to insert an unprinted place into the closing metadata.
