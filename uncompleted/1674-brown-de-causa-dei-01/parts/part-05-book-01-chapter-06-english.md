## CHAPTER VI

### On the law of nature

From what was said in the preceding chapter about *natural right*, we can
easily perceive what the law of nature is. For they do not differ greatly from
one another. *Right* is something consequent upon law, so that whatever is
commanded by law is called *right*. That statement of Cicero in book 1 of *On
the Laws* shows this: “Those to whom reason has been given by nature have also
been given right reason; therefore they have also been given law, which is
right reason in commanding and forbidding. If law, then right also; but all
have reason; right, therefore, has been given to all.” Hence the expression,
“to act with the fullest right.” Yet right and law are often confused and
taken for the same thing, as in that statement of Cicero: “If this is less
fully prescribed by civil right, it has nevertheless been sanctioned by the
law of nature and the common right of nations.” Here, however, when we speak
of the law of nature, we speak of right insofar as it proximately connotes the
authority of the one commanding, enjoining, or sanctioning such a thing. So
also, when we spoke above of right, we spoke of the law of nature too, but
insofar as right proximately connoted its effect in a human being, or the duty
enjoined upon a human being.

This natural law is variously described by Cicero. “Law,” he says, “is reason
implanted by nature, which commands the things that must be done and prohibits
their contraries,” in book 1 of *On the Laws*. And again elsewhere: “Law is
right reason drawn from the divine power of the gods, commanding honorable
things and prohibiting their contraries.” And in book 1 of *On the Laws*: “Law
is right reason for commanding and prohibiting.” Again in the same place: “It
is right reason in commanding and forbidding.” And in book 2 of *On the Laws*:
“The true and principal law, fitted for commanding and forbidding, is the
reason and mind of supreme Jove.” And in the same place: “That law which the
gods gave to the human race is the reason and mind of a wise person, fitted
for commanding and forbidding.” We shall show later, however, how it should be
defined.

The Schoolmen and others say much about the law which they call *eternal*,
from which they hold that every law is derived. We said above, however, that
this law is not properly a law. Indeed, when it is taken—as some say it can
be—for any practical rule of necessary truth to which we are naturally
obligated, such as “God must be worshiped,” “parents must be honored,” “injury
must be done to no one,” and so forth, who does not see that this law is the
same as the natural law? But when it is taken for “the reason of divine wisdom
according to which he directs the motions and actions of all creatures,” it is
apparent to everyone that it is called a law very improperly. For that reason
for the order of the things to be done by subjects, insofar as it exists in
the mind of God, can indeed be called—

[1674 logical printed p. 74 | IA leaf 92 | PDF p. 92]

—“the idea of the rectitude according to which God has determined to govern
his creatures”; yet it cannot properly be called a law, since it is properly
only a decree or purpose concerning the enactment of a law. Furthermore, this
eternal law pertains to irrational creatures—for whom no law can properly be
enacted—just as much as to rational ones, according to Thomas, part 1 of the
second part, question 93, article 1; for he says it is the reason of divine
wisdom moving all things toward their due end. And according to Thomas himself
in the same place, article 2, this law is not known and cannot be known by any
creature except from some radiation of it, that is, from the knowledge of some
truth. It is consequently restricted to rational and intelligent creatures,
contrary to the description of it previously cited.

When we deny this eternal law, however, we by no means say that there are no
conceptions of the divine mind by which it conceives and judges how every
creature ought to discharge its functions. We mean that these conceptions do
not properly assume the character of law, and that they cannot be called a law
before those for whom the law is to be prescribed exist and those conceptions
become known through some sign declaring that God wills to govern the creature
in this and no other way, and to bind it to perform such duties and omit
others. For just as God's conception of creating the world cannot properly be
called the creation or the instrument of creating, so also God's conception
by which he willed to establish the ordering of the world, and especially to
moderate and direct human acts, cannot properly be called the actual
government of the world or the law or instrument for governing the world.

The *law of nature*, however, is properly a law, because the definition of law
set down above belongs to it. For it is a sign of the divine will, or of the
supreme legislator, concerning the duty owed by rational creatures. From it
flows the rational creature's obligation to avoid certain evil and shameful
things and to embrace and do certain good and honorable things; and punishment
follows the violation of this law. Cicero also recognized this in book 2 of
*On the Laws*; see the words cited above in chapter 1, pages 4 and 5, and
chapter 11, page 12. All the Schoolmen and theologians also recognize that
this law of nature draws its origin from God.

Nor need we laboriously prove that there truly is such a law, since even the
pagans themselves acknowledge it, and those common notions which are called
κοιναὶ ἔννοιαι, or the most common practical axioms, confirm it. We have also
proved this sufficiently above.

This law is called the natural law or law of nature, not because it has its
origin from merely created nature. For it is evident from what has been said
that it draws its origin from the Parent of nature, and all reason urges this.
For from what else would it possess its force of binding, if not from the
Supreme Legislator? Rather, it is so called because it becomes known
objectively from the sight of the creatures and from the relation of a human
being to the Creator and other creatures, and so forth; and the knowledge of
it was implanted in the human mind, planted there from its first birth, and
was not gathered by experience and induction or acquired through the
instruction or tradition of successive human beings. Yet now after the fall,
with respect to certain things more remote from the first principles,
experience, induction, and tradition are required in order to learn and imbibe
them more easily.

Thomas raises this question in part 1 of the second part, question 94, article
2: whether the natural law contains several precepts or only one. It is
certain that this law contains both certain duties toward ourselves and duties
toward our neighbor. But when—

[1674 logical printed p. 75 | IA leaf 93 | PDF p. 93]

—Thomas says that the first precept of this law is that “good must be done and
evil avoided,” and that all its precepts are founded upon this, he ascribes
nothing peculiar to this law. For every law, provided it is good, has this,
and all its precepts can be reduced to it. And when he adds that “all things
which practical reason naturally apprehends to be human goods, whether to be
done or avoided, belong to the precepts of the law of nature,” he speaks far
too broadly. For our practical reason has been corrupted in many ways and
apprehends not a few things to be good which are nevertheless evil, unless he
means that our reason does not act naturally except when it acts rightly,
according to the primeval rectitude in which it was created.

He reduces the precepts of this *law of nature* to three heads according to
the order of natural inclinations. “First,” he says, “there is in a human being
an inclination toward the good according to the nature which he shares with
all substances, insofar as every substance desires the preservation of its own
being according to its nature. According to this inclination, those things by
which human life is preserved and its contrary impeded belong to the natural
law. Second, there is in a human being an inclination toward certain more
special things according to the nature which he shares with the other animals;
and in this respect the things that nature taught all animals are said to
belong to the natural law, such as the union of male and female, the rearing of
offspring, and similar things. In the third way, there is in a human being an
inclination toward the good according to the nature of reason, which is proper
to him, just as a human being has a natural inclination to know the truth
about God and to live in society. According to this, the things pertaining to
an inclination of this kind belong to the natural law: for example, that a
human being should avoid ignorance and not offend those with whom he ought to
associate.”

Yet, since it was proved above that right pertains only to human beings and
consequently that law is given to them alone, he wrongly insinuates that this
law also pertains to other inanimate creatures and to brute beasts. For here
we are speaking of law properly so called, not of divine providence by which
God disposes and governs all things according to the order instituted by him.
It is nevertheless true that these inclinations which belong to a human being
insofar as he is a substance and insofar as he is an animal pertain to this
law—but not all of them, nor insofar as they are such. For certain actions of
a human being insofar as he is a substance or an animal are purely natural and
in no way moral, such as growth in bodily magnitude and strength, the motions
of the heart and arteries, the making of chyle and blood, and things of this
kind, which are not subject to the command of reason and have no morality nor
can have any. None of these natural inclinations insofar as it is such
pertains to the law of nature, but only insofar as it assumes moral goodness
or evil and becomes a human action subject to the governance of reason. Some
refer the union of male and female not to *obligatory natural right*, and thus
not to the *natural law*, but to *permissive natural right*, because a human
being is not prompted to it by a precept properly so called or by a law, but
it is left to his choice and permitted, and thus is destitute of every force
of binding and necessity of constraining. Indeed, the union of male and female
as such is not a moral action, and so is not a work of the natural law. For
nothing properly pertains to the natural law except a human action. This law
is prescribed to a human being and prescribed for regulating human actions,
that is, actions subject to the command of the will—which is the faculty
perfecting and commanding action, the proper subject of the moral virtues,
and the principle of moral actions. Aquinas himself acknowledges this in the
same place, reply 2,—

[1674 logical printed p. 76 | IA leaf 94 | PDF p. 94]

—saying: “All inclinations of this sort belonging to any parts of human
nature, such as the concupiscible and irascible parts, insofar as they are
regulated by reason, belong to the natural law.” And in reply 3: “Although
reason is one in itself, it nevertheless orders all things which pertain to
human beings, and in this respect all things which can be regulated by reason
are contained under the law of reason.”

Thomas raises another question in the same place, article 3: whether all acts
of the virtues belong to the law of nature. He replies: “We can speak about
virtuous acts in two ways: in one way, insofar as they are virtuous; in the
other, insofar as they are considered as such acts in their proper species. If
we speak,” he says, “about acts of the virtues insofar as they are virtuous,
they belong to the law of nature. For it has been said that everything to
which a human being is inclined according to his nature belongs to the law of
nature. But everything is naturally inclined to an operation fitting to
itself according to its form, as fire is inclined to heat. Hence, since the
rational soul is the proper form of a human being, there is in every human
being a natural inclination to act according to reason, and this is to act
according to virtue. In this respect, therefore, all acts of the virtues
belong to the natural law. But if we speak,” he says, “of virtuous acts
according to themselves, insofar as they are considered in their proper
species, not all virtuous acts in this way belong to the law of nature. For
many things are done according to nature to which nature does not incline us
at first, but human beings have discovered them by an inquiry of reason as
things useful for living well.”

In reply 2 he says that according to that nature of a human being which is
proper to him, all sins, insofar as they are contrary to reason, are also
contrary to nature. But what will he say concerning faith in Christ, evangelical
repentance, and similar things? Those acts are undoubtedly acts of virtue, and
yet nature does not incline us to them, nor do we learn them by natural light,
as is evident.

To the question whether the law of nature is one among all people, Thomas in
the place cited, article 4, and others with him reply that the law of nature,
insofar as it concerns the first common principles, is the same among all,
both according to rectitude and according to knowledge; and insofar as it
concerns certain particular matters which are, as it were, conclusions from
the common principles, it is likewise the same among all people in most cases,
both according to rectitude and according to knowledge. But it can fail among
the fewer: as to rectitude, on account of certain particular impediments, just
as generable and corruptible natures also fail in the fewer because of
impediments; and as to knowledge as well, because some possess a reason
depraved by passion, evil custom, or an evil disposition of nature. Others
subdivide those conclusions into *common* conclusions, which flow immediately
from the principles, and *special* ones, which need a longer discourse and do
not follow immediately. They say that the former do not vary, but grant that
the latter can vary because of the corruption of human nature, the perversity
of education, and other causes. Yet here they appear to me to confuse the
natural law itself with knowledge of the law. I ask: because human beings do
not see the law on account of their depraved reason and evil custom, or do not
know how rightly to apply the law to particular cases, should the law itself
therefore be said to change in itself, or with respect to rectitude, even as
it concerns these particular cases? Physical acts can indeed change by reason
of circumstances, but the law always remains the same. Because the natural law
forbids anyone to kill, and there are perhaps some who judge it unlawful even
for magistrates to put anyone to death for any crime—

[1674 logical printed p. 77 | IA leaf 95 | PDF p. 95]

—whatever, I ask whether the law itself is changed with respect to these
particular matters because those who are overcome by error wrongly apply that
general rule? By no means.

There are those who say, “The law of nature is the same among all insofar as
it is a habit innate in and inhering in the mind.” But in this way they are not
speaking about the law itself, but about knowledge of it. For divine law is
not a habit innate in the mind, as we shall show later. The knowledge of it can
indeed be called a habit, or can be in the mind after the manner of a habit,
just as is the knowledge of other things.

It must nevertheless be noted that everyone is not always obligated to
perform all natural duties, although the law itself remains the same always
and everywhere. For here too that rule holds: negative precepts bind “always
and for always”; affirmative precepts indeed bind “always,” but not “for
always.” Furthermore, the same law commands me to do some things and you
others, and some things today and others yesterday. That is, the same natural
law requires some things from the wise and others from the ignorant, some
from a public person and others from a private person. It also binds a human
being to some things in a solitary state and to others when he lives in some
society. Thus, by reason of circumstances I can be bound to something to which
I am held under no obligation whatever when those circumstances are changed,
because the *law* commands only—as we suppose—the action so circumstantiated.

As regards the question whether the law of nature can be changed, it will
suffice to note a few things. *First*, Thomas and others concede—and no one can
deny it—that the law of nature can be changed by the addition of other laws.
We see that it has been changed in this way by the addition of other positive
and ceremonial divine laws. *Next*, no one will say that this law is changed
by being erased so that it no longer possesses the force of law. *Third*, it
has often been indicated above that God can change it with respect to certain
particular precepts. Thomas appears to concede this here in article 5, reply 2,
when he responds as follows to the objections brought forward: “Without any
injustice, according to God's command, death can be inflicted upon any human
being, whether guilty or innocent. Similarly, adultery is intercourse with
another man's wife, who is assigned to him according to the divinely given
law of God. Hence, if anyone approaches any woman by divine command, it is not
adultery or fornication. The same rationale applies to theft, which is the
taking of another's thing; for whatever anyone receives by command of God, who
is the Lord of all things, he does not receive without the owner's will, which
is what it is to steal. And not only in human matters is whatever is commanded
by God due by that very fact; but also in natural matters, whatever is done by
God is in some manner natural.”

*Fourth*, when some say that the law of nature was changed through the fall
with respect to its erasure from the human mind, because it has now in part
been abolished and erased, they are not speaking about the law itself but
about knowledge of the law. That knowledge indeed suffered a great change and
diminution through the fall, for now only certain little sparks have taken the
place of full and perfect knowledge. Hence, although knowledge of the first
principles and of other things flowing immediately from them cannot be wholly
erased, knowledge of some other things more remotely situated can be entirely
lost. *Fifth*, it nevertheless cannot be denied that, with respect to not a
few particular matters, the law of nature after the fall was changed from
that which first became known to the first-formed pair. That is, the law of
nature now in the state of the fall requires different things from us than it
required in the state of innocence. Therefore that distinction of the natural
law into primeval and—

[1674 logical printed p. 78 | IA leaf 96 | PDF p. 96]

—secondary law must be retained, not only with respect to knowledge of the law
or the facility of recognizing it or deducing it from principles, but also
with respect to applications, which now must necessarily vary and change on
account of sin and other miseries, as is clear in those things which concern
wars, political judgments, servitudes, and not a few matters of that kind.
*Sixth*, it must be noted that the law of nature is called immutable only with
respect to precepts and prohibitions, not with respect to demonstrations, as
the Schoolmen speak, that is, those things which are neither commanded nor
prohibited, but only agree with nature or are not repugnant to it, such as the
community of goods.

From what has been said it is also clear what should be answered to the
question whether a dispensation can be made concerning the law of nature. It
is manifest that a human being can in no way dispense concerning this law, or
remove the force and obligation of its precepts. For he did not enact it, and
only the one to whom it belongs to enact the law can remove the law's
obligation. But as regards God, the answer must accord with the various
meanings of the word *dispense*. For if *to dispense* is the same as to command
that a subject do with impunity what a law which remains in force forbids him
to do, or to command or approve an act which, adequately and according to all
its circumstances, is contrary to a law remaining in force, then God does not
dispense concerning this law; for in this way he would command or approve sin
as such. An action formally contrary to a law in force is sin. But if *to
dispense* is the same as to remove the force, obligation, or efficacy of the
law here and now, so that the same action with respect to its physical
circumstances, which would be contrary to the law, is now not contrary because
the obligation of the law has been removed by the authority of the Supreme
Lord, then it is certain that God can dispense. For the removal of the vessels
of the Egyptians by the Israelites at God's command was not a violation of the
precept of the law of nature prohibiting the removal of another's property
against its owner's will. This was not so much because those vessels were then
not the Egyptians' vessels but the Israelites', by the free gift of God, the
Lord of all things, as because God's command intervened and removed the force
of the precept here and now. For those vessels would have belonged to the
Egyptians by civil right, and it would not have been lawful for the Israelites
not to return them unless God had commanded that very non-return. Likewise,
the sacrifice of Isaac was *lawful*, not so much because God is the Lord of
the life and death of all—for notwithstanding this it would not otherwise have
been lawful for Abraham himself to kill him—as because the force of the law of
nature was removed here and now when God's command intervened. Consequently,
not only a change of the matter, which indeed follows when speaking of moral
change, comes under consideration here, but especially the authority of the
supreme legislator, and not only the power of the Supreme Lord, who can take
away life and change ownership, but the power or authority of the supreme
Commander and Legislator as well.

A famous question among the Schoolmen is whether this law of nature is an
*act*, a *power*, or a *habit*. Some affirm and defend this, others that, and
others finally the third. But I think that none of these should be said. It is
not an *act*, because a law is something permanent, while acts pass away and
consist in flux, and especially because law is the act of God the Legislator,
not ours. We do not impose this law upon ourselves. We are by no means its
authors; rather, it is imposed upon us by God. It is not a *power*, for the
same reason. Those who hold it to be a power say it is nothing other than what
they call *δύναμιν κριτικήν*, a critical power concerning general principles,
by which, immediately when
those things are proposed and set before it and as soon as the use of reason
arrives, it apprehends them and gathers and deduces certain things from them.
But in this way they confuse knowledge of the law with the—

[1674 logical printed p. 79 | IA leaf 97 | PDF p. 97]

—law itself. For those natural pieces of knowledge which the intellect,
aroused by objects, elicits and forms by its proper faculty are not the
natural law itself, but rather the perception of the law enacted by God.
Neither can it be called a *habit*, since our habits are ours and in us, but
the natural law belongs to God and is outside us. The habit by which the
intellect is enabled to assent to practical principles differs from the
practical principles themselves, and those practical principles form part of
the law of nature. But this habit which enables the intellect to perceive and
assent to these principles forms no part of the law.

Furthermore, it is evident that this natural law should properly be called
neither an *act*, a *power*, nor a *habit*, because natural light is one thing
and the natural law another. The natural law is the object which *natural
light* perceives. The natural law is the instrument of God the Ruler outside
us, or considered objectively; but *our natural light* is properly ours and is
that by which we perceive and apprehend that instrument of God. The *natural
law* is the sign of God's will concerning our duty; but *natural light* is our
understanding of that sign and knowledge of our duty, or that by which we are
rendered fit to perceive our duty and obligation. Just as our natural light
is ours, so the law of nature ought also to be called ours—which is absurd,
since the law belongs not to the subjects but to the Legislator. There are
various perceptions of this natural law according to the various degrees of
this natural light. Should it therefore be said that there are various natural
laws? This natural light is greater in these persons than in those. Does the
law of God itself therefore admit of more and less, and is it increased or
diminished as our natural light increases or decreases? Indeed, since there
are those who extinguish this light for the greatest part by their depraved
affections, it would follow that the law of God itself is also removed for the
greatest part, and that anyone could in this way free himself, at least in
great part, from the obligation of the natural law by erasing the knowledge of
the law, or that natural light, with his depraved desires. No one can fail to
see its absurdity. No one calls our faculty of sight, or our actual vision of
the light of the sun, the very light of the sun. Who, then, can with any
propriety call our natural light—whether the faculty of perceiving the law or
the actual perception and understanding of the law—the natural law itself?

Polyander therefore defines this natural law badly, as I judge, in *Synopsis*,
disputation 18, thesis 13: “The light and dictate of right reason in the
intellect, informing a human being by κοιναῖς ἐννοίαις, or common notions, for
discerning the just and unjust and the honorable and shameful, so that he may
understand what is to be done or avoided.” For the dictate of our reason is
not a regulating rule, but a rule regulated by the will of God explained to us
by some sign. And that light is only that by which we perceive the law, not
the rule by which the rationale of the goodness or evil of an action is
ultimately to be examined.

Thus a distinction must be made between this law and human reason, insofar as
human reason is taken for the mind itself or a faculty of the soul, as is
clear. For no one will say that a faculty of our soul is the very law of God.
A distinction must also be made between this law and reason taken for the act
of our faculty. The natural law is one thing, and knowledge of the law, or its
actual perception, is another. So also a distinction must be made between this
law and the judgment or dictate of our reason, because that dictate or
judgment of our reason concerning the honorableness or shamefulness of an
action—

[1674 logical printed p. 80 | IA leaf 98 | PDF p. 98]

—is not the first rule of its rectitude, but rather a judgment concerning the
action's conformity or nonconformity with its rule. But this rule, whatever it
may be, must be something other than that judgment of our reason, as is
evident.

Nor should it be said that this rule is rational nature, for this is utterly
absurd. In this way the reason of each person would be productive of law,
which Thomas denies in the place cited, article 3, saying: “Law properly,
primarily, and principally regards an ordering toward the common good. But to
order something toward the common good belongs either to the whole multitude
or to someone who bears the place of the multitude. Therefore, to enact a law
belongs either to the whole multitude or to the public person who has care of
the whole multitude, because in every other matter also, ordering toward an
end belongs to the one to whom that end properly belongs.” Concerning his
statement about “one who bears the place of the multitude,” Cajetan says that
“Thomas does not limit himself to a vicar of the multitude, but extends his
statement to God, who can supply the place of the multitude in every way in
the matter of ordering toward the common good. It is evident that God can
preeminently order toward the common good of the multitude much better than
the multitude itself can order toward it.” And Thomas responds as follows to
the objection taken from Romans 2, where the nations are said to have been a
law to themselves: “Law is in something not only as in one who regulates, but
also by participation, as in one who is regulated; and in this way each person
is a law to himself insofar as he participates in the ordering of one who
regulates. Hence it is also added there, ‘Who show the work of the law written
in their hearts.’” He indeed says in article 1, question 90, reply 1, that law
is in reason as in that which measures and regulates; but he does not thereby
mean that this reason is the first rule of all things and that which first
regulates. He contrasts it with inferior things, namely, the inclinations and
members which ought to be ruled by reason, because reason is the first
principle of human acts.

Apollonius speaks excellently in *On the Law of God*, page 32: “Hence it must
not be thought,” he says, “that the law of nature is the dictate of right
reason, indicating that moral rectitude or shame inheres in some act because
of its agreement or disagreement with rational nature itself, and consequently
that such an act is commanded or forbidden by God, the Author of nature. For
in the agreement or disagreement by which an act is honorable, the rule is not
rational nature itself, from which the dictate of reason proceeds, but more
often the divine will manifested by God, illuminating rational nature,
informing it, and teaching what is honorable and just. For just as the formal
rationale by which a statement is true or false is not only its agreement or
disagreement with the intellect or conception of the speaker, but its
agreement with the thing stated, so the formal rationale by which an act is
formally good or evil is not its agreement with the nature which dictates
that the act is good or evil, but with the divinely prescribed idea of the
good. Therefore the very law of nature is wrongly confused with the human
knowledge which recognizes and approves the good law of God; these things must
be distinguished. Hence practical theologians also rightly note that the
dictate of right reason indeed indicates and binds, but the divine law obliges
and judges the very conscience which binds. Consequently the dictate of right
reason also has its own rule and norm, to which it is subjected and conformed.”

From this it is also clear that some wrongly say that this law of nature is
the dictate of conscience, and thus a certain direction implanted by God
himself in the minds and hearts of human beings, admonishing what they should
do or omit. For every good conscience is regulated by some superior rule and
is not a rule to itself; otherwise every dictate even of an erring conscience
would obligate and would be a direction implanted by God himself in the—

[1674 logical printed p. 81 | IA leaf 99 | PDF p. 99]

—human heart, which is utterly false. Further, conscience, insofar as it is a
person's judgment about himself, always judges according to another rule. And
*synteresis* is said to be that intellectual habit by which we assent to the
principles of moral operations. But neither that intellectual habit nor that
assent of the intellect can be called the law of nature. Finally, when
conscience admonishes what is to be done or omitted, it always does so by
setting forth the law which commands the one and prohibits the other. It has
the revealed will of God as its rule, and thus is not a law to itself but is
itself obligated by the law of God. Its dictate, therefore, is not this law of
nature. It indeed binds, provided it is good, but insofar as it is informed by
God's law. Hence it is that although an erroneous conscience binds, it does
not obligate a human being to follow its dictate, as is known.

All agree concerning the function of this law: namely, that it teaches and
prescribes what is morally good and evil, equitable and inequitable, honorable
and shameful; and that it obliges us to do and pursue the former and avoid the
latter.

Therefore, not only those things which are called matters “of natural and
necessary divine right,” whose contraries God could not prescribe without
implying a contradiction, pertain to this law; but so also do all the other
things which God required from innocent man, with the exception of the
superadded command not to eat the fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good
and evil. And now after the fall other things are also imposed upon us by
force of this law. By force of this law we are required to cover our nakedness,
to which Adam was not obligated. The same must be said about not a few other
things also mentioned above.

“Every moral good,” says Apollonius in the place cited, page 38, “can in some
way be educed from the original natural law as it was co-created with man by
God. For God created human nature good and implanted in it an inclination and
faculty toward good,” Genesis 1, the final verse; Ecclesiastes 7:29. “He
inscribed in him the law which makes known and obliges, and bestowed the power
of reasoning, right reason, and pure synteresis, by which he might have
practical axioms known and set forth, draw conclusions from implanted
principles, and apply them to any things to be done. Therefore all moral goods
belong to natural right, law, or knowledge, whether directly, strictly, by
eduction, or by reduction. They are either imposed directly upon a human being
in the natural law, or they can and ought to be educed from it through
necessary consequence and subsumption by reasoning; or they are reduced to it
as things agreeing with it, or at least by no means repugnant to it. Thus all
Mosaic forensic laws, and all positive laws whatever, if they are enacted
rightly and justly, and all judgments applying law to a fact, provided they do
not oppose but agree with the obedience owed to God, are reduced to natural
equity.”

But only those things which belong directly and by eduction to natural right
are properly referred here. For the other things which are called such only
by reduction are properly referred to other kinds of laws whenever they are
considered in themselves, and not with respect to that general and moral
equity which is in them or ought to be in them.

It can be asked why this law is called the *law of nature*. Apollonius in the
place cited, page 32, says that it is called the law of nature, not so much
because it can be known under the instruction of nature itself, even by those
who do not have the written law, but rather because through it nature, the
principle of all our actions, had from the beginning a definite norm and rule
for preserving, cherishing, and expressing its holiness and rectitude in
right actions.

[1674 logical printed p. 82 | IA leaf 100 | PDF p. 100]

Zanchi, *On the Law of God*, thesis 8, does not wish it to be called the *law
of nature* because it is naturally propagated into us through Adam—“for we are
by nature blind and perverse toward truly good things,” he says—but because
“God so impressed it upon our minds, inscribing in our nature certain general
principles both of religion and of what is good, equitable, and honorable, so
that it appears to have been born with us and to be natural.”

What if we say, as we also recently admonished, that this law is called the law
of nature because it is set before human beings to be read in the nature of
things itself? That is, through creation, created things, the order,
disposition, and natures of created things, their relation to God and one
another, the manner of creating, God's rest, and so forth, God has signified
what a human being's duty is and what is to be done or avoided. Or, in the
very constitution of things and the relation of creatures to God and one
another, he has laid foundations from which it can be clearly established what
a human being's duty is, or from which a human being's moral obligation
naturally and clearly results: to avoid these evil, shameful, and unlawful
things and to do those good and honorable things. It is certain that God
admonishes us of our duty through his works. See Romans 1:18–20; Psalm 19:2–7;
8:8 and following; 147:4–6; Job 12:7–9; 38:39; Isaiah 40:12; Matthew 6:26;
Acts 17:24–29; and 14:15–17. We do not, however, exclude every kind of
revelation here, as will appear.

Therefore a sagacious person endowed with knowledge and wisdom, as man was
when first created, could easily perceive from God's glorious works, from
intimate knowledge of his own soul, from the state in which he was established,
and from things of this kind who and what God was, and God's nature and
attributes: namely, that God is the Best, the Greatest, the Wisest, Almighty,
Eternal, Omniscient, Omnipresent, Most Just, Most Faithful, and so forth. From
his own relation to God as his Lord, Creator, Preserver, Legislator, and Father,
he saw that his obedience, observance, subjection, faith, hope, love, worship,
and other duties concerning God immediately resulted. Likewise, from
considering his own nature he could know what was required of him toward
himself. And from the image of God shining in himself and in other human beings
who were afterward to exist and be produced, as well as from the perfect
harmony of the world and so forth, he could easily gather the duties required
toward his neighbor. He could also gather other natural duties from the method
and manner of creation.

We see a notable example of this in Genesis 2:21–24. When the Lord God had
caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, taken one of his ribs, closed the flesh
in its place, built that rib into a woman, and brought her to Adam, *Adam*,
having seen her and knowing well—whether because of that extraordinary wisdom
with which he had been endowed, by which he had previously discerned the
natures and names of all the animals, or, as appears more probable to Pareus
and Rivet on the passage, from revelation made to him, that is, through
inspiration or divine suggestion—that she had been made from one of his ribs,
joyfully exclaimed, “This now is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh.” He
thereby celebrates God's wonderful wisdom and goodness in having formed such a
companion of life in such a manner. Sagaciously perceiving the end which God
intended, he gives his grateful consent, approves the divine order, embraces
the spouse given to him, and gathers from it not only his own duty but also
recognizes and proclaims God's ordinance and most wise institution concerning
marriage, saying, “She shall be called Woman, because—”

[1674 logical printed p. 83 | IA leaf 101 | PDF p. 101]

“—she was taken from Man; therefore a man shall leave his father and his
mother and cleave to his wife, and they shall become one flesh.” Thus from
this dispensation or constitution of things he perceived the law of God
concerning the firm and indissoluble marriage of one man and one woman. Some
hold that those words in verse 24 were spoken not by Adam, but either by God
himself, because Christ intimates this in Matthew 19:4–5, or by Moses. The
latter, however, does not appear probable, for it is strained to say that “he
shall leave” signifies “he is accustomed to leave,” and that “he shall cleave
and they shall be” signifies “he is accustomed to cleave, and they have been.”
Indeed, Pareus says on the passage, “It appears to be the continuation of
Adam's discourse, transferring the hypothesis to the thesis, as if he said:
just as God has joined this woman of mine with me into one flesh, so henceforth
every husband will obey this divinely sanctioned order, and, leaving his
dearest parents, cleave to his wife as his own flesh. But Christ,” he continues,
“attributes these words to God because God uttered them through Adam; for Adam,
inspired by the prophetic Spirit, sanctions a perpetual law for future
spouses.”

But whoever spoke these words, this is clear: *Adam* understood that a divine
law was given to him from that constitution of things, with the aid of divine
inspiration or suggestion for perceiving the object which indicated God's
mind, namely, that *Eve* had been made in such a manner and for such an end.
Hence Christ in Matthew 19:3–6, 8, gathers from the same source that it was
unlawful for a man to dismiss his wife for any cause other than fornication,
namely, because from the beginning the Maker made male and female and said,
“For this reason a man shall leave father and mother and be joined to his
wife,” and so forth. *Malachi* likewise gathers from the same source in chapter
2, verses 14–15: “Because Jehovah has borne witness between you and the wife
of your youth, against whom you act treacherously, although she is your
companion and wife bound to you by covenant. For did he not make one, although
the remainder of the Spirit was his? He was seeking godly offspring.” From
the fact that *Adam* was formed first, the Apostle gathers in 1 Timothy
2:12–13 that a woman ought not to usurp authority over a man. And from the
fact that *Eve* was created in this manner, the same Apostle gathers in
Ephesians 5:28–29, 31, that men ought to love and nourish their wives. “From
the form of man's creation,” says Salmasius in *On Interest Paid to Bankers*,
page 209, “he shows that God did not favor divorce. In creating, he did not
give several wives to one man, but one, so that one flesh should be made from
the two. Therefore he willed that, after the example of the first-formed pair,
one wife should be assigned, joined, and made to cleave to each man by the
indissoluble bond of marriage and fellowship.”

The same can be gathered concerning the observance of the Sabbath. For from
the whole order of things, together with divine inspiration and suggestion,
*Adam* had perceived that God had made all things in the space of six days,
and what works had been created on each day. He thus could not have been
ignorant that he himself was created at last on the sixth day and that God
then rested from the works of creation. Nor could he have been ignorant that
God made the seventh day on which he rested, just as he had made distinct the
six preceding days on which he worked, and consequently that God established
a cycle of seven days, neither fewer nor more; that the seventh day belonged
to this revolution or cycle of time no less than the sixth or any of the other
preceding days; and that this seventh day was a natural day and a true and
necessary part of that cycle or week. Therefore, since from the bringing of
Eve he had known God's revealed mind concerning his marrying her, so also from
this constitution of the weekly revolution and from God's rest on the seventh
day—

[1674 logical printed p. 84 | IA leaf 102 | PDF p. 102]

—he could have known that God's will was that a human being, by resting from
his works, should consecrate the seventh part of the week to God, just as,
taught by God's example, he could understand that he himself was to work for
six days. How much more evident were these things when God indicated to *Adam*
that he had consecrated and sanctified the seventh day to himself and blessed
it, just as God's institutions concerning the marriage of one man and one
woman, the duration of the marriage bond or of that conjugal state, and the
mutual duties of spouses were evident to him when God had signified to him
that the man would leave his father and mother, cleave to his wife, and that
they would become one flesh.

I see this explained as follows by Dr. Schook himself in *Disquisitions
concerning the Decalogue*, theses 22 and 23. “The natural law,” he says, “as we
also intimated above, extends more broadly than natural right. It has regard
not only to those implanted practical principles from which natural right is
deduced, but also recognizes as its object the deeds and works of God, from
which man in the state of innocence, through the co-created habit of wisdom,
holiness, and justice, could know what his duty was. We must first prove this
distinction and then apply it. Let us therefore consider the institution of
marriage. It was indeed solely of God's good pleasure that he willed to
prohibit wandering Venus to man, who was to increase and multiply like the
other animals, by joining only one spouse to him. Yet God did not enact a law
about it; rather, according to this positive right, Adam himself prescribed a
natural law to himself as soon as he knew Eve, formed by God from his rib.
That this is clearer than the noonday light is evident from Genesis 2:23–24.
For when God had made Eve and brought her to Adam, Moses most perfectly adds
this reasoning speech of Adam: ‘Then Adam said: This at last is bone of my
bones and flesh of my flesh. She shall be called Woman, because she was taken
from Man. Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and cleave to
his wife, and they shall become one flesh.’ Christ abundantly teaches that the
common parent was not mistaken when he gathered by reasoning that monogamy
was commanded to him, Matthew 19:3 and following. The Apostle does likewise in
Ephesians 5:28 and following. That at the same time he gathered that rule over
his wife belonged to him as her head, and that being ruled by a woman
(γυναικοκρατεῖσθαι) was contrary to nature, is very probably gathered from
what the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians
11:7 and following, and likewise 1 Timothy 2:13. But in the treatise *On
Natural Right* we have abundantly proved that these laws belong to natural
right.” He continues:

“Having laid this foundation, fourth, I gather further that by the same
rationale Adam gathered, even before God set his hand to creating Eve, that
the seventh day was to be sanctified to God. We do not read that God prescribed
to Adam the worship of the seventh day; for in Moses, chapter 2, verses 2–3,
you find only these words: ‘When God had completed on the seventh day the work
which he made, he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had
made; and God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it, because on it he
rested from all his work which God had created by making.’ By these words it
is surely not enjoined upon Adam that he should hold the seventh day sacred.
But when Adam, who in the state of integrity associated familiarly with God,
heard through God that the seventh day was blessed no less than it was
sanctified, or appointed to holy use—the word is taken in the same signification
in Leviticus 8:10, Numbers 7:1, and elsewhere—according to the habit of wisdom
connate with him, he imposed this law upon himself: Venerate the seventh day
on which God rested from the work of creation and hold it—”

[1674 logical printed p. 85 | IA leaf 103 | PDF p. 103]

“—holy. All the descendants of Adam would also have preserved this as a
natural law, according to the sense previously proposed, if Adam himself had
not most shamefully stripped off the image of God by his own fault. Meanwhile,
it did not cease to be a natural law, any more than did the law concerning the
marriage of one man and one woman, or monogamy. For equally, according to the
connate habit of wisdom, he gathered αὐτοδιδάκτως that he ought to venerate the
seventh day which God had blessed; and, with the approval of Christ himself as
we have just seen, he infallibly gathered that he ought to be content with one
spouse, and so forth. Thus far he.”

It must also be maintained that *Adam* in the state of innocence could
understand, clearly and distinctly perceive, many things from this book of
God's law, namely, the book of nature, even without superadded revelation or
instruction—things which we can observe only rather confusedly and
indistinctly, although aided by repeated revelation. For he was made in the
image of God, Genesis 1:27; 5:1, and endowed with original righteousness, by
which he was equipped perfectly to worship God and consequently to perceive
perfectly his will expressed and revealed by any sign. For he was endowed with
notable knowledge and understanding, as is clear from Colossians 3:10 and
Genesis 2:2, 19. His ingenuity of intellect, swiftness of thought, ease of
perception, and gravity and sagacity of judgment greatly excelled. Hence he had
the law inscribed upon his heart, so that he could so easily perceive God's
will expressed by any sign and obey it, as though it were his natural
temperament and a second nature. For through that habit of wisdom, holiness,
and justice with which he was endowed, he was naturally fitted both to render
obedience to God in all things and to recognize the divine will itself
manifested, expressed, or declared by any external sign or word. “God
prescribed and revealed the original natural law to the first man,” says
Apollonius in the place cited, pages 43–44, “not only through the κοινὰς
ἐννοίας implanted and connate in the human intellect, but also through
original righteousness, or the co-created habit of wisdom, holiness, and
justice naturally implanted in the human mind and ἔμφυτον. For those gifts of
wisdom, holiness, and justice not only rendered man fit to perform obedience
to God; they also made known to him the regulation of honorable acts, induced
an obligation to establish acts wisely, holily, and justly, and stirred and
impelled man to produce acts conforming to integral nature. By external
teaching and word God first illustrated and confirmed that αὐτοφυεῖ law of
nature for man, and also increased it by positive institutions; yet he did not
first enact or prescribe it to man, but naturally implanted, prescribed, and
revealed it to man through the integral nature created in him.”

Although we have a faculty which, when it puts itself forth and begins to
exercise itself, immediately recognizes and perceives by its own instinct and
force certain points of theology—namely, that God exists, that God must be
worshiped, and similar things—and although, upon knowing their terms, we are
ready to approve and acknowledge other first principles of justice and equity
on account of those little sparks and remnants of the former image of God
implanted in our minds, nevertheless we are plainly blind in many things even
when they are revealed to us anew, so that without the special aid of the Holy
Spirit we cannot perceive the things which belong to the Spirit of God. For
no good dwells in our flesh, Romans 7:18, and the wisdom of the flesh is
hostility against God, Romans 8:7–8. Every formation and every thought of our
heart is only evil at all times, Genesis 6:5, and the formation of the human
heart is evil from youth, Genesis 8:21. Compare Ephesians 2:1–3; 4:17–18; 5:8;
Titus 3:3.

Therefore, although many things now lie hidden from us, and there are certain
things whose observance we do not—

[1674 logical printed p. 86 | IA leaf 104 | PDF p. 104]

—perceive among nations destitute of the light of the gospel, and although at
first sight we may by no means see how they can be taught by nature, it by no
means follows from this that *Adam* himself could not read those same things
in the book of nature. Much less can it be said that something does not belong
to the law or *original natural right* because it is not acknowledged or
admitted by all human beings now in the corrupt state. From this it is also
clear that it is wrong to say that only those things are prohibited or
commanded by the *primeval law of nature* which we observe to have been
prohibited or commanded, observed or omitted, among nations not yet
illuminated by the light of the gospel. It is far worse to deny that those
things pertain to the law of nature of which some traces can indeed be seen
among the nations, but which do not appear there perfectly and adequately
conformed to the primeval law of nature according to all their circumstances.
For *primeval nature* taught that God must be solemnly worshiped in assemblies,
and that this solemn worship must be performed sincerely, spiritually, with
reverence and decorum, and with the whole heart. We see traces of solemn
worship among the nations, but where among them will this spirituality,
spiritual devotion, sincerity, and the full presence of the heart appear?
Indeed, does not their worship appear playful and childish, if not cruel, and
such as represents the devil rather than God? Shall we therefore say that
nature by no means teaches this manner of worship because the worship of the
nations was not such? The same can be said of not a few other things. Primeval
nature taught that God must be worshiped in the manner instituted by him. Yet
the nations do not worship God in that manner, but generally in a manner
fashioned by themselves or by the devil.

By what mark, then, you will ask, shall we recognize and distinguish the things
which are commanded or prohibited by the law of nature from others? Scripture
indeed explains all God's precepts to us, for it is useful for teaching,
reproof, correction, and instruction in righteousness, so that the man of God
may be perfect, thoroughly equipped for every good work, 2 Timothy 3:16–17.
Some of these precepts, however, are newly instituted, namely, those which
proximately regard man as fallen and to be restored and prescribe a peculiar,
mutable manner of divine worship under both the Old Testament and the New.
There are nevertheless not a few others which nature itself, even now corrupt,
teaches through those still remaining sparks of knowledge and ruins of the
former state, but which are set forth more clearly, distinctly, and fully in
Holy Scripture. There are likewise certain things which nature, now corrupted
and daily rendered worse by evil customs and depraved affections, cannot
perceive distinctly and clearly enough without new revelation. Yet they do not
properly pertain to some one particular nation to the exclusion of others, or
to one unique state of the church for some centuries, but pertain to all
nations throughout all ages, so that they regard not some singular condition
of human beings, but, as it were, human nature itself. And once explained,
right reason cannot fail to embrace and approve them as most consonant with
itself, indeed, so rooted in itself that they can be supported and confirmed
by the strongest reasons, and so founded and impressed in the nature of things
itself that no one who rightly weighs and exactly searches it can fail to read
the equity of this law and perceive its force and obligation.

Now at last let us define this law of nature. *The law of nature is that sign
of the divine will by which a human being as such is perpetually obligated
necessarily to do this as something good and honorable, and necessarily to
avoid that as something evil, shameful, and unlawful; so expressed in the
nature of things—*

[1674 logical printed p. 87 | IA leaf 105 | PDF p. 105]

*—created by God and related to God and to itself that human reason can easily
perceive it or be able to perceive it, either of its own accord or when aided
by divine inspiration and revelation.*

With this definition we can *embrace* both the primeval and the secondary law
of nature by varying the explanation of the terms a little.

I say that this law is a “sign of the will of God,” because I set this down as
the genus of law in general, as is clear from the first chapter, where we
treated law in general. I also said that it obligates us to do good and avoid
evil, because it belongs to the nature of every divine law, as it belongs to
the nature of every law in general, to constitute the duty owed by its
subjects, and this duty embraces both the things to be avoided and those to be
done. The remaining words pertain to the difference of this law.

I said that by this sign of the divine will “a human being as such” is
obligated, so that this law might be distinguished from those laws imposed
upon some particular people, whether considered as a commonwealth or as a
church. For God enacted certain laws for the Israelite people which distinctly
and peculiarly regarded their political state and did not bind other nations.
He also gave others which pertained precisely to that state of the church and
do not now obligate the Christian church, just as the Old Testament church
was not held bound by commands concerning certain things now imposed upon us.
Furthermore, as was explained above, those things are called matters “of
natural right” which possess a fittingness or agreement with the rational soul
as such and are consequently common to all.

I said that a human being “is perpetually obligated” in order to distinguish
it from that law concerning not eating the forbidden fruit. Although that law
was given to *Adam* representing the whole human race and thus in some manner
to all human beings, it does not now properly speaking obligate anyone,
although because of its transgression we are all subject to God's judgment.

I said that this sign “is expressed in the nature of things itself” because I
think that this is called the law of nature since it is set forth to be read
in the book of nature. Indeed, all God's works, both in us and outside us,
insofar as they reveal and explain something concerning our duty, pertain to
this law. For God revealed his will to *Adam* not only through implanted
notions, but also through the other works of creation and through his own rest
after completing the work of six days, as was said above. By the nature of
things I do not understand merely the nature of creatures considered in
itself, but I include together with it the mode and method of creation,
similar things, and divine providence ordering and disposing creatures as he
wills. Hence, when the constitution and state of things have been changed,
nature requires something different than it formerly required or would
require if things were not in that state. For example, when a person fighting
for his country defends a fortress or stronghold against the force of enemies,
he is bound to prove himself faithful and not surrender that fortification;
and nature abhors and condemns treachery and wicked betrayal. But when the
same person is pressed by a lack of the things necessary for life and defense,
he is not bound to fight against nature. Rather, when reduced to extremity he
may redeem his own and others' lives for as honorable a price as he can, since
the law of nature permits and indeed commands it. Similarly, by the law of
nature the sons of Adam were permitted to marry their own sisters, but no one
doubts that this is unlawful after the multiplication of the human race.

I added “and related to God and to itself,” because from the rational
creature's relation to God as Creator, Supreme Lord, Preserver,—

[1674 logical printed p. 88 | IA leaf 106 | PDF p. 106]

—Legislator, and ultimate End flow the principal human duties, and in this
relation is laid the foundation of all our subjection and obedience toward
God, as could be shown more fully if this belonged to our purpose. And from
the relation to other creatures flow the other duties of the second table.

I said that “human reason can perceive it of its own accord,” because Adam had
an innate, implanted, and co-created knowledge of this law. That is, he had a
faculty endowed with such sagacity, knowledge, and acuteness that, when an
object was set before him, he could most easily perceive what the will of God
signified through that object was, as we showed above in his marriage with
Eve. He could also gird himself so readily for obedience that he needed no
other stimulus. Thus he had this law inscribed in his heart, just as the
regenerate are said to have the law of God inscribed in their hearts, its
knowledge being implanted in them and impressed anew. Even now, with respect
to not a few things, human reason can perceive it by the light of principles
known by their own light and is compelled to embrace it unless it wishes to
contradict some of these principles.

I further added, “when aided by divine inspiration and revelation,” for two
reasons. First, because even *Adam* himself in some cases perhaps needed some
divine revelation or suggestion, at least with respect to the object;
otherwise we can hardly conceive how it could have become known to him in how
many days God created all things. Second, because now after the fall in many
cases we need a revelation and renewal of this law of nature; otherwise it
would remain unknown to us, as was said and explained above. Hence those who
are endowed with the Spirit and imbued with the light of Scripture dispute
concerning this law of nature much more accurately than the most subtle
philosophers who have never seen the Holy Scriptures. And hence there are very
many things which we would never learn from this book without some teacher, or
not without the greatest labor and study, but which, once understood and
taught, appear to be inscribed there in large characters. The pagans
themselves testify that we cannot learn many things from this book. “Nature
declares by many signs what it wills,” says Cicero in the place cited, “yet
somehow we become deaf and do not hear the things which are moved by it.” And
again in the third *Tusculan Disputation*: “If nature had begotten us such
that we could behold and perceive nature itself and, with this best guide,
complete the course of life, there would surely be no reason why anyone should
require reason and teaching, since nature would suffice. Now it has given us
tiny sparks which we extinguish so quickly by evil customs and depraved
opinions that the light of nature never appears. But now, as soon as we are
brought forth into the light and received, we are continually occupied in
every depravity and the greatest perversity of opinions, so that we seem
almost to have sucked error with the nurse's milk. And when we are restored
to our parents and finally handed over to teachers, we are then imbued with
such various errors that truth yields to vanity, and nature itself to confirmed
opinion.”

I said, “or be able to perceive it,” because although the reason of many is so
corrupted by depraved affections that they cannot actually perceive many
things which they otherwise could have perceived and which others actually
perceive clearly, nevertheless it is enough to manifest the natural law if
reason purged and free from these corruptions can perceive their force and
obligation.

There are those who call that the law of nature which was formerly inscribed
upon the hearts of the nations. But how shall we know what was inscribed upon
the hearts of all the nations—

[1674 logical printed p. 89 | IA leaf 107 | PDF p. 107 | ends before CAP. VII]

—or what was inscribed upon the heart of any nation, except from its practice
or confession? But shall we say that all the things which they acknowledged
by their practice or confession belong to the law of nature? Then it must
surely be said that the sacrifice of animals or other things belongs to
natural right, because the nations approved this by their practice and
confession. Further, if so, we shall approve many things which must necessarily
be condemned, for their practice was most corrupt in many things, as we showed
above. And what finally shall we say concerning the worship of the one God
alone? No one will deny that this belongs to natural right; yet the nations by
their practice and confession commend to us the worship of many gods.

There are also others who say that the natural law “is the law of those things
which must necessarily be done or omitted, whether toward God, ourselves, or
others; and which human nature itself, even now corrupt, acknowledges to be
such, or can be compelled to acknowledge even without Scripture by arguments
deduced from those principles which still adhere in human hearts.” Or they
speak thus: “It is the law which can be proved to be not only just but also
necessary by principles deduced from the light of nature itself, with which
rational human beings are still imbued.” So Drs. Cudrey and Palmer in *The
Sabbath Revived*, part 1, chapter 1.

But these men too, as is clear, restrict the natural law to that which is such
after the fall, and do not also extend it to that which was such in the state
of primeval integrity. Furthermore, by this rationale they exclude from this
law the things which cannot now be known without revelation. Thus we shall be
bound by no natural law to believe the Trinity of Persons in the deity,
although it is wicked to suppose that *Adam* was ignorant of it in the state
of innocence. And certain other things will not be our duties, to the
performance or omission of which we are bound by the natural law, because our
now corrupt nature does not now acknowledge them and cannot know them without
Scripture, although nature illuminated by the light of Scripture cannot fail
to acknowledge that they are just and equitable.

Those therefore speak better who hold that the natural law is the law of the
things which have an intimate agreement or disagreement with a human being's
rational nature itself as such, or which are good or evil for a human being
insofar as he is a rational creature; and between which and human nature
governed by reason there is a fitting and honorable proportion, or its
contrary.
