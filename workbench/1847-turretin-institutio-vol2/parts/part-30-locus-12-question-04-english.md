QUESTION IV.

*How do the covenant of works and the covenant of grace agree with and differ
from each other?*

[Marginal heading: The covenant of works and the covenant of grace agree.]

I. The covenant of works and the covenant of grace agree in various respects.
1. In their *Author*, who in each is God, to whom alone belongs the right to
establish a covenant with the creature. 2. In the *contracting parties*, which
are God and man. 3. In the *general end*, the glory of God. 4. In the
*external form*, because a reciprocal stipulation is annexed to each. 5. In
the *promise*, which is heavenly and eternal life and happiness.

[Marginal heading: And they differ, 1. In the Author.]

II. But they differ in many more respects. 1. With respect to the *Author*:
although God is the author of each covenant, he must nevertheless be regarded
under a different σχέσει, *relation*—in the first as Creator and Lord, in the
second as Redeemer and Father. He was moved to the first by love and
benevolence toward an upright creature, but to the latter by mercy and special
grace toward a miserable creature.

[Marginal heading: 2. In the contracting parties.]

III. 2. With respect to the *contracting parties* between whom the covenant
was made: in the first there were only two, God and man, and there was no need
of any Mediator because as yet no dissension existed between them. In the
other, which was established after the fall, a Mediator was necessarily
required to make peace and to reconcile men to God and God to men. Therefore,
the former is called a *covenant of friendship*, but the latter a *covenant of
reconciliation*, which presupposes a preceding dissension that had to be
settled.

[Marginal heading: 3. In the foundation.]

IV. 3. With respect to the *foundation*: the former rested upon man’s own
obedience and upon the powers of nature or free choice, for man was placed in
the hand of his own counsel. But the latter rests upon Christ and his
obedience. Hence he himself is called “a covenant of the people” (Isa. 49:8).
Nor is man any longer in the hand of his own counsel, in which he fell as soon
as he was placed there, but in the hand of God, by whose power he is most
effectually guarded forever (1 Pet. 1:5).

[Marginal heading: 4. In the promise.]

V. 4. With respect to the *promise*, the difference is not that the former
promised only perpetual happiness to be enjoyed in Paradise, as some would
have it, without sufficiently weighing the nature of that covenant. For
heavenly happiness and life were promised no less in the first covenant than
in the second, as was proved in Part I, Locus VIII, Question 6. Rather, the
difference consists in this: in the first only *life* was promised, that is, a
perfect state in the aggregation of every good, but in this one *salvation*,
which along with life includes deliverance from sin and death. The first
promised life as a *reward owed* (Rom. 4:4), but the other as the *gift of God*
(Rom. 6:23).

[Marginal heading: 5. In the condition.]

VI. 5. With respect to the prescribed *condition*: in the former, works—*Do
this and you shall live*; in the second, faith—*Believe and you shall be
saved*.

The former consisted in man’s rendering perfect righteousness; the latter in
receiving the infinite righteousness of God. The former was demanded in the
forum of justice, where either a sentence of acquittal was rendered for the
just or one of condemnation for the sinner, because no hope of pardon was
given. The latter is demanded in the forum of mercy, where the sentence of
acquittal is pronounced upon sinners, but sinners who believe and repent.

VII. Nor can it be objected here that faith was also required in the first
covenant and that works are not excluded in the second, as was said above,
because the character of each is far different. In the first covenant faith
was required as a work and part of the inherent righteousness to which life
was promised. But in the second it is required not as a work on account of
which life is given, but as the mere instrument that apprehends the
righteousness of Christ, on account of which alone salvation is granted to
us. In the former, faith was a theological virtue from the powers of nature,
directed toward God the Creator. In the latter, it is an evangelical condition
by way of supernatural grace, directed toward God the Redeemer.

As for works, in the first they were required as an antecedent condition, by
way of a cause for acquiring life; but in the second they function only as a
subsequent condition, by way of a fruit and effect of life already acquired.
In the first they had to precede the person to be justified; in the second
they follow the person justified.

[Marginal heading: 6. In the end.]

VIII. 6. With respect to the *end*: the end of the first was the declaration
of justice, but that of the second was the manifestation of God’s mercy and
immense love. In the former, τὸ ἀκριβοδίκαιον, *strict justice*, of the
Legislator and Judge prevailed, through which, just as there is no place for
repentance, so neither is there one for pardon. But in the latter, the
ἐπιείκεια, *equity*, of the Father prevails, which opens the way to grace and
the salvation of the sinner.

[Marginal heading: 7. In manifestation.]

IX. 7. With respect to *manifestation*: just as the former was a covenant in
the state of nature, so it was known by nature and impressed upon human
consciences, in which the work of the law is inscribed (Rom. 2:14, 15). But
the second is a wholly hidden mystery, which reason cannot attain unless it is
made known to us by supernatural revelation, because it depends solely upon
God’s εὐδοκίᾳ, *good pleasure*, and his most free counsel. Consequently it
could become known to no one unless God himself disclosed it. Hence Christ
testifies that flesh and blood cannot reveal it, but only the Father who is in
heaven (Matt. 16); and in 1 Cor. 2:9 the mysteries of the gospel are said to
be “things which the eye has not seen,” and so forth.

[Marginal heading: 8. In order.]

X. 8. With respect to *order*: the covenant of works precedes and the covenant
of grace follows. From the former an appeal lies to the latter, from the
throne of justice to the throne of mercy. Hence for one who violates the
covenant of nature a remedy is given in the covenant of grace; but for one who
violates the covenant of grace, no further remedy or hope of pardon remains,
because there is no other covenant in which he can be reconciled with God.
Therefore, the sin against the Holy Spirit is unpardonable because it is
committed against the covenant of grace.

[Marginal heading: 9. In extent.]

XI. 9. With respect to *extent*: the first was *universal*, contracted with
all men in Adam. Hence there is no mortal who is not by nature under the
covenant of works, since each has the work of the law—the law being the
contract of that covenant—written in his conscience (Rom. 2:14). But the
latter is *particular*, contracted in Christ with the elect alone, who are to
be saved, as will be proved afterward.

[Marginal heading: 10. In effects.]

XII. 10. With respect to *effects*: the first gave man matter for boasting
when it was observed, but this covenant excludes all human boasting because
it rests upon the grace of God alone (Rom. 3:27): “Boasting is excluded, not
by the law of works, but by the law of faith.” After the fall, the first became
terrible, striking consciences with horror; but the latter is gracious and
saving. The first engenders slavery, the other liberty. In the first, God from
Mount Ebal

thunders curses upon sinners; but in the second, blessings are proclaimed as
if from Mount Gerizim. The first drives men away from God, because no one can
approach God through it; but the latter calls men back to God and opens the
throne of mercy, approaching which with confidence we can find help at the
opportune time.
