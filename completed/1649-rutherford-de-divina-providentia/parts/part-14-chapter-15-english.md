# CHAPTER XV

## How Annatus defends middle knowledge by denying every act of GOD'S will
concerning sin

Friar Annatus cannot make God an idle spectator of sin and defend middle
knowledge except by charging Twisse with making God the author of sin.

[Marginal citation: Annatus, *On Middle Knowledge*, disputation 1, chapter 5,
question 2, no. 77.]

First he charges Twisse: “It is necessary for one who is subject to an absolute
decree to sin; yet not necessarily: it is necessary that he sin freely.
Therefore,” he says, “this is what emerges: he will sin necessarily, yet not
necessarily.”

I answer: What absurdity is there in affirming a modal necessity—a necessity
*in a certain respect* (κατά τὶ) and of the consequent—and denying in the same
act a pure, absolute necessity and a necessity of the consequence, as in this
proposition: it is necessary that God decree wisely and freely to create the
world? Does God, then, decree the creation of the world both freely and not
freely, both necessarily and not necessarily? Certainly not.

[Marginal citation: Annatus, in the same place.]

2. “What does God permit in sin, according to the Calvinists? Does he permit
that it be an act? But then he predetermines it and effects it by antecedent
concurrence. Does he permit that it be free? But this very thing is brought
about by God in the same way. Does he permit that it be forbidden? He alone
lays down and declares the prohibition. Therefore, according to Calvin, he
makes the sin.”

I answer: 1. Strictly speaking, he permits neither the act nor the evil, but he
permits the evil act; nor, strictly speaking, does concurrence prevent
permission. 2. I turn the argument back upon the Dominicans, who agree with us
on this point. 3. Nor, on Jesuit principles, does God permit the act, because
he effects it by universal concurrence; nor does he permit it *qua* free,
because he brings that about in the same way—that is, efficiently; nor does he
permit the wickedness, because he lays down the prohibition itself. Pitiful
physician, attend to yourself and solve the problem.

[Marginal citation: chapter 4, question 4, no. 11.]

3. “GOD,” he says, “is the cause of sin, not its author”; or, “he is a cause,
but not a culpable one,” as Twisse says.

I answer: Dr. Twisse has no such disjunction. Most mendacious of men, you
fabricate it for him. “The will of GOD,” Dr. Twisse says, “is the non-culpable
cause of all things that happen—and it cannot at all be denied that the will
of GOD is the cause of all the things he wills to happen, but [wills them]
freely as well—and Augustine most expressly professed that nothing exists
unless-*

[Chapter XV; printed page 187; PDF page 238]

*GOD wills it to happen, whether good or evil.”

1. To human eyes, it is impossible to read in Twisse that GOD is the cause of
sin. One can read that he is the cause of the *existence* of sin, which is a
very different thing, as I demonstrate later against Arminius, a most cunning
little Jesuit in this matter. 2. The Dominicans call God a non-culpable cause
of an evil act, while the Jesuits refute them most strenuously. Namely, in the
category of physical—not moral—cause, God is the cause of an evil act *qua*
act by reason of universal concurrence, unless the Jesuits deprive their
general concurrence of all causality. And in that case God will have no
causative influence in good acts.

[Marginal citation: Annatus, chapter 5, question 5, no. 95.]

4. Another of Twisse's paradoxes: “GOD wills that evil happen.”

I answer: Twisse does not say that God wills evil—that is, approves it.

But the Jesuits say this, namely, that “GOD denies congruous grace and gives
incongruous grace for the purpose that the reprobate should not believe and
should not repent.” Annatus responds: “If I give this man money that I owe him,
and I do not doubt that he will spend it in gambling, can I not therefore give
it without willing and intending this very thing—that he gamble it away? Or do
those who say that GOD foreknows that something will follow apart from his
intention not count as sober? Or is antiquity to be condemned when it
asserts with St. Paul that God wills all people to be saved?”

[Marginal summary: Jesuit permission makes GOD the author of sin.]

I answer: 1. The one who gives grace for that end, at the time and in the
manner in which he foreknows that the offered grace will be maliciously
rejected—so that it issues in a far graver ruin for the one rejecting it—and
who gives it by a will as unconditional, inscrutable, and absolute as befits a
potter toward earthen vessels; and who denies the same grace in the order and
arrangement of things in which, if it were given, he foreknows that it would
be received and finally salutary: that person wills absolutely that the one
called in this manner should not believe and should not obey. It is just as if
a father were to give his son money in an arrangement in which he foreknows
that his son would lavishly spend it on fornication and drinking bouts, but
were to deny the same money in the arrangement in which he foreknows that his
son would use it frugally and with due foresight. If he did this by an absolute
will such as a potter has when disposing of vessels, that father would intend
his son to spend badly and squander the money prodigally.

2. The omniscient potter foreknows that nothing will follow apart from an
intention as inscrutable and absolute as the one a potter has toward his
vessels—namely, an intention free and independent from every work, reason,
condition, or-*

[Chapter XV; printed page 188; PDF page 239]

*motive in the clay or the vessel. The Jesuits imagine that God grants or
denies a congruous calling by such a will. But we fearlessly affirm that this
will stands diametrically opposed to that natural and most eager will to save
each and every person, no more than water agrees with fire or a wolf with
sheep.

Annatus: “But GOD permits an evil act when he concurs in such a way that he
reserves to himself full power to add the opposite concurrence, if the free
creature should will to accept it.”

I answer: But on Jesuit principles he permits in precisely this manner the act
of believing in CHRIST and loving God, with a full and indifferent power—if
the Jesuits are to be believed—to apply the opposite concurrence, should free
will so wish. Thus the betrayal of the Savior in Judas and the act of
believing in Peter will equally be GOD'S work as far as physical causation is
concerned. Let the Jesuit answer.

Annatus: “To say that GOD absolutely wills and efficaciously decrees that sin
happen, yet does not commit sin, is to will that GOD should not sin by himself,
yet to will and bring it about that another person sins.”

I answer: If God ineffectually and conditionally wills sin—provided, that is,
that the creature wills it—as the Jesuits maintain, then this will amount to
God's not willing to sin by himself, but conditionally and ineffectually
willing another person to sin. 2. Nor is the inference worth a whit. God can no
more will—in the sense of approve—the creature's sin than he can deny himself.
He can, however, will and decree the existence of sin, and do so efficaciously,
when it is committed by another while God forbids it. Nor does GOD'S
prohibition and moral prevention of sin conflict with GOD'S efficacious decree
concerning sin, if the adversaries would correctly grasp the distinction
between the will of sign and the will of good pleasure. For an efficacious
congruous decree is consistent with the prohibition of sin.

Annatus: “You efficaciously and absolutely will that Titus kill his enemy; this
is, you say, ‘I merely permit it.’”

I reply: By universal concurrence God ineffectually wills Peter to believe,
while having the will to add the opposite concurrence toward an act of
unbelief, if it should so please the freedom of God's creature [the witnesses
print *Deae*, probably for *Dei*]. Therefore, he merely permits Peter to
believe. 2. An efficacious decree and a permissive decree do not conflict. For
the congruous decree of calling is efficacious, yet the Jesuits would not call
it permissive. Indeed, an efficacious decree never lacks its effect; a command
often does.

[Source note: In the clause *si Deae creaturae libertati libitum sit*, both the
Internet Archive and BSB witnesses appear to print *Deae*. The sense likely
requires *Dei* (“God's”), which the translation adopts while preserving the
printed reading in brackets.]
