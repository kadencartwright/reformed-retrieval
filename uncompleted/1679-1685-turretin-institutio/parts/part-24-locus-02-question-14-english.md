QUESTION XIV

ON THE VERSION OF THE SEVENTY

Is the Version of the Seventy of the Old Testament authentic? We deny.

I. Among the Greek versions of the Old Testament, the first place deservedly
belongs among us to that of the seventy-two interpreters. It was held in such
honor among both Jews and Christians, both Eastern and Western, that it was
publicly read by the former in the synagogues, while almost it alone, or
versions made from it, were publicly read by the latter in the churches. From
it all the other versions anciently approved by the church were derived, with
the sole exception of the Syriac, namely, the Arabic, Ethiopic, Armenian,
Illyrian, Gothic, and Latin before Jerome. Nor do the Greek church and most of
the Eastern churches acknowledge any other to this very day, being content
with this one alone.

[Marginal heading: Status of the question.]

II. The question is not about the time or the manner in which this version was
prepared: whether it was made under the auspices and at the expense of Ptolemy
Philadelphus; whether instead it was made by the Jews at the prompting of
utility, as Scaliger supposes in epistle 14; whether it was made by seventy-two
interpreters who, each in a separate cell, completed the whole work in the
space of seventy-two days and did so with the utmost agreement, even though
each one, apart from the others, had undertaken and completed the whole work;
and other things of that kind which are reported about those interpreters,
both by Aristeas, who set out a lengthy account of the matter in a special
book, and by Josephus and Christians. Because that version was in use, the
latter readily lent themselves to those accounts, eagerly seizing whatever
tended to confer authority upon it. For these matters are merely historical
and therefore do not now pertain to our purpose. Nevertheless, if we may
disclose our opinion in a word, we readily accede to the judgment of learned
men to whom all these things are highly suspect and of doubtful credibility.
Jerome already began in his time to expose and refute their falsity, and the
more recent writers did so still more clearly and forcefully: Vives in book
11; Augustine, *City of God*, 42; Scaliger on Eusebius; Drusius; Casaubon;
Wouverus; Ussher; Rivet; Heinsius; and others. Here, however, the question
concerns only its authority: whether such authority should be attributed to
it that in accordance with it the version is regarded as truly God-inspired
(θεόπνευστος) and authentic.

III. Although the Roman Catholics do not all speak in the same manner, most
nevertheless agree that this version was made by divine agency and therefore
deservedly possesses divine authority. Thus they think the translators should
be regarded not so much as interpreters as prophets, who had the Holy Spirit
assisting them in a special manner so that they would nowhere err, as
Bellarmine says in book 2 of *The Word of God*, chapter 6. Bayly, Stapleton,
P. Carthusia in *On the Translation of the Bible*, chapter 4.5, Jean
d'Espières, treatise 2, disputation 1, doubt 10, and especially Jean Morin in
his *Biblical Exercises* join them. With great effort Morin

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 116 | IA leaf 170 | PDF p. 170]

attempted to establish the authenticity (αὐθεντίαν) of this version. From
among our writers, the most learned Isaac Vossius strove in a special treatise
to confirm the same thing by various arguments.

IV. We, on the other hand, although we do not deny that it has great authority
in the church, nevertheless regard that authority as human, not divine,
inasmuch as it was prepared merely by interpreters through human industry,
not by prophets and God-inspired men (θεοπνεύστοις) through the immediate
inspiration of the Holy Spirit.

V. The question, therefore, is not whether it ought to have some authority in
the church. For we grant that it has great weight and is deservedly preferred
to other versions. 1. It is the oldest of all, having been made two thousand
years ago, and therefore ought to be honored by everyone for its gray age. 2.
It was read publicly and privately by Jews scattered everywhere throughout
the world. 3. The apostles and evangelists are found to have used it very
often in citing passages from the Old Testament and, as it were, consecrated
it by their writings. 4. The apostles delivered it to the church after they
had subjected the world to Christ through it, so that among the Gentiles the
church was begotten through it and nourished with its milk. 5. The Greek and
Latin church held it as the common version for six hundred years. 6. The
ancient Fathers and ecclesiastical writers explained it in commentaries,
expounded it to the people in homilies, strangled heresies arising from it,
and drew from it in councils canons for shaping faith and morals. The question
is whether its authority is such that it ought on that account to be regarded
as authentic and made equal to the original texts. Our adversaries would have
this; we deny it.

[Marginal heading: The Version of the Seventy is not authentic.]

VI. The reasons are as follows. 1. It was composed by human study and labor,
not by God-inspired men (θεοπνεύστοις). Its authors were interpreters, not
prophets, who ceased after Malachi, whom the Jews call the seal of the
prophets. This is also evident from the fact that in Aristeas the interpreters
are said to have compared their work among themselves, disputing and
conferring with one another about each matter until they all at last came to
one agreement, and so forth. But if they compared their work among themselves,
they did not prophesy. For the sacred writers never compared their work with
others while disputing about each matter they were to write. Rather, according
as they had been taught by the Holy Spirit, they committed everything to
writing without any disputation or delay. 2. If they wrote by the breath of
the Holy Spirit, so great a number would have been superfluous, since one would
have sufficed. Nor would there have been a need for men distinguished in
learning and skilled in Hebrew and Greek if it had been completed without
study and human aids. 3. In many places it agrees with the original texts
neither in matters nor in words, but has various misinterpretations
(παρερμηνείας) and discrepancies, as has been shown by those who have treated
this subject. Hence Morin was at last compelled to confess in *Exercises*,
book 7, chapter 4, that no other authority is to be ascribed to this version
than to other works made by human industry. 4. It is not possessed today in a
pure form, but has been corrupted and interpolated to a remarkable degree. We
have only its ruins and remnants (λείψανα), so that it can now scarcely be
called the Version of the Seventy. It is like the ship Argo which, having been
repaired more often, was no longer either the same ship or another one. Jerome
observed this more than once in epistle 89 to Augustine, the preface to Ezra,
and the book of Chronicles. Hence it is now generally received among the
learned that it is from the common version (κοινῇ), which was also called
Lucian's (λουκιανής), as Jerome testifies in his work to Sunia and Fretela.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. If the apostles very often used this version, they did so not because
they believed it to be authentic and divine, but because at that time it was
in most common use and received among everyone. And where the meaning and the
consideration of truth allowed, they were unwilling either to quarrel
inopportunely or to create a scruple for the weaker, but through a holy
accommodation left unaltered what would have offended if changed, especially
since even when unaltered it accomplished the matter. Yet this did not prevent
them from

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 117 | IA leaf 171 | PDF p. 171]

sometimes preferring the original texts to it when the matter seemed to
require this and the Version of the Seventy appeared not only unsuitable but
insufficiently serviceable to the truth. Jerome observed this in book 2
against Rufinus, and it can readily be gathered from Matthew 2:15 compared
with Hosea 11:1; John 19:37 with Zechariah 12:10; Jeremiah 31:15 with Matthew
2:18; Isaiah 25:8 with 1 Corinthians 15:54; and not infrequently elsewhere.

VIII. The testimonies cited from the Version of the Seventy in the New
Testament are authentic, not in themselves, or insofar as they were translated
from Hebrew into Greek by the Seventy, but accidentally, insofar as they were
approved and sanctified by the Holy Spirit and were brought by the evangelists
under his inspiration into the sacred context.

IX. If some of the Fathers extol this version and proclaim it authentic—as it
must be admitted that Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Augustine, and others
inclined to do—they act more from affection than from study, being almost
unskilled in the Hebrew language. Nor are we bound to swear by their words,
since they lived no less subject than the Seventy to human errors and
affections. But those among them who were more learned, such as Origen and
Jerome, hold a very different opinion, teaching that they were interpreters,
not prophets.

X. Although the church used this version for many years, it does not follow
that it regarded it as authentic and divine, but only that it held the version
in high esteem. That regular use, however, could not prejudice its freedom to
have recourse to the original texts whenever there was need.

XI. The very great discrepancy which occurs in chronology between the Hebrew
text and the Version of the Seventy does not prove that this version is
authentic, but rather establishes its corruption. From the creation of the
world to the flood, the Hebrew codices number only 1,656 years, while the
Seventy count 2,242. The following support the Hebrew calculation. 1. There is
the agreement of the Hebrew codices, among which, by everyone's confession,
no variation of reading occurs in this matter. 2. There is the agreement
(συμφωνία) of all the ancient versions—the Chaldean, Syriac, Samaritan,
Arabic, and Latin—which here depart from the Version of the Seventy and follow
the Hebrew original texts. 3. There is the manifest error of the Greek version
in the passages under discussion, namely, that it extends the life of
Methuselah beyond the flood by at least fourteen years, if not twenty, yet
does not maintain that he was saved from the water in the ark. For if
Methuselah fathered his son Lamech in the 165th year of his life, as the Greek
Bibles of the best quality read in Walton's edition, Methuselah must have lived
that many years after the flood. Nor, if some Greek copies here follow the
Hebrew—as Vossius says concerning Africanus and the Alexandrian Codex—should
those few which were undoubtedly corrected from the Hebrew be set against the
many thousands which the churches and Fathers follow and in which such a
calculation is found.

XII. The things brought forward here in support of the Greek calculation are
readily answered. First, concerning the years of puberty, which among the
Greeks are placed at about the two-hundredth year with a greater proportion to
the whole life than among the Hebrews at the one-hundredth year: although
Walton himself is not averse to the opinion of Vossius, he nevertheless
recognizes the vanity of this argument when he says that these were “vain
conjectures which could scarcely enter the mind of a sane person,”
*Prolegomena* 9, p. 68. It is gratuitously supposed that the years of puberty,
or begetting (παιδογονίαν), must follow a fourfold or fivefold proportion to
the whole life, since they ought to correspond to the vigor of the body. It is
absurd that in those first ages, when bodies were especially vigorous, puberty
was deferred until the two-hundredth year. It is certainly evident that in the
begettings recorded by Moses the proportion of puberty to life was not
observed, since there is a great inequality among them. Noah begets in his
500th year, Mahalalel in his 65th, Lamech in his 102nd, Kenan in his 70th, and
Enoch in his 65th. And although the life of the postdiluvian fathers was far

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 118 | IA leaf 172 | PDF p. 172]

shorter, their ages at begetting among the Greeks are almost equal to those of
the former fathers, being placed for the most part at the 130th year.

XIII. Second, the things added to prove the authenticity (αὐθεντίαν) of the
Greek text—because such a corruption could not have happened by chance, since
it was made many times, nor by design, because it cannot be said for whose
benefit it was made—press far more strongly for the integrity of the Hebrew
codex, to which, other things being equal, the prerogative is not usually
denied. Even if this corruption was not made deliberately, why could it not
have happened through the carelessness or oversight (ἀβλεψίᾳ) of copyists?
Then again, there could have been a sufficiently plausible pretext, namely, to
gratify the Egyptians in the reckoning of times, because the Hebrew text is
far more opposed to Egyptian antiquities than the Greek. Although this
argument is of no account to Walton, Vossius regards it as important and for
this very reason prefers the Greek to the Hebrew, because the former accords
with Egyptian antiquities more than the latter. For through the addition of
those 1,600 years, the Egyptian dynasties are readily confined within the
bounds of creation. Indeed, Scaliger and G. Vossius propose ways by which they
can be restricted within the bounds of the flood, if the additions of the
Greeks are admitted together with the Hebrew.

XIV. Although the Greek church and most Greek writers follow the calculation
of the Seventy rather than the Hebrew, no weight can thereby accrue to the
Greek calculation. For this could have happened through error or ignorance of
the Hebrew language, through which they neglected the original texts and
followed this version received everywhere.

XV. The anachronism (Ἀναχρονισμός) which occurs among the Seventy in the
calculation of the lives of the postdiluvian fathers cannot confer authenticity
upon this version. They extend the number of years up to the birth of Abraham
to more than 1,700, while the Hebrew has only 292, because they add at least
one hundred years to the life of each of the patriarchs who lived from the
flood to Abraham. On the contrary, this manifestly demonstrates its error,
wherever that error arose. Nor are the things brought forward by Vossius and
Walton in confirmation of it sufficiently valid to overthrow on that account
the authenticity (αὐθεντίαν) of the sacred text, as Baillie shows at length and
solidly in book 1 of his *Historical and Chronological Work*, chapter 4.
