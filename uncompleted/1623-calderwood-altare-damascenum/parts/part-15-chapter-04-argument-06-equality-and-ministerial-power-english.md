6 There is no distinction by divine right between a bishop and a presbyter.
Therefore, no one man was instituted who was furnished with singular power
of ordination and jurisdiction over other presbyters or pastors. The preceding
arguments remove the singular power of the princely bishop, but not a
difference of ranks. For the apostles were superior in rank to presbyters,
although they handled the affairs of the church by the common counsel and
consent of the presbyters. And in our consistories all things are conducted by
common counsel, although the pastor is superior in rank to the elders. But
once the difference of ranks among ministers of the word—which we now
undertake to remove—has also been removed, the power of the princely bishop
falls of itself as well. That there is no difference between a bishop and a
presbyter is proved by these arguments.

1 Nowhere in the Scriptures is this difference of ranks found. Therefore it
is not by divine right. Let them show, if they can, an ordinary pastor who is
superior in rank to other ordinary ministers of the gospel—as a minister of
the word is superior to a governing presbyter or to a deacon—even if he does
not exercise singular power of jurisdiction.

2 In the other ecclesiastical functions no inequality of ranks was
instituted. It is therefore not credible that this inequality of ranks was
instituted among pastors. No apostle was

[1623 printed p. 204 | IA leaf 216 | PDF p. 216]

higher in rank than his fellow apostles, nor an evangelist than his fellow
evangelists. Likewise, in the ordinary functions no elder was superior in
rank to the other elders, and no deacon to the other deacons, by divine
institution. If no inequality of ranks can be found in either extraordinary
or ordinary functions, or among either the higher or the lower functions, who
will believe that inequality of ranks was instituted in this one function
alone?

3 In the Scriptures this bishop, superior in rank to presbyters, is
designated by no proper and peculiar title; instead, the names are always
interchanged. From the common use of the names bishop and presbyter, Jerome
infers in his *Letter to Evagrius* that the same men were bishops and
presbyters. He says: *We read in Isaiah, “The fool will speak foolish things.”
I hear that someone has broken out into such madness that he prefers deacons
to presbyters—that is, to bishops. For since the apostle plainly teaches that
presbyters are the same men as bishops, why does the minister of tables and
widows haughtily exalt himself above those at whose prayers the body and
blood of Christ are made? You ask for authority. Hear the testimony: “Paul
and Timothy, servants of Jesus Christ, to all the saints who are at Philippi,
with the bishops and deacons.” Do you want another? In the Acts of the
Apostles Paul speaks thus to the priests of one church, Acts 20: “Take heed to
yourselves and to the whole flock in which the Holy Spirit placed you as
bishops, to rule the church of the Lord which he acquired with his blood.”
And lest anyone contentiously maintain that there were several bishops in one
church, hear another testimony in which it is most clearly proved that the
bishop and presbyter are the same: “For this reason I left you in Crete, that
you might correct what was lacking and appoint presbyters city by city, as I
also commanded you. If anyone is blameless, the husband of one wife, having
faithful children not accused of riotous living. For a bishop ought to be
blameless, as a steward of God.” And to Timothy: “Do not neglect the grace
which is in you, which was given to you through the laying on of the hands of
the presbytery.” And Peter also in his first epistle says, “I, a fellow
presbyter and witness of the sufferings of Christ, and a participant in the
future glory which is to be revealed, entreat the presbyters among you to
rule the flock of Christ and oversee it, not from necessity but voluntarily
according to God”—which is expressed more significantly in Greek as
`ἐπισκοποῦντες`, that is, superintending, from which the name bishop is also
derived.* Downham replies that Jerome proves only that the names were common
and interchangeable, and that the same men were called presbyters and
bishops until one was chosen from the presbyters to be placed over the rest.
Jerome asserts in the same place that this happened at Alexandria under Mark
the Evangelist and therefore in

called apostles, because they were not chosen from the presbyters but were
companions of the apostles and apostolic men. We spoke above about
Theodoret's comment that bishops were called apostles in the apostles' time.
It does not please Bellarmine himself, who judges the commentary of
Chrysostom and many others on Philippians 1 easier: namely, that in the
apostolic era the names bishop and presbyter were common to all priests, both
the greater, whom we now call bishops, and the lesser, whom we call
presbyters, although the things themselves and the powers were distinct. And
although the name bishop is now customarily given only to the principal
analogue—that is, to the high priest—the name bishop nevertheless belongs in
their own manner to the lesser priests too, for they also superintend and
rule the people, at least in the forum of conscience and by delegation from
the bishop. Junius says: *Sacred Scripture teaches nothing about these
greater and lesser priests. Chrysostom calls them greater and lesser
according to the arrangement observed in his own time. It would have been
better, however, to call them prior and posterior.* *Bellarmine nevertheless
rightly rejects Theodoret's invention. For Theodoret asserts only this new
thing: that the first bishop or presbyter is recorded as having been called
an apostle `διακονικῶς`, in a ministerial sense, in which matter he was deceived by a
false interpretation of one or two passages of Scripture.* That Jerome was
not proving only that the names were common is evident even from the fact
that he tries to defend the dignity of the presbyters against the arrogance
and usurpation of the deacons, showing that their dignity was the same as the
episcopal. If only the names had once been common, Jerome's argument would
have carried no weight. We spoke above about Jerome's conjecture that at
Alexandria under Mark the Evangelist the bishop first began

have shown that he inferred a sharing of the things from a sharing of the
names. Epiphanius responds differently concerning Philippians 1:1. He says
that Paul was accustomed to write in this manner because at that time, owing
to a shortage of ministers, in many places there were no presbyters but only
bishops with deacons; for bishops can also perform the office of presbyters.

place if Paul were saying “with the bishop and deacons,” or if he were writing
not to one city but to several at once. Yet since Paul himself writes to the
one city of Philippi and says that he writes to the bishops and deacons, how,
I ask, can that explanation have a place? For if there could be many bishops
in one city, how much more easily could there be one bishop and several
presbyters? Theodoret admits that by bishops in Philippians 1:1 presbyters are
understood. He says, *It certainly could not happen that many bishops were
pastors of one city; hence they were plainly presbyters whom he named
bishops.* Oecumenius on Philippians 1 says: *Not because there were many
bishops in one city, but he calls presbyters bishops, for at that time they
still shared the names.* Chrysostom, Homily 1 on Philippians: *What is this?
Were there many bishops of one city? By no means; instead he called
presbyters by this name. For at that time the names were still common, and
the bishop himself was also called a deacon. But as time advanced*

*a proper name was assigned to each, so that this man was called bishop and that
man presbyter.* Therefore, those who are called bishops in Philippians 1:1
were presbyters; likewise in Acts 20, Titus 1, and 1 Peter 5, as is evident
from the passages themselves. They admit that the names were common, but deny
that a sharing of the things follows. Peter calls himself a fellow presbyter,
1 Peter 5. Does it therefore follow that the office of apostles and
presbyters is the same? The apostolate is likewise a `διακονία`, a ministry;
are deacons therefore apostles? Some counts, such as those of Flanders, and
the Count Palatine of the Rhine are lords over other counts. At Ghent, the
man who sustains the person of the consul among the aldermen is distinguished
from his colleagues by no separate title, but is called only the first
alderman. I reply that apostle, presbyter, and deacon are not reciprocal
terms, `ἀντίστροφα`. Apostles could be called presbyters or deacons because of
the etymology and broader meaning of the word, but deacons and presbyters
were never called apostles. Junius says, *But according to God's word bishop
and presbyter are reciprocal terms, `ἀντίστροφα`.* When distinguishing
dignities and functions, we use either proper titles, an added modifier, or a
description. The Count Palatine is thereby distinguished from inferior
counts because he is the Count Palatine; and the consul among the aldermen of
Ghent, because, although he presides in the senate only by order, he is called
the First Alderman. But nowhere in Scripture is the hierarchical bishop
distinguished from a presbyter by a proper title, an added modifier, or a
description. Tilenus says: *The common use of names in Scripture, which the
adversaries also acknowledge, designates a sharing of the things as well. For
a presbyter cannot truly be called a bishop unless he truly is a bishop,
especially since the text passes from the name to the description of the
thing—that is, of the office,* Acts 20:28; Titus 1:7; 1 Peter 5:1–2. Junius
says: *We, however, argue conversely as follows. Since the names were common
at that time, and since a common use of names, when stated truly, imports a
sharing of the things designated by the common name, the necessary inference
proved from this is that, as the names were common, so the things designated
by the names were common: bishops and presbyters are commonly what they are
commonly said to be. Moreover, insofar as things are the same, their
operation is certainly the same.* The Syriac interpreter interchanges the
names bishop and presbyter to such a degree that he even retains the name
presbyter where the Greek has bishop, as in 1 Timothy 3:2 and Titus 1:7. I
will add Lorinus's observation concerning the common use of names. *Sidonius
Apollinaris calls Claudianus, who nevertheless did not possess the episcopal
rank, the prelate second in order, Epistle 25. Johannes Savaro, the scholiast
on Sidonius, also presents examples of the episcopal title being attributed
to men other than consecrated bishops, so that those properly ordained to the
episcopal office were described with the addition “highest bishops” or
“priests,” while other inferior ministers were sometimes called bishops,
book 3, chapter 14; on Acts 20:21. He also notes as worthy of attention that
the name bishop is sometimes applied not to a bishop, but to a simple priest
who performs certain more important*

*ecclesiastical duties. Johannes Savaro, the scholiast on Sidonius Apollinaris,
teaches this from examples of the churches of Vienne and Auvergne.* You see
that after inequality of ranks had been introduced, where the name bishop was
common, men were nevertheless distinguished by an addition and more
important duties were permitted to them. The argument from common names and
descriptions therefore runs as follows. Those to whom no proper title,
whether simple or with a modifier, and no peculiar description belongs, but
only a common one which belongs equally to all, share the things and are
therefore peers of one another. But no proper title or description is
attributed to any ordinary pastor; only that which is common to all pastors
is attributed. Therefore. Thus we argue not only from the common use of the
names bishop and presbyter, but also from the common use of all the other
names.

5 We draw the argument from an induction of the things themselves as follows.
Those who share all the things, duties, and powers are equal to one another
in rank of power. But the Pauline bishop and the presbyter share all the
things. Therefore. By a sharing of the things we include the power of order
and jurisdiction, according to the common distinction of the scholastics.

Downham refers to the power of order the power of preaching, administering
the sacraments, remitting and retaining sins, and ordaining ministers. They
do not deny that presbyters possess the power of preaching, administering
the sacraments, and remitting and retaining sins in the forum of conscience.
Bellarmine says: *But because presbyters and bishops participate in this
power in different ways, there are accordingly two species of priests. In
consecrating the Eucharist, presbyters depend upon bishops at least with
respect to its use, for bishops can forbid them to consecrate, suspend them
for a time, or command them to celebrate in such a place, such a manner, and
such a time. Moreover, presbyters do not possess this power in such a way
that they can communicate it to others.* And elsewhere: *It must nevertheless
be observed that all these things, and especially the office of preaching,
belong properly to bishops, so that even presbyters may not preach except by
the bishop's command or consent.* The hierarchists teach the same, namely,
that bishops are greater than presbyters in the exercise of the power of
order and in the power of ordination and jurisdiction. Downham explains his
opinion by a twofold distinction. He says: *Although every minister, insofar
as he is a presbyter, possesses the power of order, it nevertheless does not
follow that he can exercise that power according to his own judgment. Every
minister possesses the power of order insofar as he is simply a presbyter,
but the power of jurisdiction insofar as he is a prelate or pastor. He has
the former delivered to him in his ordination and the latter in his
institution. By the former he is furnished with authority to preach,
administer the sacraments, and perform the other spiritual actions of his
order, which another who is not of the same order cannot carry out even if
permission has been granted. Yet he cannot administer in any congregation as
its pastor those duties which belong to his order unless a flock has been
assigned and committed to him*

[Marginalia: *Defens.* l. 3. cap. 4. sect. 24. 25.; *De sacram. ordinis* ca. 5.; *De clericis* cap. 13.; *Defens.* l. 3. ca. 2. s. 11.]

*by the bishop, upon whom the care of the whole diocese rests. Therefore,
although a presbyter possesses the power of order, he cannot perform the
office of pastor in any congregation which is part of the episcopal province
unless he is furnished with authority for this use by the bishop, from whom
in his institution he receives the power of jurisdiction, the care of souls,
and the government of the parochial church. A distinction must also be made
between the power itself and its exercise. For although every minister
possesses the same power of order, which he shares with bishops in respect of
preaching the word and administering the sacraments of baptism and the
Lord's Supper, the exercise of that power nevertheless is and always has been
subject to the authority of the bishop, who permitted, directed, or suspended
it.* Elsewhere he likewise maintains that the bishop is properly the pastor
in his diocese, while presbyters and parish priests are merely assistants and
subsidiary workers. Field has the same teaching concerning the power of order
and jurisdiction and concerning the bishop as properly the pastor of the
parishes of his diocese. Saravia says: *The bishop cannot institute a pastor
in the parish which belongs to his care, although he can do so in his
diocese—namely, he who is the true pastor of all the parishes of his diocese
and communicates to another what he himself possesses.* Spalatensis also
says that the universal care of souls in his diocese so depends upon this one
man that he is the proper and ordinary pastor, though he can and ought to
assume other presbyters for the care. He can himself fully exercise the
parish priest's offices whenever he wishes. Parish priests depend upon his
direction, institution, and removal. The use of the presbyteral power which
they received in ordination is permitted when they become parish priests;
the power itself is not then granted, for the whole, complete, and perfect
presbyteral power was bestowed upon them in ordination. It is nevertheless
bound by an ecclesiastical prohibition. But when they are sent to the care of
souls, the bond of prohibition is removed, so that they can now bring the
power of their sacred presbyteral order into act. We have seen the
hierarchists relate their dreams, but they bring forward no testimonies from
Sacred Scripture. We do not read that presbyters received the power of order
except when they were about to bring it into act in some particular church.
For since ordination is twofold—common, by which someone is absolutely
devoted and dedicated to the ministry of the church apart from assignment to
a definite flock or particular church; and singular, by which men are bound
`ἰδίως`, particularly, to particular and definite churches—the extraordinary
pastors, namely evangelists, were sent out in the former manner, while
presbyters or ordinary pastors were sent in the latter. Those who had been
ordained `ἀπολελυμένως`, without restriction, could immediately exercise the
power they possessed wherever they stayed. Although they did not care for a
definite flock, they nevertheless always cared for some flock indefinitely.
Thus the power conferred upon them was not idle and inert. But they cannot
demonstrate by even one example that presbyters were ordained by a common and
absolute ordination to wander idly through a diocese or province like
unattached individuals, bound by the bond of an apostolic prohibition—much
less that of a

bishop—so that they might not exercise the power conferred in ordination.
Baynes aptly says: The Lord does not create ministers as academics create
doctors of medicine, assigning them no patients to care for, or masters of
arts without students. Those whom he calls to the ministry he sends by that
very act to its exercise, saying, “Go, preach,” and always with a relation to
a flock—either an indefinite one, as in the case of the apostles and
evangelists, or a definite one, as in the case of ordinary pastors. Paul
commands Titus to appoint presbyters town by town, not to ordain wanderers or
idle men. Moreover, they themselves admit that the names presbyter and bishop
were interchangeable in the primitive church. But the presbyter was called a
bishop in relation to the flock, as one superintending others. Durandus says:
*But perhaps in the primitive church they did not attach as much force to the
difference of names as they now do; for this reason they called everyone who
possessed a cure a bishop.* Therefore, if the presbyter is the same in name as
the bishop, and every bishop possessed the care of souls, it follows that
there were not some presbyters ordained to the ministry of the word and
sacraments without the care of souls who were called simply presbyters and
not bishops, and others with the care of souls who were called simply
bishops. Instead, all presbyters called to the ministry were indiscriminately
called bishops, because all were called to the care of souls. A head of a
household does not ordinarily appoint stewards unless their work is needed
in the household; so it is in the church. The Lord calls no one except to a
vacant place, so that they may be pastors in act, not in potentiality only.
But below in its own place we will say more about this absolute ordination
condemned by the Council of Chalcedon. They say that not only the presbyter as
a presbyter, but also as a pastor and prelate with the care of souls, depends
upon the bishop with respect to the exercise of his power; that the bishop is
properly the pastor; and that parish priests are only assistants and
`parastatae`, attendants. Field says: *Because the first churches instituted
by the apostles were spread out so widely that they required several
ministers of the word and sacraments, while one church nevertheless ought to
have only one pastor, they therefore arranged the state of the churches so
as to institute several presbyters in the same church, but in such a way that
they appointed only one who would be the principal pastor of that place, with
the rest assisting him.* The claim that each church ought to have only one
pastor properly so called is asserted not only without Scripture, but also
without reason. Indeed, examples are not lacking by which we can show that
there were two bishops, and consequently two pastors properly so called, in
one church. From one pastor the papists similarly draw an argument for the
universal episcopate of the Roman pontiff. He is the pastor of the Lord's
sheepfold, ruler of God's house, keeper of the vineyard, spouse of the
church, and universal bishop. In the same way the bishop is the pastor,
ruler, keeper, and spouse of the diocesan church. For they are not ashamed to
assert that *the bishop's marriage with the*

*church is begun in election and consummated in consecration.* Saravia says
of the presbyters: *Those who have been assumed by the bishops only into a
share of pastoral solicitude cannot proceed any further than has been
committed to them.* Spalatensis says that from Leo's dictum in Epistle 88 to
the bishop of Thessalonica—repeated constantly in the schools of the
scholastics and canonists, *We grant our functions to your charity in such a
way that you are called into a share of the solicitude, not into the fullness
of power*—there arose *that immense fullness of power and near omnipotence,
so that they alone,* namely, the Roman pontiffs, *possess the fullest
ecclesiastical jurisdiction in the whole universal church, while bishops are
called only into a share of the solicitude; that is, they are mere assistants
of the pope and consequently only his vicars and administrators. Our pope
wishes the solicitude of all the churches to belong to himself. He says that
he entrusts to the bishops a share of this solicitude of his, so that where
he himself cannot act, they may supply his place. This is in fact to make the
bishops assistants of the pope alone, who exercise not their own office and
duty but the pope's, in the pope's own name. For they make episcopal
solicitude a share of papal solicitude, which exists wholly in the pope as in
its proper and adequate subject.* If, according to the opinion of the
hierarchists, the bishop is pastor of the whole diocesan church—or of the
particular Lord's sheepfold throughout the whole diocese—presbyters are
called only into a share of the solicitude; that is, they are mere assistants
of the bishop and consequently only his vicars and administrators, so that
where he himself cannot act they supply for him his share of the solicitude.
This in fact makes presbyters assistants of the bishop alone, who exercise
not their own duty and office but the bishop's duty and office in the
bishop's own name. But that is absurd and contrary to Scripture. For
presbyters are ambassadors of Christ and discharge their embassy in his
name; they are pastors appointed by the Holy Spirit over the flock,
`ποιμαίνειν`, to shepherd the church of God, Acts 20:28. They are stewards or
dispensers of the mysteries of God, and stewards are appointed over the
household by the head of the household. The Lord of the harvest thrusts
workers into his harvest. He is the bridegroom; pastors are appointed by the
bridegroom to minister to the bride. Presbyters derive the power of order
from Christ, not from the bishop, no less than bishops derive it from Christ
rather than from the Roman pope. Ordination and institution no more prove
that the presbyter's power is derived from the bishop than that the bishop's
power is derived from the consecrating archbishop. For although human
ministry intervenes in an ordinary call to the ministry, Christ nevertheless
confers the power. Baynes illustrates this by an apt comparison: As fellows
in colleges, he says, admit someone whom the founder's statutes describe, so
the church chooses and admits to the ministry this man or that whom the laws
of Christ, written by the Holy Spirit on the sacred tablets, point out as if
with an outstretched finger. If presbyters derive their power not from the
bishop but from Christ, they are not the bishop's ambassadors, ministers, or

vicars; nor does the exercise of their ministry depend upon the bishop, but
upon Christ, to whom they will render an account for the flock which the Lord
committed to them. Indeed, even if a subordination of ranks were granted, it
still would not follow that presbyters are the bishop's vicars and
ambassadors, any more than that evangelists were the apostles' or that the
inferior priests and Levites were the superiors'. The duties and ministries
which the inferior priests were to perform were designated by the Lord
himself just as much as those of the superiors. Thus also the evangelists
were inferior to the apostles in such a way that they nevertheless derived
their power from Christ, because it was he—not the apostles—who gave some as
apostles and others as evangelists. After they had been called, the apostles
could not restrain them from the exercise of their duty or a part of it, as a
bishop restrains whichever of his parish priests he pleases from the duty of
preaching the gospel. The evangelists served in the ministry so that they
devoted their labor not to the apostles and their persons, but to the Lord
and his work. Paul says of Timothy, *He works the work of the Lord, as I do.*
Baynes says, *Their service was not personal but real.* When Christ sent out
the seventy disciples, he did not subject their ministry to the power of the
apostles. *But Christ sent all the apostles and disciples who represented the
episcopal and presbyteral order immediately, individually, and collectively,
just as he had been sent by his Father—that is, with the rightful and
spiritual authority necessary for governing the church. It follows that the
whole hierarchical order, consisting in the episcopal and priestly order,
derives its power and jurisdiction—that is, the faculty of governing the
church—immediately from Christ, though proportionately and subordinately,
just as in France the inferior judges and magistrates, although subject to
the parlements, derive their authority from the Most Christian King just as
immediately as the parlements themselves do.* Thus the Parisians. Therefore,
even if it were granted that presbyters are inferior to bishops, it still
does not follow that the power of presbyters, just as much as that of
bishops, is not derived immediately from Christ. But they will say that they
acknowledge an ordinary, not a delegated, power in presbyters. For
Spalatensis says: *Since presbyters therefore possess their own power by
divine right, all those err who make presbyter-parish-priests mere ministers
of bishops, for it is evident that they possess a proper and ordinary, not a
delegated, power.* Here he appears to condemn the hierarchists who make
presbyters mere ministers of bishops. Yet when he adds, *ordinary,
nevertheless, in such a way that they cannot bring it into act by human
ecclesiastical law unless they are assigned by the bishop, either to a fixed
place or at large, as the bishop pleases throughout his whole diocese,* he in
fact says the same thing. What he immediately says—*where they are assigned,
however, they exercise a proper and ordinary power, not a vicarious or
delegated one, as immediate ministers of Christ*—amounts to nothing. For that
power of assignment rests wholly in the bishop's power, and the presbyter's
power cannot put itself into act except at the bishop's pleasure.
Spalatensis says that the papalists make bishops in word only absolute
princes and not the pope's ministers

or vicars. Bellarmine says of the Roman pontiff in book 4, chapter 25: *The
bishops are assumed by the pontiff to sustain his own burden. The burden of
the bishops is therefore not their proper burden, but the pope's burden; and
the bishops sustain it not as their own but as the papal burden, like mere
assistants and administrators.* Others possess jurisdiction only by
communication and participation with the pope, to the extent that he has
granted it. *For he himself calls the bishops into a share of his solicitude.
Wherever, therefore, the pope wishes to exercise his solicitude himself, an
assistant cannot hinder him, because the assistant sustains not his own
burden but the pope's in that share of papal solicitude.* In this way they say

they are not the bishop's ministers and vicars. Yet the bishop is said to
assume presbyters to sustain a share of the burden which he alone cannot
sustain in the diocesan church, which they say is the bishop's church. The
presbyters' burden is therefore not their own proper burden but the bishop's,
and presbyters sustain it not as their own but as the episcopal burden, like
mere assistants and administrators. And wherever the bishop wishes to
exercise his solicitude himself, the presbyter-assistant cannot hinder him,
because the assistant sustains not his own burden but the bishop's in that
share of episcopal solicitude. Thus, if a bishop should remain for several
years in some parish and wish to exercise his solicitude, farewell to the
parish priest. But this contrivance concerning fullness of power and a share
of solicitude is a mere invention. God granted no one a broader and fuller
power than he can exercise by himself, as Baynes demonstrates by this
argument. God cannot command anyone to undertake a greater province than he
is equal to by his own strength unless he has also granted him the faculty
of assuming others to whom he may communicate a share of the solicitude and
burden. For one who wills the end also wills the means to the end. If God had
wished to appoint an ecumenical and universal pastor of all the churches, he
would necessarily also have had to grant him the faculty of substituting
others and deriving to them the power to teach and govern particular
churches. If someone were to commit his sheep, which grazed in several
pastures, to one man, would he not also grant him the power to assume other
inferior shepherds who would serve under him? Thus, if God committed the
whole diocesan church and the particular parishes within it to the bishop
with the fullness of pastoral care and government, he would also have had to
appoint him as the head from whom the power of teaching and governing flowed
to others whom it was necessary to assume for sustaining a share of the
burden and solicitude. In that case the bishop, not the presbyters, would
render an account to the Lord for the matter well or badly conducted in his
office, just as those inferior shepherds render account to the man to whom
the sheep were committed, not to the lord and owner.

If they say that the Lord appointed the bishop as pastor on the condition
that he have other inferior ministers and assistants in the parishes, then,
if God permitted the bishop to assume whomever he wished, the matter comes to
the same thing: they are the bishop's ministers, not the Lord's. If God
wished him to assume the sort of men whom he himself designated by his laws,
then it follows that he granted the bishop no broader power than he can
exercise by himself, while the others are furnished with authority from
Christ no less than the bishops, so that they may proportionately perform
their own duty. When Christ said to Peter, *Feed my sheep,* Bellarmine says
that this is the same as if he had said, *Feed all my sheep.* Since Peter
could not perform this by himself, he could by the necessary fullness of
power feed them through others. Whitaker replies that Christ did not entrust
to Peter these sheep or those, or all of them together, but an indefinite
number and together with others. He said, Go and teach all nations. Peter fed
through himself, not through others, because this is precisely what testifies
to our love toward Christ. In feeding the sheep, the apostles devoted their
labor to no one except Christ. *A vicarious method of feeding was never
instituted by Christ. Christ requires no more from anyone in feeding than the
man himself can perform, and he requires from each precisely what each can
do.* If he does not require it from Peter, certainly he does not require it
from the bishop either. Nor did he ever appoint the bishop as pastor of a
whole diocese which he cannot feed by himself. Thus the man whom they wish to
be properly the principal and universal pastor of the diocese will not
properly be the pastor; rather, the parish priests who by themselves feed the
flocks committed to them will be. When the Fathers and theologians at the
Council of Trent disputed whether the bishops' power was by divine right or
not, Lainez, the general of the Jesuits, said that Peter was the sole and
single pastor of the church; that undivided power had been placed in the one
pastor, who, as the matter requires, imparts and communicates it to his
fellow ministers; that jurisdiction exists essentially in the Apostolic See
alone and in others by derivation and participation; that the pope could not
abolish the universal order of appointing bishops in the church, since it
came from God, but that he could abolish any particular bishop by pontifical
authority, since each was by canon law. Although Lainez's speech was praised
by the papalists, it was considered flattering by the opposing party and
heretical by some. The ailing bishop of Paris declared openly before everyone
that as soon as a congregation was held he would freely pronounce his
opinion against that doctrine: it was unheard of in earlier ages, first
devised fifty years before by Cajetan so that he might earn the cardinal's
hat, and at that very time disgracefully censured by the theologians of the
Sorbonne as a doctrine that despoils the church of the title of Christ's
bride and prostitutes her like a slave to the lust of one man. *Lainez
wishes,* they said, *only one bishop to have been instituted by Christ and
the rest to possess no power except one dependent upon him, which is just as
if he were to say*

*that there is one bishop alone and all the others are his vicars, removable

way that it is not in the pope's power to bring it about that there are no

dioceses, confer greater or lesser authority upon them, and even suspend or
deprive them—a power he does not possess in a matter which is by divine
right. For he cannot take from a priest the authority to consecrate because
the priest has it from Christ; but he can take all jurisdiction from a
bishop, for no other reason than that the bishop has it from the pope
himself.* The things falsely alleged for bishops can rightly be applied to
presbyters against the bishop who is the lesser pope. Namely, if their power
depends upon the bishops, it is just as if the bishop alone were pastor and
they only vicars removable at his pleasure. And if he can restrict or extend
their authority and their parishes, suspend it or take it away, it follows
that presbyters are not by divine right. Antonio de Grossi, the Dominican,
observed from Paul's words to the Ephesians when they were at Miletus,
exhorting them *to take heed to the flock over whose government they had
been placed by the Holy Spirit,* that bishops do not receive the performance
of their office from men. For if matters were so, they would be hirelings
whose sheep are not their own, and once the man by whom this care was
delegated to them had been satisfied, they would have nothing further to
consider. Paul demonstrates that the obligation to govern the Christian
people is divine and imposed by the Holy Spirit, so that it can be clear that
they cannot be excused from it by the force of any human dispensation; that
no mention is made of the ordainer, but the whole is attributed to the Holy
Spirit, who not only gave the authority to govern but also divided a portion
of the flock and assigned it to them to feed; that one must say with St. Paul
that the Holy Spirit places men over the flock to feed it; that the work is
never ascribed to the instrument or minister, but always to the principal
agent; and, from Ephesians 4, that Christ gives pastors after his ascension
into heaven. What Grossi observed from these passages in favor of the
independent power of bishops must be applied to presbyters, not bishops. For
Paul addresses the Ephesian presbyters, who are called bishops, not bishops
so called by antonomasia; and we spoke above about Ephesians 4. The lesser
popes—that is, the bishops—contended so strenuously for their own dignity and

complained that *the authority of bishops had been increased excessively,
