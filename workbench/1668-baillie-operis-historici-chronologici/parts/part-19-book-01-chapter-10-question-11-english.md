### QUESTION XI

#### What is the true beginning of Daniel’s seventy weeks?

Answer. I do not recall greater disagreement among either chronologists or
interpreters of Scripture—ancient or modern, Protestant or Roman
Catholic—than over the question before us. With due respect preserved for
each of the learned men, we shall set out our views on this matter as clearly
and briefly as we can.

> Marginalia, printed p. 166: *There is great disagreement among writers over
> the beginning of the seventy weeks*.

It is undoubted and undisputed, if you except a few Jews, that the weeks begin
with the issuing of the word about the rebuilding of Jerusalem. For the angel
Gabriel expressly established this as the boundary at the earlier end of this
period. But here the entire dispute arises: When, at last, did that word about
rebuilding Jerusalem issue? There is no need to linger over the grammatical
sense of the words.

Although Vatablus and others press the meaning of the word שוב, as if the
angel denoted a decree not only about rebuilding Jerusalem but also about
bringing the people back, we do not venture to embrace it, even though it
would serve our purpose. For, as Scaliger proves with many examples, the verb
שוב preceding an action signifies nothing other than the bare repetition of
that action. Thus the expression *to cause to return and to build* means
nothing other than *to rebuild*, as almost all commentators explain it here.
The Seventy Interpreters, to be sure, translate the word השיב at the beginning
of verse 25 with ἀποκριθῆναι, “to answer”—which only a very generous
interpretation can draw into any suitable sense. Yet at the end of the verse
they correctly render the same word השיב in the *qal* stem with πάλιν,
“again,” as they often do elsewhere.

> Marginalia, printed p. 166: *The meaning of the word שוב*.

Leaving grammatical considerations aside, the question concerns the matter
itself. What is this issuing of the word, or when did this word about the
rebuilding of Jerusalem issue? If we pass over the excessively extravagant
and peculiar opinions of a few, there are six more celebrated positions, each
of which has many distinguished adherents.

The first is that of Lyranus, Vatablus, Paul of Burgos, and others, who
interpret the issuing of the word as Jeremiah’s prophecy in chapter 29, verse
10. Daniel refers to it here in chapter 9, where the return of the people
after seventy years of captivity is foretold. It occurred in Zedekiah’s
fourth year, seven years before the city’s destruction. Most of the rabbis are
not far from this opinion: they begin the weeks with the first destruction of
the city and close them with the second.

> Marginalia, printed p. 166: *The first opinion, Lyranus’s, concerning
> Zedekiah’s fourth year, is rejected*.

This opinion is not acceptable. Certainly Lyranus’s first view was much

[Printed p. 167 | IA leaf 197 | access PDF 197 | OCR object 196]

more correct, when he began the weeks with Africanus from Artaxerxes’s
twentieth year. Here δεύτεραι φροντίδες, “second thoughts,” are not
σοφώτεραι, “wiser,” but φαυλότεραι, “worse.” Lyranus admits that a thoroughly
weak argument moved him from his sounder position: he thought that, if he
persisted in his first view, he would be compelled to grant Mordecai a
lifespan of 250 years. We explained above how he could easily have avoided
this difficulty. In any event, we offer these few points against his later
and worse opinion.

First, the chronology does not at all fit it, for no more than 490 years
elapsed from the first week to the last, whereas more than six hundred are
counted from Zedekiah’s fourth year to Christ. Second, no boundary of the weeks
should be placed before Daniel’s vision, but Zedekiah’s fourth year preceded
it by a long time. Third, there is no reason for us to refer the issuing of
the word to the prophecy in chapter 29 rather than to the prophecy in chapter
25, verse 12, where Jeremiah first predicts the people’s return after seventy
years—twelve years before the proposed boundary, in Jehoiakim’s fourth year
and Nebuchadnezzar’s first. Indeed, if the beginning of the weeks is to be
fixed in prophecies concerning Jerusalem’s restoration, what prevents us from
going back to the first prophecy on the matter, Isaiah 44:28, where more than
seven hundred years before the end of the weeks Cyrus is foretold as the one
who will rebuild the city and temple?

The second opinion belongs to those who interpret the issuing of the word as
the word spoken by God for the angel Gabriel to carry to the prophet Daniel,
as described in chapter 9, verse 23. They begin the weeks with the first year
of Darius the Mede. Include here those who place the issuing of the word in
Cyrus’s first or second year, since the interval is small, when in accordance
with the prophets’ predictions and the angel’s present message Cyrus by his
decree granted the Jews both a return to their homeland and the power to
build both city and temple. Very many ancient and recent writers hold this
opinion: Origen, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Beroaldus, Broughton,
Paraeus, Rainolds, Howes, Nisbet, and the English annotators expressly in
their earlier edition, although in the later they only lean this way while
professing doubt and uncertainty. We cannot embrace this opinion, especially
for the following reason: the chronology does not fit. We therefore argue
against it as follows.

> Marginalia, printed p. 167: *The second opinion too, that of Paraeus and many
> others concerning the first year of Darius the Mede, is rejected*.

The issuing of the word from which the seventy weeks begin preceded Christ’s
death by only 490 years. But the word spoken either by God to Gabriel or by
Gabriel to Daniel—or any fulfillment of it through Cyrus’s decree concerning
the Jews’ return—preceded Christ’s death by at least one hundred years beyond
490. Therefore none of these can be the issuing of the word from which the
weeks begin. The entire controversy concerns the minor premise. Rainolds
indeed concedes it and evades the major by the peculiar invention we discussed
in the preceding question. But all who support this opinion apart from
Rainolds deny the minor premise. When we prove it by the unanimous testimony
of all nonbiblical authors—Chaldaeans, Greeks, and Latins, concerning which
the Greek and Latin Fathers of the church, and later Protestants and Roman
Catholics, raise no doubt—they nevertheless turn their faces against such
great authority with astonishing boldness. Since they dare despise the
agreement of so many and such great men, they ought to accept it calmly if
others attach little weight to their own positions on this matter. Daniel’s
authority is always on their lips as a shield against every missile. Anyone
who would not gladly concede to them that Daniel alone is to be preferred to
all the human writings of all mortals is certainly no Christian. But they
must not assume their premise and beg the very point in question. Let them
prove from any testimony of Daniel or any other sacred writer that only 490
years elapsed from Cyrus’s first year to Christ’s death—or, what is the same,
that the seventy weeks began with Cyrus’s first year—and we shall yield. If
they cannot, let them permit us to reconcile the sacred authors with the
nonbiblical ones. Why should writers be set against one another and forced
into battle when with little effort they can be persuaded to a beautiful
agreement?

The entire question here concerns only the first and small portion of those
times. For they concede that the nonbiblical authors arrange the chronology
of the Greek and Roman monarchies with complete correctness. Indeed, they
admit that secular authors accurately transmit the total chronology of more
than two thousand years from Alexander the Great to the present day. They
wish to be wise only in arranging the Persian monarchy. If the question were
about a few years, one or two five-year periods, or three or four Olympiads,
we would not contend. But when in the short interval from Cyrus to Alexander,
a span of 230 years, they wish all ancient writers to be mistaken by a full
one hundred years, they will have to seek credulous Jews to believe them.
Certainly no mortal before our age fastened this charge upon those
outstanding men who deserved so well of history and chronology—as well as any
others

[Printed p. 168 | IA leaf 198 | access PDF 198 | OCR object 197]

down to the present day. Nevertheless, lest we seem to wish to crush every
spark of sound reason and clear truth under the authorities of the entire
human race, let us examine the ten objections Dr. Nisbet collected from
others against our argument.

> Marginalia, printed p. 168: *The Persian Empire must not be shortened
> contrary to the testimony of all ancient writers*.

We said that the force of our argument comes down to this: more than two
hundred years elapsed from Cyrus to Alexander. If this is true, they will not
deny that Daniel’s weeks began much later than Cyrus’s first year. They
therefore bend all their strength to proving, against the host of all learned
men, that the Persian monarchy—or the interval from Cyrus to Alexander—lasted
no more than 125 years. Let Nisbet’s whole set of ten arguments on this matter
therefore come forward.

The first is this. From Cyrus’s first year to Christ’s death there elapsed
only seventy weeks of years, or 490 years. But it is agreed that approximately
three hundred of these must be assigned to the Greek monarchy and sixty to
the Romans. Therefore no more than 140 remain for the Persian monarchy.

Answer. This argument is altogether worthless, for it proves nothing and
merely assumes the question at issue, namely, that the weeks really began with
Cyrus’s first year. Rainolds indeed concedes this to Nisbet, who is
ὁμόψηφος, “of the same vote,” with him in this argument, but this is irrelevant
to us and to the matter itself. Rainolds may satisfy himself by denying the
consequence that Nisbet draws from his concession through his aforesaid
synecdoche.

> Marginalia, printed p. 168: *Nisbet’s objection 1*.

Nisbet’s second argument. The 490 years are ten jubilees, beginning with the
issuing of the word and ending at Christ’s death. But if anything is added to
these 490 years by extending the Persian kingdom, Christ’s death will not fall
in a jubilee.

Answer. How many faults are in this argument? What necessity of reason or
authority compels Christ to die in a jubilee? Were not all feast days and
years, as much as the jubilee, referred to Christ? Must Christ therefore have
died on the Sabbath, at the new moon, at Passover, at Pentecost, at the Feast
of Tabernacles, at the Feast of Trumpets, and at every other solemn season?
Was that necessary—indeed, was it possible? Next, did the year of Christ’s
death really fall in a jubilee? By what witnesses does Nisbet prove this? By
none. Third, what prevents Christ from dying in a jubilee even if the weeks
begin in Artaxerxes’s seventh or twentieth year, or even in Darius’s second or
sixth? For 490 years always make ten jubilees. Therefore, from whatever
boundary you begin the weeks, provided you close them at the passion, Christ
can die in a jubilee. Cyrus’s first year might perhaps do something to prevent
this, but it contributes nothing at all to securing it.

> Marginalia, printed p. 168: *2*.

Nisbet’s third argument. If the Persian kingdom lasted two hundred years,
then Zerubbabel continued in the government of the Jews for 146 years, namely,
until Nehemiah’s beginning in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes Mnemon. But
this is absurd.

Answer. There is nothing sound in this argument either. It rests upon the
false principle of Rainolds and Scaliger that the king under whom Nehemiah was
sent to Jerusalem was Mnemon and not Longimanus. Yet more than sixty years
stand between the twentieth year of Longimanus and the twentieth of Mnemon.
Moreover, no reasoning can prove that Zerubbabel’s government was continuously
connected to Nehemiah’s. Scripture says nothing of Zerubbabel after the temple
was completed in the sixth year of Darius Hystaspes, no more than twenty-three
years after the Persian monarchy began.

> Marginalia, printed p. 168: *3*.

Nisbet’s fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh arguments are drawn from the
extraordinary ages of the high priest Joshua, of the people who saw both
temples, and of Ezra and Nehemiah. We dealt with all of these above. All rest
upon Scaliger’s false principles: namely, that the second temple was built by
Darius Nothus and not Hystaspes, and that Ezra and Nehemiah prolonged their
lives from Cyrus to Darius Codomannus. These are inventions confirmed by no
witness and no reasoning.

> Marginalia, printed p. 168: *4. 5. 6. 7*.

The eighth argument is the testimony of Ctesias, who asserts that Darius
Hystaspes was so far from reigning thirty-six years that he did not live that
many.

Answer. Nisbet did not see Ctesias, but refers us to Broughton. Broughton,
however, is accustomed to cite authors’ names but not passages. Ctesias is a
writer of little authority if we believe that most weighty author Plutarch,
for he stuffed his work with many falsehoods and fables. The work itself has
long since perished apart from a few fragments. But let Ctesias possess as
much authority as anyone wishes: if Nisbet wishes to stand by his opinion,
his case will fall. For however many years he takes from Darius or others, he
adds the same number beyond the common calculation to Cambyses and others, so
that he altogether increases the total span of the Persian monarchy and by no
means diminishes it. As for Darius, greater credit must be given to Herodotus,
who lived nearest to him and was σύγχρονος, “a contemporary,” of his son
Xerxes. Although Ctesias, as Howes and others cite him for Nisbet, makes Darius
his σύγχρονον, “contemporary,” he gives Darius thirty-one years—even if not
thirty-six with Herodotus—and for the four years he takes from Darius, he
adds to his predecessor Cambyses

> Marginalia, printed p. 168: *8*.

[Printed p. 169 | IA leaf 199 | access PDF 199 | OCR object 198]

a full ten beyond Herodotus’s eight.

When I examine those extracts from Ctesias as printed with Henri Estienne’s
Latin edition at the end of Herodotus, I can add to the preceding evidence
various authors there—Aristotle, Aulus Gellius, Tzetzes, and others—who amply
attest Ctesias’s trifling mendacity. Indeed, who would deny that the man is
idle and deceitful when he confidently presents to the world an entire
κυνοκέφαλον, or “dog-headed,” people that bark rather than speak; a one-legged
race, μονοσκελῆ, that nevertheless moves extremely swiftly by leaping; a
people without necks who bear their eyes on their shoulders; and many other
utterly false monsters of this sort? As for the passage Nisbet cites,
Broughton did well not to transcribe it. It carries such a rank falsehood on
its face that Henri Estienne—otherwise far too devoted to Ctesias—was ashamed
to translate it into Latin. Ctesias does indeed assert that Darius spent
thirty-one years in the kingdom, but that he was only twelve years old before
he reigned. Estienne omitted this in his translation, for he did not have the
effrontery to assert with Ctesias that the twelve-year-old boy Darius killed
the Magus and was elected king by the Persians.

Nisbet’s ninth argument. Darius Nothus, he says, is commonly assigned nineteen
years, but Scripture grants him only six, for the temple was completed in his
sixth year and Scripture is silent about him afterward. Moreover, Ezra was
sent to Jerusalem in the seventh year of Artaxerxes Mnemon, and this year is
mentioned immediately after Darius’s sixth. For Ezra 6:14 says the Jews
prospered in building the temple according to the decree of Cyrus, Darius,
and Artaxerxes. Therefore Artaxerxes reigned together with Darius and together
with him advanced the temple’s construction.

Answer. This argument commits many errors. Let us grant that Darius Nothus
built the temple and Artaxerxes Mnemon sent Ezra to Jerusalem—both of which
are false. Even so, how will this twofold inference be proved? First,
Scripture mentions Darius’s sixth year; therefore Darius had no more. Second,
Artaxerxes’s seventh year is mentioned after Darius’s sixth; therefore
Artaxerxes’s first year was Darius’s first, and the son reigned with his
father throughout all his father’s years, even the first. These consequences
are utterly rotten, and a third no less senseless than the preceding ones can
be added. The temple’s construction was advanced by the decrees of Cyrus,
Darius, and Artaxerxes; therefore Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes reigned
together, and Cyrus’s first year was not only Darius’s first but also
Artaxerxes’s.

> Marginalia, printed p. 169: *9*.

Nisbet’s tenth argument is this. The temple’s construction lasted from
Cyrus’s first year to the sixth year of Darius Nothus. According to us, 112
years elapsed between these boundaries, but sacred Scripture contracts them
to forty-six in John 2:20.

Answer. This argument too rests on the false principle that the Darius who
built the temple was Nothus rather than Hystaspes. The true chronology indeed
contracts the temple’s construction from Cyrus’s first year to Darius
Hystaspes’s sixth not merely to forty-six years, but to half that sum,
twenty-three. Nevertheless, not even one year is on that account to be
subtracted from the common total of the Persian monarchy.

> Marginalia, printed p. 169: *10*.

Thus we have answered Nisbet’s ten objections to our argument. Let us see what
he himself advances for his own position. His first argument comes from the
testimony of Jerome and Eusebius, who begin the weeks with Cyrus’s first year.

Answer. When citing testimony, αὐθάδεια, “self-assurance,” is always extremely
convenient. If Nisbet had inspected the cited authors here with his own eyes,
he would have dismissed both of his witnesses unheard. Deterred by the
difficulty of the question, Jerome in his commentary on Daniel 9 professes
that he dares pronounce nothing at all. He only reports the opinions of
others, leaving readers free to choose. But the opinion he reports first and
at greatest length is that of Africanus, who begins the weeks not with Cyrus’s
first year but with the twentieth year of Artaxerxes Longimanus.

As for Eusebius, he sometimes begins the weeks with Cyrus’s first year and
sometimes with the second year of Darius Hystaspes. But when he begins with
Cyrus, he falls from this very source into thoroughly foul errors. Neither
Eusebius nor any ancient author ever conceived of shaving a full one hundred
years—nearly half—from the Persian monarchy. Yet Eusebius was carried so far
into folly that, because he had fixed the beginning of the weeks in Cyrus’s
first year, he thought it necessary to interpret the anointed ruler mentioned
by Daniel not as Jesus Christ but as the last of the Maccabean high priests.
He also closed the penultimate, sixty-ninth week when Jerusalem was captured
by Pompey, one hundred years before Christ, and separated the final week from
the penultimate by that entire century. I do not think any of these positions
will please Nisbet.

> Marginalia, printed p. 169: *Nisbet’s arguments for his own position 1*.

His second argument is this. The issuing of the word from which the weeks
begin in Daniel 9:25 is the issuing of the word under discussion in verse 23
of the same chapter. But the issuing of the word in verse 23 occurred in
Cyrus’s first year, when Daniel prayed—namely, the first year of Darius the
Mede, which coincides with Cyrus’s first. The major premise is confirmed from
Ezra 6:14, where the temple’s restoration is said to have been accomplished
by God’s command. Yet God issued the command to no Persian king, but only to
the angel Gabriel.

Answer. The major premise is denied by almost all chronologists and
interpreters—not only by those who support the other opinions, but

> Marginalia, printed p. 169: *2*.

[Printed p. 170 | IA leaf 200 | access PDF 200 | OCR object 199]

also by most of those who defend the position now before us. For they too
interpret the issuing of the word not as the command given to Gabriel and
communicated to Daniel, but as some portion of the event expressed in
Gabriel’s message. Thus the word that went from God to Gabriel so that Gabriel
would immediately reveal God’s good pleasure to Daniel is not identical with
the portion of that good pleasure by which, through divine prompting, the
Persian kings were moved to command Jerusalem’s restoration. The former word
was God’s alone; the latter also belonged to men. The former word was inspired
to Gabriel alone so it might be communicated to Daniel alone; the latter was
inspired in the Persian kings so it might be proclaimed by public decree to
Jews and Gentiles.

The passage adduced to confirm the major premise overthrows it. We admit that
the kings’ decree coincided with the divine command. Just as in Ezra 7:28 the
kings’ decree concerning the temple’s affairs is called a divine command, so
in Ezra 6:14 the decrees of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes concerning the
construction even of the city, as many understand it, are called a divine
order. Likewise, the impulse by which God stirred the spirit of those kings
to divine works is expressly called a divine command in 2 Chronicles 36:23
and Ezra 1:2. It does not follow, however, that the word concerning the
restoration of Jerusalem which Daniel 9:25 predicts would issue from God and
the kings was the same word that the same passage says had already issued
from God to Gabriel.

In order at least to prove that the weeks begin with Cyrus’s first year, Howes
reduces Paraeus’s dispute with Scaliger and Calvisius in support of this
opinion to five arguments. The first is this. The weeks began with an absolute
issuing of the word. But only the first issuing of the word in Cyrus’s decree
was absolute, whereas the second issuing in Darius’s decree and the third and
fourth in Artaxerxes’s decrees were not absolute issuings of the word.

> Marginalia, printed p. 170: *Howes’s Argument 1 for the second opinion*.

Answer. We deny the minor premise, for the first issuing in Cyrus’s decree was
as limited as the second, third, or fourth. But the particular event to which
the issuing of which Daniel speaks absolutely is to be assigned was declared
after the fact by the outcome itself. It must therefore be understood as the
issuing from which no more than 490 years elapsed until Christ. This is not
true of Cyrus’s decree.

Paraeus’s second argument is this. The weeks began with that issuing of the
word, or that decree of the kings, which Daniel himself understood distinctly,
of which he was certain, and from which he received comfort in his grief. But
Cyrus’s decree alone, and no decrees of other kings, possessed these
qualities.

> Marginalia, printed p. 170: *2*.

Answer. Both propositions can be denied. The major can be denied because many
future things were revealed to the prophets whose times they themselves did
not know distinctly. Yet they were utterly certain of them and received great
comfort from them. The minor is not true either, for at the time of the
revelation and prayers, the decrees of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes were
equally known and unknown: all were still unrevealed future events, apart from
what was then revealed concerning the future issuing of the word, Jerusalem’s
restoration, and the Messiah’s death within 490 years—years that were to begin
not in the present but shortly afterward at God’s appointed time. Daniel knew
these things distinctly, became certain of them, and took comfort from them.
God did not think a more distinct and particular knowledge necessary for him.

Paraeus’s third argument. Unless the weeks’ beginning is fixed in Cyrus’s
public decree, the ancient church’s faith concerning the time of the Messiah’s
death would have wavered in uncertainty.

> Marginalia, printed p. 170: *3*.

Answer. The ancient church did not know by the certainty of faith the very
year in which the Messiah would die, for God did not think it fitting to grant
the church such a distinct knowledge of that event. Moreover, nothing
prevents God’s people from knowing Christ’s death at the end of the weeks with
equal distinctness, whatever the beginning of the weeks might finally have
been. For Darius’s decree and both of Artaxerxes’s decrees were as well known
to them as Cyrus’s first.

The fourth, which I consider weightier than all the rest, is this. Unless the
weeks begin with Cyrus’s first year, there will be a gap in sacred chronology.
From the beginning of the world to Cyrus’s first year a continuous sequence
of times appears in Holy Scripture. It would be inconvenient if, from Cyrus’s
first year to the seventh or twentieth of Longimanus—a period of nearly one
hundred years—the chronological calculation were interrupted and could not
be found in Holy Scripture.

> Marginalia, printed p. 170: *4*.

Answer. There is no difficulty here, since in that very year which is
manifestly connected with the earlier times from the beginning of the world,
the epoch extending to Christ was published, although it was not to begin
immediately. Moreover, according to the principles of those debating the
matter, the years of the Persian kings and their times are reduced to the
years alone that Scripture mentions. Then there is no apparent gap at all.
For we shall easily connect the last of the seventy years of captivity with
the first of the seventy weeks through the total years of those recorded in
Scripture as reigning in Persia from Cyrus’s first year to Artaxerxes’s
seventh.

Paraeus’s last argument is drawn from the absurdly great ages of Zerubbabel
and Joshua if the weeks do not begin

[Printed p. 171 | IA leaf 201 | access PDF 201 | OCR object 200]

before the second year of Darius Nothus, when they are said to have built the
temple.

Answer. We dealt with those absurdities above. They do not concern us, but
rather our opponents, who fight against us on this point with Scaliger.

Before I leave this opinion, I wish to note what the very learned man
Constantijn L’Empereur offers to illustrate it in his annotations on Rabbi
Jacchiades’s commentary on Daniel 9. He devises an altogether new and peculiar
way to defend it, with which he seems quite pleased. I find it so improbable,
not to say foolish, that I do not think it necessary to spend time refuting
it. I shall merely recount it plainly as evidence of how widely a small error
at the beginning strays by the end, and into what depths of absurdity the
reckoning of the weeks from Cyrus’s first year has carried even the most
learned and sensible men in our own day.

> Marginalia, printed p. 171: *The peculiar view of L’Empereur is to be
> rejected*.

L’Empereur wishes Daniel to count only the years in which work progressed,
omitting all those in which it ceased. Thus although he admits that more than
140 years elapsed from the beginning of Cyrus’s reign to the seventh year of
Artaxerxes Mnemon, he wishes Daniel to count only forty-nine, or seven weeks,
in the following way. He says that the temple’s construction continued for
forty-six years from Cyrus’s first year to Xerxes’s first—the years of which
he thinks the Jews spoke to Christ in John 2. Next, he supposes that the first
interruption of the work occurred in Xerxes’s first year, so that the work
continued throughout the days of Cyrus, Cambyses, Smerdis, and Darius
Hystaspes, although the temple was not brought to completion during all that
time. He then wishes the work to have been suspended throughout the lives of
Xerxes and Artaxerxes Longimanus, resumed in Darius Nothus’s second year, and
continued until his sixth, though for only three incomplete years. For, he
says, through the Jews’ own negligence rather than any external force, the
work again ceased in Nothus’s sixth year until the seventh year of Artaxerxes
Longimanus, a period of eighteen years. At that point Ezra completed the
temple in a few months. Thus those months added in Artaxerxes’s seventh year
to the part missing from Darius’s sixth make only a single year, the
forty-ninth and last of the seven weeks in which the temple was built. He
wishes the other sixty-three weeks to be counted continuously from Xerxes’s
seventh year to Christ’s passion. Yet there he finds only 434 years, which,
when added to the previous forty-nine, make only 483, so that seven years are
still missing from the complete sum of 490. He supplies these not, like
Africanus and others, by the invention of lunar years, but, following Scaliger
and Selden, by Egyptian years—years consisting of twelve thirty-day months,
which the Jews sometimes, though very rarely, used. In a span of 483, such
Egyptian years exceed ordinary, true solar years by seven years. I shall not
now recount how many absurdities without any witness appear in this very
learned man’s opinion.

I shall touch briefly on the next two opinions, the third and fourth. Both
wish the weeks to begin with a second year of Darius, but the former
understands Hystaspes and the latter Nothus. Eusebius, Cyril of Jerusalem,
Driedo, and Jansenius in chapter 122 of his *Harmony* support Darius
Hystaspes. This opinion is not consistent with the chronology, for many more
than 490 years elapsed from the second year of Darius Hystaspes to Christ’s
death. To resolve this difficulty, Eusebius interprets the seventieth and last
week—which according to this reckoning began long before Christ’s birth—as
groups of ten years. He thereby extends that single week to seventy years,
with intolerable absurdity.

> Marginalia, printed p. 171: *Jansenius’s third opinion concerning the second
> year of Darius Hystaspes is rejected*.

Those who wish to begin the weeks with the second year of Darius Nothus are
Scaliger, Junius, Diodati, Polanus, Calvisius, and Ubbo Emmius, all highly
honored men. Nevertheless, we cannot embrace their opinion, chiefly for this
twofold reason. First, Darius Nothus issued no decree concerning either the
temple or the city. We refuted this error of Scaliger above. Second, the
chronology does not fit this opinion, for not 490 years, but scarcely 450,
elapsed from the second year of Darius Nothus to Christ’s passion, as the
authors of this opinion admit. To avoid and make up this difficulty—the
deficiency of forty or thirty-seven years—they are compelled with the Jews
to close the weeks not at Christ’s death but at Jerusalem’s destruction. How

The two remaining opinions begin the weeks with Artaxerxes Longimanus: the
fifth with his twentieth year and the sixth with his seventh. Very many stand
for the fifth: Julius Africanus, Theodoret, Chrysostom, Bede, Tornielli,
Salianus, Pererius, Ussher, and Valesius. Nor can we embrace it, for, as all
its supporters admit, it does not complete the seventy weeks, or 490 years.
From Longimanus’s twentieth year to Christ’s passion they count only 475
years, or 477 if the endpoints are included, so that thirteen are missing
from the total.

They try to resolve this difficulty with Africanus’s excessively σοφῷ,
“ingenious,” φαρμάκῳ, “remedy,” by interpreting Daniel’s years as lunar rather
than solar. They consider the lunar year eleven days shorter than the solar,
which over 490 years comes to thirteen years. But this remedy must not be
accepted, for although Holy Scripture often employs lunar months, it nowhere
uses lunar years. Without intercalation, these lunar years would in the course
of a few centuries transfer Passover from spring to autumn and Pentecost from
summer to winter, and would throw all the sacred seasonal observances into
confusion contrary to God’s express command. Certainly, if we trust Lyranus
and Galatinus, both Jews, the Jews never used lunar years. And whoever did use
lunar years always equated the lunar with the solar every second or third
year through the embolism and intercalation of one month. It was therefore
necessary for every week of solar years to coincide with a week of lunar
years.

With the other five now rejected, the sixth opinion remains. Among the
ancients it is defended—if we are to trust the learned and eloquent Jesuit
Nicolás Abram—by Basil, Eusebius, who has several opinions, and Cedrenus;
among our writers by Gualther, Bullinger, Funccius, Bucholcerus, Capellus, and
Helvicus; and among the Jesuits by Cornelius à Lapide, Sanctius, Henri
Philippe, Tirinus, and Nicolás Abram. It need not be supported by arguments
other than the single one by which we overturned all the rest: it alone fits
the course of time. Precisely seventy weeks of years, or 490 years, elapsed
from Artaxerxes’s seventh year to Christ. This is everywhere admitted by all
who place any trust in human writings and who do not wish to accuse all the
ancient Chaldaean, Greek, and Latin historians and chronologists of falsehood,
contrary also to the mind of all the Fathers of the church.

I find nothing worthy of consideration raised against it except this one
rather weighty difficulty: the weeks begin with a decree concerning the
rebuilding of Jerusalem, but we read of no decree concerning the rebuilding
of the city in Artaxerxes’s seventh year.

> Marginalia, printed p. 172: *Whether any decree concerning Jerusalem’s
> restoration was issued in Artaxerxes’s seventh year*.

Answer. The authors named give various responses here. First, although the
decree granted to Ezra in Artaxerxes’s seventh year expressly mentions the
rebuilding of the temple, it also implies the rebuilding of the city. In the
same way, although Cyrus’s decree in 2 Chronicles 36 and Ezra 1 names the
temple alone, the city must also be understood, as is perfectly clear from
Isaiah 44:28 and Ezra 4:12. Under Cyrus’s decree the Jews began to build not
only the temple but also the city’s walls. Certainly, whoever grants the
existence of a thing must also be considered to grant what follows upon its
existence. Whoever decrees the end is presumed to have decreed the means
necessary for that end. Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes, who expressly commanded
that a temple be built at Jerusalem for the worship of God, must be presumed
to have wished the city to be built as well. For unless a city—and a fortified
one at that—had surrounded the temple, the priests and the people who
worshiped God would have had neither a convenient nor secure dwelling against
their enemies. Without them the temple in that place would have been useless
and of little benefit.

Moreover, Ezra obtained from the king everything he requested. Why should the
city’s construction not have been included in his request, since the temple
had been finished long before? Nicolás Abram proves at length that the
building of Jerusalem refers to filling the city with inhabitants. It is
agreed that Ezra accomplished this outstandingly in accordance with
Artaxerxes’s decree and Daniel’s prophecy. But he wishes Nehemiah’s building
of the walls to relate not so much to the building of the city as to its
fortification, which not only cities but the very powerful commonwealths of
Sparta and Athens lacked at the height of their glory. It nevertheless seems
likely that Ezra also applied himself to constructing Jerusalem’s walls. For
he had received a very great amount of money and supplies not only for
sacrifices but for whatever additional uses he wished; he had brought a great
multitude of people with him to live in Jerusalem; and he had also obtained
authority to appoint civil magistrates in Jerusalem and throughout Judea who
could punish the guilty, in proportion to the offense, by death, exile, or
loss of property. Indeed, in his prayers to God he acknowledges that God had
given the people a wall at Jerusalem.

to be those which Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed long before, but other walls
more recently built which the enemies of the Jews had lately overthrown.

Fourth, Gerardus Mercator devised another clever invention, which the Jesuit
Petavius repeatedly impresses upon very many while concealing its author. They
claim that Artaxerxes had two beginnings to his reign: one in Xerxes’s twelfth
year, so that he reigned together with his father for ten years, and another
after his father’s death. By this calculation Artaxerxes’s twentieth and tenth
years coincide. How easy it is to add to inventions! Nicolás Abram declares
that Artaxerxes reigned together with his father Xerxes for fifteen years, as
if Xerxes, moving against Greece in his fifth year, had then appointed his son
Artaxerxes as colleague in the empire. On this reckoning Artaxerxes’s twentieth
year coincides with his fifth year of reigning alone, and

the distance between Artaxerxes’s seventh and twentieth years was the tiny
span of just two years. It could then easily be preserved by the common
distinction between inclusive and exclusive endpoints.

But I altogether reject the utterly reckless audacity of these ill-advised
men, by which Scripture’s own calculation is completely confounded. Are not
Artaxerxes’s seventh, twentieth, and thirty-second years mentioned in
Scripture part of a single sequence and counted from the same beginning? But
according to this shameless Jesuit boldness, the thirty-second will be the
twenty-second and the twentieth the tenth, while Artaxerxes’s seventh will be
his father’s eighteenth. Indeed, according to the Jesuit Abram the twentieth
will be the fifth. Hence, as this rather audacious man openly professes and
tries at length to prove, the twentieth year of Artaxerxes in which Nehemiah
was sent to Jerusalem will also truly be his fifth, preceding the seventh by
a full two years—as if Nehemiah had completed Jerusalem’s walls a full two
years before Ezra set foot outside Ecbatana.

The entire foundation of this fiction is that Thucydides and Plutarch say
that Themistocles, an exile from Athens, fled to Artaxerxes, whereas Diodorus
Siculus and others say that he fled to Xerxes. Jesuit ingenuity reconciles
this contradiction through a joint reign, as if Xerxes and Artaxerxes had
reigned in Persia at the same time. But this subtlety is very badly employed
here. Pererius and à Lapide reject it. And even if it be granted that Xerxes
reigned together with his son for some time, who nevertheless dares with
Petavius to assign ten years to this joint reign, or fifteen with Abram? What
witness can they name for this invention? Indeed, all historians reject such
a joint reign. Diodorus Siculus erred here in calculating the chronology,
against the very weighty authorities Thucydides and Plutarch, when he supposed
that Themistocles reached the father Xerxes rather than his son Artaxerxes.
But both Diodorus and the others clearly indicate that Artaxerxes never
ascended the throne while his father was alive. When Xerxes first moved
against Greece, his son Artaxerxes was either not yet born or still in the
cradle. After the Greek war Xerxes was involved in no other notable war such
that he needed to declare any colleague in the kingdom. For Pausanias’s
conspiracy against his homeland was discovered and destroyed too early for
Xerxes to have based any expedition upon it. Moreover, while Xerxes waged war
against the Greeks and from then until the end of his life, he always had at
his side his eldest son Darius, whom alone he was raising in hope of the
kingdom. At his father’s death Artaxerxes was a young man with no thought of
the throne, since he was excluded from that hope not only by Darius, the
eldest of the brothers, but also by Hystaspes, the second brother, whom their
father had already placed over the province of Bactria. But no trust in the
historians can restrain Jesuit shamelessness within the bounds of modesty and
truth.
