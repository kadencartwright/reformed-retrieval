QUESTION IX

Does Reason Have Any Judgment in Matters of Faith? Or Does It Have No Use
Whatever?

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. Two extremes must be avoided here. One is that of those who err by excess,
ascribing too much to reason and holding it as the norm of religion and faith.
The Socinians do this, and they were treated in the preceding question. The
other is that of those who err by defect. Lest they appear to establish reason
as the norm of faith, they concede little or nothing to it. This is the
position not only of the Anabaptists and Weigelians, but also of the Lutherans
and papists. They would have the testimony of reason go unheard whenever it
judges certain mysteries of faith—for example, when it refuses to admit the
dogma of transubstantiation or ubiquity because that dogma conflicts with the
light of right reason. And because we do not entirely repudiate the use of
reason, they therefore write such an accusation against us: that we establish
ourselves as the final judges and arbiters in matters of faith, and so deceive
the world under a fair pretext when we boast that we acknowledge Scripture
alone as judge.

II. The question is not about the *judgment of decision*, by which some
controversy is publicly determined. None of us attributes this to reason, but
either to God alone speaking in Scripture, if the supreme judgment is at
issue, or to pastors constituted in the church, if the subordinate judgment
is at issue. The question concerns only the *judgment of private discretion*,
by which truth is distinguished from falsehood and which we judge is rightly
attributed to illuminated reason. 2. In judgment, the subject or knowing power
must be distinguished from the norm, or the law and foundation upon which the
intellect rests so as to judge by its prescription. 3. In relation to the
object to be judged, the norm concerns either the truth of propositions or the
truth of connections. Propositions in turn concern either things known by
nature or things known only from supernatural revelation.

III. With this established, I say that the judgment of discretion in matters
of faith belongs to reason: subjectively, because it belongs to the intellect
alone to know and discern the things of faith; and normatively, with respect
to the truth of connections, in absolutely all statements, whether they are
known from nature or from revelation. With respect to the truth of
propositions, however, it belongs to reason only in those things that are
known naturally, and this with three cautions. 1. The judgment of reason must
not be held as necessary, as though theology could not exist without it. 2.
The Word of God, in which these truths too have been revealed, must always be
held as the primary norm, and reason as the secondary norm. 3. When the Word
adds to a thing known by nature something that nature itself does not know,
judgment about it must then be made not from nature or reason but from the
Word. This is not because the Word and reason conflict with each other, but
because reason is perfected by the Word. Yet in matters known only from
revelation, as in the mystery of the Trinity, the incarnation, and so forth,
the Word of God is the sole norm, beyond or apart from which we must not be
wise.

IV. The question is not whether the mysteries of faith are above reason or
whether reason can attain them. We readily grant that there are things which
far surpass the grasp not only of human beings but also of angels, and that
supernatural revelation was necessary for their knowledge. We also
acknowledge not only that reason is incapable of uncovering them unless they
are revealed, and not only that it is too weak to grasp them after they have
been revealed, but also that it is unstable and fallible, readily following a
lie in place of truth and truth in place of a lie, and that it would never
believe the Word of God and its mysteries unless the grace of the Spirit

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 28 | IA leaf 82 | PDF p. 82]

illuminated it. Rather, the question is whether reason has no use whatever and
whether we ought entirely to repudiate its testimony whenever the truth or
falsehood of some dogma must be judged. Our adversaries would have it so; we
deny it.

V. Although the blindness of the human intellect is very great, it is
nevertheless certain that some rays of natural light and certain principles
of undoubted truth still remain in it. Such principles include that the whole
is greater than its part, that an effect presupposes a cause, and that *to be*
and *not to be* are incompatible terms, and so forth. Unless this were so,
there could be no sciences or arts and nothing would be certain in the nature
of things. 2. These principles are certain not only in nature but also in
grace and in the mysteries of faith. So far is faith from destroying them
that, on the contrary, it borrows them from reason and uses them to confirm
its dogmas. 3. Although reason and faith are of different orders—the former
natural, the latter supernatural—they are nevertheless not opposed. They are
related to each other and subordinated to one another: reason is perfected by
faith, and faith presupposes reason so that it may build the mysteries of
grace upon it.

VI. Reason cannot and must not propose mysteries from its own storehouse. The
Word of God alone has this right, and if they are not sought from it, they are
to be rejected by that very fact, Galatians 1:8. 2. Reason must not be heard
whenever it complains that it cannot grasp the mysteries of faith. For how
could the infinite be grasped by the finite? Therefore, to wish to reject the
mysteries because they cannot be grasped by reason is to sin not only against
faith but also against reason, which acknowledges itself to be finite and far
inferior to those sublime mysteries. 3. Reason must not be heard whenever,
under the pretext of the mysteries of faith, it wishes to establish its own
errors by overthrowing the undoubted truth of the principles of natural
light, whether with respect to things merely natural, things merely
supernatural, or mixed things, which grace borrows from nature to transfer
them to its own use. Hence right reason must reject such fabrications as
incompatible with the undoubted principles of natural light.

[Marginal heading: The use of reason is proved.]

VII. It was seen previously that reason has a manifold use in the mysteries
of faith: for illustration, comparison, inference, and proof. This is further
proved, 1. from 1 Corinthians 10:15, where the apostle appeals to the judgment
of the Corinthians, not only the teachers but also the people. 2. From the
examples of Christ, the apostles, and the prophets, who reason everywhere in
order to teach the mysteries of faith. Nor should it be said that it is
unlawful to argue from those who are inspired by God to us who are fallible,
because the force of reasonings rests not upon the infallibility of the one
proving but upon the evidence of the matter. 3. From 1 John 4:1, where we are
commanded to test the spirits, which cannot be done except by the aid of
reason. 4. The testimony of the senses is not to be entirely rejected in
matters of faith, as will be stated later. Therefore, neither is the testimony
of reason, because sense is far inferior to reason.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

VIII. It is one thing to know the meaning of some proposition, another to know
its truth. In the former manner the gospel is regarded simply as a word; in
the latter, as the divine and infallible Word. Reason is occupied with the
former, but faith alone with the latter.

IX. It is one thing for something to be *incomprehensible*, so that it cannot
be grasped; another for it to be *incompossible*, so that it cannot be
conceived. The mysteries of the Trinity, the incarnation, and predestination
are incomprehensible, and we can have only an obscure and imperfect notion of
them. But the fabrication of transubstantiation or ubiquity cannot be
conceived because of the natural repugnance of our intellect when conceiving
a thing that is utterly impossible.

X. Corrupt reason in the concrete can conflict with theology, but sound
reason in the abstract cannot. It can be ignorant of the mysteries and fail
to teach them, but for that reason it must not immediately be judged to deny
them. You would reason badly if you concluded that a physician conflicts with
a jurist because he does not cite laws. In the same way, neither does the
philosopher conflict with the theologian, although he does not treat the
theologian's mysteries and acknowledges that they are above him.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 29 | IA leaf 83 | PDF p. 83]

XI. Things are called *human* either because they arise from carnal
corruption—and in this sense Christ opposes human things to divine things in
Matthew 16:17—or because they remain from the image of God. In the latter
sense they do not conflict but are subordinated. Neither light contradicts
light nor truth truth, because God is the author of both.

XII. Although we attribute some judgment of discretion to reason illuminated
by the Holy Spirit, we do not thereby establish ourselves as the final
arbiters and judges in cases of faith, nor do we take away from Scripture its
supreme judgment of decision. For these are subordinate, not contrary, and so
reason always judges according to Scripture as the first and infallible norm.

XIII. From the fact that the mysteries of faith surpass the grasp of reason,
it indeed follows that reason must not be employed as the principle and
foundation for showing the truth of the propositions of faith. But it does
not follow that reason cannot be employed to show the truth or falsehood of
connections in controversies of faith. For the truth of connections is
perpetual in the nature of things and can be learned even in schools outside
the church, as Augustine teaches more than once in book 2 of *On Christian
Doctrine*.

XIV. When we attribute some judgment to reason in matters of faith, we do not
mean reason blinded and corrupted by sin. In this sense we acknowledge that
the natural person cannot perceive the things of God, 1 Corinthians 2:14, and
that the mind of the flesh is enmity against God, Romans 8:7. We speak instead
of right reason healed by grace. In this sense the spiritual person is said
to judge all things, 1 Corinthians 2:15, and Paul often appeals to the
judgment of believers, 1 Corinthians 10:15 and 11:13; Hebrews 5:13–14.

XV. The taking captive of the intellect that the apostle prescribes in 2
Corinthians 10:5 does not exclude all freedom of judgment, but only the desire
to contradict when the intellect exalts itself against Christ and his gospel.
The purpose is not that reason should be unable to discern, but that it should
not dare to resist, even if the mysteries proposed surpass its grasp and it
cannot attain them. He therefore does not wish to abolish reason altogether,
because grace does not destroy nature but perfects it. He wishes only that
reason serve and minister to faith, that it be an attendant who obeys, not a
mistress who commands; that it be subjected, but not utterly cast away; that
it not found faith, but nevertheless fight for faith and embrace, defend, and
illustrate faith once founded.

XVI. It is one thing to deny or attack an article of faith because it does
not appear to agree with reason; another to attack erroneous opinions about
it and false expositions of Scripture, not because philosophical rules do not
admit them, but because they are contrary to the Word of God, which the
natural truth of connections serves.

XVII. Although we use reason and its principles in theological controversies,
it does not follow that we make a mixture of philosophy with theology and of
human things with divine things. For they are not employed as the foundation
and principle of faith from which we prove mysteries, but only as instruments
of knowledge. When we see some visible object with the bodily eye and the
light of the sun, there is no mixture of the eye with the sun, because they do
not concur in the same manner but in different manners.

XVIII. A passage from one genus to another is committed when what is proper
to one discipline is assumed to demonstrate the conclusion of another
discipline. But we by no means do this in the present matter, because the
middle term for proving a conclusion of faith is assumed not from philosophy
but from Scripture. 2. The principles or axioms assumed from reason or
philosophy to prove some article of faith are not so proper to philosophy
that they are not also judged to pertain at least to natural theology, which
must be laid as a foundation for supernatural and revealed theology;

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 30 | IA leaf 84 | PDF p. 84]

and thus they may be said to pertain to the articles of faith, if not to the
formal articles, at least to the presupposed articles.
