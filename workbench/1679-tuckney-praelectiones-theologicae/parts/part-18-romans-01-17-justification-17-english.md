There is no need to add as an appendix what some of our writers bring forward
here. In his earlier argument, throughout the first three chapters, Paul had
treated both Gentiles and Jews as enemies of faith in Christ, devoid of true
faith and guilty of sin, and had thus excluded works preceding faith or done
apart from faith. To forestall the Roman Catholic invention of justification
by works following faith, he added in chapter 4 a second argument from
Abraham. There he shows that just as neither Jews nor Gentiles can be
justified by works done without faith, neither was Abraham justified by his
works, though they were excellent and issued from his very strong and
confirmed faith.

Argument 10. Maccovius writes: “If these works were not excluded, boasting
would not be excluded. According to the adversaries, the grace by which we
are fitted for works of grace and faith is not irresistible. A man therefore
has something in which to boast concerning his good will, because it did not
resist grace. He need not attribute this to grace, since he did not receive
from grace the very fact that he did not resist grace.”

As for the adversaries' objections against our view, I believe almost all
have already been sufficiently answered in explaining our position and
arguments. Examples include the claim that Paul denies justification to
works owed a wage by merit rather than grace—which Bellarmine says fits
works preceding faith rather than following it; the question Paul asks when
he banishes works from justification, “Where is your boasting?” meaning
boasting from works performed by your own powers; and Paul's purpose in
Romans and Galatians. We have answered all these already.

They further press Galatians 2:16: “A man is not justified by works of the
law, unless through faith in Jesus Christ.” They interpret it this way:
works of the law do not justify insofar as they are of the law, but insofar
as they are done by faith, so that works receive their justifying power from
faith. Since they do not justify unless through faith, it apparently follows
that works done through and joined with faith do justify. If I said, “Faith
does not work except through love,” by denying that faith works without love
I would affirm with Paul that it works through love. Likewise this negative
contains an affirmative: “Not unless through faith; therefore works justify
through faith.” Faith is required as the condition and form of justifying
works. Justification is therefore not denied but openly attributed to works
dyed in Christ's blood and formed by faith.

I answer:

The entire force of this argument rests on an ambiguous Latin translation.
The Greek says, “A man is not justified by works of the law, but through
faith in Jesus Christ.” The Vulgate renders the Greek words *ean mē* with
“unless,” as though making an exception to what precedes: a man is not
justified by works of the law unless faith in Christ is added to those
works; when faith is added, he is justified by them. Thus *ean mē* is
understood conditionally, as indicating a condition without which works
could never justify, but with which they certainly do. Our writers,

[1679 printed p. 127 | IA leaf 135]

by contrast, contend that the particles are adversative and exclusive. They
translate, “A man is not justified by works of the law, but through faith in
Jesus Christ.” Beza, Piscator, Pareus, and others take it to mean that a man
is not justified by works at all, but, in contrast, by faith alone. We must
now determine whether our translation and interpretation or the Roman
Catholic one is truer. This will immediately appear if we consider either
the matter itself and Paul's remaining words or the proper and genuine
meaning of *ean mē*, the particles chiefly disputed here.

1. As to the matter, Paul's purpose, and his words, the force of Paul's proof,
as Piscator rightly notes in his analysis, lies in a disjunctive syllogism.
Its major premise consists of adversatively opposed parts, where denying one
asserts the other: Either we are justified by works of the law or by faith.
But we are justified by faith. Therefore we are not justified by works of
the law. The Roman Catholic interpretation makes Paul's argument proceed in
an entirely different and contrary fashion: denying one thing denies the
other, while positing one posits the other, as in a hypothetical conditional
syllogism.

They understand “unless through faith” as containing a condition and take
Paul to remove the conditioned thing by denying its condition. Thus “a man
is not justified by works of the law unless through faith in Jesus Christ”
means that faith, or the requirement that they flow from faith, is the
condition necessary for works of the law to justify. If works are not from
faith, they do not justify; from this negative follows the opposite
affirmative: provided they are from faith, they certainly justify. In this
they contradict both themselves and Paul.

1. They contradict themselves by attributing to works of the law what they
always deny elsewhere and cannot dare to affirm, since Paul so often denies
it explicitly. They may reply that neither they nor Paul say works of the
law considered in themselves justify, but only if done from faith. Yet on
their view this condition is a contradiction in terms: that works should be
works of the law and yet flow from faith. This is no contradiction in our
orthodox understanding. We readily grant that good works from faith may be
called works of the law: in respect of their originating principle they are
works of faith; insofar as the law commands them they are works of the law.
The Roman view is very different, for it makes only those deeds done through
knowledge of the law without faith and by the powers of free choice into
works of the law. On their common view, then, to say that works of the law
justify if done from faith—as they make Paul say here—is incoherent.

Estius saw this. He writes: “The word ‘unless’ makes the sentence obscure.
If taken properly as it sounds to Latin ears, it excepts something from what
precedes: a man is not justified by works of the law unless faith in Christ
is added to them, in which case he is justified by works of the law. But this
sense divides justification, attributing it partly to works of the law and
partly to faith in Christ, contrary to Paul's definite and absolute teaching.
This interpretation must therefore be rejected as contrary to Paul's
meaning and purpose.” Thus those who argue from this passage and its word
“unless” for justification by works of the law done from faith contradict

[1679 printed p. 128 | IA leaf 136]

themselves.

2. Still more do they contradict Paul.

Paul argues here not hypothetically but disjunctively. He does not assert or
remove one thing by asserting or removing another as in connected things;
by asserting faith, he removes all works, as in disjunctive opposites.
Likewise Romans 3:28 does not combine faith and works in justification, but
opposes them: “We conclude that a man is justified by faith apart from works
of the law.” It says not, as they interpret here, “works of the law with
faith,” but “faith apart from works of the law,” which is the same as faith
alone. Since Paul always opposes only faith and works in justification, when
works are removed, faith alone remains.

So also here, Paul begins the verse by saying, “Knowing that a man is not
justified by works of the law but through faith.” To make clear that this
means opposition, not condition, he immediately adds at its end: “We too
believed in Christ Jesus so that we might be justified by faith in Christ
and not by works of the law, because no flesh will be justified by works of
the law.” He does not present faith as a condition of works that justify; by
denying works he clearly asserts that faith alone justifies.

This perfectly fits the people against whom Paul disputes. They were not
Gentiles or pure Jews who denied faith in Christ, but Judaizing Christians
who held faith in Christ necessary and wished to add works of the law to it
for justification. I have said this often, and the Roman Catholic Estius
freely admits it here: “The doctrine of the Judaizers against whom Paul acts
was to mix the law with faith in Christ and expect salvation equally from
both.” He therefore adds that Scripture frequently uses “unless”
adversatively so that it means “but only.” This leads directly to our second
point:

2. The proper meaning of *ean mē*, on which the entire force of the Roman
Catholic argument depends. The question is whether these particles here
denote a condition or exception, or are instead adversative, signifying
exclusion and opposition. When Paul says, “A man is not justified by works
of the law *ean mē* through faith,” must the last words be understood
conditionally or exceptively as “unless through faith,” meaning unless those
works are done from faith, since such works justify a man? That is the Roman
Catholic sense. Or must they be taken exclusively and adversatively: “A man
is not justified by works of the law, but through faith”? That is our view.

To resolve the controversy:

1. I grant that when the particle *ean* stands by itself, like *ei*, it marks
a hypothetical proposition and thus signifies a condition. This is familiar
everywhere.

[1679 printed p. 129 | IA leaf 137]

2. When *mē* is added, *ean mē*, like *ei mē*, has various uses in secular
writers, the Septuagint, and the New Testament. Sometimes it has affirming
or asseverating force, as in Mark 10:29–30: “There is no one who has left
house, brothers, sisters,” and so forth, “but will most certainly receive a
hundredfold.” It may be rendered “indeed,” “but,” or “not,” though I admit it
is most often rendered “unless.” To determine its proper force one must
especially compare the passages where it occurs, observe whether it stands
at the beginning or end of a sentence, and whether it is joined with
preceding or following affirmatives or negatives. A fuller treatment belongs
to grammarians. For our purpose:

1. I first grant that it is often conditional in the New Testament,
including a negative condition in its meaning, and should then be translated
“if not” or, most often, “unless.”

2. Since a negative condition includes an exception, it is used exceptively
in Luke 13:3, 5 and Revelation 2:5, 22: “unless they repent.” Our translators
render it “except ye repent,” “except thou repent,” and so forth.

3. Yet it is also used exclusively and adversatively, as in Matthew 26:42:
“If this cup cannot pass from me, but on the contrary must be drunk.”

I grant that our writers bring forward several other passages to prove this:
Galatians 1:7, Matthew 12:4, 1 Corinthians 7:17, Revelation 9:4 and 21:27,
and John 17:12: “None of them perished but the son of perdition.” Among our
writers Cameron, and among the Roman Catholics Lucas Brugensis, teach that
the last is adversative rather than exceptive. Yet one must not conceal that
these passages do not contain precisely the same particles as Galatians.
Here we read *ean mē*, but in all those passages *ei mē*. The adversaries
cannot therefore accuse our writers of bad faith for offering *ei mē* where
the question concerns *ean mē*, as though deceiving both themselves and
their hearers or readers. For:

1. Greek grammarians treat *ei* and *ean* alike.

2. Both their writers and ours repeatedly translate *ean mē* and *ei mē*
with the same Latin word, *nisi*, taking them as equivalent.

3. Not only our writers but Roman Catholics such as Titelmann, Adam Sasbout,
and Estius use *ean mē* adversatively here. So do Augustine and Jerome. They
retain “unless” in translation but explain it adversatively as “but.”
Augustine explains “unless through faith in Jesus Christ” as “not by the law
of works, but by the law of faith.” Jerome, or whoever authored the
commentaries attributed to him, says: “They say that if this is so, no one is
justified by works of the law, but by faith in Jesus Christ.” Even Grotius,
though he customarily attributes too much to works of faith in justification,
takes *ean mē* here for *alla*, “but,” and recognizes the same adversative use
elsewhere. Erasmus likewise says that, though this is a new form of speech,
ordinary usage requires, “not justified by works,

[1679 printed p. 130 | IA leaf 138]

but through faith.” I scarcely understand why he calls it a new form.
Cameron says on John 17:12 that this does not appear strange to those who
understand the language's idiom, since Syriac *ella* is translated into
Greek as *ei mē*, usually adversatively rather than always exceptively.

Beza says that the Hebrew particles *'im lo'* and *ki 'im* are rendered by
the Septuagint with adversative *alla* in Isaiah 59:2, 2 Kings 5:17, and
Genesis 24:38, and with *ean mē* in Genesis 22:16; hence Paul wrote *ean mē*
for *alla*. I find this done with *ki 'im* in the passages he cites. Yet *ki
'im* is too remote from our present matter. *'Im lo'* is closer, but it does
not occur in the Hebrew text, nor *ean mē* in the Septuagint, at the cited
place.

Piscator's account is more fitting: the Syriac *ella* arose from Hebrew *'im
lo'*, and Greek *alla* from Syriac *ella*. Thus, since the Syriac interpreter
has *ella* while the Greek text has *ean mē*, this supports explaining *ean
mē* by Syriac *ella* or Greek *alla*, adversatively. Both our writers and the
Roman Catholics sufficiently show that Latin *nisi* can have this sense.
Cicero says, “I can judge nothing concerning the matter, *nisi*—that is,
but—I am certainly persuaded that a man such as you has done nothing
rashly.” Terence likewise says, “I do not know, except that I know the gods
were sufficiently angry with me,” and so forth.

Piscator sees here an exegetical ellipsis of “that is,” so that the full
statement would be, “Knowing that a man is not justified by works of the
law—that is, he is not justified unless through faith.” Yet, by his own
admission, no ellipsis is needed if the Greek *ean mē* stands for Hebrew *'im
lo'*, or, as Cornelius a Lapide prefers, the Hebrew particle *'ak* signifies
opposition or antithesis. Erasmus calls this a new form of speech, and Adam
Sasbout even says he is compelled to say that Paul speaks neither Latin nor
Greek here but contrary to all grammar, unless perhaps it is a Hebrew idiom.
Yet what has been said shows that Paul speaks quite fittingly in Hebrew,
Greek, and Latin idiom, not in the exceptive sense the Roman Catholics desire,
but in a thoroughly exclusive or adversative sense.

Cornelius a Lapide himself admits this and says the antithesis makes it
plain. Paul wishes justification to be attributed not to works of the law
but to faith in Christ, and therefore “unless” is adversative, equivalent to
“but.” This much is correct, though I scarcely understand what immediately
follows. He writes: “It would be otherwise if stated simply without an
antithesis, as when I say, ‘A man does not live unless through the soul.’
Here ‘unless’ is equivalent to these two statements: ‘A man lives through
the soul,’ and ‘a man lives through nothing other than the soul.’ Hence the
innovators wrongly explain ‘unless through faith in Christ’ as ‘but only
through faith in Christ.’” On the contrary, our writers, whom this old hand
calls innovators, explain it not badly but well—indeed, precisely because of
this example, most excellently—as “but only through faith in Christ.” This
subtle weigher of words errs twice.

[1679 printed p. 131 | IA leaf 139]

1. He believes that “a man does not live unless through the soul” contains
no antithesis, but it truly does. Although the excluded things are not
individually expressed, they are stated generally or at least understood,
since, by his own admission, the sense is that man lives through the soul
alone and nothing else.

2. He says that where the antithesis is explicit, as in “a man is not
justified by works, unless through faith in Christ,” *nisi* is equivalent to
“but” but not to “only” or “alone,” unlike where no antithesis is expressed,
as in his former example. On the contrary, especially when an antithesis is
expressed and all excluded things are named and denied, *nisi* means both
“but” and “only.” Here “a man is not justified by works of the law, unless
through faith in Christ” means “but through faith,” and indeed “only through
faith alone,” since the antithesis rejects works that could otherwise appear
most conducive to justification, and with them all other works.

We therefore need not fear to affirm no less boldly than truly and safely
with Hyperius that “unless by faith,” “only by faith,” and “by faith alone”
are equivalent. As he rightly observes, Origen, Hilary, Ambrose, Chrysostom,
and Theophylact use these formulas when they contend against heretics.
Among the Roman Catholics Titelmann does not hesitate to say, “No one can be
justified before God by works of the law, but only through faith in Christ.”
He does not shrink from “only,” just as Adam Sasbout admits that neither the
ancients nor Thomas himself shrank from “alone.”

Let us conclude the whole matter with Calvin's very clear words on this
passage: “When Paul says that no one is justified by works of the law unless
through faith in Christ, he does not mean merely that works are insufficient
unless faith's aid is added. He opposes an exclusive to the negative
proposition, as though saying, ‘not by works, but by faith alone.’ Otherwise
his statement would be inept and foreign to his purpose. The false apostles
did not reject Christ or faith, but demanded that ceremonies be joined to
them. Had Paul accepted such a conjunction, there would have been agreement
between them. Paul would therefore have troubled the church in vain with so
odious a controversy.”

We have at last resolved this passage and the chief Roman Catholic argument
drawn from it. If we have lingered over it, you will easily pardon us if you
consider seriously how much effort the adversaries have expended upon it and
how important it is to explaining the whole doctrine of justification under
discussion.
