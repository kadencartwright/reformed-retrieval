## A Plain and Perspicuous Treatise on the Lord's Supper

In which Joachim Westphal's calumnies are refuted.

### In reply to chapter one: On the term “sacrament” and sacramental speech

I omit what you say by way of preface about Carlstadt and also about
Oecolampadius, of blessed memory, Westphal, since those things concern another
subject. The matter under dispute is as follows. Whenever these words of the
Lord, “THIS IS MY BODY,” are brought against us and the proper sense of the
verb “IS” is pressed, we customarily offer—not this one consideration alone,
but this one especially—which we think no one can deny: words must be
explained according to their subject matter. Next, because these words are
spoken about the Sacraments, we infer that the body is predicated of the bread
only in the way that the nature of Sacraments permits. Finally, we affirm that
the nature of Sacraments is such that they are signs of the things which they
were instituted to signify and to place before our outward senses. They are by
no means empty signs, I say, such as artists commonly represent, but effective
to this extent: our seeing them with our eyes, touching them with our hand,
taking them with our mouth, and swallowing them is no more true and certain
than it is true and certain that God offers us whatever they figure—namely,
the Lord's true body and true blood. We will treat this more fully below.

From these things, then, we say that the body is predicated of the bread only
as a Sacrament is predicated sacramentally of the thing signified—that is,
truly indeed, yet signified by an outward sign and symbol. Thus, by metonymy,
the name of the thing signified is assigned to the sign when the bread is
called the body.

What, then, do you find fault with in this argument? First you cry out that we
take refuge in a little word, and an obscure and ambiguous one at that. But why
is it ambiguous? “Because,” you say, “the term *Sacrament* means one thing in
Latin writers and another in theologians.” But if that argument is valid, why
do you not also abstain from the very term *Faith*? Let us therefore hear your
second argument. “Among theologians,” you say, “it is used ambiguously.” Is
that so? But what of it, I ask? In how many senses are the names *spirit* and
*flesh* taken in Holy Scripture? Yet who has rejected them as ambiguous for
that reason? But let us hear how you proceed. “At one time,” you say, “it is
taken for a mystery, at another for a sign.” Granted. Why, then, may we not use
what all the ancients customarily used? Indeed, if this term is so ambiguous,
why did you not reject it long ago? But let us hear where all this is going.

If we take the name *Sacrament* for a mystery, you not only acknowledge that
the expression is sacramental—that is, mystical—but think that by this line of
reasoning you have also caught us in your snares. “For,” you say, “faith
perceives the mysteries of God. The Lord's Supper is a mystery. Therefore, it
is understood by faith alone. Likewise, the mysteries of the Lord's Supper
must not be measured by philosophy or judged by human reason. That Christ's
body is contained in one place only and cannot be in several places is
philosophy or human wisdom. Therefore, Christ's body's presence in the Holy
Supper must not be judged from this.” As if the Lord's Supper could be called
a mystery in no respect except the one you defend! We acknowledge that
whatever is truly and effectually figured for us here through outward signs is
a mystery—that is, a secret—and indeed incomprehensible to human senses. For
what sense will comprehend that infinite wisdom

[PDF page 3; printed page 212]

and goodness of God which shines forth in our redemption and is, in a manner,
placed before our eyes in this action? Who will conceive in any way other than
by faith that closest union of the members on earth with the head in heaven?
But that the Lord's body cannot be in several places at once—when the Lord
himself proclaims it, the Apostles testify to it, and the whole ancient Church,
having condemned Marcion, Eutyches, and their like, declared it by perpetual
consent—even if human reason does not disagree, is certainly not human
philosophy but divine wisdom. Far from preventing the Lord's Supper from being
a mystery, this wholly confirms it. For if the Lord's body and blood are in
many places at once, what wonder is it that they are perceived by any who have
hands and a mouth? But if his body is in one place, and nevertheless, because
he promised it, the distance between places does not prevent him from truly
imparting himself to his members so as to infuse eternal life into them, is
this not truly an immeasurable mystery or secret, comprehensible by faith
alone? You may see, then, which opinion profanes the mystery of the Lord's
Supper.

Let us come to the other meaning of *Sacrament*, which you make different from
the preceding one, though it is in fact one and the same—except for this one
point, which you seem not to have understood: *mysterion* more often denotes
the entire action, while the name *Sacrament* generally denotes only the sacred
symbols employed in the action, such as the bread and wine in the Lord's
Supper, or even the ceremonies themselves. Thus we might call the breaking of
the bread a sacrament of the sufferings the Lord endured. But come, let us
excuse your ignorance of this distinction and consider the matter itself.

You acknowledge that circumcision is called a sign in the Scriptures but deny
this of the Lord's Supper. What can you prove from that unless you first show
that circumcision and the Lord's Supper do not agree in genus? Yet soon
afterward you concede “that the Fathers assign the names *Sacrament* and
*Sign* to the Lord's Supper, but,” you say, “in such a manner that they leave
no room for tropes, symbols, and signs.” You mean that it is indeed a sign, but
a sign of a present rather than an absent body. Who will grant you this? For
if the Lord's body and blood are present with, under, or in the bread and wine
in the way you maintain—we do not deny that they are present to us in a
certain respect, as will be explained in its place—what need was there for a
sign? Was it to remove the horror of fleshly eating? Theophylact indeed says
this, but Augustine speaks very differently when he writes that the saying,
“Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man,” is figurative. “For otherwise,”
he says, “he would appear to command a crime.” Likewise: “You are not going to
eat this body which you see, nor drink the blood which those men will pour
out. What I tell you is a mystery; if you understand it spiritually, it will
give life.” Elsewhere he likewise says that Christ's giving us his flesh to
eat and his blood to drink must be understood figuratively, because eating
flesh appears a graver crime than killing and drinking blood than shedding it.
[Marginal references: Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* III.16; *Tractates on
John* 26; *Against Adimantus* 12.]

Even if we granted you that the bread is the sign of the Lord's present body
in the sense in which you contend this is true, could it not still be proved
by these words that the body is predicated of the bread only as a present but
signified thing is predicated of its sign? Why, then, do you inveigh so
vehemently against the interpretation, “This,” or “this bread,” “is the body,”
that is, truly signifies the body? Why do you not instead require us to add
that it signifies a present and not an absent body? We would readily concede
even that, if we agreed about the manner of the presence.

Whichever way you turn, therefore—whether we take *Sacrament* for a mystery,
*sacramentally* for *mystically*, or *Sacrament* for a sign—it will be evident
that this foundation is sound: statements about the conjunction of symbols
and things must be taken sacramentally, that is, truly indeed, but also
figuratively and significatively. We therefore rely not on a single new and
ambiguous little word, but on the truth itself.

One part of this chapter remains. You concede, Westphal, what Calvin writes
about the analogy of the Sacraments—that one must rise from the outward and
earthly sign to the heavenly thing—provided that by the heavenly thing we mean
Christ's body and not merely its power. If this is so, what are you disputing?
How often Calvin insists that Christ's gifts flow to us only if Christ himself
is first united to us and we to him by faith! Or if you do not believe Calvin,
why will you not at least believe these words of Peter Martyr? In his treatment
of 1 Corinthians 11 he writes: “If you ask what we obtain through communion,
some have said the merits and fruits of the Lord's death. This does not
displease me; but I add that we also possess the Lord himself, who is the
source of these good things. For we do not doubt that he is present to us with
respect to his divinity. Although, with respect to their corporeal and natural
condition, his body and blood are in heaven and contained in their own place,
nevertheless by spiritual power and

[PDF page 4; printed page 213]

operation—that is, by faith and the mind—they are comprehended; and in this
way they can be said truly to be with us, since our faith is not directed to
fictitious and lying things.” What more, then, do you require of us? Namely,
that we confess the body and blood to be in, with, or under the bread, although
we wish to seek them nowhere but in heaven by faith. Why do you press this?
Because you do not believe that the Lord's body can truly be given to us in
addition to the sign in any other way.

### In reply to chapter two: On the foundation of faith in the Eucharistic controversy

Throughout the chapter, Westphal, you insist that the word of the Lord must be
*autopiston*—self-authenticating—among all Christians. As if we subjected the
word of the Lord to our understanding, rather than, on the contrary,
subjecting all our understanding to God's word alone! But happily this calumny
is refuted both by innumerable books and by the facts themselves. Our churches
would certainly be badly served if we had learned that everything must be
ascribed to God's word from nowhere but those badly sewn patchworks. In the
other articles of the Christian faith I wonder whether you would dare accuse
us of such unbelief. In this very controversy, however, your dishonesty in
complaining that we do not ascribe as much as we should to the Lord's word is
partly evident from what we said just above, and will be much clearer still
from what we will bring forward in the proper places.

### In reply to chapter three: On the trope in the words of the Lord's Supper

We prove by arguments which, in our judgment, are sound and consonant with
God's word that the bread is truly called the Lord's body, but nevertheless
not without a trope. In this third chapter you have undertaken to refute some
of these arguments.

We customarily cite passages of Scripture—most of them concerning a similar
subject—in which the same use of a trope is shown, lest we appear to introduce
some new manner of interpretation into God's Church. You, however, either
because you do not understand the kind of arguments we employ, or because you
think you are dealing with blocks of wood, assign us this argument: “Scripture
is filled with many tropes. Therefore, the words of Christ's testament are not
free of a trope.” Where in our writings did you ever find this form of
argument? You also oppose another reason: no Evangelist mentions the trope
which we posit. I answer that tropes are not invariably explained, especially
when they are of such a kind that, unless the expression is taken tropically,
something must follow which is either utterly absurd in its own nature or
contrary to the other testimonies of Scripture. Such is the statement we are
discussing. To attribute to Christ an invisible body, or one which can be in
many places simultaneously—as must certainly be done unless these words are
explained tropically—we say is contrary both to the nature of a true body and
to God's express word.

We also say that in the other symbol, namely the wine, two tropes occur, only
one of which some of the Evangelists have explained. We affirm that the cup
cannot be called either the testament in the blood or the blood itself without
a trope. It follows that tropes must not immediately be rejected in explaining
the sacramental matter. What do you answer, Westphal? You deny that there is a
metonymy in the words, “The cup is the New Testament in the blood”; instead,
you say that the container together with its contents—that is, the cup
together with the blood—is truly called the testament because of the present
blood. These are your words. So until this day we were unaware that the cup
itself also belongs to the covenant! We had believed that the attributes of
both testament and blood pertained only to what is contained, namely the wine.
Even if we granted your demand that we should also deify the cup because of
the present blood, nevertheless, since in the expression, “This cup is the New
Testament in my blood,” what you testify belongs to both container and
contents is attributed to the cup alone, will you not admit at least here the
trope of synecdoche?

Next, if we substitute the contents for the container and say, “This wine is
the New Testament in my blood,” how will you again untie this knot without a
trope? We do not doubt that by employing the symbol of wine the Lord most
truly sanctions that new covenant with us. But we ask whether, without any
trope, the wine can be called that covenant or testament which is sanctioned
with us by Christ's blood, since not even the blood itself can properly be
called the covenant, but rather that through whose shedding the Lord struck
with us the very covenant which, when received by faith, is sanctioned in us
through that blood. It remains, therefore, that in these words of the Lord not
only is the container put by synecdoche for container and contents together,
but the container is also put for its contents—that is, the cup for the wine.
This trope is another kind of metonymy, different from that in which the name
of the thing signified is assigned to the sign, as happens when the other
Evangelists say that the wine is the true

[PDF page 5; printed page 214]

blood of the new covenant, in precisely the same way that they testify that
the bread is the body. Why the Lord preferred to speak tropically rather than
use a simple expression we will explain in its place.

Finally, you demand that we prove from the Evangelists or Paul that the
container is taken for its contents when the cup is called the blood. I, in
turn, demand that you teach us these three things: how the cup can be called
the blood without any trope; how the wine can simultaneously be wine and
blood without any trope; and, finally, how these two propositions can be
equivalent without a trope: “This wine is the Lord's blood,” and, “My blood is
with, in, or under this wine.”

Our second argument is that even those who will admit no trope here
unwittingly resort to one when they interpret the words, “This is my body,” as
“Christ's body is given in, under, or with this bread.” But first of all you
deny that you summon any help from a trope here. “Rather,” you say, “we explain
that statement of Christ by certain formulas of speech of our own.” I ask you,
reader, what is evasion and tergiversation in the face of manifest truth if
this is not? What can those formulas “in,” “with,” or “under the bread” mean if
the bread is the body without any trope, and if this saying of Christ is so
plain and perspicuous that it requires no interpretation? You want permission
to employ your formulas. By what right, then, are we not permitted the same,
and to appeal, so to speak, from one dogma to another?

Once this is granted—and at last you are indirectly compelled to grant it to
us when you say that you readily allow us to extricate ourselves by the help
of a trope, provided it agrees with Christ's words and meaning; and shortly
afterward, when you say that those whom you call your leaders opposed only
invented tropes—what purpose was served by branding us as tropists,
symbolists, figurists, and other such monstrous names, and by raising those
empty outcries? You should rather have devoted yourself to showing that the
trope of synecdoche, which you are altogether compelled to posit, is better
suited to interpreting this saying than the trope of metonymy. Let us see how
you prove this.

You cry out that the second trope, by which the name of the thing signified is
assigned to the sign, is strange and invented. Yet if we consider the common
manner of speech, not even pagan authors ordinarily abstain from this sort of
metonymy when speaking about their ceremonial signs. Thus Homer, in book three
of the *Iliad*, describing the rite of sanctioning a covenant, says: “But the
noble heralds gathered the gods' faithful pledges”—that is, “the sacrificial
victims,” Eustathius says. We therefore hear the victims themselves called
“covenants” or “oaths” according to common usage, because they were employed
in sanctioning covenants. The reason is not obscure: curses were invoked so
that the blood of whoever violated the oath would be poured out in the same
way. Moreover, the mingling of the victims and the blood signified the future
concord of their minds.

And what of Virgil's words, “Those who followed impious arms and did not fear
to betray their masters' right hands”? Does he not understand fidelity and an
oath by the term *right hands*, because people customarily extend their right
hands to one another? But already I seem to hear you cry out that I am
sacrilegious for daring to compare such profane things with holy things. My
purpose is not to equate things so unequal, but to show that tropes of this
kind are by no means strange in rites and ceremonies in which things that
cannot themselves be seen are customarily placed before the eyes and deeply
impressed on minds by means of certain coverings and figures. Nor can these
examples be deemed irrelevant, since there is no doubt that the Lord very
often accommodated himself to common usage, even when explaining religion's
most hidden mysteries.

How often have we shown that many metonymies of this kind occur even in the
Prophets themselves? Circumcision is called the covenant, the lamb the
Passover, and the rock Christ. I admit that you labor to explain these without
a trope, but let others judge how aptly and suitably you do so. Will you dare
deny that Tertullian uses the word *figure* in explaining this saying of
Christ, Augustine the word *sign*, and Ambrose the verb *signify*? “But,” you
will say, “those men nevertheless do not remove the body's presence.” We will
soon consider this. For now it is enough for us to have established that the
formulas they use are neither strange nor empty. [Marginal references: Genesis
17; Exodus 12; 1 Corinthians 10; Tertullian, *Against Marcion* IV; Augustine,
*Against Adimantus* 12; Ambrose, *On the Sacraments* IV.]

You add that the trope we use is invented and contrary to the Lord's words.
Tell me why. Because, you say, it follows from it that the true body and true
blood are not present, given, and received in the Holy Supper. What, then, if
we were to offer this interpretation of yours: “THIS IS MY BODY,” that is,
“This bread truly signifies my present body”?

[PDF page 6; printed page 215]

or, “This bread is the true symbol of my present body”—would you reject that
interpretation? I think not; and yet this interpretation too depends upon the
trope of metonymy. You therefore ought not to have condemned this trope
without qualification, since it explains your view much more openly and
simply than either retaining the literal wording or positing a synecdoche—the
latter of which you deny in words but are compelled in fact to posit when
explaining the particle “THIS.” But let us come to the matter itself, lest you
complain that we are seeking evasions.

We do not simply deny that the Lord's true body and true blood—indeed, the Lord
himself—are truly present, given, and received in the Lord's Supper. We deny
only the particular manner in which our adversaries say they are present,
given, and received. They maintain that an invisible body is present, which
they also deny is circumscribed in heaven as in a place; and they maintain that
it is handed over and received by the hands with, in, or under the bread. Why
do they do so? Because they think that otherwise we cannot become partakers of
Christ in the Lord's Supper unless such a manner of presence is posited. We,
however, say that these things conflict with the truth of Christ's body and the
plainest testimonies of Scripture. Nevertheless, we confess that Christ, as
true man remaining in heaven and destined to remain there until the
consummation of the age, in accordance with his Word and by his inexplicable
power (to which all things he wills are possible), spiritually yet no less
truly presents and offers himself with all his gifts to be received and
enjoyed through faith, just as it is true that the minister presents us with
bread and wine. Therefore, so far as reception and communication are
concerned—that is, insofar as he who is in heaven can truly be communicated
with all his gifts to us who believe on earth—to that extent, I say, we do not
deny that Christ's body and blood are present, received, and given. I am not
the first to say this, since the *Consensus* openly testifies to it, and Calvin
expressed it in countless passages both before and after the *Consensus*.

Consider at last, therefore, by what right you attack the tropes which you
invidiously call “ours” as invented and contrary to God's Word, and place us
among heretics and *Schwärmer*. Consider this, I say, and think what answer you
will give before the angels and the whole assembly of the faithful for this
calumny and for stirring up those disastrous tragedies once again.

Our third argument (for this is how you chose to arrange our arguments, and I
am willing to oblige you even in this) is as follows: the literal wording in
these words of Christ, “THIS,” that is, “this bread,” “IS MY BODY,” cannot be
pressed without establishing the papal transubstantiation. For if the verb
“IS” is always taken properly and in only one manner—that is, substantially—
then these two propositions must be true in the same manner: “Christ is the
Son of God,” and “The bread is Christ's body.” But it would be exceedingly
absurd to say that the visible bread is Christ's invisible body.

How do you answer this, Westphal? First, in order to testify to your piety
before the world, you make sport of the martyrs who daily suffer the most cruel
and ignominious death among the French and other nations. Some confessions of
theirs remain which do not satisfy you. But even if they do not satisfy you,
did they therefore deserve your insults even after death? They certainly
entered the flames for Christ's name—flames which, I seriously doubt, you would
be willing to touch with even one finger. Suppose they understood the matter
of the Lord's Supper only in part—let us grant Westphal this, since not even we
approve every one of their words and deeds. Were they therefore not sacrifices
pleasing to God, when to their final breath they cursed every form of idolatry
and by faith embraced Christ as the true Son of God and our sole propitiation?
Otherwise, by what right will you number Cyprian—to name just this one man from
all antiquity—among the martyrs, since he neither thought truly nor rightly
about Baptism when he judged that those baptized by heretics must be baptized
again? Indeed, whom could we number among the martyrs except with a doubtful
and uncertain opinion?

The most illustrious princes of Germany and all its most learned theologians
judged very differently about those holy martyrs. After reading the confession
of the French churches, although they found things in it wanting, they were so
far from approving your judgment that they even considered the martyrs worthy
of their intercession with the king by honorable embassies sent more than
once. At that last judgment the Lord will doubtless acknowledge this piety of
theirs as rendered to his own members, and a grateful posterity will proclaim
it. But let this suffice as a digression.

Let us come to the matter and see how you answer. “Faith fixed upon Christ's
utterance,” you say, “frees us from fear of so great a danger; it believes that
both bread and wine, and Christ's body and blood together with them, are
given.” I answer that where there is no word, there is no faith. Now we do read
in the Lord's word that we must become one with Christ, and this too we believe.

[PDF page 7; printed page 216]

We read and believe that Christ, insofar as he is man, was taken up into heaven,
where he is to remain until he comes to judge the world. We read that the bread
of Christ which we break is a communion in Christ's body: that is, when we
receive that very bread in faith according to Christ's ordinance, we truly
become partakers of his body. We believe this too, because we do not doubt that
he truly performs what he promises. If the Lord had signified in even the
briefest words that Christ's body is present, given, and received in, under, or
together with the bread, we would certainly believe it. But Westphal will never
prove that these two statements are expressed in the same way, if the literal
wording is considered: “This,” that is, “This bread is my body,” and “My body is
with, in, or under this bread.” [Marginal references: Acts 1:11; 1 Corinthians
10:16.]

At last, Westphal—not in order to answer an argument from which you will never
extricate yourself, but to divert your readers' minds elsewhere—you cry out
that we do you and the men of your party a great injustice by burdening you
with the claim that you think the bread is naturally the body in its own
substance. But who among us has ever charged anyone but the papists with this?
We have often said what I now repeat: in fact, the literal wording of Christ's
words, “This is my body,” cannot be retained without establishing the papal
transubstantiation. Yet we have always added that those who join the body with
the bread, even if they cry out that they admit no trope, in fact posit a
synecdoche in the pronoun “THIS.” Have we not thereby distinguished this
opinion plainly enough from the papal transubstantiation? But if we say that
you agree with the papists in the very manner of communication, by what right
do you complain that an injustice is done you, when you openly summon Nicholas
and Gregory VII as your patrons and regard their councils as oracles?

What you add next is flour from the same sack. You say that the papists,
offended by the presence of different substances, devised the transformation
and abolition of one substance, while we, stumbling against the same stone,
banish the presence of the other substance far away. Thus they remove the
substance of bread and wine, while we remove the substance of flesh and blood;
you, however, by confessing the presence of flesh and blood, wish to incline to
neither side. The result will be that you maintain the middle position, while
the papists hold one extreme and we the other. But first of all you should have
learned that in very many matters those who hold the middle are not blessed,
but only less wretched. The Pelagians ascribe everything to nature, whose
corruption they do not acknowledge. We ascribe everything to grace. The
papists credit salvation partly to nature and partly to grace. What sane person
approves that middle course? Stop boasting of your middle position, then.

But to come to the matter: your claim that the papists devised their
transubstantiation because they were offended by the presence of two substances
is false and foolish. Who, in order to avoid one absurdity, would knowingly and
deliberately establish another that was still more absurd? This, then, was not
the reason transubstantiation was devised. Its causes were partly the ignorance
of those who, not knowing Christ's office or why the Sacraments were instituted,
pressed the literal wording both in Christ's words and in most of the ancients,
who followed Christ's footsteps and rightly thought that they themselves should
speak no differently about a matter which was not controversial among them;
and partly the perverse religion of unlearned people, who, being astounded by
the signs themselves, thought they could never adorn them sufficiently. I pass
over other causes: namely, God's righteous judgment and that Hildebrand's
satanic audacity. So much for the papists.

In the other extreme, however, you speak no more truly when you say that we
remove the substance of the body and blood from the Supper. Of what do we say
the bread and wine are symbols—true symbols, indeed, and full of efficacy? Of
that true body crucified for us and that true blood shed for us. What do we say
the Lord offers us in the Supper? Those very things. What do we teach is truly
received through faith in the Supper? Those very things, certainly, unto
eternal life. For we do not think that we can become partakers of Christ's
gifts and power unless we first possess Christ himself. We say that the
symbols are given on earth by ministers, while through faith, borne upward by
the Sacraments as by ladders, we rise into heaven. There at last we seek
Christ's flesh, so that by the Spirit's power we may be united to our head. We
believe this truly happens in us; and because we believe and experience it, we
therefore profess it. But you think anyone who does not couple Christ's
substance with the bread—invisibly indeed, but nevertheless really—removes that
substance from the Supper. Though you insist on this a thousand times, we will
deny it ten thousand times before God and men.

But see, at last you engage us at close quarters, and acknowledge that these
two propositions, “Christ is the Son of Man,” and “The bread is Christ's body,”
do not have the same force. Yet you deny that there is a trope in the verb
“IS.” “For,” you say, “Christ is the Son

[PDF page 8; printed page 217]

of God because of the union of the two natures; but the bread is called the
body because it is united sacramentally, yet truly, in such a way that the
thing truly present is distributed with the sign to those who receive it.” If
this is true, who does not see that in explaining the first proposition you
plainly posit the trope theologians call the *communication of attributes*?
Because of the union, what belongs to only one nature is attributed to the
whole. In interpreting the second proposition, however, you posit a
synecdoche, since what does not belong to one part is attributed to it only in
view of another, unexpressed part. For if you say, “This bread is my body,” the
proposition cannot be true unless either the papal transubstantiation follows
the utterance of the words at that very moment (which God forbid we should
profess), or the words are understood metonymically, as we affirm, or we say
that “THIS,” that is, “THIS BREAD,” is put by synecdoche for “THIS BREAD WITH
THIS BODY,” so that afterward, because of the union of the bread with the body,
the bread too is called the body. Thus either transubstantiation or a trope
must be posited.

But from where will you at last elicit that conjunction of body and bread,
when the truth of Christ's body, the article concerning the Lord's ascension
into heaven, and his remaining in heaven plainly oppose it? Even if that
conjunction is removed, the Apostle's saying nonetheless remains true and
firm: “The bread which we break is a communion in the Lord's body, and the cup
which we bless is a communion in the Lord's blood.” [Marginal reference: 1
Corinthians 10:16.]

Besides this, you did not recognize the following distinction between those
propositions. In “Christ is the Son of Man,” the name *Christ* embraces both
natures; but in the other, “The bread is the body,” the bread is not the name
of the whole, as you interpret it, but only of one part. Where, then, is the
similarity you imagine when you say that in these two propositions one nature
is predicated of the other because the different natures are united? Your
statement would have force only if you had instead compared these
propositions—as you interpret the latter: “God redeemed the Church with his
blood,” and “The bread is Christ's body.” We have already explained clearly
enough how far we are from denying that the body is joined sacramentally with
the bread—indeed, how firmly we insist on it.

Against what you call our fourth and fifth arguments you heap up all those
countless absurdities which we show necessarily follow if the body is coupled
with the bread. Yet you address none of them. You answer in a single phrase
that we ascribe everything to reason and seek praise for ingenuity and wisdom,
whereas human reason ought to yield to theology. As if God's own Word so
conflicted with the order of nature, whose author is God, that they could never
agree! It troubles you that we argue about Christ's natural body from the laws
of nature. But what fair judge will deem it absurd that Christ's body is still
subject to the laws of human nature—I say of nature, not of infirmity—when he
took a natural body not from necessity but voluntarily, and still retains it
as true and natural even though glorified?

Certainly, those absurdities which not even you can deny necessarily follow
from your antecedent premises are not drawn simply from human judgment, as you
falsely charge us. By God's grace we too have learned to submit our reason to
God's judgment even in this controversy, since we confess that the distance
between places does not prevent the members on earth from being truly united
in the Lord's Supper with their head in heaven. Those absurdities, I say, we
draw not simply from human judgment but from God's Word. Because that Word
asserts the truth of Christ's body even when glorified, it also confirms our
judgment in determining those absurdities.

### In reply to chapter four: On the demonstrative word “THIS”

You begin this chapter as follows: “In his commentary on the First Epistle to
the Corinthians, Calvin puts it beyond controversy that by the pronoun ‘THIS’
Christ indicated the bread.” Why, then, do you dispute this matter with us,
Westphal? Not only the passage you cite but also Calvin's final defense
abundantly declares that Calvin and our churches never thought, taught, or
wrote otherwise. “Nevertheless,” you say, “he still defends the opinion of John
à Lasco, who asserted that what was indicated was not the bread but the whole
action of the Supper.” Let Calvin's own book determine how truthfully you say
this. Dr. à Lasco is certainly a man who has no need of our defense; nor, even
if he had spoken insufficiently clearly in explaining this matter, would
Calvin be obliged to answer for it. But why, I ask, do you pass over the point
that most concerned you? If the particle “THIS” declares nothing other than
“THIS BREAD,” how will the predicate “Christ's body” be attributed to the
subject “bread” without any trope, whether the body is present or absent? It is
truly remarkable if a sacramental conjunction of bread and body such as you
have invented can accomplish what the hypostatic union cannot—

namely, make many propositions true without a trope, propositions which they
call the *communication of attributes*.

### In reply to chapter five: On the trope in the verb “IS”

Nearly the entire controversy depends on the correct explanation of this verb
“IS.” You say that it must be taken properly, that is, substantially. We,
however, affirm that the bread is said metonymically to be what it truly figures
for us by the Lord's institution. We add that this metonymy has great force,
for it declares how true that sacramental signification is. No one should
think that we posit an empty, merely painted image of Christ and his passion.
On the contrary, it establishes that these symbols are no more truly handed
to us by the ministers than it is true that Christ offers to us, though we are
on earth, his true body and true blood, which are and remain in heaven—that is,
offers himself with all his gifts to be communicated and enjoyed through
faith unto eternal life.

You nevertheless do not hesitate to attack this view—which detracts nothing
from the truth of either Christ's body or the Sacraments, leaves no room for
any absurdity, commends God's omnipotence most highly, accords most fully with
the nature of faith, and finally raises us into heaven—just as if we said that
the bread and wine, as mere images, nakedly and simply signify the Lord's body,
although everywhere we insist upon the force of sacramental signification.

To confirm this metonymy we cite various and almost innumerable passages. Some
show that the verb “IS” is not always used properly and substantively, as they
say. From this we infer that we are not rashly asking how this verb, which has
various and manifold meanings in Holy Scripture, should be explained in the
saying of Christ under discussion. Other passages show precisely the same
metonymy in use. What do you do in response? First you select a few and pass
over the rest as, you say, “refuted a thousand times.” We do not refuse to let
your books and our replies determine how truthfully you say this. Next you
undertake the refutation of certain passages, first treating this one: “The
Holy Spirit was not yet.” “What then?” you say. “The verb ‘IS’ is nevertheless
not taken here for ‘signifies.’” As if we explained this passage of John in
that way, and as if you yourself did not acquiesce in the interpretation Calvin
offers for it! As if, finally, we cited this passage to show that “IS” is taken
for “signifies,” rather than to prove that those who oppose their little word
“IS” to every argument establish nothing by it when they try to establish a
fleshly and substantial manner of presence. The verb is not always taken
substantially, and its meaning must therefore be assessed from the nature of
the subject under discussion. [Marginal reference: John 7:39.]

You do the same in what follows. To show that the verb “IS” is not always used
substantially, Calvin cites Paul's passage in 1 Corinthians 12, where the Church
is designated by Christ's name. If the verb “IS” in that statement is taken as
you wish it understood in Christ's saying, “This is my body,” it will follow—as
Calvin rightly infers—that all of us are truly, properly, and naturally Christ.
How do you answer this, Westphal? You admit what you cannot deny: the Church is
called Christ because of the closest conjunction of members and head. But you
add that this argument does not favor us because “IS” is not taken there for
“signifies.” As if we asked for anything more than what you concede: namely,
that “IS” does not always declare the substantial manner of presence you want.
Otherwise it would follow from your dogma that the bodies and souls of the
faithful are really and substantially in heaven with Christ, and thus are
everywhere, or at least are with the bread even before the sacramental
communication is repeated. It is not enough for you to say what we insist upon
and in which alone we rest: that through faith, by the Holy Spirit's power, we
are truly one with Christ. You also affirm that we must be really,
substantially, and corporeally united to Christ, just as you wish to couple the
body with the bread and us with the body.

But what will you do with Paul? Explaining here what he said elsewhere—that we
are bones of Christ's bones and flesh of his flesh—he testifies that this whole
mystery depends not on a fleshly manner of presence but on the Spirit's power,
and attributes the same thing no less to Baptism than to the Lord's Supper.
These are his words: “By one Spirit we were all baptized into one body, whether
Jews or Greeks, slaves or free.” And lest you quibble that the Lord's Supper is
different, Paul adds, plainly looking to participation in the cup, “And all
were made to drink into one Spirit” (that is, that we might grow together by
one spirit—namely, Christ's),

[PDF page 10; printed page 219]

we have drawn from the drink; or, “We have all drunk one Spirit.” Unless we
prefer to follow another reading, supported by the Greek scholia and certain
ancient manuscripts and even more explicit: “And all drank one drink into one
Spirit.” But perhaps you will object that this communication not only does not
exclude that other, real and substantial communication, but depends upon it.
If that is so, however, what prevents you from confessing that the Lord's body
is substantially present in Baptism too, since Paul expressly affirms that the
Church is called Christ precisely because through Baptism all the faithful grow
together with him into the same body? If you evade confessing this in Baptism,
where you nevertheless cannot deny that we are truly united with Christ, why
do you require in the Lord's Supper an additional manner of communication which
you confess is not required in Baptism? Likewise, if this communication through
the Spirit suffices for the Church truly to be called Christ, why is that real
manner of conjunction between the signs and the thing signified so urgently
required?

We show, moreover, that God's own name is applied to the ark of the covenant in
many passages, although the ark was not substantially but only symbolically
God. From this we infer that the metonymy by which the name of the thing is
transferred to its sign is neither new nor unusual, since it shows that the
sign is by no means empty. But you deny that it is written in so many words,
“The ark is God,” as the Lord expressly said, “This is my body.” Next you quibble
that the expressions occurring so often in Moses and elsewhere concerning the
sight and face of God and God's habitation in the sanctuary; David's playing
and dancing before the Lord; and the sayings, “Rise up, O Lord,” and “Return, O
Lord,” refer not to the ark but to the Lord present there. Yet to whom, perhaps
except you alone, can it be unknown that the Israelites looked toward the Lord
in such a way that meanwhile they contemplated his presence especially in that
peculiar symbol, the ark of the covenant, and that those manners of speaking
arose from no other source? Who, I say, does not know the distinction between
God's presence and habitation? Unless perhaps you think that the Lord is said
to have dwelt between the cherubim in no different sense from that in which he
is said to fill all things.

You next seize upon this passage against us because the ark was a symbol of the
truly present God, not a figure of an absent God. But it is utterly absurd to
apply to the human nature what belongs to the divine nature alone. Moreover,
however many shapes you twist yourself into, you can devise no other manner of
God's presence with the ark than that the Lord peculiarly exercised his power
there. In this respect he is also said to dwell there, although otherwise he
fills all things. These things do not oppose us in the least, since we by no
means deny that the Lord peculiarly communicates himself to us in the Supper
through faith by his Spirit's power. [Marginal references: 2 Kings 6; Numbers
10.]

Finally, even if I granted whatever sort of presence you wish, would it follow
that the ark was anything other than a sign of the present God? If it is only a
sign and not the thing itself, how will the name of the thing itself be
assigned to the sign without metonymy? You should therefore not have condemned
the trope of metonymy without qualification, but required only that we say the
bread is—that is, signifies—the present body. We do not deny the body's presence
(as I have now said a thousand times) with respect to our union with Christ
through faith; but we say that Christ's body is to be sought through faith
nowhere but in heaven, so that by the Spirit's power what is truly signified by
natural symbols may truly be fulfilled in us.

We also cite two passages of Moses, one in which Circumcision is called the
sign of the covenant and another in which it is called the covenant itself. We
maintain that a trope necessarily occurs in the latter proposition, since the
sign is necessarily distinguished from the thing signified—that is, the
covenant itself from its sign. “On the contrary,” you say, “Circumcision truly
is the compact and covenant.” Who would dare deny it when the Lord speaks that
way? The question, however, is not whether the statement is true but whether it
is to be taken figuratively or properly and simply. You affirm this too, and
yet confess that the covenant is nothing other than the promise itself, “I
will be your God and the God of your seed.” Is this really speaking according
to reason? [Marginal references: Genesis 17:10–11.]

Last, you torture yourself wretchedly in order to wrest from us those passages
in which the lamb is called the Passover, although it was not itself the
passing over but renewed the memory of that passing over—to omit its other
meanings, which there is no place to discuss now.

First, in your customary fashion, you assign us this argument: “In the words,
‘The lamb is the Passover,’ ‘is’ is put for ‘signifies’; therefore the same holds
in the words of the Eucharist.” Where did you learn that we argue this way? We
instead frame the argument thus: the lamb is said to be the Passover, of which
it is nevertheless only the symbol; therefore the verb “is” is sometimes said
of symbolic significa-

tion. We then give the reasons why and to what extent the same interpretation
has a place in the words of the Eucharist. You are therefore caught in an open
calumny. You deny, however, that there is a trope in Moses' words, “For it is
the Lord's Passover.” “For,” you say, “this explanation pertains neither to the
lamb nor to the verb ‘You shall eat,’ but to all the rites Moses had previously
mentioned. It especially concerns the command that the Israelites eat the lamb
girded and in haste; the explanation for all these things is that it is the
Lord's Passover or passing over—that is, the Lord will pass over this night, the
memory of which passing over you must retain and renew by these rites.” But
grant that it is so: what escape will remain for you if we bring forward so
many other passages which expressly say that the Passover is sacrificed and
eaten? Will you then dare deny that the name *Passover* is assigned to the lamb
itself? I think not. For when you soon return to yourself, though you stoutly
dissemble those passages, you nevertheless acknowledge that the lamb was
called the Passover—that is, the passing over—because it memorialized the event.
[Marginal references: Exodus 12:11; Numbers 9:2; Mark 14:12; Luke 22:7; John
18:28; 1 Corinthians 5:7.]

You then spend several pages showing that there is a great difference between
eating the lamb and the Lord's Supper. “For,” you say, “the Jews ate the lamb in
memory of their deliverance from Egypt. We, however, not only eat and drink
bread and wine in commemoration of the deliverance obtained through Christ,
but also receive his true body and blood at the same time.” Although we freely
confess that Christ's true body and blood are received by us through faith in
the Lord's Supper so that eternal life may be drawn from them, we recognize no
difference here but the greatest agreement. For even in the Passover there was
not only a commemoration of deliverance from Egypt, but a true and, through
faith, effective signification of the future redemption through Christ. John
above all others doubtless looked to this when he said, “Behold the Lamb of God,
who takes away the sins of the world”; and Paul spoke most openly of all when he
wrote that Christ our Passover had been sacrificed.

You therefore explained this ceremony neither rightly nor worthily when you
said that the lamb was only a type of the Christ who was to be sacrificed—no
more, if we agree with you, than the passage through the Red Sea was a type of
Baptism. On the contrary, the Passover was a true and ordinary sacrament in
God's Church. Its truth was the same as that of the Lord's Supper, although the
measure both of revelation and of efficacy was unequal. Especially false and
foolish is your confirmation of the error I just mentioned by opposing the two
adverbs *typically* and *truly* as though they were contraries. As if the Lord
formerly played games with the Israelites! As if anything prevents the Lord
from fulfilling in us, most truly and effectually through his Spirit's power,
the very thing he depicts by outward figures and signs—not to obscure it, but
to kindle the faith of the heart even through our senses. [Marginal
references: John 1:29; 1 Corinthians 5:7.]

Why answer the rest of what you recall at the end of this chapter about a
certain dream of Dr. Zwingli—mentioned, I think, more to amuse yourself than
from zeal to defend the truth? You act unjustly when you do not permit Dr.
Zwingli, a man of blessed memory, with your good leave to have had even one
learned dream while asleep, when you yourself, even awake, seem to do nothing
but dream in body and mind alike.

You spend the sixth chapter explaining the Apostle's words, “The rock was
Christ.” We maintain that Christ's name is assigned to the rock by precisely the
same metonymy by which the name *body* is assigned to the bread. You deny this,
and for that reason Calvin is to you obstinate, shameless, reckless, and bold
in corrupting the divine Scriptures. Such is your modesty—the shame of an
exceedingly modest and bashful man, no doubt! Tell me why. “Because,” you say,
“these words of Paul are spoken only about the spiritual rock and therefore
without a trope.” How do you infer this? “Our leading champions taught so,” you
say. By what arguments? “First, because the epithet *spiritual* is added to
distinguish it from the corporeal rock. Second, because this rock is said to
have accompanied the Israelites, which does not fit the corporeal rock that
did not move from its place, but fits Christ, who gave them drink from the dry
rock.” Come, then, let us weigh these claims.

I omit Tertullian, Jerome, Origen, the Chaldean paraphrast, and the ancient
Hebrew interpretation, which favor us. Let us contend by arguments rather than
authority. Your claim that the epithet *spiritual* is added is false in itself,
for it is not repeated in the second clause. But grant that it is repeated,
since Paul speaks of no rock except the spiritual rock. Who taught you that the
epithet *spiritual* cannot fit the corporeal rock? For if that is so, by the
same reasoning in the preceding verse the spiritual food cannot be manna.
Thus Paul will not have mentioned the signs, but only the things signified. If
that is true, where is the comparison he establishes between signs and signs,
not things and things? For Paul posits one thing—Christ—both in the ancients'
signs and in ours, and his whole argument rests upon no other foundation.
Likewise, why in Baptism did he expressly name the types—namely the cloud and
the sea—but in the Supper fail to name them?

Since these conclusions are utterly absurd, I certainly will not agree with
you. As the matter itself requires, by *spiritual food* I understand the manna,
and by *spiritual rock* that very rock struck by Moses. “But,” you will say,
“why are they called spiritual?” Because they were sacraments of spiritual
nourishment—that is, of our true food and true drink, Jesus Christ. I call his
true body and true blood spiritual nourishment not because they are not
corporeal by their own nature, but because, communicated to us through faith by
spiritual power, they instill in us that eternal and spiritual life. Yet some
difficulty may perhaps remain. The Apostle seems expressly to distinguish the
drink from the rock—that is, the stream from its source. As if the same Christ
did not answer to the figure of both source and stream when he offers his own
blood to be drunk through faith unto eternal life!

So much for the first argument. Let us come to the other, in which you deny
that the rock accompanied the Israelites. I readily concede this if one
considers the rock itself. But to whom can this manner of speaking be unknown:
one who drinks from the stream flowing out of the rock is said to drink from
the rock, while the source is nevertheless distinguished from the stream? Thus
in this type the rock will represent Christ himself as giver, while the stream
accompanying Israel will represent his blood; whoever drinks from it is said
to drink from Christ himself. “But,” you will say, “we do not read in Moses that
this stream continually accompanied the Israelites.” I admit that this is not
written in Moses in so many words; but neither does he name Jannes and Jambres,
whose names nevertheless occur in Paul. Besides the fact that the probability
of the history itself seems to demand that we believe neither that the
Israelites traveling in the dry wilderness rashly left this stream nor that
the stream suddenly deserted them, ancient Hebrew authority agrees with Paul:
the Hebrews interpret in no other way what Moses writes elsewhere about the
well rising up. Finally, those who want these things referred to Christ alone
should see how the verb “followed” can fit Christ as leader who goes before.
One who leads others cannot be said to follow. Untie these knots, therefore. If
you cannot, either assent to the truth or keep for yourself the obstinacy you
impute to others. [Marginal references: 2 Timothy 3:8; Numbers 21:17.]

Two matters still remain in this same chapter. At last, compelled by the force
of truth itself, you admit that the rock from which water flowed in the desert
was a figure of Christ. “But,” you say, “this does not fit the passage of Paul;
it is established from other passages of Scripture.” Demonstrate two things
for us, then: first, why Paul omitted what most needed to be said; and second,
which passages of Scripture establish it. Finally, growing bolder, you deny
that this passage favors us even if it is understood exactly as we demand.
“For,” you say, “you will establish nothing more than that it was a sign of the
present Christ, whom the Fathers spiritually perceived by faith.” We seize what
you concede under the force of truth, and say that you must now grant us two
things: first, that the interpretation of the verb “IS” as “signifies” is not to
be rejected without qualification, as you have done until now; and second,
that Paul himself uses “IS” for “signifies” in connection with a spiritual
manner of presence. If it was sufficient for the Fathers' salvation to perceive
spiritually through faith a truly present Christ, although he had not yet been
born—as you confess in so many words—by what right will you hereafter contend
that it is insufficient for us to perceive in a similar manner the same Christ
now born, though under different symbols and with fuller revelation and
efficacy?

### In reply to chapter seven: On Christ's words, “John is Elijah”

[Marginal reference: Matthew 11:14.]

We cite this saying of Christ for no other reason than to show that the verb
“IS” does not always join predicate and subject in its proper sense. You think
you have answered abundantly if you object that “IS” is not taken for
“signifies” here. Even if it is not taken for “signifies,” however, you will
never prove that John is properly and substantially said to be Elijah, since
*Elijah* and *John* are the names of two individuals. Christ's words plainly
show that Elijah's name is assigned to John only because he represented Elijah
in power and spirit. We therefore obtain the one thing we want: since the verb
“IS” is said even of similarity in qualities alone, those who prove that
Christ's flesh is substantially in, with, or under the bread by confronting us
with nothing but that little word do not rest upon a sufficiently firm
argument. Nor must those who seek to elicit its true interpretation by
comparing passages of Scripture immediately be regarded as unbelievers and
heretics. For we were never so foolish as to think that “IS” is taken for
“signifies” in this saying.

We cite this passage too in order to prove that the literal wording must not

### Chapter nine: What the Sacramentarians mean by “body” in the words of the Holy Supper

Calvin and other learned and godly men of our party have insisted a thousand
times that by the names *body* and *blood* we understand nothing other than the
very natural body of Christ which was affixed to the cross for us, and that
true blood which was shed for us, of which things the bread and wine in the
Lord's Supper are true and certain symbols. But these things do not satisfy
you. First, then, you cite certain passages from Dr. Zwingli's books published
long ago in the very heat of that unhappy controversy, in which he sometimes
interprets *flesh* as Divinity by alloeosis, and sometimes writes that by the
name *body* death or passion is understood. But to what end, when you are not
unaware how many things were written back and forth at that time which the
authors themselves afterward explained much more clearly and certainly? As if,
were we inclined to scrutinize each of Dr. Luther's writings, we would not find
very many things which we do not doubt displeased no one more than Luther
himself afterward. We certainly do not wish to imitate you in this, examining
every writing of that most excellent man in order to accuse him of either
fickleness or ignorance. Rather, we gladly pass over things of this kind that
were afterward more clearly explained, in him and in anyone else; and in our
own case we hope that fair readers will not press them rigidly, insofar as
religion and charity permit.

And yet not even those statements are such that they cannot be softened by a
suitable interpretation. Why does Dr. Zwingli interpret *flesh* as Divinity in
the passage where Christ's flesh is said to give life, except to teach that
this power proceeds from the flesh only because the Divinity is united to it?
For giving life belongs properly to the Divinity. Likewise, when he understands
passion and death by the name *body*, he certainly does not simply and in every
way remove the body's substance. He shows instead that the body must be
considered insofar as it suffered and died for us, in order to become
life-giving to us. Thus no one should tear apart the connection between
substance and fruit in your fashion, since you think even the ungodly receive
the body.

Zwingli is surely his own best interpreter. In his Confession to Charles he
expressly testifies “that he believes Christ's true body to be present in the
Lord's Supper—present to this extent, that what is corporeally in heaven is, as
it were, present to the contemplation of faith.” He speaks still more plainly
in the Confession to King Francis: “We believe that Christ is truly in the
Supper; indeed, we do not believe there is a Lord's Supper unless Christ is
present.” And shortly afterward he says: “We affirm that Christ's body is not
eaten in such a fleshly and gross manner as those men claim, but we believe
Christ's true body is eaten in the Supper sacramentally” (that is, when the
symbol of bread is received) “and spiritually” (that is, not by the teeth or
mouth, nor in a natural manner, but by faith and the Holy Spirit's power) “by a
religious, faithful, and holy mind.” These are Zwingli's own words. Since the
earlier statements can plainly be explained by them, why did you cite the
earlier ones, unless you feared that it was not enough to let the banked ashes
of the conflagration rest quietly in the Lord?

Next you attack the writing of a certain man who did not wish to be named, in
which that man, whoever he was, displayed a great zeal for peace and much
learning. But what did he accomplish with you? Only, it seems, to stir up the
hornets. Yet he, whoever he is, will surely not lack a defender, I think, since
he defends the truth. John Oecolampadius comes next, a man whose godliness and
learning everyone has always esteemed highly, except one who either never knew
him or was himself devoid of all godliness and learning. You cry out that he
preferred to posit a trope in the name *body* rather than in the verb “IS”—and
you had triumphed earlier because you said that we substitute the figure of the
body for the body. To prove this you can produce only one passage from
Oecolampadius in which he says something of the kind. You do so because you
have failed to understand that even when we say the words “This is my body”
declare the same as “This bread is the symbol, sign, or figure of the body”
(terms which we are afterward accustomed to explain carefully), although we
retain the verb “IS,” we do not change the predicate. We declare the manner in
which the predicate *body* is attributed to the subject *bread*. For these
statements do not differ: “The bread truly figures the body,” and “The bread is
a true figure of the body.” Your repeated claim therefore collapses: that we
substitute for the body a figure, type, shadow, specter, or phantom of the body.
To settle it in a word, if it is sacrilege to speak this way, why do you not
also accuse

[PDF page 14; printed page 223]

Augustine openly of sacrilege because he testifies that Christ said, “This is
my body,” when he gave the sign of his body? And elsewhere: “You will not eat
this body which you see, nor drink the blood which those men will shed.” Why do
you not number among Tertullian's errors his open interpretation, “This is my
body,” that is, “a figure of my body”? Why do you not accuse Origen on this same
charge when he plainly says that “the bread which God the Word acknowledges to
be his body is the Word nourishing souls”? Is it because those statements can
be suitably explained from other passages? As if our men did not also explain
the sacramental signification abundantly! [Marginal references: Augustine,
*Against Adimantus* 12; *Tractates on John* 26; Tertullian, *Against Marcion*
IV; Origen on Matthew 15.]

At last you come to Calvin and testify that, although in words he appears to
posit Christ's body as the thing of the Sacrament, in reality he posits only
Christ's power, not Christ himself, in the Lord's Supper. Tell me, where do you
get this? You were not ashamed to cite passages in which Calvin affirms that
what is given in the Supper is no other body than that offered on the cross,
the true and proper substance of body and blood—not a deceptive, imaginary, or
shadowy body, but that natural body by whose sacrifice sins were expiated on
the cross. “But,” you say, “that Calvin is speaking about the power and effects
of Christ's flesh is evident from what he writes.” As if one who says that
Christ's life-giving power flows to us from Christ's flesh removes communion
in the flesh, rather than showing why and to what end it is communicated to us!
Elsewhere Calvin says that by the symbol of bread the Lord figures his flesh
as food for us, and that inwardly, by his Spirit's power, he truly performs
what he displays to the eyes. Can a man who infers from this that Calvin posits
not the Lord's body itself as the thing of the Sacrament, but only its power,
be regarded as sane?

But elsewhere Calvin denies that Christ gave with the bread the same body
which they beheld with their eyes. This, of course, is your inference: “The body
is not given *with* the bread; therefore it is not given.” Elsewhere Calvin
denies the substantial presence you imagine. From this you construct a most
rigorous demonstration that Calvin excludes Christ's substance from the
Supper. For you imagine a real and natural coalescence of Christ's substance
with ours. Consequently, together with that pope Nicholas of yours—whose
blasphemy you are not ashamed to approve—you judge that after consecration not
only the sacrament, but the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, are
sensibly handled by priests' hands, broken (although it was not broken even by
the soldiers on the cross), and ground by the teeth of the faithful. Whoever
denies this is ungodly in your eyes, even if he holds that in the Supper,
through faith and by the Holy Spirit's power, we are made truly one with Christ
who is in heaven, so that we may draw eternal life from him. It is well that an
appeal from you is possible.

Finally, you dream that for Calvin “*substance* declares nothing other than the
foundation and basis of a thing, and what is principal—whether cause, power, or
force—just as,” you say, “it is ordinarily taken in the categories.” You then
add that “for Calvin, to become a partaker of Christ's body declares nothing
other than to believe and by faith enjoy Christ's benefits.” As if this were
not, among Christ's benefits, the foundation as it were of all the rest: that
he himself becomes ours. We do not separate the fruit from its ground. But
with what honesty do you repeat here that *eating* and *faith* mean the same
thing in Calvin, when you presently admit that Calvin distinguishes them
everywhere for the sake of teaching, and immediately accuse him on that very
account? What arguments support you? “Because,” you say, “in John 6 Christ uses
the terms eating, drinking, and believing indiscriminately.” What if we answer
that the Lord does not employ them as synonyms, but after mentioning eating
and drinking, added the instrument by which he wished to be eaten and drunk?
“Yet men of your own party explain it that way,” you say. I admit it, and we do
not differ from them in doctrine by even a hair. But if for the sake of teaching
we use a slightly more precise distinction, why should that be counted against
us?

You yourself admit that receiving the nourishment of Christ's flesh is a work
of faith. Although I cannot simply admit this proposition as you frame it—for
you would rather say that receiving Christ's flesh is a work of faith alone—if
it is true, how far you are from cutting your own throat, as they say, with
your own sword! The work or effect of faith and faith itself can no more be
called the same thing than a hand and its act of grasping—that is, extending the
hand and taking hold of something.

Finally, because you continually confront him with those clear and perspicuous
words, “This is my body,” Calvin asked whether they are even clearer than the
testimonies on which the Anthropomorphites relied. You answer that they are
clearer still. “For,” you say, “it is established that God is Spirit, while
Christ is man. From this it appears that the former passages must be taken
figuratively, but the latter properly.” Thus you want permission to explain
those passages, however simply and openly stated, by comparison with others so
that there may be no discrepancy; but that we should do the same

[PDF page 15; printed page 224]

you concede in the other Sacraments but by no means in the Lord's Supper. Why?
Because it does not please you. Although you so often reproach us for relying
too heavily on philosophy and the reasoning of common sense, you unwittingly
fall into the same thing, opposing the nature of Spirit to the clear words just
as we oppose the nature of the human body to them. At last you grow as enraged
against us as if we had accused you of being an Anthropomorphite. To return an
insult where none was given, you say: “It is an error to think that God is
corporeal; but the man who thinks the Son of God did not truly assume flesh and
does not give it in the Eucharist errs no less.” What a calumny this is—to
accuse of assigning Christ a phantom body those who, precisely so that they do
not strip Christ of the truth of his flesh, maintain that with respect to his
humanity his body must be sought neither in, with, nor under the bread, nor
anywhere but in heaven! You yourself should instead consider how far these
things expressly written by your Pythagoras depart from the madness of Marcion,
Valentinus, Eutyches, and other such monsters: “Christ is simultaneously in the
bread definitively and in heaven supernaturally by repletion”; and “All things
are full of Christ even insofar as he is man.”

### In reply to chapter eleven: Whether unworthy and evil Christians receive only the symbols or the Lord's true body

You spend the tenth chapter and several others besides trying to pull Augustine
over to your side. We will explain all of them together in one place, for I
have determined to proceed this way lest we confuse everything in your manner.
In one part of that chapter, then, you prove what we by no means deny: that the
question “What do unbelievers receive in the Lord's Supper?” is useful. You
prove by two arguments that even those who approach unworthily—that is, in a
word, without faith—receive the thing of the Sacrament, namely the Lord's body
and blood. First, Paul says that those who eat this bread and drink this cup
unworthily eat and drink judgment to themselves, not discerning the Lord's
body. Second, since we object that Christ's body is stripped of its life-giving
power if it does not give life to everyone with whom it is joined, you answer
that “we fail to consider that all judgment has been given to Christ even as
man, both to save the faithful and to judge unbelievers.” A splendid answer,
truly! Who denies that those who approach the Sacrament unworthily receive
judgment upon themselves, and that all judgment has been given to Christ? The
question is whether, for an unbeliever to fail to discern the Lord's body and
for someone to be condemned by the Lord, Christ must be received, rather than
being rejected when offered either without a symbol or with the symbol added.
When the symbol is added, indeed, the greater the kindness of God which shines
forth, the fouler the contempt and the more severe the judgment.

How do you answer the many other very strong arguments we use, Westphal? You
dispose of them all with one phrase, answering that all our questions are
curious but proving nothing. You then slide into tearing apart our churches:
“Because,” you say, “they admit unexamined persons to Communion without private
absolution having been pronounced.” Of these two accusations, why refute the
first at greater length? Anyone can know how carefully the ministers of the
Word and the most godly magistracy itself act in this city of Geneva so that no
unknown person, no boy or girl without first making a profession of faith as
is proper, and finally no person convicted of a disgraceful act without having
made satisfaction to the Church when necessary, is admitted to the Supper.
Excommunication flourishes here, and is at least some remedy against the gross
profanation you falsely charge us with. We will speak elsewhere about the
second accusation, namely the neglect of private absolution. For now I say only
that in our churches all persons are carefully confirmed publicly, and each
person privately—whether they give an account of their faith before the
minister, as I just said, or wish to approach him privately—in the doctrine of
repentance, faith, and forgiveness of sins. But we neither can nor wish to lay
upon every individual the burden of being examined by a minister and receiving
private absolution every time the Lord's Supper is celebrated.

### In reply to chapter twelve: On the terms “fleshly” and “spiritual,” “corporeally” and “spiritually”

It vexes you, Westphal, that we say you make the Supper fleshly, invent a
fleshly manner in which Christ's flesh is devoured, understand it fleshly, and
eat Christ's body fleshly. The reason we speak this way is not the one you
invent—as if we denied that Christ's flesh is truly offered to all in the
Supper and truly received by the faithful, so that, having become one with
Christ, they draw eternal life from it. That, I say, is not why we speak this
way. Rather, it is because for you it is not enough that through faith in the
Lord's Supper we become partakers of Christ's true body and blood, unless we
also posit that the body itself is coupled with the bread, so that although

[PDF page 16; printed page 225]

invisibly, nevertheless in a real, substantial, and finally corporeal manner,
first Christ's flesh is joined to the bread and his blood to the wine; and then
the bread joined to the body and the blood joined to the wine are joined with
us. Thus, with respect to the conjunction and reception itself, the sole
difference between body and blood on the one hand and bread and wine on the
other is that the latter are visible to our eyes and the former invisible.
Hence that ubiquity; hence that faith which admonishes us to believe that
Christ has a true and therefore finite body which nevertheless fills all
things, and besides is in the bread definitively rather than circumscriptively.
By what words, then, will we describe this opinion if you do not permit us, in
Scripture's manner, to oppose the fleshly to the spiritual? We acknowledge that
both *flesh* and *spirit* are ambiguous terms, yet we certainly know no better
ones.

As for your games with these terms, you have just maintained that the presence
you posit is nevertheless called spiritual from its effect, since Christ's
flesh received in this way makes men spiritual. We readily grant all of this
to you—although you truly clothe Christ's flesh with the nature of Spirit when
you make it invisible. Meanwhile we confess with you that the nourishment which
Christ breathes from himself into his members is spiritual. You ask whether we
dare say Christ was born fleshly from Mary because he assumed true flesh from
Mary. We ask you in return whether you dare say he was born spiritually,
insofar as he came out of the womb clothed in true flesh. You cry out that we
oppose the Spirit to the Word, and discourse at length about Spirit and letter.
Why should we defend something that never entered our minds? You then return to
your old song and say that we instead are fleshly because we depend upon human
reason and wisdom. What fair judge would believe this from you even under oath?

You show yourself truly clever in arguing as follows: “Christ did not lack
flesh because Peter ate him spiritually; likewise, the eating of Christ's body
must not be removed because it is to be eaten spiritually.” We readily agree,
for spiritual eating does not remove the body's truth. But if Peter had eaten
Christ's body according to your opinion, the Jews would no more have had
anything to crucify than a person sitting down last at table would have
anything to eat after all the food had been consumed. “Abraham,” you say, “saw
Christ spiritually through faith; it does not follow that Simeon did not see
him corporeally.” Again, “because Simeon saw Christ spiritually, it must not
therefore be denied that he saw him and carried him in his arms corporeally.”
Quite right; why should Simeon not also embrace by faith one who was naturally
present, when one may similarly perceive by faith—that is, in the same way—one
who is naturally absent? Who, then, would not think that you were abandoning
your case?

What more? “The Word of the Gospel,” you say, “must be received spiritually,
that is, by faith; yet corporeal hearing is not removed. What follows? Even if
the Lord's body is spiritually perceived by faith, corporeal eating is by no
means removed.” Quite right, if you prove what you assume and what is in
question: namely, that Christ's body is now present to us just as the sound of
the Father's preached Word is. The nature of a true body does not permit this,
and all Scripture accordingly denies it, since it assigns Christ a true body—
united to the Deity, indeed, yet still a body. Now, since not the same sound but
only a similar one can be heard simultaneously in places farthest apart, why do
you place the same body in infinitely many locations instead of, to make your
comparison valid, manufacturing at every moment new bodies of Christ like the
earlier ones?

### Chapter thirteen: On sacramental and spiritual eating

You say you labor greatly to arrange what Calvin said confusedly and
disorderedly. Let the matter itself determine how truthfully you speak. This is
the sum of the controversy. You judge sacramental eating to occur when
unbelievers, without faith and without any sense of piety, receive Christ's body
with the bread unto judgment. Spiritual eating, however, occurs when the fruit
is also added, namely in those who bring faith. Calvin condemns this, and with
excellent reason. For in your sacramental eating, things most closely joined
are torn apart: the body from its spiritual and life-giving power, or the eating
of the body from the reception of life. We therefore call your sacramental
eating nothing but a Chimera. Instead, bare sacramental eating should have been
defined as that in which, although bare bread is not given—for besides the
bread presented by the minister according to the Lord's institution, Christ
also offers his true body to be spiritually received by faith—nevertheless,
although the whole Sacrament is given, the thing of the Sacrament is rejected
and the symbol alone received. Whenever this happens—and it happens whenever
true faith is not brought to the action of the Supper—the person who commits
this crime is guilty of the Lord's body, not because he received it (for he did

[PDF page 17; printed page 226]

not receive it), but because he failed to discern it and therefore despised
it. Do you now understand clearly enough not only what our view is, but what
yours is? Let us hear, then, how you attack ours and defend your own. “Who,”
you say, “will allow the definition of the Sacrament to be restricted to the
outward eating of bread alone?” I ask the reader: who can calmly endure a man
who speaks so obscurely demanding clarity in anyone, much less in Calvin? What
does it mean to restrict a definition to eating? But I can guess what you mean.
You think that unless the body is always received with the bread and the blood
with the wine, the thing is torn away from the sign and the definition of a
Sacrament thereby perishes, since that definition requires the thing to be
coupled with the sign. As if defining a Sacrament were the same as asking about
its reception! As if this inference were valid: “In the Supper the Lord always
offers the Sacrament, that is, the thing with the sign; therefore whoever
receives the sign must also perceive the thing itself.”

Where, then, is the fault? In your customary fashion you assume the very thing
in dispute: that the thing of the Sacrament is really coupled with the symbol,
so that both are received with hands and mouth—the former invisibly, the latter
visibly. We, however, say that the thing itself is nowhere but in heaven and
the signs of the things on earth. Yet the distance between places does not
prevent the signs and things from being truly joined by the Lord's institution.
For the bread and wine are not given as bodily nourishment, but to figure to
the outward senses that true body and true blood, which the Lord no less truly
offers from heaven to be spiritually apprehended and communicated to us
through faith than he truly presents the sacred symbols through his minister
to be seen with our eyes, touched with our hands, and received with our mouth.
From this, we say, it follows that the Sacraments never cease to consist of
sign and thing, since the Lord always truly offers what he truly figures both
to believers and unbelievers. Nevertheless, unbelievers can no more perceive
the thing itself than someone without a mouth or teeth can feed upon food.

Your conclusion that we separate what God's Word joins will therefore not
stand unless you first prove from God's Word that fleshly manner of conjunction
which you posit. We cannot think it either necessary for maintaining the
dignity of the Sacraments and truly uniting the members to their head, or
compatible with the truth of Christ's body. If some prefer otherwise, let them
enjoy their opinion. But by what right are we heretics, when we defend no less
than they the true communion with Christ which is ours in the Lord's Supper?

You add shortly afterward that “Calvin, in order to exclude corporeal eating,
contends with the argument that common sense opposes it.” Calvin does not
mention common sense, but the sense of piety, so you cannot deny that you are a
falsifier here. If you do not admit the sense of piety as a rule of
interpretation, what will you admit? But let us grant that what you falsely
invent was written by Calvin. What is under discussion? That gross eating of
the flesh. Otherwise, who insists more vehemently than Calvin upon the spiritual
communion with Christ's true body, although it far surpasses common sense? We
therefore say that gross eating, even if it is posited to occur invisibly, is
nevertheless bestial rather than human, much less Christian. We consider what
is done, not how it is done; and so we call it bestial for one man to be eaten
by another even invisibly. For this reason we also affirm that the terms
*eating* and *drinking* are taken not properly but metaphorically, to signify
that what we eat and drink passes into nourishment for this life no more truly
and certainly than it is certain and true that through faith we become
spiritually partakers of the true man Jesus Christ unto eternal life.

If you deny this, at least hear why Augustine judges these expressions to be
interpreted not by some invisible form and manner, but figuratively and
tropically. “Because otherwise,” he says, “a cruel crime would be commanded. For
it is a graver crime to eat flesh than to kill, and to drink blood than to shed
it.” So much for common sense. The clever arguments you add are similar.
“Indignantly,” you say, “Calvin asks what profit there is in eating Christ's
flesh if it ceases to be nourishment.” What do you answer? “By similar
reasoning,” you say, “Calvin will ask what profit Christ brings if he is set for
the fall of many.” A splendid comparison indeed—as though Christ were set for
the fall of those by whom he is received, rather than those who reject him!
You therefore move to another answer. “Christ's flesh does not cease to be
nourishment to the godly,” you say, “even if it profits unbelievers nothing and
indeed harms them.” The answer is obvious. As if Calvin asked how Christ's
flesh profits the faithful if it profits unbelievers nothing, rather than how
it profits unbelievers if it does not feed them. Let us assist you, then, and
answer

[PDF page 18; printed page 227]

that it profits them nothing but instead harms them, and this through their own
fault. But now show that Christ's flesh harms those who become its partakers,
rather than those who reject it when offered. More than enough on these
matters. We will explain in its place what you scatter about from Augustine.

### In reply to chapter fourteen: On the thing and truth of the Sacrament of the Holy Supper

In this chapter you would gladly persuade everyone of what you have now
repeated a thousand times: that we posit not Christ's true body and blood, or
Christ himself, as the thing of the Sacrament, but only the Sacrament's effect,
namely perception of its life-giving power. Just as we acknowledge that we do
not recognize that mingling of substances, however, you falsely attribute to
us the separation of Christ himself from his gifts, as though we did not need
to be united first of all to Christ himself—as we have now declared to the point
of weariness. What thing do you yourself at last join to the outward symbols?
“Sometimes,” you say, “the thing and truth of the Sacrament signifies Christ's
true body and blood; at other times the effect, power, and grace of the
Sacrament.”

We find many faults with this description. First, you separate things always
joined by an indivisible bond—Christ's body and blood from their life-giving
power. If you do not separate them, there certainly is no division here, but a
distinction within a single member. The word *sometimes* has no place; you
should instead have employed a copulative particle. Second, even if we follow
your opinion, the division is faulty. You must admit that for believers at
least both members coalesce into one. Thus, for the division to be proper, it
should have been constructed in accordance with the laws of dialectic—which
someone promising nothing but demonstrations ought to observe—as follows: the
thing and truth of the Sacrament are Christ's body and blood, which are
sometimes received unto judgment and sometimes unto grace. But how will you
prove the former member? I hope in its place to refute so thoroughly what you
ascribe to Augustine that you will be left no support in him. Finally, when you
say that the Sacrament's truth is sometimes its effect, power, and grace, do
you not commit the very fault you falsely charged against us—confusing the
thing of the Sacrament with its effect?

### In reply to chapter fifteen: On the manner of eating Christ's body

If someone asks us how we eat and drink Christ, whether when the Lord's bare
Word is presented to us or when the Word is, as it were, clothed in Sacraments,
we answer first that if the words *eating* and *drinking* are spoken concerning
the bare Word, they are used metaphorically. They express in the most
significant way possible that, just as nourished by food and drink we live this
perishing life, so the one Christ—Christ himself with his gifts, I say—becomes
ours and supplies us eternal life. But if they are spoken concerning the Word
to which the symbols of bread and wine are joined, then these words are spoken
properly of the signs themselves and assigned metonymically to the thing
signified, so that the truth of sacramental communication may be expressed all
the better.

Having established this, as regards the efficient cause we answer that by the
Holy Spirit's power we are truly made one with Christ, as the Word announces
and the Sacraments truly figure. As regards the instrumental cause, we say
this occurs by faith alone, for as unbelief rejects the offered Christ, so
faith alone receives him as a hand. If someone continues to ask how either the
Holy Spirit or faith can accomplish this when Christ's flesh and blood are in
heaven and we on earth, we do not take refuge in philosophical terms such as
*filling a place definitively or supernaturally*; nor do we dispute about the
endowments of a glorified body or turn the union of natures into confusion. We
say instead that although Christ's true body resides in heaven and is absent
from us in this respect, as is plain from Scripture, we nevertheless do not
doubt that the Lord is truthful. Therefore his members are as truly united
spiritually and through faith to their head—for which nothing is impossible—as
it is true that the symbols are communicated to us in a natural manner. Is
this really to search God's secrets curiously?

Nor, in saying this, do we make the use of the Sacraments superfluous or equate
the bare and simple Word with the Sacraments. Although we consider neither
another Christ, nor another communication in itself, nor another Spirit, nor
another faith in the simple Word than in the Sacraments, we nevertheless
confess the fact: when what is announced and offered to us through the Word is
also, by the Lord's institution and command, placed before our outward senses,
as it were, in express symbols, the Lord moves our faith much more openly—and,
unless our faithlessness bars every entrance to God's goodness, much more
effectually—than through the bare Word, and so joins himself ever more closely
to us. Finally, we judge that our union with Christ through the simple Word
differs from sacramental union only in degree,

[PDF page 19; printed page 228]

as they say. Is this to remove or weaken sacramental union? We say that
everything stated in Holy Scripture about the truth of Christ's body, the
Spirit and spiritual life, faith, the Word, and the Sacraments favors us—and so
too does the consensus of the whole ancient Church. If we consider our union
with Christ generally, we regard Christ's words in John 6—although that passage
discusses the simple Word apprehended by faith—as no less express than those
sacramental words. Nor does Paul say less expressly that in Baptism we put on
Christ, are buried and raised with Christ, and are baptized into one body than
he testifies elsewhere that the bread we break is a communion in the Lord's
body and the cup we bless a communion in his blood. Yet, as I said, we by no
means deny that these differ in efficacy according to degree. [Marginal
references: Galatians 3:27; Romans 6:4; 1 Corinthians 12:13; 10:16.]

What of Augustine? He certainly testifies that through Baptism believers
become partakers of Christ's body and blood. Jerome, writing to Hedibia, teaches
that in Baptism we feed upon Christ's body and blood. More expressly still, in
his comments on Ecclesiastes 3 he admonishes that it is profitable for us to be
satisfied with Christ's body and drink his blood, not only in the mystery but
also in the knowledge of Holy Scripture. This is our view of eating Christ's
flesh and drinking his blood.

What do you teach? You indeed admit a communion through faith and one that is
purely spiritual. But because you think that in the Sacrament of the Lord's
Supper Christ's present body is actually coupled with the bread and his present
blood with the wine, you establish a second, sacramental mode. In this mode it
is unnecessary to seek the Lord's body and blood in heaven; they are really
received with the bread itself. Consequently, whether or not one brings faith,
whoever receives the symbols actually receives what is also really present
with them—although those who bring faith receive unto salvation and those who
do not receive unto judgment. Finally, you want the body and blood to be
received in the Lord's Supper in no different way from the bread and wine,
excepting only two points: the former are received invisibly and whole, without
injury or bruising; the latter visibly, and are swallowed morsel by morsel or
particle by particle.

From this I think the subject of controversy is clear. It concerns neither the
matter of the Sacrament—for we confess it is the Lord's true body and blood—nor
its end and effect—for we concede that the symbols are given so that we may
truly become partakers of what they truly figure. Rather, it concerns whether
the words *receive*, *eat*, and *drink* in the Lord's Supper are to be taken
properly of the symbols but metaphorically of the things themselves, although
they are spoken with the greatest emphasis; or whether this eating and drinking
differs in kind from that attributed to the bare Word of God; or whether the
Lord wishes not only that we eat and drink Christ spiritually through faith,
but also that his body and blood be received within our bodies properly and
without a trope. From this question two others arise. One is whether unbelief
receives Christ rather than rejecting him. The other is not, as you
calumniously say, whether common sense permits it, but whether Scripture permits
us to say that Christ, even insofar as he is man, fills all things and is
simultaneously in heaven and on earth.

You should have occupied yourself in proving these things before growing angry
with us for not admitting the sacramental manner of communication you
establish. But you certainly follow your customary method, assuming the very
things in dispute. You do this especially in the present chapter, where you do
nothing but inveigh against us as men who posit bare symbols and curiously
search the Lord's secrets. I confess that I find nothing here which I think
needs an answer. I wished only to set out the sum of the controversy. First, I
ask learned and moderate men to consider whether it is the kind of controversy
for which we must be condemned as heretics, the Christian religion endangered,
and churches torn apart. Second, if anyone teaches us that we depart from the
truth, I dare promise two things in the name of our men. First, we will bring
no prejudgment to examining that person's arguments—whoever he may be—which
would prevent us from measuring and weighing everything sincerely according to
our capacity. Second, upon recognizing our error, of which we are certainly not
conscious, we will gladly embrace the truth and be deeply grateful to that man.

### In reply to chapter eighteen: On the Capernaïtes

To speak briefly, we call those Capernaïtes who recognize no metaphor in the
words *eating* and *drinking*. You remove this accusation from yourself in such
a way as to transfer it to us. You think the Capernaïtes erred not in believing
that Christ's truly and substantially present flesh was eaten and his blood
drunk, but in believing this would be done visibly and his flesh, cut into
pieces, carried into the stomach, whereas it is done invisi-

[PDF page 20; printed page 229]

bly and Christ's flesh is not mangled but eaten whole, without any injury or
harm. To defend your interpretation you produce a passage from Augustine where
he says the Capernaïtes thought Christ's body was ground by the teeth and
absorbed by the stomach, and another from Cyprian where he writes that they
thought they would be taught to eat Christ's flesh roasted or boiled and cut
limb by limb. As if Augustine had not first said generally that they thought
Christ's body was ground by the teeth and absorbed by the stomach; and Cyprian,
that it seemed horrible and wicked to them to feed upon human flesh. Who does
not see that what they add was supplied by way of vivid description, not to
restrict the gross opinion of the Capernaïtes to that one form?

But why discover the Capernaïtes' thought from someone else when we hear them
speak for themselves? “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” They plainly
meant that eating flesh itself horrified them; you are greatly mistaken if you
think they asked only about the manner in which the flesh would be eaten.
Accordingly, shortly afterward Christ declares this whole discourse allegorical
so far as eating and drinking are concerned. He does not say he will actually
be eaten substantially but invisibly and without injury; he says these things
are spiritual and concern a different kind of life. Hence Augustine too says:
“Why prepare your mouth and teeth? Believe, and you have eaten.” If according
to Augustine a Capernaïte is one who judges that Christ's flesh is ground by the
teeth, what will become of you, who agree with Pope Nicholas? Nicholas expressly
judges that after what he calls consecration Christ's body is not only handled
sacramentally but truly by priests' hands, broken, and ground by the teeth—and
that sensibly. [Marginal references: John 6:52,63; Augustine, *Tractates on
John* 25,27.]

You cannot evade by claiming that you do not think that papal synod understood
it so grossly, but only attributed to the body because of its presence what
properly fits flesh; nor can you object that you approved Nicholas's view only
conditionally. In those words truth is plainly distinguished from sign, and
the adverb *sensibly* is separately assigned to both. You know, Westphal, that
the very man whose many excellent virtues you should have delighted in instead
of his hyperboles added no exception when he wished that all popes had acted as
Christianly as Pope Nicholas acted toward Berengar in that confession. Act
with similar fidelity toward us, Westphal, and confess yourself as much a
Capernaïte as Pope Nicholas.

It is also worth hearing the reason you offer why Christ must be eaten whole
and in such a manner as to escape the teeth's edge. “For Christ's body,” you
say, “is adorned with heavenly glory, immortal, and free from all suffering.”
Did you really have to desert Pope Nicholas so suddenly? What if he were to
object that Christ's body was not yet glorified when he first gave himself to
his disciples, and that Christ is received now in no other manner than the
disciples then ate his flesh and drank his blood? I would add that those who
judge Christ to be swallowed whole and invisibly by the mouth seem to me not
much less gross than those who want him ground by the teeth before being
swallowed. They readily evade every inconvenience by positing that Christ is
whole in every part of the symbol no less than in the whole symbol. Those who
once depart from the right path must be carried ever farther toward the
precipice.

I find it utterly astonishing that you think it scarcely compatible with
Christ's glory for his body to be said to be ground by the teeth, although you
do not fear to say that, even if unto judgment, he is nevertheless received no
less truly and wholly by the mouths of the ungodly than of believers, and thus
is even introduced into ungodly bodies—unless perhaps you want him immediately
to vanish when received by the ungodly, or wish to number this absurdity too
among curious questions. Why refute the reasons why you number us instead
among the Capernaïtes? Namely, because we think fleshly about this mystery as
they did, although you admit that we utterly deny the corporeal manner of
presence which you cannot deny sharing with the Capernaïtes to some degree.
“They murmured,” you say, “and you too murmur.” On the contrary, we do not
murmur. With the angels we openly say that we seek Christ's body nowhere but
in heaven. Finally, to say everything at once, the Capernaïtes did not believe,
and, if you are to be believed, neither do we. I grant that we do not believe
what you believe together with the Capernaïtes. But I think no sane person would
believe you even under oath that we deserve accusation for the same unbelief
of which the Capernaïtes were guilty.

### In reply to chapter nineteen: On the argument drawn from the property of a human body

Among the other arguments by which we show that Christ's flesh and blood,
although truly communicated to us through faith in the Lord's Supper, are
nowhere but in heaven, we especially offer this: since Scripture testifies that
even after the resurrection the Lord's body truly consists of flesh and bones

[PDF page 21; printed page 230]

and is contained in heaven, it also appears necessary that the Lord's body is
not invisible or uncircumscribed, and therefore cannot be contained in
many places simultaneously—or rather, cannot be everywhere. You have determined
to overthrow this argument, but with what mighty reasons? First you complain
that we wrong you when we say that you remove the truth of a human body. We do
not write that you say this in so many words. We affirm—and here repeat—that you
posit an antecedent from which it necessarily follows. Otherwise demonstrate
to us that one and the same body consisting of true dimensions can be
everywhere.

We affirm that the capacity to be everywhere belongs not even to the angelic,
much less the human, nature, but only to the divine essence—and belongs to it as
a property in the fourth mode, as dialecticians speak. If the union of natures
is raised against us, we deny that the inference follows. We know that the
natures were united in such a way that the properties of each remained intact.
Thus what belongs properly to the divine nature will no more fit the human
nature than the reverse. If it were permissible to say that Christ's body is
everywhere, then—since the human nature is united with the divine no more and
no differently than the divine with the human, and since the Divinity was not
separated from the body in death itself—by the same argument it would be
permissible to say that Christ's Deity suffered, died, was buried, and indeed
rose again. These are plainly the doctrines of Eutyches.

The statements that the Son of Man was in heaven while speaking with Nicodemus,
that God acquired the Church with his blood, that Mary is *Theotokos*, and
others of this kind do not oppose us, for two reasons. First, it is certain
that these are spoken by the *communication of attributes*. This communication
does not exist in the thing itself—for if it did, that is, if what belongs
properly to the Deity actually belonged to the human nature, or the reverse,
there would be not union but confusion—but it is posited so that the unity of
the person may be understood. Second, as theologians teach, it is far different
to speak of the concrete, so to say, than the abstract. Hence by the
communication of attributes Mary is said to have conceived and borne God, but
not the Deity; and the Son of Man to have been in heaven while on earth, but
not the human nature, nor indeed his flesh or soul. [Marginal references: John
3:13; Acts 20:28.]

If the endowments of a glorified body are invoked, I first deny that this is
relevant, since it is certain that the body was not then glorified but at its
weakest when the Lord instituted his Supper. It seems to me absurd and plainly
Origenistic either to take refuge in some dispensation or *economy*, or to
think that the propositions “This is my body” and “This is my blood” were true
then in a different manner from the way they are now—especially since without
all these evasions our communion with Christ's flesh and blood does not cease
to be true in the manner we teach and that is necessary for salvation.
Finally, we know certainly that the ascension absorbed not the nature but the
infirmity of Christ's body. Let the temporal and perishing attributes—such as
grieving, suffering, dying, and even corporeal heaviness—be removed. Let the
natural ones remain: possessing body and soul, skin, hair, blood, flesh, marrow,
and bones, necessarily finite and visible.

Thus, to speak briefly—and indeed using the words of the man from whose
writings you should at least have learned our view before attacking it so
ignorantly—we confess that the whole Christ is everywhere if you understand him
personally, so to speak; yet we do not grant that everything belonging to
Christ is everywhere. Wherever the Son of God is, he is of course the one who
has the human nature joined to himself; yet he does not make that nature
actually present wherever he himself is. Its truth—to which he willingly
subjected himself—requires it to be circumscribed by its own limits and
contained in a definite place. Hence we also confess that the whole Christ is
offered to us in the Lord's Supper and truly apprehended by us through faith
and the Spirit. But for the true union of the members with their head we by no
means require Christ's body actually to penetrate either our bodies or our
souls.

You will cry that these things were drawn from the storehouse of philosophy
and human reason, and hurl the name *faith* at us, as if the question were
whether, whenever what God's Word signifies to us surpasses human understanding,
we ought to believe the one Word rather than every argument of every man. The
question instead is whether the corporeal manner of presence established from
the words “This is my body” agrees with the remaining testimonies about the
truth of Christ's flesh, his absence from us with respect to his human nature,
his ascension, and his remaining in heaven. If you show even one syllable in
which the Lord testifies that it is so, we will believe, even if common sense
opposes it a thousand times. But if we draw what we bring forward from the
Lord's Word alone, stop charging us with the very thing which unde-

[PDF page 22; printed page 231]

servedly can be retorted upon you. Otherwise, if everything dictated by common
sense must be rejected and Scripture's teaching agrees with common sense in
nothing—if, indeed, being a theologian means being stupid—then deny that God is
one and governs the world; deny that Christ was a true man, since Thomas was
compelled to confess it from touch itself. [Marginal reference: John
20:25–27.]

But come, let us examine your arguments more closely. Of the many testimonies
we cite you produce only two, and once you have reduced us to that few you deny
that our inference is valid. “For the Arians, Donatists, Pelagians, and
Anabaptists,” you say, “also cite far more, yet do not cease drawing their
inventions from the dregs of philosophy instead.” A fine and modest man you are!
You have plucked those flowers, as it were, from the catalogue of heretics in
order to weave us this garland: because, of course, Servetus would confess
Calvin to be an Arian if he returned to life; the papists profess us to be
Donatists and Pelagians; and countless Anabaptists profess us to be
Anabaptists. “But I do not mean this,” you will answer. Granted. Yet if you had
any shame you should have blushed to compare these monsters with us in any
way. See how learned you show yourself in thinking that the Donatists had
anything in common with the philosophers' doctrines, when they tended toward
the opposite side. You certainly would never have numbered Anabaptists among
philosophers if you had even greeted philosophy from the threshold. Those who
sometimes share conclusions do not always share principles.

But to the matter. Our men cite not only the passage from Thessalonians but
almost every other book of Scripture where the Son of Man is said to be coming
from heaven to judge the world. We say it follows from this that Christ,
insofar as he is man, is in heaven and not on earth; for no one is said to come
to the place where he already is, but to one from which he is absent. What do
you answer? You quarrel with us as though we denied that the whole Christ is
with his Church. We have partly stated our view of this in the present chapter
and will speak of it elsewhere. Then, in Christ's saying, “Then they will see
the Son of Man coming,” you admonish us to consider the word “THEY WILL SEE.”
You maintain that Christ's flesh is already present in the world no differently
from his Deity, because for you distinguishing Christ's natures is the same as
tearing Christ apart. You think he will be present then in no different manner
from now, except that he will then be visible to our eyes while now he is
invisible. Thus, if we believe you, the Lord will come with respect to form,
not substance. The form alone will be different, while the substance of his
flesh already fills all things just as the Deity itself does. Who will grant
you this unless you prove it from the Lord's Word? We think that certainly
cannot be done. [Marginal reference: Mark 13:26.]

The other passage is cited from Philippians, where Paul says Christ will
transform our body, which is now lowly, so that it is conformed to Christ's
glorious body. From this we say it follows that our bodies too will then fill
all things if this now befits the glory of Christ's body. You answer that the
glory of head and members differs. We agree, but by degree. The qualities
themselves must therefore agree in kind. It also follows that you are foolish
to imagine in Christ's glorious body a quality proper to the Deity and wholly
incommunicable, one which admits no more or less at all, as they say—such as
being everywhere, that is, being infinite and plainly God. You would speak a
little more aptly if you said this was peculiar to Christ because he alone is
both God and man. But this too appears sufficiently refuted by what we said
about the union rather than confusion of natures. [Marginal reference:
Philippians 3:21.]

You offer another explanation of this passage. You say Paul is not discussing
quantity and dimensions there, and also adds God's omnipotent power, which can
truly be advanced against our questions. To the former I answer briefly that
we take from Paul only what they call the antecedent. You must show that the
inference we draw from it is false. We will speak presently about the latter.

### In reply to chapter twenty: On Christ's entry to the disciples through closed doors

The ancients interpret the passage of John which says that the Lord came to
his disciples through closed doors and stood in their midst as a miracle, just
as when he walked upon the sea and burst from the tomb upon which the stone
lay, which the angel afterward rolled away. Calvin, however, prefers to think
the Evangelist mentions the closed doors

[PDF page 23; printed page 232]

to signify that they opened of their own accord for Christ as he approached.
You attack this as though explaining the passage differently from the ancients
were a crime, although you cannot produce even a syllable overturning Calvin's
interpretation. It does no violence either to the words themselves or their
meaning, nor does it remove the miracle from such an entrance. “But,” you say,
“the Evangelist does not expressly say the doors opened of their own accord.”
Nor does he say that Christ penetrated closed doors or walls, but that he came
and appeared among the disciples while they were together behind closed doors.

But let us grant what you desire so greatly. The very ancients you cite
unanimously affirm two things: that Christ had a true body, as he himself
testified by showing the marks of the nails; and that this happened by a
miracle, just as he also walked upon the waters. If it was a true body, it was
therefore not invisible and uncircumscribed, as you want it to be in,
under, or with the bread. And if it happened by a miracle—as we readily confess,
and as Calvin expressly asserts against those who imagine someone opened the
doors for Christ—this is therefore not the nature of a glorified body. This
inference is no stronger than if you contended that even before the resurrection
Christ's body was weightless by nature because Christ once walked upon the
waters. Indeed, the same may be affirmed of Peter's body if the inference is
valid. Finally, however we explain these things, even if we grant that after
the resurrection Christ's body became so fine, subtle, and spiritual that it
could penetrate the closed doors like air, will it follow that it can be
everywhere—that is, that it became God? Twist yourself into every shape: you
will never show that the capacity to be everywhere can belong to any nature
except the one which is infinite. Indeed, if Christ was already everywhere,
how is he said to have come to them? If we believe you, must we again say that
he is said to have come because he suddenly made himself visible from being
invisible? A remarkable trope indeed! I pass over what we proved abundantly in
the previous chapter: when the manner of presence in the Lord's Supper is the
question, a discussion of the endowments of a glorified body is irrelevant.
[Marginal references: John 20:19; 6:19.]

### In reply to chapter twenty-one: On the argument drawn from God's omnipotence

When you raised God's omnipotence, Calvin answered what no one can ever shake:
God is by no means subject to our inventions so that he must perform whatever
we imagine; rather, his power must be joined with his good pleasure, as the
Prophet admonishes: “Our God is in heaven; he has done whatever he willed.” How
do you answer, Westphal? Again you proclaim the greatness of faith and the truth
of God's oracles. Indeed, you compare us with the papists, who teach men to
doubt God's will and grace. As if the whole world did not know well enough what
we think about the authority of God's Word! You customarily make this single
objection: that the words whose interpretation is in question are sufficiently
clear. Yet you produce not even a syllable from Scripture reconciling your
exposition with the other passages of Scripture diametrically opposed to it.
Calvin therefore answers that this is nothing other than begging the question,
as they say, since one commits the supreme fault in argument by assuming as
sufficiently proved the very thing in dispute. “But I gladly seek the first
principle and source of the cause,” you say. “For from where, I ask, will we know
Christ's will more certainly than from his ordinance and Word?” Reader, when
you hear this, do you not marvel at the man's ignorance? [Marginal reference:
Psalm 115:3.]

Finally, you had written that Augustine ordinarily brings forward God's power
in miracles surpassing the human mind's grasp. Turning this very thing against
you, Calvin asks: if Augustine saw in the miracle of the Supper a proof of this
power of God, why did he nowhere admonish us with even a word that, contrary to
nature, Christ's invisible body lies hidden in the bread, fills heaven and
earth, and is whole in a thousand thousand places? What do you answer,
Westphal? You deny this inference: “Augustine was silent about this miracle;
therefore it does not exist.” As if it were unnecessary to show that you falsely
claim Augustine's authority. Even if this argument is not necessary, it is
highly probable, since nowhere would there have been a more fitting place to
proclaim God's power. But if the argument from silence does not satisfy you,
let Augustine's express words do so. He says that “religious signs can possess
honor, but not the amazement due to wonders.” If you wish, add these words of
Chrysostom: “It is dangerous to transfer these sanctified things to private
uses. They contain not Christ's true body but the mystery of his body.”

[PDF page 24; printed page 233]

### In reply to chapter twenty-three: On Christ's words, “Behold, I am with you always, to the consummation of the age”

Christ does not seem sufficiently present to his Church, as he promises here,
unless his flesh is truly and essentially coupled with the bread. Thus
throughout this chapter you do nothing but cry that we separate the members
from the head, deny that the whole Christ is present to us, and substitute the
Deity or the body's power for the body. How false these things are can be
abundantly clear from what we answered to chapter nineteen just above. Calvin
had sent you to Lombard's school so that there you might at last learn the
distinction between *the whole Christ* and *the whole of what belongs to
Christ*. Why should certain expressions of this kind not be retained in the
schools for teaching's sake if they agree with God's Word and promote clarity?
The matter itself shows that Calvin does not draw what he writes from the
sophists' dregs. You cannot bear this; yet you should have pardoned us for
thinking that Lombard would possess some authority with a man who does not fear
to summon Pope Nicholas to his aid.

As for this passage, hear, if you wish, what the ancients thought and, if you
can, reconcile their writings with your ubiquity while you trust in the defense
of invisibility. Augustine says: “According to his majesty, providence, and
ineffable grace, what he said is fulfilled: ‘Behold, I am with you to the
consummation of the age.’ But according to the flesh which the Word assumed,
according to the fact that he was born of the Virgin, seized by the Jews,
affixed to the tree, taken down from the cross, wrapped in linens, and manifested
in the resurrection: ‘You will not always have me with you.’”

Cyril likewise says: “Christ says that he will be with his disciples a short
time, not because he was going to depart from them entirely—for he is always
with us to the consummation of the age—but because he would not associate with
them as before. For the time was near when he would depart to the Father in
heaven.” I would be surprised if you did not press the phrase “associate with
them as before” to teach that this concerns the form of presence rather than
presence itself. Hear, then, what Cyril adds: “The faithful must believe that,
although he is absent from us in body, nevertheless all things and we ourselves
are governed by his power, and that he is always present to all who love him.
Therefore he said, ‘Wherever two or three are gathered in my name, there I am
in their midst.’ For just as, when he lived on earth as man, he also then filled
heaven and did not leave the company of angels, so now, although he is in heaven
with his flesh, he nevertheless fills the earth and is with those who love
him.” And shortly afterward he says: “He was going to depart only according to
the flesh, for he is always present by the power of the Deity, as we have said.”

Hear that the body is absent and does not fill the earth; that the flesh is
opposed not to invisibility but to the Deity; yet Christ is present to his own
by the power of his Deity—the whole Christ, surely, not half—and therefore in
his flesh according to another respect. Hence arose the true and suitable distinction
used in the schools between THE WHOLE CHRIST and THE WHOLE OF WHAT BELONGS TO
CHRIST.

Let us also hear Vigilius against Eutyches. “According to his humanity,” he
says, “the Son of God departed from us; according to his Divinity he says to
us, ‘Behold, I am with you to the consummation of the age.’” And shortly
afterward: “Those whom he left and from whom he departed in his humanity, he
neither left nor deserted in his Divinity. Through the form of a servant,
which he took from us into heaven, he is absent from us; through the form of
God, which does not depart from us, he is present to us on earth. Yet the one
and same person is both present and absent to us.”

Lest you evade by means of the word *form*, let us hear what the same author
writes elsewhere against the same Eutyches: “If the Word and flesh are one
nature, how is the flesh not found everywhere, since the Word is everywhere?
For when he was on earth, he was certainly not in heaven; and now because he is
in heaven, he is certainly not on earth. So far is he from being on earth that
according to that very flesh we await Christ's coming from heaven, whom
according to the Word we believe to be with us on earth. Thus according to you,
either the Word is contained in a place with his flesh or the flesh is
everywhere with the Word, since one nature does not receive anything contrary
or diverse into itself. Yet to be circumscribed in a place and to be everywhere
are diverse and utterly dissimilar. Because the Word is everywhere but his
flesh is not, it appears that the one and same Christ is of both natures: he is
everywhere according to the nature of the Deity and contained in a place
according to the nature of his humanity; he was created and has no beginning;
he is subject to death and cannot die.” He then adds: “This is the catholic
faith and confession which the Apostles delivered, the martyrs confirmed, and
the faithful preserve to this day.” Thus far it is not so much those ancient
teachers of the churches who speak as the Holy Spirit himself. If you have
determined to contend with them, at least cease opposing God's Word and the
authority of the ancients to us.

### In reply to chapter twenty-four: On God's promise in Exodus 20:24, “In every place where I cause my name to be remembered, I will come to you and bless you”

You say, Westphal, that you produced this passage not to establish a corporeal
presence of God, but to show that although God is everywhere according to his
essence, he is not everywhere present to us by grace, but only where he promised
to be with us. Therefore

[PDF page 25; printed page 234]

you think Calvin wronged you by taking these words as though they had been cited
concerning corporeal presence. You must pardon us for thinking you spoke more
relevantly. Why insist on matters about which there has never been any dispute?
Who among us ever taught that, with respect to Christ's grace, he must be sought
anywhere except where he reveals himself to us for salvation, that is, in the
Word and Sacraments? We therefore preferred to apply even this passage you cited
to the question at issue—the corporeal manner of presence—rather than think you
were wandering outside the course.

Yet I think what you yourself have just repeated makes it clear either that you
thought exactly what Calvin charged you with, or that you disagree with
yourself. You are not content with Calvin's statement that Jacob called Bethel
the gate of heaven because, aided by the vision, he learned to penetrate heaven
by faith. You want it added that Jacob had the truly present Son of God there
and heard him speaking with him—the same whom Paul testifies accompanied the
people of Israel in the wilderness. Since you are not satisfied that Jacob
penetrated heaven by faith, from where he also awaited the coming Messiah, tell
us, I ask—not how Christ could appear to Jacob in a vision, for that would be a
curious question, but what kind of presence this was. You are not content with
what we call a spiritual presence, which we say differs from his omnipresence
only because here a vision was employed to assist faith's weakness and testify
not only to God's presence but also his grace. Since, I say, you are not content
with this, what presence will you establish except a corporeal one? If so absurd
an invention does not please you, either show us some middle between these
extremes or confess with us what Calvin says: God revealed himself from the
beginning by visible symbols in order to lift the faithful by degrees to himself
and lead them through earthly rudiments to spiritual knowledge. [Marginal
reference: 1 Corinthians 10:4.]

How often do the Prophets rebuke as superstitious those who did not do this but
thought that, with respect to his essence, God was present in the temple
differently than in any other place? “But this does not concern us,” you say,
“for they wickedly trusted in their merits and works, which we do not do.” It
is true that they are rebuked as superstitious on that account too, but I deny
that this reason concerns the present matter. Those who brought no opinion of
merits were nonetheless superstitious if they imagined that, with respect to
his essence, God was present in the temple differently from any other place—
although he willed sacrifices to be made and solemn prayers offered only in the
temple, to testify to his grace there by definite symbols, and to present
himself as though to be beheld by their very eyes. You should instead have
shown that the end and rationale of our Sacraments is not what that of the
ancient mysteries was. I will freely grant you many differences, but not to
such a degree that I will say Christ, with respect to his flesh, is any more on
earth after it was taken into heaven than before he assumed it—unless you teach
us this from the divine Word, to which, behold, we profess with both mouth and
heart that we assent in all things.

But let us consider how deservedly you accuse Calvin in this chapter of
maliciously perverting what you wrote. You had written: “Wherever God has
promised that he will be present, and where he exhibits himself in the Word and
Sacraments, let us not hesitate that heaven and the house of God are there, and
that Christ is found there.” Calvin inferred that you think heaven and the house
of God are in the Sacraments and that Christ is certainly found there. How do
you answer? “The particle *there*,” you say, “should not have been referred to
the Sacraments, but to the preceding adverb *wherever*. The construction should
be: ‘Heaven and the house of God are there, wherever he promised that he would
be present, where he exhibits himself as present through Word and Sacraments.’”
Pardon us again for thinking that you were speaking of the Word and Sacraments
themselves rather than the Church. Where does God exhibit himself as present
through Word and Sacraments except in the Church? If you understand the bread
or wine, you act just as though you said Christ must be sought *wherever*, that
is, in whatever Sacraments he exhibits himself as present in Word and
Sacraments.

We have never doubted that God communicates this grace of his in Christ only
with his Church through the preaching of the Word and administration of the
Sacraments. Nor do we enclose God in heaven. Nevertheless, from Scripture we
have learned that heaven is the place into which Christ ascended according to
the flesh, toward which we aspire, in which he reigns, and from which through
the Holy Spirit he joins himself to his members to quicken the Church's body
and each of its members. We certainly cannot place that heaven everywhere or
seek it on earth in the symbols of bread and wine unless someone demonstrates
from the Lord's Word that we must do so.

### In reply to chapter twenty-five: On Christ's promise, “My Father and I will come to him”

Since you had also applied this passage to your presence of Christ's flesh,

[PDF page 26; printed page 235]

Calvin asked whether you think the essence of the Deity descends to us as you
affirm of Christ's flesh. You are compelled either to admit that you think so,
or that you should have distinguished—though not separated—the Father's presence
from the Son's. Now if you distinguish them by saying that the Father is said
to come and dwell in us in one manner—only by the power of the Deity and
spiritual activity, because he is God alone, so that although he otherwise
fills all things with his essence, the saying is understood only of his saving
efficacy in the saints—while the Son, because he is God and man, is also present
substantially and really in an invisible manner, who does not see that this is
a plainly forced interpretation of the saying? It is openly refuted by the
other passages which testify that Christ carried his flesh away from us into
heaven. Thus, although we say that with respect to his flesh we are made one
with Christ, and Christ considered distinctly in this respect is said to dwell
in us differently from the Father, we nevertheless think that Father and
Christ alike offer themselves to us spiritually and through faith, both in the
Word and Sacraments, so that, having been made one with Christ, we may truly
have the same Father with him, the same kingdom, and the same eternal life.
As for what you argue in this chapter against us about the union of natures—as
though by implication we do not distinguish but separate them, although by
implication you seem to us not to unite but confuse them—how truly these things
are said against us can be understood especially from our reply to chapter
nineteen.

### In reply to chapter twenty-six: On Christ's invisible presence in the Eucharist

In this chapter you are certainly not carried above the heavens nor, as the
poet says, “strike the stars with uplifted head,” but crawl upon the ground—and
in a manner which can neither be seen nor even perceived. In the previous
chapter you had said that you believe with Fulgentius that Christ's flesh is
local, but with this interpretation: “That is,” you say, “it can be circumscribed
in the manner of bodies.” Thus, if we believe you, Christ's humanity is called
circumscribed like ours not because it is such in itself, but because it can be
such whenever it pleases—or, lest you complain that we fasten upon every
syllable, because it is indeed such by its own nature but can also become
invisible whenever it pleases. Yet why dispute with you about an invisible
manner of presence when you posit that Christ's body is not only invisible but
also everywhere? Our present dispute about whether Christ's body can be joined
actually and substantially with the bread in an invisible manner is therefore
conducted by way of concession.

You say that Augustine posits a twofold manner of presence. I freely admit it;
why deny the fact? But we steadfastly deny that Augustine posits the manner of
presence you make twofold—namely, that the essence of Christ's flesh is now
actually on earth as well as in heaven. Indeed, we say that whoever claims this
opposes manifest truth as shamelessly and stubbornly as one who denied that the
sun rises for us on the summer solstice. I hope this will appear partly from
what we said to chapter twenty-three and partly from what we will discuss in
its place concerning Augustine's view. Let us now see what arguments you
produce.

You cite Christ's words, “Behold, I am with you”; likewise, “Where two or three
are gathered”; and that saying of yours repeated a thousand times, “This is my
body.” What can be proved from these? Precisely the single thing in which we
place our hope: that Christ, the God-man, is truly present to believers by his
Spirit's power, so that by the same power he gives life to and preserves
eternally those joined to him through faith, as he truly testifies in his Word
and truly figures by employing the Sacraments. We leave to you your fleshly
manner of presence on earth, since we prefer with the angels to seek and find
Christ in heaven.

You add Christ's words, “I go to the Father, and now you will not see me”; and
that “Christ was taken from his disciples' eyes.” Thus you think *going to the
Father* and *ascending into heaven* mean nothing other than ceasing to appear
among men in visible form, not actually departing from us with respect to his
flesh. You also refer here what the angels say about Christ's advent, that he
will come as he ascended, as though this referred not to the flesh's presence
itself but its manner of presence—as if they said he would then become visible
again from being invisible, just as after the ascension he became invisible
from being visible. Pardon us, we ask, if we do not regard Christ as an
illusionist who pretends to depart in his flesh from a place where he actually
remains, and feigns that he will return in his flesh to a place from which he
never departed. We also wonder how a man so hostile to every trope can in this
controversy refer the words *descend*, *ascend*, *be lifted up*, *be taken from
the eyes*, *come*, and *return* so improperly, not to the essence itself but to
form, not to what the fact is but to what is or is not seen. Otherwise,

[PDF page 27; printed page 236]

know this: although we believe that wherever Christ's body actually and
essentially is, it is visible by its nature—since it is a true body—and therefore
now truly and essentially in heaven, not on earth, nevertheless (as I have now
repeated a thousand times) we neither deny the presence of Christ's flesh with
respect to true communion nor establish empty symbols. Through these we do not
doubt that it is truly figured to the outward senses that God inwardly, by the
Holy Spirit's power, most truly and effectually performs for us as by faith we
ascend into heaven: namely, that, being made one with our head, we truly become
partakers of eternal life in him.

You next employ marvelous arguments: “He passed through his enemies invisibly;
therefore he is now invisible in the Lord's Supper.” But if I deny the
antecedent—as indeed I do—how will you prove it? Becoming invisible signifies
not changing location but changing the form and appearance of something visible
into one which cannot be seen. From Luke's account of the history nothing more
can be inferred than that Christ openly passed safely and unharmed through the
midst of his adversaries when their hands were restrained by God's wonderful
power. Thus, when you contend that Christ's body became invisible in this way,
who does not see that you act like those who, placed in some extreme danger,
rashly grasp at anything? [Marginal reference: Luke 4:30.]

Your second argument is of the same kind: “He penetrated closed doors;
therefore his body is invisible.” If I deny the inference, how will you prove
it? Because one corporeal mass appears to have yielded? Even if I granted this—
which we showed in its place is unnecessary—who taught you that the miracle was
performed in Christ's body rather than the doors? However it occurred, even if
the mass of one body temporarily yielded to the mass of another, or one body
yielded to the other in a way by no means difficult for Christ, does it follow
that either Christ's body or the wood from which the doors were constructed had
no mass?

The third argument is similar: “He vanished from the disciples' eyes; therefore
he became invisible. For his body was there and yet was not seen.” You should
have consulted the Greek text, where no mention of eyes is made. It says not
“he became invisible *to them*,” but “he vanished *from them*.” Thus it does not
signify that Christ, without changing location, became invisible from being
visible, as you falsely and ignorantly infer, but that suddenly and in one
moment he withdrew himself from them. Among the Greeks it is a common manner
of speech to say that those who die, or cease to be seen by men, “vanish from
human society.” Indeed, those expelled from a place are said to “vanish” from
it because they are no longer seen there, as everyone not wholly ignorant of
Greek knows.

Even if we admit that Christ withdrew himself suddenly and, if you please,
miraculously, will we rightly infer that his glorified body—doubtless much more
agile than before—became invisible? Even if we inferred it, how often have we
now said that the question of a glorified body's endowments does not concern
this matter? At last you therefore flee to your hiding place, God's
omnipotence, as though we disputed that rather than God's good will revealed in
his Word.

Shortly afterward you nevertheless emerge to engage again and cite Paul's
passage, “We now walk not by sight or appearance, but by faith,” which in your
interpretation means: “While we sojourn in this world, he deals with us covered
under an alien appearance.” A splendid interpretation indeed! Paul testifies
that as long as we live here we are absent from the Lord, whom, though absent,
we nevertheless behold by faith in Word and Sacraments. You, on the contrary,
deny that we are absent from the Lord—for if he is present, whether visible or
not, we cannot be said to be absent from him—and instead say the Lord is absent
from us, not properly, but because he is invisible and lies hidden under a
covering. What will happen when, having received our bodies, we reign with him?
“Then,” you say, “we will see him face to face”—that is, he will no longer be
invisible. I would surely believe this metamorphosis Ovidian rather than
Christian. [Marginal references: 2 Corinthians 5:6–7; 1 Corinthians 13:12.]

What more? Calvin presses Augustine's saying that bodies do not exist if
spatial extension is removed. What could you do here, when Augustine speaks
this way about Christ's own body? You now openly desert Augustine, whom you
nevertheless tried in so many ways to draw to your side, and to evade the
saying you contend it is physical and philosophical. Finally, you say you
press us more weightily with Christ's Word, truth, and power. This is familiar
practice for you: assume the very point in question, then, if you cannot
extricate something, object that it is taken from human philosophy. Thus you
appeal from Augustine's saying to what Augustine asserted not from physics but
Scripture: namely, that Christ entered through closed doors and left the tomb
while it was closed by the mass of the stone.

[PDF page 28; printed page 237]

You then exclaim, “Where was the place, where the space?” I answer that there
was a place and space where Christ's true body then was. “Then two bodies were
simultaneously in the same space,” you will say. What? If an invisible body is
posited, will this difficulty be avoided? Meanwhile, what are you doing but
suddenly slipping down to physical principles yourself? But there is no need.
What prevents the wooden body from yielding momentarily to Christ's body and
then being joined again? This is where the miracle lies, just as, contrariwise,
the water was made solid so that it did not flow away beneath Christ's feet
when he walked upon it even before his glorification. Who would therefore
assign Christ a body so fine that it escaped every sight? Indeed, even if we
granted this, would it also be everywhere because it was invisible, as you
contend?

### In reply to chapter twenty-nine: On the angel's words, “He has risen; he is not here”

[Marginal reference: Mark 16:6.]

If we believe you, we wish to draw even the angels into defending our error
when we say they testify that Christ's body was not in the tomb, from which we
infer that it is not everywhere. How will you escape? You invent this argument
for us: “It is not in the tomb; therefore it is not in the Lord's Supper.” We
instead argue as we said just above, and you will no sooner prove our inference
invalid than demonstrate that the sun does not shine at midday. Thus everything
you build upon a foundation we did not lay is ridiculous. Although we certainly
grant that Christ's flesh is neither in the Supper nor in the tomb as you
demand, but only in heaven, where it withdrew.

It is worth seeing how you extricate yourself. “The women,” you say, “sought
Christ's lifeless body.” Thus, if we believe you, the angels do not simply deny
that Christ's body is in the tomb, but deny that it is lifeless there because
it was alive there. If so, it certainly has not yet emerged from the tomb,
since it is invisibly everywhere. And you marvel that we say you bury Christ?
“But they sought without the Word,” you say, “indeed against the Word.” The
question is not whether the women acted rightly in seeking Christ's flesh in
the tomb, but whether it was where they sought it. The angels simply and
absolutely deny this. Yet because of the unity of the person you affirm that
Christ is substantially everywhere according to the flesh—just as if the angels
denied his presence only on the women's hypothesis and said: “He is indeed
here, although you do not see him; but you will see him”—that is, he will at
last become visible to you—“in Galilee.” What is twisting the Lord's Word if this
is not?

### In reply to chapter thirty: On Christ's words, “The flesh profits nothing”

[Marginal reference: John 6:63.]

If this passage of John may be interpreted only in the manner Chrysostom
understood it, what standing will remain for the other interpreters? Augustine's
explanation especially pleases me. He shows that Christ indicates that his
flesh, considered distinctly and by itself—as it was considered by those with
whom he had held the preceding discourse—is by no means life-giving, but has all
this power from the Spirit, that is, from the power of the Divinity dwelling
corporeally in it. For Christ was not like other men, but was man in such a way
that he was simultaneously the author of life and salvation, because he was the
Son of God. Why did Christ say this? To disclose to them why he had called his
flesh true food, and then to teach them that this eating is spiritual and
concerns eternal life. He immediately explained the latter point when he added,
“The words which I speak to you are spirit and life.” This interpretation, I
say, appears to me most natural. If you think you may decline to follow
Augustine, why may we not use our own judgment by the same right? Neither
exposition introduces a new doctrine into the Church. But if Chrysostom's view
is admitted, against whom are those words spoken if not those who think
Christ's body is eaten essentially and corporeally with the bread, even without
faith? We spoke of these matters more fully in our reply to chapter eighteen.

### In reply to chapter thirty-one: On the Lord's words, “I go to the Father,” “I leave the world,” and “I am no longer in the world”

I beg you, Westphal, to determine from this chapter alone into what precipices
you prefer to rush in order stubbornly to retain your opinion of Christ's
body's ubiquity, rather than—I do not say learn the truth from us, for perhaps
that would be too arrogant—recognize the truth together with us by mutual
comparison. Because you acknowledge no trope either in the words *eat* and
*drink* or in explaining sacramental speech, see how many improper metaphors
you not only admit but even invent. They are entirely unknown to every author,
sacred and profane alike, and indeed pave the way to manifest impiety—the error
of Eutyches.

[PDF page 29; printed page 238]

For you, “leaving the world,” “passing out of the world,” “going to the Father,”
and “entering his glory” mean nothing other than suffering, dying, rising from
the dead, and putting off bodily weakness so that a body formerly visible and
consisting of its own dimensions suddenly becomes divine, invisible, indeed
not only uncircumscribed, but filling earth and heaven.

First I answer—as I have often said—that the question of a glorified body's
endowments does not seem relevant to the question at issue unless it is first
proved, which we do not think can be proved from God's Word or any orthodox
interpreter, that Christ's body is in the Supper after glorification differently
from the way it was when the Lord instituted it before glorification. Second,
since you compel me to enter this question, I ask you and your entire party
first to teach me what it means to become God if this is not it; and then to
remember how often you have charged us that when the Lord consoled his disciples
at that time he wished, more than ever, to use plain and perspicuous speech—as
the disciples themselves confess.

What is plainer than *leave*, *go away*, *depart*, and similar terms repeated a
hundred times in that final discourse before his death? If these words are not
referred to a change of location, but with you we posit that they declare only
a change of form and qualities, what kind of trope is this? By what passage of
Scripture, reason, or authority will it be confirmed? What else will this
entire excellent discourse of Christ, and indeed his ascension itself, be but
mere illusion? We confess that Christ ascended into heaven only through death
and resurrection. Will we therefore establish that *suffer*, *die*, *be buried*,
and *rise* are synonyms with *go away from the world*, *pass over*, *depart to
the Father*, *ascend into heaven*, and *enter into glory*? Certainly not, since
the former must precede and the latter follow.

Your statement that in chapter thirteen John explains Christ's passing out of
the world to the Father as his passion is false—unless you think *passing out of
the world to the Father* means nothing more than going to death, an idea which,
I dare say, never entered the mind of anyone sufficiently sane. What shall we
make of your confusing the words to Peter, “Where I go, you cannot follow me
now, but you will follow afterward,” with Christ's saying, “I go away to the
Father”? These two are plainly distinguished throughout Christ's discourse, so
that the verb *go away* sometimes concerns the journey itself—the cross and
death—and sometimes the journey's destination—heaven and the Father. What point
was there in repeating so often the coming Paraclete's presence if Christ was
not really going to depart from them but only become invisible? “He indicated
the fruit of his passion,” you say, “when he said, ‘It is expedient for you that
I go away; for if I do not go away, the Paraclete will not come.’” By what trope
will we say that *going away* means nothing other than becoming glorious—that
is, in your interpretation, invisible and uncircumscribed?

“But Christ did not say, ‘I will not be with you,’ but ‘You will not see me,’”
you object. What kind of argument is this? Christ joins these two: “I go to the
Father” and “You will no longer see me.” Yet these are not plain enough for you
to remove the corporeal manner of presence. You would have wished him to add,
“And I am no longer with you.” Then add it from what precedes: “Little children,”
Christ says, “I am with you yet a little while.” Likewise: “I did not tell you
these things from the beginning because I was with you; but now I go to him who
sent me.” What more do you require? You will again flee to your invisibility.

Finally, we must see how you twist Christ's words: “Now I am not in the world,
and they are in the world, and I come to you”; likewise, “While I was with them
in the world, I kept them in your name”; and “I came from the Father and entered
the world; again I leave the world and depart to the Father.” “By *world*,” you
say, “let us understand the form of the world, so that leaving the world means
leaving what weighs down and grieves in this world, putting off mortality,
contempt, and the world's ignominy.” What more? “It means leaving certain bodily
properties, such as being circumscribed and confined in a place and seen by
the eyes.” A marvelous exposition indeed. I everywhere find *world* used for
the terrestrial globe; indiscriminately for the people who inhabit the earth;
for those who disregard heaven and are the devil's slaves; sometimes for the
perverse wickedness and vices which hold men captive; and indeed for everything
that opposes God's Spirit. I never find it with the meaning you assign.

I do not doubt that Christ put off all bodily infirmity. The Lord himself
denies that he put off the natural qualities without which flesh is not flesh
and bones are not bones; we assent to him rather than you. For conversely, in
chapter twenty-five you believe Christ's flesh to be local so that it may be
circumscribed in the manner of bodies whenever it pleases. We ask you to
reconcile this putting off of a visible and circumscribed nature with what you
yourself wrote in chapter twenty-one, when you

believed

[PDF page 30; printed page 239]

and said that Jesus gave and gives his disciples his body and blood without
change to his body and blood. What is change, if a person is not changed who
becomes invisible by nature from visible and, instead of consisting of definite
dimensions, becomes uncircumscribed by nature—indeed, so as to be
everywhere? “But it does not cease to be flesh and blood,” you say. Of course—
just as if I contended that a body lived while admitting that its soul had been
separated from it.

Finally, you answer Christ's saying, “You will not always have me with you,” in
this way: “We do not have him as he was among his disciples during the time of
his dispensation, namely poor and mortal.” We freely confess that Christ is now
different with respect to the infirmity of his flesh. But if he did not truly
and actually depart from us into heaven with respect to his human nature, why
did the Lord say simply to his disciples, “You will not always have me with
you”? If Christ's entire life on earth was nothing but a certain dispensation
or *economy*, as they call it, what prevents us from passing over to the party
of Marcion, Valentinus, Eutyches, and other such monsters? To affirm that
Christ's body is true, yet say it is so invisible and uncircumscribed
that it is everywhere and was visible only by a dispensation—what else is this
than to posit the antecedent and deny the consequent? To call a body not a
body? Finally, to wrest from us the only weapons by which we can defeat those
heretics? What could they not evade by that tergiversation?

The ancients certainly did not speak so, but with perpetual consensus—Augustine,
Cyril, Vigilius, whose words we recited above in reply to chapter twenty-five.
Indeed, what orthodox writer ever said or wrote that Christ became invisible
from visible, or that the human nature was absorbed by the Deity or is
everywhere? Did they not instead testify that, his true, visible, locally
circumscribed flesh having truly been taken into heaven, Christ is now absent
with respect to his human nature, while by his power, as he promised, he rules
and governs his Church both generally and in every member—joining us to himself
and truly feeding us unto eternal life, as he announces in his Word and truly
testifies in the Sacraments?

### In reply to chapter thirty-two: On the Apostle's words, “They ate the same spiritual food and drank the same spiritual drink”

[Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians 10:3–4.]

If anyone wonders why in this chapter I do not follow the best method—first
discussing the genus, the ancients' Sacraments under the Law, and then the
species, the manna and drink from the rock—let him know that I preferred to
follow your footsteps, Westphal. I do so not because I approve the order you
established, but so that your arguments may be compared more readily with our
answers. There are three points in explaining this passage on which we disagree.
First, you contend that in Paul's words “THE SAME FOOD” and “THE SAME DRINK,”
the pronoun “THE SAME” refers not to a comparison between us and the Israelites,
as we maintain, but between the Israelites themselves, signifying that all
Israelites, faithful and unbelieving alike, used the same food and drink.
Second, you interpret spiritual food and drink differently from us. Finally,
you do not think that in this passage of Paul the manna and rock are presented
as Sacraments corresponding to our Eucharist, but only as types and figures of
certain Sacraments of the New Testament Church—and this not by their own nature
but by Paul's “induction,” as you put it, because Paul chose to apply them this
way.

To the first I answer that there are many serious reasons why we think the
pronoun “THE SAME” compares the Israelites with us rather than with one another.
Since Paul determined to recall the Corinthians from idolatrous banquets by the
Israelites' example, let us consider how much stronger is the argument we frame
than yours. You think Paul argues thus: “God bestowed great benefits upon the
Fathers, performed great miracles for them, gave them manna from heaven and
drink from the rock. Yet, being ungrateful, they paid the penalty. Likewise,
although God heaps great benefits upon you, if you are ungrateful you will not
escape severe punishment.” In these words of yours, I ask, what force do the
word “ALL” and the pronoun “THE SAME” have? Certainly none, unless perhaps you
omitted it. I hope you cannot deny that I have transcribed your words faithfully.

Let us therefore assist you and adorn your argument this way: all the Jews had
the same spiritual food and drink and the passage through the sea, yet not all
pleased God; those who defected to idolatry were grievously punished. Likewise,
although you Corinthians are partakers of the same mysteries among yourselves,
you will nevertheless pay penalties to God if you pollute yourselves with
profane banquets. Yet if one argues this way, who does not see the ready answer?
We possess far greater testimonies of our reconciliation with God—namely, the
Sacraments—which make us far dearer to God than for him to act so se-

[PDF page 31; printed page 240]

verely toward us. Moreover, if the Apostle had wished to compare the Israelites
with us only in this respect, that they received benefits from the Lord as we
do, and therefore that just as their faithlessness destroyed most of them, so
we too will not be spared—if, I say, this alone was his purpose, and not also to
compare the mysteries themselves, theirs and ours—why did he mention Baptism,
and likewise food and drink with express reference to the Holy Supper? Finally,
why did he expressly add, “The rock was Christ”? When Christ himself in John
calls himself the living bread which descended from heaven, does he not say the
same about the manna which Paul says here when he adorns it with the epithet
*spiritual food*? [Marginal reference: John 6:51.]

We therefore frame the argument this way instead. Do not be proud, Corinthians,
because you have given your name to Christ and are partakers of his mysteries.
Do not abuse this benefit of God as though an idolaters' banquet could not
pollute your conscience once you have been devoted to God. The Israelites too
could formerly have claimed the same, since through Moses' ministry they
received the same Baptism as you with respect to the thing itself, though
different signs were employed: the sea, which signified outward washing, and
the cloud, which signified the inward Baptism of the Holy Spirit. Likewise they
ate the same spiritual food as you and drank the same drink—namely Christ, whom
the manna and the water flowing from the rock represented to them. The rock
itself truly figured the same Christ offering himself to them for food and
drink. What happened to them? They all received those Sacraments, yet most were
laid low for the very thing of which I accuse you: craving flesh contrary to
God's Word and polluting themselves with a profane banquet before the golden
calf. Thus you must beware lest, although all of you are partakers of God's
mysteries, the same thing, neglected through the same crime, bring the same
destruction upon most of you. This is Paul's argument—much more forceful and
solid, it seems to me, and more consonant with Paul's own words than the one you
offer. So much for the first difficulty.

Let us come to the second: why is the manna called spiritual food and the rock
spiritual drink? You offer two reasons. The first is “because this food and
drink were supplied to them not naturally but by God's power.” I would like
this interpretation confirmed by at least one example, unless the spiritual
use for which they were created is also added, as Ambrose by no means omitted.
Otherwise, if that view is valid, why would I not also call the quails spiritual,
and everything else God does outside nature's order? Your second reason is
“because those who believed ate spiritually.” If this satisfies, what else
follows but what you are least willing to concede: not all ate the spiritual
food or drank the spiritual drink, if they are called spiritual only with
respect to those who believed, since not all believed? Moreover, if you admit
they were spiritual for those who received them spiritually, why soon afterward
in this same chapter do you contend that these things were nothing but shadows
and types, from which you also separate Christ? Indeed, in chapter six how
stubbornly you contended that the epithet *spiritual* added to the rock fits not
the corporeal rock itself but Christ! Or do you think the epithet must be
explained differently in the words *food* and *drink* than in *manna* and
*rock*?

Away with these things. Let us establish that the food, drink, and rock are
called spiritual from the second use for which they were destined: not to feed
bodies, but souls. That is, they were true and certain Sacraments for believers
and unbelievers alike of spiritual things—the Lord's body and blood—unto eternal
life. In this sense all are said to have received spiritual food and drink,
although believers alone perceived the thing itself. It is one matter to
determine what a thing is in itself and another to ask what someone perceives
from it. Not only reason itself and usage, but—what may surprise you more—the
very words of Ambrose you cite for your side plainly confirm this explanation
of the epithet. He says they are called spiritual “because they were prepared
not according to the world's law, but created for a time by God's power,
containing in themselves a figure of the future mystery which we now receive in
commemoration of Christ the Lord.” So much for this point.

Let us at last come to the third question: in what sense we call the manna and
rock Sacraments, and to what extent Paul compares them with ours. You maintain
that they were not properly Sacraments but only miracles which Paul presents as
types and figures of our Sacraments—as if they were not even figures by the
Lord's institution, but only by Paul's “induction,” as you put it. From this you
elicit two things. First, they cannot be compared with our Sacraments, since
the outward things—the manna and

[PDF page 32; printed page 241]

water from the rock—were granted to the Fathers by a greater miracle than the
bread and wine are to us in the Lord's Supper, while conversely greater
spiritual gifts are conferred upon us. Second, it cannot be inferred from this
that only symbols are given in the Lord's Supper which figure our spiritual
communion with Christ. We say those conclusions follow well, provided the first
foundation is firm. Let us see by what reasons you confirm it.

You cite a passage of Chrysostom where he compares the figure—the manna given
to the Fathers—with the truth exhibited to us, showing a great analogy between
them. How does this oppose us? We by no means deny that truth excels figures
and the body shadows; we ask wherein the distinction consists. You also cite
two passages from Cyril. One tends to the same point as the preceding opinion
of Chrysostom; the other you either do not understand or twist against us in
bad faith. In John 6 the Lord speaks of the manna from the Jews' hypothesis—
they regarded nothing in it beyond the outward substance which filled the
stomach. Therefore, comparing it as corruptible food with the true food, he
assigns it nothing more than any food by which a person can be nourished only
temporarily. Why is it surprising if Cyril offers similar statements when
explaining that passage? Had he spoken about the manna according to its
spiritual use, who thinks he would have used different language from Paul, who
calls it spiritual food?

Thus, elsewhere Paul, evaluating Circumcision according to its true use, calls
it the seal of the righteousness of faith; but from the false apostles'
perspective he contemptuously calls it mutilation and denies that Christ
profits those who are circumcised. He utterly depreciates outward circumcision
when he does not join it with the truth—circumcision of the heart—but instead
opposes the outward to the inward, as those he determined to refute did. You,
however, distinguishing nothing, heap everything together. Meanwhile you fail
to notice that if your suspicion were true, Cyril would be brought into
conflict not with us but Paul himself. The same judgment applies to the passage
of Ambrose you cite from the book *On Those Who Are Initiated into the
Mysteries*. There he considers the manna not insofar as it was a Sacrament or
spiritual food, but as food intended to sustain this perishing life temporarily,
just as if I compared daily bread either with the mystical bread or the Lord's
body itself. [Marginal references: Romans 4:11; Philippians 3:2; Galatians
5:2.]

Since you offer no further arguments, who will grant what you demand when it
wholly contradicts Paul's view? Indeed, your proof also limps because you offer
no argument at all by which one could understand that the manna and rock became
figures of the Sacraments solely by Paul's induction, as you say, rather than
by the Lord's own institution—much less that they are Sacraments. How will you
prove this when not only Paul's own words but all the ancient interpreters must
be your adversaries? We demonstrated just above that the Apostle's proper
purpose was to announce a similar punishment to the Corinthians for contempt
of similar things, and therefore that Sacraments are compared with Sacraments
and called the same spiritual food and drink. Moreover, from what does the
dignity of either figures—if you prefer that name—or Sacraments and mysteries
depend? Certainly from the nature of the thing they figure. The Apostle
expressly testifies that the rock, and therefore the manna too, was a Sacrament
of Christ. Show, then, that there is something more worthy and august in the
Lord's Supper than Christ, or permit us to equate these Sacraments to that
extent with respect to the thing itself. Why should we not do so with the
Apostle, who for this very reason adorns the crossing of the sea and the
overshadowing of the cloud with the name *Baptism*?

“But the Apostle says these things were types.” Quite so. First, I deny that
the Apostle calls either the sea, the cloud, the manna, or the rock types.
“What, then?” you will ask. The fact that God did not approve most of them and
that they were laid low in the wilderness. The sequence of the context, the
Apostle's purpose, and the words themselves all show this. The Apostle does not
say these were types presented to the Israelites, but that they are types for
us. To what end? “That we should not desire evil things as they desired,” he
says, for otherwise we will undergo a similar judgment. But even if we grant
that they can be called types with respect to us, since we behold in them the
antitypes, as it were, of our Sacraments, will they therefore cease to be true
Sacraments for the Israelites themselves? [Marginal reference: 1 Corinthians
10:5–6.]

So much for the third question. Although when its foundation is overturned
both conclusions you built upon it must fall, I think the matter deserves
closer consideration so that we may know what you teach generally about
comparing the ancient Sacraments with ours. First of all you teach that a
Sacrament must be defined. Quite right; but why

[PDF page 33; printed page 242]

did you not follow that order yourself? We nevertheless shall. You say some
things are called Sacraments generally—namely, signs which in some manner testify
to grace and confirm faith in Christ. You want the rites and figures of the Law
and the various miracles shown to the Israelites to belong to this kind. Other
Sacraments, however, were specially instituted by the Lord and have attached
to them a promise of grace, forgiveness of sins, and salvation. You maintain
that under the Law Circumcision alone was of this kind, while you acknowledge
that all the ancients now number only two. First, I condemn this division as
defective; second, because indiscriminately you think miracles form one species
of Sacraments together with the rites of the Law; and finally, because its
members collapse into one. What difference is there between Sacraments which
by God's institution confirm faith and testify to grace, and those which have
attached to them a promise of forgiveness, when neither grace nor faith can be
separated from the promise?

I therefore distinguish what are called types in a peculiar sense from what
are properly called Sacraments. Thus we say that Isaac's sacrifice foreshadowed
Christ's death, David in many respects foreshadowed Christ, and the image of
the two covenants existed in Abraham's one household. These and similar things
are called types and figures sometimes with respect to those men, but at other
times with respect to us rather than them. Next, in this discussion I
distinguish Sacraments from signs destined either to signify certain peculiar
things or confirm promises: Pharaoh's seven cows and ears of grain, the burning
bush, Moses' hand at one time leprous and at another sound, Gideon's fleece,
the Prophets' many visions, and everything similar. [Marginal references:
Genesis 41; Exodus 3, 4; Judges 6.]

Finally, I properly call Sacraments everything instituted by the Lord to place
before the Church's eyes, not as empty pictures but as sure and effective signs,
that grace of eternal life in Christ, so that, faith being kindled, we may
become ever greater partakers of Christ and his gifts. We affirm that under the
Law Sacraments of this kind included not only Circumcision but also the
purifications and sacrifices of the Law, whose use continued until Christ's
advent. We also number among them other more temporary things: the crossing of
the sea before the Law, eating the manna, and drinking the water from the rock.
Other things could be added if this were the place to discuss them. You,
however, number Circumcision alone in this catalogue, maintaining that the
promise of forgiveness was attached to it alone. Why? “Because,” you say, “all
the ancient teachers compare Circumcision alone with Baptism.” On the contrary,
who among the ancients disagreed with Paul, who so compares the crossing of the
sea with Baptism that he testifies all the Fathers were baptized in it? But
even if it were so, what kind of argument would this be, unless perhaps you
think either every ancient Sacrament must be opposed to an individual new one,
or every individual ancient one to each new one?

You add that “the other rites of the Mosaic Law are nowhere equated with our
Sacraments.” I answer that neither do we equate them simply and altogether, as
you know, but say they are equal in this: those rites signified the same thing—
Christ with his benefits—just as truly and certainly to the Israelites as
Baptism and the Lord's Supper signify him to us. Hence we also place them under
one genus. You initially concede this, but shortly afterward try to overthrow
it. You should not be surprised if we do not believe you when you disagree
with yourself. On page 182 you say: “We concede what we have always conceded:
the Fathers possessed Sacraments equal to ours in the thing, with respect to
Christ, whom they spiritually ate by faith, and they were made partakers of his
flesh together with us. We also receive what was admitted before: the Fathers'
Sacraments and ours differ by degree; according to our fuller measure of
revelation we enjoy Christ more abundantly; Christ feeds us more fully than he
formerly fed the Fathers with the substance of his flesh.” These are your words,
requiring of us in that passage only that we additionally confess that in a
corporeal manner we become partakers of Christ's body and blood together with
the bread and wine.

I therefore ask which Sacraments you think are actually equal to ours. What
faith, what perception of Christ, could have a place in those Sacraments if
Circumcision alone had attached to it the promise of forgiveness? If you have
no answer, why on pages 188 and 189 do you disagree with yourself instead of
remaining in the truth? Earlier you made the ancient Sacraments equal to ours
with respect to the thing—that is, Christ. But when you came to the matter, you
teach that the legal victims were nothing other than certain admonitions of
Christ's future sacrifice, which they apprehended through faith and in it
simultaneously received forgiveness of sins. The very matter shows that the
name *admonition* is far weaker than *Sacrament*, and you yourself take it no
differently

when

[PDF page 34; printed page 243]

you speak in such a way as to say that Circumcision alone can be compared with
our Sacraments. Yet what equality in the thing remains if you do not even leave
them a shared name in a similar sense?

But what arguments do you offer? You ask, “Does the fact that something is so
often called a sacrifice, an offering, or a victim for sin have the same force
as a promise added to Baptism and the Supper?” I answer that the promise is not
merely similar but the same in each case, although in Baptism and the Lord's
Supper it is more explicit and effective. I say that this cannot reasonably be
called into doubt by someone who shortly before confessed that the ancients'
Sacraments—Sacraments, I say, not one Sacrament, lest anyone suppose you were
speaking earlier of Circumcision alone—were equal to ours with respect to the
thing, that is, with respect to Christ. Even if we granted you the one thing
you asked for before, namely that corporeal mode of presence in the Lord's
Supper, would it therefore follow that those Sacraments were unequal with
respect to the thing itself, rather than with respect to the conjunction of the
thing with the sign and its reception? With what face, then, will you deny that
the promise of forgiveness of sins and eternal life was attached to those
Sacraments in which you confess that Christ was offered and spiritually
apprehended through faith? For where grace and faith are present, surely the
Word and Christ offered for righteousness are present too. Now add signs to
these, however numerous and diverse, and though they lack an equal analogy or
efficacy of signification: will not the same definition of a Sacrament apply to
them as applies to Baptism and the Lord's Supper? If you also require particular
testimonies concerning each one, how often will you find in Scripture an
explicit promise of atonement for sins attached to the sacrifices? Why seek
testimonies about the crossing of the sea and the cloud, when Paul does not say
that these were types of our Baptism for us, but that the Fathers themselves
were baptized in them—that is, that these were for them what Baptism is for us?
And concerning the Lord's Supper, what could be said more majestic than this:
“The rock was Christ”?

If you require testimonies from the ancients, do you not hear Augustine clearly
and expressly testify, not what you assert—namely, that the manna and rock were
not Sacraments but types of our Sacraments, and that not by their own nature but
by Paul's “induction,” as you call it? Do you not hear Augustine, I say, speak
quite differently? “The Jews' Sacraments,” Augustine says, “were diverse in
their signs but equal in the thing: diverse in visible appearance, equal in
spiritual power.” Elsewhere he likewise says: “In diverse signs there is the
same faith, for words change their sounds over time, and words are surely
nothing other than signs. The Fathers drank the same spiritual drink, though
not the same corporeal drink. See, therefore: while faith remains, the signs
vary. There the rock was Christ; for us, what is placed upon the altar is
Christ. And they drank water flowing from the rock as a great Sacrament; the
faithful know what we drink. If you attend to the visible appearance, it is
different; if to the intelligible signification, they drank the same spiritual
drink.”

You now understand, therefore, that we have not forgotten what you asked of
us—that we should not neglect to prove from Scripture that the manna and water
from the rock were Sacraments such as Baptism and the Supper are. For the man
who says that the Fathers were baptized in the sea and cloud, that the manna
was spiritual food, and that the rock was Christ says everything. Yet we do not
cease to distinguish properly between signs that precede the exhibition of the
thing and signs that follow it, though you falsely charge us with doing so.

But let us also hear the arguments by which you think it can be proved that the
manna, the drink from the rock, and similar things were not Sacraments on the
same footing as Circumcision or our Sacraments. “Because,” you say, “the Fathers
would not have been inferior but superior to us, since they possessed promises
and Sacraments of grace in greater abundance than the Church now possesses.” A
sharp point indeed—as if the promises of salvation in Christ made to the
Fathers did not pertain to us, although to them have now been added the
exhibition of Christ himself and an explanation of those same promises that is
infinitely clearer and repeated a thousand times; as if the Lord did not also
pour his Spirit upon the Church much more richly; as if, finally, the Church's
dignity were to be judged by a multitude of Sacraments, rather than by their
fewness. For why were there more Sacraments then, except because the light was
not so great and weakness was greater? Indeed, if we were no longer weak and
did not know and believe only in part, what need would there have been to
institute any Sacraments? Augustine certainly thought very differently, for he
also lists this excellence of our Church: once Christ was revealed,
“Sacraments were instituted fewer in number, more majestic in signification,
and more excellent in power.” [Marginal citation: Augustine, *To Januarius*.]

What next? You cite the Epistle to the Hebrews, which teaches that the Levitical
sacrifices could not perfect their worshipers; that the blood of bulls and
goats sanctified the defiled for purification of the flesh, not of the spirit;
that the Law possessed a shadow of the good things to come, not the very image
of the things; and that daily offerings could not take away sins.

[PDF page 35; printed page 244]

Is that so? Why, then, is the Lord said to be appeased by sacrifices? Why was a
sacrifice for sin instituted? Why does David so ardently long for the
tabernacle? Whence comes that praise of the Levitical priesthood? The Holy
Spirit certainly does not contradict himself. But when signs are separated
from the things themselves, they cease to be signs and return to their natural
condition. It is therefore no wonder that the argument against the Jews, who
separated the signs from Christ, proceeds as if the discussion had been framed
about natural things rather than Sacraments. Thus Paul calls Circumcision the
seal of the righteousness of faith, though elsewhere he not only denies that it
helps but even affirms that it excludes a person from Christ. In the former
place he evaluates it from the Lord's institution and its lawful use under the
Law; in the latter from the Jews' false supposition, as we also said shortly
before. [Marginal references: Romans 4:11; Galatians 5:2.]

Some matters remain on which we must call you to account. Concerning the mode
of communicating and receiving Christ, Calvin writes that this distinction
between us and the Fathers who preceded Christ's advent must also be observed:
although they were true partakers of Christ spiritually by faith in their
Sacraments, their faith did not actually reach into Christ's very substance,
since it did not yet exist, but only into the benefit that comes to us from
Christ's being ours by faith, so that we draw salvation from him. The thing was
therefore the same and the benefit the same, but the mode differed in this
respect: their faith embraced Christ with the same benefit, but through hope,
since he was yet to come; our faith truly apprehends the same Christ, but one
who now actually exists and in this manner communicates himself to us. What do
you find to criticize here? Because you either do not understand what Calvin
writes or pretend not to understand, you treat him as though he disagreed with
himself, since he seems either to concede that Christ's substance is given to
us—which you say he denies elsewhere—or to deprive the Fathers of communion
with Christ himself. But so many published books testify that neither claim is
true and that Calvin never separated the communication of Christ from
participation in his power; no new proof is required.

Finally, you contend that even if everything we have said about the other
Sacraments were true, it would still not follow that in the Lord's Supper we
become partakers of Christ in no different way than in Baptism—that is,
spiritually through faith. For you say that an argument from genus to species
is invalid unless a universal qualifier is added. I grant it; but why do you
not add the universal qualifier? “Because,” you say, “the Lord's Supper has
something peculiar to itself.” I freely admit it, but the peculiarity lies
neither in the thing itself nor in the mode of communication. It lies in the
analogy of the signs and in a particular aspect of Christ's quality, so to
speak: in Baptism Christ is presented to us as regeneration; in the Lord's
Supper, as nourishment for the regenerate. Nevertheless, the same Christ is
offered for communication by the same power of the Spirit and the same faith.
You deny this and require in addition a corporeal mode of presence and
communication. But from where, at last, will you prove it? For these statements
seem to us equally explicit: “As many of you as were baptized have put on
Christ”; “Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved”; “We were all
baptized into one body”; and whatever Paul recalls about Baptism in Romans 6;
and also these: “This is my body”; “This is my blood”; “The cup which we bless
is a communion in the blood of Christ”; and “The bread which we break is a
communion in the body of Christ.”

### In reply to chapter thirty-three: On local inclusion

At the outset, because we thought that with Scripture you attributed to Christ
a true body—that is, one possessing its own dimensions—we also believed it
necessarily followed that whoever joined Christ's body with the bread in a
substantial and corporeal mode thereby located Christ's body in the bread as in
a place. But you complain that this does you a great injustice. Then tell us at
last how you place Christ's body really in, with, or under the bread. Elsewhere
you deny that this should be investigated, and now you are content to say that
it does not occur naturally, doubtless so that you can draw the blessed Dr.
Bucer into your camp. Yet what, after all, will you gather from Bucer except
what we too insist upon: although Christ remains in heaven, he is nevertheless
joined sacramentally with the bread—that is, just as the symbols figure, he is
most truly communicated to us spiritually through faith, so that we draw
eternal life from him?

But the mode that you dare not confess openly, lest you appear to philosophize,
I will explain in such a way that you will not dare deny it. You think Christ's
body is in the bread definitively and is everywhere

[PDF page 36; printed page 245]

repletively in a supernatural manner. We gladly leave this philosophy and
these monstrosities entirely to you. Since we know that Christ's true body—and
therefore a finite and circumscribed body—ascended beyond the heavens, and that,
as Peter testifies, the heavens must receive him there, we do not curiously
investigate whether there is a place above the heavens or how he is contained
there. Nor do we deny that he nonetheless becomes truly ours and breathes life
into us just as he promises in the Word and figures in the Sacraments—indeed,
far more effectively than if he were joined to us naturally. Nevertheless, we
believe that with respect to his humanity he is nowhere but in heaven and must
be sought there spiritually. [Marginal reference: Acts 3:21.]

### In reply to chapter thirty-four: On the adoration of Christ in the Eucharist

By God's kindness we profess, not only in speech but also in practice and by
example, that everything in God's Church must be done honorably and decently,
and especially that the sacred mysteries must be celebrated reverently. This
reverence is principally situated in the heart's disposition, which considers
with a certain pious awe that God is present to it, the heavenly table is
present, and the angels are present. Nevertheless, we gladly make a practice
of testifying to it also by external signs—for example, kneeling during common
prayers, the head uncovered, properly greeting those from whose hand we receive
the mysteries, and indeed by our whole bearing and gesture. Nor would anyone
who acted otherwise escape severe censure. What, then, do you require? That we
prostrate ourselves before the mysteries? That we elevate the symbols so that
others fall upon their knees, strike their breasts, and do other things of this
kind? Far be it from us either to do this ourselves or to command others to do
it. “But,” you say, “this is rendered not to the bread but to Christ.” We do
not seek Christ in the bread, but in heaven. Although we know that these are
sacred symbols of Christ's own true body and true blood, far be it from us to
adore them or venerate them in any way other than the manner we have described.
For Christ commanded us to receive, eat, and drink—decently, of course, as
everything, and especially these things, must be done in the Church—but he did
not command us to adore. What is adoration without God's Word but sheer
superstition? And who will discern whether you give this honor to the bread
itself or to Christ? Most of all I marvel that you leave this adoration free,
although you confess that Christ is actually present corporeally with the bread
just as he is in heaven, and that he is given and received. For if I believed
this to be so, I would consider his adoration not only tolerable and religious,
but necessary.

### In reply to chapter thirty-five: On absurd and perplexing questions

Since you have indeed added this chapter *ἐκ περιουσίας*—as something beyond
what was needed—what point is there in disputing against you? You testify
that curious questions are condemned by God's Word. To confirm the point you
produce Basil, Cyril, Chrysostom, Ambrose, Augustine, Nazianzen—and whom do you
not produce? Yet we have no controversy about this matter. Consider for
yourself which party these testimonies refute: those who establish the truth of
Christ's body from Scripture and require the interpretation of Scripture to
conform exactly to it; or rather the man who, in order to fabricate an
invisible and uncircumscribed body—that is, a body that is not a body—utterly
overturns everything said about the Lord's true ascension into heaven and his
second advent by referring it all to a mere change of form.

### In reply to chapter ten: On passages collected from Augustine

I decided to postpone until this place the passages you wrench from Augustine,
lest I mix many matters together. If, in doing so, I again confuse the
questions, it is fair that you bear the blame, since I am compelled to follow
your footsteps. I shall omit your lengthy prefaces, in which you inveigh
against the living and the dead as if we simply abolished every presence of
Christ, rather than only that single mode of presence which you establish
against all Scripture and without any necessity. I think we have said more than
enough about this. I shall touch only one point. You say Calvin reduced the
whole matter to this one question: “Are Christ's words to be understood
properly, or improperly by metonymy?” Then you raise a great outcry over this
adverb *improperly*. For you ask which of the Apostles or ancient interpreters
advanced with such boldness as to say that Christ spoke improperly when
establishing his testament. First of all, I say that you are a forger not
*improperly*, but properly and openly. For these are Calvin's words: “The only
question,” Calvin says, “is whether he means properly and without figure that
the bread is his body, or whether he transfers the name of the thing signified
to the symbol.” There is no mention here of improper speech, but of figurative
speech. If you make the two the same, why

[PDF page 37; printed page 246]

do you raise disputes about nothing? Figurative speech is so far from obscuring
or deforming discourse—unless perhaps the metaphor is excessively obscure or
far-fetched—that, on the contrary, nothing illuminates or adorns it equally
well. We have proved this more than sufficiently in the very example of this
metonymy. As for why the Apostles did not ask about this trope, you could have
learned two reasons from Chrysostom: first, they had already been sufficiently
taught from the sermon delivered at Capernaum that these things were
spiritual; second, they could not have been much disturbed or astonished when
they saw the Lord himself eat and drink the same food and drink before everyone
else. They therefore could not fail to understand the mystery, since no one
corporeally eats or drinks himself. And among the ancients, does Augustine not
say openly and in at least three places that the speech by which the Lord wills
his flesh to be eaten and his blood drunk is *figurative*? Does Tertullian not
interpret it as a “figure of the body”? But enough of this. Let us come to the
matter.

To show that Augustine explained the words of the Lord's Supper only
metonymically, Calvin produces this passage from Letter 23 to Boniface: “If
Sacraments did not have some likeness to the things of which they are
Sacraments, they would not be Sacraments at all. From this likeness they also
generally receive the names of the things themselves. Therefore, just as in a
certain manner the Sacrament of Christ's body is Christ's body, and the
Sacrament of his blood is his blood, so the Sacrament of faith is faith.” What
do you answer? You acknowledge the likeness and explain it in many words, as
you are accustomed to do in matters that are not at all disputed. But when you
come to the point, what do you finally offer? You say that Augustine plays with
the word *body*, as if, of course, he were speaking about the Church's mystical
body. But what similar evasion will you offer in the second clause, where the
same thing is said of the blood? Will you quibble there too that Augustine is
speaking of the Church's blood?

You next ask, “Must the whole business of the Supper be transferred to
signification?” I answer that this question has no bearing on us, for we do not
make the signification empty in the least; we say that what is truly figured by
the symbols is truly communicated to us through faith. You add, “You turn
particular propositions into universal questions.” I reply that Augustine is
expressly dealing with the signs of the Lord's Supper and discussing the reason
why the name of the thing is attributed to the signs themselves. Show, then,
that we do not remain within those bounds.

You ask, “Does the name *body* in Augustine nowhere signify the substance of a
present body, but the figure of an absent body?” I answer that whenever
Augustine treats this very question—how the Sacrament is called the body and
blood—the name *body* is never taken otherwise than for the true sign of the
body: I mean the visible and finite body which is not on earth but in heaven,
and yet is truly offered and received, though spiritually by faith. If you deny
this, produce a passage by which you confirm your position. Calvin objects that
Augustine calls what the Evangelists call the body sometimes its figure and
sometimes its sign. You answer: “This happens because of its relation to the
things signified and because of the appearance presented to the senses.” I
grant the former, but add this: if, besides signification, that corporeal
presence which you posit were also required, there would have been no reason
for Augustine to use that explanation. For who could doubt that a Sacrament is
a sign of the thing it signifies? But one could doubt how the bread is said to
be the body. Augustine answers that it is so called, not because it is the body
or because the body is really there with it, but because it is the sign and
figure of the body. You will certainly find no other explanation in him of the
saying, “This is my body.” I therefore reject completely the latter reason,
concerning the appearance presented to the senses, not only as fabricated but
also as foolish. For the external senses do not perceive the bread or wine
insofar as they are Sacraments—that is, signs of the body and blood. Faith alone
sees this, because it is announced to faith by the Word spoken concerning this
bread and wine.

You cited a passage of Augustine in which the Lord's body is said to fall to
the ground and enter the mouth. Calvin answers that this is in the same sense
in which Augustine elsewhere said it is consumed: namely, the name *body* is
applied to the sign itself for greater emphasis and to express the sacramental
conjunction, a trope whose author is Christ himself. But here you deny that
Augustine says the body is consumed, maintaining that he says the bread is
consumed. Yet it is agreed that in Augustine what is placed upon the altar is
said to be consumed. Teach us, then, what that is. If you say it is the bread
and the body, or the body with the bread, you must confess that the whole is
also consumed, or else show why you think the pronoun in Augustine's statement
must be explained differently from the pronoun *this* in Christ's words. Is it
because Augustine says that it is consumed, while Christ commands this

[PDF page 38; printed page 247]

to be received, eaten, and drunk? I think that the verb *to be consumed*
signifies nothing other than what Christ signified by the verbs *to eat* and
*to drink*. For Augustine speaks of infants and therefore has in view what is
perceived by children's external senses, not what natural heat does within.
Come forward, then, and turn yourself every way you please: you will never show
that in this passage of Augustine the verb *to be consumed* signifies anything
other than to be eaten and drunk. Why, then, do you shrink from the verb
*consume* more than from *eat* and *drink*? Is it because you fear that we will
think the thing is reduced to nothing? That is indeed some concern. But we
release you from this suspicion, since we acknowledge that Augustine had
nothing in view except the external action of eating and drinking. Yet I
certainly marvel that you shrink from the verb *consume*, though you are not
ashamed to confess with Pope Nicholas that what is placed upon the altar, after
consecration, is sensibly handled by the priests' hands, broken, and ground by
the teeth—not merely sacramentally but also in truth.

But what if we grant what you request and say that the bread alone is consumed?
With what shame will you thereafter charge us with separating what God has
inseparably joined? Indeed, why is it more objectionable to you to say that
Christ's body is consumed than that it is eaten, drunk, received by the mouth,
broken, or ground by the teeth? What more? Even that it falls to the ground?
For Augustine speaks this way, as you yourself quote him; and this last claim,
at least, agrees with Christ's true body scarcely better than saying it is
consumed. Thus those subtleties collapse by which you strive to prove that
Calvin empties the holy Supper of the Lord's body and blood and leaves
corruptible elements in their place. “Because,” you say, “he attempts to prove
from Augustine that the body is consumed; but Christ's body is not consumed,
only the bread; therefore he thinks the bread, not Christ's body, is eaten.”
On the contrary, Calvin acknowledges equally that in Augustine the body is
consumed and that it is eaten, and that these two are synonymous. But from
Augustine himself he proves that all these expressions are metonymical.
Augustine certainly would never have said these things about Christ's body
itself, which, with Peter, he says is in heaven, and indeed in some definite
place because of the manner of a true body. [Marginal reference: Acts 3:21.]
Otherwise, show us just this one thing: how Christ's body is said without any
trope to fall to the ground, especially if, as you maintain, it is invisible
and everywhere, and therefore uncircumscribed.

You press passages in which the ancients, doubtless following Christ's
footsteps, say that Christ's body enters the mouth and is received by mouth,
lips, and tongue, as though there were no obvious trope in these expressions
by which the truth of the sacramental conjunction and also of our spiritual
reception is expressed more vividly. If you deny this, why do you say that one
thing is seen in the Supper and another believed? For these too are
Chrysostom's words: “O how many now say, ‘I would like to see his form and
appearance; I would like to see his garments and sandals!’ You see him; you
touch him.” If all this must be taken without a trope, why do you not confess
that an invisible body is seen and an uncircumscribed body touched? You cite a
passage of Irenaeus in which he says our flesh is fed and nourished by the
Lord's body and blood. Which of us ever denied this? But we say that the flesh
receives it spiritually through faith, unto the everlasting life of body and
soul.

Nor do we simply recoil from the Fathers' expressions. But since we see you
abuse, in the manner of the Capernaïtes, things that the ancients said
figuratively after Christ's example, we therefore explain their opinions
partly by comparing their writings with one another and partly from God's Word
itself. And so that we may give no one an occasion of stumbling, we strive to
speak as plainly and clearly as possible in this controversy. I gladly pass
over the other insults you vomit against us here, and especially against
Calvin—as though we snatched the Lord's body and blood out of the sacred
symbols, as you put it, or taught that they are nothing but certain reminders
and admonitions; as though, finally, we were no different from the coiner, the
Anabaptist king of Münster, and the other Enthusiasts. For I have reason to fear
that together with those insults you have vomited out all common sense as well.

### In reply to chapter eleven: Whether unworthy and wicked Christians receive the symbols alone, or the Lord's true body and blood

We agree that the complete Sacraments—that is, both the signs and the thing—are
presented equally to all, since Christ's liberality or faithfulness does not
depend upon a person's worthiness. Thus we profess that the Gospel of salvation
is preached indiscriminately to all and the Baptism of regeneration given to
all. But we say that Christ, who is the substance both of the simple Word and
of the Sacraments, is received by faith alone and therefore by believers alone,
and is rejected by the rest. Consequently, both the simple Word and the
Sacraments conduce to life for believers alone, but to destruction for the
others. You, however, maintain that in the Lord's Supper the signs are joined
with the things by so inseparable

[PDF page 39; printed page 248]

a conjunction—in a corporeal and essential manner, though invisibly—that
whoever takes the symbols also receives the thing itself, namely Christ:
believers to life, but the rest to judgment. And you strive to drag Augustine
into your camp. We are accustomed to show what he thought from his transparent
testimonies. For example, he plainly says, “The person who is not in Christ's
body does not eat Christ's body.” Again: “When Christ says, ‘Whoever eats my
flesh,’ he shows what it is to eat Christ's body and drink his blood not merely
in the Sacrament but in reality.” Again: “The person who is at variance with
Christ neither eats Christ's flesh nor drinks his blood, even if every day he
receives the Sacrament of so great a thing to his own judgment.” To this also
belongs the fact that he very frequently distinguishes between the Sacrament
and the thing of the Sacrament, and expressly writes that “the other disciples
received the bread which was the Lord; Judas received only the Lord's bread.”
[Marginal citations: Augustine, *City of God* 21.25; *Tractates on John* 26 and
59.]

What do you say to these things, Westphal? You cite a passage from book 1
against Cresconius, where Augustine says, “Christ's body becomes destructive
to those who use it badly.” If you please, add what is written elsewhere:
“The Lord's body, which is good, is evil to the wicked.” Why should we not grant
all these and similar statements? But show that to use the Lord's body badly is
the same as to receive Christ himself without faith, rather than to reject the
offered body and blood through one's unbelief. “But,” you will say, “why then
does Augustine call the life-giving food, our ransom, and the holy thing the
body itself?” Obviously because he considers what is offered both to the worthy
and to the unworthy, not what they actually receive. For if bread alone and not
also the body were offered to unbelievers, they certainly would not be said to
use the body badly, but only the bread. Consequently, they would be guilty not
of failing to discern or of despising Christ's body, but only the bread.

But you will object that Augustine says not only that “the body is offered,”
but also that it is “eaten, drunk, taken, accepted, and received unworthily.” I
grant it, but in the sense in which elsewhere he explained that the name of the
thing signified is attributed to the sign, when he said, “The seals are common
to the wicked and the good; the thing itself belongs only to the godly.” Thus
in Letter 165 he does not say that the body, but that the “Sacrament of the
body,” was given to Judas the traitor. In book 2 *Against the Letter of
Petilian*, chapter 57, he does not say that Judas and Peter alike shared the
body and blood, but the “holy thing”—that is, as he himself explains, the
Sacrament. For what? Shall we say that unbelievers have access into heaven,
where Augustine likewise testifies that Christ's body finally is and that it
must be sought not by the hand but by faith? Those sayings must therefore be
understood as when he says, “Take care how carefully the body is administered,
so that none of it falls to the ground,” and Chrysostom says that “Christ
himself is seen and touched.” For who would say that Christ's body itself falls
to the ground, is touched, or is seen, rather than the Sacrament of his body?

Jerome therefore speaks rightly in book 4 on Jeremiah, chapter 22, as others
have long since cited: “Those who will not have been,” he says, “will not eat
and drink”—with the body and blood of the Savior understood. He was speaking of
heretics. Likewise, on Isaiah 66 he says: “While they are not holy in body and
spirit, they neither eat Jesus' flesh nor drink his blood.” And if
Chrysostom's opinion is true when in the *Incomplete Work on Matthew* he
testifies that “in the holy vessels there is not Christ's body and blood, but
the mystery of Christ's body and blood,” how will everyone who receives the
mystery be said to receive the body and blood themselves, which not even the
saints receive except by faith? Therefore acknowledge that whenever elsewhere
Chrysostom says that the body, the blood, or the divine gifts are received
rashly or unworthily, regard is had to what the Lord offers entire but the
ungodly receive only as far as the sign and despise in its entirety. Let the
same judgment apply to Cyprian and, to say it once, to all the ancients. In
keeping with the Church's custom and following Christ's footsteps, they
generally called the sacred mysteries by the name of the thing signified; and
yet, whenever need arose—at that time these matters were not in controversy—they
plainly and clearly distinguished the Sacraments from the things themselves.

As for your attempt to make three members of the Sacrament—the visible element,
the thing itself (that is, the body and blood), and the power or effect—even if
I granted this to you, how will you prove that the third member can be separated
from the second, unless perhaps you teach that Christ can be separated from his
life-giving power? It is certainly astonishing that a theologian could approve
of asserting that Christ can be torn from his efficacy more readily than from
the signs, when it is sufficiently clear that the conjunction between Christ's
flesh and the life-giving power that inheres completely in it is infinitely
closer than any conjunction we can imagine between the signs and the thing
signified. If you object that you separate this efficacy not from Christ but
from us, you will still accomplish nothing. For it is certain either that this
efficacy is not in Christ, or that those who have Christ within them—and those
who receive him must have him—also experience his efficacy. Your argument from
the preaching of the Gospel, which is announced equally to the good and the
wicked,

[PDF page 40; printed page 249]

to the former as an aroma of life and to the latter as an aroma of death, is
especially favorable to us. For who says that the substance of the Word—that
is, Christ offered in the Word—is received by unbelievers? Or, if we prefer to
use Christ's own words, who says that Christ's flesh and blood offered in the
Word are eaten and drunk by unbelievers, rather than rejected? Christ does not
employ a two-part statement: “Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood by
faith will live forever; but whoever eats or drinks, though not from faith,
will die forever.” Instead, the first statement alone is everywhere impressed
upon us. For just as Christ's flesh, when received, necessarily gives life, so
its rejection necessarily kills. Life therefore flows from it; death, however,
from contempt for it. It is no objection that somewhere Augustine, applying
this saying of Christ to the Lord's Supper, establishes a twofold eating of the
flesh, since this saying of his remains firm: “Those who are not of Christ's
body do not eat Christ's body.” It therefore follows that when Augustine
establishes an ineffective eating of the flesh, he calls the symbol of the
flesh *flesh*—not to show what unbelievers receive, but what they reject.

The same must be maintained of Baptism, for what else does it offer but Christ
to be put on? The sign is common to the good and wicked, but believers alone
put on Christ. Who ever heard of putting on Christ unto judgment? These are
equal in reality: putting on Christ in Baptism, or being baptized into one body
and therefore into Christ who is the head of this body; and eating the body and
drinking the blood in the Lord's sacred Supper. Thus, to state the matter
briefly, if you ask what is offered to unbelievers in the Supper, we will say
that the true body and this bread, the true blood and this wine, are offered:
the former to be received spiritually through faith, the latter naturally. But
if you ask what is received by them, we answer: this bread and this wine. If
you ask what is despised, we reply: first of all, the Lord's body and blood,
which are entirely repelled through unbelief, and not merely their power and
efficacy; then also this bread and this wine, contempt for which is also most
serious, because they were offered to them not as bread and wine but as symbols
of the body and blood. We say that liability to eternal death flows from this
twofold contempt, not from Christ's flesh or blood itself. You have neither
shown nor ever will show that the Lord's Word or the writings of any ancient
author conflict with this view.

### On the passages Westphal cites from Augustine, chapter thirteen

You contend that in Augustine sacramental eating comprehends the whole
Sacrament—that is, the sign and the thing signified—while spiritual eating
pertains to its effect and use and is therefore peculiar to believers. I hope
we have abundantly refuted this sheer invention, partly earlier in the second
response to this very chapter and partly in what we have just answered to the
preceding chapter. I shall therefore touch only this one point, from which
anyone may judge the matter. Calvin objected that sacramental eating, of the
kind you posit, must not be separated from spiritual eating. The reason is
obvious: otherwise Christ's body will be stripped of its efficacy. What do you
say in reply? “We are so far from separating the one from the other,” you say,
“that we confess spiritual eating is necessarily required. Otherwise we would
not assert that the unworthy eat unto judgment when, though receiving Christ's
body, they do not receive spiritually in like manner. Unbelievers separate the
one from the other by their own fault, through unbelief.” What bearing does
this have, I ask? Calvin objects that, if your doctrine holds, Christ is
stripped of his Spirit's power, because although he is received within the body
he will not give life. You answer that these things are separated not by you
but by unbelievers. Is this what it means to untie the knot?

### On the passages Westphal cites from Augustine, chapters twenty-four through twenty-seven

You would readily persuade us that in Augustine the thing of the Sacrament
means sometimes the Lord's body and blood and sometimes power and grace. But
from where do you gather this? From the fact that Augustine often says the
Lord's body and blood are received and eaten even by the unworthy? I grant it,
but in the sense in which he said Christ gave the sign of his body and, in
Letter 165, that the Sacrament of his body is given to all in common. For these
principles are certain and firm: Christ's body is in heaven as in a place,
although it is truly communicated to believers on earth; those who are not of
Christ's body do not eat Christ's body; the Sacrament is received by some unto
life and by others unto destruction; the thing of the Sacrament is unto life
for every person and unto destruction for none. [Marginal citations:
Augustine, *Against Faustus the Manichaean*; *On Christian Doctrine*; and
others.] These principles, I say, are firm, and from them follows what we
maintain: the thing of the Sacrament—that is, the body and blood which are truly
figured by the symbols—is received by faith alone. “But,” you say, “Augustine
said that from the one holy thing Judas received judgment and Peter salvation.”
I freely grant it. Are not the symbols of the Lord's body and blood a holy
thing? From it Judas received judgment, because by his perfidy he both rejected
the body and blood and profaned the sacred symbols.

[PDF page 41; printed page 250]

But you will object again that the name *Sacrament* is understood to include
both the sign and the thing signified, and therefore that when Augustine says
the Sacrament is received by the unworthy he also includes the thing itself. I
answer that the name *Sacrament* is not always taken in such a way that it
jointly signifies the thing itself together with the sign. Sometimes it
signifies nothing but the sign or symbol itself, with a relation indeed to the
body and blood, but considered distinctly and separately. That this is so is
perfectly clear from the fact that otherwise expressions such as “Sacrament of
the body,” “Sacrament of the blood,” and others like them would be utterly
nonsensical. The same is also clear when the Sacrament—that is, the whole—is
resolved into its parts in interpreting these expressions. For what would
*Sacrament of the body* signify? Bread or sign together with a body of the
body. In the same way, *Sacrament of the blood* would be the same as wine or
sign together with blood of the blood.

Let us therefore maintain that whenever Sacraments are contrasted with the
thing—as Augustine contrasts them when distinguishing the eating of the
ungodly—and likewise when the name of the thing, such as the Lord's body and
blood, is joined with the name *Sacrament*, then *Sacrament* signifies nothing
but the sacred sign, namely bread and wine: not insofar as they are natural
things, but insofar as they are heavenly gifts. Paul therefore does not simply
say “from bread,” but “from this bread”—that is, the bread which has been made
the Sacrament of the body and is therefore sometimes itself called the body.
Thus we do not say that bare bread is presented to unbelievers in the Lord's
Supper, as you falsely slander us, but that besides it the very thing which this
bread truly figures and from which the bread is denominated *body* is also
given to be received spiritually through faith. Nor do we say that bare bread
is profaned by the unworthy, but the sacred symbol of the Lord's body. Yet you
will never show that anything besides bread and wine is received by the body's
organs in the Lord's Supper, whether by the worthy or the unworthy.

Since this is so, and Augustine mentions only two adverbs by which a twofold
eating is signified—namely, *visibly* and *spiritually*—it necessarily follows,
unless the division is faulty, that whatever is not received visibly is said
to be received spiritually. Therefore spiritual eating pertains to the Lord's
body and blood, not merely to their power and efficacy, as you maintain. But
here you evade the issue once again. You say that the adverb *visibly* pertains
not merely to the signs but also to the Lord's body and blood—not because these
too are received visibly, but because on account of the conjunction of the
signs and thing they are said to be received visibly. But who ever granted you
that the ancients spoke so improperly that they said the Lord's body and blood
themselves are visibly received? Chrysostom indeed says, “You see him; you
touch him.” But either you must confess that this is hyperbole, by which the
supreme reality of the reception is expressed, or you must demonstrate to us
that what you contend is invisible and uncircumscribed is seen and touched,
either alone or with the sign. Otherwise you must also acknowledge that we
ascend into heaven corporeally and without a trope, since the same Chrysostom
says in book 3 of *On the Priesthood*: “Do you still think that you stand among
mortals and remain upon earth? Are you not rather immediately transported into
heaven and, casting aside every thought of the flesh, do you not survey the
things in heaven with a naked soul and pure mind?”

### In reply to chapter twenty-two: On Augustine's passage from the letter to Dardanus

In this chapter, because you perceive that the throat of your case is being
attacked and that you are so hard pressed that you cannot escape unless you
abandon Augustine, you fortify yourself with three ramparts, as it were, before
you engage at close quarters. First, you say that Augustine is doing nothing
there but philosophizing. Thus whenever Augustine allows himself to be twisted
by you, he will be a theologian; otherwise, he will suddenly become a
philosopher and natural scientist. Next you flee to the Lord's Word. Quite
rightly—but what if you are shut out from there too? Augustine rests on no
principles other than those drawn from the Lord's Word. If the interpretation
of the Lord's words is ambiguous, please forgive us for thinking that it is
unworthy of any wise man or theologian to abandon Augustine in order to follow
Westphal. Finally, so that you need not fight alone, you labor strenuously to
set Philip Melanchthon—whom, as I ought, I name with honor—against us. Your aim
is doubtless to hide securely in the meantime, like a fighter under Ajax's
shield. But relying upon Melanchthon's learning and fairness, I now dare tell
you that you will never make him a supporter of the controversy you have
undertaken with us or of ubiquity. Disciples of this kind, through whom even
now that excellent and most learned old man is not permitted to rest, certainly
ought to have been ashamed to sell him peace and quiet on the condition that he
allied himself with them to mix earth

[PDF page 42; printed page 251]

with heaven and pledged his conscience and reputation to them. But let us come
to the matter. You say the question is whether Augustine denied that Christ's
true body is in the Supper. On this point, indeed, you may rest as far as we are
concerned. For we have never doubted that Augustine thought what we too think:
that in his sacred Supper the Lord's true body and true blood really are
present, given, eaten, and drunk, but spiritually, through faith, sought and
apprehended in heaven, to which the symbols themselves carry us upward. If on
this account you continue to mock us as “air-walkers,” we shall at least set
Chrysostom against you. Speaking of this matter, he says: “In this life we must
become eagles and fly to heaven itself—or rather, above heaven. For where the
carcass is, there the eagles are.” By the carcass he means the Lord's body on
account of its death. Elsewhere likewise he says: “They were about to depart
from Egypt into Palestine and therefore wore the garb of travelers; you are
about to ascend from earth into heaven.”

Nor is the question whether Christ's human nature is abolished if one believes
that Christ's flesh is distributed as present in Communion. For we too hold
that the Lord is as truly present in the Supper to the eyes of faith, though in
a spiritual manner, as the symbols are truly present in a natural manner to the
eyes of the body. Therefore, so that there may be no ambiguity in the word
*presence*, let this rather be the question: Is Christ's body so circumscribed
by its dimensions that it now actually, with respect to position, cannot be in
several places at once, but is only in heaven as in a definite place, from
which it will not descend before he comes to judge the living and the dead?
This, I say, is the question in which we bring before you as judge Augustine
and every one of the orthodox ancients—not so that they may render a judgment
from philosophy, but from the one Word of God.

Let us see what you bring from your own resources. “It is agreed between us,”
you say, “that Christ is in heaven; but the question is what heaven is and how
Christ is in heaven.” Is that so? You, who a little before said that the
question *how* does not belong to faithful and Christian people, suddenly fall
into this question? But to the point. Calvin says heaven is God's magnificent
palace, above the whole fabric of the world. Oecolampadius says, with exactly
the same meaning, that it is a dwelling most worthy of Christ. This explanation
is proved by all the testimonies in which the Lord is said to dwell in heaven,
in which we are commanded to strive toward heaven, and in which the Lord is
said visibly to have ascended into heaven, above all these heavens. But what do
you say? “Those who know that when God speaks of heavenly things he
accommodates himself to our capacity do a grievous wrong,” you say. What
bearing, I ask, does this have? Was Christ not speaking about heavenly things
when he treated the sacramental conjunction? Yet in your eyes we are
sacrilegious when we ask what the meaning of Christ's words is, even though we
produce plain and transparent examples of sacramental metonymy. Produce
yourself even one example in which *heaven* signifies *everywhere*, or in which
*go away, return, pass over, ascend*, and *descend* are said of a true human body
without an actual change of place.

The Apostle expressly says that Christ ascended above all the heavens. From
this we gather that the heaven which Peter says must receive Christ is above
all the visible heavens. Scripture also locates God's majesty there, though not
as in a place—for God is uncircumscribed, which does not apply to Christ's
body—but because the fullest light of the divine essence and majesty,
incomprehensible to us, is displayed there. [Marginal references: Ephesians
4:10; Acts 3:21.] Show us, then, from God's Word that a heaven which is
everywhere can be said to be above all these heavens, and explain to us by what
trope this occurs, since you have now crossed into the camp of the tropists.
The Lord says to the thief, “Today you will be with me in paradise,” doubtless
signifying that heavenly dwelling. Show that the thief is everywhere, if
heaven and paradise are everywhere. [Marginal reference: Luke 23:43.]

But it is better to hear what you say. You set against us that genuinely godly
man Bucer, who, if he were alive today, would certainly have restrained you
long ago. What does Bucer say in sum? “The heaven into which Christ ascended
according to his humanity is the inaccessible light and glory of the invisible
God.” What else do we teach? We say that your view—that this designates the
glorious quality with which the Lord's body is clothed, rather than the place
in which that light and glory shine—is utterly false and alien to Bucer's
meaning.

Next you set Hilary against us, who says that “Christ's body is truly a
dwelling worthy of God.” Who but a plainly ungodly person ever denied this? But
what sane person could infer, as you think follows from it, that heaven is
everywhere? First, Hilary expressly distinguishes this habitation from the
other by which God is said to be in heaven. Second, by what right do you equate
the nature of a dwelling with the nature of the one who dwells in it, unless
you plainly follow Eutyches' dogma? Moreover, if this inference is valid,
since God dwelt in Christ *bodily*, as Paul says—that is, so as to make himself
manifest essentially on account of the hypostatic union—from the moment Christ
was conceived in the Virgin's womb, why do you not also

[PDF page 43; printed page 252]

say that from that moment he was in heaven and everywhere? Was it because he
had not yet ascended—that is, because he had not yet put on the glorious
quality? Then the argument should have been drawn not from habitation but from
ascension. And yet immediately, as if the matter were settled, you say: “What
it means that Christ is in heaven and what heaven is has been explained; the
question remaining for discussion is how he is in heaven.” Augustine settles
this question in a single statement: “Remove spatial extension from a body,
and it will not be a body.” But you are not content with Augustine's saying,
which you call physical, and continue to argue in this way: “Augustine varies
on this subject and is inconsistent with himself, because in the book *On the
Creed and Faith* he attributes to curiosity the inquiry how the Lord's body is
in heaven and affirms that it is enough to believe that it is in heaven.” It is
astonishing that Augustine did not see in his *Retractions* what you have seen.

But if things are as you judge, why do you commit the very fault that you
criticize in another? For does not a man who says that Christ's body is in
heaven—that is, everywhere, invisibly, after the likeness of the Deity—and
condemns those who say it is in heaven as in a circumscribed place define one
mode and attack another? Surely either you do not understand what curiosity is,
or knowingly and deliberately you afflict that distinguished bishop and
prophet of God with a grave insult. A curious person is one who raises questions
that are either useless, irrelevant to him, or concealed from us by God's
Spirit. You cannot say that the question how Christ's body is in heaven—that
is, whether in the manner that befits a true, finite, and circumscribed body or
otherwise—is useless or irrelevant to us without at the same time confessing
that it is useless to ask whether after the resurrection Christ ceased to have
a true body, that is, a finite and circumscribed body, and therefore ceased to
be man. Would that the impudence of the Eutychians did not compel us to ask
these questions, for otherwise we would never inquire into what we did not
doubt.

If you say that this has not been explained to us by the Lord's Word, I ask
again: Why then is the Lord said to have gone away, returned, been taken from
us, ascended, been received by heaven until the times of the restoration of all
things, to be coming, and to be descending? Why is he now said to govern his
Church by his Spirit and not by his body? Why are we said to be absent and away
from the Lord? Why are we said to be destined for dissolution so that we may be
with him? What, then? These questions are not curious, since the Holy Spirit
does not sharpen us for curiosity. But if anyone asks how Christ's body, since
it is a true body, can be contained in heaven where there seems to be no place;
what its condition and state are there; in what region of heaven it is; and
other matters of this kind—that person is truly curious, because eye has never
seen those things, nor ear heard them, nor have they entered the human heart.
Augustine explains this clearly in that very passage. He defines such curiosity
as belonging to those who ask *where*—that is, in what place of heaven—and
*how*—that is, in what position and condition—Christ is in heaven; in short, to
those who scrutinize the secrets of our faith and do not think thoughts lofty
enough about the dignity of the Lord's body. Are you not ashamed to charge
Augustine, who agrees so well with himself everywhere, with fickleness and
inconstancy, and indeed with curiosity itself? Finally, you may consider with
what right you call curious those who are content to believe that Christ's true
body is not on earth, since it is said to have ascended, but in heaven, to which
it is said to have ascended.

You cite another passage in which Augustine says, “The Lord's body is in heaven
as it was when it ascended into heaven.” “Unless this passage is understood of
the body's substance,” you say, “it does not seem to agree with the one in
which he says that it does not belong to our frailty to scrutinize the secrets
of the heavens, but that it belongs to our faith to think lofty and honorable
things about the dignity of the Lord's body.” The matter is certainly as you
maintain. Those words must indeed be understood altogether of the body's
substance, which Augustine affirms Christ carried into heaven as true—that is,
visible, finite, and circumscribed—and will bring to us from heaven. In other
respects we agree with you that those who attribute no different qualities to
Christ's body in that inexplicable glory and inaccessible splendor than during
the days of the flesh's weakness do not think thoughts lofty enough about its
dignity. We also agree with Cyril and Chrysostom, who attribute to dispensation
the fact that the Lord showed himself—not before the resurrection, as perhaps
you suppose, but afterward—to the women and disciples on earth, and indeed
after the resurrection in heaven to Stephen and Paul, not in the glory proper
to his body but in its former form. [Marginal references: Matthew 28:9; Acts
7:55; Acts 9:4, 17.]

For who could have endured that splendor of divine glory? But in turn we ask
you to confess with us that to shine like the sun, or even more brightly than
the sun, is one thing, while to belong to the divine nature—that is, to an
invisible, uncircumscribed substance which is everywhere—is another. Likewise,
in the summary of the Apostles' Creed, Christ is not simply said to have sat at
the Father's right hand. Those words signify, with respect to Christ's flesh,
its full participation in eternal glory and infinite power, and therefore its
taking possession of the heavenly inheritance both in his own name and in the
common name of all his members. But first and distinctly he is said to have
ascended into

[PDF page 44; printed page 253]

the heavens, which must receive him until the times of the restoration of all
things. It follows that either a tautology must be admitted in the Creed, or
the ascension must be distinguished from the session—that is, from
participation in glory and dominion. Otherwise there is no reason for you to
wonder that we object that Christ's body is diffused by you. For we do not
consider which words you use, but what is being said. Thus, just as *a body
separated from the soul* and *a dead body* signify the same thing, so we think
these statements signify the same: “Christ's body is everywhere or in several
places at once”; and, “Christ's body, and indeed the whole of it, is diffused
everywhere”; or, “Christ, even insofar as he is man, is God.” Meanwhile I pass
over—though it ought by no means to be passed over—that below, in chapter 27,
you expressly contend against Calvin that the verb *to be diffused* applies to
the head as well as the members.

If in this argument you believe neither us nor Augustine, at least believe
Vigilius against Eutyches, whose words we have already quoted several times. Or
if you command Vigilius too to go into exile among the philosophers, at least
believe Cyril. He so insists that these two things are simultaneously true—that
a body is a true body and is circumscribed in some place—that he expressly
affirms: “If a body were understood in the Deity, quantity too would necessarily
be understood, as would being in a place, possessing magnitude and quantity;
and therefore it would not escape circumscription.” Thus, to say it once, even
if Christ according to the flesh were God, he would not possess a substance
free of quantity if he had a body. Consequently, he would neither be everywhere
nor—which wholly coheres with your ubiquity—exist substantially and in his
entirety in different places, simultaneously in heaven and on earth.

But let us hear you further. “Following Augustine's counsel,” you say, “and
leaving aside the curious inquiry how Christ's body is in heaven, let us now
investigate whether, because it is in heaven, it is therefore absent from the
Eucharist.” You would certainly say that Augustine, after first straying from
the road into philosophy's byways, is led back to the path no less gently than
Plato dismisses his own Homer from his city after anointing him with perfumes.
Indeed, you even wish to strike a bargain with him, as we shall soon understand.
But above all I want the reader warned that, in your customary fashion, you
deviously play with us over the words *presence* and *absence*. For, as I have
repeated a thousand times, we do not simply deny that Christ's true body and
true blood are present in the Lord's Supper. We say that they are truly offered,
given, and received spiritually—that is, by the Spirit's almighty power. We
seek them in heaven through faith, and on earth they are truly and certainly
figured for us in the symbols by God's sure institution: that is, Christ
himself with all his gifts unto eternal life. We deny only that mode which you
posit and which we say conflicts with the truth of Christ's body and the whole
Word of God.

You deny that Augustine anywhere says that Christ's body is nowhere but in
heaven. Yet whenever he says that it is finite and circumscribed; that if space
is taken away, body is taken away; that the body in which Christ rose must be
in one place; that it was said according to the flesh, “You will not always
have me with you”; and that Christ no longer speaks on earth but from heaven
and in heaven—does he not clearly enough say that, with respect to a real and
essential mode of presence, it is nowhere but in heaven? If you wish to be
blind at midday and shut your eyes so that you do not see the light, do we
therefore see nothing? Cyril certainly says openly and in so many words that
“Christ is absent from us in body and does not fill the earth with his flesh.”
Vigilius testifies that “he departed from us according to his humanity; he left
us in his humanity; by the form of a servant”—that is, his body—“he is absent
from us since he departed; while he was on earth he was not in heaven, and now,
because he is in heaven, he is not on earth; the Word is everywhere, but the
flesh is not.” Know, then, that you must also contend with these men or confess
the truth with us. For Vigilius—a holy and learned man, as you know—says that
“this is the catholic faith and confession which the Apostles delivered, the
martyrs confirmed, and the faithful have guarded until now.”

You dispute about the *truth* of the body which Augustine says is diffused
everywhere. But since he clearly contrasts that truth with the body itself,
which is circumscribed in one place, who does not see that his point is that
the truth is a spiritual reality and therefore is always present to believers?
That is, the efficacy of Christ's true body, which died for us, flows out to the
whole Church, and Christ himself is thereby truly joined with the whole Church
dispersed throughout the world. You next return to the argument from Christ's
walking on the water and his passage through closed doors: in explaining these
miracles Augustine removes the body's weight and bulk. I grant it, but only to
this extent: not that the body had neither weight nor bulk in itself, but that
neither its weight hindered walking nor its bulk hindered passage. Otherwise,
why do you not attribute the very same thing to Peter's body, since Augustine
affirms the same of Peter? But let us grant that the weight and that dense bulk
of body were entirely removed—for I do not doubt that glory took away those
qualities of weakness and also perfected the natural qualities. Did the
Divinity therefore swallow up the nature of a body, that is, its being finite
and circumscribed?

[PDF page 45; printed page 254]

Since the other matters reported in this chapter merely repeat the same claims
to which we have already responded a thousand times—about invisible presence,
the excellence of faith, the absence of the body, and the emptiness of the
signs—why refute them? If this foundation of Augustine remains firm, concerning
Christ's body circumscribed in heaven and absent from earth with respect to a
corporeal mode of presence, though not with respect to communication, which
occurs spiritually through faith, why, I ask, do you contort yourself in order
to drag Augustine's remaining statements about the presence of the body and
the eating of the ungodly into your camp?

### In reply to chapter twenty-seven: On Augustine's passage from Tractate 30 on John

In Augustine's words where he says that we must hear the Gospel as though we
were hearing the Lord present and speaking, we say that the particle *as
though* must be considered, because it sufficiently indicates the absence
according to the flesh of him who is present by the Spirit in his Word. But you
wish this to be restricted not to the presence but to the mode of presence,
which is invisible. Yet with what shame do you do so, when the person who is
present, although invisible, is nevertheless present, so that there is no place
here for the particle *as though*? How much better it would have been to take
refuge in the varying use of this particle! But this did not occur to you; and
if it had, it could readily have been refuted by what we discussed in the
preceding chapter concerning Augustine's view of these matters.

You then range widely to prove that this passage must be understood of
preaching alone, which you also prove from the writings of a certain old friend
of ours. But what does this have to do with Calvin? Not only did he hold no
different view, but on the last page of Defense 85 he expressly contends that
the truth of the body diffused everywhere must be referred only to the doctrine
of the Gospel. Yet what is said of presence generally may fittingly be applied
to the Sacraments of both Baptism and the Lord's Supper. For if we consider the
thing itself, we say that Christ is present in the Sacraments more effectively,
but not in a different manner, than in the Word.

Augustine writes on Psalm 33 that “the sacrifice of Christ's body is diffused
throughout the whole world.” Calvin affirms that this must be understood of the
fruit of his death which flows to the Church diffused throughout the whole
world. You, however, wish it to be referred to the Eucharist. Let it be so;
let it refer to the celebration of the Supper. Will the body therefore be
diffused, rather than the communication of the body—which is truly the fruit of
the Lord's death—being diffused to the individual members? But you wish this to
prove that the body is everywhere present. Then show us that “the sacrifice of
the body” signifies the same as “the slaughtered body.” Even if we granted what
you ask, namely that from this passage it is rightly inferred that the Lord's
body is present to the whole Church whenever and in however many places the
Lord's Supper is celebrated, would it therefore follow that it is present in
the manner for which you contend?

Finally, Calvin said that the verb *to be diffused*, which can be said only of
the members, was perversely applied to the head. You mock this: “For otherwise,”
you say, “those who apply certain Psalms both to Christ and to the Church would
act perversely.” A sharp point indeed—as if Calvin had said generally that
nothing can properly be applied simultaneously to head and members, rather
than saying this specifically of the verb *to be diffused*. But since you now
contend so vigorously that this verb applies as much to the head as to the
members, with what shame will you henceforth deny that you posit a diffusion of
Christ's true body?

### In reply to chapter thirty-six: On ceremonies, and the other following chapters

When you first began to write against us, Westphal, fearing, I suppose, that
there was not enough turmoil already, you also began to harass our churches:
because they had abolished ceremonies that were not useless; because they did
not use private absolution; because they did not administer the Supper
separately to the sick; because in a case of necessity they did not baptize
infants outside the regular order; likewise concerning feast days; because
they did not follow the order of postils; and finally because they divided the
Decalogue differently, with the result that they had no images in their
churches. A point-by-point answer to these charges had made it easy for every
fair judge to understand that we abolished most ceremonies only for the most
just reasons, some as unnecessary and others as superstitious; and that among
the very things you criticized in us were many which we thought could in no way
be tolerated in the Church. Yet you rose again to mix heaven with earth. I
shall answer briefly, so as not to do what has already been done.

First of all, I maintain that ceremonies in God's Church must be as few and as
pure as possible. Besides the fact that we must now worship the Lord in spirit
and truth, experience itself, the teacher of fools, ought at least to warn us
not to imitate the example of those who, by placing no limit on ceremonies, did
not enlarge God's worship but completely buried it. We therefore thought that
we must consider abolishing ceremonies rather than establishing them. But
here, since the character of peoples differs, and the progress of the Word

[PDF page 46; printed page 255]

also differs, what injury has been done to the Saxon churches if
the Lord granted us freely to obtain many things from the peoples entrusted to
our faith which elsewhere might perhaps have been attempted without success?
Was that a reason for you to slander us as though torn away from the other
churches and expose us to everyone's ill will? Yet we do not conceal this
either: it seems to us that some have perhaps been, and still are, excessively
fearful in cleansing the churches from every dreg of the papacy. Indeed, we
think some things are tolerated which ought not to be borne at all. We are not
so greatly troubled by those linen vestments that we would wish the progress of
God's Word to be disturbed even slightly on their account. Nevertheless, we
judge that those who cast out from the churches that entire costume, more
suited to actors than ministers of the Word, and those remnants of paganism,
acted rightly and wisely. For they can certainly do the greatest harm, as the
event itself has taught, and can do no good whatever. But we certainly do not
number the restoration of things once cast out among matters indifferent.

We think that placing images and statues in churches is forbidden a thousand
times by God's express Word. For why are they placed there except for worship?
Is it for the sake of remembrance? Yet the Holy Spirit is wiser than we are.
Although we do not doubt that those who first brought them into churches had
that purpose in view, the horrible image-mania with which the world still
seethes ought sufficiently to have warned us that images must in no way be
tolerated in churches. Epiphanius' deed is well known: he tore a curtain on
which there was an image of Christ crucified, although, as I judge, it had not
been set forth for worship. From this deed at least we ought to have learned
what the Church's appearance was while she was still like a pure virgin. What
serves the Church's use should have been measured by this form rather than by
the inventions of our brain. The matter itself certainly cries out that those
who thought they could adorn the Church better than those Apostles who were her
bridal attendants instead painted cosmetics upon her. If Hezekiah most justly
destroyed the bronze serpent erected by God's own command, will it be held
against us that we cleansed our churches of the very nurses of idolatry?
[Marginal reference: 2 Kings 18:4.] Meanwhile, which of us cast down idols
except through the magistrate? And would that, just as ministers piously labor
to tear the roots of idolatry from human minds, Christian princes would also
remove every object of idolatry entirely from the sight of the godly.

We have already spoken about the adoration of Christ in the bread. Why defend
the ringing of the bell, when it has no other purpose? What place can there now
be for the elevation where there is no offering of the things collected? And
why was it necessary to defend the lighting of candles under the pretext of
winter? For when summer comes, you are left without any defense, though you
deny that it is Judaism. It never occurred to us to accuse anyone there of
Judaism. And although we cannot approve any of those practices, who ever heard
from us the haughty censures of which you thought us worthy? Indeed, we have
never even wished to deal with these matters publicly. We do not doubt that
they displease all the best and most learned men; and in our own case we
experience daily how many things must often be tolerated in that laborious
office, matters of which no one can judge impartially unless he knows every
circumstance. We have therefore continually prayed, and do pray, that the Lord
would grant obedient peoples to ministers and prudent ministers to peoples;
and, above all, that he would govern and strengthen us daily by his Holy Spirit
so that we may see what serves the churches' use and each of us may happily
rebuild the ruins of the old building in his own place.

In the Lord's Supper our effort has been diligently to impress a doctrine that
does not diminish the Sacraments in the least, but above all lifts minds to
heaven; to have solemn prayers; and in the action itself to conduct everything
with the greatest reverence, yet the greatest possible simplicity. For we wish
at least to learn wisdom from our forebears' losses, and we judge nothing purer
than the Lord's Word and the purity of that most pure Church under the Apostles.
Reclining at the table, which you reproach us with, Westphal, is no more known
to our churches than something they have never heard of. Yet we would not wish
to condemn any brethren for that custom, although it is not necessary.

As for infant Baptism, we have testified more than sufficiently that we think
of Baptism only as the holy washing of regeneration. Meanwhile, we do not think
our salvation depends upon the Sacrament of Baptism, but upon our adoption
according to the covenant formula, “I will be your God and the God of your
offspring.” [Marginal reference: Genesis 17:7.] We carefully see to it that this
adoption is sealed in the children of the saints from the Lord's Word by the
Sacrament of Baptism. If any parents neglect this, they are admonished as
severely as is fitting. Since everything in the Church must be done decently,
we regularly administer Baptism only in the church at appointed hours, and by
ministers. But if anyone's child should happen to die before it could be
presented

[PDF page 47; printed page 256]

for Baptism, we have not the least doubt of the child's salvation, because we
think that contempt for Baptism, not deprivation of it, is deadly. When the
Lord has not granted that an infant be baptized according to the Church's
customary order, there can be no contempt for Baptism. We administer no
Baptisms in private houses. If we did administer them, we would employ only
ministers and would especially reject women. It is certain that these
corruptions arose from no other source than the opinion—which we judge utterly
false—that Baptism is absolutely necessary for salvation. On the contrary, a
person must be adopted by God before he can rightly be baptized. Otherwise,
why is a confession of faith required of adults before they are baptized? From
the covenant formula, however, we judge that the children of the saints are
already adopted in the womb itself, and therefore we baptize them. Your
inference from this that we think believers' children are born without sin is
an extremely gross slander. We consider your claim that they are holy before
Baptism—that is, within the Church's external society—to be foolish. On the
contrary, those whom we already maintain to have been truly adopted by the Lord
according to the covenant formula are brought into the Church's external
society through Baptism. For, as is fitting, we leave hidden judgments to God.

You offer an analogy to teach that Baptism is not profaned by women if they
administer it: they are also permitted to comfort the sick and teach their
household at home, you say, and yet they do not thereby invade another's
office. This analogy, I say, is exceedingly foolish. The private and domestic
handling and application of the Word belongs to everyone. But who would bear
women holding assemblies and preaching at home? The administration of the
Sacraments is the other part of the public ministry. Therefore, women and all
private persons must no more be admitted to administering Baptism than the
Supper. They are no less forbidden to baptize than to speak in the Church,
since the rationale of these parts of the public ministry is the same. Indeed,
the ancient councils also condemned this boldness of women, which arose from
no other source than that opinion of necessity I mentioned. If you approve
baptizing in private houses—which we do not approve when the Lord grants that
the Church can assemble publicly—why are ministers not called instead? Or why
do you not permit women the same thing in the Lord's Supper itself, if someone
who is sick and soon to die urgently requests it? We therefore ask that these
matters be carefully weighed. If anyone teaches us something better from God's
Word, we will gladly change our opinion.

We do not administer the Supper privately to our sick, and they themselves do
not demand it. They have been so instructed that they know their salvation does
not depend absolutely upon the Sacraments, deprivation of which is not deadly
in itself, though contempt is. A person whom the Lord does not enable to come
to the public assembly cannot seem to despise them. For the Sacraments are a
public action; and private Suppers also seem to us to conflict with the nature
of this Sacrament, which is called *communion*. Nevertheless, if this can be
observed somewhere without superstition and offense, and indeed with the
Church's edification, and the infirmity of the sick so demands, we would
certainly not wish to split churches for that reason. But in turn, let us be
permitted to consider what is expedient to retain in our churches.

We have said elsewhere in this same little book what honor we accord private
absolution—from imposing its necessity upon the Church it is certain that
auricular confession arose—and Calvin taught it copiously in book 4, chapter
19 of the *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, where he discusses repentance.
Those who so press its necessity that they make it a third Sacrament must also
contend with you, since above in chapter 32 you numbered with all the ancients
only two Sacraments to which the promise of forgiveness of sins was attached.

For two reasons we do not follow the division of the Decalogue in which the
commandment against making images is joined with the first commandment. First,
we see that in this way the second commandment was entirely removed from human
memory, surely by a satanic fraud, so that image-mania might the more easily be
established. Second, the tenth commandment against coveting is thereby torn in
two. Yet our distinction is not new, as appears from some of the ancients; and
even if it were new, no one of sound judgment, I think, will deny that it is
suitable, nor can anyone seize from this a just occasion of offense. Otherwise,
provided that the second commandment is plainly expressed, we will contend with
no one on this matter.

As for feast days, by God's grace we know what the Church's liberty is. Indeed,
we ourselves use this liberty in such a way that we are not at all offended
that our churches do not all follow the same practice in observing them. The
matter itself cries out that leisure has been nourished and countless vices
have grown from the multitude of feasts. Especially from those

[PDF page 48; printed page 257]

feast days consecrated to the memory of Mary and the saints, would that the
world had never experienced the flood of superstition that flowed! Since each
person must consider what the Church's benefit demands here, what injury have
we done anyone if, insofar as the Lord granted it to us, we took care to abolish
even the traces of superstition as far as possible? Would that in this matter,
as in all others, every minister in other regions surpassed us; and may the
Lord grant them to obtain from their princes and peoples everything purest and
best. But there was certainly no reason for us to be harassed about these
matters by your agitation; and we hope to prove this to every fair judge.

As for what they call *postils*, it is certain that this dissection of the
Divine Word was unknown to the purer Church and hardly ever prevailed in the
East and Africa. The continuous homilies and expositions of the teachers to the
people show this, although on certain days—which at first were very few—special
histories were read and explained in the Church.

Some think that this custom was introduced to take account of private persons
who are less suited to reading or even hearing the books of both Testaments in
continuous order. But what if this custom too instead gained strength from the
bishops' laziness, as if it were sufficient to keep a certain number of
passages ready to hand? To those most negligent pastors must also be attributed
the fact that the exposition even of those passages was soon removed, leaving
only their recitation in the Masses, and that in a foreign language with no
interpreter employed, contrary to the Apostle's express decree. Finally, as
the number of festivals increased—to such a degree that the Roman Church calls
individual days *feriae*—what they call Gospels were consecrated, some to
martyrs, some to confessors, and finally particular Gospels to most of their
gods, whether real or fictitious. At last Satan prevailed so that not even one
pastor in a thousand inspected the sacred books themselves throughout his
whole life.

The very name *postil* certainly betrays barbarism, and to tear apart in this
way things that cohere seems almost sacrilege to us. In our churches,
therefore, the expositions of the sacred books are continuous. On this occasion
we also customarily exhort the people diligently to attend sacred sermons
regularly rather than at intervals, so that they may receive doctrine in a
continuous sequence with greater benefit. You certainly could not rightly
condemn this custom of ours, Westphal, which rests both upon reason and the
example of antiquity and whose very great usefulness we experience in our
churches. But if experience has taught some people that God's Word can be
taught fruitfully in another way, by all means let them enjoy their own
judgment, provided that they too leave us ours.

As for the insults which you were not ashamed to vomit against the Lord's holy
martyrs, whom papal tyranny daily seizes from our congregations, see for
yourself how you will answer before the Lord. Their writings survive, and,
whether you wish it or not, they will leave their blessed memory to a grateful
posterity. In the name of all Christian churches, I am certainly ashamed that
a character so insolent could be found in any church that it sharpened its
tongue even against those dead men whom their own executioners revered in
death. The Lord surely will not leave such an insult, more than savage and
barbarous, unavenged; to him we commend the cause of his martyrs.

In the remaining part of your little book you complain of Calvin's insults and
proclaim your own modesty. We do not refuse to let the books themselves render
judgment. “I mixed in nothing,” you say, “that pertains to anyone's insult.”
What shamelessness this is! I will cite just one passage from page 101 of this
very *Apology* to which I respond, so that the whole world may know what your
modesty, shame, and conscience are, speaking so foully about matters so false,
so unverified, and so entirely alien to your undertaking. In opposing a carnal
mode of eating by an argument from absurdity, Calvin several times used the
words *devouring* and *gorging*. What do you say to this, Westphal? “Calvin
speaks very religiously and reverently,” you say, “belching devouring and
gorging from his upset stomach.” Clearly you know Calvin well, I see: this
whole city can testify that he gives such little consideration to himself in
food and drink that in this respect he sometimes appears to his friends to err
not slightly.

When you were aggrieved that Calvin had reproved you for drunkenness, Calvin
answered what the case is: he had spoken of drunkenness of the spirit. He
explained at length why he had been driven to that harshness of words. But you
did not consider yourself answered unless, outside the case and with the
greatest license to lie, you hurled back a twofold insult. What more? “Calvin
thrusts forward words suited to whores' ears and eyes,” you say, “which perhaps
he learned from his mother, a concubine of a papal priest.” Is that so, you
buffoon? Rather than deny yourself, you chose to afflict with truly whorish
taunts a most honorable matron long since dead and the mother of that man to
whom the many labors he has undertaken testify how much the Christian Church
owes—and, I trust, a more grateful

[PDF page 49; printed page 258]

posterity will testify in the future. But I will restrain myself and consider
what befits us rather than what befits you. If it were necessary to prove by
witnesses that Calvin was born both of an honorable station and to parents of
the most unblemished reputation, and was raised from childhood in a most noble
household, we could summon not just one witness but the entire city of Noyon.
We therefore expend no effort refuting this insult. You should instead
consider with what shame you dare proclaim your modesty and speak of anyone's
insults or filthy language, when you are not ashamed to lie so shamelessly
about the living and the dead.

Why ask who originated this renewed division? Whom did the *Consensus* harm?
What book had we written in which there was even the slightest harshness? We
openly professed what we thought. Disputations against the papists were held in
England by command of the king. What was said or written there that was not
moderate? If you could not tolerate this, did you therefore have to provoke us,
name us as heretics, hand us over to be corrected by the magistrate's sword,
banish us beyond Scythia, and summon all the churches as if by a trumpet blast?

But come: I, the least of all, beseech every one of you who will inspect this
*Apology*. Let us at last consider seriously, not who kindled these fires, but
by what means they may finally be extinguished completely. There have been more
than enough disputes, more than enough insults, more than enough accusations
and apologies; and at last we truly ought to repent that for so many years the
progress of the Gospel has been delayed by these grievous contentions. Thus
far, since our sins have deserved it, let us have contended in hatreds. Why do
we not at last strive instead in love? We certainly have common enemies. We
confess the same God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We think the same about the
Mediator's office, faith, good works, the Lord's Word, the Church, and the
magistrate. Whatever division exists in the sacramental cause, is it, I ask,
of such a kind that we must be split apart and provide sport for our common
enemies?

We do not equate the preaching of the simple Word with the Word clothed, as it
were, in the Sacraments. We agree concerning a true communication of the true
body and blood in the Lord's Supper. We profess that Christ is so present that
he truly feeds us with his flesh and blood unto eternal life. The controversy
concerns only the mode of communication, because we are content with the
spiritual mode that occurs through faith. We understand well enough that this
controversy draws two others with it; but what kind of controversies, I ask,
are they? We know that Christ ascended into heaven, entered the heavenly
kingdom in our name, and that we are governed and preserved by his power,
might, and love alone; we credit wholly to his Spirit whatever good can proceed
from us. Some confuse the session at the right hand with the ascension, but
what disagreement is there in the matter upon which our salvation depends? We
acknowledge that all who unworthily—that is, without faith—eat of the holy bread
and drink unworthily from the sacred cup are guilty of the Lord's body and
blood. Thus we agree on the thing itself here too. This controversy alone
remains: whether they unworthily receive only the symbols, rejecting the body
through unbelief, or whether they also receive the body itself. For the sake of
this controversy about the ungodly, I ask, will the minds of the godly be
disturbed any longer?

If the Lord has revealed anything to anyone, let it be brought forward, but in
a manner that befits Christians. Let a colloquy of learned and godly men be
instituted. Let the authority of Christian princes intervene to soften every
bitterness and—since the insolence of some requires it—to restrain these
unbridled commotions. Let them bring a settled prejudice to neither party, but
a mind prepared to yield to whatever candid and simple truth teaches. In
writing this, it does not occur to me that I, a little man among persons of
such learning and authority, should play the role of arbiter and judge. Who am
I to entertain that thought? Nevertheless, since the occasion has so required,
I wished to testify to God's Church what my disposition is and what my daily
sighs are. I have personally found that not long ago this desire of mine was
not displeasing both to several most illustrious princes and to the most
learned theologians of Germany. In the Lord's name I again humbly exhort and
implore them to consider the settling of this miserable division in the Church
before any other business, for I think nothing equally concerns Christian
princes. And as for our people, I trust that whenever the conditions of a free
and Christian colloquy are offered in which even the smallest hope of future
agreement shines forth, they will not only not shrink back but embrace them
most eagerly.
