Alfonso writes: “One may answer this argument another way: Paul speaks in
those passages about works done before faith was received. By everyone's
agreement these have no merit before God, and we too confess that they
contribute nothing whatever to a man's justification.” Bellarmine, Becanus,
and many others abandon the former view and adopt this answer: Paul excludes
not works following faith and flowing from grace, by which they contend we
are justified, but only works preceding faith and done by the powers of
nature and free choice alone.

Does the apostle exclude from justification only works preceding faith and
done by the powers of free choice alone?

The Roman Catholics named above vigorously defend this; all our writers,
except Grotius and his followers, oppose it with approximately these
arguments.

First, we use the very argument by which Bellarmine refuted the former view:
the apostle simply excludes works, without mentioning the Mosaic law; hence
he excludes not only legal ceremonies but moral works. By the same right we
may say this of works following faith. Paul excludes works generally without
specifying ceremonial works or moral works preceding faith. Therefore he
excludes not only ceremonial works or moral works done by free choice without
faith, but also works following faith and flowing from grace. What Salmerón
said against the former ceremonial-works opinion applies equally against this
second opinion concerning moral works preceding faith: one must not
distinguish

[1679 printed p. 114 | IA leaf 122]

where the law does not distinguish.
