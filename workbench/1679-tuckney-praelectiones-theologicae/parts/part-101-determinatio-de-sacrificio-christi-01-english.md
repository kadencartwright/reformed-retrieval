DETERMINATION AT THE PUBLIC COMMENCEMENT

July 6, 1652, when he was acting in the place of the venerable Dr. Love,

Lady Margaret Professor, who was then ill.

Through his death Christ was a properly expiatory sacrifice for sins.

Christ’s obedience does not abolish Christian obedience.

THE FIRST thesis says two things: first, that Christ was a properly expiatory sacrifice for our sins; and second, that he was so through his death. Both assertions cut down Socinus. For he,

first, as regards Christ’s death, wishes the whole work of the priest to have been performed only in heaven. He maintains that Christ’s death was no part of this work, but its beginning, commencement, or rather some preparation for it, and locates all its use and efficacy almost entirely in these four things:

1. That by decree he could not rise and ascend into heaven to perform his priestly office there and send the Spirit from there without the intervention of this death.

3. That it confirms his doctrine and the way of salvation disclosed by him, so that we may believe him more securely.

3. That it also serves us as an example of patience.

4. That from undergoing this death he has greater care for us in our afflictions and in death itself. This is everything that Socinus attributes to this death. But if more things appear to be attributed to it—as indeed, even by the adversaries’ own admission, most and nearly all the benefits of our redemption are everywhere in sacred Scripture attributed to Christ’s death—he contends that this is said only metonymically, as when what properly belongs to the effect and consequence is said of the cause and antecedent.

[Marginalia: Volkelius, book 3, chapter 37, page 151; Racovian Catechism.]

Second, when we call this death a properly expiatory sacrifice, Socinus indeed acknowledges that it is a sacrifice and, in his own sense, expiatory—indeed a price, a ransom, and a counter-ransom, and thus in some sense satisfactory. Yet he will have none of these terms taken properly, but all metaphorically and figuratively. That is, in a general sense and by analogy: just as a guilty man is absolved when a sacrifice has been offered, and a captive is redeemed when a price has been paid, so through the intervention of Christ’s death the sinner is freed first from sin itself, as they would have it, and then from guilt and punishment. His death intervenes, I say, but not as some price paid in our name. This happens through God’s free remission, not through Christ’s satisfaction; or, if through satisfaction, then satisfaction of the divine will, which Crellius admits, but not of justice; or, if moreover of justice, which he locates in the rectitude of the divine nature, by no means of vindicatory justice. This is Socinus’s mind and, so far as I understand it, the unanimous opinion of his followers.

When, by contrast, we say that through his death Christ was a properly expiatory sacrifice, by “death” we understand:

[1679 new-series fol. 46 | IA leaf 366]

1. Not only bodily death and pains, but also the anguish and torments of his soul—and those arising not merely from the soul’s sympathy with the body, but from God’s wrath immediately sent into the soul, which was owed to us and therefore had to be endured by our surety.

2. Nor, second, do we mean only the final act of this most sorrowful tragedy, completed in the crucifixion itself and its attendant pains, but also all the sufferings of his preceding life. Since, for us now fallen, it remains established that “without shedding of blood there is no remission,” and since God pronounced, “You shall surely die,” our surety had to suffer not only the miseries of life but also the pains of death. We name death especially as his lowliest act of obedience, Philippians 2:8, and as that in which the price of our redemption was finally paid in full. Thus in his undergoing it we have not merely some preparation for his priestly office, but rather its primary and principal part, by which he obtained all those things that, after his ascension, he grants and applies to us by the worth and merit of his death. Hence nearly everything is so frequently attributed to this death, as we said before.

[Marginalia: Hebrews 9:22.]

They say that this was done in this way:

1. His death was necessary so that access to heaven might stand open to him, where he performs everything belonging to a priest. But if no other reason underlay it, what prevented Christ, like Enoch and Elijah of old, from ascending to heaven without the intervention of death? Furthermore, it seems astonishing to us that so many and such great things should be attributed to death, which according to their opinion was merely a necessary condition and some antecedent preparation, rather than to exaltation, which according to them is the proper and immediate cause and accomplishes the whole matter.

2. They also say that sacred Scripture attributes so much to Christ’s death because it chiefly displays to us the love of God and Christ. But in the very thing in which we chiefly find the love of God and Christ, we also expect chiefly to find merit and efficacy. Let this suffice concerning Christ’s death for the present purpose.

We say further in the thesis that this death is a sacrifice. It was typologically foreshadowed by all those legal expiatory sacrifices—not only by the one offered once each year on the Day of Atonement, which the adversaries alone press into their service, but also by the rest—whatever contrary view they may have adopted.

We say, moreover, that it is an expiatory sacrifice, insofar as “to expiate” signifies not only to cleanse but also to pacify and render propitious. The Socinians dwell heavily upon the former sense when they locate expiation largely in the cleansing of hearts.

Although we join sanctification to justification as its perpetual companion, in the matter of expiation we chiefly look to liberation from guilt. This follows Scripture’s usage, where we repeatedly read, “The priest shall make expiation for him, and it shall be forgiven him,” Leviticus 4:20, 26, 31, 35. Volkelius himself draws this from the Hebrew word *kaphar*, which signifies both to expiate and to cover. He would be right if he were maintaining that expiation consists in the removal of guilt, but his design would be utterly bad if he therefore wished to locate it in simple pardon without any satisfaction. To search out these hiding places more fully, then,

we say finally that it is a properly expiatory sacrifice. Thus Christ properly expiates our sins through his death. Properly, I say:

[1679 new-series fol. 47 | IA leaf 367]

1. Not typologically, as did the ancient sacrifices.

2. Not merely ministerially, as did the legal priests.

3. Not merely declaratively, as did those priests and as do ministers of the gospel. For in Christ’s death we seek and find a Redeemer who is not indicative, as they imagine, but truly effective.

4. Finally, not merely metaphorically by delivering us in some generic manner from sin and punishment. For we seek not only the thing, event, or effect produced in any manner whatsoever, but also the manner and means of producing it—truly and really, namely, when as our surety he took our place, paid our debt, and thereby properly expiated us.

To state briefly and once for all our meaning and the state of the controversy, we affirm these three things:

1. In the matter of his death and sacrifice, Christ was our pledge, guarantor, and surety, and most graciously took our place.

2. Consequently, he transferred to himself the whole guilt of our sins and the punishments thereby owed by us, took them upon himself, removed them from us, and thus truly and properly expiated us.

3. To accomplish this completely, he made full satisfaction to divine justice, and particularly to vindicatory justice armed against us by sin.

He made full and complete satisfaction, I say:

1. Not by mere entreaty and petition, but meritoriously, earning pardon for us by strict desert.

2. Not by equivalence, but by paying the debt itself properly and in kind.

3. Finally, not imperfectly and through God’s gracious acceptance, but by perfectly and fully rendering whatever divine justice could require from us as guilty persons.

This is the orthodox faith concerning Christ’s death; this is the plain meaning of our thesis. The Racovians loudly denounce it as deceitful, erroneous, and pernicious; we, by contrast, shall prove by the following arguments, drawn from Scripture’s various forms of speech, that it is most true and above all saving.
