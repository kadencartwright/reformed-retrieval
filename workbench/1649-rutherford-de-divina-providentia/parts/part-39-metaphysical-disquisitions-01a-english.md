### QUESTION I

Whether being is simply prior to nonbeing.

Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics*, book 1, chapter 22: ἡ δὲ καταφατικὴ τῆς
ἀποφατικῆς προτέρα καὶ γνωριμωτέρα, “the affirmative is prior to and better
known than the negative.” *To be is simply prior to not being. The affirmative
is prior to the negative.* Aristotle also, *Metaphysics*, book 4, chapter 4:
ἡμεῖς δὲ νῦν εἰλήφαμεν ἀδύνατον ὄντος ἅμα εἶναι καὶ μὴ εἶναι, καὶ διὰ
τούτου ἐδείξαμεν ὅτι βεβαιοτάτη αὕτη τῶν πασῶν ἀρχῶν, “we have now taken it
to be impossible for something both to be and not to be at the same time, and
through this have shown that this is the most certain of all principles.” This
principle is therefore first, and it is the most firmly established. (*It is
impossible for the same thing both to be and not to be at once.*) But since the
simple is prior to the complex, this is an earlier principle: (*The same is the
same.*) Yet because an identical predication teaches nothing, this supposition
is the simplest—perhaps more properly a supposition than a principle. Thus an
earlier principle still is: (*GOD is,* *GOD understands,* *GOD wills,* and so
forth.) 2. Now this proposition—(*It is impossible for the same thing both to
be and not to be*)—must first obtain its truth in him who is the first same,
whose most delightful name, and more than a name, is אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה
יְהוָה, “I AM WHO I AM, YHWH.”

### QUESTION II. Whether GOD is the ruling cause of beings and nonbeings

It is unnecessary for us to prove that being is prior to nonbeing and the
affirmative prior to the negative, since nonbeing and negation presuppose being
and affirmation, but not conversely. 2. Nonbeing is a removal and distance from
being simply, and a terminus simply *from which*. What is withdrawn from that
terminus is later than it; and an injury—or, as it were, a privation of
being—is later than being simply, just as darkness is later than light and
blindness than sight. But nonbeing is an injury to being and a removal from
being. 3. The first infinite act, whose nonexistence involves a contradiction
(and it is impious to attribute that contradiction to the most beautiful and
supremely excellent Being of beings), is prior to nonbeing simply and in every
way. A nonbeing which denies being, or denies uncreated being and thus asserts
a non-GOD, is posterior to GOD. Or, 2. it denies something belonging to GOD,
namely that most vast brood of possibilities in the womb of most fruitful
Omnipotence. But Omnipotence, as it is GOD’s essence, is prior to that brood,
which has merely possible being because God is omnipotent. Or, 3. it denies
created being, and uncreated being is prior to created being.

Indeed, since God is absolute Lord, as it were, of that most ample brood of
possibilities laid up in the treasury of Omnipotence, he will equally be Lord
of impossibilities by creating the specific forms of things from which their
incompossibilities result, just as he is Lord of possibilities. God exercises
dominion over possibilities in this way: he is omnipotent; and because he is
omnipotent, all possibilities are possible. It is not because they are possible
in themselves and intrinsically that God is therefore omnipotent. Thus God is
Lord of things which will not be—which he could raise to the state of
futurition—just as much as he is Lord of future things. An earthly king has
dominion, though a far inferior one, over a kingdom which, by his created
dominion, he can confer upon a beggar and captive whom he never raises to such
honors. Therefore, in those passages of Scripture in which GOD’s dominion over
created things is asserted, his supreme dominion over uncreated things,
nonfuture things, possibilities, and impossibilities is also vindicated. Nor,
properly speaking, does God’s dominion begin with things or end if creatures
cease to exist, since it is eternal. 1 Chronicles 29:11, 12, 14, 16;
Deuteronomy 10:14; Psalm 89:11.

possible through any real power actually or objectively inhering in those

the possible is something capable of being made through a power outside that
possibility, in God alone who is omnipotent. But if a comparison is made
between future beings and future nonbeings, then just as beings are beings by
dependence upon and participation in the First Being as efficient cause, so
nonbeings and those things

little creatures themselves and to their intrinsic, objective, and imaginary
nature. For their perpetual lack of effective being from the Creator must not
be ascribed to a necessity in the nature of nonbeings, but to GOD’s free will.

### QUESTION III. Whether GOD is the origin and cause of impossibilities and possibilities

The question is not whether God is the cause of the same thing’s being able
both to be and not to be, or of contradictories being true at once, for GOD
cannot effect such things. Rather, it is whether possibilities and
impossibilities come by participation and originally from the First Being as
their efficient cause. We affirm that they do.

1. The principle (*It is impossible for the same thing both to be and not to
be*) first obtains its truth in GOD, the First Being, the most beautiful
fountain, the most causal cause, and pure and most pure truth, before it obtains
truth in beings by participation, in streams, in effects, or in truth by
participation. For because it is simply and absolutely impossible that GOD,
being GOD, should not be the same first and uncreated being, it is therefore
impossible that a created same should not be the same, and it is impossible
for being by participation simultaneously to be created being and noncreated
being. For the most delightful and perfectly united co-identification or
identity shines forth more richly in him who is more truly first and infinite,
and more truly the same, most simple and most high. He alone says, (*I*), אֲנִי
אֲנִי הוּא וְאֵין אֱלֹהִים עִמָּדִי, “I, I am he, and there is no God with me”
(Deuteronomy 32:39). This identity shines more richly in him than in a being
which is the same through the First Being and by participation alone. For God
himself, who gives nature and form, is the cause of identity, concord, union,
and truth in creatures, as he is also the cause of their entity.

2. Προτέρα ἐνέργεια δυνάμεως, “actuality is prior to potentiality,” as the
Philosopher says in *Metaphysics*, book 9, chapter 9. *Act is prior to potency.*
Therefore infinite active power in GOD will be prior to passive and receptive
power in creatures, whether it is called objective, terminative, or by any
other name. God will therefore be prior to every possibility.

3. That is prior from which the consequence cannot be converted. Yet the
principle (*It is impossible for the same thing both to be and not to be*)
would have had actual truth in God under a hypothesis easily within GOD’s
power—namely, if God had produced no things outside himself. But he produced
freely; therefore he could have refrained from producing them. Yet in that case

more absurd. Something would thus be creatable before there was a Creator, or
even if there were no Creator, and passive possibility would be antecedent to
active possibility.

3. That which is infinite, simply being, and absolute—as the attribute of

### QUESTION IV

But the other part of the question is much more difficult: Is anything
impossible unless it is originally impossible from GOD? The answer is that
nothing is impossible in this way.

1. Because God the Creator, to be celebrated forever, creates the
constitutive specific differences—for example, of a goat and a deer—he at the
same time establishes divisive differences. For, as Porphyry says, the same
specific difference both constitutes the species and divides the genus. One
who creates a being creates one, creates something diverse, and creates
something mutually repugnant. One who produces cold produces something which
unites heterogeneous things, and by the same act produces something repugnant
to heat. Therefore, by creating the possible or actual essences of a goat and
a deer, God by the same act creates the incompossibilities between the natures
of the goat and deer. Consequently, the proposition (*It is impossible or
incompossible for the same animal to be both a goat and a deer*) is traced
originally to the Creator, who created the goat specifically and naturally
distinct from the deer; for since those natures are specifically and
incompossibly diverse, that same thing would at once be a goat and not a goat.

[Marginal summary: GOD is the origin of impossibilities.]

2. What is properly and simply impossible is only an incompossible thing which
involves contradiction. It is the complex and interwoven repugnance of two
extremes which are incompossible with one another, of which one cannot be
predicated of the other without contradiction. Therefore, if the specific
forms themselves are not possible, and cannot be produced apart from the First
Omnipotent, then neither can the incompossibilities which result from those
forms. But no possibility exists apart from the First Omnipotent as its cause.
Therefore, much more will nothing be incompossible which results from the
repugnant combination of two specific forms apart from that First Omnipotent.
For if an incompossible is posterior to a possibility as an effect is to its
cause—if, that is, the incompossible or impossible is posterior to the
possible as resulting from it—then much more will the impossible be
originally from GOD and posterior to him.

[Marginal summary: There is no simple or incomplex impossible, but only what
is complex—that is, impossible or incompossible.]

Meanwhile, it must be proved incidentally that there is nothing simple and
incomplex which is impossible among created things. Otherwise GOD’s
nonexistence would be an infinite simple impossibility. Rather, what is simply
impossible is only

mere free nonexercise of creative efficiency. They are therefore possible, and
no repugnance would follow if they were made, taking them all distributively
(for whether all possibilities taken collectively can be actually created is
another question). Neither repugnance nor contradiction would follow, but only
that the same created thing would be the same created thing—not that the same
thing would both be and not be. A possibility is accordingly defined as
*something of such a kind that, if it were produced, nothing impossible or
repugnant would follow.* But if all simple nonbeings were made, nothing
repugnant would follow.

3. In the simple and first operation of the intellect, which lacks composition
and division, there is neither truth nor falsehood, because all truth is in
affirmation and negation, *according as the thing is or is not so,* in the
objective connection of the things which occupy the places of subject and
attribute. A word signifies, but affirms and denies nothing, says Aristotle in
*On Interpretation*, chapter 4: ἄνθρωπος (a simple word or a simple being)
σημαίνει μέν τι, ἀλλ᾽ οὔπω ὅτι ἔστιν ἢ οὐκ ἔστιν, “signifies something, but
not yet that it is or is not.” In precisely the same manner, where there are
not two things in a simple being, one of which can be predicated of the other,
there can be no impossible affirmation, no falsehood, and likewise no
negation; consequently, there can be no incompossibility. Hence philosophers
and theologians unanimously acknowledge that every being and nonbeing is an
object of Omnipotence, except that which involves contradiction, which is
simply impossible—or, more properly, incompossible.

4. The impossible or incompossible conflicts only with that principle which
first obtains its truth in GOD before it does so in creatures: (*The same is
the same*) and (*It is impossible for the same thing both to be and not to
be*). But nothing simple and incomplex is impossible in that way. Whatever
simple being God created, if it had one simple specific form, would not imply
that the same thing both was and was not. You will say: *If GOD created a
goat-stag—that is, an animal consisting of a single

[Marginal summary: A goat-stag can be created by God, and it cannot be created
with diverse simple specific forms.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Part A; printed page 542; IA PDF page 599; BSB canvas 594]

simple essence—he would make contradictories true at once.* I answer: As the
word *goat-stag* is commonly used for an animal to which the essence of a goat
and the essence of a deer simultaneously belong, such an animal is not
creatable, for it would be a goat and not a goat, a deer and not a deer.
Omnipotence cannot draw such a contradictory animal out from the understood
ideas so delightfully united and identified with one another in the First
Being. But an animal which possessed neither the nature of a goat nor of a
deer, but was a creature coalesced and joined from those two forms into a third
essence distinct from each—from both goat and deer—would be as creatable by
GOD as a goat, lion, or deer, and would involve no repugnance or
incompossibility with that first principle.

3. If the impossible is impossible in itself and intrinsically, and receives
the account of its impossibility from nothing—not even from GOD—as Fonseca and
the other Jesuits commonly maintain, then either the negatively impossible or
the privatively impossible, or incompossible as they call it, will be
impossible from itself and intrinsically. But neither should be said. Not the
first, because I proved above that a purely negative simple impossible is not
simply impossible, but possible, and is the simple terminus from which creation
begins, over which God has exercised absolute dominion from eternity. Every
possibility is such in GOD’s Omnipotence; but in itself it is nonbeing, and so
it is nonbeing, not intrinsically, but because of the free denial of creative
influx. Thus darkness is not darkness in itself and intrinsically, except
because the sun is absent—if we suppose the sun freely to emit light, freely
to withdraw light, and freely to make darkness, as God freely creates and
freely does not create.

[Marginal reference: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 9, question 3, §3.]

But concerning the privatively incompossible, Bradwardine proves that it does
not exist apart from GOD: *If this is true—(It is impossible for a Chimera to
be a Tragelaph)—the cause of this truth is not the terms alone, (Chimera) or
(Tragelaph), nor any nonbeing, because what altogether does not exist can cause
absolutely nothing. Therefore some being is its cause, and there is no being
besides GOD.* 2. The complex impossible presupposes a possibility, but the
possibility is causally from the First Power alone. Therefore this impossible
(which by nature is simply impossible) is a repugnance or incompossibility by
which one possible thing or being cannot be another being without
contradiction, and unless the same thing were at once the same and not the
same. And in truth nothing is simply impossible except what implies that a
contradiction should come to be.

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 13.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Part A; printed page 543; IA PDF page 600; BSB canvas 595]

4. Henry of Ghent, Scotus, and Bradwardine rightly think it should be said
*that it is not true to say of something simply impossible, “GOD cannot do this
because it cannot be done”; rather, “This cannot be done because GOD cannot do
it,”* although the Jesuits, especially Fonseca, maintain the contrary. But the
impossible is impossible because it is a nonbeing which involves a
contradiction and is repugnant to being, and especially to the First Being to
whom it is repugnant. For if it is impossible because it is incompossible with
a created being, much more is it impossible because it is repugnant to the
First Being and to the practical idea in the divine intellect according to
which the being to which it is contradictory is formed. The impossible is
therefore a certain defect and departure from the exemplary cause and from the
First Efficient, as blindness departs from sight, and privation or negation
from the substantial habit and act upon which it originally depends.

[Marginal reference: Henry of Ghent, thus in *Quodlibet* 8, question 3.]

[Marginal reference: Scotus, book 1 of the *Sentences*, distinction 43.]

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, question 13.]

[Marginal reference: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 9, chapter 1, question 3,
section 3.]

5. Something cannot be called impossible intrinsically and in itself because
it cannot be made, possessing a passive impotence by which it is repugnant to
being, unless this is because the active power in the First Cause first cannot
make it. But God is not impotent to do something outside himself unless it is
not inwardly contained in the power of Omnipotence or of the uncreated essence.
Yet nothing in GOD involves both being and nonbeing in GOD and is repugnant to
the practical idea of a possible being. Therefore something’s being passively
impossible extrinsically outside GOD is itself impossible only because it
arises from the repugnance it bears to the active power in GOD. Consequently,
it will be impossible, not because it is such outside God and cannot be made by
GOD, but because it is first impossible within God and God cannot make it.

[Marginal summary: Something cannot be made because God cannot make it, not
conversely, “GOD cannot make this because it cannot be made.”]

Hence Oviedo says badly in his *Theological Course*, controversy 10, point 5,
section 2, no. 11: *GOD is able to produce through the intrinsic concept of
Omnipotence, because Omnipotence is presupposed as the principle of possibility,
which is formally possibility through itself; but it is not presupposed for a
Chimera, because, although on God’s side the power which must be presupposed for
the Chimera’s possibility does not fail, on the Chimera’s side intrinsic
possibility—which is possibility through itself—is lacking.*

[Marginal reference: Oviedo, *Theological Course*.]

I answer: there is indeed a distinction between a possibility and a Chimera,
but not in this respect, that the possibility arises from Omnipotence as from a
cause, while the Chimera is antecedent to Omnipotence. For the Chimera is
impossible because it is incompossible; indeed, an incompossible is ultimately
resolved into two possibilities, one of which cannot be predicated of the other
without contradiction. But since possibilities are originally from GOD, and

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Part A; printed page 544; IA PDF page 601; BSB canvas 596]

the incompossible arises originally from two possibilities, it is false that
the impossible is presupposed antecedently to Omnipotence as its origin. I
acknowledge that Omnipotence is not the internal and formal cause of an
impossible thing, but it must be its origin.

6. This impossibility—(*It is impossible for the same thing both to be and not
to be*)—is true in GOD before it is true in a created being. It would be
objectively true in GOD from eternity if we suppose that GOD had freely decreed
from eternity to create nothing outside himself. And since this is true in
created beings because it was first true from eternity in GOD as the Being of
beings, the falsehood and impossibility of the contrary assertion in created
things will exist because it was first impossible in GOD. Thus it seems to me
that this impossibility in creatures—namely, that the same creature cannot at
once be and not be, and cannot fail to be the same as itself—flows from this
origin: that the same Creator and First Being cannot be at once Creator and
non-Creator, and the First Being by essence and not the First Being by essence.
Indeed, that most delightful and most simple unity and co-identification of the
First Being with himself is the reason why it is absolutely impossible for the
First Same not to be the same as himself. It is the per se cause of all created
identity. Consequently, the first repugnance and impossibility, by which simple
nonbeing and the absolutely and essentially impossible—or an infinite
substantial privation of the First Substance—is repugnant to the First Being,
is the origin of every impossibility in creatures.

[Marginal summary: Impossibility in the infinite First Being is the origin of
every impossibility in creatures.]

Objection. *Contradiction is equal and the same in an infinite being and in a
finite being. For it is no more impossible that GOD should not be GOD, and that
the same uncreated being should not be the same uncreated being, than that a
human being should not be a human being. This proposition—(A worm is a
worm)—is just as true as (A lion is a lion), and both are just as true as (God
is God), because it is simply impossible for this or that proposition not to be
true.*

I answer: Allow me to deny that either truth or necessity is equal in (*A human
being is a human being*) and in (*GOD is GOD*). For infinite God is not only a
greater being, but is more truly being—since he is Being through essence,
infinite Being, the First through the most absolute necessity, and per se and
essentially necessary, to whom all contingency is repugnant—than a human being
is being, substance, living thing, or animal. And just as God is more truly
being than a human being, so he is more truly the same and more truly one, with
a far simpler unity and an incomparably more absolute and independent
necessity, than a human being is being,

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Part A; printed page 545; IA PDF page 602; BSB canvas 597]

the same, or one. And thus it is more impossible for GOD not to be GOD than for
a human being not to be a human being, because the first and greater necessity
and truth is in GOD rather than in (*A human being is a human being*). In a
human being, indeed, there is a borrowed necessity and truth, and therefore a
borrowed co-identity and unity, having change and much potentiality and
contingency mixed in. For I judge this saying of Nyssen to be true: *He himself
(GOD) not only stands above every (created) necessity, but is also its Lord and
Maker; for being Omnipotent, and by his own nature Power itself, he does nothing
(outside himself, among creatures) either by necessity of nature or by the
ordering of an (obligating) law; and to demonstrate this, though the sun and
moon are moved by necessity and always behave in one way, at one time he made
them stand still.*

[Marginal reference: Gregory of Nyssa, *On Fate*, chapter 4.]
