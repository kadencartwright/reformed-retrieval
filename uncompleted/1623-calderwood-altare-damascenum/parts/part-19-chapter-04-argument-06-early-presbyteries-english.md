# David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum* — Part 19

Fresh English translation of the paired normalized Latin transcription from
the 1623 first edition. The Princeton/Internet Archive page images for
`altared00cald` are the controlling witness; OCR and the 1708 Leiden reset are
reading aids only. This part begins in the lower portion of printed p. 259
exactly at `Quod ad rem ipsam attinet`, immediately after the conclusion of
the treatment of Jerome’s position. It contains Calderwood’s reply to the
claim that bishops were successors appointed by the apostles and his full
treatment of the earliest bishops as permanent presidents in presbyteries,
not princes. It ends on p. 267 after `Concilium tamen istud recentius est`;
the immediately following argument from presbyters’ votes in provincial
councils is excluded. All quotations, marginalia, citations, and Greek in the
paired Latin are retained. This AI-assisted draft has not received human
review and is not publication-ready.

As to the matter itself, when they say that in the very beginnings there

[1623 printed p. 260 | IA leaf 272 | PDF p. 272]

were no bishops, nor any need of them, because the apostles themselves
visited the churches, but that after bishops had been instituted by the
apostles, the apostles left substitutes behind when they moved on and
designated successors when they died, this is utterly false in both its
parts. For the apostles’ general care and oversight did not remove the need
to exercise ordination and jurisdiction in each particular church. As though
the presbyters of the dispersion could exercise no jurisdiction before Peter
sent letters or a legate from Babylon, or came himself! The apostle rebukes
the Corinthians for having been so sluggish in casting out the incestuous
man, and acknowledges that they possess the power of judging those who are
inside. There is no need to repeat the arguments we presented above, by which
we proved that the power of ordination and jurisdiction belonged to the
presbyters. It is no less false that, when absent, the apostles appointed
vicars and, when departing or dying, designated successors. They bring
forward no examples except Timothy, Titus, and men like them, who were not
ordinary pastors or bishops but evangelists. Of these Chrysostom speaks as
follows in Bilson, sermon 11 on Ephesians 4: *He calls those men pastors and
teachers to whose trust the whole people had been committed; and those who
traversed every region evangelizing were below them, because they were not
occupied in one place.* Moreover, in establishing churches they appointed
presbyters, not bishops; and, when departing, they commended the established
churches to presbyters. Paul did so in Acts 20 when he now thought that they
would see his face no more, and when he stood on the border of death, as is
plain from the Epistle to the Philippians. In churches being established,
presbyters were the first appointed by the apostles, and in churches already
established they were the last left behind. Therefore, by divine institution
they are the apostles’ genuine successors, not bishops who were placed over
presbyters by the custom of the church and by human institution. If Jerome’s
conjecture is certain, then we do not hesitate to assert that the plan for
removing schisms was not the Holy Spirit’s, but Satan’s plan for laying the
foundations of Antichristianism and providing material for schisms. The
outcome proved that the antidote was turned into poison and the remedy was
more harmful than the disease itself.

It must not be supposed that the episcopate about which we are disputing was
introduced immediately after the apostles’ departure. Aristocracy was not
immediately changed into monarchy. The first men who were bishops after the
apostolic age were merely permanent presidents in presbyteries, not princes.
But Bilson says: *This is indeed a genuine description of a city’s mayor and
senators, or of the prefect and decurions of some small town among us; but it
is by no means a description of such a bishop as Christ’s church had or ought
to have had.* And Dounamus says: *Under the name of the ancient bishops they
describe for us deans, who were sometimes called archpresbyters and sometimes
πρωτοπρεσβύτεροι.* Calvin says: *Yet the bishop was not so superior in honor
and dignity that he possessed dominion over his colleagues; rather, the duty
the bishop sustained in the assembly of presbyters was the same as the
consul’s in the senate: to report on affairs,*

*ask for opinions, take the lead among the others by advising, warning, and

man, they say, is not a bishop but a dean. What then? They do not wish the
ancient bishops in presbyteries to have been like consuls in the senate, but
like kings among counselors, whose counsel they used not from necessity but
by their free choice. See the passages we cited above on pp. 113–115. If none
but such men are bishops, those first bishops were bishops in name only, but
in reality merely permanent presidents and πρωτοπρεσβύτεροι. Does not the
author of the commentaries circulated under Ambrose’s name say that a bishop
was nothing other than the first presbyter? What, I ask, does *first
presbyter* signify other than πρωτοπρεσβύτερος? *The first presbyters were
called bishops, so that when one departed the next would succeed him,* says
that author. *For this man is a bishop who is first among the presbyters.* The
same author says in the *Questions of the Old and New Testaments* (for we
showed above on p. 228 from Bellarmine and others that the author was the
same): *What is a bishop but the first presbyter, that is, the highest
priest?* And a little below: *It is disgraceful to call a judge a primicerius.
For in Alexandria and throughout Egypt, if the bishop is absent, a presbyter
consecrates.* So wrote that author, a contemporary of Ambrose or,
as others hold, a little older. On the other side, Pseudo-Ignatius says of the
presbyters in the Epistle to the Trallians that they are *ὡς συνέδριον τοῦ
θεοῦ, καὶ σύνδεσμος Ἀποστόλων Χριστοῦ,* “as the senate of God and the union
(or assembly) of Christ’s apostles.” Likewise: *What is the presbytery but a
sacred assembly—σύστημα—counselors—σύμβουλοι—and the bishop’s
assessors—σύνεδροι?* In the Epistle to the Ephesians he likewise desires a
presbytery united to the bishop as we see strings joined together in a lyre.
In Irenaeus presbyters are called princes. In the epistle entitled *On the
Seven Orders of the Church*, which stands among Jerome’s works, presbyters are
called judges: *For this reason presbyters were commanded from the beginning
to be judges in affairs and to participate in the councils of priests, since
the presbyters themselves, as we read, are called bishops, according to what
is written to Titus,* etc. In Augustine they are *patrons and governors of
the earth*; in Origen’s third book against Celsus, *the church’s βουλευταί.*
Aerius said that the bishop sat on a throne, but so too did the presbyter, and
Epiphanius did not deny it. Nazianzen recounts how divine providence moved
Basil *τοῖς ἱεροῖς τοῦ πρεσβυτερίου θρόνοις, to the sacred seats of the
presbytery*; he says that Basil went from the office of reader to that of the
presbyters, and from there to the bishop’s throne. *In the church of
Carthage,* says Bilson, *the presbyters were of the order of clerics who sat
together with the bishop and took common counsel concerning the church’s
affairs.* He adduces

to a single bishop. *We assemble to call the divine writings to mind,* says

preside, each having obtained that honor by testimony, not by payment.* Grant
that this passage provides no help for the elders whom they call laymen; it
nevertheless teaches that teaching elders presided in chastisements and
divine censures, even in what they call major excommunication. He describes
a particular church and yet says that several elders or presbyters presided.
Irenaeus says: *It is necessary to obey those presbyters in the church who
possess the succession from the apostles and who, together with the order of
the presbyterate, furnish sound doctrine for the instruction and correction
of others. The church nourishes such presbyters.* Bilson admits that the
passage must be understood of presbyter-pastors, while the context shows that
it must be understood of judicial correction: unjust presbyters who condemn
the innocent are condemned there, and upright ones are praised. Jerome says:
*Before God it is not the priests’ sentence but the accused persons’ life

permitted to hand me over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the
spirit may be saved.* Siricius, bishop of Rome, recounts in an epistle to
Ambrose that Jovinian, Auxentius, and the others were condemned for heresy by
the examination of his presbytery and consent of the whole clergy.

of the bishop, as Ignatius records; they governed the church by common
counsel, as Jerome relates; nothing was done in the church without their
counsel, as Ambrose asserts; nor did they lack the vigor of the priesthood,
as Cyprian testifies for us.* From the beginning of his episcopate Cyprian had
determined to do nothing by his own private judgment without the counsel of
the presbyters, and therefore he says: *When every proceeding had been
reported to me, I decided that the presbytery should be assembled.* Elsewhere
he says that he did not dare to prejudge a matter that pertained to the
counsel and judgment of the whole presbytery, nor to claim a common matter
for himself alone. He testifies, in writing to the presbyters and deacons,
that he did this not merely at his discretion but from duty and the bond of

common both what has been done and what is to be done, as mutual honor

pertained to everyone’s counsel and judgment. Bilson says that this is the
genuine sense of Jerome’s words: bishops ought, according to the truth of the
Lord’s ordinance, to govern the church in common with presbyters; but the
government of bishops to the exclusion of presbyters was introduced not by
any institution of Christ or the apostles, but in the course of time, and
gradually grew strong. In Jerome’s age some bishops were indeed aspiring to
this exclusive power, but they had not yet obtained it by any law. *The
church has a senate, an assembly of presbyters, without whose counsel no monk
is permitted to do anything. Rehoboam son of Solomon lost his kingdom for
this reason, that he refused to hear his presbyters. The Romans also had a
senate, by whose counsel they conducted everything; and we have our own
senate, an assembly of presbyters,* says Jerome. See Gratian. Jerome says that
bishops ought to govern the church in common with presbyters because in the
beginning everything was governed by the common counsel of presbyters, before
a bishop was placed over them. This is why Cyprian did not dare to claim for
himself alone an entire matter that pertained to everyone’s counsel and
judgment—not because he had voluntarily bound himself when he undertook the
episcopate, as Bellarmine pretends. *If our brother did this beyond the
requirements of his office, he sinned voluntarily, and all the more gravely
to the extent that the papists cry out that the laws of the church and its
prelates are diminished with greater harm and danger than the laws of the
commonwealth and its princes,* says Tilenus. From what has been said we infer
that all cases and disputes were decided by the common counsel of the
presbyters and bishop, and that bishops did not determine or decree anything

metropolitan had been appointed president of the provincial council, the
bishops could not assemble while neglecting and scorning the metropolitan
unless the very order and degree of metropolitans had first been abolished;
so, once a permanent president of the presbytery called a bishop had been

attempt anything in matters that pertained to their common solicitude and
care, unless that permanent presidency itself had first been abolished. This
is what Cyprian censures in his presbyters when he says that *some of the
presbyters, forgetful of their own station and not considering the bishop
placed over them, claim everything for themselves with insult and contempt
for their superior.* Therefore, when the presbyters just mentioned are said
to have done nothing without the bishop, this must not be understood as

ecclesiastical senate which could render void the votes of the greater part
of the presbyters. Neither must the assertion that bishops could do nothing

metropolitan was bound by the votes of several. That presbyters were judges

Council of Carthage. Augustine too, when elected bishop, calls his presbyters

the saying goes, as a beneficent cause.* Thus, if presbyters are the
colleagues and brothers of bishops, they are not merely counselors. When
