# Response to Several Questions of Laelius Socinus of Siena

John Calvin — 5 June 1555 (date reported by the *Calvini Opera* editors)

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
`latin.txt` and checked against the 1871 facsimile. It does not reproduce or
revise the Beaty–Farley translation. Biblical wording below translates
Calvin's Latin quotations and paraphrases; it is not taken from a modern
English Bible. See the translator's notes after the text.

**[CO X/1, p. 160]**

## Answer to the first question

A familiar rule is that things standing in a subordinate relation do not
conflict.[^1] Therefore, nothing prevents human justification from being free,
arising from God's sheer mercy, while Christ's merit intervenes at the same
time. Accordingly, both God's free favor and Christ's obedience are properly
set over against our works, each in its own order. For Christ could merit
anything only by God's good pleasure; yet he was appointed for this purpose:
to appease God's wrath by his sacrifice and, by his obedience, to blot out our
transgressions. In short, since Christ's merit depends upon God's mercy alone,
which established this way of salvation for us, it is no less properly opposed
to every human righteousness than that mercy is.

### Proofs

God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son, so that whoever
believes in him should not perish (John 3:16). We see that God's love holds the
first place, as the highest cause or origin, and that faith in Christ follows
as the second and more proximate cause. If anyone objects that Christ is only
the formal cause, he diminishes Christ's power far too much. For if we attain
righteousness by faith that rests upon him, the matter of our salvation must
be sought in him.[^2] This is clearly proved in many passages: “Not that we
loved him first, but he first loved us and sent his Son as a propitiation for
our sins” (1 John 4:10). These words clearly show that God established in
Christ a way of reconciliation so that nothing would obstruct his love toward
us. The word *propitiation* carries great weight: in an ineffable manner, God,
at the very time he loved us, was nevertheless offended with us until he was
reconciled in Christ. All the following statements bear on this: “He is the
expiation for our sins” (1 John 2:2). Likewise: “It pleased God through him to
reconcile all things to himself,”

**[CO X/1, p. 161]**

“making peace through the blood of his cross,” and so forth (Col. 1:20).
Likewise: “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not imputing
their sins to human beings” (2 Cor. 5:19). Likewise: “He made us acceptable in
the beloved Son” (Eph. 1:6). Likewise: “That through the cross he might
reconcile both to God in one man” (ibid. 2:15). The rationale of this mystery
must be sought in the first chapter of Ephesians. There, after Paul teaches
that we were chosen in Christ, he adds at once that in him we have obtained
grace. How did God begin to embrace with his favor those whom he had loved
before the world was made, unless because he displayed his love when he was
reconciled by Christ's blood? Since God is the fountain of all righteousness,
it is necessary that, for as long as a human being remains a sinner, he has
God as an enemy and judge. The beginning of love, therefore, is righteousness
of the kind Paul describes: “He made him who knew no sin to be sin for us, so
that we might be the righteousness of God in him” (2 Cor. 5:21). For Paul
means that we, who by nature are children of wrath and alienated through sin,
obtain a free righteousness by Christ's sacrifice so that we may be pleasing
to God (Eph. 2:3).

That Christ truly acquired and merited favor for us with the Father by his
obedience is certainly and firmly gathered from numerous passages of
Scripture. For, as Paul testifies, we were reconciled and received
reconciliation through his death (Rom. 5:10). Yet reconciliation has no place
unless an offense has preceded it. The sense, therefore, is that God—to whom
we were hateful because of sin—was appeased by the death of his Son, so that
he might be favorable to us. Careful attention should be paid to the contrast
that follows shortly afterward: “As through one man's transgression many were
constituted sinners, so through one man's obedience they will be constituted
righteous” (ibid., v. 19). The meaning is that, just as we are alienated from
God by Adam's sin and destined for destruction, so by Christ's obedience we
are received into favor as righteous. The verb's future tense does not exclude
present righteousness, as is apparent from the context. For Paul had already
said that the *gift* arising from many transgressions issues in justification.
Moreover, when we said that favor was obtained for us by Christ's merit, we
mean that we were cleansed by his blood and that his death was an expiation
for sins: “his blood cleanses us from sin” (1 John 1:7). This is the blood
poured out for the remission of sins. If the effect of shed blood is that sins
are not imputed to us, it follows that God's judgment was satisfied at that
price. To this pertains the Baptist's declaration: “Behold the Lamb of God who
takes away the sins of the world” (John 1:29). For he sets Christ over against
all the sacrifices of the law, teaching that what those types represented was
fulfilled in him alone. We know what Moses repeatedly says: “Iniquity will be
expiated; sin will be blotted out and forgiven.” Thus the ancient types teach
us very well the power and efficacy of Christ's death. And this

**[CO X/1, p. 162]**

matter the apostle skillfully explains in the epistle to the Hebrews, taking
this principle: there is no remission without the shedding of blood (9:22).
From this he concludes that Christ appeared once for the
abolition of sin through his sacrifice, and likewise that he was offered to
take away the sins of many. He had said earlier that Christ entered the holy
place once, not through the blood of goats or calves but through his own blood,
having obtained eternal redemption. Then, as he reasons in this manner (ibid.,
v. 13 and following)—“If the blood of a heifer sanctifies with respect to the
purity of the flesh, much more are consciences purified from dead works by
Christ's blood”—it is readily apparent that Christ's grace is diminished far
too much unless we grant to his sacrifice the power of expiating, appeasing,
and satisfying. He adds shortly afterward (v. 15): “He is the mediator of the
new covenant, so that, his death intervening for the redemption of the former
transgressions that remained under the law, those who are called may receive
the promise of the eternal inheritance.”

Above all, we should weigh the analogy Paul describes: that Christ was made a
curse for us, and so forth (Gal. 3:13). It would have been pointless, indeed
absurd, for Christ to be burdened with the curse unless, by paying what others
owed, he acquired righteousness for them. Isaiah's testimony is also clear
(53:4–5): the chastisement that secured our peace was laid upon Christ, and by
his bruise healing came to us. For if Christ had not satisfied for our sins,
he would not be said to have appeased God by taking upon himself the penalty
to which we were liable. This corresponds to what follows in the same place:
“I struck him for the crime of my people” (v. 8). Peter's interpretation also
leaves no ambiguity: Christ “bore sins upon the tree” (1 Pet. 2:24). Peter says
that the burden of condemnation, from which we have been relieved, was cast
upon Christ. Nor do the apostles speak obscurely when they declare that he
paid the price by which he redeemed us from the guilt of death. We are
“justified by his grace through the redemption which is in Christ, whom God
set forth as a propitiation through faith in his blood” (Rom. 3:25). Paul
commends God's grace in this, because God gave the price of redemption in
Christ's death. He then bids us take refuge in Christ's blood, so that, having
obtained righteousness, we may stand before God's judgment. Peter's statement
has the same force: “You were redeemed not with gold and silver, but with the
precious blood of the spotless lamb” (1 Pet. 1:18–19). The contrast would not
fit unless satisfaction had been made for sins at this price. For the same
reason Paul says that we were bought at a great price (1 Cor. 6:20). Nor would
another saying of Paul stand (1 Tim. 2:5): “One mediator, who gave himself as
a ransom,” unless the penalty we deserved had been cast upon him. Therefore
Paul defines redemption in Christ's blood as the forgiveness of sins (Col.
1:14), as if he said that we are justified, or absolved before God, because
that blood answers as a satisfaction. Another passage

**[CO X/1, p. 163]**

agrees with this: “The bond that was contrary to us was blotted out in the
flesh” (Col. 2:14). A payment or compensation is signified, which absolves us
from guilt. There is
also great weight in these words of Paul: “If we are justified by the works of
the law, then Christ died for nothing” (Gal. 2:21). From this we gather that we
must seek from Christ what the law would confer if someone fulfilled it; or,
what amounts to the same thing, that by Christ's grace we obtain what God
promised in the law to our works: “The one who does these things will live in
them” (Lev. 18:5). His later teaching looks to the same point: “God sent his
Son, subjected to the law, to redeem those who were under the law” (Gal. 4:5).
What was the purpose of this subjection unless, by undertaking to discharge
what we were unable to pay, he procured righteousness for us?[^3] Hence comes
that imputation of righteousness apart from works which Paul discusses (Rom.
4): namely, the righteousness found in Christ alone is credited to us. Nor is
Christ's flesh called our food for any other reason (John 6:55) than that we
find in it the substance of life. That power comes from nowhere else than the
fact that the Son of God was crucified as the price of our righteousness. Thus
Paul says that Christ gave himself as a sacrifice with a sweet-smelling aroma
(Eph. 5:2). And elsewhere: “He died because of our sins and rose again because
of our justification” (Rom. 4:25). It follows from this not only that salvation
was given to us through Christ, but also that the Father is now favorable to
us for his sake.[^4] For there is no doubt that in him is solidly fulfilled
what God declares figuratively through Isaiah: “For my sake I will act, and
for the sake of my servant David” (Isa. 37:35). The apostle is the best witness
to this when he says: “Our sins are forgiven because of his name” (1 John
2:12). Although the name of Christ is not expressed, John nevertheless
designates him, in his usual manner, by the pronoun *his*. It is also in this
sense that the Lord declares: “As I live because of the Father, so you too will
live because of me” (John 6:57). Paul's statement corresponds to this: “It has
been granted to you for Christ's sake”—“on behalf of Christ”—“not only to
believe in him, but also to suffer for him” (Phil. 1:29).

## Answer to the second question

Although none are enlightened unto faith or truly feel the efficacy of the
gospel except those who were foreordained to salvation, experience nevertheless
shows that reprobates are sometimes affected by almost the same perception as
the elect, so that even in their own judgment they differ in no respect from
the elect. It is therefore not absurd that a taste of heavenly gifts and a
temporary faith are attributed to them. This does not mean that they
thoroughly perceive the power of spiritual grace and the sure light of faith.
Rather, to convict them more fully and render them inexcusable, the Lord
insinuates himself into their minds to the extent that, without the

**[CO X/1, p. 164]**

Spirit of adoption, his goodness can be tasted. If anyone objects that
believers are then left with nothing by which they may judge their own
adoption with certainty, I answer that, although there is a
great similarity and affinity between God's elect and those who are granted a
transitory faith for a time, the confidence Paul celebrates nevertheless
flourishes only in the elect, so that they cry out with full voice, “Abba,
Father” (Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:6). Just as God regenerates the elect alone forever
by incorruptible seed, so that the seed of life planted in their hearts never
perishes, so he firmly seals in them the grace of his adoption, making it
stable and sure. But this by no means prevents that lower operation of the
Spirit from running its course even in the reprobate. Meanwhile, believers are
taught to examine themselves anxiously and humbly, lest carnal security creep
in under the guise of certainty of faith. Add that reprobates never perceive
grace except confusedly, so that they grasp a shadow rather than the solid
body itself. For the Spirit properly seals the forgiveness of sins in the
elect alone, so that through a special faith they may apply it to
themselves.[^5]

Reprobates are nevertheless rightly said to believe that God is favorable to
them, because they receive the gift of reconciliation, although confusedly
and without sufficient distinctness. This does not mean that they share the
same faith or regeneration with the children of God, but that they have a
beginning in common with them. I certainly do not deny that God illumines
their minds to such an extent that they acknowledge his grace. But he so
distinguishes that perception from the special testimony he gives to his
elect that they do not arrive at its full effect and enjoyment. For he does
not show himself favorable to them in such a way as to receive them into his
keeping, truly rescued from death; he only shows them a present mercy. He
favors the elect alone with a living root of faith, so that they persevere to
the end. This answers the objection: “If God truly displays his grace, it must
be fixed forever.” Nothing prevents God from illuminating some people with a
present perception of his grace which afterward vanishes.

## Answer to the third question

Although faith is knowledge of God's will toward us and a sure persuasion of
its truth, it is nevertheless no wonder that a perception of divine love
vanishes in temporary believers. Such a perception, though akin to faith,
differs greatly from it. God's will is immutable, I admit, and his truth is
always consistent with itself. But I deny that reprobates advance so far that
they penetrate that secret revelation which Scripture reserves for the elect
alone. I therefore deny that they comprehend God's will in its immutability,
or steadfastly embrace his truth, because they stop at a fleeting perception.
In the same way, a tree not planted deeply enough to put down living roots
dries up with the passage of time, even though for several years it produces
not only blossoms and leaves

**[CO X/1, p. 165]**

but also fruit. In short, just as the first man's fall could efface God's image
from his mind and soul, it is no wonder if God shines upon reprobates with
certain rays of his grace which he afterward permits to be extinguished.
Nothing prevents him from lightly tinging some with the knowledge of his
gospel and thoroughly imbuing others.

Meanwhile, the following distinction must be maintained: however scant and
weak faith may be in the elect, because the Spirit of God is a sure pledge and
seal of their adoption, his engraving can never be erased from their hearts;
the reprobate, on the other hand, are sprinkled with a light that afterward
perishes. Yet the Spirit is not deceitful, even though he does not quicken the
seed that he casts into their hearts so that it may remain incorruptible
forever, as it does in the elect.

## Answer to the fourth question

Since the teaching of Scripture and daily experience establish that
reprobates are sometimes affected by a perception of divine grace, a certain
desire to love in return must necessarily be stirred up in their hearts. Thus
a pious affection flourished in Saul for a time, so that he loved him;
knowing that he was treated by him in a fatherly manner, Saul was captivated
by a certain sweetness of his goodness.[^6] But just as God's fatherly
love does not take root in the reprobate, neither do they love him back
steadfastly as children; instead, they are moved by a certain hireling
affection. For that Spirit of love was given to Christ alone on this
condition: that he instill that Spirit into his members. Paul's statement
certainly extends no farther than the elect (Rom. 5:5): “The love of God has
been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us”—that
is, the love which produces the confidence in invocation that I discussed
above.

I also say that, in a wondrous manner, God is angry with his children whom he
never ceases to love. This is not because he hates them in himself, but because
he wishes to terrify them with a perception of his wrath, so as to humble the
pride of the flesh, shake off their sluggishness, and summon them to
repentance. Thus, at the same time, they perceive him both as angry with them,
or with their sins, and as favorable to them. For they sincerely pray that his
wrath be turned aside, yet they flee to him with tranquil confidence.

## Translator's notes

[^1]: *Subalterna* is a technical term for things ordered beneath or in
    dependence upon another. The point is that subordinate causes need not be
    rival explanations.

[^2]: Calvin deliberately uses the causal vocabulary *formalem*, *materia*,
    *summa causa*, and *causa propior*. “Formal cause,” “matter,” “highest
    cause,” and “proximate cause” preserve that structure.

[^3]: The Latin is compressed: *suscipiens praestandum cui nos solvendo non
    eramus*. *Praestandum* is a substantivized gerundive, “what had to be
    discharged”; the translation makes the implied obligation or payment
    explicit.

[^4]: *Eius gratia* is morphologically compatible with “through his grace,”
    but the postpositive idiom and the argument favor “for his sake,” with
    Christ as the antecedent.

[^5]: *Speciali fide* is literally “by special faith.” The following words,
    *in usum suum applicent*, supply the personal appropriation: “apply it to
    themselves.”

[^6]: The Latin (*ut eum amaret, a quo ...*) leaves the pronoun's referent
    implicit. The surrounding argument indicates God: Saul temporarily loves
    God in response to a perceived experience of God's fatherly goodness.

The Greek terms embedded in the Latin are rendered contextually:
*ἱλασμόν* (“propitiation”), *χάρισμα* (“gift”), *ἱλαστήριον*
(“propitiation”), and *ἀντίλυτρον* (“ransom”). Calvin also prints *αὐτοῦ*
(“his”) and *ὑπὲρ χριστοῦ* (“on behalf of Christ”). Scripture references
and, occasionally, their verse numbers follow the 1871 edition even where
they differ from modern English editions.
