CHAPTER VI.

VERSE 1. There is an evil I saw under the Sun, and it is very great upon
human beings.

Now, to return to that earlier matter of riches preserved to their owner's
own destruction: I observed this evil spreading and extending as widely as
the habitable world illuminated by the Sun's splendor.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA37 | printed pp. 37–38]

I also observed that it is exceedingly grievous and dreadful among human
beings.

VERSE 2. A man to whom God himself gives riches, resources, and honor, and
whose soul lacks nothing in which it might delight, yet God himself does not
grant this man the ability to enjoy that thing, but a stranger enjoys it:
this is vanity and the worst affliction.

What I mean is this: I saw a man to whom God most generously granted riches,
resources, honors, and, in short, everything that can be sought and desired
for living comfortably and pleasantly in this life. Yet he did not give him
the disposition by which he might cheer himself through the use and enjoyment
of these goods. Instead, he preserves them until they are plundered from him
by someone who did not lift even a finger to obtain them and who devours them
all when they have passed to him. I call this not only the uttermost vanity,
but also something filled to the utmost with troubles and grief.

VERSE 3. If someone fathers a hundred children and lives many years, however
numerous the days of his years may be, if his soul is not satisfied with that
good, and consequently no burial is his, I say that a stillborn child is
better than he.

Even if he fathers a hundred sons and daughters and becomes the parent of a
fine and numerous offspring—which is rightly counted among God's blessings—and
even if he prolongs his life for many years—which itself is also rightly
reckoned among God's blessings—nevertheless, if his soul is not refreshed by
sharing in his own goods, and if, because he is stripped of every good, a
proper and honorable burial is not paid to him, I would prefer to him a
stillborn child, however despised by human beings.

VERSE 4. For he comes in vanity and walks in darkness, and in darkness his
name is covered.

Since the avaricious man has obtained none of the things for whose sake human
beings come into this world, he may be considered to have been brought forth
into the light in vain. Then, lacking the honor of burial, he departs from
this life as though in darkness, and afterward no mention is made of him
among human beings except with disgrace.

VERSE 5. This one also has not seen the Sun or experienced it; it is more
tolerable for this one than for that one.

In the same way, the stillborn child has not looked upon this light or
experienced it. To this extent, indeed, the condition of each is equal. But
the lot of the stillborn child is better because, beginning and ending life
at once, it has not experienced the calamities and miseries of this life in
which the avaricious man is entangled.

VERSE 6. Even if he lives two thousand years and does not enjoy good, do not
all these go to the same place?

If someone should object here that the avaricious man must be preferred to
the stillborn child because he survives for many years, I reply that even if
he prolongs this life for two thousand years, but receives no comfort or
pleasure from his accumulated riches and honors, I regard his condition as no
better than the former. This is especially so because by this length of life
he has done nothing to prevent himself, just like the stillborn child, from
seeking the same place, namely, the dust.

VERSE 7. Though all a person's labor comes to his mouth, his desire is still
not satisfied.

Since everything a person obtains by grievous labor is directed toward the
food and care of the body, and since nature is content with few things, this
insatiable desire to possess must be regarded as entirely mad and monstrous.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA39 | printed pp. 39–40]

VERSE 8. For what more does the wise man have than the fool himself? What is
there for the poor man to enjoy as he walks before the living?

To this is added the fact that the intelligent man has no advantage over the
fool either in the possession of riches or in receiving their fruits. And
what does the poor person lack, provided he is prudent and understands how to
conduct himself among everyone, that the rich and likewise intelligent person
possesses for passing through this life?

VERSE 9. You will say that the sight of the eyes is better than the wandering
of desire; this too is vanity and affliction of spirit.

Someone may object that the condition of the possessor is better than that of
the poor man who sets neither end nor measure to his desires. But this very
possession of riches, when separated from use and enjoyment, is vain and
empty. I add that it is by no means separated from the mind's trouble and
anxiety over preserving riches.

VERSE 10. Whatever exists has already received its name, and it is known to
be man; nor can he contend with one stronger than himself.

Whatever may exist, no accumulation of riches, however great, can cause man
not to be man or to surpass the fragile human condition to which, because of
sin, he has been subjected by God's decree. Although he may struggle
vigorously against it, he can never struggle free from it.

VERSE 11. When possessions are many, they multiply vanity. What more does
that man have?

If riches and household property increase, vanity and the care of guarding
and preserving them grow in proportion to their growth, so that no advantage
returns to a person from this abundance and opulence of possessions.

VERSE 12. For who knows what is good for man in this life, during the number
of the days of his utterly vain life, which he passes like a shadow? Who will
tell a person what will afterward befall him under the Sun?

To the frailty and uselessness of riches is added the weakness of the person
who possesses them. For who has grasped what is useful and expedient, from
which a more advantageous use of the riches he possesses might arise? This is
especially difficult since he is confined within such narrow spans of this
life, which resemble a fleeting and vanishing shadow, that he is not granted
the opportunity to learn more. And if he is often ignorant of the things
before his eyes, who can tell him what will happen under the Sun in the
future? Yet this would be necessary for the advantageous preservation or
distribution of his riches.

CHAPTER VII.

VERSE 1. A person's good name is better than the finest ointment, but the day
of death is better than the day on which he is born.

Having considered the vanity of riches in itself and separately, I now wish
to pursue their vanity more fully by comparing them with other things, so
that, if possible, we may at least diminish an immoderate zeal for them, even
if we cannot remove it completely. First, I prefer an honorable reputation
and esteem obtained by excellent deeds, though fleeting and exposed to
slanders, to the most precious riches, among which I place costly ointment. I
also judge that the day of death, which cuts off every use of riches from
mortals, should be preferred to the day of birth, which opens the way for
enjoying them.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA41 | printed pp. 41–42]

VERSE 2. It is better to enter the house of mourning than to enter the house
of feasting, because the former is the end of every person; and one who lives
will lay it upon his heart.

To make more evident this superiority of death over riches, I would venture
to affirm boldly that it is better to enter a house in mourning over someone
who has died than a house where a sumptuous and lavish banquet is being held.
For in the former, if you consider the dead person, there is the final end and
conclusion of everyone; if you consider the living, excellent fruit is
received from inward meditation upon death. But at a banquet the ease of a
fall into evil is too plain to require explanation.

VERSE 3. Indignation is better than laughter, because through sadness of
countenance the mind is made better.

I shall say more. Though it may seem foreign and absurd to the corrupted
reason of human beings, it is beyond dispute that the sorrow brought by a
person's death far surpasses the gladness or laughter arising from a banquet.
For although a gloomy countenance caused by sorrow is both more unpleasant to
the sorrowing person and more disagreeable to others who look upon it,
nevertheless, when people consider that it supplies medicine against the
diseases of the mind and crushes the fibers of vice, it does not come to them
as something unwelcome.

VERSE 4. The mind of the wise will be in the house of mourning, but the mind
of fools in the house of gladness.

What I say will be even clearer from the fact that the minds of the wise
dwell in the house of mourning even while they celebrate joys and glad days;
conversely, fools think about glad things when they are invited to mourning.

VERSE 5. It is better to hear the rebuke of a wise person than for someone to
hear the song of fools.

Furthermore, since things that cause mourning must be preferred to those that
produce gladness, it follows that mourning is to be preferred to joy. The
condition of the person who accepts the correction administered by a wise
person is better than that of one who listens to the flattering praise of
fools, who express a kind of musical harmony.

VERSE 6. For as the crackling of thorns under a pot, so is the laughter of a
fool; this too is vanity.

I do not deny that in these verbal blandishments there is a certain
titillation as if of pleasure. But this delight of fools resembles the
crackling of thorns placed under a pot. For although they make a brilliant
fire for a time and send forth a somewhat larger flame, they are extinguished
in a very short moment, so that this joy cannot be free of vanity. By
contrast, just reproof and chastisement of fault, although it causes pain for
a short time, afterward fills the mind with solid and lasting joy.

VERSE 7. When oppression drives the wise person to madness and a gift
destroys the heart.

Having explained the benefits the living receive from death, it remains for
me to touch briefly upon the pleasure the dead person himself receives. It is
this: he will no longer be subject to the injuries of the powerful, which
nevertheless have such force that they drive even a wise person to madness
unless he is fortified by divine power. If, however, he possesses authority
and is powerful, there is a danger that he may be corrupted by gifts, which
often blind the eyes of the wise. Death alone can free and disentangle
mortals from both of these snares.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA43 | printed pp. 43–44]

VERSE 8. The end of a thing is better than its beginning; one who is patient
in spirit is better than one who is proud in spirit.

When we see some wise people driven in a manner to madness by the oppression
they suffer, and others by the corrupting gifts they exercise upon others, I
would boldly affirm that the end of each affair, which crowns the business as
with a garland, is generally better than its beginning and is more suitable
for passing judgment on the same business. By the same force of reasoning it
follows that a person who is patient and moderate in mind, who bears injuries
calmly and restrains himself from inflicting injury, must be preferred to one
who, swollen in mind, neither knows how to bear injury calmly nor restrains
his hands from the corruption of gifts, because, moved by pride, he is not
content with his present condition.

VERSE 9. Do not be rashly indignant in your spirit, for indignation rests in
the bosom of fools.

Therefore, beware that you are not carried by a certain headlong and reckless
impulse of mind into indignation and impatience. This is especially so
because those who leap suddenly into anger and indignation must be regarded
as belonging nowhere but among shameful fools, particularly if this
indignation is so customary and familiar to them that it rests, as it were,
in their bosom.

VERSE 10. Do not say, “Why were the former days better than these?” for you
would not ask this from wisdom.

In every kind of indignation and impatience, you must diligently avoid
accusing God's most wise and watchful administration. Yet you make yourself
guilty of this when you say, “How is it that the condition of past times is
better than that of the present?” For by not being content with present
things, you plainly pronounce upon yourself the verdict of foolishness.

VERSE 11. Wisdom is good with a possession, and it is an advantage to those
who behold the Sun.

What I have said about contempt for riches is not intended to suggest that
riches have no lawful use. Their use is great, especially if they are joined
with wisdom, which they also adorn, so that, when joined and associated,
riches and wisdom bring mortals a great protection for life.

VERSE 12. For some are in the shadow of wisdom, others in the shadow of
money; but the greater advantage of knowledge is that wisdom can preserve its
possessor alive.

There are times when, through the benefit of wisdom, someone hides as if
shaded from most evils, which scorch a person like the Sun's rays. There are
also times when, through the aid of money, someone rests from other troubles
as if beneath the widely spreading branches of a leafy tree. But if at any
time, as happens, they are separated and divided, I would regard wisdom as
far superior, since it often preserves a person's present life and always
preserves his future life.

VERSE 13. Consider the work of God himself; for who can straighten what he
has made crooked?

Through the work of this wisdom, a person calmly and patiently bears those
things that God, by his most just and wise judgment, has made crooked and
confused in the administration of the commonwealth, the Church, and the
household, when it is plainly evident that no mortal can resist them.

VERSE 14. In a time of good, dwell in good; but in a time of evil, make use of
it. God himself also makes this over against that, so that a person cannot
discover anything of the things that lie before him.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA45 | printed pp. 45–46]

So that you may bear more equitably the evils sent by God that you cannot
drive away, freely enjoy prosperous circumstances, of which God grants an
abundance, provided you do so moderately. Conversely, when adversities press
upon you, take care that you do not succumb through impatience and dejection
of mind. For God has weighed and balanced each of them as upon a goldsmith's
scales so that we may be fit for endurance. Yet he sometimes executes this by
such a hidden and secret method that no mortal can follow by human reason the
footprints of God administering these things.

VERSE 15. I saw all these things in the days of my vanity: there is a
righteous person who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked
person who prolongs his days in his wickedness.

I set forth all these things not only theoretically but also as one taught by
experience, things I learned within this narrow span of my life. In
particular, I learned by experience what seems foreign and absurd to the
natural man: the righteous person not only perishes, but is overwhelmed on
account of righteousness; likewise, I discovered that the wicked person not
only prolongs his life but extends it on account of his wickedness.

VERSE 16. Do not be excessively righteous, and do not appear to yourself to
be wiser than you are; why should you bring desolation upon yourself?

Since righteous people perish on account of righteousness, just as we must
not turn aside from the rule of righteousness through fear of danger, so it
is foolish to exceed the proper measure in righteousness and, deceived by an
appearance of righteousness, to bring grave danger upon oneself. To avoid
this more conveniently, take care not to trust too much in your own prudence;
rather, remember to be wise with sobriety. Unless you do this, certain ruin
awaits you.

VERSE 17. Do not be excessively wicked, and do not be foolish; why should you
die before your time?

Although wicked people sometimes prolong their lives by their wickedness,
take care that you do not take encouragement from this and devote yourself to
evil. We sometimes fall even amid the highest integrity and incorruption of
life, and we act wickedly. This one thing must be accomplished: although sin
dwells in us, it must not reign in our mortal body. Therefore, just as being
excessively wise is not without danger, so being foolish and remaining in
ignorance of everything is not far from grave harm. For by pursuing
wickedness and ignorance, you hasten an untimely and premature death, either
immediately from God or mediately through human beings.

VERSE 18. It is better that you take hold of this, and also not withdraw your
hand from that; the one who fears God will escape from all these things.

If you wish to keep a safe course between these two extremes of excessive
righteousness and wickedness, you will find it nowhere by seeking except in
the true fear of God, by which alone you will disentangle yourself from all
the difficulties of this life that surround us. Therefore, it is prudent to
take hold of this fear of God with intense zeal of mind, as with an
outstretched hand, and to hold tightly to it once grasped.

VERSE 19. This wisdom gives more strength to the wise person than ten rulers
who are in the same city.

For this fear of God, which is most justly and deservedly adorned with the
title of wisdom, has more power to ward off evils so that they do not assail
the person imbued with it, and to drive them away when they press upon him,
than many people furnished with forces and authority can exercise for the
safety and preservation of one city alone.

VERSE 20. Yet there is no person so righteous upon earth that he does good
and does not sin.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA47 | printed pp. 47–48]

For no one is so righteous that, while pursuing things good and worthy of
praise, he does not sometimes stray from the aim of what is right and fail in
his duty toward his neighbor.

VERSE 21. Also, do not apply your mind to every word that is spoken, lest you
hear even your servant cursing you.

Do not apply your mind to all the things said or done against the duty owed to
you in such a way that you pursue every injury inflicted upon you. I shall say
more: there are times when you should pass over in silence the curse and
reproach of your servant or subject, which you overheard without his
knowledge or learned from the speech of others, and turn a blind eye to it as
though entirely unaware.

VERSE 22. For your mind is also conscious that you yourself have often cursed
others.

So that you may more willingly pass this over in silence, call to mind—as
your conscience will readily suggest to you—that you have sometimes slandered
others with impunity and gnawed at their reputation.

VERSE 23. I tested all these things by wisdom when I said, “I am wise,” but
wisdom itself was far from me.

I attempted to investigate the things that follow, like those I treated
before, when the fear of God—which most deservedly bears the name of the
highest wisdom—prevailed in me. Although, relying on the incomparable gift of
wisdom and understanding that God had bestowed upon me most abundantly, I
promised myself an accurate knowledge of the things I was about to examine,
I nevertheless finally perceived by experience that I was far removed from
that absolute perfection of wisdom.

VERSE 24. Whatever has always been is exceedingly distant and exceedingly
deep; who can find it?

For wisdom lies at such a great distance, as it were, and is sunk so deeply,
as if in the very center of the earth, that no one can attain it by striving
after it or penetrate and reach it by searching.

VERSE 25. I turned myself, and indeed my whole mind, to knowing, exploring,
and seeking wisdom and its reasoning, and likewise to knowing the wickedness
of folly and the foolishness of every madness.

Yet I did not lose heart because of this difficulty and cast aside the study
of wisdom I had once undertaken. Rather, I turned my mind in every direction
and applied it completely, so that I might know, explore, and investigate;
that is, so that I might enter upon every path and method by which I could
grasp and understand wisdom and the causes upon which it rests and relies. To
achieve this more exactly and accurately, it seemed good to me to consider in
my mind every form of the worst foolishness, the most foolish and wicked
madness of ungodly people, their wandering from virtue, and the causes and
reasons for the same.

VERSE 26. But I found something more bitter than death, namely, that woman
whose heart holds nets and snares and whose hands hold bonds. The one who
appears good before God himself is delivered from her, but the sinner is
captured by her.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA49 | printed pp. 49–50 | ends before Chapter VIII]

Applying myself to this matter and first directing my mind to the
investigation of folly, I discovered that a woman may be found whose wicked
company it would be profitable to redeem even by death, since, though sweeter
than honey and softer than oil in words and outward appearance, she is in
reality more bitter than death. Her mind is so clever and ingenious in
devising evils that she weaves frauds and deceits for destroying human beings
just as bird-catchers weave nets and traps for catching birds. Once they are
ensnared, she holds them captive as though she had defeated them in battle.
No one enticed by her escapes being overcome by her plots except the one whom
God embraces with his free love; but the slave of sin is not rarely held
entangled in her nets by God's most just judgment, especially when he is
allured by her blandishments.

VERSE 27. Behold, I found this, says Ecclesiastes, by seeking once and again
to attain the reasoning.

Pay attention, then, so that you may guard yourself against this plague. For
I learned this with complete certainty when, coming to my senses, I had been
gathered again to the Church, so do not withhold your trust from me. I found
it by diligent investigation of this truth, repeated again and again, so that
you may never call it into doubt hereafter.

VERSE 28. The further my soul seeks the reasoning, it nevertheless does not
attain it: I may understand the reasoning of one man out of a thousand, but
not that of a woman furnished with all these things.

Yet I could not attain what I further investigated concerning this woman's
manifold and tortuous manner of acting. And certainly, although the designs
of wicked men are sunk in the depths so that no one readily grasps them, it
would nevertheless be easier to understand the reasonings of several men
than those of a single woman.

VERSE 29. Behold, I found only this: God himself made man upright, but they
have sought out many reasonings.

I would not have you refer the perversity and wickedness found among men, and
especially among women, either to the origin of creation or to the distinction
of sex, which were created and instituted very good and even holy by God Most
High. Rather, refer it to man himself, who abandoned the heavenly light with
which God had imbued him in the purity of nature and instructed him by his
most holy command, and who fell away from divine obedience by lending his ear
to Satan. This perversity, taking its beginning in Adam and being conveyed
and derived to all his posterity by the law of propagation through God's most
just judgment, acquired strength as it advanced, so that human beings, by
indulging their own thoughts and speculations, made themselves guilty both of
the foulest sins and of the most exquisite punishments.

VERSE 30. Who is equal to that wise person, or to one who knows the
interpretation of a matter? A person's wisdom so illuminates his face that
its firmness is doubled.

Now, to return to the investigation of wisdom that I promised above: just as
I demonstrated infinite and unsearchable folly, so the admirable power of
wisdom must be explained. For who by searching will be found equal to the
wise person, especially to one who, being well imbued with wisdom himself,
knows how to teach it to others? Wisdom makes the face of the person
instructed by it gracious and venerable, and causes others to honor him. Just
as it adds strength to an otherwise despised person, so it adds double
strength to one already endowed with power, making him dreadful and
formidable to the ungodly.
