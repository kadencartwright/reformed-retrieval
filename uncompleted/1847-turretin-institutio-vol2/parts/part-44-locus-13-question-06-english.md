QUESTION VI.

THE TRUTH OF THE INCARNATION AND THE HYPOSTATIC UNION.

*Did the Son of God assume human nature into the unity of his Person? We
affirm, against the Socinians.*

I. There are two questions in the Christian religion more difficult than the
rest. The first concerns the unity of three Persons in one essence in the
Trinity; the other concerns the union of two natures in one Person in the
incarnation. Although they differ greatly from each other, because the first
treats the unity of essence and the Trinity of Persons, while the other treats
the unity of Person and the diversity of natures, nevertheless knowledge of
one assists knowledge of the other not a little. For just as in the Trinity
the unity of essence does not prevent the Persons from being distinct from
one another, along with their incommunicable properties and operations, so
the union of natures in the Person of Christ does not prevent the natures and
their properties from remaining unconfused and distinct.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question does not concern the truth of the human nature, because both
sides acknowledge it. Nor does it concern the truth of the nature or
hypostasis of the Son of God, because that has been treated elsewhere. Rather,
it concerns the *truth of the incarnation:* Did the Son of God assume human
nature into the unity of his Person, so that the same one who was the Son of
God became the Son of Man through the hypostatic union? The orthodox affirm
this; the Socinians deny it.

III. The question does not concern a *physical* and essential union of two
things to constitute one third nature, as the soul is united to the body to
constitute a human being. It does not concern a union σχετικῇ, *by relation,*
and *relative,* which consists in a union of minds and agreement of wills,
such as the union of friends. It does not concern a union παραστατικῇ, *by
presence,* through *mere assistance,* such as that of angels with their
assumed bodies. It does not concern a union δραστικῇ, *by operation,* with
respect to general *efficacy and sustentation,* by which all things are in God
and in him we exist, live, and move (Acts 17:28). It does not concern the
*mystical and gracious union* of believers with Christ. It does not concern
the *union οὐσιώδει, essential in being,* of the Persons of the Trinity in one
essence. Rather, it concerns the *hypostatic union* through the assumption of
human nature into the unity of the Person of the Λόγου, *Word.* It is so
called both by reason of its *form,* because it is in the Person of the Word,
and by reason of its *terminus,* because it terminates in him. Thus it is a
*personal union,* but not a union of Persons, just as it is a *union of
natures,* but not a *natural union.* If it is sometimes called by the fathers,
as by Cyril in Anathema 3, a union φυσικὴ

[Printed p. 275 | Internet Archive PDF 303 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*natural,* this must be understood in a sound sense, so that it considers not
so much the nature of the union as its extremes, because the extremes united
are two natures.

IV. This *union* can be considered either with respect to its *principle* or
with respect to its *terminus.* In the former sense it is attributed to the
whole Trinity, by whose power such a union was effected through a transitive
act of God by which he united Christ’s humanity in the Person of the Word. In
the latter sense it belongs only to the Word, because it terminates in him.
Hence, although the Person of the Word is rightly said to be incarnate, the
Trinity itself is not, because the incarnation does not terminate in the
divine nature absolutely, but in the Person τοῦ Λόγου, *of the Word,*
relatively.

V. Therefore nothing else is designated by this union than the intimate and
perpetual conjunction of two natures, divine and human, in the unity of a
Person. Through it the human nature, which was destitute of its own
personality and was ἀνυπόστατος, *without a hypostasis,* because otherwise it
would have been a person, was assumed to the Person of the Word and either
conjoined or adjoined to him in the unity of a Person. It is thus now
ἐνυπόστατος Λόγῳ, *enhypostatic in the Word,* insofar as it is sustained by
him—not by the general sustentation by which all creatures are sustained by
God, but by a special and personal sustentation, insofar as it coalesced with
the Word in one person. Although the human nature is rightly called
ἐνυπόστατος Λόγῳ because it was assumed into the unity of the Person and is
sustained by him, it is nevertheless less accurately said to subsist by the
subsistence of the Word, because then the human nature would be a divine
Person.

VI. The human nature in the θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man,* can in a sound sense be
called both his *adjunct* and his *part.* It is an *adjunct,* not because it
was adjoined to him or adheres to him as an accident to a subject or clothing
to a body, but because it was conjoined in time, in the unity of a person, to
the Word, who had existed from eternity without flesh. It is also a *part* by
analogy and in a broader sense, not as though the Person of Christ were
composed of or consisted of two incomplete natures as properly so-called
parts, which would savor of imperfection. Rather, the Person subsists not in
one but in two natures and belongs to each not accidentally but essentially.
Hence it is called σύνθετον, *composite,* of *number* rather than of *parts,*
because in it exist multiple things in number, namely, the human and divine
natures. If some call the human nature the *instrument of the Word,* this is
not with respect to existence but operation, because he assumed it for the
sake of operation and operates through it.

VII. Therefore this assumption was not made for the *completion of the Person
of the Word* as such *reduplicatively,* since he was most perfect in himself
from eternity, but for the completion of the *Person of the θεανθρώπου,
God-man,* or Mediator *specifically.* It was not for the intrinsic acquisition
of a new perfection with respect to the Son of God, but for the communication
of perfection with respect to us and for outward operation, so that through
that nature he might accomplish what was necessary for our redemption.

VIII. When the Person of the God-man is called *composite* by the fathers—ἓν
πρόσωπον σύνθετον ἐκ θεότητος ἐπουρανίου, καὶ ἀνθρωπίνης σαρκὸς, *one
composite person from heavenly deity and human flesh,* as the fathers of the
Synod of Antioch speak against Paul of Samosata—a composition *properly so
called* is not understood, as though the divine and human natures were related
as incomplete parts toward the constitution of a whole. Rather, it is an
improper composition, insofar as that which consists of different things is
called composite. It is not a composition *from these physically,* that is,
from essential parts, as a human being consists of soul and body; nor a
composition *with these accidentally,* that is, of a subject and accident. It
is instead a *hyperphysical and extraordinary composition of this for this,*
that is, of this assumed nature for this suppositum, in which one of the
extremes completes and perfects and the other is completed and perfected. Nor
is it absurd for the divine nature to enter into composition with another in
this manner, because this does not occur after the manner of a part properly
so called, but after the manner of one that assumes another nature. Thus it is
said to compose something with another not essentially but personally, not
for its intrinsic perfection but only for its outward operation.

IX. Although we acknowledge a twofold union in Christ, one personal union

[Printed p. 276 | Internet Archive PDF 304 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of the divine and human natures in one person, and the other a natural union
of soul and body in one human nature, nevertheless certain Lutherans wrongly
charge us with positing two hypostatic unions in Christ and so favoring
Nestorianism. For those two unions differ from one another in diverse ways:
1. In *kind,* because one is physical and the other hyperphysical; 2. In
*terminus,* because there it is a nature and here a person; 3. In *subject,*
because there it is the human nature alone and here both natures; 4. In their
*adjuncts,* because the former is separable and was dissolved by Christ’s
death, while the latter is inseparable, since the Word never laid aside what
he once assumed.

X. The question therefore comes down to this: Is there a true incarnation?
That is, did the Son of God, the second Person of the most holy Trinity, unite
to himself in the unity of his Person not a person but human nature, not by
conversion and transmutation but by assumption and sustentation, so that the
Son of God became the Son of Man and our Mediator and is truly θεάνθρωπος,
*God-man*? The Socinians deny this, teaching that there is only one nature in
Christ and that he is not only a true man but also a mere and simple man.
Consequently, they hold that his whole preeminence lies in the excellence of
his attributes and office, not of his nature. We affirm it.

[Marginal heading: The hypostatic union is proved: 1. From John 1:14.]

XI. The reasons are: First, “the Word became flesh” (John 1:14), where distinct
mention is made of: 1. Two extremes, namely, the *Word,* who is shown to be a
divine suppositum from the preceding verses, where he is said to have been
God, to have been with God, and to have made all things (vv. 1, 3), and the
*flesh,* which *synecdochically* denotes human nature; 2. The union by which
they were joined to each other, surely not by conversion and mutation, of
which the immutable God is incapable, but by assumption. Through it, while
remaining the same in himself, namely, true and eternal God, he became what he
was not by participating in flesh and blood, according to the familiar
saying: *I am what I was,* namely, God; *I was not what I am,* namely, man;
*now I am called both,* namely, God and man.

XII. Nor can Socinus’s impious gloss be admitted. In order to corrupt this
most transparent passage, he insists that the word ἐγένετο should be rendered
not *became,* as it is commonly rendered by the consensus of interpreters,
but *was* or *existed,* as it is used elsewhere in that sense: “ἐγένετο there
was a man sent from God” (John 1:6), and Luke 24:19. For the gloss is openly
contrary to the text, where John plainly distinguishes the being τοῦ Λόγου,
*of the Word,* from the being of the flesh—what Christ was from eternity from
what he became in time. He signifies the former by the verb ἦν, *was,* in
verse 1, ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ Λόγος, *in the beginning was the Word,* without any
indication of a beginning or origin, because in the beginning the Word
already was, was with God, and was God. He denotes the latter by the verb
ἐγένετο, *became,* which designates a beginning and origin, because in the
fullness of time God sent his Son γενόμενον, *made from a woman* (Gal. 4:4),
who is elsewhere called γενόμενος, *made from the seed of David* (Rom. 1:3).
Although the verb γίνομαι, *to become,* is used elsewhere in a different
sense, it does not follow that it cannot be used here in the sense necessarily
demanded by the sequence of the discourse. Socinus adds in vain that the word
*flesh* denotes Christ’s humble and abject condition. For it connotes his
infirmities in such a way that the human nature, which is their subject, is
always presupposed. Thus the Word is said to have become flesh, that is, a
lowly and weak man like us in all things.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Phil. 2:6–7;]

XIII. Second, from Phil. 2:6–7: “Christ, existing in the form of God, did not
reckon it robbery to be equal with God, but emptied himself, taking the form
of a servant, being made in the likeness of men, and being found in bearing as
a man,” and so forth. Various arguments lie concealed here, not only for
proving Christ’s divinity, as was shown in Part 1, Locus III, Question 28,
Thesis 2, but also the truth of the incarnation. 1. It makes mention

[Printed p. 277 | Internet Archive PDF 305 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

expressly of Christ’s two natures: the *divine* through μορφὴν Θεοῦ, *the form
of God,* in which he is said to have existed, and through *equality with God,*
which he did not reckon it robbery to attribute to himself; and the *human*
through μορφὴν δούλου, *the form of a servant,* which he assumed, and the
*likeness of men,* in which he was made. 2. It makes mention of the union of
these two natures in one Person, because the same one who was in the form of
God is said to have assumed the form of a servant and to have been
made in the likeness of men. He did not merely appear in the form of a
servant, as he formerly appeared in human form under the Old Testament by
putting on this form for a time as a garment or symbol of his presence.
Rather, he *took that form,* signifying the true assumption of our nature into
the unity of his Person. The word κένωσις, *emptying,* also pertains here. It
must not be taken *simply and absolutely,* as though God ceased to be or was
reduced to nothing, which it is impious even to think of the eternal and
immutable God, but with respect to his *state and comparatively.* For he hid
and as it were laid aside his divine glory under the veil of flesh, not by
relinquishing what he was but by assuming what he was not. And as the word
ἐκένωσε properly signifies, he *emptied himself,* or appeared to be emptied,
of all that glory which is rightly called the fullness of deity, and assumed
our lowly nature, which is mere vanity and as it were nothing in comparison
with God, and in it the most abject and wretched lot of a servant. Thus from
omnipotent he became weak, from richest poor (2 Cor. 8:9), from Lord of angels
servant of men, and from the most glorious and blessed state which he enjoyed
with the Father he entered the most sorrowful condition. In it he became a
worm and not a man, a reproach of men and despised by the people (Ps. 22:6),
without form or beauty (Isa. 53:2), and cut off and as it were reduced to
nothing, according to the oracle of Dan. 9:26.

XIV. In vain, to evade this, our adversaries say: 1. *μορφὴν Θεοῦ does not
designate the divine nature, which cannot be emptied, but the excellence and
dignity of the human nature, considered in the performance of miracles and
in those rays which he exercised beneath the veil of flesh.* For, first, in
this sense Christ would have assumed the form of a servant before he was in
the form of God, directly contrary to Paul, who asserts that Christ already
subsisted in the form of God when he took the form of a servant and that,
while so existing, he emptied himself by assuming the form of a servant.
Second, the form of God here is *to be equal with God,* which cannot be said
of any excellence of angelic nature, much less of human nature. The apostles
performed stupendous miracles, indeed greater than Christ’s own miracles
(John 14:12), yet they cannot therefore be said to be equal with God. Third,
God cannot be emptied by a diminution of glory, but by its concealment—not
*intrinsically* before God, but *extrinsically* with respect to human beings.
2. They fare no better in maintaining that *the form of a servant connotes
not Christ’s human nature, but only Christ’s most abject condition, because
upon taking the form of a servant he is said merely to have been made in the
likeness of a man, that is, as a man, but not simply to have been made man.*
For they separate things that must be joined. Christ’s servile condition,
which is designated by the form of a servant, does not exclude the truth of
his human nature but presupposes it, because he could not enter that servile
state except in human nature. Nor, if he is said to have been made in the
likeness of men, is the specific identity of his human nature denied, but only
its numerical identity. Otherwise Seth would not have been of the same nature
as Adam, because he is said to have been made in his likeness (Gen. 5:3).

[Marginal heading: 3. From 1 Tim. 3:16;]

XV. Third, “God was manifested in the flesh” (1 Tim. 3:16), where two natures,
divine and human, are connoted, which coalesced into the Person of Christ
through the incarnation. Otherwise it could not be said that Christ himself,
God and flesh, was *manifested in the flesh.* This is the same as elsewhere
being *made a participant in flesh and blood* (Heb. 2:14), *becoming flesh*
(John 1:14), and ἐν σαρκὶ ἔρχεσθαι, *coming in the flesh* (2 John 7). God is
said to have been manifested not οὐσιωδῶς, *essentially,* but ὑποστατικῶς,
*hypostatically,* namely, in the Person of the Word, and not merely by giving
human beings some sign

[Printed p. 278 | Internet Archive PDF 306 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of his presence, as in the burning bush (Exod. 3) or in the cloud and thick
darkness (1 Kings 8:10–11); nor by merely assuming a human form for a time, as
in Gen. 18; nor through empty φάσματα, *apparitions,* in the manner by which
false gods rendered themselves visible, *putting on bodies,* as Servius says
on *Aeneid* 7, ἑαυτοὺς εἰδοποιοῦντες εἰς ἀνθρώπους, *fashioning themselves
into human beings,* as Heliodorus speaks. Rather, he was manifested *in the
flesh,* that is, in mortal and wretched human nature. The text does not speak
simply of divinity in the abstract, but of θεὸν, *God,* in the concrete,
namely, that the Person of the Word was manifested, because the incarnation
belongs not to the divine nature absolutely, but to the Person.

[Marginal heading: 4. From Rom. 1:3, and so forth.]

XVI. Fourth, from the passages where the two natures are clearly set forth in
Christ’s Person, such as Rom. 1:3–4: “Made from the seed of David according to
the flesh, and declared the Son of God with power according to the Spirit of
holiness.” The qualification *according to the flesh and the Spirit* implies
his two natures: one according to which he was made from the seed of David,
namely, the human; and the other, the divine, according to the Spirit of
holiness, through whom he was declared the Son of God by the resurrection, an
indubitable argument of his divinity. Although τὸ, *the phrase,* *according to
the flesh* is elsewhere taken in a somewhat different sense, either for
corruption, as in *walking according to the flesh* (Rom. 8:1), or for the
outward appearance that falls under the senses, as in *judging according to
the flesh* (John 8:15), it does not follow that it cannot here signify Christ
with respect to his human nature. Both the seed of David and the antithesis
with the Spirit necessarily demand this. Nor, if the same man can be said to
have been born according to the flesh and according to the Spirit, as is said
of Isaac in Gal. 4:29, must it therefore be denied that elsewhere a twofold
nature is designated. It is one thing for the qualification to be applied to
the *agent with respect to the mode of generation.* In this sense *Isaac* is
said to have been begotten *according to the Spirit,* although he was begotten
according to the flesh, because by the power of the special promise he was
brought forth supernaturally. It is another thing for it to be applied to the
*subject with respect to the natures* of which it is constituted, as occurs
here.

XVII. The same qualification is applied in Rom. 9:5, when it is said, “Christ
according to the flesh from the fathers, God blessed forever.” Here the human
nature according to which he arose from the fathers and the divine nature
according to which he is God blessed forever are designated. Thus in 1 Pet.
3:18 he is said to have been *put to death in the flesh,* that is, to have
suffered in the human nature, but *made alive by the Spirit,* that is, raised
by his divinity, through that eternal Spirit through whom he had offered
himself to God (Heb. 9:14). Thus in Acts 20:28 *God* is said to have *purchased
the church with his own blood.* Here the word ἰδίου, *his own,* shows that the
nature which suffered truly belongs to that divine Person who is said to have
purchased the church.

XVIII. Although Christ’s human nature is a primary, intelligent substance and
perfect in substantial being, it is not therefore a Person, because it does
not possess incommunicability and its own subsistence. Thus a separated soul
is an individual, intelligent substance, but not a person, because it is a
partial and incomplete being which does not subsist but must be joined to
another. The lack of its own personality does not detract from the truth and
perfection of Christ’s human nature. For we measure the truth of human nature
from matter, form, and essential properties, not from personality. Hence the
definition of nature is one thing and that of subsistence another. Moreover,
personality is neither an integral nor an essential part of nature, but as it
were its terminus. Because the human nature obtains that terminus in an
uncreated subsistence, it is by that very fact far more perfect than if,
separated from the Word, it subsisted by itself.

XIX. The Son of God did not assume a man so called *suppositally,* but *human
nature,* that is, a man so called *specifically.* And whenever Christ is called
man and the Son of Man in Scripture, the name belongs not to the nature but to
the Person denominated from the other nature.

XX. *God and man* are considered either in the *abstract,* to denote divinity
and humanity, or in the *concrete,* to designate the Person who is God

[Printed p. 279 | Internet Archive PDF 307 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and man at once. In the latter sense they are not disparate but diverse things
which can belong to one subject, but in the former they are truly disparate
things which can never be predicated of each other. Thus although it is rightly
said that the man is God, it would be wrongly said that humanity is divinity.
Although such disparate things cannot ordinarily and physically be one in
number, nothing prevents this from being affirmed theologically and in that
extraordinary suppositum of the θεανθρώπου, *God-man.*

XXI. We do not think that much labor should be spent explaining the nature and
kind of propositions employed in this mystery, such as *God is man* and *man
is God,* since nothing similar occurs in the whole universe of things. In this
sense they are rightly called *unusual,* not *disparate* propositions, in
which one disparate thing is predicated of another, because no person is
disparate from himself, but *identical* propositions, in which one thing is
predicated of itself nonformally. In this sense they can be called
*synonymous* under a logical consideration and *tropical* under a rhetorical
consideration. They are *synonymous* because the subject and predicate are
taken concretely on account of the unity of the Person, so that the sense is:
The Person who is God is also man. Thus the lowest species is predicated of an
extraordinary and singular individual. They are *tropical* because the subject
and predicate indeed designate the same Person, but κατ’ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο,
*according to one thing and another,* that is, with respect to deity and
humanity. Therefore the tropical predication *God is man* and *man is God* is
not to be resolved into this proper predication, “Deity is God; humanity is
man,” as our adversaries wrongly object, but into this: “Christ, God according
to deity, is man according to humanity,” and conversely. So *God is said to
have suffered,* that is, Christ, who is God according to deity, suffered not
in the deity but in the *flesh.*

XXII. The *communication of the hypostasis of the Word* made to the flesh can
be understood in three ways: *Effectively,* as though he effected another
hypostasis in the flesh; *transitively,* as though he is held to have formally
transfused his own hypostasis into the flesh; or *assumptively,* because he
assumed the flesh into the same hypostasis and united it to himself. In the
first sense the phrase is heterodox and Nestorian, because if he had effected
another hypostasis, there would be two hypostases and consequently two
persons. The second is no less heretical, because in that case the flesh would
subsist formally by the subsistence of the Word and so would truly be a
person. Add that a personal property is simply incommunicable. Only in the
third sense, then, is the phrase true and orthodox: namely, the Word is said
to have communicated his subsistence to the flesh by assuming it into the
unity of his hypostasis. Thus the flesh is not a hypostasis but ἐνυπόστατος,
*enhypostatic,* not existing separately but sustained in the Word, as an
instrument and adjunct personally conjoined to him for accomplishing the work
of our redemption.

XXIII. Contradictory things cannot belong to the same subject in the same
manner and respect. But being dependent and independent, finite and infinite,
belongs to Christ κατ’ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο, *according to one thing and another:*
the former with respect to his human nature and the latter with respect to his
divine nature.

XXIV. An *infinite local distance* can prevent the union of two natures, but
not the *infinite distance of perfection* that exists between the human and
divine natures.

XXV. If the Son of God assumed our nature, nothing was thereby added to him
intrinsically to perfect his nature, which already eminently possessed every
perfection of humanity. Rather, something was only extrinsically adjoined to
him for accomplishing redemption.

XXVI. He who becomes what he was not by *transmutation* is changed, but he who
only becomes by *assumption* what he was not is not therefore changed. Thus,
if any change is granted here, it is in the human nature, which is sustained
by the Word in an extraordinary and singular manner, not in the Person of the
Word, who, remaining always the same, united it to himself.
