### QUESTION VII

#### How is Ezra 4:6–7 to be reconciled with Ezra 6:14–15?

In the former passage it is asserted that the enemies of the Jews, who
interrupted the building of the temple and the city, brought complaints to
Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes, kings of the Persians, and that the work was halted
by those kings’ commands. The question is who that Ahasuerus was: was he the
father of Darius the Mede, Esther’s husband, or someone else? And who was that
Artaxerxes—Longimanus, Mnemon, or someone else altogether? In the latter
passage, certainly, Artaxerxes is said to have advanced, not hindered, the
building of the temple together with Cyrus and Darius. And indeed, what
Artaxerxes could either hinder or advance the temple’s construction when the
latter passage says it was completed in the sixth year of Darius, at a time
when Artaxerxes had not yet been born and even his father Xerxes appears
scarcely to have been in the cradle?

> Marginalia, printed p. 157: *Who were the Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes who
> hindered the building of the temple?*

Answer. These doubts would be easy enough to solve if the very different
opinions of great men had not rendered perplexed and entangled matters that
are clear enough in themselves. Joseph Scaliger, by his authority—which is
deservedly great—and imperious words, led most of our writers astray,
especially Junius, Diodati, Rainolds, Calvisius, Emmius, Bowe, Nisbet, the
English annotators, and others. But Capellus, Ussher, and others guarded
themselves against his error and, with the ancient interpreters, kept to a
path that seems to us straighter and easier. Scaliger and his followers hold
that the Ahasuerus named in the former passage was Xerxes, Esther’s husband,
and that Artaxerxes was his son Longimanus; but they hold that the Artaxerxes
named in the latter passage was Mnemon, who was joined with his father Darius
Nothus in rule and issued with him the decree for completing the temple.

In the preceding question we stated how greatly Scaliger erred in identifying
Xerxes as Esther’s husband, and in the next question we shall see what a vast
confusion of times he caused among his followers by asserting that Darius
Nothus completed the temple. For the present we argue briefly against his
current opinion.

First, Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes, named by Ezra in the former passage, were the
first kings of the Persians who hindered the building of the temple contrary
to Cyrus’s command. But Xerxes and Artaxerxes Longimanus were not the first to
hinder that work. Otherwise the Jews would have continued in it without any
hindrance until their reigns and easily finished the temple before those
kings were born, for after the interruption they resumed the work in Darius’s
second year and put the finishing touch on it in his sixth, within four years.
How, then, did they fail to finish that work before the reign of Xerxes and
Artaxerxes when no one hindered them during all seventeen years of Cyrus and
Cambyses and the forty-six years of Darius Hystaspes?

> Marginalia, printed p. 157: *The Ahasuerus here was not Xerxes, as Scaliger,
> Junius, and others maintain*.

Second, Esther’s husband and his son Artaxerxes, as they would have it, were
benefactors of the Jews, not enemies. Who, then, could believe that both men,
to the end of their lives, conspired with the enemies of the Jews to hinder
the construction of the temple?

Third, if the construction of the temple had been hindered throughout the
reigns of Xerxes and Artaxerxes until the second year of Darius Nothus, there
would then have been at least 107 years—as Scaliger admits—from the laying of
the temple’s foundation to its completion, and throughout that entire time
Zerubbabel continued in the principate and Joshua in the high priesthood.
Indeed, each man held that office for some time both before and afterward;
yet after the sons of Noah no mortal has approached a hundred years in any
public office.

Moreover, this opinion makes many Jews in those days live for two hundred
years. From the first year of Cyrus’s monarchy to the second year of Darius
Nothus were 117 years; from the destruction of the temple to Cyrus’s first
year are reckoned, if not seventy, at least fifty-two years; and those who
remembered the glory of the former temple must have been eighteen years old
when they observed it. Together these amount to 187 years. And surely not
everyone who saw the later temple expired immediately upon seeing it, but
could have survived for some years. Both calculations entail the difficulty
that many Jews in that age reached 187 years, something few sensible people
will concede.

To avoid these things Scaliger denies that Haggai 2:3 asserts that any of the
Jews saw both

> Marginalia, printed p. 157: *Scaliger’s evasion*.

[Printed p. 158 | IA leaf 188 | access PDF 188 | OCR object 187]

temples, because the prophet’s words are interrogative rather than
assertive. Yet this evasion is unworthy of Scaliger’s candor, for nothing is
more evident than that by those questions the prophet presupposes that some
of the people had not only seen both temples with their own eyes, but also
had found the latter temple’s outward glory contemptible and nothing in
comparison with the former.

Nisbet seeks another refuge, one he admits he devised himself, but it seems
far more wretched. He holds that no more than forty-nine years elapsed from
Cyrus’s first year to the second year of Darius Nothus, but in this way, on
his sole authority and contrary to the testimony of all ancient and modern
writers, he subtracts at least seventy years from the reigns of the Persian
kings before Darius Nothus—an absurdity entirely intolerable even to Scaliger
and his followers.

> Marginalia, printed p. 158: *Nisbet’s wretched invention*.

Leaving the former opinion, therefore, we proceed to the latter: Ahasuerus and
Artaxerxes are names common, if not to all, at least to many Persian kings.
By Scaliger’s own admission, *Abasuerus* signifies nothing other than “great
leader,” from *Abash*, great, *vav*, and, and *rosh*, head. *Artaxerxes*
signifies almost the same thing, namely “great warrior,” from *art*, great,
and *Xerxes*, Ares or warrior. Scaliger assigns this surname Ahasuerus to the
father of Darius the Mede, whether Astyages or another Mede, husband even of
Esther, whether he was Cambyses, Xerxes, or someone else; just as the surname
Artaxerxes is assigned by everyone both to Longimanus and Mnemon, and even to
Ochus. According to the second opinion, these names in Ezra 4 are assigned to
Cambyses, the son of Cyrus. Although he was an evil man, he was nevertheless
a great leader and warrior, for he subdued all Egypt by his arms. All the
Jewish commentators, as may be seen in Lyra and Münster, interpret the
passage of Ezra in this way. We do not approve what many of them add, that
Cambyses was Esther’s husband; but we embrace as true their claim that
Cambyses was the Ahasuerus who first hindered the temple’s construction. This
is Josephus’s opinion and, according to Lyra, the common Christian opinion.
Lavater certainly follows it in his commentary on the passage, as do Vatablus
and Münster. It requires no arguments for its proof beyond those with which
we opposed the former opinion.

> Marginalia, printed p. 158: *Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes were names common to
> the kings of the Persians*.

But if the impious Cambyses, son of Cyrus, was Ahasuerus, who forbade God’s
temple to be built contrary to his father’s edict, who was that Artaxerxes to
whom Ezra ascribes the same action of hindering the temple?

Answer. He too was Cambyses himself, as not only the Jewish interpreters but
also most Christian ones assert. In Jerome’s Royal Bible, called the Latin
version, the former passage reads in different words, “This Artaxerxes is
Ahasuerus.” I admit that Lyra rightly notes that these words were added to the
text, for they are not in the Hebrew original. Yet although Lyra rightly
condemns the corruption of a text by any additions, he does not reject the
substance itself, but embraces it as a true comment on the text, one that
could rightly have been placed beneath the text or added in the margin.

> Marginalia, printed p. 158: *Cambyses was both Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes*.

If this answer is unsatisfactory, there is another which many, both among our
writers and among Roman Catholics, approve: that Ahasuerus was Cambyses, and
Artaxerxes was Tanyoxares the Great, Cambyses’s successor, as though the
enemies of the Jews had brought their first accusation about the building of
the temple to Cambyses and the second to the Magus after Cambyses’s death.
Capellus certainly places them in the wrong order—as Henricus Philippi
notes—when he would have the Magus be Ahasuerus and Cambyses Artaxerxes, for
Cambyses would then have reigned after the Magus, contrary to all authors and
even to Capellus himself. But the former explanation seems to fit the text
better. For if both Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes are supposed to have been
surnames of the one Cambyses, everything flows beautifully and becomes
perfectly clear. While Cyrus was away, occupied with the Massagetan war,
accusations against the Jews came to Cambyses. These are first presented in
general terms in verse 6, and then in verse 7 and those following the specific
complaints themselves are set out in a letter to Cambyses, who is there
called Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 158: *Artaxerxes could have been Tanyoxares the
> Great*.

The same or a similar answer explains the latter passage: the temple was
built by command of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes, taking the conjunction
“and,” as Lavater says, disjunctively and explanatorily, as though Darius was
also surnamed Artaxerxes, or “great warrior”—which he truly was, above all
the kings of the Persians except Cyrus alone. Scaliger indeed ridicules this
answer and holds that to surname Cambyses Ahasuerus or call Darius Artaxerxes
is to create a two-bodied monster. But these sarcastic remarks are gratuitous
and without any foundation. Are those who would call Solomon Jedidiah,
Zedekiah Mattaniah, Jehoiakim Coniah, Astyages Ahasuerus, and Xerxes
Artashasta to be judged creators of two-bodied monsters? In any event, the
empty bogeyman of this sarcasm produced two other answers to the passage.

> Marginalia, printed p. 158: *Darius was called Artaxerxes*.

The first belongs to those who oppose us here. They hold that the Artaxerxes
of Ezra 6:14 should be understood as Mnemon, whom they suppose to have reigned
together with his father Darius Nothus and to have issued with him the decree
for completing the temple. This is wholly unsatisfactory to us, for besides
the presupposition that Darius Nothus built the temple—soon to be refuted—

> Marginalia, printed p. 158: *Artaxerxes Mnemon did not reign together with
> his father Darius Nothus*.

[Printed p. 159 | IA leaf 189 | access PDF 189 | OCR object 188]

they join Mnemon to his father in rule without any witness, and precisely at
the beginning of his reign. Mnemon was certainly so far from reigning with
his father at the very beginning of the reign that his mother Parysatis,
intending her second son Cyrus as Darius’s successor, used all her strength
until the father’s death to render Mnemon’s right of succession doubtful. In
any case, it is wholly improbable that the son was joined to him in Darius’s
second year, for it is not certain that Mnemon had even been born so early.

I therefore prefer what others assert concerning Artaxerxes Longimanus, from
whom Ezra and Nehemiah obtained many gifts and favorable decrees for the
Jews. Although the temple itself was completely built in the sixth year of
Darius Hystaspes, many ornaments and additions, both inside and outside, were
attached to the buildings adjoining and neighboring the temple. These are
rightly ascribed to Artaxerxes Longimanus, who after Cyrus and Darius was the
most benevolent of all the Persian rulers toward the Jews.

> Marginalia, printed p. 159: *Artaxerxes Longimanus adorned and completed the
> temple*.
