## A Scholastic Disputation on Divine Providence

Delivered in various lectures to candidates in sacred theology at the renowned
University of St Andrews, insofar as concerns the principal heads of the
subject. In it the dominion of God, his effective action concerning sin, the
concurrence of the first cause, and predetermination are argued and contested
against the Jesuits, Arminians, and Socinians.

Metaphysical investigations concerning being, possibility, and God's dominion
over beings and nonbeings are appended, together with various questions which
contribute especially to a fuller and more exact knowledge of the doctrine of
divine providence.

By the study and labor of Samuel Rutherford, professor of sacred theology in
the renowned University of St Andrews.

## Grace and peace to students who are candidates in sacred theology and to
other Christian readers

Chosen students and beloved brothers, if you expect an account of my purpose
and counsel in undertaking this province of the professorship, I gave that
account in my inaugural disputation and shall try to satisfy you in words. A
Punic proverb says, “The field ought to be weaker than the farmer.” From this
they infer that one should not wrestle with a Sparta unequal to one's strength.
I had been living on almost the remotest coasts of this kingdom beside the
Irish Sea, intent upon the sacred ministry with as much diligence as I could,
even if not as much as I ought. I seemed to be hiding, “far from Jupiter, far
from the thunderbolt,” as the pagans say—that is, beyond the hazard of
episcopal fury—although my hope deceived me.

I have always judged this burden unequal to my shoulders, this chair in its
doctoral purple worthier than my ability, and the academic buskin more elevated
and suitable than my slender reed. But perhaps, as Cicero says, “Even those
philosophers who write books about despising glory inscribe their own names
upon them; in the very thing in which they despise renown and fame, they wish
themselves to be proclaimed and named.” So too the proclamation of one's own
**inability** (*adynamia*, ἀδυναμία) and frailty is a crafty pride, a deceitfully
vainglorious and swollen way of lowering oneself. “Even by the wise,” as
Tacitus says, “the desire for glory is the last thing cast off.” Yet “the
person who does not perceive how badly he sleeps sleeps well.” He lies buried
in an exceedingly deep sleep who is neither conscious nor weary of his own
inability.

It was, indeed, almost the fifteenth year since I had written a funeral song
for the academic arena and bid farewell to the dust of polemical disputation
when I was called to this office. Why, then, should I

[Address to the readers, fol. *2v; PDF page 8]

descend into this gladiatorial arena? Or why should I commit myself to this
sea in so fragile and useless a skiff, furnished with no sails of learning, so
to speak, and no oar? Through disuse of disputation, even if I have not grown
old, I have at least contracted rust and decay.

Yet it is no small comfort to me that I have a witness in heaven that I did
not rush into this province through zeal and passion to pander to my own
desires, hunt after fame, or serve my private estate shamefully. The necessity
of feeding souls and the flock entrusted to me forbade me to write whatever
burden this is; my rusticity delayed me, and the dullness of my ability
prohibited me. But what resolution could I have while tossed in the troublesome
back-and-forth of counsels and thoughts? The duty of spending the slender talent
which the Lord measured out to me urged me to enter this course; the
university's invitation persuaded me as I resisted; and reverence for obedience
to the venerable National Synod convinced and impelled me. In this office may I
satisfy my conscience—which is praiseworthy—and serve the interests of Christ
and his church—which must be earnestly prayed for. But I shall never be able to
yield to slander, which must be stoutly despised.

In this disputation I strive above all to set my sail and direct my course
between the Scylla of wicked **war against God** (*theomachia*, θεομαχία), which
the papists, Socinians, and Arminians have falsely imputed to us, and the
Charybdis of Epicureanism and a slumbering divine providence, which our
adversaries firmly maintain and carefully defend. Great men have gone before
me as I sail: foremost my Bradwardine, if he is not too great to be called
mine; the most renowned Dr. Twisse; Paul Baynes, a vigorous disputant; and
Gisbertus Voetius, who for solid learning—and, what cannot be concealed because
it cannot be obscured, for true piety—and for intellectual ingenuity is now an
outstanding glory and ornament of the professors in the Reformed churches. I
would not say this to the detriment of the reputation of those most excellent
servants of God who now shine in the universities.

My aim and labor in this little treatise, whatever its quality, is to expose
the character and disposition of the Jesuits and Arminians. Nothing is more
important or dearer to their desires than to dethrone from the seat of
providence the God who is to be praised forever, the Lord of hosts, “wonderful
in counsel and glorious in work,” and, as has been said elsewhere, to establish
altars, prayers, vows, sacrifices, and religious praises for the free will and
contingency of the creature—the Jesuits' new idol and counterfeit petty goddess.
Would that

[Address to the readers, fol. *3r; PDF page 9]

it were in my power to serve in the reserve ranks for the glory of providence
and most abundant grace. I acknowledge the debt but possess no means by which
to free myself from what I owe.

I have added certain byworks of metaphysical contemplation which may perhaps
be useful in the present disputation.

But you, most excellent students, must make a vigorous choice among your
studies. You must not permit your mind to wander over the paper ocean, if I may
speak so, of books which have neither measure nor end. “To travel is sometimes
to go astray; the person who is everywhere is nowhere.”

To the sick and aging disposition of this age, old things please and old things
displease; new things charm and new things are foul. Thus Buchanan, easily the
foremost of our age's poets, writes:

[Marginal citation: Buchanan, *Baptistes*, prologue.]

> For if someone brings out an ancient play,
> They make a disturbance; they cough and turn sick.
> But if someone brings a new one, they at once
> Seek out, approve, praise, and love the old.

Yet we pine with desire for fame six hundred or a thousand years old, as though
time, just as with wines, rendered the writings of learned people better. Time
discloses truth; it does not create it. For many learned people the end of life
is the beginning of fame; for others life and the renown of their name are shut
in the same urn, and the final period of their days is the tomb of their windy
fame. “Give Pythagoras back to the world,” Petrarch says, “and the despisers of
his genius will return to you. Let Plato return to Greece, let Homer be born
again, Aristotle revive, Varro be recovered in Italy, Livy rise again, and
Cicero shine once more: they will find not only followers and admirers but
also biting and envious detractors.” Rare and unusual wares also sell better
because of their novelty.

Whatever is at home is cheap; whatever is commonplace becomes foul. Affection
obstructs truth, and prejudice not only obscures but repels it. Hence the
oft-repeated saying among the blind pagans, “To be wise and to love has hardly
befallen even Jupiter.” But how beautiful, how comely, and how delightful is
naked truth! It will command the mind; wise or foolish, one will serve it.
Those pure in heart will see God and the mysteries of the kingdom of God.

Nothing should be more important to you than transferring theology into the
solid practice of the Christian life. No controversy calls for more earnest
toil, no commonplace for more serious labor, and finally no

[Address to the readers, fol. *3v; PDF page 10]

theme for a more strenuous effort with sail and oar than holiness of life. This
is the prow and stern of all learning, the soul and marrow of all sacred
literature. Everything else is worth nothing. There is neither measure nor end
to making books, and laborious reading tortures flesh and mind. Human ability
is indeed greedy and more voracious than the grave, but revering God and
religiously observing his commands form both pages of true wisdom.

Godliness—godliness, I say—is the shoot, offspring, and blood of every sounder
theology. What are the skirmishes of disputants but bark and skin? Students,
the truth must be believed rather than disputed and loved rather than merely
contemplated. Yet alas! The inquiries of the universities, finer than spiders'
webs, multiply daily, while faith according to godliness grows cold, languishes,
and nearly expires. The learned daily weave thorny and labyrinthine questions
which seek spots even in the sun; but few know how precious a conscience that
obeys God is, fewer value it, and very few strive to order and cultivate it
according to the rule of God's word.

On this account Christ's vineyard in Great Britain, which he has leased to
keepers, owes far more to the Lord of all vineyards than does any church in
the inhabited world. For the army of the English and Scots who follow in
heaven that King of kings seated upon the white horse is far more numerous than
in all the Reformed churches of the Christian world. I judge this to be nothing
more than the begun fulfillment of the prophecies that the farthest ends of
the earth and the islands—of which our Britain is first and most renowned—will
be given to Christ by the Father as his hereditary right. I therefore
unflinchingly believe that Britain, that is, England and Scotland, to which one
may add the little sister in Ireland who will become much greater, will be the
praise of the whole earth, a crown of glory and a royal diadem in the hand of
the Lord. For the Holy Spirit foretold this in Psalm 2:8; 72:10; 92:1; Isaiah
24:15; 49:1; 42:4, 10; 51:5; 60:9; and Zephaniah 2:11.

Hence all our enemies—the accursed crowd of Malignants and sectarians in
England, Familists, Antinomians, Antiscripturists, Enthusiasts, Arians,
Socinians, Arminians, and so forth—together with their troops must perish and
be destroyed. Your one task, therefore, is this: let your mind and care be to
know Christ. How greatly it matters for candidates

[Address to the readers, fol. *4r; PDF page 11]

in sacred theology to delight in, be captivated and enticed by, and even become
intoxicated with the sweetness, excellence, and incomparable preeminence of our
Lord Jesus Christ! How sweet is his name, how beautiful his face, how delightful
and persuasive his words. A creature brought forth from nothing by creative
power is plainly a thing of nothing; Christ is everything. The dignity of kings
is clay and fragile; he is King of kings. A human being is a breathing fable,
smiling and dying vanity, clay gilded as if with gold leaf for a few days by the
color of life. Christ alone is wise, the Father of eternity, the Ancient of
Days, the King of ages. Heaven is beautiful, the stars comely, and the sun most
splendid; but Jesus, through whom all things were made, is more beautiful and
more comely. Let us therefore swear allegiance to godliness and to the love of
Christ; let us serve, and the love of Christ will cling all the more tenaciously.

Perhaps here love is taken up, not laid down, at the mind's choice. Everything
else flows away just as water passes through a sieve. Nothing is more vain or
fragile than other things; for establishing the immortal soul, this whole world
is a muddy, rancid, hoary foundation. Let all who have excelled in gifts of mind
be compared with Christ: the prophets fall silent, the apostles become mute,
and God's martyrs cannot even speak while Christ is speaking.

> Whom charm adorns, and grace poured forth
> from rosy lips.

Let the shadowy little sparks of created petty glory, and all who have become
renowned through the celebrity of their name, be compared with that uncreated
Wisdom of God the Father. Plato raves, Aristotle lies, Cicero stammers,
hesitates, and does not know how to speak Latin; Demosthenes stands dumb,
speechless, and amazed; Seneca does not know the way of virtue; Homer sings
nothing, and Virgil sings badly. “Where is the disputant? Where is the scribe?
Where is the inquirer? Has God not made foolish the wisdom of this world?”

Let those who shine with the glory of virtue approach Christ. Aristides falsely
claims virtue; Fabius stumbles and strays from the way of justice. Socrates does
not even know this—that he knows nothing. Cato is frivolous and futile. Solon is
a slave and bondman of the world and its pleasures, not a lawgiver. Pythagoras
is neither a sage nor a philosopher; Bias despises neither the world nor empty
glory; Alexander the Macedonian is cowardly. Greece boasts in vain of its seven
sages, and Rome should not vaunt that she possesses Laelius, Scipio, or Augustus.
Let Mantua not boast that she gave birth to Virgil, nor Sulmo to Ovid. Cyrus is
no glory or honor to the Persians; Themistocles does not shine at Athens; and
Agesilaus is no splendor to the Lacedaemonians. But the Bride sings very truly
of Christ in a far different way: “My beloved

[Address to the readers, fol. *4v; PDF page 12]

excels in whiteness and redness; he is the standard-bearer among ten thousand.
His head is distinguished, most thoroughly refined gold; his curled locks are
black in their divisions; his eyes are like doves, washed in milk itself and
elegantly set in it; his cheeks resemble beds of spices grown thick with
perfumed plants; his lips are lilies dripping liquid myrrh,” and so forth. In
short, he is altogether most desirable.

And Paul says: “He is the image of the invisible God and the firstborn of every
created thing. Through him all things were created; he himself is before all
things, and all things hold together through him. He is the head of the body,
that is, the church, and he is the beginning and firstborn from the dead, so
that he might hold first place among all. In him all the fullness of deity
dwells bodily. He is the heir of all things, the radiance of the Father's glory
and the exact imprint of his person, the Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the
end, who was, who is, and who is to come.”

Nothing is so narrow-minded as to gape after wealth; Christ is that one
necessary thing. A good man withdraws himself from great fortune and has not
given himself up as its captive. Those who abound in honor and glory and in the
splendor of kingdoms and diadems pretend to possess happiness, but do not.
Those who have become renowned through victories and the glory of war have
prepared names and titles for their tomb, a fable for the common people, and
shade and smoke for themselves. Some little intervals for remaining in this
small cottage are torn from us by force, while others are withdrawn as if by
theft.

It is shameful neither to rule tomorrow nor to lay hands upon today, since we
divide this very day with death. We must depart: Christ is the way. We must be
wise: Christ is the truth. We must live: Christ is the life. Nothing must be
left undone; we must sweat, run, and keep watch so that we may live blessed
with him forever. Not to live today is folly; not to gather our baggage is
astonishing drowsiness; and to hope that we will live tomorrow is by far the
greatest foolishness. Unless we possess Christ, we accomplish nothing; if we
enjoy him, [we are] blessed forever. Direct your strength here; contend. The
Lord will come soon, his reward with him; he will repay each person according
to his work. Watch, pray, live forever, and farewell.

Your brother in Christ, most closely bound by every bond of duty,

S. R.
