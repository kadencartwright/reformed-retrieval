Bellarmine's third objection remains. It is his answer to the argument
Chemnitz and others draw from Philippians 3 against justification by works
following and arising from faith. Paul counts all things dung so that he may
gain Christ and be found in him, not having his own righteousness from the
law but the righteousness through faith in Christ, righteousness from God
through faith. Chemnitz further confirms this because Paul speaks not only
in the past tense—“I counted all things loss for Christ” (verse 7)—but in the
present after his conversion: “Indeed I now count all things loss and dung,
that I may gain Christ.”

Bellarmine gives two replies.

1. Although Paul speaks in both past and present tenses, Bellarmine says, he
does not speak of different things but of the same works done before faith.
At his conversion Paul counted the works he had done as a Pharisee losses,
and now still considers those same works worthless—not merely losses but
dung—because he increasingly understands their emptiness. Bellarmine seeks
to prove this from Paul's antithesis: “That I may be found in him not having
my own righteousness which is from the law, but that which is through faith
in Christ.” Paul allegedly opposes his own righteousness—works performed
from faith and truly righteous before God—to his former righteousness—works
performed under the law without faith and incapable of true righteousness
before God.

2. Bellarmine charges Chemnitz and us with blasphemy for calling works done
from faith and Christ's grace “dung,” though Paul calls them fruits of the
Spirit in Galatians 5. Are the Holy Spirit's fruits dung? How can Paul boast
in 2 Timothy 4:8, “I have fought the good fight, finished the course, kept
the faith; henceforth the crown of righteousness is laid up for me, which
the righteous Judge will render”? Ought so great an apostle to boast in dung?

[1679 printed p. 136 | IA leaf 144]

What crown of righteousness is due to dung, and what righteous Judge
condescends to crown it? What thanks do we owe God for creating us in Christ
Jesus for good works (Ephesians 2), if those works are nothing but dung? And
if they are dung, they should not be performed and embraced but shaken off
and rejected. “Let Chemnitz now go and complain that we call him an enemy of
good works.”

Bellarmine speaks pompously and magisterially, befitting his
cardinal-Jesuitical disposition. Saint Augustine is very different. While
expounding the passage he piously and humbly addresses Paul: “I beg you,
Apostle, explain this a little.” He immediately adds, “Rather let us ask God
to enlighten us also—the God who enlightened the one who wrote this epistle
to us not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God.” This is an
outstanding passage which, if examined deeply, brilliantly illuminates the
manner of our justification and the present question. To Bellarmine's
exceptions I answer:

1. I grant that the bare change of tense from “I counted” to “I count” does
not by itself sufficiently prove a change in the thing discussed. With a
change of tense the matter could remain the same, if Paul spoke in both
verses only of his Pharisaic righteousness and Jewish privileges. He could
say: I counted all these losses at my conversion, and now, having advanced
in Christianity, having been better taught their emptiness and Christ's
perfect righteousness, and having greater experience, I judge them losses
in comparison with Christ.

Our writers therefore do not press the change of tense separately, but join
it with everything else in Paul's sentence: “Indeed I now count all things
loss.”

First, the full and unusual constellation of five particles, almost heaped
together into one, indicates something great—something much greater than the
preceding verse. The Vulgate wrongly rendered them “nevertheless,” which
corrects the former statement. They ought to be “indeed even,” amplifying
it. The Roman Catholics Cornelius a Lapide and Estius agree. Estius says:
“The particle ‘and’ or ‘even’ is not idle, but serves the rhetorical
increase of the sentence. Its sense is: I shall say something more.”

Second, the change concerns not only tense but subject: the earlier “these
things” becomes “all things.” Paul counts not merely Jewish privileges but
all things loss beside Christ—not merely things viewed as human or Jewish,
but simply and absolutely all things. Chrysostom explains “all things” as
“every affair.” Lyra says, “Not only these things, but all things,” and
Theodoret likewise, “Not only what Paul related about himself, but every
other thing.” In Cornelius a Lapide's words, “It was more worthy and fitting
to Paul's mind to esteem lightly before Christ not only all the goods of
Judaism, but all

[1679 printed p. 137 | IA leaf 145]

things absolutely.” Surely the scope of this universal “all things” is not
so narrow that even the best works of regenerate people cannot be included.
That Paul actually includes them becomes clearer for two reasons.

First, consider those against whom Paul acts: Judaizing Christians or Jews
professing faith in Christ. They commended for justification not merely
legal works without faith, but insisted that such works be joined to faith
to justify a sinner. Paul explicitly disputes their position, and so excludes
from justification precisely these works even when joined with faith.

Second, this appears from Paul's antithesis in verse 9, from which Bellarmine
tries to carve out his view: “That I may be found in him, not having my own
righteousness from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the
righteousness from God upon faith.” Bellarmine interprets: “He opposes his
own righteousness, works done from faith and truly righteous before God, to
his own former righteousness, works done under the law without faith and not
truly righteous before God.” How Jesuitical—crafty but utterly false! This
gloss is not merely alien to Paul's mind and words but directly contrary to
them.

1. Paul does not oppose his own righteousness to his own righteousness, as
Bellarmine does, but his righteousness to God's righteousness.

2. He does not oppose righteousness from works before faith to righteousness
from works done through faith, but righteousness from the law to
righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ.

Paul's own words make these two points certain. The righteousness he rejects
in justification is first “our” righteousness and second righteousness “from
the law.” The righteousness by which he wishes to be justified and in which
he wishes to be found before God's tribunal is, first, God's righteousness,
and second, the righteousness of faith in Jesus Christ.

As to the first, “our righteousness” must mean not only righteousness from
works done by our own powers without faith but also righteousness from works
we perform through faith and God's inherent grace. Although the latter are
done through God's special aid, as the former through more common aid, they
may truly be called our righteousness on two grounds: they inhere in us and
their acts are performed by us, though aided by grace. Faith is likewise
called “ours,” as when Christ repeatedly says, “Your faith has saved you.”

By contrast, the righteousness of faith—the righteousness through which we
are justified by means of faith—is in no way ours. It is entirely alien and
external, wholly God's: as Chrysostom says here, “It is God's
righteousness,” namely Christ's righteousness as Mediator, freely given by
God without works or merits. It is applied through our faith, not because
faith causally produces it as faith produces good works,

[1679 printed p. 138 | IA leaf 146]

but only because faith apprehends it and is appointed in the gospel's
economy to receive what God alone offers, gives, and imputes.

The same may be said of the second contrast, between righteousness of the law
and righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ. The former is called
righteousness “in the law” in verse 6, “from the law” in verse 9, “through
the law” in Galatians 2:21, and “the requirement of the law” in Romans 8:4;
hence it is also called righteousness in Romans 9:31. Whether described as
in, from, or through the law, or simply as legal righteousness, these names
designate the same thing under different considerations. Righteousness of
the law embraces not merely ceremonial or moral works performed without
faith and grace but also works that regenerate people perform from faith
according to the law's command.

Works of the unregenerate without faith differ greatly from works done by
the regenerate from faith. The regenerate person's works are works of grace
and faith with respect to their originating principle. Yet in substance and
rule they are works of the law and may rightly be called so. Righteousness
of the law is therefore righteousness of works however performed. It is
called righteousness “in” or “from” the law because the law prescribes it
and it consists in observing the law. It is righteousness “of the law”
because the law commands it and pronounces the one acting accordingly
righteous: “The doers of the law will be justified” (Romans 2:13); “The one
who does these things will live by them.” This is precisely the righteousness
of the law in Romans 10:5.

When Paul denies justification by works of the law to any flesh in Romans
3:20 and concludes in verse 28 that a man is justified by faith without
works of the law, “of the law” is added not distinctively but explanatorily.
He does not distinguish works of law and free choice from works of gospel
and faith so that the former fail to justify while the latter do. Rather, he
explains legal righteousness as righteousness of works done according to
law. To be justified by works and by law are therefore the same, just as Paul
treats justification “freely” in Romans 3:24 and “without works” in Romans
4:6 as equivalent.

Roman Catholics introduce justification by law when they invent
justification by works, miserably sending wretched sinners back from gospel
to law and from the covenant of grace to the covenant of works.

They cannot reply that their view especially establishes the covenant of
grace, gospel grace, and faith because it makes only works flowing from
gospel faith and grace effective for justification. Their view merely makes
gospel grace and faith aids by which a man acquires the power to do good
works, and then, assisted by faith and gospel, obtains justification through
those works. He is therefore justified under the new covenant by a new aid
but precisely the same way as under the old: by working and satisfying the
law in his own person, though aided by faith in Christ. In the substance of
the matter, the material of justification, and the covenant condition, the
way and rationale of justification remain exactly those of the old covenant.

[1679 printed p. 139 | IA leaf 147]

Whether a laborer demands his contractual wage as a debt using his own spade
or one borrowed from his master makes no difference. The righteousness of
the new covenant, established upon other and better promises (Hebrews 8:6),
is altogether different. Paul significantly calls the righteousness opposed
to legal righteousness “righteousness from God.” It is not merely from God,
as legal righteousness is in its manner, but is Christ's righteousness,
because by his death and obedience he fully satisfied legal righteousness in
our place. In Christ the condition of the covenant of works was thus
fulfilled for our justification.

With respect to us, it is “through faith” not because faith causally effects
it, but because faith takes hold of Christ and his offered external
righteousness as with a hand. It rests wholly “upon faith,” that is, depends
upon and is appointed only to faith apprehending Christ. This is the truest
interpretation of “upon faith,” not the Roman claim that through faith we
recognize this righteousness to be from God, or that faith obtains it and
is its beginning and foundation. Faith may rightly be called the foundation
of this righteousness insofar as it rests on Christ alone apprehended by
faith, not as a beginning from which righteousness is efficiently produced.

They may object: Paul opposes his own righteousness to the righteousness of
faith, but faith and good works are not opposed; they are subordinated.
Therefore the righteousness of these works is not the “our righteousness”
Paul opposes to the righteousness of faith.

I answer:

1. Even on Roman doctrine it may be called ours because it inheres in us and
is produced as we act and work—indeed, partly from our own operation, since
they join our free choice with God's grace in producing it and assign free
choice the determination of the operation and therefore the primary role.

2. Faith and the good works it produces are indeed subordinated: works are
subordinate to faith insofar as they flow from it as their cause and, after
justification previously obtained through faith, lead toward glory along
the way of sanctification. Roman Catholics instead subordinate faith to
works and Christ to Moses. They introduce Christ and faith in him merely as
a supplement enabling us to perform good works, while making those works the
material of righteousness and the very form of justification. So much for
their position.

As to the matter itself, faith and good works are subordinated as
justification and sanctification are: each keeps its own place and order and
they agree amicably. But in justification itself they are directly opposed
and mutually remove one another. If we are justified by faith, then not by
works; if by works, then not by faith. If by works, it is not by grace
(Romans 11:6); if by faith, then by grace.
