VERSE 22. For there is a man whose labor has been performed with wisdom,
knowledge, and equity; yet to a man who did not labor in it, he will hand it
over as his portion. This too is vanity and a great evil.

The second part of the reasons follows. Here he both confirms this disparity
and amplifies, by comparison with a greater case, the preceding reason
concerning things acquired by labor being transferred to another. For they
are transferred not only to descendants, concerning whose prudent management
there can be doubt, but they also often come into the hands of those who are
connected to us by no bond of blood, affinity, or friendship. It is grievous
for things acquired by one's own labor to be transmitted to posterity
suspected of folly; but it is much more grievous for them to be dispersed to
strangers and indeed—as sometimes happens—to our enemies.

The torment of mind which the flesh suggests would have been either mitigated
or entirely removed by piety. For in the case of Job it appears that this
punishment is inflicted chiefly upon the wicked but rarely befalls the godly.
Job 5:5. Hence Job's friends condemned him for wickedness because he was
plundered by dreadful and barbarous men—namely, Chaldeans and Sabeans. Against
so great an evil, therefore, a ready and prepared remedy exists in piety.

Next, by distributing those things to the poor or appointing the Church and
schools as heirs, a person stores up treasure for himself in heaven, Matthew
6:20, where the dreadful man cannot reach, and makes friends for himself who
will receive him into the eternal dwellings when he has been dismissed from
the office of steward. Luke 16:9. Here it is not beside the point to consider
the mind of men turned away from this piety. Although no son, brother, or
kinsman survives them, yet rather than appoint the Church as heir, they are
accustomed to transfer their possessions to a relative, or to someone who has
the same surname but is joined to them by no bond of blood. Thus they prefer
to bestow what they have upon anyone whatever rather than upon God, from whom
they received everything. At the Last Judgment these men will be condemned by
the papists, who, in order to maintain superstition, have adjudged no small
part of their inheritance away from their sons and heirs.

Finally, the godly person reckons that God takes away with perfect justice and
wisdom those goods which men carry off unjustly. Since God is the true owner
of all things, he can transfer them by his own right, especially since

he gave those goods as a gift and gratuitously, even when we labored
strenuously to acquire them.

He further aggravates this evil from its efficient cause—namely, the wisdom
and skill by whose aid the advantages of this life were acquired. For there
is no lack of men who grow rich through their own folly and are held in honor,
especially when they serve and comply with the caprice of fools. But it does
not burn a man so deeply if something he acquired without industry and
difficulty is taken from him as it does when the thing was acquired with
exceptional art, industry, and knowledge of affairs. This, to be sure, reflects
the judgment of the flesh.

For when things acquired through folly are taken away, there is a twofold
grief: both that they have been taken from the man, and that they were acquired
through folly. Yet it is fitting to grieve over only one of these—the fact
that they were lost. As for the fact that he acquired them by wise industry,
that is so far from being a cause for grief that it may even supply an argument
for rejoicing and consoling himself. Indeed, in the former case one is granted
to recognize nothing but God's wrath in punishing; in the latter, however, one
is permitted to behold a trial of patience and faith, but not God's angry
countenance. This can be clearly established from the example of Job.

He next continues to amplify this evil from the accompanying innocence and
equity which men sometimes employ in acquiring these goods by labor, as though
the fact that they were despoiled of things justly acquired had stung more
sharply than if those things had been acquired by violence or fraud. In this,
the thoroughly perverse judgment of this world's wise men again betrays
itself: they torment themselves all the more by that with which they ought to
console and sustain themselves amid the plundering. For it ought to have been
considered more grievous if they had lost things unjustly acquired than if they
had suffered the plundering of goods amassed without corruption.

If someone suffers an injury inflicted by another, it is immediately asserted
that the injured man would not bear it painfully if he had deserved it. What
else is this but to make a pronouncement from the judgment of the flesh,
contrary to what the Holy Spirit has declared? For it is better, Peter says,
to suffer evil while doing good than while doing evil. 1 Peter 3:17.

He adds that this matter is vain, as he said concerning the previous one. But
he further adds, to aggravate the gravity of the evil, that it is a great evil
in comparison with the former case when posterity enjoys things acquired by
us. He gives the reason for this in verse 23: nothing remains to that man from
all the grievous labor with which he wearied himself. Yet provided only that
piety is present, by which he bears this injury calmly and patiently,
something greater and more precious accrues through this affliction than what
departs through the plundering of his goods. For a single drachma, so to speak,
of true patience is better than a thousand talents of gold and silver, since
by that patience the Holy Spirit bears witness to our spirit that we are God's
children.

*Translator's notes:*

1. Although the exposition cites `vers. 23` and paraphrases its rationale, the
   edition does not set a separate repeated verse-23 lemma here. This unit
   therefore continues through that discussion and ends at the next repeated
   lemma, `VERS. 24`, on printed p. 140.
2. The edition prints the grammatically irregular `comparato majoris`, where
   the context appears to call for an ablative construction such as
   `comparato majore`. The Latin retains the printed reading; the English
   renders the contextual sense, “by comparison with a greater case,” without
   silently changing the transcription.
3. After the printed reference `Matth. 6. 20`, the edition unmistakably prints
   `quo horridus pertingere nequit`. The English renders `horridus` literally
   as “the dreadful man”; the heavenly-treasure context would suggest a thief,
   but neither paired text silently substitutes one. The same adjective occurs
   earlier in the description of Job's Chaldean and Sabean plunderers.
4. In `Quos in Judicio ultimo Pontificii condemnabunt, qui ...`, the English
   takes `Pontificii` as the subject and `qui` as referring back to it: the
   papists will condemn the preceding men although they themselves have
   alienated inheritances from their sons and heirs to sustain superstition.
   The compact Latin permits some attachment ambiguity and is retained without
   alteration.
5. The printed `Iob 5. 5` supports the claim about calamity befalling the
   wicked, but the Chaldean and Sabean raids occur in Job 1:15 and 1:17. The
   paired bodies retain the printed reference. They also retain the printed
   `Matth. 6. 20`, `Luc. 16. 9`, and `1. Pet. 3. 17` without silently expanding
   or changing them.
