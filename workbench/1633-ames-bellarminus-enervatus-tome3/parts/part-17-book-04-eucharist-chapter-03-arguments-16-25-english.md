#### Bellarmine's arguments

16. Bellarmine. To imply a contradiction is to be repugnant to the essence.
But for magnitude not to occupy a place or not to exclude another body is not
repugnant to magnitude, because these things are posterior to magnitude
itself.

Protestant reply. 1. What is repugnant to existence implies a contradiction
just as much as what is repugnant to essence, if existence is in question. 2.
Although things which necessarily follow either essence or consequence are
posterior by nature, nevertheless their denial is repugnant to the essence or
existence of the affirmation. 3. Occupying a place belongs to a body prior by
nature to its being organic or human.

[1632 witness | printed p. 105]

17. Bellarmine. Magnitude can be conceived, and even defined, without place.

Protestant reply. It cannot be conceived or defined without space.

18. Bellarmine. The outermost sphere is without place.

Protestant reply. It is not without its own space.

19. Bellarmine. Filling a place and excluding other bodies is the office and
function of magnitude; therefore it does not pertain to its essence.

Protestant reply. It is the kind of function that it is for a form to form,
for a relative thing to have reference to another, and for a place to contain,
and so forth.

20. Bellarmine. Bulk or extension stands in relation to magnitude as gravity
stands in relation to a heavy body. But weighing is not of the essence of a
heavy thing, for water or air does not weigh in its proper place. Therefore
filling is likewise not of the essence of magnitude.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine either confuses extension with the filling of
a place, two things which he distinguished above, or constructs a syllogism
with four terms. 2. He takes gravity for the act of weighing or tending
downward, an act which he also absurdly attributes to air; but in this sense
the proposition is false. 3. If this comparison proves anything, it proves
that the extension of magnitude can be separated from a body not only by a
miracle but even by nature. For he wishes actual weighing to be separated
naturally from heavy bodies in their own places.

21. Bellarmine. In Matthew 19 the Lord says that it is possible for a camel to
pass through the eye of a needle.

Protestant reply. The Lord does not say this.

Bellarmine. He says that the more difficult thing is possible, namely, for a
rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven. He therefore signifies that the
easier thing is possible: for a camel, that is, to pass through the eye of a
needle.

Protestant reply. 1. In proverbial speech, such as this was, something of the
same impossibility is called more difficult. For the meaning is: if anything
is more difficult, this is it. 2. By the rich man is understood the one who
trusts in riches, as in Mark 10:24. It is plainly impossible for such a man to
enter the kingdom of heaven while he remains such, but God can so change him
that he enters. In the same way, a camel, while remaining a camel, cannot
possibly pass through the eye of a needle; but it can be so changed by God
that it becomes fit to pass through.

22. Bellarmine. In John 20 Christ entered through closed doors.

Protestant reply. He is indeed said to have entered after the doors had been
closed, but not through closed doors. He could easily have opened an entrance
for himself in another way. Thus Durandus also maintains, on Book 4,
distinction 44, question 6, in his reply: “It is said that Christ entered to
the disciples when the doors were closed, but it is not said that he entered
through closed doors. For since a glorified body can move through the air,
and since Christ by divine power could show and conceal his body as he

[1632 witness | printed p. 106]

wished, he could have entered to the disciples through windows or some other
place, and then first shown himself when he was before the disciples. Thus he
truly came when the doors were closed, but not through closed doors. And if
he entered through the doors, the doors could have been opened very suddenly
and closed suddenly, so that their opening and closing escaped the disciples.
And because a thing is not said to happen when it does not become known, the
doors are therefore said to have been closed, since their opening appeared to
no one.”

Bellarmine 1. Then entering when the doors were closed would have been no
miracle.

Protestant reply. It does not follow. For in Acts 12 it was a miracle when
Peter did not go out through the closed iron gate, but that gate opened for
him of its own accord.

Bellarmine 2. It is apparent from the circumstances that no noise was made,
but the whole house remained unmoved.

Protestant reply. As though some part of the house, or the door itself, could
not have been opened without noise in such a way that everything seemed
unmoved, because nothing was moved in the ordinary manner.

23. Bellarmine. The Lord came out of his mother's closed womb with her
virginity inviolate, that is, while the integrity of her flesh remained. For
the blessed virgin was a virgin even in childbirth.

Protestant reply. The natural opening in childbirth no more diminished the
blessed Mary's virginity than did the extension which naturally precedes
childbirth in pregnant women. Although that fiction is the older one, it has
the womb more tightly closed than would allow it to bring this more recent
fiction forth into the light of truth. Durandus, in the place cited, replies
to the same effect: “There is another possible manner, namely, that by divine
power the dilation of the members and natural passages occurred without any
rupture,” and so forth.

24. Bellarmine. Christ and the blessed pass into heaven, but heaven is a solid
body like bronze.

Protestant reply. God seems to have hidden the nature of heaven from us
mortals, but he has made it very clear that it does not have solidity like
that of bronze. For the whole expanse has no greater solidity than air, and
what is higher seems to surpass air in subtlety just as it does in nobility.
This argument, therefore, is like the rest: that is, it is an enormous and
shameless postulate. Durandus also replies here: “Whether heaven is posited
as divisible by nature or by divine power, Christ's body in its ascent to
heaven need not have been simultaneously together with the bodies of the
spheres; rather, body yielded to body. Nor are the heavens called most
solid in such a way that they cannot be divided by divine power and provide
passage to glorified bodies. They are instead called most solid because they
are naturally incorruptible.”

25. Bellarmine. Among the punishments of the damned there are many miracles
which are more difficult, or equally difficult, than this matter with which
we are dealing.

[1632 witness | printed p. 107]

Protestant reply. We cannot render judgment about infernal miracles until the
ordinary dispensation which concerns them is known to us in particular. But
it did not seem good to God to reveal to us the particular condition of those
things.

Bellarmine. It belongs no more to a quantified body to occupy a place than it
belongs to a heavy thing to weigh, a colored thing to be seen, a luminous
thing to shine, or a hot thing to heat.

Protestant reply. We simply deny that proposition, because occupying a place
differs from a body not in reality but only conceptually, whereas the
difference between a second act and the first is greater. Dimensions and
their space are necessarily required for a body's existence, but operation is
not necessarily required for the power or capacity to operate.
