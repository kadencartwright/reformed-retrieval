Question 8. Can the essence of created freedom properly be made to consist in
an absolute power to act or not act when all the requisites for acting are in
place? I answer that it cannot. For such an absolute and independent power is
the second cause’s absolute dominion over its own acts, and GOD’s absolute
freedom possesses nothing more than this. Therefore the Jesuits and Arminians
contend with us over whether absolute dominion over free acts belongs to
JEHOVAH or instead to little men of clay and unstable angels. They contend for
the dominion of creatures; we, for the Creator’s dominion. We flee to the
Scriptures and to JEHOVAH; they flee to the asylum of a reason gone mad.

Jesuits and Arminians.]

Question 9. Does GOD’s decree concur in the numerically same act that the will
itself would elicit if, per impossibile, we supposed that no decree existed in
God? I answer affirmatively, because the necessity of the decree and the
necessity of the actual exercise of freedom remove the same indifferences of
freedom. Nor does God’s decree twist an unwilling will elsewhere than toward
the very point to which it will in fact incline. This is evident from the
following diagram, which was corrupted in my earlier exercises through a
printer’s fault and inexcusable carelessness.

Question 10. Would Esau not have hated Jacob and refused to receive with a
placid countenance the brother whom he had long before resolved in his heart to
kill, unless God had decreed to turn Esau’s mind from hatred to favor? I answer:
if GOD’s decree to furnish concurrence to Esau’s freedom were set aside, Esau’s
free will would have broken forth neither into an act of hatred nor into an act
of favor. Yet I grant that the mind’s turn from hatred to favor must be
attributed to God and to his decree and motion. But the turn on the part of the
free will would then have been equally free—if we speak of created freedom—even
as subjected to the decree as it would have been had it not been subjected to
the decree. For the decree does not forcibly twist the free will away from what
it would in fact have willed, nor violently constrain it to will something else
against its inward inclination of choice. Rather, the decree concurs in what
the created will will in fact embrace, and that by the will’s own internal
motion. Thus the free act subjected to the decree is freer because it is
subjected to the decree, since the decree

determines upon GOD’s concurrence, which in a connatural and most gentle manner
acts as midwife to the free will of the creature. That will is, as it were,
pregnant and naturally fitted to give birth—not to twins, namely both not
willing and willing jointly, but only either not to will or determinately to
will. Nor does a free cause produce two effects at once, nor is it
naturally fitted to produce two at once, for the members of a disjunctive power
are not multiplied and do not constitute a number in actual existence. But if
one is posited, the other necessarily is not posited.

Question 11. Is any free act simply contingent to either alternative, and that
essentially? I answer negatively. For although a free cause is active and free
at its origin with respect to all internal causes—since, all things being
posited on the part of second causes, it can either not will or will—nevertheless
the formal indifference of created freedom is not its own, whether in relation
to the decree or in relation to the reception of divine predetermination. For
by reason of each it is free not simply but dependently, and from each—from the
decree and from the determination—it receives a determination that is
indeflectible on GOD’s part. Yet this indeflectibility is free: a contingent
necessity and a necessary contingency.

Question 12. Can freedom stand together with antecedent necessity? I answer
affirmatively; otherwise the Lord of Lords would not exercise governing
providence and sovereignty over all the actions of creatures.

Question 13. Since the invincible and indefeasible first cause acts upon all
second causes—and therefore upon the free will—with absolute necessity, and
since from an absolutely necessary antecedent, in an absolutely necessary
consequence, undoubtedly nothing but what is absolutely necessary can follow,
does it not follow that free acts are absolutely necessary from this antecedent
necessity? I answer negatively, because GOD’s will, with its invincible and
indefeasible necessity, does not act by an absolutely necessary influx, but in
accordance with the manner, genius, and disposition of the free cause, without
any violence. And as the influx of the first cause into the second is, so is
the consequence. For a logical inference follows the manner of being and
acting; but the influx is not absolutely necessary. Therefore the consequence
too is not absolutely necessary.
