Castellio especially ridicules this. Thus he writes at the end of his
treatise on justification: “No one should bring me that worn-out claim that
God is unwilling to free a person from sins lest the person become proud.
That is just as if you said that God is unwilling for him to be freed from
sin lest he sin, as though God either did not wish or were unable to cleanse
him from pride as well as from the other sins. But the thorn in the flesh
was left to Paul lest he become proud. I agree; but if you wish that thorn
to be sin, you make God marvelous indeed: he would leave sin in his disciple
lest the disciple sin. This is no less absurd than saying that Christ was
unwilling to heal the sick man lest the sick man become sick.”

I answer these things as follows.

1. Augustine confesses that he does not know what this thorn in the flesh
was, so there is no reason for us to inquire too curiously. We have no
leisure now to dispute whether it was some bodily sickness and disease, as
many have thought; persecution from the devil, his followers, false
apostles, and other adversaries, as Chrysostom and almost all the other
Greek commentators hold; or, finally, impulses of lust sent and kindled by
Satan.

I acknowledge that Erasmus is amazed that this final view could please any
learned theologian. Calvin also calls those who dream up such an
interpretation ridiculous, though he himself explains the thorn as
temptations according to the flesh by which the flesh—that is, the part of
the soul not yet regenerated—is pricked. Many fathers and recent writers,
including Roman theologians, interpret it as those impulses of lust. Nor is
reason opposed; on the question, see Estius and Cornelius a Lapide on the
passage and Bellarmine, chapter 30 on monks. If this exposition stands, the
passage contains much that confirms our position, including these two
points.

First, by God's permission and even appointment—for the thorn is said here
to be given, and given by God according to Augustine—believers are
restrained by lighter temptations and falls so that they do not tumble into
more serious ones. Thus Paul's thorn in the flesh restrained him from
pride, “a poison so great,” according to Augustine, “that it can be cured
only by poison.”

Second, this sad and sufficiently troublesome antidote, the remnants of sin
used against graver sins, is not employed for a short time only but clings
tenaciously until death. Though Paul bore that thorn with difficulty and
prayed to God three times to be delivered from it, he confesses that he did
not obtain his request. According to Theophylact, the thorn was given “not
so that I might be delivered from it altogether, but so that it should
remain continually.” According to Calvin, this evil was long-lasting.

Bellarmine speaks more boldly: “The Apostle was tempted by great and
long-lasting thorns of the flesh.” Augustine is most confident of all,
believing that Paul did not lack this thorn of the flesh until death. If
Cornelius a Lapide is to be believed, these thorns are so embedded in our
flesh that they cannot be torn out in this life. The Apostle agrees, for he
expects to be delivered from them together with the body, Romans 7:24.
