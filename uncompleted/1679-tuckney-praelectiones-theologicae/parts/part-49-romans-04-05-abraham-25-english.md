Third, it remains to inquire whether faith is so abolished in the heavenly
homeland that it does not remain there at all. In 1 Corinthians 13 we read:
“Whether prophecies, they will be abolished; whether tongues, they will
cease; whether knowledge, it will be destroyed.” Yet the Apostle does not
say the same there expressly of faith. It may be said that at the beginning
of the chapter “all faith” is numbered among several things above all of
which the Apostle prefers love. The easy answer is that miraculous faith is
meant there, as is evident from the immediately following words and from
chapter 12:9. Chrysostom and Theophylact agree: “Observe how in brief he
summed up all the gifts, mentioning prophecy or faith, the former in words,
while the latter's miracles are in deeds.” For the Apostle speaks of faith
separable from love, as miraculous faith can be, and by no means of
justifying and saving faith. Moreover, the beginning of that chapter stands
so far from its middle or end that, even if he had spoken of saving faith
at the beginning and in another respect had preferred love to it, it would
not immediately follow that when he proceeds further and prefers love to
other gifts on account of its perseverance, he also subordinates faith to
love on this ground. For that conclusion, as before, he would have had to
mention faith expressly here too, which, as I have said, he did not do.

[1679 printed p. 264 | IA leaf 272]

But it may be objected that, with regard to love's continuance, he prefers
it to prophecies, tongues, and knowledge, with which faith had been joined
at the beginning of the chapter. Faith should therefore be numbered with
them here too in this respect. I answer: certainly, if it were named here
just as it was there. But since it is not, and while naming the rest he
keeps silent about faith, it seems rather to follow that faith should in
this respect, at least, be exempted from the category of those other gifts.

It may be urged further, as Estius does, that the Apostle affirms in verse
10 that “when what is perfect has come, what is in part will be abolished.
But faith belongs to those things which are in part, for we believe in part
just as we know and prophesy in part,” as verse 9 says.

I answer:

Here we not only prophesy and believe in part, but while we are here we also
love in part—that is, imperfectly. Yet although the imperfection of
affection and love will be completely abolished in the future life, love
itself will always remain. We also know in part, but knowledge remains in
heaven, according to Jerome's statement: “Let us learn on earth the things
whose knowledge will continue for us in heaven.” Nothing in this argument,
therefore, prevents the same from being said of faith.

This is especially so since, as Durandus repeatedly emphasizes, the Apostle
speaks there of faith in the same way as of the knowledge and understanding
that will always endure there.

Indeed, after saying that prophecy and knowledge would eventually be
abolished, he nowhere makes such a statement about faith considered in
itself.

On the contrary, in the final verse he affirms that it remains together
with hope and love. I acknowledge that many interpreters explain this
“remaining” as continuance in the present life. Thus Calvin says:
“‘Remaining’ means that, when everything is subtracted as in an accounting,
this sum remains. Faith does not remain after death, for elsewhere the
Apostle opposes it to sight and teaches that it endures only until the time
when we cease to journey away from the Lord.” Beza likewise says: “I think
*now* and *then* are contrasted—that is, the condition of the present life
with the future—as though he said, ‘That, then, will be our condition; but
as we are now, three things are present with us and necessarily remain,
provided that we belong to Christ.’ Yet he uses the word ‘remain’ to
distinguish these gifts from others which are not necessarily bound up with
religion,” and so forth. Estius has parallel remarks on the passage.

Grotius, however, takes it differently: “*Now* here is not temporal but
adversative. For they will remain in the other age.” But how? He answers
that they remain not in themselves but with respect to their effects, in
the way that the rock followed the Israelites through its effect and works
follow the dead insofar as they have a reward. The dispute therefore still
awaits judgment.

Irenaeus, in book 2, chapter 47, grants a place in the future age to faith
and hope as well as love, for which his annotator Gallasius rebukes him.
Tertullian too, in chapter 12 of *On Patience*, says that when tongues,
forms of knowledge, and prophecies are exhausted, faith, hope, and love
nevertheless remain. But very many others hold the contrary.

Indeed, if we attend more carefully to the Apostle, we must believe that at
least some abolition of faith

[1679 printed p. 265 | IA leaf 273]

will occur in the next age. Where he says that hope, faith, and love remain,
he also says that love is greater in this respect. Although he does not
explicitly mention faith in the preceding words, he nevertheless contrasts
it as the mirror-like and enigmatic sight of this life with the face-to-face
sight of the next life in verse 12. Similarly, 2 Corinthians 5:7 sings what
seems a recantation: “to walk by faith and not by sight.” Hebrews 11:1
defines faith as “the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things
not seen.” These two descriptions denote futurity and lesser evidence. But
how can things be future or hoped for when they will then be present? What
“things not seen” will remain in that beatific vision?

This is why Lombard, book 3, distinction 31, concludes that faith and hope
will be entirely abolished. Aquinas, Durandus, and others, however, grant
that something of faith remains in glory. Aquinas says that hope is totally
abolished because it is related to the enjoyment of beatitude as motion is
to rest at its destination, but that faith remains generically the same
with respect to knowledge, though not specifically and numerically the
same. This applies to its habit, not its act.

Aquinas affirms that in the former sense it is most truly said that faith
remains. Durandus proves that it is by no means heretical to say that faith
can remain both in habit and in act, because no testimony of Holy Scripture
says the contrary. Indeed, he says it is not even false, because Scripture
“says the same about knowledge and faith with respect to abolition: ‘when
what is perfect has come, what is in part will be abolished.’ Yet according
to all the Doctors, knowledge will not be abolished with respect to its act,
but only with respect to its imperfect mode.” Cornelius a Lapide holds that
faith is abolished with respect to both act and habit, since the habit would
be pointless if it were never reduced to act. Durandus answers this
objection in the place cited above.

This, then, is all they maintain: faith specifically and numerically
identical with the wayfarers' faith here does not remain in those who
possess the goal, nor are its acts actually exercised there. Different
writers try to confirm this from different principles.
