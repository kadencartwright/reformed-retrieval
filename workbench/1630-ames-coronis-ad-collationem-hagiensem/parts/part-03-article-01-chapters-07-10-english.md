### Chapter VII

#### Fifth argument

Page 101.

Scripture frequently mentions a certain book which is called the Book of God,
of the Living, and of Life, in which some, but not all, are said to be
inscribed.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 51 | printed p. 39]

This inscription is taken to be nothing other than an appointment to salvation by
God’s immutable decree, and therefore an appointment of certain people to life
by a singular favor and privilege. Certain passages of Scripture are brought
forward for this: Philippians 4:3; Revelation 21:27; Luke 10:20. This argument
primarily opposes that general predestination which the Remonstrants seemed to
have posited by itself in their thesis, but it also has force against the
special election from foreseen faith which they deduce from the former. The
matter itself illustrates the point in this way. Soldiers who used to be
enrolled for war, senators enrolled for a public council or even a palace,
and all who are appointed to some office or dignity and are, as it were,
entered upon a register, do not customarily exercise that office first and
then get enrolled. They are enrolled first and afterward called to the actual
exercise of it. In exactly the same way, the elect are first appointed in the
divine decree to the place and office of those who are to be saved, and
afterward they are called to discharge their office and enjoy their ascribed
dignity. Let us therefore see what the Remonstrants bring forward to the
contrary.

1. They deny that “to be inscribed in the book of life” signifies nothing
other than to be appointed to salvation by God’s eternal and immutable
counsel. 2. They affirm that sacred Scripture treats inscription in the Book
of Life in two ways. Sometimes, they say, it treats inscription in the book of
life, whose opposite is to be erased from it (Rev. 3:5; 22:19). This means
nothing other than to be regarded as upright and as a Son of God according to
the present condition of faith, righteousness, and obedience (Luke 10:20–21).
But the other inscription does not have erasure as its opposite and signifies
election to eternal life according to God’s eternal and immutable counsel.
This applies to none except those who persevere in faith, considered precisely
as such (Ps. 69:29; Rev. 13:8; 21:27).

But 1. they have formed this distinction in such a way that it obscures the
matter proposed rather than illuminating it: an inscription

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 52 | printed p. 40]

opposed to erasure, and an inscription not opposed to it. Logic and right reason
forbid forming a division out of contradictories, for, as they say in the
schools, all things are contradictory and can equally be fitted to anything.
Furthermore, it is rashly and thoughtlessly said that one inscription is
opposed to erasure and another is not—as though they were to say that one kind
of heat is opposed to cold and another kind is not. All things that differ in
logical account and reality, so that they cannot be attributed to the same
thing in the same respect, in relation to the same thing, and at the same
time, are truly opposed. Yet they will not say, I hope, that this latter
inscription in the book can coexist with erasure from that same book. This
second inscription is therefore no less opposed to erasure than the other;
indeed, it is much more opposed to it. For it excludes erasure not merely for
the present, as the former does, but for eternity. It is a fault of no less
importance that they do not clearly identify the respect in which the
difference between these inscriptions consists—whether it is placed in the
one who inscribes, in the manner, or even in the end of the inscription.

2. The former inscription, which they define as being regarded upright and a
Son of God, is stated far too indefinitely. Someone is regarded as such
either by people or by God himself. People do so according to the rule of
charity, from effects and indications that meet the eyes. Many are so
regarded by people who nevertheless are not approved by God. Hence certain
theologians customarily employ a distinction between the book of the church
and the book of God, the common and the singular book, the book of calling and
that of election; and likewise between an apparent and a true inscription, an
inscription simply speaking and one in a qualified respect, and so forth. But
the Remonstrants appear to approve none of these. They therefore ought to have
explained more distinctly what they meant.

3. When they deny that to be inscribed in the book of life is to be
predestined to life, they have as opponents not only the theologians of our
Confession but also the Augustans—indeed, the Wittenberg theologians
themselves. For Hunnius writes: “It lies

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 53 | printed p. 41]

beyond the hazard of dispute that to be inscribed in the book of life is the
same as to be elected and predestined to life. Hence the *Formula of Concord*
plainly regards as the same people those who are elect and those whose names
are written in the book of life.”

4. Since they have stated their entire reply ambiguously, its refutation
requires a general demonstration which admits no opening for escape. I shall
attempt to construct it as follows. The book of life properly exists with
God, not among people. Inscription is an act of God, not of man. It is either
the decree or something flowing from the decree. This decree concerns
particular and definite people. For no one ever said that soldiers or
senators had been enrolled because the condition required for such an office
had been laid down. Such an arrangement is not called inscription but the
election and definite enrollment of particular people. Inscription in the
Book of Life is therefore the ascription of particular people to life in the
divine mind.

Now this ascription is either apparent or true. The apparent is not presently
in question; the Remonstrants understand the true inscription (unless I am
mistaken) in each part of their distinction. This true inscription or
ascription to life is either absolute or conditional. If they call at least
the former inscription conditional, then either the inscription itself is
suspended upon a condition, or only the life to which the inscription is
made. If it is the inscription itself, then from the beginning it does not
exist and has not truly, actually, and in reality been made. For what depends
upon a condition does not occur before that condition is fulfilled. “A
condition properly speaking,” the jurists say, “exists when something is
conferred in a doubtful case which can issue in being or nonbeing, and thus in
a future event.” From a conditional stipulation there is only the hope that
something will become due, as Justinian teaches. But if they call the
inscription absolute and the life conditional, then they separate what God
has joined, and they make that award of life which is given to the believer in
the sentence of justification just as uncertain

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 54 | printed p. 42]

as every future contingency. If the inscription is absolute, it was made either
from eternity or in time, and it is either firm and immovable or mutable as
occasion arises. If it is temporal and mutable, then this inscription does
not properly suit either God or the Book. For “book” and “inscription” imply
the highest firmness and constancy, while “God’s Book and Inscription” plainly
denote the eternity of that firmness in the divine decree and knowledge. I
leave it to them and to the reader to consider what escape they can devise
here.

5. As for the opposition they repeatedly press between the one kind of
Inscription and its contrary, Erasure, this can properly be understood as a
negative and contradictory opposition. Thus, to be erased from the book
signifies nothing other than not to be written in it, as David seems to
explain in express words in Psalm 69:29: “Let them be erased from the book of
life,” that is, “Let them not be written with the righteous,” or numbered
among the righteous. This is also sufficiently indicated in the very passages
the Remonstrants bring forward here. In Revelation 3:5 the words immediately
following show that “to be erased from the book of life” is nothing other than
not to be acknowledged by Christ before his Father and his angels. Revelation
22:19 must be explained in the same way. The addition of plagues in verse 18
does not signify that those threatened with them had previously experienced
those plagues and would suffer still heavier ones from an addition made to
that book. In the same way, “the removal of a part from the book of life” in
verse 19 does not prove that they previously possessed a part or share in
that book which they will no longer retain. This would imply the contradiction
that the same people, at the same time (for the Apostle adjures the same
people in each part), both possessed those plagues and possessed that part. It
would also make the actual possession of life—which is understood by the
“part,” just as actual plagues are placed over against it—liable to removal.
Rather, the Apostle pronounces that those plagues will be inflicted and life
denied to all who attempt to corrupt the sacred volume.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 55 | printed p. 43]

This warning is expressed by phrases which display the just proportion between sin and
punishment: God will add to those who add; God will take away from those who
take away. For the Greek is stated as if by anaphora: ἐὰν εἴ τις ἐπιτιθῇ
ἐπιθήσει ὁ Θεὸς, ἐὰν εἴ τις ἀφαιρῇ, ἀφαιρήσει ὁ Θεὸς—“if anyone adds,
God will add; if anyone takes away, God will take away.”

6. If they wish this opposition to be privative, so that a simple, real, and
true Inscription precedes and a similar Erasure follows behind, then 1. they
openly contradict Scripture, which expressly affirms that the followers of
the beast did not have their names written in the book of life from the
foundation of the world (Rev. 16:8). This is not denied of them because they
had never given their names to the church, but on the contrary because they
had been Christians by profession and now, as it were, worshiped Antichrist.
That clause is interposed so that such apostasy might not prejudice divine
election and so that we might learn that the profession of apostates is very
far removed from the state of true believers. Therefore, if either the names
or the parts of people of this sort are said to be taken from the book of
life, this must either be taken negatively, as I explained above, or declared
in Christ’s words: “Whoever does not have, even what he thinks he has will be
taken from him” (Luke 8:18). For both John and Christ himself affirm that such
people were never truly numbered in the register of the living: “I never knew
you” (Matt. 7:23). Surely, if he had ever truly entered them in the book of
life, he never would have sent them such a message. “For God’s foundation
stands, having this seal: God knows those who are his” (2 Tim. 2:19). This
interpretation also seems incapable of standing with God’s constancy and the
indelible force of his decrees. The divine book admits no insertion and
endures no erasure. Augustine rightly says: “If a man said, ‘What I have
written, I have written,’ does God write anyone down and erase him?” 7. It is
not rightly said that the names of the seventy-two in Luke 10:20 are called
“written in heaven” with regard to the fact that they were Christ’s disciples.
For then the name of

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 56 | printed p. 44]

Judas the traitor would also have been written in heaven as a son of God, even when
Scripture had given him the wholly contrary name: the son of perdition.
Furthermore, it seems insufficiently coherent to add that this is also said
with respect to the revelation mentioned in verse 21. They wish that
revelation to be nothing other than that of the object or proposed word, and
they make it common to all who have the word preached to them. They must
otherwise say that the names of all the Jews—even the most abandoned, who at
some time heard Christ’s discourses—had been written in the Book of Life.

8. When they say that the Book of Life which they concede to be immutable
embraces only those believers who have now persevered to the end, they seem to
remove the rationale and credibility both of the book and of its inscription
from that divine book. An inscription of this kind principally denotes the
firmness of a future thing. But their limitation requires that believers be
considered as belonging to the past and life as present, for there is no
distance between the end of perseverance and the beginning of life.

9. It is ridiculous that they make such a great difference between the Book
of Life (Rev. 3:5; 22:19) and the Book of the Lamb (Rev. 13:8; 21:27), as
though the Lamb’s book were immutable and belonged to a certain decree
concerning believers who persevere, while the other, called the Book of Life,
were much more general and of uncertain outcome. Yet elsewhere they maintain
that all have been redeemed in the Lamb, or Christ, and that only those who
persevere in faith are appointed to life.

### Chapter VIII

#### Sixth argument

Page 104.

Christ Jesus says in John 10:16 that even those among the Gentiles who had not
yet heard his word so that they might believe in him were his sheep. God also
says in Acts 18:10 that those who had not yet all believed were his People.
They could not be or be called such except by virtue of election, and a
singular election at that. Therefore there is a singular election even of
those who do not yet believe. This is the meaning and force of the argument by
which it prevails against the Remonstrants’ position on singular election. The
Remonstrants reply to it with the following two points.

1. God and Christ speak according to foreknowledge, by which they know who
will believe and who will become Sheep and a People; election to salvation is
also founded upon this foreknowledge. This is demonstrated by the words
themselves, since they are called not people but Sheep and a People. They were
not actually such; therefore they were such by foreknowledge, just as Dr.
Calvin also correctly explains Acts 18:10. From these passages it can
therefore be rightly gathered that election is founded upon foreknowledge,
for they are so named with respect to God’s certain foreknowledge that they
would become that upon which election is founded. 2. The passage in Acts 18:10
can be understood of those who actually believed, since many Corinthians
already believed at that time.

Here 1. it will be worthwhile to note how uncertain and doubtful this entire
reply is. They do not concede that these passages concern eternal election,
yet neither do they deny it; they leave the matter in the middle. They say
that foreknowledge is regarded—and who denies it?—but are silent on whether
they mean bare foreknowledge alone. They affirm that the passage in Acts can
be understood otherwise, but do not

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 58 | printed p. 46]

pronounce whether it ought to be. They certainly could and should have shown
what they mean more openly and sincerely. Socinus, with whom they agree in
this matter, lifts the veil and
discloses the position of his mind: “Those are here called Christ’s Sheep who,
because of some uprightness that was in them, were fit and suited for
believing” (*Theological Lectures*, ch. 12, sec. 12).

2. It must also be observed that they entirely invert Christ’s words. Christ
gives the reason why he had to bring many from the Gentiles (δεῖ ἀγαγεῖν) to
his fold: because they were his sheep. These men, on the contrary, maintain
that they are and are called sheep because they would come to Christ’s fold,
that is, because they would believe. Christ deduces their being brought and
called from the relation of shepherd and sheep. These men reverse it and make
the whole relation of sheep depend upon their being brought and their coming.

3. In this manner the entire order of election is also perverted. They say
that election to salvation is founded upon that foreknowledge by which God
knows who would become, or would be willing to become, his Sheep and People.
Divine election is therefore founded at least upon foreknowledge of human
election. For to approach Christ and God in this manner is nothing other than
to choose them as Shepherd and God. Christ himself expelled this from the
minds of the faithful as an altogether inverted notion: “You did not choose
me, but I chose you” (John 15:16).

4. The reasoning they repeatedly press by itself is wholly trifling: “They
were not actually sheep; therefore they were sheep in God’s foreknowledge.”
For who denies that they were Sheep in the divine Foreknowledge? Does it
follow from this that they were not also in divine Predestination? Christ as
Mediator was in God’s foreknowledge from eternity (1 Pet. 1:20), but I suppose
no Christian will deny that he was also in Predestination.

5. Their attempted deduction from the names “Sheep” and “People” has similar
value: “They are called not People but sheep and a People; therefore it is
presupposed that they were believers in God’s foreknowledge and are regarded
here—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 59 | printed p. 47]

as adorned with the quality of faith. Christ also laid down his life for the
Sheep, verse 15, and saved his People from their sins (Matt. 1:21). Yet he did
not regard them as adorned with the quality of faith when he undertook the
work of redemption.

6. It is remarkable that they claim Dr. Calvin’s agreement with their
interpretation, since in his commentary on Acts 18:16 not even the word
“foreknowledge” is found, and our position is explained and confirmed so
clearly that nothing could surpass it. For he writes: “The Lord calls them his
People. Although they could then deservedly be regarded as strangers, yet
because they were written in the Book of Life and soon to be adopted into the
family, they are not improperly adorned with this title. For we know that many
sheep wander outside the flock for a time, just as many wolves are mingled
with the sheep. Therefore, those whom the Lord decreed shortly afterward to
acquire for himself, he already acknowledges among his people with respect to
their future faith. But let us remember that those who by God’s eternal
adoption belong to Christ’s body are engrafted into it. As it is written,
‘They were yours, and you gave them to me’” (John 17:6). He affirms that they
are called a People with respect to future faith—but a faith made future by
their inscription in the Book of Life, by the decree of their most certain
acquisition, and by the eternal adoption through which they were given to
Christ to be saved.

7. The substitute interpretation, according to which God is said in Acts
18:10 to speak of those who had already been converted, is refuted from the
context itself. For 1. this was said to Paul through a vision, but Paul knew
the number of the converts well enough from the evidence of the matter; there
was no need for a vision and new revelation. 2. Although many Corinthians are
said to have believed, “a numerous people,” or “a multitude of people,” conveys
something more than can be attributed to those Gentiles already converted.
They are called many by comparison with the few among the Jews of that place
who then received the gospel’s teaching. 3. The reasoning of the consequence
does not hold: that Paul should proceed there with confidence and expect
certain protection because a numerous people had already been converted.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 60 | printed p. 48]

Unless, perhaps, they mean that Paul was encouraged by God with a great
retinue, like a military guard, by which he was surrounded and kept safe from
the unruliness of the petty Greeks that could have raged against him, so that
he might conduct the work fearlessly. Everyone sees how far this departs from
the rationale and practice of this embassy. 4. If he had gathered a proper
church there, it would then have been fitting for him to go elsewhere and
devote himself to similar work where it was most needed. If so numerous a
people had already been converted, this would not have been an argument that
Paul would be immune from the oppression of his enemies. Rather, because a
numerous people still remained to be converted by Paul’s labor according to
the divine decree, Paul had to continue, and he did not need to fear the force
and tumult of his enemies.

### Chapter IX

#### The doctrine of the Belgic Confession and Catechism on singular election

Page 105.

Thus far we have heard the Remonstrants’ position and defense in this part of
the article fighting against the Scriptures. Now the extra weight deduced from
the Belgic Catechism and Confession must be added to the scale. Here the
ecclesiastical formularies are contested in the same way that the women who
had given birth once contended before Solomon about the living son (1 Kings
3), when each cried out with passion, “The living son is mine!” Thus in this
case we have the same protest from each side: “The Catechism that lives and
flourishes is mine! The Confession too is mine!” We therefore need some
Solomon as judge in whom God’s wisdom might shine for the exercise of right
judgment. He would immediately observe whose affections burn for the Catechism
and

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 61 | printed p. 49]

Confession, lest they be put to death, and whose voice and wish in this dispute is, “Let
them be neither mine nor yours; let them be cut apart.” But since those whose
interest it is have not yet pronounced a judgment of this kind, we must try to
determine from certain signs and marks what is true and what is feigned in the
whole contest.

Four passages were produced against the Remonstrants’ thesis: Catechism,
questions 54 and 52; Confession, articles 16 and 37. To all of them they reply
that the passages not only do nothing against them but do the utmost for them.
We must therefore consider briefly, not what is asserted, but what weight of
reasoning supports it.

The first passage is from Catechism question 54, where it is stated that God
chose for himself from the whole human race a company for eternal life. The
Remonstrants reply: This is nothing against us; indeed, it is clearly for us,
since the words immediately following—“through his Spirit and Word, agreeing
in true faith”—show that God regarded this Church as agreeing in true faith.
But it will be helpful to see the complete answer: “I believe that from the
beginning of the world all the way to the end, the Son of God gathers,
protects, and preserves for himself, out of the whole human race, a company
chosen for eternal life, through his Spirit and Word, agreeing in true faith;
and that I am a living member of that company and will remain so forever.”
These are the words, from which it must be observed that the Catechism here
teaches that Christ gathers, protects, and preserves a company chosen for
eternal life. It is therefore evident that in the Catechism’s position this
company is regarded as elect in the very act of gathering or calling, and so
effectual calling depends upon election. Is this nothing against the
Remonstrants? 2. “Elect company” and “Church” are used in the same sense in the
Catechism, so that there is no part of the true Church which is not rightly
called elected to eternal life. But the Remonstrants teach that

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 62 | printed p. 50]

perseverance to the end is a prerequisite condition for a person or company to be
elected to life. Thus Election cannot be attributed to the Church Militant,
which has not yet reached the end, but only to the Church Triumphant. Is this
nevertheless nothing against the Remonstrants? 3. Election is placed there as
the first cause of the whole distinction between this company and the rest of
the human race: “Out of the whole human race he gathers a company elected to
eternal life.” The Remonstrants maintain that election is last and follows
consummated obedience of faith. Is this not something?

4. It is also of some significance that the catechumen is there taught to
believe that he is a living member of this elect company and will remain so
forever. For this answer does not require perseverance to the end before a
person is constituted elect. Instead, it makes every believer a vessel of
election in the present and deduces perseverance from election, not election
from perseverance. 5. When the words “through the Spirit and Word, agreeing in
true faith” are referred to Christ as the one who gathers, they plainly denote
the means by which divine election attains its end. When they are referred to
the elect company, they contain its description—its place, causes, and marks.
In either way they overthrow the Remonstrants’ cause. But they no more prove
that agreement in true faith is prerequisite to election than that it is
prerequisite to the first gathering or calling, or even to redemption through
Christ. For Christ gathered and redeemed his Church; yet it only becomes such,
the Remonstrants say, when it agrees in true faith.

The second passage is in question 52, where it is stated that Christ will
bring all his elect to himself into heavenly joys and glory. If these words
are joined with what precedes and explained from them, they contribute
something toward establishing the argument.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 63 | printed p. 51]

They were therefore not rashly added to the preceding passages in the
*Collation*. Taken separately, they do not do much for the point proposed,
except that the catechumen here believes himself to be a participant in election in the
present, which cannot stand with the Remonstrants’ position. Yet the
exceptions they oppose are entirely worthless. 1. “According to Scripture’s
manner of speaking, believers are understood by the elect.” It is true that
the same people who eventually become believers are called elect both in
Scripture and in the Catechism. But the further question is whether they are
elect before they become believers. Those who so maintain observe the manner
of Scripture, which teaches that it is God’s prerogative not to choose the
worthy—which belongs to human beings—but to make people worthy by choosing
them. 2. “The elect whom Christ will receive into his glory are those blessed
by the Father (Matt. 25). Believers are those blessed by the Father, and
therefore believers are those elect.” Who has ever denied that believers are
those elect? Yet the proof sins both in form and matter. Every dialectician
will perceive the formal fault. In the substance of the matter they err most
gravely if they think that no one is blessed by the Father except one who has
now persevered to the end, or if they suppose that such perseverance is
prerequisite to receiving even the very first of all blessings. For in that
case they would erase Redemption, Calling, Adoption, justification, and all
the firstfruits of eternal life from the register of blessings.

The third passage is from the Belgic Confession, article 16: “God delivers and
saves from condemnation and destruction those whom he elected in his eternal
counsel.” The Remonstrants reply: Those who are rescued from destruction are
believers. But the Confession establishes a clear division of fallen people,
some of whom it says are rescued from sin and misery while others are left
behind. It places the first cause and source of that rescue or deliverance in
the mercy of God who elects. Hence it is evident that faith is made not
something antecedent to election but its consequence. For there is nothing
intervening between

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 64 | printed p. 52]

death in sin and election to life. Furthermore, the gift of faith itself is
part of that rescue from sin and death. It therefore flows from election—if
the Confession is true—and is given to one elected; it is not prerequisite in
the act of choosing.

The fourth and last passage is from article 37 of the same Confession: “When
the number of the elect has been completed, Christ our Lord will come again
from heaven.” The Remonstrants reply: “The number of the elect is the number of
believers.” This is not badly said, but the phrase seems to denote something
more: namely, that God chose a definite number of people and therefore delays
the end of the world so that he may execute that election in every generation
just as he determined in himself from eternity.

### Chapter X

#### The second part: on the cause of singular election

Thus far we have discussed the first member of this question, that is, the
Object of Election. Now the second follows: the Character of this Object. Our
men maintain that the sole cause why God decreed to elect certain people in
this manner while passing over others is his good pleasure and sheer grace,
and not at all that he foresaw that one would believe in Christ while another
would not. Consequently, Faith is not a cause or condition preceding Election
to salvation, but a fruit which proceeds from it and follows it. What is
maintained is also proved by various valid arguments. But before we come to
them, our eyes must be turned toward the general exceptions which the
Remonstrants bring against this formulation of the question.

1. After begging pardon for the expression, they say that the Brethren
shamefully

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 65 | printed p. 53]

err when they also place the cause of God’s decree concerning certain people
who are not elected in God’s good pleasure—indeed, when they make this very
thing, that God does not elect someone, grace and sheer grace. But 1. whatever
shamefulness this error may have lies in their inference, not in the assertion
from which it is supposedly drawn. The inserted phrase “while passing over
others” indicates a comparison not of similar or equal people sharing the same
case, but of dissimilar people whose quality or condition is not the same.
Moses once said of the Israelites: “Jehovah your God chose you to be his
peculiar people above all the other peoples, because Jehovah loves you”
(Deut. 7:6, 8). Will these men also mark error and shamefulness here, as if
Moses made love the cause why almost all the peoples were passed over by God?

2. I have already observed this same error in the Remonstrants’ writings. For
Nicolaas Grevinckhoven writes on page 107: “I establish the pure good pleasure
of God as the cause of Paul’s election rather than Caiaphas’s, the foundation
of which is Christ—namely, that good pleasure by which he willed in Christ to
elect everyone who believes or will believe, and to reprobate one who does not
or will not believe.” He places no other foundation of this decree, in which
reprobation is contained, than Christ, and no other cause than God’s sheer
good pleasure. Nicolaas and the rest therefore acted both inopportunely and
imprudently when they seized this handle for making an odious objection,
since they also blacken themselves with the same charcoal. I need not linger
over examining the consequences they attempted to form from this fictitious
principle, much less over the ambiguity which they say arises because the
qualities of the elect are not expressed. For we acknowledge no qualities of
the kind in view in this question—namely, none having a causal or occasional
relation to election.

2. They complain that they are wronged because it is

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 66 | printed p. 54]

charged against them that they establish faith as the Cause of election,
although they expressly affirm the contrary. But it does not much matter what
they affirm or deny in words. It is no wrong to attribute to them what they
teach in reality and what is most openly contained in the position they
defend. Indeed, that they concede to Faith the force and power of a cause with
respect to Election cannot possibly escape one who rightly examines what they
wrongly hand down.

First, in this very passage they all profess that they are not especially
concerned about the word “condition,” and Nicolaas Grevinckhoven speaks much
more affirmatively (p. 111): “Scripture perpetually describes faith as a
condition prerequisite by God.” What sort of condition they understand is
sufficiently taught elsewhere by the same Nicolaas—on the next page—where he
calls it prescribed and required. The same author contends that it is wholly
consistent with the nature of prescribed laws and conditions (p. 24) “that
the will of a judge should be moved to the reward by a condition required and
fulfilled.” If they can show any difference between a condition which moves a
judge’s will and an impelling cause, that difference will certainly be placed
among the omissions of the logicians.

2. They make believers the adequate object of Election and attribute to faith
the status of the formal character of this object. Now the following axiom is
accepted among those versed in these matters: “The formal character of an
adequate object is always the specifying cause of the habit, power, or act of
which it is the object.” Thus, in vision, light—which is the formal character
of the visible object—is also a cause of seeing. In the same way, a good
apprehended by the intellect is a cause of volition because it has the
character of its formal object. In exactly the same manner, since the
Remonstrants make faith the formal character of the object with respect to
Election, and also teach that God has outside himself proper causes of
willing, they cannot give a reason why those are said to wrong them who take
it as confessed that they make Faith the Cause of Election. 3. Arminius
teaches that Election is founded upon the

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 67 | printed p. 55]

foreknowledge of faith (*Declaration of Sentiments*, p. 47), as do all the
Remonstrants (*Collation*, p. 104). Nicolaas Grevinckhoven shows what force
this word has on page 102: the expression “to be founded upon” plainly seems
to signify that faith is laid beneath election as its foundation. Hence, in
the Remonstrants’ writings, “election is founded upon Christ” and “Christ is a
Cause of Election” mean one and the same thing. It is therefore clear, even by
this reasoning, that they regard faith as a cause of election. Nicolaas does
not seem to deny this altogether, but only takes care that they not be thought
to establish Faith as the foundation, as the first cause, or as a cause which
moves by its own merit and dignity (pp. 105–106).

4. “The relation of order”—according to the Remonstrants’ position—“between
Election and Reprobation, as species mutually opposed to one another, seems
altogether to require that faith occupy in Election the same place which
unbelief occupies in Reprobation.” These are the words of Nicolaas
Grevinckhoven on page 176. But the Remonstrants not only affirm that unbelief
is the Cause of Reprobation—on this very page 107—but call and detest the
contrary position as horrible.

5. If Faith determines the will of God—otherwise indifferent—to elect one
rather than another, then it is a cause of election. The matter itself shows
that the Remonstrants attribute to faith this determining force. For since
nothing apart from faith precedes election which is not also common to the
non-elect, and since nothing common determines, the reason for the
determination must be placed in faith. Nicolaas appears to profess this on
page 206 when he affirms that free choice determines grace. Yet they make its
concurrence a condition for the efficacy of grace no more powerful than they
also establish faith to be for election.

6. To make indubitable what the Remonstrants attribute to Faith, we must
present the syllogism which they themselves formed to explain the order of the
divine counsel. It is as

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 68 | printed p. 56]

follows: “I will to save believers. Peter believes. Therefore I will to save
Peter.” Thus they portray God as deliberating and, as it were, reasoning. The
major premise is that general pronouncement which Arminius makes the second
decree (*Declaration*, p. 47). The minor premise is the foreknowledge upon
which he says singular election is founded in the same place. The conclusion
is singular election itself. Now who is so foreign to common sense and usage
that he does not understand that in deliberations of this kind the conclusion
depends upon the minor premise as upon a true cause?

7. The following can also be added to these arguments: the Jesuit Suárez
either confesses or testifies that “if someone says that only election to
glory, or the will to give glory, is predestination, and then maintains that
there is no prior election to glory before foreseen merits”—which is plainly
the Remonstrants’ position, except that they do not use the word “merit”—“then
he must consequently assert that there is a Cause of Predestination on the
part of man” (*On Predestination*, bk. 1, ch. 17, sec. 13). It is therefore too
clear to be denied any longer—whatever they have previously done for
caution—that for the Remonstrants Faith is the Cause of election.

3. They also rebuke the Brethren for setting grace and faith against each
other, or at least seeming to do so. But here they inflict the very wrong
which they just said was inflicted on them. For they take badly what is well
said. The Brethren did not oppose grace and faith, but election from sheer
grace, without any preceding and moving external cause, to election from
foreseen faith. Thus we have explained these general exceptions, such as they
are. Let us now proceed to the arguments.
