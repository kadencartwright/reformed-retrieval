SECTION FIVE.

JUNIUS. God begets children for himself by his own will, not by their quality—whether good, as in
the case of the elect angels, or evil, as in our case. He regards all in Christ, not in themselves,
so that Christ may be the firstborn among many brothers (Romans 8). Nature begets natural children
through parents without regard to their quality; and shall God not beget adopted children without
regard to them? Shall nature claim the whole for itself in begetting, and grace only a particle? God
forbid. Of the same kind is that proposition that the denial of happiness made to a human being
cannot be considered otherwise than as punishment. For, first, the denial of happiness is wrongly
treated here when the subject under discussion is the denial of adoption, which is the proper and
nearest terminus of election, as we said above. The former is not the proposition primarily and in
itself. Then, if adoption is the subject, the assertion is false. For punishment is not properly the
denial of that adoption. The denial is indeed prior to punishment, because it is also prior to sin;
but it is not therefore punishment. For who would say that an antecedent is identical with its
consequent, and a very remote consequent at that?

ARMINIUS. “But God,” you say, “begets children for himself by his will”—yet from sinful human
beings. “He regards them in Christ, not in themselves,” you say. Therefore he regards them as
sinners, I reply: not in themselves as having within themselves a cause why he should regard them,
but in themselves as needy, so that they may be considered in Christ as such a mediator. “God
begets adopted children without regard to them,” you say. I grant that he does so without regard to
anything in them by which God might be moved to beget them, but not without regard to that condition
of theirs by reason of which what they need is not generation but regeneration. Let grace claim the
whole for itself in generation; but it claims the whole for itself still more in regeneration. I
think that to say God begets children for himself from human beings, taking “generation” in any
sense other than regeneration, is *atheologon* and contrary to Scripture. We are speaking, however,
about adoption arising from the decree of predestination.

2. Let us come to the reason by which I had confirmed the premise of my syllogism. I said that in
that premise this denial of supernatural happiness cannot be considered otherwise than as
punishment. I said the denial of happiness, not of adoption. For here I am speaking about the
denial of glory, not the denial of grace. Non-adoption pertains to the latter, whether to it alone
or also to the former [the Latin prints *istam* for both references]. But I should like to be taught
how the denial of adoption to a human being created in God’s image does not have the character of
punishment and was not made on account of sin. You do indeed say that it is prior to punishment
because it is also prior to sin. I deny both. The burden of proof lies upon the one who affirms. Yet
although I am the one denying, I will give the reason for my denial, so that I may make clear the
goodness of my case. Whoever was created in God’s image is by the grace of creation a son of God
(Luke 3:38). I would add that he was, of course, God’s son not by being begotten but by being made,
as the marginal annotation in Beza’s Testament has it. What anyone possesses by the gift of
creation is not taken away from him by God’s justice unless the desert of sin precedes. Supernatural
happiness—whether it is given under the condition of obedience to the law or according to the
condition of the covenant of grace—must always be reckoned an inheritance. But it was promised to
Adam under the condition of obedience. Therefore Adam was then considered a son of God. Sonship,
then, cannot be denied to him except on account of sin and disobedience.

RESPONSE. “God begets children for himself by his will, but from sinners.” In these words Arminius
conceals his own position. For Arminius’s distinctive position is not that God adopts some sinful
human beings as his children and does not adopt others, which no one denies. Rather, it is that he
maintains that God is moved by the faith and repentance of the former to adopt them as his
children, and is moved by the unbelief and impenitence of the latter to refuse to adopt them. And
indeed he sometimes openly derives the cause of preterition or reprobation from human sins (although
at other times he even conceals this; sometimes he removes from them the character of a cause, and
sometimes he wishes to seem to rest in common rather than particular sins). Yet he does not proceed
with equal boldness to derive some cause of predestination from human beings. The present question,
however, concerns the causes of non-election or preterition. Junius denies that there is any cause
on the human side, because there is no cause of either adoption or non-adoption on the human side;
rather, the adoption of these and not those must be ascribed to God’s mere good pleasure. How much
more, then, must no cause on the human side be assigned to the decree to adopt or not to adopt? For
although there may be a cause of the decree’s execution, it by no means follows that there is also a
cause of the decree. Thus it does not follow that because human beings are sinners before adoption,
they either were sinners or were foreseen as sinners before God decreed to adopt them. This is
Arminius’s perpetual *asyllogisia*. So too when he argues: “He regards them in Christ; therefore he
regards them as sinners.” To “regard,” I think, is to bestow some favor for Christ’s sake, such as
the remission of sins and other things of that kind. But, I say, it does not follow that because sin
precedes this gracious bestowal, it therefore preceded the decree to bestow the favor, even in God’s
foreknowledge. Yet with what candor does Arminius deny that they have in themselves a cause why God
should regard them? Has he not professed that serious sorrow over sins is of such a kind that God is
moved “to,” and so forth? What then? Will that by which God is moved not have the character of a
moving or initiating cause (*prokatarktikē*)? They are certainly sinners before regeneration, but
it does not follow from this that they either were sinners or were foreseen as sinners before the
decree to regenerate them. Good works also precede the conferral of salvation; but beware of
inferring from this that the foreseeing of good works preceded the decree to save.

2. Grant that the denial of supernatural happiness is considered as punishment. It follows from
this that sin preceded that denial; but it by no means follows that the foreseeing of sin preceded
the decree to deny. Arminius asks for a reason why the denial of adoption to a human being created
in God’s image does not have the character of punishment. I give the reason: if non-adoption had the
character of punishment, then adoption would have the character of a reward. But adoption does not
have the character of a reward. For what obedience pleasing to God can be imagined in one who is
not a son of God for God to reward by adopting him as his son? Here is another reason: it is a
matter solely of God’s good pleasure to beget as his children those whom he wills and not to beget
others. I also undertake to confirm Junius’s reason. To adopt is to confer the grace of sonship and
sanctification. Not to adopt is to deny that same grace; and just as adoption precedes obedience,
so non-adoption, or the non-conferral of grace, precedes sin. But Arminius denies that the denial of
adoption is prior to sin and punishment. He wishes to appear to give a reason for his denial—a
thing that ought to be all the more welcome because it occurs more rarely in Arminius, whether we
look at his denials or his affirmations. Let us therefore see. It proceeds as follows: What anyone
possesses by the gift of creation is not taken away from him by God’s justice unless the desert of
sin precedes. But by the gift of creation the human being possessed sonship insofar as he was
created in God’s image. We deny the minor premise from Arminius’s own position, which he professed
just a few lines above. “We are speaking,” he says, “about adoption arising from the decree of
predestination.” By predestination, however, he means predestination to eternal life. But whatever
arises from creation does not for that reason arise from the decree of predestination. Yet the

denial of this adoption, proceeding of course from reprobation—for if adoption arises from the
decree of predestination, it is fitting that the denial of adoption should arise from the decree of
reprobation—always precedes both sins and punishment. Therefore, however Adam may have been
considered a son of God by the gift of creation, he was nevertheless not a son by that adoption
which Arminius had shortly before affirmed to arise from the decree of predestination and which he
professed to be discussing. Certainly the inheritance was promised under the condition of
obedience, not adoption—especially that adoption which Arminius discusses and which he professes to
flow from the decree of predestination. Moreover, even the inheritance was promised as something to
be obtained under the condition of obedience; it was not decreed from the foreseeing of obedience.
