VERSE 2. I said with my mind, Come now, let me test you with gladness; therefore
enjoy the good. But behold, that too is vanity.

Thus far we have seen why he was unwilling to place his highest good in the
investigation of wisdom and knowledge; let us see to what he withdrew after
departing from them. He transferred his mind—as he says—to the pursuit of
pleasure, and what else was this than, as the proverb says, to fall from smoke
into flame, that is, from bad to worse? Nor is this surprising, since, when a
man follows the judgment of human nature and the dictate of depraved reason,
it can scarcely fail that the more changes of plan are made, the more
disgracefully men fall. For if Solomon, guided only by worldly wisdom, with
which he was instructed almost to a miraculous degree, had chosen a thousand
kinds of life, each later one would have been worse than the one preceding it,
until he had been driven and hurled headlong to that which was most vile; nor
would he ever, under the guidance of his own reason and wisdom, have thought
even in a dream about true happiness and man's highest good. Thus we see men
driven headlong from excessive labor to complete idleness, from lavish
distribution of goods to excessive attention to property, and from idolatry
to the profanation of true religion—in short, from bad things to worse.
Therefore care must be taken, lest in pursuing happiness we trust in our own
moral wisdom or, indeed, anyone else's.

But the fact that he addresses his soul and commands it to enjoy the good—as
in Luke 12:19—shows with how much zeal and mental exertion he gave himself to
pleasure. For there are those who live in the midst of delights but
nevertheless have a mind absorbed in thoughts about sorrowful things.

He notes, however, that the outcome of this plan is the same as that of the
preceding ones, namely vanity, since it no more filled the mind with pleasure
and made it tranquil than did the study of wisdom and knowledge.

*Translator's note:* The edition prints `bonofrui` without an internal space
in the phrase `illam jubeat bonofrui`. The Latin transcription retains the
printed form; the translation construes it as `bono frui`, “to enjoy the
good.”
