### QUESTION XXI

#### In what year did Alexander die, and how many years does the epoch before us contain?

Answer. Alexander’s death is especially notable as a chronological landmark,
for many Greek, Latin, and Arab chronologists begin their eras from it, and it
marks the end of the Philippic years and of the empire of the Greeks,
particularly among the Egyptians. In Syria, Judaea, and Babylon, years were
indeed numbered from the beginning of the kingdom of the Seleucids, which
began twelve years after Alexander’s death, and the Asians established this
as the beginning of the kingdom of the Greeks. But the Macedonians, Egyptians,
and most Europeans used the Philippic years immediately from Alexander’s
death, when Philip Arrhidaeus held the title of his brother’s empire, although
others elsewhere held the actual power, Ptolemy son of Lagus being among the
first in Egypt.

Scaliger contends that this Philippic period beginning with Alexander’s death
takes its name

[Printed p. 195 | IA leaf 225 | access PDF 225 | OCR object 224]

from Philip Amyntas, Alexander’s father, not from his brother Philip
Arrhidaeus. Petavius rightly reproves him here, however, and the view,
criticized by Ussher and others, is abandoned by chronologists. For there is
no fitting reason why the name “Philippic” should be assigned to the Philip
who died thirteen years before those years began, and denied to the Philip
with whose nominal reign everyone acknowledges they began.

> Marginalia, printed p. 195: *Scaliger wrongly explains the Philippic period
> as deriving from Philip Amyntas*.

As for the year in which Alexander died and Philip Arrhidaeus and Ptolemy son
of Lagus began to reign, although Scripture is silent, Josephus, in Book I
*Against Apion*, reports with the great agreement of the learned that it was
Olympiad 114. There is as much agreement about this among later writers today
as there once was among the earlier ones. Jerome alone, on Daniel 9, makes it
Olympiad 113. Capellus considers this a foul blunder, but one made by the
copyists, not the author, and says that the scribal error should be corrected
by reading Olympiad 114 with the other authorities. Nevertheless, Henricus
Philippi retains the earlier and common reading, though he does not try to
reconcile the discrepancy. Until a better reconciliation is brought from
elsewhere, we offer this one.

> Marginalia, printed p. 195: *Great agreement among the authorities about the
> year of Alexander’s death*.

Each Olympiad comprised four years, a complete τετραετηρίς, and the first of
them began around the summer solstice, so the last also ended around the same
point. All agree that Alexander died in the first year of the Olympiad. The
first half of that year, however—as of every Olympic year—fell within one
Julian year, which began in January after the winter solstice, and the second
half within another. Thus, in this very passage Capellus distinguishes the
Egyptian year from the Julian, and says that Alexander died in Nabonassar’s
424th Egyptian year but the 423rd Julian year. In the same way, with better
and clearer right, we distinguish the Olympic year from the Julian. What
happened in the first Greek or Olympic year of Olympiad 114 could rightly
have happened in the last Julian year of Olympiad 113, which Jerome gives.

> Marginalia, printed p. 195: *Jerome reconciled with the others*.

Just as there is great agreement among the authorities about the Olympic year
of Alexander’s death, so there is about its year in the eras of Nabonassar and
Christ. Both Protestants and Roman Catholics place Alexander’s death 424
years after Nabonassar and 324 years before Christ. The authorities differ by
only one or two years in either era, a difference that is not hard to
reconcile. But there is much greater disagreement among them all in the era of
the world. Following Scaliger’s calculations, Calvin places the death in the
year of the world 3627; Ubbo Emmius follows him, and Isaacson differs by only
two years. But Capellus favors 3607; Ussher, 3641; Henricus Philippi, 3717;
Torniellus, 3729; Petavius, 3060; and Salianus, 3730. What Palaemon will be
able to settle such great disputes?

> Marginalia, printed p. 195: *Great disagreement about the era of the world*.

I acknowledge that the greatest, most useful, and most agreeable task in all
chronology—though also the most difficult—is to connect the eras with one
another and reconcile, as far and as fully as possible, the disagreements of
authorities about various matters in one or more eras proceeding from their
different starting points. This work and labor would surely be far more
fitting for the most learned chronologists than the besetting disease
perpetuated to this day by everyone in this science: multiplying and
magnifying controversies, to the shame already noted in my digression, a
shame they have all too richly deserved. This burden is wholly unequal to our
shoulders, nor is this a suitable place to attempt such a thing. Yet, in
keeping with the scantiness of our ability—which we recognize to be scant
enough—we have resolved to spend some labor upon this task, if the Lord allows
us to put the finishing touch to the first little book on the chronological
difficulties of the Old Testament. Before we arrive there, we think it
untimely to touch this sacred inner shrine of chronology and the deepest
mysteries of the art concerning the connection of eras.

> Marginalia, printed p. 195: *Connecting the eras is a task of great value*.

The other part of the question concerns the total length of this epoch. We
have spoken about the great agreement of the learned concerning the year of
Alexander’s death, in the first year of Olympiad 114. There is certainly just
as much agreement about Cyrus’s first year. Scaliger, Petavius, and many others
celebrate the statement of Africanus in Eusebius that it is certain to
everyone that Cyrus’s kingdom began in Olympiad 55. If it were clear that the
ancient writers spoke of the first year of this Olympiad and of Cyrus’s
kingdom, whether the Persian alone or the Babylonian, everything here would
be easy. But Africanus plainly seems to mean Cyrus’s Babylonian kingdom, when
he released the Jews from captivity, while most others understand the Persian
kingdom. Africanus’s words in Eusebius also leave it uncertain whether he
speaks of the first, second, third, or fourth year of that Olympiad. I looked
for the resolution of these difficulties in Diodorus Siculus, whom Africanus
cites there as his first witness before the others. But after turning
Diodorus’s books over and over, I finally discovered that the ones Africanus
refers to have perished—namely, all those after the fifth through the
eleventh. In any event,

> Marginalia, printed p. 195: *What is uncertain and what is certain about the
> era of Cyrus*.

[Printed p. 196 | IA leaf 226 | access PDF 226 | OCR object 225]

Scaliger, Petavius, and most others understand this kingdom of Cyrus as the
Persian, not the Babylonian, between which there is an interval of twenty
years, or five Olympiads. Given these points, which I observe to be considered
indisputable among chronologists, just as there are 236 years from the
beginning of Cyrus’s Persian kingdom—that is, from the first year of Olympiad
55—to Alexander’s death in the first year of Olympiad 114, so there are 216
years from the first year of his Babylonian kingdom—that is, from the first
year of Olympiad 60—to the first year of Olympiad 114.

This number of years is also demonstrated from the individual totals that the
most celebrated ancient writers assign to each Persian king. If from Cyrus’s
thirty you assign ten to his Babylonian monarchy; to Cambyses and the Magi,
eight; to Darius Hystaspes, thirty-six; to Xerxes, twenty-one; to Artaxerxes
Longimanus, forty-one; to Darius Nothus, nineteen; to Artaxerxes Mnemon,
forty-four; to Darius Ochus, twenty-five; to Arses, one; to Darius Codomannus,
six; and to Alexander in his monarchy, seven—the totals agree exactly with
the full sum of 236. We are not dealing here with Broughton, Beroaldus, Howe,
Nisbet, and a few others—otherwise learned men—who childishly and
without any cause seek to strip all credibility from all ancient history and
from the whole company of chronologists, Protestant and Roman Catholic alike.
Add to the preceding total of 236 the thirty years from the destruction of the
temple to the first year of Cyrus’s Persian kingdom, as Ubbo Emmius counts
them, and the whole epoch will be 266 years.

## Additions to Question XXI

### On the Hebrew letters used today: whether the Patriarchs and Prophets used them, or Ezra invented them

There are two very celebrated questions that may suitably be inserted between
Questions XX and XXI of Chapter 10, Book I. The first is whether in Ezra’s days
the Jews received from the Chaldeans all the forms of letters that have since
then been used as Hebrew down to the present day, leaving to the Samaritans
from that time the characters formerly used among the whole people of Israel,
in which God wrote the Decalogue on Mount Sinai and Moses and all the Prophets
wrote out their books. The other is whether the accents and vowel points of
the Hebrews were first added to the letters of the sacred books in the same
days of Ezra, or whether they were finally invented in the days of the
Masoretes, five hundred years after Christ’s birth.

On the former question there is a fairly sharp difference among the learned.
Joseph Scaliger, followed by Drusius, Grotius, Gerardus Vossius, and especially
Ludovicus Capellus and Brian Walton, maintain that the law and all the writings
of Moses and the Prophets were written in the characters used by the
Samaritans today. They pronounce the letters now in use, and used among the
Hebrews from Ezra’s days, to be innovations and Chaldean. But Johannes Picus
Mirandulanus, Postellus, Bibliander, Junius, Fullerus, Crinesius, and especially
the younger Johannes Buxtorf teach that the modern Hebrew characters are the
very ones in which God wrote the Decalogue and Moses and the Prophets wrote
all their works. They do not deny that the Israelites once used the Samaritan
letters, but say they used them as common and ordinary characters, while the
modern Hebrew characters were more sacred and more rarely used before Ezra’s
days. At that time the latter came into common use, while the characters that
had previously been common were left to the Samaritans alone.

> Marginalia, printed p. 196: *The state of the question*.

Scaliger handles this controversy with extremely great abuse of those who
disagree with him, whom he calls half-Jews, half-theologians, half-men, true
asses of the utmost madness and ignorance. The greatest intellects should not
be measured by the brief and infrequent intervals of human passion. Indeed,
among those asses and half-theologians Joseph Scaliger ranks some above all
the theologians who lived after the Apostles. I mean Franciscus Junius, who in
Book II, chapter 1 of *On the Word of God Against Bellarmine* calls those
doctrines about Samaritan and Hebrew letters “pure Milesian fables.” Although
we pass over these excessively harsh censures in Scaliger, we do not take them
so equably in Capellus and Walton. Certainly Capellus, though he exerted all
his strength against Buxtorf, brought forward so few and slight arguments that
he drove me, previously wavering, wholly into the opposite opinion. After
Capellus, Walton works this saw back and forth, seriously and forcefully, in
Prolegomenon 3, section 29 and following. I intend to follow him κατὰ πόδας,
step by step, as far as they considered necessary for the question.

> Marginalia, printed p. 196: *Scaliger’s rashness*.

The first argument he takes from Scaliger for his opinion is this. Ancient
coins struck in Solomon’s time bore inscriptions in Samaritan letters, not
modern Hebrew ones. Therefore, Samaritan letters, not the Hebrew letters of
today, were in use among the Israelites in Solomon’s time. Walton pronounces
this argument so full of light that no one could disagree unless he were
half-blind or closed his eyes. Answer. 1. Harsh and abusive words add no
strength to any argument.

> Marginalia, printed p. 196: *Walton’s arguments refuted. 1*.

[Printed p. 197 | IA leaf 227 | access PDF 227 | OCR object 226]

Second. It is acknowledged among the learned that there is a very great deal
of falsehood and fraud in excavated coins, statues, and other underground
monuments. Waserus professes to possess a half-shekel, a third of a shekel,
and a quarter-shekel struck with Samaritan letters; Morinus declares them all
counterfeit. Why should we not affirm the same about Scaliger’s whole shekel,
impressed with the same letters and the same figures of an urn and a rod? I
doubt whether there survives anywhere on earth today any artifact of wood,
stone, or metal that reaches the days of Solomon, much less those of the
Judges or Joshua. Certainly, when Sicardus brings forward his silver shekel,
with the temple engraved in modern Hebrew letters on one side and Solomon’s
name on the other, Walton rejects it on an argument that does not seem
sufficiently effective. He says the Jews were forbidden by the second
commandment to use any images, whereas Sicardus’s shekel has Solomon’s face
engraved on one side. If anyone replies that the model of the temple Walton
himself presents to us contains images of angels with complete and perfect
human figures, only with wings added, in a very great number perhaps exceeding
a hundred or a thousand, Walton anticipates the objection by granting the
fact but alleging a divine dispensation. Yet he ought to have proved that
dispensation. Certainly there seems to have been greater danger of idolatry in
so many figures of angels depicted in a sacred place and engraved everywhere
upon its very altars, tables, gates, and walls than in a single human face
impressed on profane coinage. Moreover, Walton alleges no privilege for his
Samaritan coins, all of which have images on both sides. The second commandment
certainly forbids images of anything on earth, in heaven, or in the waters as
expressly as it forbids images of parts of the human body. Walton rejects as
spurious various other shekels impressed in Samaritan as well as Hebrew
letters. He therefore ought not to be angry with others who think an argument
from coins struck three thousand years ago has little force.

> Marginalia, printed p. 197: *Little credence belongs to excavated coins*.

Third. I answer with Buxtorf that the ancient Jews had two scripts: one that
they used in ordinary writing, letters, and coins; another that they reserved
for more sacred and solemn things. The Decalogue was written in the latter by
God’s hand, as were some primary copies of the Law and the Prophets. In the
former, which is now peculiar to the Samaritans, were written most private
matters and the more ordinary public ones, as well as most coinage and the
ordinary copies of the sacred books that were worn by the hands of the common
people. Walton and Capellus bespatter this answer with many false insults, but
overturn it with no solid argument. It is certain, and they do not dare deny,
that the Egyptians had various scripts: some sacred, some hieroglyphic, and
some common. Even today, do not the ancient Greek characters in which the
London Alexandrian Bible is written, and Scaliger’s ancient Ionic alphabet,
differ as much from the form of Greek letters in common use today as modern
Hebrew letters differ from Samaritan ones? Is the difference not equally great
between modern Hebrew characters and rabbinic writing? One skilled in Hebrew
will read Samaritan much more easily than rabbinic. Moreover, Buxtorf brings
forward a great company of the most learned ancient and recent rabbis, who
acknowledge this twofold script among their forefathers. Walton and Capellus
have an easy answer for them all: all Jews are liars, and all invent things
that never happened. This is indeed a precise and accurate mode of answering,
one that will never fail anyone; but few sensible people will deign to use it.
More on this shortly.

> Marginalia, printed p. 197: *The Jews once had a twofold script*.

Walton’s second argument comes from the testimony of Eusebius in his
*Chronicles* and of Jerome in his prologue to the former Books of Kings,
Epistle 106, and his commentary on Ezekiel 9. Walton speaks magnificently of
these witnesses: “When I produce them,” he says, “I produce all the Fathers
who wrote anything on this matter.” Capellus judges it fair that the testimony
of these two be accepted as the testimony of the whole Jewish nation.

> Marginalia, printed p. 197: *Walton’s second argument, from the testimony of
> Eusebius*.

Answer. 1. Of the two, Eusebius is earlier and more important, for he wrote his
*Chronicles* before Jerome, who translated them into Latin and transferred
much from them into his various writings. But after repeatedly examining both
the Greek and Latin copies of Eusebius’s *Chronicles* published with great
care by Scaliger from the best manuscripts, I could not find one word that
supported Walton’s opinion. Nevertheless, I did not wish to question the
learned man’s reliability in his citation here—although in this passage he
very undeservedly questions the excellent Johannes Buxtorf’s reliability in
his citation of Postellus. At last, after much investigation, I suspect I have
found the πρῶτον ψεῦδος, the original error. I see that the cited words
expressly occur in two old Latin editions, both published at Basel; but these
editions are corrupt and were rightly corrected by Scaliger, who would not
have omitted the words if he had found them in any reputable Greek or Latin
manuscript, especially since they seemed to support the opinion he vigorously
defends.

[Printed p. 198 | IA leaf 228 | access PDF 228 | OCR object 227]

From these things it is plain that Eusebius, the first and primary witness of
Capellus and Walton, does not say even one word favoring their thesis. But let
us grant that Eusebius said what is read in the corrupt Latin version of some
old editions. Walton will still gain little from it. First, Eusebius himself
affirms nothing; he only says, “It is affirmed,” without saying by whom. What
he says is affirmed has two parts: first, that Ezra reconstructed the whole
Scripture of the Old Testament from memory; second, that he changed the Jewish
letters. The latter affirmation can have no greater authority than the former.
Yet the former, by Capellus’s own testimony, is a thoroughly false fable taken
from the apocryphal 4 Ezra.

As for Jerome, I acknowledge that his opinion is contrary to ours. But I say,
first, that the opinion of any one Father has little authority if he alone
holds it and supports it with no reason; here Jerome stands alone. As for Bede,
who follows him, both Capellus and Walton profess that he adds no weight to
Jerome. Jerome declares that Ezra discovered other letters that the Jews
afterward used, and that in the ancient letters of the Hebrews, which the
Samaritans use today, the final *thau* has the likeness of a cross. Jerome
proves what he declares certain here by neither reason nor authority. Walton
knows well enough that Scaliger, the principal patron of Walton’s opinion,
ascribes centuries of errors to Jerome and Eusebius in his notes on the
*Chronicle*. Here, however, both Walton and Capellus convict Jerome of two
errors. Neither holds that Ezra invented or discovered the letters the Hebrews
use today. Both profess instead that they are Chaldean letters discovered some
thousands of years before Ezra was born and as well known to the common Jews
in his days as the Chaldean language. Further, if we believe Scaliger, it is
utterly false that the final Samaritan letter has any likeness to a cross.
Jerome could rightly have affirmed this about the Ethiopian alphabet, as
appears from the typefaces of Oriental alphabets in Walton and others, but it
is very false of the Samaritan, as will appear below. Finally, Capellus admits
that once Eusebius is removed—or even if Eusebius remains—the sole foundation
of Jerome’s opinion is the testimony of the Jews. Yet Capellus affirms that
all credence in the Jews, as consummate liars, must be denied, especially in
the present argument.

> Marginalia, printed p. 198: *And of Jerome*.

Nevertheless, for the third foundation of his opinion Walton brings forward
from Capellus ancient and recent rabbis: Rabbi Chasda, Rabbi Jose, Rabbi Joseph
Albo, and others. Answer. Neither Walton nor Capellus brings forward any rabbi
except one Buxtorf placed in their hands, and Buxtorf gave solid answers to
their testimonies, which the two conceal or at least pass over untouched.

> Marginalia, printed p. 198: *The third argument, from the rabbis*.

As for Rabbi Chasda, the first of them, Buxtorf shows that he supports the
opposite opinion because he teaches that *mem* and *samekh* were written by
God as closed figures on the Mosaic tablets. In Samaritan writing, however,
neither *mem* nor *samekh* ever had a closed figure. Therefore, in this rabbi’s
opinion, the tablets of the Law could not have been written in Samaritan
letters. Why are Capellus and Walton both silent about this? Next, Buxtorf says
that the opinion ascribed to Chasda destroys itself and can be defended by no
one: namely, that the Assyrian script and the Aramaic language were given to
the Jews in Ezra’s days; that the Jews chose the Holy Language and the
Assyrian script; and that the Hebrew script and Aramaic language were left to
the Cutheans. No one, Buxtorf says, ever held that the Aramaic language was
left to the Cutheans, since they always wrote the Law in the very Hebrew
language itself. Walton answers that in Ezra’s days many Jews wrote their
Bibles not only in Chaldean letters but in the Chaldean language, and that
Ezra destroyed every copy. Reply. This answer has no bearing on the point, for
Buxtorf is speaking not of the Jews but the Samaritans, who possessed the Law
written not in the Chaldean but the Hebrew language. Moreover, Walton’s claim
about Bibles among the Jews in Ezra’s days written in the Chaldean language
is a mere conjecture without any foundation. Before the Chaldean paraphrases
that survive today, who ever dreamed of other Chaldean Bibles?

> Marginalia, printed p. 198: *Rabbi Chasda*.

As for Walton’s second rabbi, Rabbi Joshen, who in chapter 2 of the Babylonian
Talmud tractate *Sanhedrin* affirms that through Ezra the script of the Law was
changed into the Assyrian, which came up with them from Babel: Answer. Walton
knew Buxtorf’s objections to this testimony well enough, but did he attempt to
remove them? Buxtorf says that the passage of the Talmud contains a dispute
among the doctors of the Mishnah about the antiquity of Hebrew letters. First
it gives Rabbi Joshen’s opinion in favor of the novelty of the Hebrew letters,
as if Ezra had invented them, and the antiquity of the Samaritan letters, as
if the tablets of the Law had first been written in them. Rabbi Judah the
Holy, Rabbi Simeon, and Rabbi

> Marginalia, printed p. 198: *Rabbi Jose*.

[Printed p. 199 | IA leaf 229 | access PDF 229 | OCR object 228]

Eleazar are brought forward against him and answer Rabbi Josha’s arguments.
Those arguments, as Buxtorf recites them from the Talmud, are certainly utterly
vain. In any event, the Talmud rejects Rabbi Josha’s opinion and decides in
accordance with his adversaries because they were more numerous. The Talmud’s
rule and custom is to decide according to the majority of the doctors,
especially in this passage where Josha’s arguments are answered and rejected
while his adversaries’ arguments remain untouched. Finally, among Rabbi
Josha’s adversaries is Rabbi Judah, called κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν, “preeminently,” “the
Holy,” and “the Prince.” The Talmud is accustomed to decide according even to
his opinion alone. Here this one rabbi outweighs Josha and a thousand other
common rabbis.

From the more recent rabbis Walton takes from Capellus, who in turn takes from
Buxtorf, only two: Rabbi Joseph Albo and Rabbi Moses bar Nachman. Answer. As
for the former, I grant that his opinion is contrary to ours. But what weight
does that one man have against the multitude of his colleagues, especially
since Albo is plainly a falsifier here, citing various things from the Talmud
that never occurred in it? As for Nachmanides, Buxtorf demonstrates that he
wrote that the letters in which Moses wrote the Law had what the Hebrews call
strokes and crowns. Samaritan letters never had those crowns. According to
Nachmanides, therefore, the Law could not once have been written in Samaritan
letters.

Walton offers no further arguments for his opinion. He ascribes to us various
ones from Buxtorf, though without naming him. The first is the testimony of
both ancient and recent rabbis. Following Capellus’s example, he answers this
briefly: the Jews have no authority here when their decision concerns their
own interest and the glory of their nation. Reply. This answer is far too
short. Buxtorf answers distinctly and fully each ancient and recent rabbi
brought forward against his opinion. When he himself adduces a great company
of ancient rabbis possessing the highest authority among their people—
Tannaim, Gemarists, authors of the Tosafot, Geonim, and Kabbalists—Capellus
and Walton erase them all with one brief stroke. “The men,” they say, “are
consummate liars when the glory of their nation is at issue.” With this single
pot of whitewash they neatly cover over all Buxtorf’s long passages from the
rabbis, elegantly set down in Hebrew, well pointed and translated. Yet when
Buxtorf himself supplies one or two ancient or recent rabbis, they immediately
extol the words of these very few to the stars and wish one of them to be
preferred to a hundred others. The sole reason for this great paradox, which
Capellus repeats to the point of nausea and Walton also takes from him, does
not seem sound: namely, that it is the glory of the Jewish nation that Moses
wrote the Law in the modern Hebrew letters, which are Assyrian, but a reproach
and disgrace to the nation if the Law was written in Samaritan letters. What
is solid here on Capellus’s assumptions? For he teaches that no sanctity,
glory, or any honor at all is due to the language or letters in which God,
Moses, and the Prophets wrote sacred things, any more than to any language or
letters whatsoever in which the most profane nations wrote down their
abominable mysteries. Moreover, they maintain that the ancient Hebrew language
and letters belonged properly to the Canaanites, Philistines, Tyrians,
Sidonians, and their Punic colonies, whereas the Chaldean language and letters
belonged to the Assyrian monarchy and were Abraham’s mother tongue and
letters, which he always used until he learned Hebrew from the Canaanites.
What honor, then, came to the Jews from using the language of the Canaanites,
who were servants to the Assyrians, cursed by God, and devoted to destruction?
And what reproach could come to the Jews from using the language and letters
of the Assyrians, which properly belonged to the nation ruling the world and
were native to Abraham and the earlier Patriarchs?

> Marginalia, printed p. 199: *Buxtorf’s arguments confirmed against Walton’s
> objections. First*.

Walton takes most of the more recent rabbis for himself and leaves only a few
to us. Is this a new kind of arithmetic? Only one, Joseph Albo, stands for the
majority, while twelve whose testimonies Buxtorf adduces are merely a few in
comparison with that one. Capellus rightly warns that votes must be weighed,
not counted, so that one can stand for a thousand. Indeed, one Jew from whom
Jerome learned the Capellian opinion—though it cannot be proved that Jerome
had two Jewish teachers, or indeed that Jerome drew this opinion from one Jew
or any Jew—is supposed to establish the public and settled mind of the whole
Jewish nation, not merely the opinion of one or two teachers. What astonishing
φιλαυτία, self-regard, and confidence!

The second argument Walton ascribes to Buxtorf comes from ancient coins.
Answer. We dealt with this above. It is enough for us that Buxtorf’s coins
have no less a likeness of truth than Walton’s.

> Marginalia, printed p. 199: *2*.

The third comes from three ancient inscriptions. The first consists of the
letters inscribed on the Patriarchs’ tombs, which Benjamin of Tudela saw in a
cave near Hebron. The second was on the tombstone of a commander of King
Amaziah at Saguntum in Spain.

The third was on the gold plate the high priest wore upon his forehead, which
had the name Jehovah inscribed in Hebrew, not Samaritan, letters.

To the first he answers that Benjamin’s itinerary abounds in fables, as
L’Empereur demonstrates in his commentary on it, or else that if Benjamin saw
anything of the sort, Christians or more recent Jews invented it. Reply.
Benjamin is not so fabulous that his work contains no truths L’Empereur does
not contradict. 2. Nothing prevents those tombs and their inscriptions from
being as old as the times when the Jews began to build and adorn the tombs of
the Patriarchs and Prophets. Indeed, what prevents them from preceding Ezra’s
days?

To the second he answers that one is blinder than the blind if he does not see
the weakness of this argument. Reply. Villalpandus’s fables about Adoniram,
who came to Spain to collect tribute for Rehoboam son of Solomon, are indeed
ridiculous. But Walton approves Capellus’s conjecture about some Jewish
commander of King Amaziah who was buried there under a certain Moorish petty
king when the Moors ruled Valencia and many Jews fled there from the East to
establish a home. Why should this conjecture not please us just as well: that
one of those Jews, when migrating from his homeland to Spain, brought with him
as a notable monument the tombstone either of King Amaziah himself, which the
Hebrew inscription names, or of one of his commanders, and that it could have
endured so many years? It is certainly not probable that from the Moorish rule
in Valencia down to the days of Rabbi Jom Tobi the letters on a tombstone had
been so worn away by age that they could not be read. Indeed, Jews in Spain
and elsewhere use the language and letters of their country so familiarly
that nowhere are they accustomed to inscribe Hebrew words or letters on their
tombs.

To the third he answers that Jerome, in the letter to Fabianus—there is no such
letter of Jerome; he should have said to Fabiola—wrote no Hebrew letters but
only Latin letters, which officious editors changed on their own into Hebrew.
Reply. This censure is excessively bold and lacks all proof. Neither Marianus
nor Fronto Ducaeus observes anything of the kind in his notes. The Paris
editors used the best and oldest manuscripts. Walton should not have corrected
them except on the evidence of more numerous and older manuscripts that
lacked all the Hebrew words in this letter—and they are many. Buxtorf,
moreover, cites from the Talmud an authority much older than Jerome, Rabbi
Eliezer Barjose, who declares that he saw that plate in Rome with his own
eyes. Walton adds that the high priest’s tiara was inscribed after Ezra’s
days. This is improbable, for we read that nothing in the high priest’s
garments had been changed from the days of Moses, much less that a new gold
plate inscribed in novel letters was hung upon his forehead. Many sacred
vessels were indeed changed in Solomon’s days for the use of the new temple,
but we do not read that Aaron’s frontal plate was touched.

Walton ascribes to Buxtorf a fourth argument from the forms of three letters,
*yod*, *tau*, and *samekh*. In the ancient Hebrew alphabet, says Buxtorf,
*yod* was the smallest letter, but in the Samaritan alphabet it is the
largest. 2. In the true Samaritan alphabet, *tau* once had the likeness of a
cross, but in the modern Samaritan alphabet *tau* has no such form. 3. In the
Hebrew alphabet before Ezra, final *mem* was closed, and no final letter was
distinguished from its initial or medial form. Capellus answers the first by
attacking the person of Postellus, the originator of this argument, as a man
of accursed memory. Walton does not touch Postellus’s person, for whatever
delusions he suffered in his old age, no one denies that he was a man of
wonderful knowledge of Oriental languages and of the highest merit for his
rare diligence in this study. Instead Walton expressly addresses the learned
Seuchardus, Dillerus, and Crinesius, pronouncing their argument made of straw.
Nevertheless, one who looks deeply into it finds some iron and steel there.
For it proves that the Hebrew letters used exclusively by Christ and the
Apostles were the modern ones, not the Samaritan, among which *yod* was never
the smallest. Walton does not deny these points. He says those who bring such
arguments against him and his associates wrestle with phantoms. Let it
therefore remain fixed that Christ manifestly taught that the Law was written
in the modern Hebrew letters, among which *yod* is the smallest. Walton and
his associates still have to prove that there were once other letters in
which God wrote the Law in Moses’s time. Until they bring arguments harder
and less straw-like than those we have seen thus far, they must permit us to
believe on Christ’s authority that the Law was written in the modern Hebrew
script. Whether God wrote it in another script on Mount Sinai must await their
proof before we abandon our prior belief.

Scaliger grants the second part of Buxtorf’s argument, taken from the shape of
the final Samaritan letter *tau*, but Walton strongly denies it, affirming that
the Samaritan *tau* represents a cross exactly. Answer. Whether it is fair to
trust Walton’s words

more than our own eyes, let him judge. In the Samaritan alphabet Scaliger

the one Crinesius printed in elegant type from a manuscript parchment brought
from the East to Nuremberg, and even in the one printed at Rome in the Vatican

followed this distortion by Villalpandus.

The third part of the argument derives from the closed *mem* situated in the
middle of the word *Lemarbe* at Isaiah 9:7. The Prophet did not use the
Samaritan script in his writing, for it had no final and closed *mem* and
still has none. Yet this is how the Prophet wrote, both because it so stands
in all Hebrew copies and because many Christian and Jewish interpreters think
a mystery lies concealed in this closed letter. Walton answers: 1. These
mysteries are vain. 2. As Grotius rightly observed, it is an error of scribes
who wrote final *mem* in the middle of the word. Reply. We do not defend the
soundness of any mystery here. Nevertheless, in addition to a crowd of Jews,
very many Christians, not only ancient but recent—namely, Lyranus, Fabricius,
Capito, Galatinus, Mirandulanus, Oecolampadius, and Bibliander—are cited for a
mystery in that letter. But for our argument it is enough that a final *mem*
was written in that word. We do not ask whether this was from a mystery or
without one, nor does Capellus’s variant reading weaken the argument. For if
that reading of the closed *mem* was one of the ancient and true readings, the
argument holds, however many other readings there may be. I acknowledge that
Walton’s answer from Grotius correctly meets and overturns the argument if it
is true. But far be it from us to grant errors in the authentic text to either
Walton or Grotius unless we are constrained by a much greater necessity than
presses us here.

> Marginalia, printed p. 201: *The Samaritans do not have a closed mem*.

Walton ascribes another argument to Buxtorf: the closed *mem* and *samekh*
stood miraculously in the tablets of the Law, since they had no connection to
any continuous part of the tablets. Walton declares these to be rabbinic
fictions unworthy of an answer. Reply. This is not one of Buxtorf’s arguments,
but merely a convenient anticipation of the first and principal rabbinic
testimony that Walton himself brings against him. What Walton cites from
Rabbi Chasda demonstrates that this rabbi thought the tablets of the Law,
written by God’s hand, were in the modern Hebrew script, not the Samaritan.
Walton grants here that this was Chasda’s view but says it rested on a false
fiction. Buxtorf approved neither this false fiction nor any other foundation
of Chasda’s view; he merely cited Chasda’s testimony for his own opinion. Here
both Walton and Capellus freely grant it, though shortly before each had cited
Chasda as a principal witness for his contrary opinion.

> Marginalia, printed p. 201: *Whether mem and samekh stood miraculously in
> the tablets of the Law*.

For Buxtorf’s final argument, borrowed from the Jewish Fuller, namely the size
of the Jewish nation: it was easier for them to preserve their characters and
language than for the few Samaritans confined within a narrow corner. Following
Capellus, Walton answers that the question is not what could have happened but
what did happen; that the change was made not through any negligence of the
Jews but by Ezra’s command, so that he might separate the Jews from the
Samaritans and write God’s Law in the letters of the Assyrians, who then ruled
the world, thereby opening a door for the nations to know the sacred letters.
Reply. What happened is precisely what is chiefly denied, and the reason for
denying the event—namely, the change through Ezra—is the removal of its cause:
the monstrous negligence of the Jews in preserving their language and
letters, and their embrace of both the letters and the language of the
Chaldeans. In one breath here Walton seems both to affirm and deny that
negligence. Whatever vices the Jewish people were immersed in, they were
certainly not more negligent than the Cuthean Samaritans in preserving their
letters and language.

> Marginalia, printed p. 201: *Capellus rashly accuses the Jews of monstrous
> negligence*.

As for Ezra’s command, that is the very question at issue. Many other reasons
were more obvious for blocking the people’s path to the Samaritans than
casting away the ancient letters of the Prophets and leaving them to the
Samaritans. This did nothing to aid separation so long as the language
remained common, for language, not letters and written characters, is the
instrument of intercourse. What does it matter to us that we have the same
written characters as the Latins, Italians, Spaniards, Flemings, French, and
others, when we share no language with them? As for the letters of the
Assyrians, these did nothing to spread the Law of the Jews among the nations
under the Chaldean Empire so long as the Law was not written in the Assyrian
language.

Capellus ascribes yet another reason to Buxtorf: the very obstinate and

any mark of superstition. The Jews’ immense zeal in the days of Antiochus for

they regarded the errors Capellus alleges in them as an abomination—was far
removed from all superstition. Capellus accuses the Jews of such negligence

Judaea or anywhere else in Ezra’s days, not a single sacred codex remained
apart from the one Ezra himself possessed. This is a fiction for which I do
not know whether Capellus has any predecessors or followers.

> Marginalia, printed p. 202: *Buxtorf’s remaining arguments vindicated*.

Capellus too lightly dismisses what Buxtorf asserts about Ezra’s zeal for the
Hebrew language and letters themselves—when he punished with beatings and
reproaches children who spoke Ashdodite and Ammonite—as merely zeal against
the Jews’ marriages with neighboring peoples, not for their language and
letters. Ezra did not rebuke only those marriages; this effect of them also
gravely displeased him when he heard Jewish children speaking the language of
the Palestinians. This indicates his zeal not only against intermarriage but
also for the Jews’ language and letters, that they might be preserved
unchanged according to the ancient custom. Yet the whole dispute here is vain
if the hypotheses of Capellus and Walton are true. On their account, the
language and letters of Ashdod and of all the Palestinians were wholly the
same as those of the Hebrews, since the Hebrews had learned them from the
Canaanites.

Buxtorf brought forward one further argument to which neither Capellus nor
Walton replied: the simplicity of the Hebrew characters, whose strokes are
not so elaborate and composite as those of the Samaritans, as will appear to
anyone comparing the two alphabets. But the simpler letters are, the older
they are, and the more composite they are, the more they borrow from earlier
ones.

Scarcely anything seems to remain in this question except Buxtorf’s famous
distinction, repeatedly advanced by him and attacked with all their strength
by Walton and Capellus: the ancient Hebrews had two scripts. One was ordinary,
in which letters, all common matters, and the ordinary copies of the Bible
worn by the hands of the common people were written. The other was for more
private use, in which God wrote the tablets of the Law and the primary copies
of Kings and the Prophets in the sacred codex. In the same way the Egyptians,
Chaldeans, Greeks, Latins, and other peoples had one script for sacred,
solemn, and higher uses, distinguished from the more common and everyday
script. The former letters were called Hebrew from their common use by all
Hebrews; the latter, Assyrian, either because their more divine use caused
them to be considered holy and blessed, or because Ezra, their advocate,
brought them up from Assyria and the whole people received them for common
use, abandoning the ordinary Hebrew letters that had previously been in
common use. Walton and Capellus rise against this distinction in a great mass
of anger. “Who,” they say, “does not see that these are mere trifles and the
most vain fictions of vain men, of which Christian theologians ought to be
ashamed? I truly do not know what it is to talk nonsense if this is not it.
Away with trifles and ravings of this sort!” Reply. Speak more sparingly of
these men. These words are far harsher than any arguments brought against the
distinction. Indeed, even if the distinction were nothing, our case would lose
little thereby. But Buxtorf proves it from the Jews, not merely the recent but
the most ancient authors of both the Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmuds, whom
Walton himself, though in vain, tries to use to establish his opinion. Neither
Walton nor Capellus answers in detail any of the great abundance of their
testimonies produced by Buxtorf.

> Marginalia, printed p. 202: *Whether the Jews once had a twofold script*.

They first assert in general that all rabbis, ancient and modern, are utterly
vain men. Reply. We do not deny this in the dogmas of the Christian faith. Yet
in the meaning of Hebrew words and in customs—whether of language, letters,
or the various rites their forefathers once used—it is the common judgment of
the learned that they say many things correctly.

> Marginalia, printed p. 202: *Rabbis are useful in some matters*.

Second, they assert that the different scripts among the Egyptians and others
were intended to conceal from the people, under obscure and mystical letters,
the abominable mysteries of obscene idolaters. Nothing of the sort ever
existed among the Jews, all of whose sacred things God wished every individual
among the people to know. Reply. Walton does not prove that the concealment of
dreadful mysteries under the veil of letters unknown to the common people was
the sole, principal, or even any cause of the diversity of scripts among the
nations. Certainly the most dreadful idolatry of the Egyptians, Chaldeans,
and other Gentiles was clearly enough expressed by priests, poets, and
historians in the ordinary characters of their respective

nations. Nothing worse lay hidden under mystical letters than what was
publicly divulged about Apis, Isis, Dagon, Baal, Jupiter, Juno, Venus, Apollo,
Vulcan, and the other idols. God’s will and command that his Law be
communicated to all the people did nothing to prevent a diversity of scripts
in which the divine Law was written and read, both by the priests and by the

Finally, Capellus and Walton assert about the meaning of the word *Ashurith*
that it is supposedly never used adjectivally for “blessed,” but always
properly for the Chaldean language, and that no letters can be called blessed
because they are incapable of blessedness. Reply. 1. Grant that this word is
always used properly; this is no objection to us, for we do not reject the
opinion of the rabbis who call the letters in which God first wrote the Law
“Assyrian,” because Ezra, coming up from Assyria and Babylon, first brought
them into common use. 2. Those who are ancient writers in their languages
must be presumed especially knowledgeable about the meanings of words in each
language: among the Greeks, Eustathius, Hesychius, and Pollux, from whom
Stephanus and others compiled their dictionaries; among the Latins, Virgil,
Cicero, Quintilian, Priscian, Gellius, and the like. Similarly, the ancient
rabbis most probably know the meanings of words in the language of their
forefathers.

> Marginalia, printed p. 203: *What the word Ashurith signifies*.

Walton appeals here to dictionaries. Let us turn to Buxtorf’s Chaldean
dictionary, by far the finest to have appeared in the world thus far. From
very many testimonies of both ancient and recent rabbis, it tells us that
*Ashurith* is used adjectivally for “blessed.” Nor should it be disputed that
anything God employs for his special service may fittingly enough be called
blessed. Why are the Persians called blessed in Psalm 137? Is it not because
they dashed the heads of Babylonian infants against the stones? Why could not
those letters God alone used in writing his Law be called blessed because of
that prerogative above all other letters? I do not know what spirit drove
Capellus to deny not only every blessedness to the Hebrew letters and
language, but even the holiness all the learned are accustomed to ascribe to
them; indeed, contrary to what I take to be the common sense of mortals, to
strip them of even the slightest honor from their use by God and the Prophets.
Sacred letters are not so scrupulous: from the root of the word before us,
אשרי, they frequently call even trees, woods, and groves blessed.

We shall add no more here. As for the latter question concerning the age of
the Hebrew vowel points, we are not presently able to discuss it adequately,
nor is there much need. For anyone who has the inclination and leisure to
compare seriously and without prejudice Capellus’s *Mystery of the Points*—
and what Walton briefly derives from it in his prolegomenon on the points—with
the elder Buxtorf’s *Tiberias* and the younger’s dissertation on the points
will not, I think, incline very strongly toward Elias Levita’s novel and
entirely singular opinion that the Masoretes invented the points a full five
hundred years after Christ.
