VERSE 4. I explored with my mind, continuing drinking in my flesh; and I
explored this while guiding my mind in wisdom itself, embracing folly, until
we should see whether this is the good for the sons of men to do under the
heavens during the number of the days of their life.

VERSE 5. I made very great works; I built houses for myself and planted
vineyards for myself.

VERSE 6. I made gardens and orchards for myself, in which I planted every kind
of fruit-bearing tree.

VERSE 7. I made pools of water for myself, to irrigate with them a grove
abounding in trees.

VERSE 8. I acquired male and female servants, and I had servants born in my
house; I also possessed many herds and flocks, above all who were before me at
Jerusalem.

VERSE 9. I also amassed silver and gold for myself, and the treasure of kings
and provinces; I acquired male and female singers for myself, and finally the
delights of the sons of man—music, and indeed of every kind.

VERSE 10. Thus I became great and increased these things more than anyone who
was before me at Jerusalem; my wisdom also remained with me.

VERSE 11. And since I had not withheld from my eyes anything they desired, nor
restrained my mind from any gladness, but my mind rejoiced in all my labor,
and this had fallen to me as my portion from all my labor.

VERSE 12. I looked back upon all my works which my hands had made, and upon the
labor I had laboriously performed; but behold, all these things are vanity and
affliction of spirit, so that there is no advantage under the Sun.

VERSE 4. In this verse the principal result of Solomon is considered: the
embracing—or rather the continuation—of folly, that is, carnal pleasure. He
calls it folly because he eventually discovered by experience that it was
such, although at first sight a certain image of happiness appeared in it. An
adjunct of this pursuit was that he determined to retain true wisdom, that is,
the worship and fear of God, and neither to fall away from God through the
allurements and enticements of pleasure nor to depart from the guidance of
reason and wisdom, but to devote himself to pleasure in such a way that he
would keep the pledge he had given to God. Nor is there any doubt that while
he pursued bodily pleasures, he went to the temple at the appointed times,
offered sacrifices, heard the Word, poured out prayers, participated in the
sacraments, governed the Commonwealth, and, as customary, dispensed justice
while sitting at the tribunal. For it never enters the mind of a man
thoroughly imbued with piety, such as Solomon was, to renounce piety all
together and at once; neither does he resolve in his mind that he is going to
perpetrate some great crime or practice enmity with God. Through this
combination of pleasure with wisdom, however, he could seem to have entered a
highly plausible road to happiness. For pleasure softened the torments and
pains arising from the study of wisdom, while wisdom, as though a bridle had
been applied, restrained the madness into which the use of pleasure was
driving him. From this it is clear toward what end the use of delights and
honorable pleasures is granted to men: namely, lest they be overwhelmed by
the mass and burden of the labors they undergo, and so that the harshness of
their labors may be softened for them. Yet how rare is the person who sets
this purpose before himself in taking respite from studies and labors? Indeed,
how many are there who give themselves wholly to pleasure and spend their time
and life in it as though it were the occupation of their life? What of the
fact that men illuminated only by the light of nature, not yet covered with
crimes or prostituted to wickedness, would bear minds recoiling from this
thought of falling away from God, if it were suddenly proposed to them that
they should do this? What, then, is that in which Solomon sinned, since he
resolved within himself that the fear of God would be joined with pleasure?
The error is this: he thought that he could at the same time

be wise and foolish, religious and pleasure-loving, and serve both piety and
pleasure. For just as no one can serve God and Mammon, Matthew 6:24, so neither
can anyone serve God and Bacchus, nor indeed two masters at all. What further
deceived him was that he did not consider within himself that, as pleasures
flowed into his mind, piety and the fear of God gradually flowed out. For
although men who apply their minds to pleasure continue to show the same
regularity in the outward worship of God as they were formerly accustomed to
show, the inward fervor of the mind—which God chiefly requires—is gradually
and step by step relaxed, until at last even that outward mask of divine
worship is stripped away. This accords with the saying of the Savior in
Matthew 13:12: from him who does not have, even what he has will be taken
away. It is wholly beyond controversy that the beginning of Solomon's
defection arose from this: that by giving himself wholly to pleasures, he grew
cold in the worship of God.

Next he descends to particulars. First, under pleasure in general he puts
forward wine, by which, through synecdoche, he designates all delicacies in
food and drink, just as by the word *bread* every kind of food and drink
necessary for nourishment is comprehended. He declares that he exceeded
moderation in these things, since he was constant in them and entertained
himself with them daily. Not that he spent his days and nights feasting, which
is something beastly; nor was he accustomed to stuff himself here with
delicacies so that excessive satiety rendered him unfit and unsuitable for
his duties. Rather, he did what the banqueter in Luke is recorded to have
done: he lived in delights daily, Luke 16:19. How great the power in these
delights is to enervate a man's mind is amply declared, first, by that saying
of Solomon in Proverbs 20:1, “Wine is a mocker,” and then by the example of
Isaac, a man of the most incorrupt life. Because Isaac was too devoted to
game, he held Esau, the provider of game, dearer than Jacob, that excellent
man, to the point that he wished to transfer the right of inheritance to Esau
against God's opposition, Genesis 27:6. It is therefore not without cause that
delicacies are called “deceptive or lying food” in Solomon, Proverbs 23:3.
From this evil, as from a seed, flowed prodigious lust—for these things, as the
Apostle testifies in Romans 13:13, are joined to one another by the closest
bond—by which it came about that Solomon took seven hundred wives and three
hundred concubines for himself, 1 Kings 11. Against this constant and daily
pursuit of feasting, set the perpetual feast of a good conscience, Proverbs
15:15. Just as it is fitting to shrink from the Epicureans, so too it is
fitting to shrink from that ancient severity practiced by the first monks,
who ate herbs, bread, and salt and for whom it was a religious scruple to take
any pleasure in food. Thus History testifies that a certain man used to eat
while running for that very reason, lest he experience pleasure in eating.

Next Solomon's plan must be noted, and the end he had in view when he applied
his mind to pleasure. It was the same end he had when he devoted himself to
the study of wisdom: that he might explore and ferret out what is man's
highest good in this life, in which the human mind may find its greatest rest.
He therefore did not become so brutish as to inquire what is man's highest
good in heaven or in the future life, for he knew very well that man's highest
good consists in the full enjoyment of the divine presence. Therefore, if
someone certain and secure about eternal life in heaven were given the choice
of entering upon that manner of living which he determined in his own mind to
be the most direct route to the greatest tranquility of soul, then, if he
wished to judge without corruption and employ sound judgment in this
deliberation, he would put all other things whatsoever after the pursuit of
piety and the fear of God. From this it is finally clear that Solomon did not
pour himself into pleasures so as to determine to remain and dwell in them,
unless he should attain in them the tranquility of mind he was seeking. Since,
therefore, he escaped these snares of pleasure with such pain

and difficulty, what will happen to those who are carried toward this alluring
mistress with the whole force and utmost exertion of their minds? But if they
say that they wish, after Solomon's example, to test what goodness this way of
life possesses, I answer that they would act only wickedly, since this matter
has been fully discovered by the experience of so great a man, and a lesson
has been issued by the Holy Spirit by which all mortals may learn thoroughly,
not without loss to Solomon. For they would act just as if they attempted to
carry upon their own shoulders that burden which Samson was unequal to
bearing. For just as the latter surpassed all mortals in bodily strength, so
Solomon surpassed them all in counsel of mind for accomplishing things; hence
it is utterly foolish to undertake what Solomon was unable to attain.
