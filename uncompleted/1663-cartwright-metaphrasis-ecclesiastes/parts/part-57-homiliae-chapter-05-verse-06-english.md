VERSE 6. Do not permit your mouth to bind your flesh with sin, and do not say
before that Angel that it was an error. Why should God himself grow angry
because of your voice and destroy the work of your hands?

He brings another argument by which he overthrows rash vows, from the
accompanying penalty of destroying oneself. For by the word “flesh” he
designates the whole person by synecdoche. Here he also marks the serious harm
caused by inconsiderate speech, or by the mouth through which speech is
uttered—a metonymy of the efficient cause. Although the mouth is only one
member of the body, and indeed among the smallest, it nevertheless casts the
whole person into certain danger of death. For just as the whole body is set in
order when the mouth is rightly governed—James 3—so when it is disordered and
too unrestrained, danger is created for the whole person, both soul and body.

Here the papist vows of chastity, as they call it, voluntary poverty, foolish
obedience, pilgrimages, and abstinence are condemned, since they entangle those
who vow them in various bonds of sin. Yet our own people are not free from
blame here either. While suffering danger at sea, in childbirth, or from some
serious illness, they promise many things which they soon forget when, rescued
from the danger, they live at ease. In this they act exactly like children, who
give something to their playmates with one word and take it away with another.

He explains another sin in rash vows: these people are accustomed to seek
hiding places and evasions by which they may free and extricate themselves
from performing their vows. He sets out one example, namely, a person's claim
that he did not know what vow he had made or what kind it was. He rebuts this
excuse from the accompanying

presence and knowledge of the Angel. Here the demonstrative article designates
not just any blessed angel, but the uncreated Angel and the Angel of the
Covenant: Christ, the true and eternal Jehovah, the searcher of the heart, who
sees through vain and futile excuses and duly punishes them. He is the one who
answered Sarah, when she denied her laughter out of fear, that she had laughed.
Genesis 18.

But here it is asked why he presented this Angel in particular as a warning and
a terror, since, because he is the Mediator between God and men, he is the most
favorable and kind to the human race among all the angels. I answer: just as he
infinitely surpasses the other angels in holiness, so he is least able of all
to tolerate the crimes of his people. An outstanding example of this is set
before us in Moses. When the people of Israel had provoked God against
themselves to the point of destruction by setting up the calf as an idol, and
when life had been granted to them through Moses' intervention and intercession,
Christ nevertheless—who had made the journey with them out of Egypt into the
wilderness—declared that he wished to send a created angel to lead them into
the land of Canaan, but that he himself was prevented from going with them,
because his presence would consume those stubborn people. See Exodus 33:1–3,
12, 15–16 and Exodus 23:20–21.

Elsewhere Solomon indicates the danger of misappropriating by any name or
under any description a thing devoted to God, when he teaches that the person
who sins in this respect is held entangled in a snare. Proverbs 20:25.

He confirms the same point about offering an error as a pretext before that
Angel, in order to escape performing a vow, from the Angel's effect: namely,
the destruction of everything the person undertakes or in which he is occupied
by his settled way of life. Here it is worth observing that God opposes their
avarice and utterly frustrates their effort to make a profit by withholding
what was vowed. For when they think to enrich themselves by withholding or
diminishing a thing devoted to God, the opposite happens to them: they suffer
not only the loss of what they try to divert from God, but at the same time the
loss of all the possessions they own. From this it may be understood how great
an evil it is not to pay vows, since the destruction both of the one who vows,
as is clear from the beginning of the verse, and of everything he possesses
follows from it.
