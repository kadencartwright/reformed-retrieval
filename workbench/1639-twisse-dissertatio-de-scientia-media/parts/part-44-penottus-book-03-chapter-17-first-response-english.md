RESPONSE.

I entirely agree with Penottus when he rejects the distinction concerning the existence of all
things from eternity—not in time but in eternity—not only as alien to truth itself but also as
alien to the doctrine of Aquinas. Yet the reasoning on which he relies does not please me as much.
The Dominicans do not doubt that when Thomas says creatures according to their existences are
present to GOD from eternity—although this is by no means Thomas's own manner of speaking—he speaks
of the being of actual existence which a thing possesses in itself when it is placed outside its
causes, and which is distinguished from future being. Yet it is distinguished not only from future
being, but from present and past being in time just as much as from future being. They maintain that
each thing is present to God from eternity according to the real being which it will possess in
time, though not that it is present to GOD in time from eternity—for time is not eternal—but in
eternity. They also maintain that time itself according to real being is present to GOD from
eternity, though not in time but in eternity. Since eternity both coexists with every part of time
and is wholly simultaneous, they think it necessary that whatever at some point—say, in time—
coexists with eternity has coexisted with it from eternity, since eternity is wholly simultaneous.

Again, I am wholly displeased that Penottus derives the cause of certainty from one's attaining a
thing's present being rather than merely attaining its future being. What then? Does an astronomer
know less certainly from the causes that an eclipse will occur at a definite time than someone who
beholds the sun or moon undergoing eclipse at that same time? Perhaps you will say that it is easier
for the astronomer to err in discerning the causes of a future eclipse than for the sense to err in
seeing the eclipse itself; or that even if he does not err about the causes, those causes might be
impeded. But I say that the supreme cause cannot be impeded in any way, nor can God err even in the
smallest degree in discerning the causes of things. The divine intuition must not be conformed to
human intuition, as though God's intuition were acted upon by an extrinsic object in the way our
sight is.

Moreover, with whatever certainty someone sees that something will be future at a definite time,
with precisely that certainty he must know it to be present as soon as it is present. I greatly fear
that Penottus imposes upon both you and others through his phraseology. For example, he says: “One
who attains only the future being of a thing in its causes cannot have certain knowledge of it,” as
though for GOD to know that a thing's being is future were not as much to behold the thing itself as
to know that its being is present. Yet it is commonly said that God knows possible things by the
knowledge of simple intelligence,

[Book I, printed p. 86; BSB canvas 00112]

but knows future, present, and past things by the knowledge of vision.
