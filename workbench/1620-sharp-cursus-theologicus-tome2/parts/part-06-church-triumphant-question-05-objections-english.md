## OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. Job 42:8: *Go to my servant Job*—behold the command; *and he will
pray for you*—behold the example; *and I will receive*—behold the promise.
Therefore the invocation of the saints has a command and a promise in
Scripture.

Response. The inference is denied, because Job was then alive, and God commands
his friends to approach him while he is alive, because he was performing the
office of a priest through sacrifice and prayers. For similar statements, see
Genesis 14:19 and 29:7.

1. Furthermore, Bellarmine himself concedes in chapter 19 that Job, while
alive, then prayed for the living. Therefore the dead are to be invoked—this
inference is denied.

Instance. Although the saints are absent in place, they are nevertheless not
absent with respect to hearing: neither are the angels, as in Psalm 138:1 and
1 Corinthians 4:9, nor the departed, Revelation 11:9 and 19:14. Therefore.

Response. Psalm 138 does not concern angels, but gods or kings of the earth,
as verse 4 teaches.

2. First Corinthians 4 speaks of the presence of angels as ministers of the
Church. But this does not follow: “Those angels who minister to the Church see
and hear what takes place in the Church; therefore any angels and saints who
are absent in place hear us.”

3. Revelation 11 and 19 are a vision and allegory, not an event.

Furthermore, nothing is said there about hearing prayers. Therefore the
antecedent is denied.

Objection 2. From Genesis 48:16: Abraham, Isaac, and so forth, are to be
invoked. But they are dead. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, nor does the cited passage prove it,
because Jacob wishes only that in them there be called

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 23 | printed col. 46 | bsb10175568]

his own name and the name of his fathers, that is, that Joseph’s sons be
adopted as sons, or named from him and his ancestors as his other sons were,
and that they succeed with them to possession of the land of Canaan, so that
they constitute two tribes. A similar statement is made in Isaiah 4:1: *Seven
women will take hold of one man, saying, Let your name be invoked over us.*
Therefore the seven women invoked the man—this inference is denied, for it is
a fallacy arising from the form of expression. Furthermore, we do not read
that the Israelites invoked Jacob. Finally, as the Papists maintain, Jacob was
in Limbo with Abraham until Christ’s ascension. Therefore they did not see
God, nor did they hear the prayers of those making supplication, because, as
Bellarmine maintains, they did not see in God the things done on earth.

Instance. Jacob invoked the Angel when he said, *The Angel who rescued me from
all evils, may he bless these boys.* Therefore he invoked angels.

Response. The Angel whom he invoked is Christ, as in Genesis 31:11; Exodus
14:19; and Malachi 3:1, who is called יהוה in Exodus 13:21 and 14:24.

Objection 3. From Job 5:1: The one whom Job is commanded to invoke is to be
invoked. But he is commanded to invoke the angels. Therefore.

Response 1. I distinguish the proposition: “The one whom he is commanded by
God to invoke is to be invoked” is true. But God is not speaking there;
Eliphaz is. Therefore Bellarmine admits that those words do not establish the
point.

2. The assumption is denied, because those words are spoken as a question.

Furthermore, by “saints” he means the godly living, to whom he opposes the
ungodly in verse 2.

Instance. Elsewhere he means angels by “saints,” as in Job 4:18; 15:15; 19:21;
and 33:23. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because wherever the word

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 24 | printed col. 47 | bsb10175568]

“saints” occurs, holy angels are not always meant. Furthermore, in Job 19 he
addresses living friends, and in chapter 33 he speaks of people in this life.
Nor does this follow: “In chapter 15 he calls angels saints; therefore all
whom he calls saints are angels.” The same applies to Job 4:18.

Objection 4. From Exodus 32:13: *Remember Abraham, Isaac*, and so forth.
Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because that remembrance is neither
invocation nor a cause for invocation, for the passage speaks of the covenant
made with the Fathers. Neither Moses nor the Fathers, but God, was invoked.
Thus Bellarmine concedes that, because before Christ’s coming the saints did
not enter heaven or see God, and could not ordinarily know the prayers of
those making supplication, it was therefore not customary in the Old
Testament to say, *Saint Abraham, pray for me.* Instead, the godly only prayed
and pleaded the merits of the dead, so that their own prayers might be helped
by them. Therefore, even by Bellarmine’s testimony, there was no invocation
of the dead in the Old Testament.

Objection 5. Psalm 132:1, 10; 2 Chronicles 6:16; and 1 Kings 4: Solomon prays
to God through the merits of his father David, now dead. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied.

2. In Psalm 132 Solomon is not praying, but David, when about to bring the ark
to Jerusalem, as is clear from the circumstances of the text.

3. Second Chronicles 6 and 1 Kings 15 do not speak of merits, but of the
covenant.

Objection 6. From Daniel 3:35: Daniel’s companions invoke God’s mercy on
account of Abraham, and so forth. Therefore the prayers of the saints are
helped through the merits of dead saints, and consequently they are to be
invoked.

Response. The inference is denied. Furthermore, the cited passage is
apocryphal, from the addition to the third chapter of Daniel.

Furthermore, they speak of the covenant, not

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 24 | printed col. 48 | bsb10175568]

of merits.

Objection 7. Exodus 6; 34:7; Jeremiah 32:18: God is said to show mercy to
thousands; that is, on account of the righteousness of the Fathers he often
has mercy on their children, which was to plead the merits of the righteous
of old. Therefore.

Response. This explanation of the passages is false, because the continuation
of mercy to descendants occurs not on account of the Fathers’ merits, but on
account of the covenant. Furthermore, these passages do not prove the
intercession under discussion.

Objection 8. From 1 Samuel 7:7; Job 42:8; Romans 15:30; Ephesians 6:18; 1
Thessalonians 5:25; 2 Thessalonians 3:1; Colossians 4:12; Hebrews 13:18; 1
Timothy 2:1; James 5:14: In both Testaments we often read that the living were
invoked by the living. Therefore it will also now be lawful to invoke the
saints reigning with Christ.

Response 1. The inference is denied, because there is a command that we pray
for one another, but not that we invoke the dead.

Furthermore, the cases of the living and the dead are not alike, because the
living hear us and know our needs, and so forth, but nothing of this kind is
established concerning the dead. Thus you cannot be certain whether they will
hear you. If, however, you pray doubtfully, you obtain nothing, James 1:6.
Indeed, the dead are said not to know the living, Isaiah 63:16.

3. This is a fallacy of equivocation in “invocation,” because in the
conclusion it signifies religious worship and adoration, but in the premise
the same thing as “we exhort,” “we ask,” and so forth. For they will not
concede that the living were invoked by Paul with religious adoration.
Finally, not even the Vulgate edition uses the word “invocation” in the cited
passages. Bellarmine’s argument is therefore inept.

Objection 9. This is the argument which Bellarmine says his adversaries were
never able to answer, book 1, chapter 19, *On the Blessedness of the Saints*.
If the saints reigning with Christ are not to be invoked by the living,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 25 | printed col. 49 | bsb10175568]

this is either because they are unwilling—but not so, since they are endowed
with greater love than before; or because they are unable—but not so, because
if they were able while they were pilgrims here, how much more now that they
are in the fatherland? Or it is because they do not know what we pray—but not
so, because from the same source from which the angels know of the conversion
of sinners, over which they rejoice, Luke 15:10, the saints know our prayers.
Or it is because injury is done to God and Christ if another is invoked—but
not so, because then it would not be lawful to invoke the living. But [none of
these alternatives holds]. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because all its members are defective.
First, no passage of Scripture teaches that the saints wish to be invoked by
us, but in several places it teaches that the saints refuse invocation, as in
Revelation 19:10 and 22:9; Acts 10:25; and elsewhere. Furthermore, the saints’
love is primarily toward God. Therefore they do not wish his honor to be
given to another.

Second, the second member is false, for the saints cannot hear us or wish to
be invoked by us, because there is no command, promise, or example, nor are
they everywhere so as to hear us. There is a command that we ask the living
to pray for us, yet the living are not invoked by us, as was stated in
Objection 8.

Third, the third member is false concerning knowledge in particular, nor does
this follow: “The angels know of the conversion of sinners; therefore the
departed hear our prayers.” For the angels are ministers of the Church by
office and see its condition, Hebrews 1:14, but the saints rest in heaven.

Fourth, the fourth member is false. Furthermore, we do not invoke living
saints with religious adoration, but with

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 25 | printed col. 50 | bsb10175568]

a brotherly request.

Objection 10. From Baruch 3:4 it is said, *Hear the prayer of the dead of
Israel.* Therefore the dead were invoking God for the living.

Response. First, the book is apocryphal. Second, the assumption is denied,
because it speaks not of those physically dead, but spiritually dead, as in
Ephesians 2:1; or it speaks of Israelites invoking God who, because of their
calamities, were like the dead, as the following words teach. It does not,
however, speak of the departed, as is clear from chapter 2, verse 17. Thus
Lyra interprets this passage.

Objection 11. Matthew 25:40: What we do to one of the least of Christ’s people,
we do to Christ. Therefore whatever honor we expend upon the saints we give
to Christ.

Response. “Therefore they are to be invoked”—this inference is denied,
because they are not honored by invocation, and thus they refuse invocation.
Furthermore, the passage concerns works of charity rendered to the living,
not the adoration of the dead.

Objection 12. If the living know future things, absent things, and secrets of
the heart, much more do those who reign in heaven, and therefore they too are
to be invoked. But the former is true, 2 Kings 5:26; 1 Samuel 9:10; Acts 5:4
and 14:5; and elsewhere. Therefore.

Response. First, the proposition is denied, because in the cited passages the
living knew by an extraordinary prophetic spirit. But in heaven not all are
prophets or endowed with such extraordinary gifts.

Objection 13. If, when about to ask something from a king, we employ
intercessors, much more should we do so when approaching God, because he is
more remote from human nature, superior to human beings, and so forth. But the
former is true. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 26 | printed col. 51 | bsb10175568]

the comparison is poorly fitted. Earthly kings either do not wish to hear,
neglect to hear, or cannot hear. But nothing of this kind is true of God, who
invites everyone to himself and is not like a man, Hosea 11:9. Furthermore,
our intercessor before God is Christ.

Objection 14. Many miracles have been performed at the invocation of the
saints. Therefore they are to be invoked.

Response. Miracles without the testimony of God’s Word are not to be believed,
Deuteronomy 13:1 and following, much less those performed contrary to God’s
Word. Second, these miracles which the Papists allege are those which were
foretold in 2 Thessalonians 2:9, namely the miracles of Antichrist, and lies.
Therefore.
