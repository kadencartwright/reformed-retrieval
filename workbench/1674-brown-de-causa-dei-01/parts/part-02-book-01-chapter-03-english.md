## CHAPTER III

### Does all moral goodness and evil in our actions consist in God's will and constitution?

This is a celebrated question, debated among learned and orthodox men and
beset on both sides by sufficiently arduous difficulties. Let us therefore
inquire briefly, and with the modesty that is fitting, what should be said
here, since the discussion of this question will contribute something (as
will appear later) toward explaining our principal controversy.

Everyone grants that some of our actions are just and good because God freely
commanded them, and others unjust and evil because he freely prohibited them;
namely, those which are commanded or prohibited by some positive precept of
God. For whatever he had commanded to be done by such a precept he could have
prohibited, had it seemed good to him; and whatever he had prohibited, he
could have commanded.

As for the precepts of the Decalogue, the question among the Scholastics is
whether God can dispense with them all. The Thomists deny that God can
dispense with any of them. The Scotists, however, affirm that by his power God
can dis—

[1674 logical printed p. 19 | IA leaf 37 | PDF p. 37]

—pense with the precepts of the second table. But, as our Dr. Strang notes in
*On the Will and Acts of God concerning Sin*, book 1, chapter 13, the dispute
on this question between those families of Scholastics is merely about a
name. For in Thomas's opinion, to *dispense* is for God, without changing the
meaning of the law, to exempt someone from its obligation and observance,
whether by permitting or commanding something to be done against the law. But
according to Scotus, to *dispense* is not to make it lawful, while the precept
stands, to act against the precept, but to revoke the precept or declare how
it ought to be understood. *For*, he says, *there is a twofold dispensation
from law: revocation, and declaration of the law.*

Hence Thomas, in replying to the spoiling of the Egyptians, the slaying of
Isaac, and similar examples, denies that there was any dispensation in those
actions, but only a change in the matter with which the law deals. Thus that
action of the Israelites was not theft, because the Egyptians' goods were owed
to them by the judgment of God as absolute Lord (as Strang adds), who gave the
Israelites a right to those goods. Consequently, in taking those goods the
Israelites took not another's property but their own. Nor did Abraham, when he
consented to slay his son, consent to homicide, because Isaac was due to be
killed by the command of God, who is Lord of life and death. For he himself,
Thomas says, inflicts the penalty of death on all people, just and unjust, for
the sin of the first parent; and if a person, by divine authority, is the
executor of that sentence, he will no more be a murderer than God is. Scotus,
however, affirms that in these examples God revoked and changed the law so far
as those particular deeds were concerned. Thus God caused those acts, while
remaining the same in all their circumstances and otherwise unlawful, to
become lawful solely by removing the prohibition.

Whatever verbal dispute may be raised here—whether there was some revocation
or change of the law with respect to these particular deeds, or only some
change in the matter—this is certain: those particular actions, while
remaining the same in all their physical circumstances, were lawful, though
they would otherwise have been unlawful; and God could have made the same or
similar actions lawful for others. That is, God could have commanded others
to take other people's goods, as he commanded the Israelites to take the
Egyptians' goods; others to slay their own sons, as he commanded Abraham; and
could have made it lawful for others to marry their own full sisters, as this
was lawful for Adam's sons. So too he could have permitted others, when a
brother or nearest kinsman died childless, to marry the deceased man's widow.
It is therefore plain that God did not prohibit or command these or those
things from any necessity of nature, even though they are said to belong to
the law of nature, but that all moral goodness and evil in them depends upon
God's free will. The same actions clothed with the same physical circumstances
are now good and now evil, according as it has seemed good to God to command
or prohibit them. Thus such actions are not good and just, or evil and unjust,
antecedently to God's will. Nor are they commanded because they are just and
prohibited because they are unjust; on the contrary, they are morally good
and just because they are commanded, and morally unjust and evil because they
are prohibited. All moral goodness and evil in these and similar actions
therefore depends upon God's free good pleasure. God could have established a
law by which anyone might take another's goods, children put their parents to
death, and similar things be done, no less than he permitted the former to the
Israelites and commanded Abraham to slay his only son. For God has absolute
dominion over the goods of all people no less than he had over the Egyptians'
goods, and he is Lord of the life and death of all—

[1674 logical printed p. 20 | IA leaf 38 | PDF p. 38]

—others no less than of Isaac's. He can by right inflict death on all people,
just and unjust, for the sin of the first parent, and anyone armed with God's
authority can be the executor of this sentence. It is therefore evident that
the reason from which Thomas infers that those acts were lawful can also hold
in other cases, and all the more confirms that the moral goodness and evil of
those acts which concern the sixth and eighth precepts—and the same must be
said of acts concerning the seventh—depend upon God's free good pleasure.

Nor does Cajetan remove this difficulty in his comments on Thomas, I–II,
question 100, article 8, when he replies to Scotus's first argument by saying
that the precepts of the Decalogue can be considered in two ways: *first*,
according to the order of justice which they contain, and thus they are
indispensable even by God; *second*, according to their application to some
individual act, and thus God can dispense with the precepts of the second
table. He further adds that *such a dispensation is not a relaxation of the
precept, or a declaration of it in this case or act, as Scotus argues, but
causes this act not to be homicide, though without this authority it would be
homicide.* For since a dispensation properly occurs, as some hold, when in a
case where the law truly has force and binds, the law's obligation is removed
from some particular person, so that he may do some particular thing which is
not lawful for others, it is evident that the dispensation causes this act,
which would otherwise be homicide, not to be homicide. Moreover, when the
question of *dispensation* is raised, the question is not whether God could
cause such a precept not to belong to the order of justice already
established. For that would be to ask whether he could abrogate it—that is,
abolish it simply and absolutely, so that it no longer bound anyone. Rather,
the question is whether, while the law stands and binds others, God could
exempt someone from the obligation of that law with respect to a particular
act forbidden or commanded by it, so that the particular act which would
otherwise be a transgression of the law is regarded as no transgression.

Nor does it help to say, as Cajetan further does there: *In the precept “You
shall not kill,” the order of justice is that you should not kill an innocent
person when the other required conditions are present. Thus it contains an
order of justice which God cannot deny, nor is there ever a dispensation in
that order. For it is impossible that this act—namely, deliberately killing an
innocent person by private authority, while defending neither oneself nor
another—should receive a dispensation. But when God is said to dispense, as
when he commanded Abraham to sacrifice Isaac, he did not relax or declare this
precept, “You shall not kill,” but caused that act of sacrifice not to be
homicide, because it was a killing commanded by the Supreme Power, though
without such authority it would have been homicide.* I reply: This does
nothing to the point. The reason is that if Abraham had sacrificed Isaac
without God's command, he would have killed an innocent person and violated
the order of justice, because he would deliberately have killed an innocent
person by private authority while defending neither himself nor another; and
if anyone else had attempted it, he too would have been guilty. Why, then, was
Abraham not guilty, except because divine authority commanded Abraham to do
what otherwise would have been lawful neither for him nor for anyone else?
And what is this but so to relax or declare the law that it loses its
obligation in this particular deed? How does this differ from a dispensation?
To say that the act of sacrifice was not homicide because it was a killing
commanded by the supreme power is to say nothing other than that the supreme
power can dispense and actually did dispense in that command. This is precisely
what we maintain.

Cajetan further says there that *when God commands an act to be done which
without his command would be homi*—

[1674 logical printed p. 21 | IA leaf 39 | PDF p. 39]

—*cide, adultery, or theft, he operates and commands not apart from or against
the precept, but as though above it, when he commands that an act ordered by a
higher power be performed. For all these precepts*, he says, *have this
general clause tacitly understood: namely, that these acts are to be done or
not done according to the natural measure; for in this way they contain an
indissoluble order of justice. Thus the meaning is that an act underlying
homicide, adultery, theft, and so forth is not to be committed according to
the natural rationale. This is the same as saying that one must not kill, and
so forth, because those underlying acts, when related to the natural rationale
and measure from which they deviate, assume the character of homicide, and so
forth. But it is altogether indispensable that those acts, when exercised
according to the natural rationale, not be homicides, adulteries, and so
forth, although it can happen that those acts, when exercised according to the
divine rationale, are not homicides, and so forth.* I reply: But one may ask,
what is it to exercise an act according to the natural rationale? And what is
it to exercise an act according to the divine rationale? Further, does this
distinction regard the act insofar as it is an act, or the act insofar as it
conforms or fails to conform to the law? If it is taken in the latter sense,
so that the meaning is that an act exercised according to the natural
rationale, not conforming to the law and without God's command, is contrary to
the law, while an act exercised according to the divine rationale—that is, at
God's command—is not contrary to the law although it would otherwise fail to
conform to it, who does not see how irrelevant this distinction is and how
little it accomplishes? For the question always returns whether that divine
rationale, according to or by which the act is done, is a dispensation from
the law. But if the proposed distinction regards the act insofar as it is an
act, so that an act exercised according to the divine rationale is an act
exercised by divine power, while an act exercised according to the human
rationale is one performed by human strength in the ordinary manner, then it
must be said that Isaac's sacrifice, the intercourse of Adam's children, the
taking of the Egyptians' goods, and similar things were not human but divine
actions, or at least acts exercised by divine power granted miraculously or
outside the ordinary course. Who does not see how absurd that is? Indeed, the
example Cajetan adduces to illustrate the matter proves that this is his
meaning: *For just as*, he says, *it cannot happen that a dead person rises by
natural power, so it cannot happen that a person kills lawfully according to
the natural rationale, although by divine power a dead person rises and
according to the divine rationale a person kills lawfully.*

From these things it is evident that, at least as regards these precepts—that
is, the precepts of the second table—human actions have all their moral goodness
and evil from God's free will, and are not commanded or prohibited because
they are good or evil in themselves; on the contrary, they are good or evil
because they are commanded or forbidden. And the argument by which Scotus
proves that the precepts of the second table do not belong to the law of
nature as necessary conclusions from the principles of nature sufficiently
establishes this. Namely, *Because it would follow that the divine will is
absolutely and necessarily determined concerning some objects of will other
than itself. The consequence holds*, he says, *because it would be determined
to these precepts as necessarily good and their opposites as evil, just as the
intellect is determined to understand necessarily true things.* Cajetan's
reply—namely, that *it is not unfitting*, as he puts it, *that God's will be
determined to the rectitude and crookedness of something*—does not satisfy.
For although God's will can be determined to the rectitude or crookedness of
certain propositions, such as those which Thomas Bradwardine calls prior
rational truths and which are not other than God himself—for example, *God is
eternal, immutable, and so forth; God ought to govern the creature, and the
creature ought to be subject to God*—it does not therefore follow that his will
is necessarily determined to all those things comprised in the second table
of the Decalogue. And although Scotus grants that God's will can in this way be
determined to the rectitude and crookedness of the things which are prohibited
or commanded in the first table—

[1674 logical printed p. 22 | IA leaf 40 | PDF p. 40]

—because they touch God himself more nearly, he would not grant this regarding
the precepts of the second table. For him, those precepts do not belong to the
law of nature either as principles or as necessary conclusions, but only as
very consonant with natural right.

Dr. Strang says in the place cited, pages 84, that this in which Thomas and
Scotus agree is undoubtedly most true: namely, that there are some precepts of
the Decalogue with which God cannot dispense, such as the first three precepts
of the first table, however dispensation is understood. That is, God can in no
way exempt a person from the obligation of those three precepts, whether by
abrogation, derogation, or interpretation of the law, by equity, or because of
a variation in the matter or in any circumstance. He adds that the same seems
to be said of at least some precepts of the second table, for example, the
prohibition of false witness. He also grants with Thomas that God changed the
matter in some precepts of the second table, and thus caused what otherwise
would have conflicted with the laws of nature to be done without transgressing
them. And because he thinks that this cannot be done in the first three
precepts of the first table, he grants that there are degrees in the law of
nature, so that some duties are more natural than others and some sins violate
natural principles more than others. It is therefore evident that at least
some natural duties depend upon God's free will, and are just because God
commands them, not, contrariwise, commanded because they are just. This is
confirmed by the reason which he himself adds, saying: *because those things
which depend upon God's free will (as “free” is commonly taken here) can also
be freely changed, so that good things can become evil and evil things good.*
It is therefore established that in the actions commanded or prohibited in
all the precepts of the Decalogue except those four, all moral goodness or
evil depends upon God's free good pleasure.

But when he says that those precepts are indispensable—that is, as he explains
on page 82, of such a kind that it would conflict with the divine nature to
absolve an intelligent creature from them or command their opposites—he must
explain the second precept of the Decalogue differently in its particular
application from the way it is commonly explained by the orthodox. For the
affirmative part of the second precept is said to be instituted worship, which
we know God freely instituted and changed, so that it is now different under
the New Testament from what it was formerly under the Old. Further, even as
to the negative part of the second precept, *It is questioned*, says the most
distinguished Voetius in *Selected Disputations*, part 1, the disputation *On
the Right and Justice of God*, page 350, *whether every negative element of it
can in some way be changed through a change of matter and precept. For example:
whether God could exhibit some likeness of himself, of whatever kind, or some
material sign through which, when painted or formed for the person to whom he
deigned to exhibit it, it would afterward be lawful for that person to worship
God. We do not see that either answer, if asserted, implies a contradiction;
and we infer the former both from extraordinary signs or images, such as the
dove in Matthew 3, and from ordinary ones, such as the cherubim and the ark of
the covenant with the mercy seat.*

Although our most celebrated Rutherford, both in the *Apologetic Exercises for
Divine Grace* and in the *Disputations on Divine Providence*, asserts and
proves that the divine will is the first rule of created justice, and thus
appears to differ from certain other orthodox men, the difference will not
appear so great when everything he grants is duly weighed. For he never said
that God's good pleasure and free decree is the first rule of all moral
rectitude without exception, whether that rectitude is created and in a simple
act of the creature, or uncreated and in God. Indeed, he restricted the
disputation—

[1674 logical printed p. 23 | IA leaf 41 | PDF p. 41]

—to that justice which relates to creatures. Therefore it does not follow from
his opinion that the dependence of a rational creature as such upon God—in the
class of morals and in relation to right reason—is not so necessary that its
opposite would be a contradiction in terms or imply a contradiction. For he
readily grants that it is immutably necessary that a rational creature depend
upon God and be bound to obedience if God commands anything, whether in
matters of positive, ceremonial, or natural right, as may be seen in *On
Providence*, page 320. He never said, nor dared to say (as he himself speaks
there), that God could freely decree whether it was or was not right for a
rational creature to be subject to God and obey him in all things. He says
only that it falls under God's free decree whether this simple act, or this
omission of an act, is obedience owed to God or not. He therefore does not
remove that natural and necessary right of God to subject his creature to
himself and bind it to obedience, which flows from and is founded upon God's
independence, primacy, perfection, supreme power, and other attributes.
Concerning this right of commanding in God and obeying in the creature, he
never raised a dispute with anyone, knowing well that a creature independent
of God the Creator is a contradiction in the adjective—namely, not a creature,
but another God. There is no question about obedience in general, but about
this or that obedience in particular: that is, about this or that particular
act, whether it is necessarily good or evil before every act of the divine
will. Even those who think differently grant this concerning at least some
acts: namely, that they are not necessarily and intrinsically good or evil
except consequently upon God's will commanding or prohibiting them.

And when it was objected that there would be no necessary obedience unless it
were granted that there are certain particular acts through and in which a
person is necessarily bound to manifest obedience—because a genus is in
reality nothing other than its species—he readily granted that there are also
certain necessarily required species: namely, *to love God, to fear the one
true God, and not to adore the devil*, the contraries of which he acknowledges
God could not command (page 344). And no one, I hope, can infer from this that
the same must be said of every species of obedience, as though all particular
acts of obedience were as necessary before every act of the divine will as
obedience in general is.

But if anyone asks for the reason for distinguishing these acts which are
necessary antecedently to God's free will from those whose goodness or evil
depends purely upon God's free constitution, Rutherford formerly indicated it
clearly enough when he raised the question only about *created rectitude*. And
now, again, in his book *On Providence* he explains the matter more plainly
when he says that complex moral rectitude, which arises from the connection of
the terms of the predicate with the subject, is in one case purely necessary,
when both terms are God or something belonging to God, as: *God is God; God is
infinitely good, wise*, and so forth; *God is one.* He says that this rectitude
flows from God's very nature. So also when one term is God or something
belonging to God and the other is a created being, as in the proposition, *It
is right for the creature to be subject to God*, here too, he says, moral
rectitude precedes God's free decree, whether the discussion concerns moral or
physical subjection. By saying this, he does not overthrow his assertion,
which concerned created rectitude, for this is not rectitude created in every
respect. But there is still another complex rectitude where both—

[1674 logical printed p. 24 | IA leaf 42 | PDF p. 42]

—the subject and the predicate are created beings which do not necessarily
cohere; and this rectitude, about which alone the question had been raised, he
says depends upon God's free will.

He explained all this in the words of the great Bradwardine, *On the Cause of
God*, book 1, chapter 21, page 233A and following, which are these: (*Here,
however, one can answer in every respect concerning justice or what is just as
was answered above concerning reason and what is rational; and just as
rational things were divided there, so just things can be divided here. For
some just things are naturally prior to the divine will and do not depend upon
it, such as that God exists, that God is eternal, and similar things; and these
just things and their justice can well move, and actually can move, God's will
to willing. Other just things are posterior, and some others are mixed.
Legal things and laws are similarly divided. Mixed things are perhaps such as
that the good be rewarded, the evil punished, and the like. And just as in
another genus there is some proper and first principle by which the rest are
regulated, so in the genus of legal and just things there is necessarily some
first law and justice which is the first rule and first principle from which
all other subsequent legal and just things originally derive and by which
they are directed in all things. This proper principle appears to be the
infallible truth of the divine essence, upon which, as upon an immovable
foundation, all judgments of justice rest. Hence from this principle the
whole law too is named truth. This first justice, however, is just through
itself, first, necessarily, and altogether absolutely, in no way depending
upon the divine will but naturally prior to it. From this first just thing many
other just things follow necessarily; and they are, as I think, all those
things which are necessarily and absolutely just, not because they are
commanded by God or any human being, and whose opposites are also necessarily
and absolutely unjust. Neither God nor man can dispense in the least with
these just or unjust things, any more than they can change what is absolutely
necessary or impossible. For these are in no way subject to the divine or
human will, just as neither are the antecedent nor the consequence from which
and through which they follow most justly from it. Such are that a creature,
if it exists, be subject to the Creator; that one benefited be bound to the
benefactor, at least when God is the benefactor; and that no one ought to sin.
These, however, are natural rights simply, immovably founded in nature itself
as it creates. From this first rule of all legal and just things many other
legal and just things follow, not necessarily as the former do, but freely and
contingently, because through a free and contingent medium, namely the divine
will; and these are all legal things instituted in time. A certain kind of
just thing follows created nature necessarily upon a supposition and
inseparably, such as that man is rational, that the whole is greater than its
part, and similar things. This is natural right simply of created nature, and
altogether necessary; neither God nor natural man can dispense with it while
such a created nature stands. Another kind follows created nature not
altogether necessarily but for the most part, which almost everyone everywhere
does by an instinct of nature rather than a human constitution. Such are that
one do to another what one wishes done to oneself, and similarly in the
negative; love and education of offspring; repayment of benefits; violent
repulsion of violence; keeping faith; and similar things. In this sense
authors and laws frequently and almost always speak of natural right, and with
this natural right a dispensation can be made for a cause. There is still
another kind of just thing derived from the first rule of justice not
necessarily but freely, which is natural in neither of those ways—namely, one
for which before it is established there is no stronger reason in favor than
against; and such is merely positive and legal right.*)

It is certain that whatever God commands or prohibits, he always commands
what is good and prohibits what is evil, and this by necessity of nature. For
he is essentially and by necessity of nature good, wise, and upright; and in
commanding or forbidding he does nothing except what—

[1674 logical printed p. 25 | IA leaf 43 | PDF p. 43]

—his infinite wisdom dictates to be good and right—indeed, best and most
equitable. Yet it does not follow from this that whatever he commands or
prohibits is in itself or by its nature just or unjust before every act of his
will, or that nothing specifically commanded or prohibited is just or unjust
from his free will. For to prohibit Adam from eating the fruit of the tree of
the knowledge of good and evil was best and most equitable, all relevant
considerations being taken into account. Yet no one will say that before his
decree God necessarily gave this precept, or that before every act of his will
it was just and good, and its contrary evil and unjust.

Our Rutherford readily grants that adorableness belongs necessarily and
immutably to God; and, given a rational creature, God can no more decree that
he is not to be worshiped by that rational creature than he can decree that
the creature does not depend upon its Creator. But this, as said above, is not
the created justice about which the question was raised.

When it was objected that from his opinion it would follow that there is no
atheism, Epicureanism, diabolism, magic, hatred and denial of God, blasphemy,
and so forth which God could not freely will to command and by that very fact
command as morally or spiritually good and pleasing to himself, since it was
performed from the obedience owed to the divine Majesty, he frankly professes
and promises on page 344 that he will renounce his opinion if such
consequences are proved to follow from it. He distinguishes between entitative
acts which underlie evil and the evil itself, or the act insofar as evil is
joined to it. No one denies that the act—which in its physical being is not sin
or evil itself, but the physical foundation of evil, and of which God is the
efficient cause as he is of all beings—is distinguished from the evil itself.
For the act is one thing, and the privation of the rectitude which ought to be
in the action another, although perhaps the lawlessness cannot really be
separated from that entitative act of hating God. Yet the entitative act,
physically considered in itself, is not evil. The subtle Bradwardine formerly
demonstrated this in *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 26, where, in order
to prove that the whole universe of things is good and nothing is evil in
itself, he says many things pertaining to this point. On page 255B, for
example, he says: *Furthermore, if anything were evil in itself, this would
appear especially true of a morally evil act. But every such act is some
motion, and therefore an act and a perfection of the movable or moved thing
insofar as it is such.* He proves and confirms this there with several
testimonies of philosophers. Further, on page 256A–B he says: *Furthermore, it
appears that no interior act is evil in itself, not even blasphemy or hatred
of God. This can be shown as it was shown concerning external acts, in the
movements even of the insane, and because every such act is a certain natural
perfection of such a natural power. Besides, understanding and willing are
different operations. Suppose, therefore, that God creates some nature which
can freely will and refuse, and perform the other works of the will, but does
not judge intellectually or rationally and merely apprehends as beasts do;
then such a nature, in blaspheming or hating God, would not sin, because it
would not act against judgment or the rule of reason. Such acts, therefore,
are not evil in themselves.* Thus far Bradwardine.

And although this reasoning makes it plain that the entitative acts underlying
that evil are not evil in themselves, but physically good and morally
indifferent, entitative acts which are not merely simple acts but include some
morality, goodness, or evil from their object—such as the acts of loving or
hating God—are not indifferent but good or evil, including both the physical
act and that act's conformi—

[1674 logical printed p. 26 | IA leaf 44 | PDF p. 44]

—ty with the law, or its lawlessness. The celebrated Rutherford never said
that the goodness or evil of these depends upon God's free decree, because
God does not decree that sin be sin, or that hatred of God, which includes
evil from its object, be sin. For such an act is essentially sin and evil,
which cannot be said of any pure act remaining in the purely physical line;
and whatever is essentially such is such before every decree of God. For the
coherence of essence and the thing possessing the essence does not depend upon
God's free decree. Man, related to his internal metaphysical parts—namely,
genus and difference—is such not freely but by necessity of nature and
essentially: that is, he is a rational animal, and for man not to be a
rational animal implies a contradiction. Consequently it does not arise from
God's decree that man, thus formally considered, is a rational animal, for it
does not arise from God's decree that the same is the same. Rutherford thinks
the same must be said of those acts which are intrinsically evil or
intrinsically good by reason of their object.

Dr. Strang says in the place cited, page 88, that in the act of loving God,
beyond the entity which is in the love, something of entity is added when it
is determined to the proper object; but in hatred of God, beyond the entity
which is in the hatred, no other entity is added in its determination to an
unlawful object. But one may ask: what or what kind of entity is added to
entitative love when it is determined to the proper object? Is it *some
physical entity*, or something in the purely physical line? Or is it only a
certain *moral entity*, that is, conformity with the law? If the former is
said, there will be two physical entities here. But how does that second
physical entity differ from the legal rectitude which ought to be present? If
the latter is said, what will follow from it? Those two entities can be
separated and considered differently. And when the same entitative act is
determined to an unlawful object, although the evil is not an entity but a
privation, that lawlessness may nevertheless be called something moral—namely,
evil—which accrues to the physical act, though it can quite improperly be
called an entity since it is only the privation of rectitude.

*Moral evil*, Dr. Strang further says, *can accrue to that which is physically
good; but nothing which is morally evil as such is physically good.* On what
grounds does he assert this? Nevertheless, that which, morally considered, is
evil can be good under another aspect, namely, insofar as it is considered
purely in the physical line. No one says that moral evil is metaphysically
good, but only that the action to which moral evil accrues by reason of the
object is metaphysically good insofar as it is considered as an entitative
action in the purely physical line.

It is true that God cannot command hatred of himself. Yet it in no way follows
from this that the entitative act of hatred cannot be distinguished from the
evil or deformity attached to it by reason of the prohibited object. It is
also true that God can neither command nor prohibit the entitative act of
hatred or love insofar as it is considered in the purely physical line without
determination to some prohibited or commanded object, because it is purely
indifferent to moral good or evil, all of which it has by reason of the
object. But it does not follow from this that God could not command that
entitative act which now underlies hatred of God to another creature—namely,
one distinct in species and nature from man and angel, as Rutherford speaks;
or that he could not also command those acts remaining in the physical line,
so that they tend toward the right and commanded object.

Rutherford indeed admits in *On Providence*, page 345, that the account of
atheism is different, and that God can command no creature to believe a
falsehood, that there are multiple gods, or that the devil is God, because,
he says, such propositions intrinsically involve a contradic—

[1674 logical printed p. 27 | IA leaf 45 | PDF p. 45]

—tion, and God cannot cause contradictories to be true at the same time—not
from impotence, but because he is the first truth. Elsewhere, on page 574, he
says that it is utterly false that God can command the creature to believe
that God does not exist, either freely or by necessity of nature, because what
is repugnant to God's approving will is repugnant for God to command. For God,
by necessity of nature, both exists and wills himself to exist, and cannot
fail to exist without an execrable contradiction. He explains this further on
pages 569–70 as follows: (*God cannot command us to believe that
contradictories are true at the same time, because for the same not to be the
same is resolved into this: the first and infinite same is not the same, just
as the negative principle in created things is resolved into the affirmative
one in the first Being, which is this: God exists; the first same is the same,
on account of that most delightful unity and harmony in God. It is unfitting
that God could will, and therefore could command, that people believe to be
true what is repugnant to the infinite first truth.*) He says, moreover, that
this is repugnant because the object of God's approving will is moral good;
what God approves and commands as good is good and true. But for us to believe
that *God does not exist*, and so forth, is intrinsically and by necessity of
nature impious, false, and insulting to the first truth, and intrinsically
opposed to it. Thus on page 572 he recognizes a certain fittingness consequent
upon God's nature and natural mode of acting. Accordingly, although acts of
believing that *God is not God* and the like do not conflict with God's nature
(for he judges the existence of such acts repugnant to God's nature to be
utterly incompatible with God's nature and most blessed existence), God
nevertheless cannot command such things with respect to the moral fittingness
of acting. Thus whatever he commands, by the very fact that he commands it,
is morally good, either from the act of willing and commanding or antecedently
to that act from fittingness, congruity, or the most excellent mode of acting.
On page 575 he says that this fittingness accompanies all God's attributes and
all his acts of understanding, willing, acting, commanding, prohibiting,
promising, and threatening, so that he does or commands nothing which, if done
or commanded, would have been unfittingly done or commanded.

Our Dr. Strang is displeased that the most distinguished Rutherford—whom he
often attacks without naming him, both in this chapter and elsewhere—said in
*Exercise* 2, chapter 3, argument 4, that all moral justice, whether revealed
by the law of nature, in writing, or in another way, is from God's wise, free
will because it does not flow from him by necessity of nature. But how does he
refute this? *These and similar things*, he says, *appear so absurd that to
recite them is to refute them.* This response is certainly brief. Has he
nothing further? *It was indeed free for God*, he says, *to promulgate the
written or external law. But the rationale of the law of nature, natural
right, or some internal rule of morals is far different.* What sane person
would deny that written and external law is in some way distinguished from the
law of nature? But because they differ in some way, do they differ in every
way? Or do they differ in this: that written and external law arose from God's
free will, whereas the law of nature flowed from God by necessity of nature—
that is, that God imposed it by some impulse of nature? This the learned man
ought to have stated and demonstrated.

Nor does he approve what Rutherford said shortly afterward in the same place:
*With respect to the divine will nothing is shameful—which is partly true,
namely, because it is from God's free constitution that something moral is
unjust. For he could have prescribed no rule for people, as he did for natural
agents, so that whether people and angels followed the guidance of reason or
did not follow it, they would not be guilty of sin, since he could have left
them free to follow or not follow that guidance. And since law is God's most
free statute, if he had established no such thing,*—

[1674 logical printed p. 28 | IA leaf 46 | PDF p. 46]

—*there would have been nothing shameful or sinful.* Let us now hear Strang's
arguments. *How absurd it is*, he says, *that God could have created people and
angels endowed with intellect and reason and yet destitute of every rule to
which they might conform their actions. Even brute animals are seen to possess
naturally implanted inclinations, and as it were rules, for discerning their
good. Could God have created intelligent creatures so that they understood
nothing beyond cattle?* I reply: Who are we, blind and foolish little people,
that we should dare to set limits to the Almighty? Can we comprehend those
infinite ways by which that wisest Author of all things could have manifested
his glory? Rutherford did not say that God could have created people and
angels endowed with reason and intellect yet destitute of every rule to which
they might conform their actions. Rather, he said that he could have created
them endowed with reason and intellect in such a way that whether they
followed the guidance of that reason or did not, they would not be guilty of
sin. The most distinguished Rutherford was speaking of this law whose
violation contracts guilt, not of some rule by which they might discern their
good. For the latter would imply saying that people or angels could be created
endowed with intellect and reason and yet understand nothing beyond cattle.
But there is no contradiction in saying that they could have been created
without a law constituting duty, punishment, and reward, just as there is now
no contradiction in the existence of brutes without law, though they follow
inclinations implanted toward their natural good.

*Whatever*, Strang further says, *God created in the beginning, he made good
and perfect in its kind; and it would be absurd for works immediately created
by God to have been left in a state that was not good or was imperfect. Are
reason and intelligence not natural to people and angels? Even the pagans
acknowledge that reason is the rule of human actions, that virtues conform to
the norm of reason, and that vices deviate from it.* I reply: No question about
creation is raised here, but about legislation, which is distinguished from
creation. It is most true that whatever God created was good and perfect in
its kind. But does it follow from this that God could have created them in no
other way and that they would not even then have been good and perfect? No one
denies that reason and intelligence are natural to angels and people, and that
reason is the rule of human actions; but what do these things contribute to
the point at issue? The question is not about the state in which Adam was
created, but the state in which he could have been created without a
contradiction in terms.

Nor do the following contribute more to the point when he says that *a person
is directed by right reason toward virtue*. For who denies this? But he asks
*whether God could have formed a person in the beginning without right
reason.* One may answer that God could not have formed a rational person
without right reason, because this would imply a contradiction, but it does
not imply a contradiction to say that God could have formed a person without
law. We acknowledge that the practical common notions, conscience, and
synderesis belong to this right reason, that they cannot be entirely
extinguished and obliterated even in corrupted reason, and that this is the
inborn law called the law of nature. For we do not deny that God resolved to
govern man by laws and actually prescribed this law of nature to him. But does
it follow from this that God could in no way have resolved otherwise?

Afterward he says that *all our theologians affirm that original righteousness
was natural to man before the fall, or an endowment most agreeable and due to
human nature.* And who denies this once this order of things was decreed? But
what does this establish against Rutherford? Will anyone say that God could
not have constituted another and different order of things, or that by natural
necessity he constituted and effected everything as it is now constituted and
effected? Therefore we can also say with Jansenius, whom he cites—

[1674 logical printed p. 29 | IA leaf 47 | PDF p. 47]

—that *the grace in which Adam was created was owed to him, not indeed by
reason of Adam, but by reason of congruity and agreement with God's wisdom and
goodness*, presupposing, namely, this constitution of things according to which
he willed all things to be disposed and governed. Will it follow from this
that by his absolute power God could not have constituted another order of
things, also fitting and congruent with his wisdom and goodness? Or that this
order has utterly exhausted all the treasures of divine wisdom and goodness?
I confess that whatever God does and determines to do is done in the best and
wisest way. Nevertheless, I acknowledge that God has infinite ways to declare
his infinite wisdom and goodness; and had it seemed good to him to institute
another order of things, his wisdom and goodness would also have been
manifested in that different order.

There are, besides, those who urge that God cannot command a rational creature
to believe that God is not God or to hate God, and therefore that God's free
will is not the first rule of all justice. They confirm the antecedent in this
way: it is impossible for God to create a rational creature of such a kind
that logical and rational principles—concerning modeling oneself upon the first
truth in the intellect and loving the first and highest object of love—are not
engraved and created together with it. Rutherford readily grants in *On
Providence*, page 570, that no created or creatable intellect can by God's
command be directed toward that abominable object, namely, *God does not
exist*, because the repugnance and impossibility in the object itself is
uncreated and infinite. Nor can a creatable intellect terminate so execrable
a lie and blasphemy. But he further added that he did not dare penetrate into
God's unrevealed secrets or determine which rational creatures, specifically
distinct from these, are creatable among the infinite treasures of
omnipotence, and which are not. He sees no contradiction in saying that God
can create a rational appetite specifically distinct from that which is in
people and angels, which by its own inclination would tend no more toward love
of God than toward not loving God. This is far different from saying that God
can create a rational creature of another species with a co-created and
engraved principle of hating God. For he says this conflicts with that
fittingness and congruity with which God acts externally among creatures. He
further explains this on page 571: if that intellect were created by God with
such an innate command and principle, it would be both rational and
non-rational, and hence an object simply incapable of being created. He
further adds that if such a rational creature, distinct in species and nature
from people and angels, were created with such a co-created principle, its
hatred of God would not be that hatred of God which is now prohibited as evil
intrinsic to the rational creature which now exists, nor perhaps would it be
sin. Rather, it would be a pure aversion or an innocent non-direction of such
a rational appetite toward God, like the sun's non-direction from rising to
setting, or a retrograde motion through ten degrees contrary to its ordinary
motion, which nevertheless is not a culpable motion in the sun.

Thomas Bradwardine said similar things formerly in *On the Cause of God*, book
1, chapter 26, page 256: *Furthermore*, he says, *understanding and willing are
different operations. Suppose, therefore, that God creates some nature which
can freely will and refuse, and perform the other works of the will, but does
not judge intellectually or rationally and merely apprehends as beasts do;
then such a nature, in blaspheming or hating God, would not sin, because it
would not act against judgment or the rule of the act.* Rutherford further
explains this on page 337—

[1674 logical printed p. 30 | IA leaf 48 | PDF p. 48]

—that if God created a creature endowed with a will whose will was averse from
God as the supreme object of love, and yet this creature, in place of
intellectual cognition, possessed only sensory cognition such as is in brute
animals, that creature, in hating God, would not sin. For the absence of
intellectual cognition would make that hatred of God an invincibly imperfect
act, since it would not be directed toward the supreme object of love; and yet
that act would not be against the law co-created with man, and therefore would
not be a sin, nor would such a creature be rational.

Passing over this skirmish, which was undertaken only to explain briefly the
meaning of the great Rutherford on this question and vindicate it from the
prejudices of certain men, let us proceed to determine the question itself so
far as our plan permits.

Strang indeed thinks, in the place cited, pages 90–91, that this is the simple
meaning of the question, once circumlocutions and every ambiguity have been
removed: namely, whether all things which are just and good depend upon God's
free decree, so that he can will all of them and can will their contraries or
opposites; or whether some things are by their own nature so just and good,
and that according to God's nature, that God cannot fail to will them or will
their contraries or opposites. But with the good leave of this learned man, I
would say that this question differs somewhat from ours. For the question does
not concern God's immanent, natural, necessary, immutable, and uncreated right
when it is asked whether God's free good pleasure is the first rule of created
moral goodness and evil, or of moral goodness and evil in human actions. It is
one thing to ask whether every right of God, from which and according to which
he commands or can command something to a rational creature, is mutable and
free, so that he could or could have decreed otherwise and given another or a
contrary precept, or whether it is simply necessary and immutable. It is
another thing to ask whether every human action is morally good because it
conforms to God's revealed will, and morally evil because it fails to conform.
It is one thing to ask about the origin and nature of God's own right; another
to ask about the nature and origin of the moral goodness and evil of human
actions. It is one thing to ask why God gives people these precepts and not
others, or commands such things and, with the relevant suppositions in place,
cannot command the contrary or fail to command the same. It is another to ask
why this action of a person or rational creature is morally good and
acceptable to God, while another or contrary action is evil and hateful to
God.

I do not deny that these questions are indeed related: whether God's will is
the supreme rule of justice, and whether all things that are just and good are
just and good because God wills them. And since Strang, in the place cited,
pages 89–90, did not deny that both could be answered affirmatively, he will
all the more confirm our opinion, which concerns only that justice which is
created and regards the duty owed by rational creatures. Yet we judged it
safer to limit the question in such a way that it was openly established that
we raise no dispute with anyone about that uncreated and immanent right of
God, but ask only whether all the moral goodness and evil of our actions is to
be referred to God's will commanding or prohibiting them.

Indeed, the definition of God's law given in the preceding chapter abundantly
confirms the affirmative. For it is from law that this is my duty and that is
not; that I am bound to do this and omit that. And the law which constitutes
this duty owed is a sign of God's—

[1674 logical printed p. 31 | IA leaf 49 | PDF p. 49]

—will. It is therefore from God's will that this action is morally good and
another morally evil, because he prohibits this one but commands that one.

The definition of sin as *lawlessness* also proves this. For if sin is a
transgression or violation of law, and law is, as shown above, a sign of the
divine will, sin proximately and properly expresses a relation to God's will
signified in the law. I do not mean his decreeing will, by which he decrees
what will actually exist and what will not, for no one has ever resisted this
will, as Paul testifies in Romans 9:19; and law is not properly a sign of this
will, because law as such says nothing determinate about the event. I mean the
will signified by the law, or the *will of sign*. Sin is opposed to this
approving and commanding will, removing, namely, the rectitude and moral
goodness—or conformity to the law—which ought to be present in our actions,
thoughts, words, inclinations, and faculties of the soul. Yet in saying this I
do not assert that the sinner cannot be said to sin against God himself, for I
know that Scripture testifies to this very thing in 1 Samuel 2:25, Psalm 51:6,
Daniel 9:8, and frequently elsewhere; and one who violates a law can well be
said to sin against the legislator himself.

As for the controversy whether and how human sins conflict with the divine
nature, it is not worth the effort to discuss it here. This is certain: sin
does not *privatively* remove God's nature, as though, once sin were posited,
God's nature were privatively removed. Nor can it be said to be opposed to
God *contrarily*, because this opposition is properly between accidents, or
between substances by reason of accidents, whereas our most simple God is
free from every accident. *Those who maintain*, says the most distinguished
Rutherford in *On Providence*, page 312, *that sin is contrarily opposed to
God or to his nature or holiness, in my poor judgment sin in no small degree
against God's most sacred imperturbability and blessedness. I believe this is
abundantly refuted by Eliphaz and by David in Job 22:2–4 and Psalm 16.* Nor is
sin opposed to God *relatively*, both because this opposition is only between
accidents and because, when sin as one of the correlatives is posited or
removed, we cannot say that God as the other correlative is posited and
removed. Nor is it opposed *contradictorily*, except in the way all created
beings are contradictorily opposed to God—that is, they are *not God*. Yet in
a broad sense sin can be said to be opposed to God's nature insofar as it is a
transgression of a law given by him and privatively removes that rectitude
which, according to God's will of sign, ought to be in us.

Since, therefore, as said above, the duty we owe is constituted through law,
which is a sign of God's will, and what is to be done and avoided is prescribed
to us, it is established that this law is the norm by which judgment about our
actions can be rendered. If it had not been given or constituted, there would
be no norm according to which we could judge whether this action was morally
good or evil. Indeed, it would be neither morally good nor morally evil, since
it would conform or fail to conform to no law. *As regards us*, says the most
distinguished Voetius in the place cited, pages 374–75, *and our knowledge, or
on our side and as regards the right of obedience, there is no right, nothing
just, and no form or rationale of justice or moral goodness before our
existence and the actual communication and revelation of right reason,
synderesis, and any divine law.* And shortly afterward: *The eternal law is
the remote or mediate rule of human acts; right reason is the proximate rule.*

When Dr. Strang says in the place cited, pages 89–90, *Just as whatever is true
is true because it conforms to the first truth, so whatever is good is good
because it conforms to the first goodness; and just as God's truth pertains
to his intellect, so goodness pertains to his will*, and confirms this with a
statement of Voetius in the disputation *On Right and Justice*, page 351F,
say—

[1674 logical printed p. 32 | IA leaf 50 | PDF p. 50]

—ing that *God's will is most upright, the source and rule of all goodness,
indeed the first goodness*, he sufficiently confirms what we maintain.

Although eternal law, as commonly understood, should improperly be called a
law, its definition given by Apollonius in *On the Law of God*, chapter 2,
pages 27–28, nevertheless confirms our opinion here. *The eternal law*, he
says, *is not that holiness, justice, and perfection of the divine essence
which is conceived in God as an attribute, but the idea of good resulting
from the act of the divine will by which he determined to prescribe these
rules of holiness and justice and bind rational creatures to observe them.
Consequently it is nothing other than the idea of the will of sign, insofar as
that will is God's eternal and immanent vital act and is abstracted from every
external sign by which it is revealed in time. By this will of sign God from
eternity decreed and determined the moral rectitude of those human acts which
would be pleasing and acceptable to God, constituting what would be man's duty
and what would not.* To explain this he adds: *Just as archetypal or exemplary
theology is not that knowledge by which God knows himself and which in reality
does not differ from God's essence—since this knowledge is neither expressed
nor to be expressed in ectypal theology—but is the idea of the doctrine to be
learned by us, predefined for us from eternity in the divine mind by the
choice of God's decreeing will; so too eternal law is that idea of God's will
by which he determined that the thing which he wills and approves by an
external word in time is good and acceptable to him, while what he forbids
and disapproves is evil and to be rejected, and that man is to be bound by
duty to that good thing, but by duty ought to avoid the evil thing.* Hence it
is evident that since this law, which is the idea of good and the source and
fountain of all divine laws enacted in time, is not that holiness, justice, or
perfection of the divine essence but something resulting from the divine will,
all the moral goodness in our actions, of which that eternal law is the remote
norm, has the divine will as its supreme rule.

The most distinguished Voetius, in *On the Right and Justice of God*, page
347, answers the problem, *Is divine natural right the same as eternal law?*
He says that eternal law can signify any practical rule of necessary and
eternal truth to which we are naturally bound, for example, that God is to be
worshiped, parents honored, and injury done to no one. He denies that eternal
law, understood in this way, is the same as divine natural right, for this
reason: *because there are many practical rules which are indeed of eternal
truth and hypothetically necessary, which God nevertheless could have willed
differently and prescribed differently to us, and which consequently are not
of divine natural right.* He also denies it when eternal law signifies,
second, the act of the divine mind by which God conceives and judges that
those rules are to be observed by a rational creature, together with the will
to bind it. He gives this reason: *Because the indifferent will to bind which
intervenes and precedes here, indeed which constitutes a good part of this
rule, shows that it is a free right so far as that part is concerned, not a
natural right absolutely necessary from the necessity of the divine nature.*
But since no practical rule, however much it may be of eternal truth, binds us
unless the divine will intervenes and constitutes the obligation, it is
established that all those rules, as regards us or insofar as they formally
bind us, are from the divine will, whether that will could have constituted
otherwise or not.

It is true that God does nothing except from the wisest counsel and wills
nothing except what he judges upright and good. For God's will ought not here
to be contrasted with his wisdom, but rather considered as it is determined
by God's wise intellect. Yet since the will alone is the effective and
operative faculty without which absolutely nothing would exist among
creatures, it can with the best reason be called the first rule of moral
goodness—

[1674 logical printed p. 33 | IA leaf 51 | PDF p. 51]

—in creatures. For suppose that God had not willed to prescribe a law to
people: would there be any sin? Would there be any moral goodness or evil? No
one will say so, since law is the norm of moral goodness and where there is no
law there is no transgression. If it is said that once an intellectual
creature is posited, some law of God to which it is bound to submit must
necessarily be posited, no one can therefore infer that this particular law
is necessarily posited, or that it does not arise from God's will that such a
law is posited.

Indeed, even if we granted that once a rational and intellectual creature is
posited, a law—and this specific law—is necessarily posited, it could
nevertheless well be said that this law depends upon God's free will, since it
arises from God's free will that such an intellectual creature is posited. And
God's will is the first necessarily free principle in God; if it were removed,
all other powers in God, since they are purely natural, would purely naturally
and necessarily do whatever they did, as Bradwardine shows in the corollary to
*On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 10. Nor can divine knowledge alone be
posited as the cause of all things, as Bradwardine proves in chapter 8, since
it is indifferent to doing and not doing; nor the absolute essence of God,
which, so far as it is concerned, as Bradwardine says in chapter 9, bears
itself equally and indifferently toward possibilities which are never made and
toward things to be made, toward remote and near futures, and toward moving
and resting things, whence all these things would always exist similarly and
equally. And it is certain that God freely does whatever he does externally.

Therefore, since it arises from God's free will that an intellectual creature
exists, it can consequently be said to arise from God's free will that it is
actually subjected to God and his laws. Bradwardine explains this as follows
in the place cited, book 1, chapter 21, page 231C: *Besides these*, he says,
*some rational truths appear to be mixed in a certain way from both kinds,
such as that this creature is subject to the Creator and that God is Lord of
this thing. For this is not necessarily and altogether absolutely rational,
because then it would naturally precede the divine will and would always be so
by absolute necessity. But it is conditionally rational, or rational upon a
supposition, namely, if that creature or thing exists. And this conditional is
simply and absolutely rational: if any creature exists, it ought to be subject
to the Creator and he ought to rule over it. This is naturally rational before
God's will so wills, and its reason or rationality does not depend upon that
will. Therefore this conditional, “If this creature exists, God is its Lord,”
is absolutely rational and naturally prior to God's willing it to be so. But
the existence of the condition—namely, that this creature exists—is caused by
and depends upon the divine will, and it is rational that this creature exists
because God wills it to exist, not the contrary. For there was no stronger
reason why he willed to make this creature and omit another possible creature
equal to it in every respect than the contrary. Therefore it appears that the
proposition “God is Lord of this thing” is rational in a certain mixed way, as
was said. For if this thing exists, that God is its Lord is simply rational
before the divine will; but that this thing exists is caused by and depends
upon that will, and therefore is rational after that will. For the second
member of these rational truths, however, it must further be noted that there
is one kind of obligating reason, another outweighing, and another congruent
and accompanying. Obliging reason is that which dictates that someone ought
and is bound to do or omit this or that, and whoever has such reason and does
not follow it sins. Such reason is not in God with respect to posterior
objects of will, but is frequently in us. Outweighing reason is that which
dictates that it would be better to do this than that, or better to do this
than omit it. Such reason neither moves nor concludes the divine will,*—

[1674 logical printed p. 34 | IA leaf 52 | PDF p. 52]

—*nor determines it to act, for God could make better things than he makes and
many good things which he does not make. Congruent and accompanying reason,
however, is that which dictates that it is fitting and good to do or omit this
or that. Speaking of reason in this way, God rationally wills all posterior
objects of will; and in this way a person rationally wills and chooses one of
two equal things, and so forth. But God cannot in every respect follow an
outweighing reason unless he made all possible things, and each of them
infinite, which includes a contradiction. Therefore in such things his will
suffices for him as reason, or at least the congruent and accompanying reason
described above.*

This will hold much more in those things which are to be numbered neither
among those prior nor mixed rational truths (such as *God exists; God is
omnipotent*, and so forth; and *God is Lord of this creature; this creature
ought to be subject to the Creator and obey him*, and similar things which do
not concern the present question), but are purely created, as very many things
commanded by God are, which depend upon God's sheer will, as is confessed.
They neither are God nor are predicated of God insofar as he is related to
another.

And as for other things which are thought to be so co-created with a rational
or intellectual creature that their obligation cannot be separated from the
nature of a rational creature, these too can well be said to depend upon God's
free will. For it arises from God's free will that there is such a creature
possessing such an essence, together with which such an obligation is
co-created, or cannot fail to be co-created. Nothing external to God could
determine his will. Nothing external could necessitate him to give such
metaphysical parts any more than to give the physical parts which he actually
gave. Rather, he freely decreed to give creatures being and existence, and
freely decreed that they should be of such kinds as he willed, or possess such
essences as he willed. Who will deny that he could have refrained from creating
such a creature as an angel, human, tree, or stone now is? Do all those
creatures therefore have the essence which they possess from God's free good
pleasure? Certainly God's freedom and absolute power over possible and
creatable creatures is no less than a potter's over clay or any craftsman's
over a work skillfully made by him, to which he gives whatever form or shape
he wills. The reader may see Rutherford proving this more fully in *On
Providence*, pages 565–68.

Further, our opinion is confirmed from the nature of obedience, which always
expresses a relation to the legislator's will. And what, I ask, is the moral
goodness of the actions of inferiors other than obedience rendered to
commands—that is, conformity required to be present in actions by reason of
the command? *One who obeys*, says Thomas in II–II, question 104, article 4,
*is moved by the command of the one whom he obeys, just as natural things are
moved by their movers.* And Cajetan, on article 2 of the same question, says
that the object of obedience and disobedience is command taken generally.
*And this*, he says, *is made manifest from the act of each. For the proper act
of obedience is to do or omit something in order to fulfill the superior's
will known to oneself; but the proper act of disobedience is to do or omit
something in order to act against the superior's will, however intimated or
believed.* It therefore arises from the superior's will that this action is
obedience and that one disobedience; therefore it arises from the same will of
the legislator that this action is morally good and that one morally evil.

Moreover, properly speaking, we cannot say that an act is commanded because
it conforms to God's law. On the contrary, we say that this act conforms to
the law or is morally—

[1674 logical printed p. 35 | IA leaf 53 | PDF p. 53]

—good because it is commanded, and that one fails to conform to the law or is
morally evil because it is prohibited. The reason is that it arises from the
command that this act is an act of obedience and from the prohibition that
the other is an act of disobedience. Hence it is evident that every act of
obedience as such is posterior to the command and results from it, not prior
to it, for it presupposes the command. Therefore all morality in our actions
arises from the will of the one commanding, for the formal principle of
obedience arises from the precept. And to do even what is called intrinsically
good because it is commanded is the purest and most religious obedience.

The celebrated Voetius freely grants whatever we maintain here in his
disputation *On the Right and Justice of God*. When responding to this
objection, “If the law and its promulgation are removed, there can be no
distinction between just and unjust,” he answers on page 376: *There is no
distinction between good and evil for a person, or as regards the cognition
of the person to whom an enacted law has not been revealed, but there is for
God. Where there is no right reason in a person—indeed, where there is no
person—there is no distinction between good and evil, indeed not even between
true and false.* But we are not asking whether there is some distinction
between good and evil, true and false, with God, but whether the fact that
such a distinction is found among people arises from anywhere other than the
legislator's will. Thus on page 380 he says: *The will of command is properly
said by theologians to regulate our will and our actions, and in relation to
us; and so it is in no way regulated. But it is therefore not repugnant that
the same will, compared with the will of good pleasure whose effect it is, be
regulated by it as a norm. Again, the will of good pleasure, or God's decrees,
are the rule of command and indeed of all God's external works. Yet it is not
repugnant that those volitions and decrees be regulated (understand this
proportionately) by God's first and most perfect essence, manifested and
described to us in God's word through various attributes—namely, primacy,
simplicity, immutability, eternity, and so forth—so that according to it and no
other God wills and decrees whatever he can will or decree about and
concerning creatures.* This last statement does not oppose me, for I judge
that we should abstract from every such question; and the preceding statements
confirm what I say.

Further, on pages 386–87 the most distinguished man speaks as follows: *The
cause and measure of all truth and of everything true, whether theoretical or
practical, as regards us or in relation to our knowledge, is God's natural or
supernatural revelation, so that this is true for me because God revealed it
thus to me, not because it is or was true for me and therefore he revealed it
thus and not otherwise. For the first revealing truth is the cause, principle,
and measure of all truth and cognition in man, not the contrary. Yet it does
not follow that the first root and principle of truth, considered absolutely
and in itself, is to be sought in divine revelation—that is, in natural light
or sacred Scripture—for it would follow from this that God, the supreme and
first truth, is measured by and depends upon some external effect and work of
his own. Therefore distinguish in God's revelation independence and priority
as regards us or things external to God, and dependence and posteriority (in
the order of reason, namely) as regards God's own nature and the perfection of
truth. Thus God cannot reveal anything other or otherwise than agrees with his
nature and perfection. In the same way, in the order of good, God prescribes
nothing by his law except what is good, for he can no more be the moral cause
of shameful evil than its physical cause. And nothing for us—note well—or as
regards us has the nature of goodness except because and insofar as God
prescribes it to us by some law of his as the sole measure of our moral
goodness or uprightness. God's law is indeed the first and last root and norm
of goodness as regards us or our obedience; for the will of the divine good
pleasure as such*—

[1674 logical printed p. 36 | IA leaf 54 | PDF p. 54]

—*is not a norm for us except mediately and consequently, insofar as it is
apprehended by us a posteriori and from a sign.* These things abundantly
support us; but we do not defend that other point which the most distinguished
man rejects.

There are some who maintain that the first rule of all goodness outside God
is God's own justice and holiness, or, as others put it, consonance with God's
essential purity and justice. They confirm this with the passages of Scripture
Leviticus 19:2, *Be holy, because I, Jehovah your God, am holy*, and Matthew
5:48, *Be perfect, as your Father who is in heaven is perfect*. From this they
infer that the holiness which God requires from us is not conformity with his
commands but with his nature, justice, and holiness, or with his will so that
we may at length be conformed to his nature.

But against this, *first*, it would follow that everything God commanded to be
done had so close an agreement with his essential purity, holiness, and nature
that he could not have failed to command it or have prohibited it in any case
without doing violence to his nature; and that everything he forbade so
conflicted with his essential purity and holiness that he could not have
failed to prohibit it or have commanded it in any way. But is this to be said
of the prohibition against eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and
evil? Or of all ceremonial and judicial precepts? *If anyone says* that the
discussion here concerns only moral precepts and morality in those actions,
*one may reply* that obedience is also demanded in ceremonial and positive
commands; and thus the conformity of the actions of the people to whom they
were given with those commands is morality. But let the matter be restricted
to moral commands: is this to be said of them all? How, then, did God himself
sometimes command certain things to be done which, apart from the command,
would have been atrocious sins and contrary to the moral law, as when he
commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son? But if it is *replied* that Abraham's
act of sacrificing had an agreement with God's essential purity and nature,
one may answer that not only is this sufficiently difficult to demonstrate,
but a reason must also be given why God immediately prohibited it. Does not
God's prohibition today of a deed commanded the day before yesterday
sufficiently indicate that the deed has no such connection in itself with his
essential purity, but is good or evil by reason of God's will, now commanding
and then prohibiting it? What is this but to say that not God's nature, but
rather his will, is the first rule of this goodness? This can also be
illustrated with other examples if necessary.

But *second*, when they say that the first rule of all moral goodness in human
actions is God's essential purity, justice, and holiness, do they mean that
people are bound to propose it as the immediate rule to which they ought to
conform all their actions? If they affirm this, then we shall have nothing to
do with God's written word or revealed will. But if they say that conformity
is required with God's essential purity and holiness as he willed it expressed
in his law, the former difficulty returns: namely, that it would follow from
this that God could not have prohibited, and still cannot prohibit, anything
which he expressed in his law as to be done, or command anything which he
forbade, unless he willed to injure his most sacred nature. It would then
follow that the proximate and principal rule for us of all moral goodness is
rather God's will expressed in the law than God's nature or essential
holiness. Further, would it not also follow from this that everything God has
expressed in his law—that is, either com—

[1674 logical printed p. 37 | IA leaf 55 | PDF p. 55]

—manded or prohibited—was good or evil in itself and simply before every act
of the divine will, and thus that absolutely nothing is good or evil because
God commands or prohibits it?

*Third*, do they believe that God's own justice and holiness, or his essential
purity and nature, is a rule of morals for us to which we ought to conform
ourselves? Was this not utterly impossible even for Adam himself in innocence?
Is it not impossible even for the angels now confirmed in grace, in whom he
puts folly, Job 4:18? Indeed, is this not utterly unlawful? If, as before, they
again *reply and say* that they understand only God's holiness and justice
insofar as he expressed it in his law, they must then say that God did not
express his holiness and purity in the laws as it is in itself, but as he
willed us to imitate it. And what is this but to say that we ought not to
imitate God's nature and essential holiness as it is in itself, but only God's
revealed will, which is our sole rule of morals?

Moreover, *fourth*, if from these two passages of Scripture, or perhaps as many
parallel passages, they think they establish that God's own nature, holiness,
and perfection is the first rule of the moral goodness of our actions, can we
not more probably establish from six hundred testimonies of Scripture that
God's will and good pleasure is that rule, since they clearly and distinctly
set before us for imitation God's will signified to us in laws?

*Fifth*, let us come to discuss the proposed passages. *First*, the words *Be
holy, because I am holy* propose God's holiness to us not so much as a norm as
a motive. For it does not say, Be holy in the way that I am holy, but be holy
*because* I am holy, כִּי קָדוֹשׁ אָנִי. It is read in the same way in
chapter 11:44–45, and in Peter, 1 Peter 1:16, it reads: Ἅγιοι γίνεσθε ὅτι
ἐγὼ ἅγιός εἰμι.

Further, the purpose of the Holy Spirit in those words is to move them by this
argument to render obedience to the enacted law, even though it is (at least
in some places) ceremonial. For in Leviticus 11:44–45 he was giving precepts
about beasts, birds, every creature that moves in the waters, and every
creature that crawls upon the earth, to distinguish between unclean and clean,
and between an animal which is eaten and an animal which is not eaten. See
verses 46–47. Therefore he addresses them thus in verse 43: *Do not make
yourselves abominable by any crawling thing that crawls, nor pollute yourselves
with them.* Verse 44: *For I am Jehovah your God, that you may sanctify
yourselves and be holy, because I am holy. Therefore do not pollute yourselves
by any crawling thing that crawls upon the earth.* Verse 45: *For I am Jehovah
who brings you up from the land of Egypt, that I may be your God, that you may
be holy because I am holy.* What, then, could be said more clearly?

Similarly, in Leviticus 19 by this argument he exhorts them to render the fear
owed to parents, to keep the Sabbaths (verse 3), and to preserve themselves
pure from idols (verse 4); and then in verse 5, by this same argument, he
presses certain things concerning sacrifices and the like throughout the rest
of the chapter. By this he teaches that the holiness which we ought to imitate
is not God's essential holiness, but his free good pleasure declared to us in
various precepts.

This is further evident from 1 Peter 1:16, where by a similar argument,
confirmed by this testimony from Leviticus, he exhorts to obedience, saying in
verse 14: *As obedient children, do not conform yourselves to the former lusts
which were in your ignorance.* Thus he—

[1674 logical printed p. 38 | IA leaf 56 | PDF p. 56]

—teaches us that our holiness consists in pure and spiritual obedience
rendered to the divine precepts.

So too Matthew 5:48 teaches wherein that perfection required from us consists:
namely, not in assimilating ourselves to God's essential perfection, which we
may not propose to ourselves for perfect imitation, but specifically in this:
that we love our enemies, bless those who curse us, do good to those who hate
us, and pray for those who are offended with us and persecute us, as may be
seen in verse 44. In this he wills us to be like our Father who is in heaven,
who causes his sun to rise upon the evil and the good, and sends rain upon the
just and unjust (verse 45). What, shall we say that God's essential perfection
consists in these things, and thus that God would not have been essentially
perfect if he had not caused his sun to rise upon the evil and good, or had
not sent rain upon the just and unjust? Who will say this? Was it not free for
him to do or not do this? Does he not have mercy upon whom he wills? Does he
not do all these things according to his free good pleasure? *This conclusion*,
says Aretius on the passage, *is related to that whole disputation in which
the Lord, in interpreting the Decalogue, showed his disciples that a fuller
justice is required than that of the scribes and Pharisees. I therefore
understand “to be perfect” as to be just in the way Christ prescribed. Further,
let the mark of likeness be understood soberly. For God is perfect by nature
and through himself; we are not, but receive perfection by his beneficence as
a gift. Then, God is perfectly perfect; we, imperfectly and in part. Thus by a
certain imitation we follow his perfection. In sum, he wills to kindle in us
zeal and emulation for pursuing integrity, so that by our life and morals, in
every interaction with anyone, we may adorn our profession.*

Gualter speaks excellently on the passage: *He is speaking*, he says, *of the
perfection of love, namely, that in it we may be sincere and free from every
disguise or simulation. Otherwise, if the discussion concerns God's
perfection, which consists in his essence, immutable condition, majesty,
wisdom, and other attributes, it would be not only impossible but also impious
and wicked to aspire to it. In this the first parents certainly sinned: not
content with the condition assigned to them by God, they willed to be
altogether equal to him. Therefore Christ by no means commands us here to do
what he so severely punished in them. Hence what is said here ought to be
accommodated to the present argument and extended no further: namely, that in
gratuitous liberality and unwearied zeal for doing good we imitate God's
example, and not only do good to the undeserving, but also undertake a contest
with the ingratitude of wicked people, that by our goodness we may overcome
it. Therefore in Luke he says, “Be merciful, therefore, as your Father is
merciful.”*

See also Calvin on the passage.

If it is therefore asked whether there is no goodness in actions antecedently
to a command, I reply that a distinction must be made between foundational
goodness and moral goodness. There is no moral goodness in actions before a
command is posited. Yet in certain actions there is foundational goodness,
which is a certain agreement or fittingness adhering to or inhering in such an
act by reason of the relation which a rational creature has to the Creator or
to other rational creatures. It is unfitting to nature for some brute animal
to destroy its offspring; yet it is not sin or moral evil, because it is not
prohibited. In the same way it is unfitting for a rational person to hate God
and devour other rational creatures at pleasure; yet, on the supposition that
no law prohibiting it were posited, it would not be moral evil and sin. That
founda—

[1674 logical printed p. 39 | IA leaf 57 | PDF p. 57 | ends before CAP. IV]

—tional goodness, although it does not flow from the precept but is
presupposed, nevertheless arises from God's free good pleasure as he
constitutes nature in such a way and so orders all things. Therefore, speaking
accurately, a distinction must be made here between this good pleasure of God
and the law or precept. For, at least in priority of nature, this will which
constitutes foundational goodness precedes, and the commanding will follows.
Thus, when this foundational goodness is actually created, it precedes the
actual legislation itself in priority of nature. Therefore, when God commands
or forbids something in certain moral matters (the rationale is different in
ceremonial and purely positive matters), that thing is antecedently good or
evil foundationally because of the aforesaid fittingness or unfittingness,
which flows from God's good pleasure constituting it so; but the action itself
then becomes morally good or evil when it is commanded or prohibited.
