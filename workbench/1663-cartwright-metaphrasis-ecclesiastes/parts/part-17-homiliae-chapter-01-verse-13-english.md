VERSE 13. Applying my mind to inquire and to explore wisdom concerning
everything that is done under the heavens (God gave this occupation as an
evil to the sons of men, that they might occupy themselves in it.)

At this point he embarks upon a new kind of argument. For the preceding
arguments proved universally that nowhere in the whole universe of things can
anything be found that renders a man blessed. Those that follow aim to deny
that blessedness lies in those things in which the greatest appearance and
likeness of happiness is evident. Among these, the knowledge of all things,
both divine and human, rightly holds first place. It promises happiness to its
possessor, not only by its own delight with which it pleases him, but also
because it generally has riches and honors joined to it as companions. And so,
since the scope of that wisdom is bounded by two things—the knowledge of
natural and moral matters—he deals first with the investigation of nature.

In searching the secrets of nature, to show that he had made very great
progress, he sets forth the diligence and industry that he employed along with
it, and he designates this when he says that he applied his mind to that
matter. For since he possessed a mind enlarged by wisdom like the sand of the
sea, and devoted all of it to acquiring this wisdom, it could not happen that
he failed to make great progress in this study. To this is added the result of
his diligence, namely investigation and exploration. By this repetition he
indicates a most exact inquiry into the matter, since the knowledge of natural
things is hidden and, as it were, submerged in the depths, so that it cannot
be drawn out of darkness except by the most keen-sighted inspection. With his
mind stretched out toward the investigation of these things, his eyes, ears,
hands, and the other members of his body stood ready in all things as
providers and servants. From the same readiness of mind it followed that he
also incurred great expenses in discovering these things, arranging for
foreign birds and other small animals to be brought and conveyed to him from
distant regions. The result of all these things is set forth: he described the
nature of all trees, from the cedar to the hyssop, and likewise of beasts,
birds, reptiles, and fish.

Here he uses a prolepsis, whose objection is based upon curiosity as the
efficient cause, which drove him to pursue these secrets of nature. Therefore,
it ought not to seem remarkable if he found nothing among them in which his
mind might rest. The reply is based upon the true efficient cause, namely
God's calling, by which he was driven to undertake this work, so that no one
may doubt that in studying these things he attained as much as the nature of
the things themselves and the condition of human frailty permit. From this we
learn that the mind must be directed and applied to the things we seek

so that we may make progress in them: the eye, ear, and hand can do nothing
greatly worthy of praise where the mind is absent or turned elsewhere. Above
all, however, the mind must be applied to acquiring heavenly wisdom. Hence the
word of Solomon, Proverbs 23:26: Give me your heart. For if Solomon, endowed
with such incredible wisdom, did not acquire this knowledge of earthly things
unless his mind was thoroughly attentive and intent upon it, how much more
will we little men have labored in vain in comprehending heavenly things
unless we bring to them a mind that is kindled and aroused?

Next, nothing is to be attempted, nor is labor to be devoted to anything,
unless God's calling carries the torch before us in it. Moreover, since
prayers are to be offered for everything we undertake, and since it is impious
to summon God's blessing by prayers in things that God has not permitted, it
follows that our occupations must be determined by God's will. Finally, we see
that the study of searching and thoroughly investigating the nature of
creatures has been enjoined and commanded upon everyone, since they are books
in which we may read God's power and wisdom. To each of them, though they lack
words, God has in a certain manner given a mouth with which they proclaim the
praise of God their Creator. From this it is evident how undutiful toward God
we are in this respect, and how deaf we are while the creatures continually
address us and resound with God's praise. How very few will be found who have
devoted any effort to investigating the nature of things, or who know even the
names of herbs, unless perhaps they serve to fill the belly; the same can be
said of beasts, birds, and fish. Hence also it comes about that we are so mute
in proclaiming God's praises—for those who are deaf must be mute. Hence also
it comes about that we are not affected with greater reverence for God and his
majesty. Scripture summons us to this study when it has expressed many
corrections and teachings in the natures and properties of creatures. But the
chief use of creatures is not so much that we may be fed and clothed by them
as that we may learn the Creator from them; nor is it only that we may
experience his mercy, but that we may be instructed by his wisdom and power.
