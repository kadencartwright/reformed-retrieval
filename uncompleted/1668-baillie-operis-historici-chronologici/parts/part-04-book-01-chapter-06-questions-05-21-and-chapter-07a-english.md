### QUESTION V

#### How there were three hundred years from the exodus to Jephthah

Fifth it is asked how Jephthah at Judges 11:26 says that in his day Israel had
lived in Heshbon for three hundred years, when that many years had not passed
from the killing of Sihon and occupation of Heshbon in Numbers 21, in Moses’
last and the fortieth year after the exodus, to Jephthah’s government. Answer:
both sacred and profane authors put round numbers for exact ones, adding to a
hundred if some are lacking and subtracting if some remain. From the exodus
(the king of the Ammonites questioned Jephthah about it alone, and Jephthah’s
answer therefore concerned it) to that time there were 305 years by our
calculation. The five above three hundred are dropped by rounding. If instead
we wish Jephthah to answer not from the exodus but from the occupation of
Heshbon, the exact years will be thirty-five fewer than three hundred; but in
rounding those are added as easily as five are subtracted. Only observe here
that Jephthah’s calculation strongly supports our reckoning, which includes
the years of servitude within those of the judges, and opposes those who
exclude them. For if you count separately the servitudes recorded before
Jephthah—eight years under Mesopotamia, eighteen under Moab, twenty under
Canaan, seven under Midian, and eighteen under Ammon, seventy-one in all—and
also the ten customarily given to the elders after Joshua and before the first
servitude, we shall have no fewer than 386 years from the exodus to Jephthah,
a greater number than any rounding can accommodate.

### QUESTION VI

#### Did Phinehas live three hundred years?

Sixth it is asked how Phinehas is said to have served at the sanctuary in
advanced age at the time of the Benjaminite war in Judges 20:28, since he was
born in Egypt before the exodus, while more than three hundred years passed
after the exodus to Samson’s death, which is narrated before the Benjaminite
war. Scripture records no mortal after Moses’ days as living so long. Answer:
all interpreters acknowledge that in Judges, as often elsewhere in sacred
history, there is a *hysterologia*, by which later in the narrative are told
things that happened earlier. Although Judges describes the Benjaminite war
after Samson’s death, it does not affirm that the war occurred after his
death. A suitable reason why the Spirit-inspired writer cast that history in
this place may be that all the judges’ deeds should first be woven together in
one continuous, uninterrupted narrative. When these are finished, the
idolatry of Micah and the Danites and the Benjaminite war are appended. They
occurred in no judge’s time but in an interregnum, when no king, prince, or
single person held the authority over the people that could restrain the
wicked from whatever crimes pleased their eyes and minds. What is related of
the Danites can therefore without inconvenience be assigned to the first
interregnum after the death of Joshua and the elders, before Othniel’s
government, when Phinehas at Shiloh administered the Lord’s ark as high priest
in place of his recently deceased father Eleazar.

### QUESTION VII

#### Until which captivity did the Danites’ idolatry endure?

Seventh it is asked how the Danites’ public idolatry in Judges 18:30 is said
to have endured until the captivity of the land, since Samuel, David,
Hezekiah, Josiah, and other pious rulers removed every public idol in their
days. Answer: the passage means neither the Assyrian captivity of the ten
tribes nor the Babylonian captivity of the two, but the Philistine captivity
of the ark in the days of Eli and Samuel, when it was moved from Shiloh among
various places until it rested on Zion, as the verse following the one cited
indicates.

### QUESTION VIII

#### To what period should Ruth be assigned?

Eighth it is asked: since Ruth 1:1 reports that the events which follow
happened in the days of the judges, to which judge’s days should Boaz’s
marriage and Obed’s birth be assigned? Answer: on the calculation made in
Question IV, the history of Ruth belongs to Deborah’s government.

### QUESTION IX

#### How many years do the books of Joshua and Judges contain?

Ninth it is asked: since we observed above how many years of history each book
of the Pentateuch embraced, what span is exhausted by Joshua, Judges, and
Ruth? Answer: given what was said above, Joshua contains only seventeen years,
from Moses’ death to Joshua’s; Judges and Ruth contain three hundred, from the
death of Joshua and his companions to Samson’s end and the beginnings of
Eli’s government and high priesthood.

### QUESTION X

#### To what time should the Trojan War be assigned?

Tenth it is asked what the condition of the rest of the world outside the
church was during this epoch. Answer: no writer yet existed anywhere among
the nations. What Latin and Greek authors, writing many centuries later, say
about this epoch is, by their own admission, so wrapped in the most worthless
fables that it deserves no historical credit. Yet this certainly appears to
be the time in which poets after Orpheus and Homer place the Trojan War and,
slightly earlier, the Argonautic expedition and Hercules’ labors. The fall of
Troy is not inconsistent with Jephthah’s age. For the lying petty Greeks’
inventions about Agamemnon’s daughter Iphigenia being offered to Diana,
whether as sacrifice or priestess, arose from the true history of Jephthah’s
daughter: she is reported to have been offered to God, whether as a burnt
offering or, more probably, as a perpetual virgin devoted throughout life to
God’s particular service.

### QUESTION XI

#### How is Judges 13 reconciled with chapters 15 and 16?

Eleventh it is asked how the Philistine servitude is said in Judges 13 to have
lasted forty years when, after twenty years, it appears to end at Samson’s
death in the last verses of Judges 15 and 16, though it might also appear to
have continued to the sixtieth year, namely to Eli’s death, when the captivity
of the ark brought Israel’s most severe oppression by the Philistines.
Answer: there was more than one Philistine servitude. The one in Judges 15
could rightly be said to end at Samson’s death, when so great a multitude of
Philistines was buried with him that we do not read of this enemy harming
God’s people for many years afterward.

[Printed p. 55 | IA leaf 85 | access PDF 85 | OCR object 84]

The defeats inflicted by the Philistines in Eli’s last years must be assigned
to another servitude, one which Samuel and Saul began to drive away but which
was not completely shaken off before David’s reign. The most exact
chronologers judge that the first Philistine servitude ended at Samson’s death
and began many years before his birth.

### QUESTION XII

#### Was Samuel descended from Korah?

Twelfth it is asked how Samuel’s lineage is traced to Korah, since in 1
Chronicles 6:16–17 Samuel is counted seventeenth from Korah, although Korah’s
whole progeny appears to have been destroyed with their father; for Numbers
16:10 says that 2,050 of Korah’s descendants were killed by heavenly flame.
Answer: that is indeed what the small-octavo vernacular Bibles said to have
been printed at London by Robert Barker in 1638 contain. But for your own and
others’ caution, you must be warned that this edition swarms with hundreds of
the grossest errors and bears an utterly shameful lie on its very front. It
was printed neither at London, by Barker, nor in 1638, but recently in
Amsterdam, Delft, and elsewhere in the Netherlands. Dutchmen driven by greed
for profit—as I learned from the printers themselves—published more than
seven hundred thousand copies of these thoroughly corrupt Bibles, to the
great injury of our churches. In any case, where these depraved copies read
2,050, the Hebrew text and all versions, including our more correct vernacular
ones, have 250. Not all Korah’s sons perished among them, as the next verse
after the one cited expressly states. Certainly many Psalms were entrusted to
Korah’s sons to sing, and some of them appear to have been composed by Heman,
Samuel’s descendant and Korah’s twentieth descendant.

### QUESTION XIII

#### How did Levites take up priestly functions?

Thirteenth it is asked how Samuel, a descendant of Korah, dared take up a
priestly function by offering sacrifice to the Lord when his father Korah paid
for that attempt with so great a calamity. Answer: not every part of the
priestly office was so attached to Aaron’s family that other Levites were
excluded when they were extraordinary prophets, as Moses and Samuel were.

### QUESTION XIV

#### On the years of Saul’s reign

Fourteenth it is asked how 1 Samuel 7:2 is reconciled with Acts 13:21. In the
former, the ark remained at Kiriath-jearim twenty years after Eli’s death,
while Samuel judged Israel, and then many more years until Saul’s reign. In
the latter, forty years are assigned to Saul’s reign. It is incompatible with
the epoch’s total of 480 years from the exodus to the temple’s foundation to
assign forty to Saul and perhaps twenty to Samuel. Answer: the apostle’s words
in Acts must be taken jointly of Saul’s and Samuel’s governments, as anyone
who inspects the preceding verse will see, not of Saul separately. According
to most interpreters, his reign did not extend much beyond ten years. During
them Samuel always retained great authority until his death, which is
reported to have occurred two years before Saul’s.

If you object that Saul must have reigned much more than ten—indeed, forty—
years because he left Ishbosheth, one of his younger sons, who nevertheless
was forty when his father died (2 Samuel 2:20), grant that Saul begot
Ishbosheth, his fourth son, at age twenty-one; it will follow that Saul reigned
sixty years. For he began to reign in the first year of his life, if we trust
the vulgar Latin version, which the Romanists wish to be authentic and which
reads 1 Samuel 13:1, “Saul was a son of one year when he began to reign.”
Answer: this is a manifest error of the Latin translator, one which no
Romanist chronologer has attempted to defend, though they refuse to admit the
shameful blunder of the Council of Trent in preferring this most corrupt
version to the Hebrew sources. The Hebrew text says here that Saul had then
reigned one year, or that Saul was the son of one year in his reign. The Old
English reads, “Saul now had beene King one yeare,” rightly adding “now” by
way of explanation to connect these words with the previous chapter’s
history. The new translators’ correction, besides being unnecessary, also
makes matters worse. The very judicious Italian translator therefore did well
to illuminate the text by expressing the old English translators’ explanatory
addition instead of obscuring the words by omitting that particle with more
recent translators.

Yet another difficulty still arises from this text. It expressly affirms that
Saul reigned two years over Israel; so read both the Hebrew sources and every
version derived from them.

[Printed p. 56 | IA leaf 86 | access PDF 86 | OCR object 85]

Answer: the common solution to this knot is that Saul reigned legitimately
for only two years, but afterward acted as a tyrant rather than a king,
deprived of God’s Spirit and driven by the fury of an evil demon; indeed, the
right to the kingdom had manifestly been transferred by the Lord to the
already anointed David. Whatever merit this has, it is unnecessary to go so
far. The words before us do not deny that Saul reigned more than two years,
though they affirm he had been king for two years when the history that
follows occurred. This verse must therefore be joined to the next, as the
Hebrew does by an express conjunction. Since the old English translators
followed the Hebrew, they ought not to have been corrected by the recent
ones, contrary to the examples of the old Latin, French, and Italian
translators.

### QUESTION XV

#### On reconciling 1 Samuel 15 with chapter 28

Fifteenth it is asked how verse 35 of 1 Samuel 15 is reconciled with 28:15.
The former affirms that after that time Samuel never again saw Saul; the
latter relates that Samuel and Saul met after then. Answer: the former does
not affirm that Samuel and Saul never afterward met or saw each other, but
that Samuel never afterward visited Saul familiarly as he had been accustomed
to do or greeted him in his home. This does not prevent Saul, led by a good or
evil spirit, from afterward coming into Samuel’s presence.

### QUESTION XVI

#### How was David Jesse’s son, both seventh and eighth?

Sixteenth it is asked how David is counted the eighth of Jesse’s sons and is
called younger than seven older brothers in 1 Samuel 16:10 and 17:12, but is
nevertheless expressly named seventh, and no more than seven sons are assigned
to Jesse in 1 Chronicles 2:15. Answer: it is certain that David was born to
his father at a very advanced age, one hundred or more, as proved above.
Jesse could therefore easily have had grandsons and great-grandsons older
than David, his youngest son, one of whom, while living in Jesse’s household,
could be counted among his sons. Alternatively, when Samuel came to anoint
David king Jesse may have had eight sons. If one either died unmarried or
lived childless, he was not counted in Jesse’s lineage when Chronicles was
written, so that those who had first been eight later became only seven.

### QUESTION XVII

#### How was David both known and unknown to Saul?

Seventeenth it is asked how what we read in 1 Samuel 16:21 agrees with 17:55.
In the former David was familiarly known to Saul as his household servant,
armor-bearer, and greatly loved courtier. In the latter David was so unknown
to Saul and Abner as if he were a complete stranger. Answer: we do not approve
of alleging a *hysterologia* here with Junius, as though Goliath’s death had
actually preceded Saul’s fury and David’s first summons to court, although it
follows in the narrative. For 1 Samuel 18:2 expressly says that after Goliath’s
death David did not again depart from Saul to remain in his father’s house as
he had been accustomed to come and go after his first summons to court in
17:19. The order of the history must therefore necessarily be preserved
without any *hysterologia*. Saul’s failure to recognize him should instead be
attributed to forgetfulness, which David’s long absence from court and some
change in clothing and bodily appearance could produce in anyone, especially
a king whose mind, memory, and attention were disturbed by madness and a
demon. So too with Abner, who because of military and other business was
seldom at court and perhaps never saw David even though he was sometimes
familiar with Saul.

### QUESTION XVIII

#### How did Ishbosheth reign both two and seven years?

Eighteenth it is asked how Ishbosheth in 2 Samuel 2:20 reigns over Israel two
years, while there David is said to have reigned over the house of Judah seven
years and six months, since it is certain that Ishbosheth reigned over Israel
as long as David did over Judah. Answer: the former passage does not assert
that Ishbosheth reigned over Israel only two years, but that he had reigned
two when Joab and Abner fought the battle described there. After it ended,
Ishbosheth reigned more than five further years until the end of his life; only
then did David begin to rule all Israel.

### QUESTION XIX

#### From where did the fortieth year of Absalom’s conspiracy begin?

Nineteenth it is asked: 2 Samuel 15:7 says that Absalom conspired against his
father after forty years; from where do these forty take their beginning? For
hardly thirty years had then passed since the beginning of David’s reign.
Answer: we must beware of flying against the sacred codices with many others
because of a difficulty of this sort and changing forty years into four,
contrary to the fidelity of the Hebrew source and all versions derived from
it. They untie the knot more correctly who place the beginning of these forty
years either in the year when David was first anointed king or at the
beginning of Saul’s reign, three years before David’s anointing. That was a
notable epoch and mark of time among God’s people, the moment when their
government was first changed from a commonwealth into a kingdom.

### QUESTION XX

#### How did David buy Zion for fifty shekels of silver yet six hundred
#### shekels of gold?

Twentieth it is asked how David bought from Araunah the threshing floor on
Mount Moriah where the temple was later built for fifty shekels of silver in
2 Samuel 24:24, when in 1 Chronicles 21:25 he paid six hundred shekels of gold
for it. Answer: Masii’s solution is wholly wrong, as though the former
passage meant gold shekels and the latter silver ones, whose ratio to gold is
nearly twelvefold, so that fifty of the former would be six hundred of the
latter. The former passage expressly speaks of silver and the latter of gold.
Nor does Junius’s solution satisfy, as though the former passage contained
the price only of the oxen and farm implements with which the sacrificed oxen
were burned, and the latter the price of the floor on which the altar was
erected. Both passages express the purchase of the floor no less than of the
oxen. We therefore answer third that the former appears to mean the price both
of the floor and of everything received from Araunah for the sacrifice, the
latter the price of the field surrounding the floor. David here bought the
whole space dedicated to the temple and its surrounding courts and buildings,
which was very large and rightly worth six hundred gold shekels.

### QUESTION XXI

#### How incredible was the sum of gold and silver spent on building the
#### temple?

Finally it is asked how David prepared for the temple in 1 Chronicles 22:17
one hundred thousand talents of gold and one million of silver, when Exodus
38:24 says no more than thirty talents of gold and only one hundred of silver
were spent on the tabernacle. Indeed, 1 Chronicles 29:4 says David offered for
the temple only three thousand talents of gold and seven thousand of silver.
And if the former sums are joined to the latter, with the rulers’ gifts in 1
Chronicles 29:7—five thousand talents of gold and ten thousand of silver—the
resulting total is almost incredible. Answer to the first: Moses’ tabernacle
was a small thing beside Solomon’s temple, so the calculations of the latter
should not be estimated by the former. To the second: the smaller sum of very
refined silver and gold, as the text’s words indicate, came from David’s
private treasures, while the larger came from more public ones. But we admit
that the third point presses very hard, for the sum is nearly incredible and
truly astonishing. It appears to us that so great a mass of gold and silver
was never collected anywhere on earth, nor is so much gold now possessed by
all the world’s princes combined. We construct this astonishing sum as
follows. From Exodus 38, by comparing the half-shekels contributed for each
person with the talent total contributed by so many thousands, it appears
that a talent consists of no fewer than three thousand shekels. The shekel,
which contains two bekas, twenty gerahs, or four drachmas, weighs half an
ounce and is therefore our half-crown, or half an imperial thaler, about
thirty Scots or two and a half English pennies. I speak of the silver
sanctuary shekel; the common one was half as small, worth only ten gerahs, a
quarter imperial or crown coin, fifteen of our pennies, or one and a third
English pennies. In the Old Testament gold’s price was ten times silver’s,
though today the ratio has grown beyond twelvefold. Three thousand shekels
make 4,500 of our pounds and 375 English pounds; therefore a gold talent in
David’s age was worth at least ten silver talents.

With these foundations laid, what we seek is easily found. Our question has
108,000 talents of gold and 1,017,000 of silver. Multiply the former number,
108,000, by 3,750, the number of English pounds in each gold talent, and by
45,000, the number of Scots pounds in each gold talent.

[Printed p. 58 | IA leaf 88 | access PDF 88 | OCR object 87]

You will see the stated sum of gold grow to 405 million English pounds—that
is, pounds a thousand times a thousand (not “hundred-thousands,” as the recent
English annotations on 1 Chronicles 22 very carelessly say)—£405,000,000.
The silver is £380,625,750. Join these sums and you will find £785,625,750
English or £9,425,509,000 Scots. So vast and astounding are these sums that
the most learned Brerewood, followed by the most industrious Purchas, lean
toward the opinion of those who do not take “talent” in these passages of
Chronicles in its ordinary sense but for a rather small amount or lump of
money. Such an invention, however, manifestly contradicts Scripture. In the
passages cited, as if to cut off every opportunity for such an escape, we have
fractions of a talent amounting to ten thousand drachmas in 1 Chronicles 20.
A talent consists of three thousand sanctuary shekels, each of which has four
drachmas. For the beka, half a shekel, which the people paid per head first
for the tabernacle and temple and later to Caesar, was the same as the
didrachma. Therefore a talent has no fewer than twelve thousand drachmas by
our calculation and, according to the passage before us, must have more than
ten thousand. This fantasy of reducing the talent must therefore vanish.
There are reasons why we should judge the sum we have made very great to be
even greater, but none for making it less.

## CHAPTER VII

### The deeds embraced by the fourth epoch, from the temple’s foundation to
### its destruction

In three epochs we have brought the series of years from the world’s beginning
to the foundation of Solomon’s temple through 2,932 years, more than half the
time that has passed from the first beginning to the present day. We have
explained it by a sufficiently certain thread, as we hope, from clear passages
of Holy Scripture. The next epoch runs from the first temple’s foundation to
its destruction in the year of the world 3,360. It embraces the 492 years
during which Solomon’s temple stood. We shall see in its proper place how
this total is made up from its parts. Meanwhile, here is a brief summary of
what happened in the world during this interval, lest a bare skeleton of
mere time utterly exhaust and kill already rather nauseated stomachs and
repel them from this study.

Solomon appears to have reached at least age twenty before his father’s death,
for Rehoboam had then been born to him by Naamah the Ammonite. As soon as he
sat on the throne, his first concern was to fulfill his father’s commands by
applying every effort to building the temple. Thirty thousand Israelites were
occupied in this work for at least seven years (1 Kings 5:15), as were 153,600
foreigners found by David among God’s people and assigned to it (2
Chronicles 2:18). Over these were 3,300 Israelite supervisors according to 2
Kings, or 3,600 according to 2 Chronicles. After completing the temple he
spent thirteen years on other buildings, so that he appears to have exercised
all these thousands of men in construction for twenty years. That is less
astonishing if what is related of Chemnis, king of Egypt, is true. In Saul’s
day he employed three hundred thousand men for twenty whole years in building
the largest pyramid, which still stands intact a few miles from Memphis,
modern Cairo, by the Nile, so that he might be buried in it. Yet his hope was
greatly disappointed: the people were so indignant that he had harassed so
many thousands of men in useless labor for so many years that his son did not
dare deposit his father’s remains either there or in any other known place,
lest the raging mob tear them apart.

After the buildings were finished, God again spoke familiarly with Solomon in
a dream, made a splendid covenant with him, and raised him above all mortals
of his age in wisdom, wealth, and every glory. Yet these favors did not
restrain him from the foulest ingratitude and apostasy even into idolatry. So
carefully must mortals, however advanced in piety and virtue, guard against
the itch of lusts and plague of complacency. Most learned men think he
lamented his sins and vanities in Ecclesiastes. In any case, it is certain
that his last things did not answer to his first, although he died in the
fortieth year of his reign with considerable glory and peace. From his
monstrous lust, with seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, he
begot no more than one son and two daughters,

if indeed they were King Solomon’s daughters, who are mentioned in 1 Kings
4:11 and 15.

The justice of the supreme Judge customarily punishes crimes and scandals
most severely in those dearest to him. The All-wise sometimes delays his
vengeance, but its weight ultimately makes up for its lateness. Late
grandsons, persisting in their ancestors’ footsteps, pay for what fathers,
grandfathers, and great-grandfathers sinned. David’s and Solomon’s offenses
were punished in the kingdoms of Rehoboam and his descendants. Rehoboam’s
stubborn persistence in the most foolish counsel was madness sent by the
Lord’s right hand to punish his, his father’s, and his grandfather’s sins and
to fulfill at the appointed time the kingdom promised to Jeroboam through
Ahijah. Ten of the twelve tribes were torn from David’s house and given to
Jeroboam. This very great wound did not bring Rehoboam to due repentance. As
he continued to despise the Deity, God sent Shishak king of Egypt, who took
everything Jeroboam had left to David’s house. Broken by this calamity, both
king and people in Judah sought and obtained the Lord’s help. Shishak stripped
the temple and royal house of the immense treasures of gold and silver David
and Solomon had gathered and returned to Egypt while leaving the people their
liberty intact. These evils crushed Rehoboam in his reign’s fifth year.

The possession of wealth is shifting and uncertain. Riches have wings and
fly: when they appear bound by iron and adamantine chains, they break their
bonds in an instant and fly away. Solomon left Jerusalem richer in gold,
silver, and gems than every city on earth. Five years had not passed when
Egyptian greed changed that golden city into a bronze one: Rehoboam
substituted bronze shields for Solomon’s gold ones carried to Egypt by
Shishak. Rehoboam left
twenty-eight sons and sixty daughters. Passing over older sons, he made Abijah,
a younger son born to him by Maacah of Absalom’s line, king after him, and
died in his reign’s seventeenth year.

God’s judgments were most just in taking ten tribes from David’s house and
his mercy great in leaving sinners for David’s sake so little light in Israel—one
tribe, much less the three of Judah, Benjamin, and the Levites. Yet the rebel
Israelites’ ingratitude toward David and Solomon was most unjust and foul and
ended very badly for them. From this rebellion’s first day an Iliad of evils
invaded them. Idolatry’s poison ruined the salvation of souls, and civil and
foreign wars ruined peace, until the whole race of these rebels was swallowed
in the abyss of perpetual captivity.

Jeroboam first established his separate kingdom under Rehoboam in the year of
the world 2,969. The unregenerate have an altogether astonishing inclination
to their immediate affairs and advantages while wholly despising God and
neighbor and remaining unconcerned about every evil that can befall them in
the future or the coming age. Jeroboam very gladly embraced Rehoboam’s rebel
subjects as soon as they offered themselves. When he saw the obvious danger
of their return to Rehoboam if they went to Jerusalem for worship according
to God’s law, he broke off that intercourse. Trampling God’s command, he set
up golden calves at Dan and Bethel for all his subjects to worship with the
same ceremonies with which they had worshiped God in Jerusalem’s temple. The
Levites who adhered to divine worship abandoned Jeroboam. Despising God’s
ministers, the profane king consecrated priests for his idols from the dregs
of the people. They were more pleasing to the people because, for three
obols and of no account, with tithes remitted and the Levitical inheritance
God established abolished, they hired out their cheap labor almost free.

Jeroboam knew that for a lesser idolatry the kingdom he possessed had been
transferred from Solomon’s descendants to him. The Lord had testified against
his crime from heaven by manifest miracles and terrible threats. Yet human
counsels and carnal policy choked the clearest light from God, and wrath at
last blazed against the despiser of God. After many years of great prosperity
and deep peace, he applauded himself as though the Deity not only overlooked
all his crimes but approved and smiled on them. At last the avenging hand of
God broke him.

Abijah made war on Jeroboam to recover his father’s kingdom and led four
hundred thousand men of Judah and Benjamin into battle. Jeroboam immediately
met him with eight hundred thousand Israelites. They collided with most
hostile spirits, and Abijah was almost conquered by the double host, but
Jeroboam’s crimes were then ripe and demanded the divine sickle. Five hundred
thousand Israelites were therefore pressed in the winepress of the Lord’s
wrath, a number we nowhere else read as killed in one day. This great example
of the Lord’s fury did not sufficiently crush either Jeroboam or Abijah, so
new rods were prepared for both. Abijah was removed in his reign’s third year,
leaving the scepter to his son Asa. Shortly afterward, at the very beginning
of Asa’s reign, Jeroboam was struck by a divine plague and died in his sins.
His son Nadab succeeded him, but in his reign’s second year the rebel Baasha
of Issachar violently removed him with his whole lineage. See the fruit that
remained to Jeroboam from a kingdom wickedly acquired and worse administered.

[Printed p. 60 | IA leaf 90 | access PDF 90 | OCR object 89]

Baasha, God’s avenger against Jeroboam, did not abstain from Jeroboam’s sins,
so his kingdom was not lasting either. Although after ruling some years he
obtained a peaceful death from God as a reward for usurping Jeroboam’s place,
he was punished with a similar penalty because he refused to be separated
from Jeroboam’s sins. In his second regnal year his son Elah was rooted out
with his entire family by Zimri. Although God was well pleased with vengeance
on Baasha, by Zimri’s swift destruction the Lord showed how greatly the crime
of conspiracy displeased him. Zimri enjoyed Israel’s throne no more than
seven days. As soon as Omri with his strong army besieged Tirzah, then Israel’s
royal seat, Zimri, unequal to its defense, shut himself in the palace and
burned it over himself. By this self-burning alive he gave Sardanapalus and
others an example of desperate courage.

Yet Omri’s kingdom was not peaceful. Half of Israel preferred Tibni as king,
so that the wretched people consumed itself for some time in civil war under
these two heads. When Tibni died, Omri alone obtained the scepter and passed
it to his son Ahab, a worse egg from an evil crow.

One king of Judah, Asa, saw these eight kings in Israel. This man, notable for
both piety and arms, had ready 580,000 mighty warriors: the tribe of Judah
supplied 300,000 and Benjamin 280,000. Yet the pious king placed no confidence
even in so strong an army, no less prudently than piously, for unless God had
brought help the whole force would soon have been overwhelmed by a greater.
Zerah invaded Asa with a million Ethiopians; pouring out prayers to God, Asa
routed and put them to flight.

No mortal is perfect or fortunate in every respect. Asa, who had received so
illustrious an example of divine protection, displayed such weakness of faith
that when attacked by Baasha of Israel he sought the Syrian king’s weapons
rather than God’s help. Reproved by a prophet for this sin, he became angry
and imprisoned his admonisher, for which the Lord punished him with very
severe gout. Another blemish marred Asa’s otherwise very handsome face: to
relieve the sharpest pains he looked more to the physicians’ arts than God’s
help. Marked by these censures, an otherwise excellent king died at Jerusalem
in his reign’s forty-first year.

Asa’s son Jehoshaphat, age thirty-six, succeeded him in Judah. He immediately
brought church, state, and army into the best condition with nearly equal
care. He established two supreme councils independent of one another, one to
administer God’s affairs and the other those of the kingdom. Besides garrisons
for fortresses and cities, he had 1,100,000 mighty men ready for field
warfare. Undoubtedly God’s singular blessing made the land of Judea sufficient
to feed so many millions, although all Canaan from Dan to Beersheba did not
extend 160 miles at its greatest length or exceed sixty Italian miles at its
greatest width. Judea, Benjamin, and thus all Jehoshaphat’s kingdom did not
comprise a quarter of this area. Today, and for many centuries, all Canaan is
incapable of feeding a few thousand half-naked, famished, and rapacious Arabs.
It lies almost everywhere deserted and uncultivated, the divine curse turning
its once most fertile fields into sterile soil and arid wastes.

The otherwise excellent King Jehoshaphat sinned by contracting an alliance
and cultivating a close friendship with Ahab king of Samaria, contrary to the
prophets’ warnings. Ahab married the worst of women, Jezebel, daughter of the
king of Sidon—an idolatress, harlot, murderess, and poisoner—and rushed into
every crime.

Hence, according to Elijah’s declaration, heaven was shut to Israel for three
years and six months. When drought and famine had consumed almost the whole
nation, Benhadad the Syrian besieged Samaria with a vast army to devour the
few survivors. In absolute despair Ahab turned to Israel’s God, however
hostile to him. For his own name’s glory the Lord delayed vengeance on the
impious king and destroyed the Syrians with great slaughter by a little band
of servants. When their pride was not subdued but they returned the following
year with an equal army, the Lord again used a tiny number of Israelites to
kill one hundred thousand Syrians in a single day; the fall of Aphek’s wall
crushed another twenty-seven thousand who had fled there after defeat. These
splendid benefits of the Lord did not restrain Ahab from crimes. This wretched
man craved Naboth’s vineyard so desperately that he did not shrink from
devouring it when it was sprinkled with its owner’s blood through Jezebel’s
arts. Yet he was forced to vomit it up, hardly digested, together with his
life and kingdom.

He enticed Jehoshaphat into an alliance for war against the Syrians, despite
a prophet’s futile warning.

The good man, deceived by foxlike fraud through excessive pliability, wore
the royal insignia in battle, which Ahab had taken off to hide. The charging
Syrians nearly killed him, but God’s right hand delivered him from immediate
death. Yet the masked hypocrite did not escape: an arrow shot randomly by a
Syrian but directed to its just destination by God pierced his middle. The
royal chariot was washed in Jezreel by Naboth’s vineyard and offered his blood
for dogs to lick.

Ahab had seventy-two sons. The flame of the Lord’s fury soon reduced these
flourishing branches, trunk, and root to ash. Ahaziah, the eldest, sat on the
throne only two years before falling through an upper-room window and
contracting the disease from which, according to Elijah’s prophecy, he died.
The second, Joram, after ruling thirteen years, was wounded at Ramoth-gilead,
the same place as his father, and carried to Jezreel. While he lay there, he
heard that the rebel Jehu, driven to it by a divine oracle, was coming against
him and vainly confronted him with his available forces. Supported by God’s
hand, Jehu pierced Joram with an arrow in his chariot and cast his corpse into
Naboth’s vineyard. He had Joram’s mother Jezebel thrown from the window and
trampled beneath horses. Dogs tore apart her trampled body, leaving only her
skull, palms, and soles. At Jehu’s first command, all seventy of Ahab’s
remaining sons were beheaded at Samaria. Indeed, all Ahab’s relatives and
Baal’s priests were struck by the sword along with the family. Wicked men are
rarely alone in their sins. They have far too many companions in crime, whom
they usually involve together in all their disasters. This zeal against Ahab
and Baal preserved Israel’s throne for Jehu’s descendants to the fourth
generation. Nevertheless, Jehu clung tenaciously to Jeroboam’s idols. That
most cruel impiety under a great pretense of piety rooted out him and his
entire posterity too.

Jehoshaphat’s friendship with Ahab cost him very dearly. He himself barely and
with great difficulty escaped lethal dangers twice in this wicked fellowship,
while his descendants were almost buried in Ahab’s ruin. His eldest son
Jehoram, Ahab’s son-in-law by his daughter Athaliah, showed himself Ahab’s and
Jezebel’s son-in-law as soon as he obtained Judah’s scepter. Despising God,
he embraced idols. He killed all his brothers, Jehoshaphat’s sons, without
even a pretense of any crime, and many princes of Judah too. Hence the revolt
of the Edomites and Arabs and the prophet Elijah’s letter threatening the
profane king with the most dreadful punishments, all of which occurred after
his short reign. Arab and Ethiopian arms devastated the land and killed all
the king’s sons except the youngest; after terrible torments the king’s own
bowels flowed out. His son Ahaziah survived only one year after being placed
on the throne. When he went down from Jerusalem to Jezreel to visit his uncle
Joram as he recovered from his wound, Jehu killed him together with his uncle.
Indeed, Jehu caught and killed all forty-two of Jehoshaphat’s grandsons, the
sons of Ahaziah’s brothers, as they traveled to visit Ahab’s sons.

Athaliah, Joram’s wife and Ahaziah’s mother, spilled what little remained of
Judah’s royal blood after Joram, the Ethiopians, and Jehu had nearly exhausted
it. One infant, Joash, was hidden in the temple by his aunt Jehosheba, wife of
the high priest Jehoiada, and snatched from the mad fury’s rage. Having
perpetrated this crime, the worst of bipeds dared sit on Judah’s throne.
Indeed, the long-suffering patience of God permitted her to rule Jerusalem
for six full years, mingle everything sacred and profane, right and wrong,
and flood the whole land with idolatry and blood. But at last the Deity awoke
and an ember blazed from the ash into flame. Jehoiada summoned the princes to
the temple, anointed Joash, now seven, as king, and ordered Athaliah, a fourth
Fury, slaughtered in Jerusalem’s very streets. After restoring the kingdom,
this man who had deserved the very best from king and people died at age 130.

King Joash repaid the high priest’s descendants with most unworthy gratitude
for these very great services. As soon as he was freed from Jehoiada’s
tutelage and instruction and began following his own bent, he fell into
idolatry. When Jehoiada’s son Zechariah rebuked the apostate king in the
course of his high-priestly duty, the impious king had his admonisher buried
under stones in the temple itself at the Lord’s altar. Divine vengeance did
not delay long. A little Syrian force routed Judah’s great armies, afflicted
the people miserably, and destroyed before others the princes who had
instigated the king’s idolatry. This disaster did not yet satisfy divine
justice. The avenger of innocent blood and corrupted worship did not cease
until King Joash fell at the bloody hands of conspirators.

Joash’s son Amaziah succeeded his father. Once established on the throne, he
rightly ordered those guilty of his father’s murder punished and applied
himself to purging divine worship of his father’s corruptions. Hence
everything at home and abroad went very prosperously for him. He destroyed
twenty thousand rebel Edomites, but as a flighty man learned idolatry from
the conquered Edomites and answered proudly when a prophet reproved him for
it. This was the origin of his miseries.

God made him mad through evil counsel in order to destroy him. At his own
whim he began an unnecessary war with Joash king of Samaria. His forces were
routed and he captured, and the Israelites plundered Jerusalem and the temple.
Falling into his subjects’ contempt through this defeat, he was killed by
conspirators from Jerusalem.

His son Uzziah succeeded him. While he delighted in God’s law, everything at
home and in war flowed according to his desire. But when he wished to join the
priestly censer to the royal scepter, God struck his forehead with leprosy and
forced him to withdraw from both temple and throne to private houses until
life’s end. His son Jotham administered the kingdom piously, prudently, and
bravely both in his father’s name while he lived and in his own after his
death. But all was the opposite with Ahaz, the most wicked son of the best
father. When he despised God and loved his fathers’ idols, the Lord stirred up
Rezin of Syria and Pekah king of Israel to punish him. To divert their arms,
Ahaz summoned the king of Assyria to his aid with great gifts. Tiglath-pileser
came at Ahaz’s call, captured Damascus, destroyed Syria’s kingdom, killed
Rezin, and led the people captive to Assyria. But when at Damascus Ahaz
congratulated the Assyrian on his victory, the utterly impious man carried the
conquered Syrians’ gods with him to Jerusalem. To please the king, the high
priest Uzziah erected a new altar in God’s house after the Damascus model.
God’s hand then expelled Ahaz from the number of the living so that a place
would be open for his far better son Hezekiah.

While affairs stood thus among the Jews, the kingdom of Israel wore a very
different face. Jehu enjoyed considerable prosperity as long as he lived for
rooting out Ahab, as did his descendants for many years after his death,
although both he and they were afflicted by various plagues from the Lord for
Jeroboam’s idols and finally rooted out. Jehu reigned twenty-nine years, his
son Jehoahaz seventeen, his grandson Joash sixteen, and his great-grandson
Jeroboam forty-one, but his great-great-grandson Zechariah only six months.

In Jehu’s and Jehoahaz’s days Israel was most miserably afflicted by the
Syrians Hazael and Benhadad, but Joash by his prayers and those of the prophet
Elisha obtained several very splendid victories against them. Jeroboam II
received so much help from the Lord that he captured Damascus and extended
Israel’s frontier to the farthest boundary prescribed by Moses and Joshua.
Yet no favors or work of the greatest prophets drew Jehu’s descendants from
their idols. At last, therefore, the branch of judgment flowered from the root
of justice. At the very beginning of his reign the conspirator Shallum cut off
Zechariah with Jehu’s whole family. From then on a continuous succession of
conspiracies shook and finally overturned Israel’s throne.

Shallum held Samaria’s kingdom hardly one month before Menahem deprived him
of reign and life together. Menahem did reign ten years, but his son Pekahiah
sat on the throne no more than two before Pekah removed him by violence.
Neither Pekah’s death was dry nor his life peaceful. A most hostile enemy of
Judah, he arrived after Rezin king of Syria had afflicted God’s people with
great defeats in the idolatrous Ahaz’s time. Pekah killed 120,000 men of Judah
in one day and carried 200,000 captives to Samaria; the Samaritans’ mercy,
however, sent them all home free. Neither Israelite nor Syrian went unpunished
for serving as a chastening rod against Judah. Tiglath-pileser the Assyrian
utterly destroyed Syria’s kingdom at that time, and he carried Israel’s
transjordanian tribes and others captive to Assyria without return. Pekah too
perished in the snares of the conspirator Hoshea.

Israel’s last king, Hoshea, although somewhat better than his predecessors,
was nevertheless captured at Samaria by Shalmaneser in his reign’s ninth year
after a three-year siege, and in Hezekiah’s sixth was carried with Israel’s
remnants to Media.

The Lord’s patience toward Israel was great. For more than two hundred years,
from Jeroboam’s first to Hoshea’s ninth, he contended with their stubborn
hardness. Their inflexible rebellion refused to be conquered: they would
return neither to God’s worship nor to their legitimate rulers, David’s
descendants. After perpetual idolatry and continuous, exceedingly cruel wars
at home and abroad, they were finally expelled from Canaan and mingled with
the nations without any distinction later noticed by learned men. In the
Israelites’ place, Shalmaneser king of Assyria led colonies of Babylonians and
other gentiles into Samaria’s lands. This rabble and their descendants
cultivated a religion mixed from paganism and Jeroboam’s idolatry, together
with a Vatinius-like hatred of the Jews, into late ages.

What Shalmaneser completed in Israel, his son Sennacherib attempted to
accomplish in Judah. After devastating most of the region and capturing
almost all its cities, he closely besieged Jerusalem itself, which without
doubt

he would have captured had the Lord not been entreated by the prayers of
Hezekiah, Isaiah, and other pious people of that time and killed 185,000 in the
Assyrian camp in one night. Freed from this fear, Hezekiah fell into a mortal
illness. This excellent man still had no children and had not yet carried the
church’s reformation to completion. Standing at death’s door and seized by a
great desire for a longer life, he poured out humble prayers to God about it.
The Lord heard him, granted fifteen new years, and made the sundial’s shadow
go back ten degrees.

After this most pious and fortunate ruler, his son Manasseh reigned in Judah,
a boy of twelve with such depravity of character that it erupted savagely into
every crime against God and men. He contaminated the temple with idols,
Jerusalem with blood, and the whole land with every pollution. This kindled
such monstrous wrath from God against Judah that it would not be appeased
until a terrible disaster flooded the guilty land. Manasseh himself was
captured by enemies, bound, and thrust into prison at Babylon.

Broken by evils, he became in every age an example of divine pity. First he
received the spirit of grace and supplication; then he obtained favor from God
and his enemies, was permitted to return to Jerusalem, lamented and amended
all his former crimes, and even administered the kingdom into deep old age
with great piety, justice, and peace.

This singular example of repentance did not deter his son Amon from crimes.
The impious youth wished to follow his father’s earlier wicked footsteps, not
his later right ones. Hastening divine vengeance removed him most miserably by
conspirators’ hands in his reign’s second year. But to show how greatly every
wicked deed displeases God, even when it executes his just judgments, the
people inflicted the extreme punishment they deserved on all the conspirators.

The father’s wicked life and disastrous death could not deprive his offspring
of God’s greatest favors. Amon’s son Josiah obtained the throne at age eight
and became the best of kings. With supreme zeal he broke not only the new
idols but the most ancient, abolishing the remnants in Jerusalem of Solomon’s
idol shrines and at Bethel of Jeroboam’s. He sprinkled the altars with the
blood of several living priests of Baal and the ashes of many dead ones, as
the Lord’s prophet had foretold of him many centuries before. Yet this great
reformation did not turn the Lord from Jerusalem’s decreed destruction,
especially for Manasseh’s crimes. At age thirty-nine the most pious king,
following the very worst advice, wished to rush against Pharaoh Necho as he
passed into Assyria. This rashness took his life and the kingdom from his son.
After him the people ordered his younger son Jehoahaz to reign, but when he
had ruled hardly more than three months Pharaoh carried him bound to Egypt
and appointed his elder brother Eliakim, otherwise named Jehoiakim, to the
kingdom. In Jehoiakim’s fourth year Nebuchadnezzar first captured Jerusalem,
where he took many temple vessels, a large part of the people, and Daniel with
the others to Babylon. King Jehoiakim does not appear to have been taken to
Babylon at that time, or if he was then led there captive he must have
returned, since he reigned at Jerusalem until the eleventh year. Only after
his death did his son Jehoiachin—or Jeconiah, or Coniah—sit on the throne for
three months. Taken bound to Babylon with another part of the people,
Jehoiachin remained captive until the days of Evilmerodach, Nebuchadnezzar’s
son, when the Assyrian king freed him from prison and treated him with royal
honor to his life’s end. Immediately after this first captivity,
Nebuchadnezzar had ordered Jehoiachin’s uncle Zedekiah, Josiah’s son, to reign
at Jerusalem. When he and the people’s princes persisted in sin,
Nebuchadnezzar came a third time to besiege Jerusalem, whose sins, now brought
to their height, wrested extreme punishments from the Lord. In Zedekiah’s
eleventh year the city was destroyed, the temple burned, and all survivors
among the people apart from a very few remnants carried to Babylon, where
they endured a seventy-year captivity.

Here is the epoch’s end, in the burning of the temple 427 years after its
construction began and the overthrow of the Hebrew kingdom, which had endured
more than five hundred years from Saul to Zedekiah under twenty-three kings.

Nothing in ancient history is more noteworthy than the first dispersion of
Noah’s descendants through the lands. Nowhere else can the true origin of
each nation be known with certainty. Many have invented much here with great
boldness, both now and formerly; we shall select a few of the most probable
things. The first source of the human race which now occupies the earth was
at Mount Ararat’s roots. Here the ark rested, here Noah lived. But as to which
location suits Mount Ararat,

[Printed p. 64 | IA leaf 94 | access PDF 94 | OCR object 93]

whether it should be placed in Greater Armenia below the Tigris’s and
Euphrates’ sources, as the entire chorus of learned men has thought, or with
more recent writers beyond the Indus’s sources, where the Taurus’s meeting
with the Imaus forms the Caucasus, I do not dispute at all. Take what is
certain.

Noah divided the world’s lands among his three sons: the east fell to Shem,
the south to Ham, and the west with the north to Japheth. The foremost of
Shem’s sons were Elam, to whom Persia fell; Asshur, to whom eastern Assyria;
and Arphaxad, whose descendant Heber had two sons, Peleg and Joktan. Abraham
was Peleg’s descendant. Joktan had thirteen sons, of whom Havilah is thought
to have occupied India both beyond and within the Ganges; another, Ophir,
migrated to the opposite Moluccan and Philippine islands. The fertility of
the colonies of this time was altogether astonishing. Two hundred years had
not passed from the Flood when swarms of so many people overflowed everywhere
that various kings, even while Noah lived, could lead more than a million
warriors against the enemy—if the things many authors report about the armies
of Ninus, Zoroaster, and the Acabians are credible, although others deny so great a
number.

Babylonia—western Assyria—and all Arabia became the seat of Ham’s firstborn
son Cush. Egypt fell to the second, Mizraim; Libya and the rest of Africa’s
coast to the third, Phut; and the later home of the Jews to the fourth,
Canaan. For Canaan’s sons were the Jebusite, Sidon, the Amorite, and others
whom the Jews expelled. The Philistines who inhabited Canaan’s coast were
Mizraim’s descendants.

Japheth had varied offspring. His son Magog—or his descendants—founded a city
of his name, once called Hierapolis and now Aleppo, whose ruler is customarily
called Gog in Scripture. Magog with his brothers Meshech and Tubal traveled
north and widely spread their progeny not only in Iberia but also beyond the
Black Sea into Tartary and Muscovy. The fourth brother, Tiras, is celebrated
as father of the Thracians. The fifth, Gomer, with his sons Ashkenaz and
Togarmah, is thought to have founded colonies that ultimately occupied all
Germany, Gaul, Poland, and Britain. The sixth, Javan, was the surest progenitor
of the Ionians, from whom the Greeks first and later the Italians drew their
origins. The Spanish owe their origin to Africa.

Slightly fewer than one hundred years after the Flood, when by the power of
the divine blessing the number of mortals grew beyond belief, fathers
everywhere sent out swarms of young men to seek new homes. The most famous of
these colonies was the one which settled in Mesopotamia under Nimrod, Ham’s
grandson and Cush’s son, not far from where the Tigris mingles its waters with
the Euphrates. At that place they then began to build a tower of immense
height against the force of the waters should it ever again assail them. It
was called Babel from the ensuing confusion: *Ba* and *Bel* come from *Balal*,
“he confused,” or from the simple *Balal* with its first root letter doubled.
God could easily have checked the impious boldness by fire from above or a
chasm in the earth below, but he disrupted their effort without any violence.
He confused the language, which had previously been one, and divided it into
seventy-two, the number some maintain are the mother tongues in the world.
Curbed by this easy rein, they were forced not only to cease attacking heaven
with their insane substructures but also to dissolve their compact and
disperse themselves voluntarily into various regions of the earth as chance
and the fellowship of a common language required. Nimrod seized rule over
those who chose homes beside the mass of the begun tower; whether they were
willing is uncertain, but it certainly happened by God’s singular providence.
The Lord saw that the mild paternal rule over households, the only government
from the world’s beginning, had given crimes great opportunity. He therefore
chose to restrain the wicked’s wantonness by harsher bonds, adding the new
dominions of kings and monarchs to the fathers’ ancient power.

This Nimrod is said to have founded the Chaldean monarchy in the year of the
world 1,782, and it lasted almost two thousand years. At first, like all
things, it was very small. We do not read that Nimrod ruled more widely than a
few cities he built on the Tigris and Euphrates. His son Belus extended the
empire’s boundaries farther, but his grandson Ninus, more certainly a monarch,
subdued everything widely around him. Setting out into Bactria with an army
of 1.5 million, he is said to have defeated Zoroaster, king of the Bactrians
and that famous Magus. There Semiramis is said first to have come to his
notice. She had followed her husband Memnon, governor of Coele-Syria, into the
army and taught King Ninus, then besieging the Bactrian capital, a stratagem
by which the otherwise almost impregnable city finally came into his power.
Captivated at once by the woman’s intelligence and beauty, the emperor ordered
her husband to surrender her to him and threatened to tear out his eyes if he
refused. He complied, but in grief threw himself headlong into the waters.

Ninus was no more unjust toward men than impious toward God, setting up his
father Belus’s image for worship. Before him we do not know certainly who made
war or committed open adultery. But as the first inventor and author of
idolatry, wars, and adultery, he soon paid the penalty to his now-wife
Semiramis. If we believe the Greeks, she asked her husband for supreme

power for five days. When he granted it, on the first day she invited the
nobles to a solemn banquet; on the second she ordered her husband arrested
and killed; in the remaining days she secured the empire for herself while
her son Ninus grew up. Having arranged this, she immediately girded herself
for manly tasks. Nimrod had laid the foundations of Babylon, Nineveh, and many
other cities. Ninus had undertaken to enlarge Nineveh for himself by the
Tigris, and Semiramis Babylon for herself by the Euphrates. Ninus brought the
former’s walls to such height and thickness that they would surpass belief
had we not read that his wife surpassed him in Babylon’s walls. Nor was it
enough for the high-spirited virago to conquer a man in one thing: she wished
to do so in everything, or at least in the chief glory of military virtue.
After subduing Arabia, Egypt, and Persia, she set out for India with the
greatest force ever known or likely ever to be known, if Xerxes’ forces alone
are excepted: three million infantry, one hundred thousand chariots armed with
scythes, and as many armed camels. She is said to have lost these incredible
forces and been defeated by Staurobates on the Indus. She was Philistine by
birth, born at Ashkelon from a secret liaison; consequently exposed by her
mother, raised first by birds and then shepherds, and finally married by the
provincial governor because of her singular beauty.

The Chaldean monarchs’ virtue appears to have died with her. Apart from two,
both named Balaeus, all who followed degenerated into women. We read nothing
else of any of them than that they spent their days in women’s quarters among
flocks of concubines. At last, after enduring this for many centuries, the
provincial governors Arbaces of Media and Belochus of Babylon rose against
the half-men emperors and took up arms. The ruler then was not a man but a
pig, Sardanapalus, thirty-eighth from Nimrod. Broken in battle, he brought his
treasures, concubines, and everything dear to him into one place and cast it
all with himself onto a burning pyre so that it would not come into the
victors’ hands.

The empire was afterward divided. Arbaces the Assyrian took Media with his
descendants, and Belochus Babylon and Nineveh with the title of empire.
Belochus’s descendant Shalmaneser led the ten tribes into perpetual captivity.
Shalmaneser’s son was Sennacherib, whose history is known from Scripture.
While Sennacherib’s parricidal sons struggled for the kingdom, the Babylonian
Merodach arose, defeated all of them, and seized the empire for himself and
his descendants. His grandson was the great Nebuchadnezzar, of whom Scripture
says so much. He had a son, Evilmerodach, who appears to have worshiped the
true God under Daniel’s instruction, but a grandson, Belshazzar, so impious
that by a human hand on the wall God signified his wish to break off in him
the monarchy that had stood so long.

Before we descend to Persian history, it is worth observing the condition of
other nations while the Assyrians held the world’s reins. There were only four
peoples about whom it is possible to speak: Jews, Greeks, Egyptians, and
Romans. Lack and silence of writers wrapped the others in darkness and buried
them in the grave of oblivion. You read the Jews’ affairs every day in the
sacred canon. What certain things we possess concerning Egypt are so few that
they can be passed over without loss. We therefore intend to linger only on
Greeks and Romans.

Greek antiquities are covered by so many wrappings of fables that a mind which
loves naked truth is weary of examining them. Yet everyone who wishes
profitably to read any human writings, old or new, sacred or profane, must
sample these histories enveloped in fables. From the great heap, therefore,
we have selected a few of the following things as a foretaste.

It is certain that even before Abraham some sort of kings flourished in the
peninsula once called the Peloponnese and now Morea, among the Sicyonians and
Argives. Pelasgus, Prometheus, and the sons Deucalion and Atlas were not
younger than Moses. Pelasgus is granted the kingdom of Arcadia for teaching
people to build little houses against the weather’s injuries and grind acorns
into flour easy to eat. Prometheus received a worse reward for a better
invention: he formed men from mud and animated the figures with fire stolen
from heaven. He imbued the then-barbarous breasts of the Greeks with the
precepts of heavenly wisdom. For this, bound on the Caucasus by jealous Jove
with an adamantine chain, he is eaten by a vulture that devours his
ever-renewed liver. On the highest mountain’s summit, subtle and anxious
contemplation of heaven and the stars was consuming his dear heart.

Atlas devoted much effort to the same study on the Africans’ highest
mountain, on which the support of heaven is imagined to stand lest it fall.
His brother Deucalion in Thessaly, when a sudden flood swallowed the others,
preserved himself safely with his wife Pyrrha by reaching Parnassus’s ridges
in a little boat.

Apollo caused almost as much evil in various places of Italy and Africa as
Neptune did in Thessaly when he made his rays burn hotter than usual. Phaethon,
a petty king in Italy, is celebrated as the cause of this heat because by
astrological skill he predicted its coming long before.

In Moses’ same age Cadmus came from Egypt to Greece, bringing letters with him
and founding Thebes. A few years earlier Cecrops came from the same Egypt to

I pass swiftly over the cartloads of trifles about gods, goddesses, and their
heroes that the lying petty Greeks heap up. Yet lest we seem excessively
fastidious, we shall not be reluctant to relate a few things about the three
Greek expeditions the poets praise most highly: the Argonautic, Theban, and
Trojan.

This is given as the occasion of the Argonautic expedition. Deucalion’s
grandson, who reigned at Iolcus in Thessaly, had many children. The three sons
of chief name were Athamas, Cretheus, and Salmoneus. When their father died,
Athamas’s children, fearing their stepmother, fled beyond the Black Sea to
Chalcis with their father’s stolen treasure, which received the name “golden
fleece” because the ship carrying it had a ram as its emblem. The kingdom
passed to Cretheus. But when he died prematurely and his son
Aeson was likewise untimely removed from the living, while his grandson Jason
was too young to rule, Pelias, an illegitimate descendant of the third
brother Salmoneus, occupied it. He decided not to relinquish the kingdom once
seized, although Jason, the legitimate heir, came of age. To make this safer,
he tried by every device to remove Jason.

While sacrificing, he asked a god from whom he should fear the greatest
danger and was answered: from the man who met him unshod. As Pelias returned
home, Jason met him with one foot bare, for as he hastened to the uncle who
had summoned him he had left one shoe stuck in deep mud along the road.
Mindful of the oracle, Pelias asked his nephew, “What would you do to the man
of whom a god warned you to beware?” “Send him to Colchis,” Jason said, “to
recover the golden fleece.” “Then you,” Pelias replied, “set out there to
bring back your ancestral treasure.” The high-spirited young man accepted the
condition. News of the distant expedition gathered all the strongest men in
Greece as companions. Castor and Pollux, Hercules and Theseus, Telamon and
Peleus, Zethes and Calais—the winged offspring of Boreas—and another great
company of heroes appeared at once. A ship was built and named *Argo* after
its builder. Tiphys sat at the helm; Orpheus, armed with his lyre, among the
benches; and each prince drove an oar with his own hand.

They came first to Lemnos. By a common decree the women had recently killed
every man found on the island so that they could live thereafter like Amazons.
Their queen Hypsipyle received Jason into both home and bed. She was soon
forced into exile when it was discovered that she had rescued her father
Thoas from the common destruction. After leaving Lemnos, Hercules broke his
oar as he drove his arms more strongly. He therefore went with his beloved
Hylas into the nearby Mysian forest to obtain a new one. Faint from the day’s
heat and the journey’s labor, he ordered Hylas to bring a water jar for drink
from the nearby river. Hylas obeyed, but while drawing water dropped the jar.
As he tried to seize it, he fell into the river, was swallowed by its waves,
and carried off by nymphs. While Hercules delayed in searching for him, the
Argonauts continued their journey. Hercules, however, skirted every shore and
called continually for Hylas until he came upon a very beautiful maiden bound
on the Phrygian shore to become a huge sea monster’s meal. She was Hesione,
daughter of Laomedon king of Troy.

Neptune and Apollo had once built Troy’s walls for Laomedon at an agreed
price, which he neglected to pay the gods when their work was done. They
therefore afflicted the city with plague and neighboring lands with floods
and did not withhold their hand until the Trojans covenanted to offer each
year as a solemn sacrifice to Neptune whichever virgin the lot demanded.
Hesione’s first lot would have been fatal if Hercules had not brought
unexpected help as if from a machine. He declared himself ready to fight the
whale if Laomedon established immortal horses together with his daughter as
the prize of victory. The king accepted the condition. Hercules went down to
the sand, killed the whale, and demanded the agreed rewards, but Laomedon
openly broke his pledge. Hercules was unwilling to compel him immediately,
but returned several years later with an army from Greece, destroyed Troy,
killed Laomedon, gave Hesione as wife to Telamon, who first leapt onto Troy’s
walls, and took Priam captive, though he later sent him home, purchased with
gold.

Meanwhile the Argonauts landed on the shores of the Thracian Bosporus, met
King Phineus, and consulted him about the rest of their journey. He humbly
sought the heroes’ help against the Harpies’ continual injuries. To please
his later wife Phineus had blinded his children by the former. As punishment
for his crimes, Jupiter not only deprived the old man of his eyes but also
sent avenging Harpies, which always either defiled or plundered his table as
soon as it was laid. The rapacious maidens flew in before the heroes’ eyes.
The sons of Boreas were not content to drive them away with swords but pursued
them aloft in the air, overcame them, and forced them to promise they would
never again cause Phineus trouble.

The old man then returned their service by faithfully warning them of the
great danger of the Symplegades. He taught them that the Cyanean rocks were
near, between which Neptune flowed through so narrow a strait and the sea
rolled in so rapid a whirlpool that it was scarcely passable by any art or
force. He advised that when they arrived they send a dove ahead; if it flew
through, they should follow,

but if the winds repelled it they should turn back. They obediently followed
his warning. The dove sent ahead passed safely, losing only a few tail
feathers. The eager Argonauts followed, bending to the oars with all the
strength of their limbs, and left the Symplegades behind without any harm
apart from slight scraping of the rudder against the rocks’ sides.

After finishing many labors, they finally reached Colchis. Jason approached
King Aeetes and explained the cause of his coming. Aeetes answered that the
fleece could not be obtained without the dust and heat of new labors: Jason
must yoke bronze-footed, fire-breathing bulls and sow serpent’s teeth.
Moreover, the fleece hung on an oak in Mercury’s grove, guarded by a huge
snake that remained vigilant day and night. “If your heart is set on these
desperate dangers,” he said, “you have my permission to approach, seize, and
carry home the golden fleece you desire so much.” The monsters did not
frighten Jason, especially after counsel from Aeetes’ daughter Medea. Falling
madly in love with Jason at first sight, she disclosed her affection, demanded
a pledge of marriage, and promised as dowry a safe route to the fleece by her
arts. Jason drank Medea’s words with open ears. He was bathed in magic oil.
Protected by it against the bulls’ fiery breath, he boldly mastered them,
imposed the yoke, turned the fields with the plow, and cast Cadmus’s teeth into
the furrows. An armed crowd immediately sprang from the seed. By throwing a
few stones he set them against one another and watched them fall to earth,
pierced by mutual wounds. He continued into the grove, put the snake to sleep
by incantation or drug, took the desired fleece from the oak, and joyfully
placed it in the ship.

Jupiter did not wish this to be the end of the evils. Aeetes formed a plan to
slaughter his guests amid the sacred rites of the meal, but Medea warned Jason
of the danger. She urged flight by night and offered to accompany him. Whether
he wished or not, she carried her brother Absyrtus with her onto the *Argo*.
There was no delay: sails were given to the wind, oars extended, and waves
crossed swiftly. As soon as Aeetes learned of his guests’ and daughter’s
flight, he immediately called for ships and pursued with all possible speed,
coming very near to overtaking them by force of oars. Medea did not fail now.
She instantly tore her brother apart and scattered pieces of his mutilated
body here and there through the sea. While the stunned father gathered them,
the *Argo* escaped.
