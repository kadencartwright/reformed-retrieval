### QUESTION IX

#### Does the Fall of the Very Great Cities Nineveh and Babylon Belong to This Epoch?

The greatest cities tend to meet the most disastrous fates. I remember few in
ancient history which did not often groan under signal calamities; indeed,
most now lie buried in their own ruins, if even ruins survive. Easily foremost
among the ancient cities are Nineveh and Babylon, the royal seats of the
Assyrian and Chaldean monarchs; the greatest authors dispute which was larger.
Diodorus Siculus, drawing upon Ctesias, whom Strabo follows, would have Nineveh
surpass Babylon by approximately twelve miles. He assigns Nineveh a circuit
of sixty miles: a three-day journey on foot, as the prophet Jonah has it, or
480 stadia. To Babylon, however, he assigns only 385 stadia. Herodotus assigns
Babylon not merely as many stadia as Diodorus assigns Nineveh, while Pausanias
in his *Arcadica*, or eighth book, explicitly prefers Babylon in size not only
to Nineveh, or Ninus, but also to every city the sun has ever seen. He flatly
opposes Diodorus and Strabo, who affirm that Nineveh was not merely much larger
than Babylon but proclaim it the largest of all cities that ever have been or
will be. Salian’s reconciliation does not resolve such open contradictions.
He distinguishes here between Babylon as founded by Semiramis and the same
city as enlarged by Nebuchadnezzar, as though the former were smaller than
Nineveh and the latter larger. But this distinction is empty, since the
authors speak about each city in its prime. Moreover, it is gratuitously
asserted, without any witness, that Nebuchadnezzar added anything at all to
Babylon’s old circuit, much less a full hundred stadia.

> Marginalia, printed p. 283: *Whether Nineveh was larger than Babylon*.

I do not, indeed, approve Diodorus’s hyperbole that Ninus built Nineveh on
such a scale that it surpassed not only every earlier city but also every city
yet to come. For apart from the fact that truth is not determined concerning
future contingencies, and `τὸ μέλλον`—what is to come—is `ἄδηλον`, unknown, to
every mortal, certainly, if more recent reports about Cambalou in Tartary,
Quinsei in China, or Cairo in Egypt are true, these cities appear greatly to
exceed Nineveh. In any event, it is beyond dispute that those very great
Assyrian cities, Nineveh and Babylon, surpassed every city the sun has ever
seen in hostility and cruelty toward God’s ancient people. For if Nineveh did
not destroy the

[Printed p. 284 | IA leaf 314 | access PDF 314 | OCR object 313]

ten tribes, it nevertheless led them into that captivity from which they have
not returned to the present day. Indeed, every mortal is ignorant where on
earth they now exist so that they might one day be brought back. Babylon,
however, oppressed the remaining two tribes with seventy years of the harshest
servitude and captivity, after the temple at Jerusalem and all the cities of
Judah had been utterly destroyed.

God, the surest and most severe avenger of his people’s calamities, announced
through the mouths of various prophets the final destruction and complete
extermination of each city for its oppression of Israel. The question is at
what time the Lord carried out the threats of Isaiah, Jeremiah, Nahum,
Zephaniah, and others against Babylon and Nineveh.

To reconcile the apparently contradictory opinions of both more recent and
ancient scholars, we must distinguish the different stages of these cities’
ruin, which occurred at different times. As regards Nineveh, it must at a
minimum have been overthrown three times. First, a few years after the prophet
Jonah, Sardanapalus, the last of the Assyrian monarchs—whom, if we believe
Salian or even Jerome, Jonah brought to repentance—relapsed into his former
sins and was deprived by Arbaces the Mede of both kingdom and life. Nineveh,
the seat of empire, was captured and leveled to the ground before it had
sinned at all against God’s people, whatever its crimes against God himself
may ultimately have been. Diodorus Siculus relates distinctly, after the
middle of his second book, that Nineveh was destroyed when Sardanapalus had
been killed and its citizens were dispersed by Arbaces among the neighboring
villages.

> Marginalia, printed p. 284: *Nineveh first destroyed by Arbaces*.

After this ruin Nineveh must have been rebuilt, for sacred literature
testifies that at least five rulers—if not monarchs, certainly kings of
Assyria—held power there: namely, Pul, Tiglath-pileser, Shalmaneser,
Sennacherib, and Esarhaddon. Undeterred by the fate of Sardanapalus, these men
left nothing undone in exercising the utmost cruelty toward the Israelites,
until they had first exterminated the ten tribes and mingled them with the
nations in the mountains of Media. These new kings of Assyria occupied parts
of Media, which was not difficult for them because, after Arbaces had granted
the Medes their liberty, that people, dissolved into a democracy for many
years, could not protect their own possessions. At last Dejoces resumed the
government of the kingdom over willing subjects. His grandson Cyaxares the
First, father of Astyages—as Herodotus has it in book 1—destroyed the Assyrian
kingdom and overthrew Nineveh a second time, near the period in which Byzas of
Megara laid the first foundations of Byzantium, or Constantinople. Many make
this Cyaxares Nebuchadnezzar’s father-in-law. In the siege and second overthrow
of Nineveh, he had as his ally his son-in-law Nebuchadnezzar, the husband of
his daughter Nitocris, sister of Astyages.

> Marginalia, printed p. 284: *A second time by Cyaxares*.

Herodotus, the only ancient witness I find to this second fall of Nineveh,
passes over so illustrious an event in only one word, promising elsewhere that
he will relate the history fully. He either never fulfilled that promise, or
this writing of his has been lost among very many other works of reputable
authors.

Both Petavius and Salian labor greatly here to reconcile Tobit’s anachronistic
fables with true chronology. They are compelled to invent a new
Nebuchadnezzar who ruled over the Medes after Esarhaddon and was nevertheless
the same as Dejoces the Mede, the builder of Ecbatana. These are putrid
offenses against chronology as well as history.

> Marginalia, printed p. 284: *There was no third destruction of Nineveh*.

Besides these two destructions of Nineveh, a third, if not more, appears to
have to be posited. In his *Notes on Eusebius*, p. 74, Joseph Scaliger
confidently affirms from Ammianus Marcellinus that Nineveh survived long after
Nebuchadnezzar’s time; the most learned Bochart likewise affirms it from
Philostratus in his *Phaleg*, book 4, chapter 20. If these things are true, we
must maintain that Nineveh was founded a third time so that it might be
destroyed a third time, for no one doubts that it is destroyed today. In his
*Pilgrimage*, book 1, chapter 14, Purchas introduces the eyewitnesses Sherley
and Cartwright, who viewed Nineveh’s ruins, as did Aitonius Armonius long
before, cited earlier in Bochart, where he testifies that he saw this city
wholly desolate. But among all the authors who have come into my hands, I find
profound silence about the person by whom it was either founded or devastated
a third time. I therefore strongly suspect that Bochart and Scaliger are both
mistaken when they would have Nineveh flourish even in Roman times. Certainly
Lucian—an exceedingly learned and sufficiently reliable authority in such
matters, equal to Ammianus and Philostratus, if not greater in both age and
authority—affirms in his dialogue *The Observers* that Nineveh had been so
thoroughly devastated in his day that no trace of it remained and that no
mortal then knew for certain where on earth it had once stood. Cyril of
Alexandria, who was close to that period, affirms the same thing on Nahum 2,
and Pausanias in book 8 also professes the same opinion.

As for Ammianus, the three passages Bochart cites from him seem rightly
capable of being interpreted as referring to Nineveh, which was once the
greatest and most celebrated of cities, rather than to any city existing in
Julian’s day. After the Chaldean monarchy’s overthrow, nearly every author is
silent about such an existing city, while much testimony to the contrary can
also be adduced.

Philostratus, however, has no authority with any sensible person. I therefore
wonder that the most learned Bochart should wish with such confidence to prove
any conclusion from this most mendacious writer. Baronius notes various errors
in Philostratus, and Possevinus does so after him, but any attentive reader
will observe many more exceedingly gross lies. I acknowledge that Apollonius
of Tyana, whose life constitutes Philostratus’s entire book, was a notable
magician, since he did not conceal his frequent conversations with demons.
Nevertheless, the many things Philostratus relates about him as though they
had been handed down to him by Apollonius’s servant Damis are of precisely the
same stamp as the magical fables in Apuleius’s *Golden Ass*. It is as though
Apollonius himself, without any study or labor, knew not only all languages,
even those of birds, fish, and beasts, and knew future things of every kind
with absolute certainty, but an entire nation in India neighboring the
Brahmans also knew Greek perfectly and could address people wholly unknown to
them by their own names the moment they first saw them. Let us believe without
scruple that Apollonius and even a crowd of Indian Magi raised many truly dead
people at their pleasure, much less cured every fatal disease. Let us also
grant that little bronze men placed dishes prepared by demons upon the table
with their own hands, and that those little men of bronze or iron performed
the entire service of the dining room. Yet I no more trust either Damis or
Philostratus for the existence of ancient Nineveh in Domitian’s time than I
trust that pepper and spices grow in great quantity on the rocks of the
Caucasus and are carried down into the valleys by the labor of monkeys; that
the Caucasus extends to the shore of the Persian Sea; or that there are
peoples in Arabia and Persia who, although they do not approach the Tropic of
Cancer, can see neither the pole nor even the Bear itself. Who would place
faith in Philostratus on any subject when he blunders so disgracefully in
astronomy, geography, and physics, to say nothing of theology? This Lemnian,
if any writer among the ancients deserves the name, is certainly a most
worthless author, teeming with errors of every kind.

> Marginalia, printed p. 285: *Philostratus full of lies*.

Bochart’s invention of two Ninevehs, each existing in the days of Philostratus
and Ammianus, appears very poorly founded. Apart from the most mendacious
Philostratus, I recall no author who places any ancient Ninus west of the
Euphrates. On p. 62 of his *Itinerary*, Benjamin of Tudela records that he saw
the very splendid city of Mosul on the western bank of the Tigris. Opposite it,
across the river to the east, were villages and hamlets where ancient Ninus
was believed once to have stood. No more about Nineveh. Neither its third
foundation nor its third destruction is in evidence. Its first and second
destructions belong to the last period of the first monarchy, not at all to
the epoch now before us or to any later one.

> Marginalia, printed p. 285: *Bochart’s error*.

Babylon experienced various turns of fortune until at last all the threats
seen by the prophets, and everything God had formerly threatened against it,
came down upon it in full. Babylon’s first captivity began when Darius the
Mede, or Cyaxares the Second, the maternal uncle and father-in-law of Cyrus,
captured the city by force and skill. At that time, after Belshazzar had been
slain and the other deeds had been done which a furious soldiery customarily
perpetrates in a city subdued by arms after a long and difficult siege, it was
plundered and forever cast down from every imperial dignity. Everything in
the walls and the public and private buildings nevertheless remained intact,
until under Darius Hystaspes the second stage of Babylon’s misery occurred.

> Marginalia, printed p. 285: *Babylon’s first calamity*.

For at the time when Zerubbabel, by Darius’s favor, was restoring Jerusalem
and the temple, Babylon felt the great and deserved fury of that same Darius
arising from divine wrath. Rebelling against Darius, the Babylonians fortified
their city for a siege and, to make their provisions last, strangled the
greater part of their own women with their own hands. How many were strangled
may be conjectured from the fact that, once the city was captured, Darius had
to procure for the Babylonians no fewer than fifty thousand women brought
from the surrounding country in order to replenish their offspring. They
suffered many evils during the eighteen-month siege. But when through Zopyrus’s
devices the city came into Darius’s hands, besides the slaughter and plunder
carried out by the force of the soldiers breaking in, Darius leveled all the
walls of Semiramis—one of the wonders of the world—to the ground and reduced
the city to a village. Herodotus and Diodorus Siculus relate these things at
length.

> Marginalia, printed p. 285: *The second*.

Nor did God’s wrath against Babylon stop here. Darius’s son Xerxes not only
stripped the temple of Bel of its most precious ornaments, but also destroyed
that immense tower, a work of Nimrod or something still more ancient, which
had begun to be built at the confusion of languages. For when Alexander the
Great came to Babylon, he found this enormous structure buried in its own
ruins.

After Xerxes, under the Persian monarchy, Babylon recovered much of its former
splendor. Thus, at the beginning of the Macedonian empire, it is described by
Curtius in book 5 as a very flourishing city, although reduced to a third or
fourth part of what it had once been. Of its former 300 or 480 stadia, no more
than ninety were then built up; the rest had given way to gardens, fields,
and game preserves.

> Marginalia, printed p. 285: *The third*.

What Aristotle has in *Politics* 2.3 about Babylon’s size being approximately
three times equal to the Peloponnese was never true, much less in Alexander’s
time.

But the fourth and most lethal wound was inflicted upon Babylon by Seleucus
Nicator, the divine hand directing his heart toward the total ruin of that
ruling city without weapons or any violence. Alexander had resolved to restore
Babylon’s entire former expanse. The location’s supreme pleasantness and the
fertility of the neighboring fields—where the wine was the very nectar of
antiquity and the grain sometimes yielded a three-hundredfold increase and
commonly a two-hundredfold—so delighted his mind that he contemplated placing
the royal seat of his empire here. Death, however, broke the unfinished thread
of this and all his other designs. Seleucus long fixed the metropolis of his
great kingdom at Babylon. Eventually, however, at the confluence of the Tigris
and Euphrates, ninety or at least forty milestones south of Babylon, he built
Seleucia and established there a colony of six hundred thousand citizens, a
large portion of them Babylonians.

> Marginalia, printed p. 286: *The fourth, from the rise of Seleucia*.

Once this had been done, as Pliny in book 6, Strabo in book 16, and Pausanias
in book 1 rightly note, Babylon was soon desolated and reduced to a wilderness.
Jerome affirms on Isaiah 13:14 that he learned from an eyewitness, a Persian
monk, that Babylon had nothing except its ancient walls. These served as an
enclosure in which the king of the Parthians’ lions, boars, and other wild
animals intended for the royal hunt were confined. Theodoret, a little after
Jerome, testifies in his works on Isaiah and Jeremiah that the city was
entirely desolate in his day, having no Assyrian and no Chaldean inhabitant,
but only a few Jews. In his *Charon*, Lucian likewise proclaims Babylon’s utter
desolation as though he were prophesying it.

Although the most learned Bochart cites these things, I do not know how it
happens that he places such abundant trust in the most fabulous and mendacious
Lemnian magician Philostratus, who pretends that in Domitian’s day the king of
the Parthians, Vardanes, had his court at Babylon, then still a most glorious
city. Certainly, in that age the kings of the Parthians—as the Greeks had done
before when they were in the south—wintered at Seleucia and, to escape that
city’s enormous commotion, in the neighboring village Ctesiphon, which itself
soon grew into a large and very strongly fortified city.

But among the true historians of those times, so far as I recall, there is not
a word about Babylon. Anyone who wishes may examine the expeditions of Trajan,
Gordian, Julian, and other emperors against the Parthians: amid frequent
mention of Nisibis, Ctesiphon, and many other Assyrian cities, he will always
find profound silence concerning Babylon.

It is true that in Saracen history and the more recent history of the Turks a
caliph and pasha of Babylon are frequently mentioned, but this Babylon is
entirely different from our ancient city. It is the Saracen Baghdad, which
Almanzor built by the Tigris upon the remains of Seleucia, three milestones
from Ctesiphon. As Seleucia was once distinguished among many by the name
Babylon, because Nicator had filled it with Babylon’s citizens, so Baghdad
later, upon Seleucia’s ruins, likewise received even the name Babylon from very
many. But no scholar now doubts that Baghdad is as distant from ancient
Babylon as Seleucia once was, namely at least thirty-eight miles.

> Marginalia, printed p. 286: *The Turks’ Baghdad is not ancient Babylon*.

Although Baghdad is now the seat of a Turkish pasha, it is far removed from
the splendor in which Benjamin of Tudela saw it five hundred years ago. For
since that time it has often been badly mauled and almost devastated, at one
time by the Tartars, at another by the Saracens themselves, at another by
Christians, and at another by the Turks. Nevertheless, it is still a large
city and something like the capital of all Assyria. Ancient Babylon, however,
has for many centuries been entirely buried.

What Pausanias, Jerome, and others report concerning its walls and the other
remains surviving from the city must be understood of new ones built by an
unknown person. For Darius Hystaspes overthrew Semiramis’s old bricks bound
together with bitumen. And what some authors record about the tower and tomb
of Bel cannot be true, given that Xerxes destroyed that tower together with
the other Chaldean temples. When Benjamin passed this way, he testifies that
he saw Babylon’s ruins, with nothing existing among them except the remains
of Nebuchadnezzar’s palace; these had been transformed into the lairs of so
many serpents and savage beasts that no one dared approach. After him, Texeira,
traveling from India to Spain, records—as Bochart cites him—that those remains
of Babylon were more desolate than any place in all Mesopotamia. Theodoret
certainly affirms that by his day the Euphrates had abandoned its former
course, and that only a slender stream from it passed through Babylon’s ruins.

> Marginalia, printed p. 286: *Babylon’s ruins do not survive today*.

Joseph Scaliger does not explain what he means in his *Notes on Eusebius*, p.
318, when he asserts that ancient Babylon survived, inhabited by many
thousands of Jews, beyond the year of Christ 1100; nor can I attain it by any
conjecture.

In any event, from the foregoing I answer the question as follows. The first,
second, and third stages of Babylon’s fall, under Cyrus, Darius, and Xerxes,
belong not to our epoch but to the early period of the Persian empire. The
fourth occurred at the beginning of our epoch, when Nicator transferred the
Babylonian people to Seleucia. But the fifth and final stage of its ruin can
be referred to no

fixed time, for without any human violence, by God’s hand alone, the city
gradually vanished into bare walls with a few buildings, and from there into
mere ruins and rubble, until for several centuries even the ruins themselves
could scarcely be recognized with certainty by passersby. The things which
certain travelers in Purchas, book 1, chapter 20, took to be the ruins of
ancient Babylon cannot be those ruins, since they lie nearly halfway along the
road between Baghdad, or Seleucia, and ancient Babylon.
