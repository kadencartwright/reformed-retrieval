# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 18.* Romans 7:6: *But now we have been freed from the law.*

*Answer.* The commentators commonly say that the apostle explains more
clearly here what he had said above: namely, that sin does not have dominion
over us because we are no longer under law, but under grace. He does this with
the most suitable and beautiful example of marriage. Consult Marlorat on the
passage. Bullinger sets out the whole matter as follows: [*"We have been joined
to sin as to a domineering and loathsome husband. While he has not yet been
destroyed, we are not free from the law. But those who have crucified the
flesh with its affections are dead to sin, so that they are now also free from
the law and can therefore marry Christ, who has destroyed that most dreadful
beast of sin, so that mortals, freed from its companionship or tyranny, may be
joined to Christ himself as their kind and most beautiful bridegroom."*] And
Beza explains the matter clearly in this way on verse 4: [*"That is,"* he
says, *"the thing in us which, because of the law's prohibition, was becoming
more and more sinful has died; or, that former husband in us, whom he
describes afterward, has now lost those powers which he seized from the law
in order to rule you at his pleasure, as a husband rules a wife. To be put to
death to sin and to be put to death to the law correspond mutually to one
another—not absolutely, however, but in a certain respect: namely, insofar as
the law is the power of sin. In this respect too we are said not to be under
law, as we explained above on 6:14. In precisely that sense those who are
under law are said to be under sin, while those who are not under law are no
longer under sin, because sin no longer has dominion over them as regenerate
people, but the later husband has been brought in after the death of the
former husband. For the law itself cannot properly be said to be dead in us,
or indeed to be dead or rendered void even in itself, whenever the law of the
Decalogue is under discussion, as in this passage. For apart from the fact
that the law is something placed outside us, while sin is within us, below in
verse 20, the law considered absolutely in itself is so far from being dead
in those in whom sin is dead that, on the contrary, it is truly effectual by
its nature only in those whom it finds made fit through the Holy Spirit to
will and to do what God commands through it. Therefore the apostle said above
in 3:31 that the law is not rendered void through faith, but rather
established."*] And after some further remarks: [*"It should therefore be
known*

*that both the husband and the wife are considered in one and the same person
in each marriage. The apostle therefore rightly and properly said that those
who are to be regenerated are dead to the former, dead husband, since he is
not something outside the unregenerate, but lives and reigns within them, so
that they are wholly fleshly."*] Hence it is also clear from the accompanying
diagram that by *law* nothing else is to be understood here than that sin
which sins, which moves the affections of the unregenerate toward sin by the
occasion taken through the law, by which it was of course provoked. It is also
clear that being under law and being under the dominion of sin are corollaries
of each other. We have therefore been freed from the law, not absolutely, but
in a certain respect: namely, insofar as it provokes to sin, as may be seen in
verses 11, 13, and 14. See also Piscator on the passage. This is confirmed from
verse 5: *For when we were in the flesh, the sinful passions which arose
through the law were at work in our members to bear fruit unto death.* Here,
*to be in the flesh*, and *to have sinful passions arising through the law
and at work to bear fruit unto death*, are set in opposition to freedom from
the law and signify the condition of the first marriage. The later marriage,
or the condition of the later marriage, must therefore indicate what is
opposed to the former. To be freed from the law, then, is to be freed from
that flesh which had the power of the law in our corrupt members. [*"To be in
the flesh,"* Bullinger says on the passage, *"is the same as to be under sin
or under law: namely, to be driven by the impulse of the flesh and to live
according to the disposition of the flesh."*] Nor should this be omitted: in
verse 4 he does not say that the law was put to death for us—ἐθανατώθη ὑμῖν
ὁ νόμος—but ἐθανατώθητε τῷ νόμῳ, *you were put to death to the law.*
Nor does he say in verse 6 that the law was rendered empty or void for us, but
that we were freed, although the logic of the analogy would have required the
other application. Whether this was done to avoid hostility, so that he would
not offend the Jews by such a manner of speaking, as many suspect, or because,
as Beza holds, both husband and wife are considered in one and the same person
in each marriage, this much is certain: he is not speaking here about the law
considered absolutely, but with respect to its threats and provocations,
through which sin has dominion in those not yet regenerate and becomes more
and more sinful. It is therefore evident that we are not freed from the
directive and commanding force of the law when we are said to be freed from
the law and put to death to it. Furthermore, the context abundantly supports
this interpretation. For what else is meant by those words in verse 11, *sin,
taking occasion through that commandment, deceived me and by it killed me*,
and in verse 23, *I see another law in my members warring against the law of
my mind and bringing me into captivity to the law of sin which is in my
members*?
