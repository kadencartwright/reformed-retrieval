The power and glory of Alexander’s successors were now declining, while those
of the Romans were rising. After Pyrrhus and the Carthaginians, the Romans had
dealings with Philip of Macedon; while he stood firm, they dealt moderately
enough with Antiochus. After a reign of many years Antigonus Gonatas left his
son Demetrius as his successor. When Alexander, the son of Pyrrhus, had
deprived Antigonus of Macedonia by the desertion of his soldiers, Demetrius,
though still very young, drove Alexander not only out of Macedonia but also
out of Epirus. Yet in adulthood he performed nothing worthy of his youthful
reputation for courage. His wife was the daughter of Antiochus Hierax;
Alexander, the son of Pyrrhus, had married his own sister Olympias. Two sons
and several daughters were born from this incestuous marriage. After
Alexander’s death, when Olympias was hard-pressed in war by the Aetolians, she
sought aid from Demetrius of Macedon and gave him her daughter Pythia in
marriage. This second marriage so offended Demetrius’s first wife that she
fled complaining to her father Antiochus. A little later, both Alexander’s
sons died of consumption, their mother Olympias of grief, and indeed all the
rest of Pyrrhus’s posterity perished.

Demetrius too died after a reign of twelve years, leaving a very young son,
Philip, and appointing his brother Antigonus as the boy’s guardian. Antigonus
was surnamed Doson because he was sparing with gifts in the present but lavish
with promises for the future. This brave and prudent man governed the
Macedonian state well. When he wished to exchange the title of guardian for
that of king, the people rebelled. He immediately placed the diadem and the
whole government in the people’s hands, urging them to give it to whomever
they pleased, for he would most willingly surrender it. By this timely
submission he soothed the mutinous people so thoroughly that they killed the
authors of the rebellion and restored to him the full administration of the
kingdom according to his own pleasure. He repelled the Dardanians, a Thracian
people dwelling near Mount Haemus, who frequently broke into Macedonia, and
by fear of his arms restrained the Thessalians and Greeks when they were
preparing to revolt.

> Marginalia, printed p. 218: *Antigonus Doson, Philip’s guardian*.

But his greatest and most glorious campaign was against the Lacedaemonians.
After Alexander’s time, all the strength of the Greeks, divided into tiny
portions, was despised, since dynasties either of republics or of tyrannies
had arisen in nearly every

province and city. The powerful confederacy of the Aetolians, rapacious men who
inhabited the mountains, flourished for a long time and often troubled all its
neighbors. In the Peloponnese, the Achaeans flourished for a time after many
cities were joined into a commonwealth by the prudent industry of Aratus of
Sicyon and the great courage of Philopoemen. The Lacedaemonians and Athenians,
still proud of their ancient glory, wished neither to submit to anyone nor to
ally themselves with anyone voluntarily. Yet the Athenians had fallen to such
weakness that they possessed no more than three ships—and those uncovered—and
could not protect their property outside the walls against either pirates at
sea or robbers on land. The Lacedaemonians, meanwhile, had completely
consigned the ancient discipline of Lycurgus to oblivion. They numbered no
more than seven hundred citizens, of whom only one hundred possessed land;
the remaining poor, oppressed by the richer men, had laid aside their
ancestors’ noble spirit.

King Agis, a very brave young man aspiring to the ancient courage and glory,
strove with all his strength to restore the old discipline. But the other
king, Leonidas, who had been imbued with Asiatic customs at Antiochus’s court,
stood for the prevailing luxury. Agis renewed Lycurgus’s law that the lands
should be divided equally among all the citizens and debts forgiven. The
division of the lands and cancellation of debts was most welcome to the mass
of citizens, who had no land and very little money, but most unwelcome to the
richer men. Leonidas joined these men against Agis’s innovations. Agis’s
stronger faction drove Leonidas into exile and made Cleombrotus king in his
place. Agis’s counselors, deeply in debt, arranged for the debt records to be
canceled as quickly as possible, but the owners of many great estates delayed
the division of the lands. Then the people, disappointed in their hope of
possessing other men’s lands, rebelled again. They recalled Leonidas and
confined Agis and Cleombrotus in prison. Cleombrotus was driven into exile,
while Agis, his mother, and his grandmother—women of the highest
character—were strangled in prison by the ephors. Leonidas compelled Agis’s
wife to marry his son Cleomenes. The young man, of a mighty spirit and
captivated by ardent love for his wife, was drawn by her into her former
husband Agis’s efforts to reform the commonwealth. When his father had died,
Cleomenes secured himself upon the throne in one or two campaigns. He
slaughtered the ephors, canceled the records of debt, divided the lands
equally, restored life to Lycurgus’s statutes, granted citizenship to several
thousand brave men selected from the neighborhood, and formed a fairly large
army according to the ancient discipline. So often did he defeat the Achaeans
in great battles that they fled to Antigonus of Macedon. Cleomenes, often
victorious, dealt earnestly with the Achaeans so that they might be willing to
live at peace. He required nothing else from the defeated but that, in the
manner of their ancestors, they should grant the Lacedaemonians leadership of
the Greek military. Indeed, if the Achaeans had agreed to that most equitable
condition, which was also most honorable to the Greek name, Cleomenes came
very near completely restoring both the fallen fame and the fallen courage of
the Greeks. But Aratus’s most wicked ambition and his excessive authority
among the Achaeans drew the servile Macedonian yoke upon them in place of
Cleomenes’s rightful leadership. In the common council of the Achaeans,
Aratus persuaded them to summon Antigonus for aid against Cleomenes and to
deliver Acrocorinth—the true citadel of the entire Peloponnese—into his hands
as a great pledge of faith. Summoned from Macedonia, Antigonus arrived with an
enormous army. After a bloody battle, Cleomenes was overwhelmed by the number
of his enemies and fled in ships prepared beforehand to Egypt. There he was
kindly received by Ptolemy Euergetes, who promised both land and naval forces
with which he might be restored to his kingdom. But when the effeminate prince
Philopator came to the throne, this hope collapsed for Cleomenes. Although he
pressed for permission to return to his country even with his household
alone, he could not obtain it. Sosibius, governor of Philopator’s court,
observed Cleomenes’s keen spirit. He feared that if Cleomenes regained his
kingdom, he would turn his arms against Ptolemy, for he was thoroughly aware
of the Egyptians’ weakness. Therefore Sosibius, not daring to release him,
threw him into prison. The great-hearted man expected no further favor. So
that he would not grow old amid the prison’s filth, he drugged the guards with
wine and burst out with his companions into the streets of Alexandria,
calling the people to liberty. But when no one listened to his exhortation and
no one even dared launch an attack, he and his companions killed themselves.
Ptolemy fixed Cleomenes’s corpse to a cross and cruelly killed his wife,
mother, and children.

Antigonus was the first mortal to enter Sparta with victorious arms, almost
all its citizens having been killed in the battle. He allowed the few who
survived to live in their former liberty. Within very few days, a bloody
Illyrian invasion of Macedonia recalled Antigonus home. Thus, if Cleomenes had
delayed the battle for even three days, or had hidden with the remnants of his
army in Laconia for that long, the Lacedaemonian kingdom could have been
saved; but divine power rules over all human affairs.

> Marginalia, printed p. 219: *Doson’s great good fortune*.

Antigonus defeated the Illyrians in a great battle, but while he was urging
his soldiers on with excessive straining of his voice during the fight, a
vein burst and so much blood poured out that he died a few days later. He left
no children and left the kingdom to the one man to whom it belonged, Philip,
the son of Demetrius.

Illyria, today Dalmatia, a part of ancient Slavonia,

[Printed p. 220 | IA leaf 250 | access PDF 250 | OCR object 249]

was a kingdom between Italy and Greece, facing the Adriatic Sea. At the
instigation of Demetrius of Macedon, Agron, king of the Illyrians, sent a
large force into Aetolia. Falling unexpectedly upon the Aetolians, it returned
home after great slaughter and with enormous plunder. When Agron died, his
widow Teuta, enticed by the sweetness of plunder, sent out both land and naval
forces to rob everyone they encountered without distinction. When many Roman
subjects and allies brought complaints about the queen’s wrongs before the
senate, men were sent to Illyria to remonstrate with her and demand
reparations. She promised that she would observe the public peace, but said
that it was not the business of kings to answer for private injuries. One of
the envoys, Titus Coruncanus, said that it was the Roman custom to compel
kings to correct wicked customs and publicly redress crimes committed by
private persons. This frank speech pierced the proud woman’s heart so deeply
that she arranged for Coruncanus to be killed within Illyria’s borders. This
atrocious injury first drew the Romans outside Italy and into Illyria.

> Marginalia, printed pp. 219–220: *The Romans move against the Illyrians*.

Teuta had sent Demetrius of Pharos, a native of the island of Pharos near
Corcyra, with a fleet to seize Corcyra. The Aetolians and Achaeans sent aid to
the city. Demetrius defeated the fleets of both, took Corcyra, and besieged
Dyrrachium; the Roman fleet arrived.

Demetrius learned that the queen was angry with him and allied himself with
the Romans. The queen was easily defeated, yet she obtained peace from the
Romans, though on harsh terms: she must abstain entirely from the sea,
possess no decked ship, keep her kingdom within very narrow borders, make war
upon no one without the consent of the Roman Senate, and enter into a treaty
with no one. The Romans reported these things through envoys to the councils
of the Achaeans and Aetolians. They added that a great reason for their war
with Teuta had been to protect the Greeks from Illyrian injuries, confidently
hoping that they would be invited into Greece against the Macedonians, who
were oppressing many free cities under their harsh tyranny. But when the
envoys heard not a word from the Greeks about this, they returned. Yet what
was not ripe at present fell into the Romans’ bosom a few years later, when
the Greeks themselves eagerly summoned Roman arms into Greece.

The Aetolians, a people ignorant of peace, vexed all their neighbors,
especially the Achaeans, almost every year crossing the Corinthian Gulf into
the Peloponnese with a strong army. Aratus, almost perpetual praetor of the
Achaeans, was a man much more prudent than brave. Having several times been
badly mauled by the Aetolian soldier, he fled to the customary refuge, the
king of Macedonia. The king easily repelled the Aetolians, but Demetrius of
Pharos then came to him. The Romans had first made Demetrius king of most of
Illyria as his reward for betraying Teuta, and afterward, when he was detected
in a new betrayal against them, had driven him from that kingdom. When he came
to Philip, he insinuated himself into the young man’s confidence through the
art of flattery in which he excelled, filling him with the utterly vain hope
of defeating the Romans and even subjugating the entire West.

Philip did indeed flourish in his youth with a reputation for great virtues,
but afterward he dishonored that reputation when his concealed character
burst into the light. He gave poison to Aratus of Sicyon, foremost among his
friends. He carried the younger Aratus’s wife away to Macedonia, after also
driving her husband out of his mind with poison. He often extorted much money
from the Achaeans to support his soldier, and made a close alliance with
Hannibal while he was triumphing in Italy. But all Philip’s efforts were in
vain, for various disturbances in Greece diverted him from Italy. The
Carthaginians, while Hanno seethed with envy against the Barcid family, were
filled with fear that Hannibal, joined with Philip, would crush the liberty of
Carthage after defeating the Romans. They sent neither Hannibal nor Philip
the aid they had promised. As a result, each was subdued by the Romans, and
the Roman yoke was imposed upon Carthage.

While Hannibal was still in Italy, the Aetolians obtained a modest Roman force
against Philip. But Philip inflicted such defeats upon both the Aetolians and
the Romans that each gladly accepted peace from him, although this peace did
not last long.

Acarnania was dependent upon the Macedonians. Two young Acarnanian men at
Athens intruded into the Eleusinian rites, though they had not been initiated
into the mysteries of Ceres, out of mere ignorance. For this innocent error
both young men were put to death. To avenge their countrymen, the Acarnanians
ravaged Attica with fire and sword. At that time the Athenians had fallen to
such weakness that they were not even capable of waging war with the
Acarnanians; only their old ready instrument, the tongue, remained to them.
With a great outcry against Philip, the protector of Acarnania, they called
Ptolemy, the Aetolians, the Romans, and others to their aid. Invited into Asia
by his son-in-law Prusias, king of Bithynia, Philip besieged Cius, a city very
advantageous to Prusias because of its location and wealth. At that time the
Rhodians were powerful in naval forces. Together with Attalus, king of
Pergamum, they earnestly pleaded with Philip to leave Cius alone, since the
city had done him no wrong. Philip promised the Rhodian envoys that for their
sake he would leave the city in peace. Nevertheless, he attacked it with all
his strength and, upon taking it, killed very many and sold the survivors.

conceived an implacable hatred against the Macedonians. Therefore they,
together with Attalus and the Athenians, sent men to Rome to urge the Senate
to enter a treaty with them against the Macedonians. The Roman people,
exhausted by the Carthaginian war, greatly recoiled from a new war with the
Greeks. The Senate, however, already aspiring to dominion over Asia after
Hannibal’s defeat, drew the people into war against Philip by various devices
and, though only with difficulty, enticed the Aetolians into their alliance.
At last, somehow, after the peace was broken on mere pretexts, the consul
Titus Flamininus crushed Philip in a great battle. When Philip petitioned, the
Senate granted him a new peace. Stripped of all Greece and Thrace, he was
allowed to reign within Macedonia’s narrow boundaries. Full liberty was given
to all the Greeks. Philip was ordered to pay a large fine for the expenses of
the war, half immediately and the rest in several payments over the following
years; to surrender all his decked ships and his elephants; and to send his
son Demetrius to Rome as a hostage.

This kindness of the Romans toward both Philip and the Greeks arose from
prudence. They foresaw a great war from Antiochus and therefore desired peace
with the defeated Philip, lest he be compelled to flee to Antiochus. They also
bound the Greeks more firmly to themselves against Antiochus through the gift
of the liberty which they desired to the point of madness and which Philip
had suppressed by his many garrisons in their cities and citadels.

The Aetolians alone were dissatisfied with this peace. They had expected to
receive many Greek cities and Philip’s Macedonia itself from the Romans as a
reward for their alliance. They were also grieved that their rapacious hands
were bound by the Roman peace, so that they were no longer allowed to ravage
their neighbors as usual. They kept these causes of anger confined within
their hearts. What they openly accused the Romans of, however, was
Flamininus’s injustice toward Lacedaemon. While all the rest of Greece was
free, Lacedaemon remained under the most wretched yoke of the most savage
tyrant Nabis. Flamininus besieged Sparta with an army of fifty thousand, but
so that his successor would not deprive him of the praise for pacifying
Greece by taking the city, he made peace with Nabis, with the Achaeans’
approval—as though Nabis could not have been destroyed without the ruin of
the Lacedaemon over which he ruled. After defeating the Macedonian,
Flamininus celebrated the most splendid triumph Rome had seen to that day.
The praises of the Greeks raised him to the stars as the chief instrument of
their complete liberty, for he led his entire army home from Greece without
leaving even one garrison anywhere.

The Aetolians, who had brought the Romans into Greece against their
neighbors, left no stone unturned to incite Antiochus and all the Greeks
against the Romans, everywhere proclaiming that the Roman victory over Philip
had been won especially by their own courage. The Achaeans firmly closed
their ears to the Aetolians, nor would Philip be drawn into their alliance.
Nabis the Laconian, impatient of peace, readily allowed himself to be
persuaded, but Philopoemen inflicted a great defeat upon him.

The commonwealth of the Achaeans was renowned for prudence, resources, and
mercenary soldiers. But before Philopoemen their domestic arms were held in
little esteem, for the infantry’s shields were light and small, while the
cavalry fought with arrows and slings like light infantry. When Philopoemen
obtained the praetorship, however, he persuaded them to take up heavy armor
and to practice military discipline so diligently that in a short time he
assembled an army of citizens which routed the Lacedaemonians and Nabis in a
great battle and was considered equal to the Roman legions and superior in
courage to all the Greeks of that time.
