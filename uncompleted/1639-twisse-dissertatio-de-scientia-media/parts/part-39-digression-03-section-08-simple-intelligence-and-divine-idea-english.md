THIRD DIGRESSION

SECTION EIGHT

What is to be said about the knowledge of simple intelligence by which God would know the future,
and about the determination of that same future in its cause and in the Divine Idea.

NAVARRETE.

Sixteenth Conclusion. Even if GOD did not know a contingent future by intuitive but by abstractive
knowledge, or by the knowledge of simple intelligence, he could not know it as future. This
conclusion is directed against those authors who say, on the one hand, that the knowledge by which
God knows Antichrist is not terminated at Antichrist in himself but at Antichrist as future, and, on
the other hand, that it is terminated at Antichrist as though in abstraction. We have already spoken
above about the contradiction between these positions. Our present contention is that, even granted
that God's knowledge were terminated at Antichrist himself according to that kind of abstraction,
it would be impossible for Antichrist to be known as future. The proof is this. In whatever way
Antichrist is known as future, he cannot be known by certain and infallible cognition. The antecedent
is therefore established from Thomas, question 2 *On Truth*, where he says that God infallibly sees
all contingent things because they are not future to him. He had said earlier that no cognition
incapable of falsity can know a contingent as future. He teaches the same thing in this article, and
it is proved by Thomas's own reasoning. A contingent can be prevented before it is brought into
existence, because it then exists only in its causes, which can encounter an impediment that keeps
them from attaining their effect. Therefore there can be no infallible knowledge of a contingent
future insofar as it is future.

RESPONSE.

Certainly, if God did not know a contingent future by intuitive knowledge, he would not know it as
future, for we assign to intuitive cognition alone the knowledge of things future, present, and past
in time. The character of intuitive cognition in God differs from that in human beings. Even the
knowledge of present things is not always intuitive cognition in human beings. For example, a
mathematician sometimes already knows from the causes that an eclipse is occurring although perhaps
he does not see it at all. Again, the knowledge of simple intelligence is solely cognition of the
quiddities of things and of necessary or even accidental combinations, but only as possible. If GOD
knew by no other cognition, it would be no wonder if he did not know future things at all. Yet
because that by which things are known in their causes is also called abstractive cognition of
things, let us see whether it obstructs divine intuitive cognition. It seems to me that it cannot,
especially since God cannot behold things otherwise than in his own essence: either under the
aspect of essence, as he beholds all possible things, or under the aspect of the divine will, in
which he beholds all future, present, and past things. I freely profess that I am among those who
deny that the knowledge by which God knows a future thing is terminated at it as really present, but
only as future. Although we say that this knowledge is terminated at its real existence, it is not
terminated at that existence as really present to GOD for as long as it is future, but only as
future. Nor do we say that it is terminated at the future thing as though in abstraction, as if God
knew things present in time differently from things future in time—and that on the side of the
knower.

We have submitted what you have already said to examination; now we are about to examine what you
will say. We deny that it follows that Antichrist cannot be known by certain and infallible
cognition if he is known as future. You yourself have professed more than once that things are known
by God as future in time. Is it right, then, to attribute to God a cognition of this kind which is
uncertain and fallible? He certainly knows that contingent things, by reason of certain causes which
act fallibly, occur fallibly. Yet GOD does not know this fallibly but infallibly. Let us attend to
your proofs. The first rests on the authority of Thomas, question 2 *On Truth*, who asserts that GOD
infallibly sees all contingent things because they are not future to him. But, I say, it is not
fitting to rest at this point on the authority of Thomas, whose opinion has both long ago been and
still is called into controversy by many, even among the Roman Catholics, and which the Thomists
themselves profess to be exceedingly difficult to explain. Yet I answer without injury to Thomas's
authority. Contingent things are denied to be future to GOD because their presence in time
contributes nothing to God's vision of them in the way that it contributes to our intuition. God
beholds all things in himself, whether they are future, present, or past, according to the various
differences of time. As possible he knows them all in his essence; as future, present, or past he
knows them in his will, or in willed being. He does not know them otherwise when they are present,
and in this way knows both future and past things as intuitively as present ones. I do not say this
as though willed being were constituted the cause of divine cognition, but because volition or the
decree makes things which by their own nature are merely possible also knowable within the class of
future, present, and past things. As for Thomas's statement, as you report it, that “a contingent as
future cannot be known by any cognition to which falsity cannot belong,” it proceeds solely with
respect to secondary causes, not the first cause. For with respect to the first cause and its
cognition it makes no difference whether

[Book I, printed p. 73; BSB canvas 00099]

things are really present or merely future or past, since the real presence of things contributes
no more to God's cognition than does their futurity or pastness. For he beholds all things in willed
being, in which they are present before God when they are future. They are present to divine
cognition in no different fashion when they are really present in time. Again, Thomas considers the
contingent future under the aspect of a contingent not yet determined to one outcome; therefore he
considers it only with respect to secondary causes. For with respect to the first cause contingent
futures are determined to one outcome just as necessary futures are, namely through God's eternal
decree. The argument drawn from Thomas on which you rely confirms this very point. Without doubt a
contingent effect can be prevented while it is only in its causes, but solely with respect to
secondary causes. As for the first cause, it is irresistible; hence “the counsel of the Lord shall
stand,” and whatever he decrees to be future shall infallibly come to pass.

Near the end of his first member Navarrete himself acknowledges that these statements in Aquinas
proceed solely with respect to secondary causes, when he says: “And so, if a thing is known by God
as future, this is insofar as such a thing shines forth in its cause; because in the immediate
relation of things to God himself it is impossible for this aspect of futurity to intervene.” Here
he manifestly distinguishes the cognition of things as future in their causes from their cognition
in relation to God.

For the same reason that necessary futures can be known infallibly—not, however, by just anyone or
in just any cause, but only by God and in his will—contingent futures also can be known infallibly by
God through that same will. Indeed, I deny that any astronomer can infallibly know a future eclipse,
because God can both stop the course of nature and at his pleasure turn the direct motion of the
heavens and stars into a retrograde motion, not merely astronomically but physically. Nor does the
distinction he draws between the complete determination of a necessary cause to produce its effect
and that of a free cause to produce its own effect have any force. This distinction applies only
with respect to secondary causes, all of which, whether necessary or free, depend upon God's will
and its determination. If you say that not only the created will but also the uncreated will,
insofar as it is free, possesses indifference toward this or that effect, I in turn will reply that
this indifference extends as much to the effects of necessarily acting causes as to those of
contingently acting causes. Thus, in respect of this indifference, it is as indifferent for heaven
to rest or to revolve in motion, and for fire to burn or not burn, as it is for the human will to
will or not will something. Once God's will to restore the Jews through Cyrus is posited, God knows
that the restoration of the Jews will occur just as infallibly as he knows that some eclipse will
occur. Without doubt God's covenant with his people is as firm and irresistible as God's covenant
with day and night. Although with respect to secondary causes some things arise absolutely
necessarily and others contingently, with respect to the first cause and upon its supposition all
things occur with equal necessity or contingency—that is, necessarily in a qualified sense but
absolutely contingently. They are necessary in a qualified sense because they are necessary only on
the supposition; they are absolutely contingent because, since God's will is free, whatever proceeds
from God's will, precisely insofar as it proceeds from that will, occurs absolutely contingently, as
Durandus observed long ago. Therefore, although God by no means infallibly knows contingent futures
in a contingently acting secondary cause—and cannot infallibly know even necessary futures in a
secondary cause, however necessarily it acts, since these too can be impeded, perhaps even by
created power and certainly by divine power—this does not at all prevent him from infallibly knowing
contingent futures in the first cause, namely in the determination of his will.

Nevertheless, Aquinas regards the being which things possess before God in willed being as
determined to one outcome, and rightly so, since by this very fact they are placed outside their
causes within the class of the future. Their intentional being in God's will is of such a kind that
it is impossible for their real being not to follow in its own time. The Idea of a thing,
presupposing not only the cognition of the thing as possible but also the determination of the
divine will to produce it, represents the thing as present in willed being—that is, according to
the intentional being of things—and consequently as future according to real being. Yet it no more
represents it in future being for as long as it is future than in present being at the time when it
is present, or in past being when it is past. Therefore it is false to say that nothing can be
future with respect to the divine Idea. Certainly nothing is future in willed being before God with
respect to the divine Idea—that is, according to the intentional being of things. For according to
Navarrete the Idea presupposes the determination of the divine will; but all future things, for as
long as they are future, are absolutely future according to their real being, and no relation can
make the matter otherwise. When a thing is said to be known by God “as future,” the conjunction “as”
is not taken formally, as though it denoted some ground of divine cognition drawn from the futurity
of things, but materially. It means no more than if one said that God knows future things to be
future. Thus we say that God knows them as shining forth in their cause, namely in his will. For
God's willing a thing to exist at a definite time means both that it will be future until that time
arrives, that it will be present as soon as the time arrives, and that it will be past after the
time has passed. Hence, in relation to God, the futurity of things arises no more than does their
presence or pastness; and their presence according to real being arises no more than their futurity
or pastness. But all these shine forth in willed being before God, not as different things but
rather as one thing known by God. For to be willed by God to exist at a definite time is both to be
future while that time is approaching, to be present as soon as it is at hand, and to be past after
that time has ceased to be. Navarrete's pronouncements have no force to carry us off course; relying
on some inexplicable confidence in their author, they proceed imperiously rather than scholastically.
We acknowledge that things are infallibly known

[Book I, printed p. 74; BSB canvas 00100]

as determined to one outcome in the Idea which represents not only what God can bring about but
also what God wills to bring about. Yet this is not to be known by the cognition of simple
intelligence, but by the knowledge of vision. I add that to be known as determined to one outcome in
this way is only to be known as determined to one outcome before God in willed being and according
to the intentional being of things, but as things which are to be determined to one outcome at a
definite time according to their real being. That is, before that time arrives they are known as
future; when it arrives, as present; after it has passed, as past. Yet this cognition of things which
stand differently according to the various differences of time is one and the same cognition,
standing in the same manner before God, since to be willed by God for a definite time—as has been
said more than once—is both to be future before that time, present when that time arrives, and past
after the time has passed.

NAVARRETE.

Seventeenth Conclusion. It is wholly improbable that God should know contingent futures as future
on the side of the knower. We lay down this conclusion because authors opposed to the school of St.
Thomas concede absolutely that God knows those futures as future. Since, on the other hand, an
absolute statement ought to be formal, they seem to understand in the formal sense that God knows
future things as future, and consequently that this aspect of futurity is taken from the mode of
knowing which stands on God's side, or on the side of the object toward which God is primarily
directed. This, however, would be chimerical. For divine cognition always remains the same on God's
side and without any mutation on the side of its primary object. It is therefore impossible that
God should, of himself, now know a contingent future as future and afterward, when that future
exists in reality, know it as present. It follows from this that if, from the mode of knowing, a
thing future in time is present to God, such presence must be real as distinguished from what is
really future. For if a thing cannot be known as really future, and must necessarily be known as
present on the side of the knower, it consequently must be known as really present. It must in no
way be conceded that God should on his own side at one time have things really present to him and at
another time not have them present in this manner.

RESPONSE.

They concede, he says, “absolutely that God knows future things as future.” By this he intimates that
if they conceded it in another way, but not absolutely, he would have nothing for which to accuse
them of error. What that other way might be, however, I do not yet grasp. Navarrete abounds
everywhere in qualifications of this kind whose purpose and ultimate target are not easily
understood. Hence he also solemnly infers, by entirely unnecessary circumlocutions, that they
understand “that God knows future things as they are future,” which is precisely the same as the
former statement and differs from it by not a jot. Navarrete therefore seems to labor to force from
the words of his adversaries some alien sense which he may afterward call chimerical. Thus far this
has succeeded most unhappily. He accordingly adds, as though by consequence, that this aspect of
futurity comes from the mode of knowing which stands on God's side, or on the side of the object
toward which God is primarily directed. He seems to regard these as one and the same, though before
this he wretchedly tore apart the considerations “on God's side” and “on the thing's side” as widely
different and wholly incapable of coalescing into a single consideration. Yet I see the distinction:
here he speaks of the primary object, which is God himself, and not of the secondary object, which
created things constitute. Unless my logic fails me, Navarrete errs somewhat in his logic. For if
the expression “God knows future things as future” is reduced to the formal sense with respect to
its final element, it seems to signify that the ground of divine cognition is drawn from the
futurity of things rather than that the ground of futurity is taken from the mode of knowing which
stands on God's side. I do not think, however, that even this entered the minds of your adversaries
when they framed their opinion with the precision of the formal sense. They deny that any real
presence of things before God is required for him to know future things intuitively. But once your
account is removed, they do not substitute another account drawn from the futurity of things under
which God knows future things. The reason is manifest: we maintain that by one and the same
intuitive cognition God knows not only future but also present and past things.

There is, however, something which Navarrete regards as chimerical—while he detects nothing
chimerical in his own fiction about the real existence of things in eternity, and that from
eternity—which becomes clearer to us from what follows than from what precedes. We acknowledge that
divine cognition on God's side always remains in the same manner and without any mutation on the
side of its primary object. But what then? “Therefore,” he says, “it is impossible that God should,
of himself, now know a contingent future as future and afterward, when that future exists in
reality, know it as present.” But I say that we deny this consequence. Even if this were granted, it
would not follow that divine cognition underwent any mutation on God's side. Indeed, I appeal to
you yourself: will you deny that God knows things as future while they are future, and later knows
the same things to be present when they are present, and, I add, to be past when they are past? You
yourself have already confessed this in your response to the fifth argument, although you did not
confess it so roundly, as though you had feared sincerely and candidly to embrace the truth shining
upon you. At last I also see why you did this: lest, if you openly asserted what you had to assert
in a direct and clear response to that argument, you yourself would already have cut the sinews of
this objection of yours. For the distinction you laid down there comes to this when expressed
clearly and without wrappings: although God knows Antichrist as future for the time in which he is to
exist, nevertheless he knows Antichrist as present throughout all eternity. Unless I am mistaken, I
see the goal of your sophistry, crude as it is. You want your readers to regard as absurd the claim
that God begins to know something as present. Here you protect yourself with the shield of a notable
ambiguity. For although you hold that things are present to God in eternity from eternity, and thus
are known by God from eternity as present—namely, in eternity—you nevertheless cannot deny that
things newly begin to be present in time and that God also knows this, just as before they were
present in time God knew well that they were not yet present in time. This must be so, or God would
either be ignorant of something or err. You neither are ignorant of this nor dare deny it, although
you splendidly dissemble it for the advantage of your cause. Moreover, the better to advance your
artifice in

[Book I, printed p. 75; BSB canvas 00101]

obscuring the truth, you hunt for advantage in the ambiguous character of that conjunction “as.” We
have no need of that conjunction. We say that God knew things to be future before they existed, and
that as soon as they exist he knows them to be present. Deny this if you dare; yet I do not think
that on this account you will concede that some mutation is attributed to divine cognition. We have
explained this more than once; you have never explained it, but use its supposed absurdity as an oar
to propel your fictions with better success. For when the waters are disturbed, you will be able to
fish much more conveniently in the service of your cause. Therefore, although when the future thing
itself exists in reality God does not know it as present in eternity, you will not deny that God
knows it as present in time, even though this was known by God before the thing existed. For to know
that a thing is present in time when it is not present is not so much to know as to err. Perhaps you
will say: but it was already known by God as present in eternity. Granted—what then? Will you dare
affirm that it was also known by God to be present in time, although it cannot be denied that God
afterward knew it in this manner as well? Yet we will not therefore need to subject divine cognition
to mutation. How this can be so, however, we have not yet received any explanation from you. And
what, I ask, does the presence of things in eternity contribute to the knowledge of their futurity
in time, since while they exist in eternity they are no more future than present or past? If it
cannot be denied that God knows things which are really future to be really future, with what face
do you deny that a thing can be known by God as really future? Although it is known as present in
eternity, nevertheless, since it is also really future in time, not even this can be unknown to God,
even if on the supposition of your fiction it must also be known as really present—namely, in
eternity. Yet you everywhere confuse these differences of real existence lest the truth shine
forth. You trust that beneath this confusion, as beneath Ajax's shield, you can lie safely concealed,
but in vain. Even despite this entire worthless dissertation of yours, it must be altogether
conceded that things which formerly were not present sometimes become present to all in time,
however much, according to your fiction, it were conceded that these same things are always really
present to God in eternity. You have indeed tried thus far to prove this real existence of things in
eternity, but you will discover that you have spent most unhappy labor in proving it. No presence of
future things before God is required in order to attribute their cognition to God except that which
they possess in willed being. This is sufficient for the cognition of all things, whether future,
present, or past, and without any mutation in divine cognition.

NAVARRETE.

Eighteenth Conclusion. To say that God knows future things in an infallible cause, such as the
divine essence insofar as it is the Idea, is the same as to say that he knows those same futures in
themselves. This conclusion is laid down to reconcile two opinions disputed among the interpreters
of St. Thomas. Divine cognition attains things in themselves because they are present in eternity.
Since, therefore, divine cognition is measured by eternity insofar as it has the divine essence for
its moving object, it follows that it attains things in themselves because it attains them in their
ideal grounds.

RESPONSE.

Your argument of this kind can therefore be framed more briefly and plainly as follows: divine
cognition has as its object the divine essence, in which it beholds the Ideas of things; therefore
it attains things in themselves. But we deny this consequence. For the Ideas of things, insofar as
they include the determination of the divine will, proceed no farther than to represent things in
willed being before God. But to represent things only in willed being is to represent them solely
according to their intentional and not their real being; therefore it is not to represent things in
themselves. Hence any cognition which knew the divine essence would by no means proceed farther to a
cognition of things. Meanwhile, neither would it know things in willed being by knowing the divine
essence unless it also knew the divine will. How that could occur except through revelation I
frankly do not understand. Nor can I comprehend how the cognition of any creature could be measured
by eternity. He readily concedes that the certainty of knowledge of future things is by no means
drawn from the presence of things in eternity as from what is prior, but only as from what is
posterior.

He proceeds to exhibit the insufficiency of the objective presence of things devised by Suárez,
namely on the ground that the being which a thing is to possess in its own time is a sufficient
foundation on the side of the terminating object for it to be seen by God, and a sufficient ground
on the side of that same object for it to be present to the act of intuitive knowledge. He argues
for its insufficiency because it posits no greater presence in those terminating objects with
respect to God than is posited in the terminating objects of the angelic species through which an
angel knows singular things when they actually exist in reality. Thus such a future thing is later
understood by God without any mutation of itself, just as a present thing is understood by an angel
without any mutation of itself. Granted—but what then? He says that this cannot be affirmed. Why
not? Because it would then follow that, just as when an object is of itself intelligible by a
created power and is afterward actually understood, mutation intervenes only on the side of the
power and not on that of the object, so if future things were said to be actually present
objectively to the divine intellect, there would be no mutation on the side of those things. That is
certainly so—but what then? Consequently, he says, mutation would intervene on the side of divine
intellection. I say, on the contrary, that there is no probability in such a consequence. Although
the angelic intellect newly understands what it did not understand before, the divine intellect
does not; it understands nothing which it did not understand from eternity. But, he says, when a
future thing actually terminates divine cognition, a denomination of reason follows, yet no
sufficient foundation for it is given, since from this presence of things before God no particular
perfection newly arises either on God's side or on the side of the things. But every denomination
of reason newly consequent in God requires a real mutation in the other extreme or at least in
something extrinsic

[Book I, printed p. 76; BSB canvas 00102]

himself. I answer, however, that this denomination of reason does not newly arise for God, since
these things are posited as known by God from eternity. Although they are understood as knowable
prior to being understood as known, this priority is only one of reason and not of duration. In the
same way, things in the class of the possible are understood as knowable prior to being understood
as known; yet this denomination of reason does not require some particular perfection accruing to
God or to the things themselves to be antecedently posited. Nevertheless, Suárez's opinion is by no
means pleasing to me, although I make nothing of Navarrete's argument against it. He draws that
argument out at length and in exceedingly obscure language, when it could have been dispatched both
more plainly and in far fewer words. Yet I judge the Suarezian opinion to be rejected for this
reason: it introduces future things which it makes the object of divine cognition without any
account whatever being represented by which they pass from the condition of possible things, which
they are by their own nature, into the condition of future things. We say, however, that this occurs
from the determination of the divine will, by which they are constituted present to God in willed
being so as to exist at a definite time, and consequently become knowable within the class of future
things. Nor do we otherwise say that God makes things present to himself before they are known. Nor,
after presupposing that real presence of things in eternity which you invent, did Suárez introduce a
new presence. Rather, after rejecting yours, he thought it sufficient for asserting intuitive
cognition of future things in God that those future things were knowable within the class of future
things. But since those things are by their own nature merely possible, Suárez's opinion is
deficient in this respect: he devised the futurity accruing to possible things without any cause at
all.
