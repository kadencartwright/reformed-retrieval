## QUESTION VII

On the first precept.

Is God alone to be worshiped and invoked, or may departed saints also be
invoked and religiously worshiped?

The former is affirmed and the latter denied, against the Roman Catholics.

I. Just as the true *object* of religious worship is established by the first
precept, *On having no other gods before God*, so this precept forbids not only
*atheism*, which acknowledges and worships no God, and *polytheism*, which
invents innumerable gods for itself, but also every superstition and
*idolatry with respect to the object*, by which something that is not God is
regarded and worshiped as God (Gal. 4:8). This may be either a mere human
fabrication, as most of the fictitious gods of the Gentiles were, or something
that really exists but is created and finite, to which the religious worship
of trust, adoration, and invocation due to God alone is rendered in any way.
Because the Roman Catholics sin in many ways on this point through the
religious worship of creatures, angels, saints, relics, the host of the Mass,
and the pope himself—and thus are guilty of more than one kind of
idolatry—we must dispute this most weighty article of religion that is
controverted between us. Earlier, in Locus VII, Question 9, we discussed the
*worship of angels*; now we treat the *invocation of saints*.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

II. It is not asked, first, whether saints who have died piously in the Lord
should be treated with any regard and honor. For we do not deny that they
should be honored by us according to the degree of their excellence: by
thinking nobly of them as most blessed servants of God admitted to the
Lord's fellowship; by recalling their memory with grateful and pleasant
remembrance (Luke 1:48; Mark 14:9); by proclaiming their contests and
victories, preserving their doctrine, celebrating and imitating their virtues
(Heb. 12:1); and by praising and thanking God in them and for them because he
raised up such people for the good of the church. Rather, it is asked whether
they should be venerated with worship properly called *religious*. Our
adversaries would affirm this; we deny it. We raise this distinction at the
outset against the calumnies of the Roman Catholics, who represent us as
despisers and enemies of the saints because we reject their worship, as
though the saints were insulted if things belonging to God and Christ alone
were not bestowed upon them. Yet although their memory is sacred and
inviolable to us, we think we must diligently take care that they are not
worshiped to God's injury. Indeed, we judge that those who turn them into
idols and misuse God's friends to provoke him to jealousy do them a serious
injury. Nor do we hesitate to assert that if the saints could perceive from
the height of glory what is done here, they would not only refuse the worship
rendered to them but also express the gravest indignation toward such
undutiful worshipers.

[Printed p. 36 | Internet Archive PDF 64 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

III. It is not asked about *invocation* improperly so called, that is, the
*civil, social, and loving appeal* that takes place among living people and
belongs to the second table. Rather, it concerns invocation properly so
called and the *religious invocation of piety*, which is commanded in the
first table as a principal part of religion and is often placed by synecdoche
for the whole worship of God (Rom. 10:13). Under this the adversaries include
the whole worship of the saints. According to them, this consists partly
*inwardly, in a judgment about the saints* that they know our necessities and
can help us; partly in *trust in them*, that they also will to do so; and
partly *outwardly*, in sacrifices and invocations offered to them and in
feasts and temples consecrated to them. The question is whether they should
be worshiped, not with the worship of love and fellowship with which holy
people of God are honored in this life *for imitation*, but with the sacred
worship of piety *for religion*, as Augustine speaks in book 20 of *Against
Faustus the Manichaean*, chapter 21. The Roman Catholics affirm this; we deny
it.

IV. It is not asked whether the saints are our mediators and intercessors
before God, for this pertains to another question that will be treated when
the intercession of Christ is discussed. Rather, it is asked whether they
should be invoked as our mediators and intercessors—and not only as
intercessors who obtain for us by their prayers and merits the good things
asked from them, but also as the givers of those things. Although the Roman
Catholics conceal it, they nevertheless maintain this, as is evident from
the prayers directed to the saints that are considered below.

V. The question therefore amounts to this: Is the worship of adoration and
invocation due to God alone, to the exclusion of all creatures of whatever
order and dignity, or can it also be bestowed upon angels and saints—not
militant, but triumphant? The Roman Catholics maintain the latter; we assert
the former.

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Roman Catholics.]

VI. The opinion of the Roman Catholics is gathered not only from their
practice but also from its sanction in session 25 of the Council of Trent, in
the decree on the invocation of saints and the veneration of relics, and so
forth: “It commands them to teach the invocation of saints, the honor of
relics, and the use of images, and to declare that those who teach otherwise
hold an impious opinion.” Bellarmine, in book 1 of *The Beatification of the
Saints*, chapter 19, says, “Saints, whether angels or human beings, are piously
and profitably invoked by the living.” The *Roman Catechism* on the first
precept, question 12, proves the invocation of saints by various arguments,
but especially “because they continually offer prayers for the salvation of
human beings, and God confers many benefits upon us on account of their merit
and grace.” Perron, against the king of Great Britain, attempts to prove that
the invocation of saints is not only useful but also necessary. Maldonado on
Matthew 5:34 says that “the error of those who bestow religious honor upon God
alone is impious and ignorant.” Hence the Expurgatory Indexes so carefully
see to the deletion of everything in either Bibles or the writings of the
fathers that pertains to the worship of God alone. From the notes in Vatablus's
Bible on Isaiah 8:32 they delete, “Those who believe in God will be saved, but
those who do not will perish.” From the index to Athanasius they delete, “It
belongs to God alone to be adored, and no creature is to be adored.” From
Augustine, book 1 of *On True Religion*, they delete, “The saints are to be
honored by imitation, not adoration,” and, against those who say, “I do not
worship images, but through them I am exhorted toward that which I ought to
worship,” in Psalm 11, and so forth.

VII. As for their practice, no one is ignorant that they rank this worship
among the chief parts of Roman Catholic religion. Saints are honored with
temples, chapels, altars, images, heads surrounded by rays, masses, feasts,
and candles. Gifts and sacrifices are offered to them, prayers are directed
to them, oaths are sworn by them, vows are made to them, and the hope of
salvation and trust are placed in them. Thus they are invoked not only as
intercessors but also as protectors from evils and givers both of grace and
of glory. Thus an invocation is directed to all the saints in the Roman
Breviary for November 1:

[Printed p. 37 | Internet Archive PDF 65 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

> You blessed hosts as well
> Of heavenly spirits,
> Drive away past, present,
> And future evils.

And to the apostles in the *Little Garden of the Soul*, p. 184: “O blessed
apostles, release me from sins, defend me, strengthen me, and lead me to the
heavenly kingdom.” Likewise: “I ask to be saved through you at the last
judgment.” To St. Peter in the Roman Breviary, on the feast of Peter and Paul:

> Now, good shepherd Peter, mercifully receive
> The prayers of those who plead, and loose the chains
> Of sin by the power delivered to you,
> Who by your word close and open heaven to all.

To James the Greater in the *Little Garden of the Soul*:

> O James, hasten
> To blot out my crimes,
> Giving me the gifts of righteousness
> Away from the leaven of malice.

The same could easily be shown concerning the other apostles and saints.
Hence it is perfectly clear that they are invoked not only as intercessors
who pray for us, but also as givers of good things, in whom hope and trust are
therefore to be placed. Consequently, in the *Little Garden of the Soul* they
command each person at the moment of death to invoke in this manner whichever
saint he was more devoted to in life: “O most glorious Saint N., male or
female, while I lived I always placed a singular hope and confidence in you.
Help me now as I labor in this final moment of necessity.” But this is
especially evident with respect to the Blessed Virgin. They everywhere
bestow upon her not only divine names and titles, calling her “Goddess and
Lady, Queen of heaven, Mother of mercy, Redemptress, our omnipotent Lady,
refuge of sinners,” and so forth, but also render her divine worship, both
internal and external. This could be proved by countless examples, but even
the *Psalter of Mary* composed by Bonaventure—and never condemned by the
Roman Church or the Expurgatory Indexes—proves it with perfect clarity, for
things that the psalmist attributes to God are impiously transferred there to
the Blessed Virgin. See what we have said in Disputation 2, *On Separation*,
and Downame, *On Antichrist*, part 1, book 5, chapters 1–2, and especially
Drelincourt's French treatise *On the Honor Due to the Virgin*.

VIII. To understand their position more fully, it must be observed that the
Roman Catholics define *worship* in general as the honor owed by an inferior
to a superior, and they require three things for it. First is an act of the
intellect by which we apprehend someone's excellence. Second is an act of the
will by which we inwardly incline ourselves toward him and will to attest his
excellence and our subjection. Third is an outward act by which we bow the
head or bend the knee or exhibit some other sign of subjection. Hence they
infer that there are three kinds of adoration or worship, corresponding to
the three kinds of excellence that they establish in this question. There is
*divine* excellence, to which corresponds the kind of worship they call
*latria*; *human* excellence, which consists in the dignities, virtues, and
various ranks of human beings, to which corresponds *civil worship*, or human
respect; and *intermediate* excellence, which arises from grace and glory, to
which corresponds the third kind of worship that they call *dulia*. This last
is in turn either simple dulia, which

[Printed p. 38 | Internet Archive PDF 66 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

is bestowed upon angels and saints, or *hyperdulia*, which is assigned to the
humanity of Christ considered separately, although united to the Word, and to
the Blessed Virgin. They claim that this last is religious worship.

IX. We do not deny that a diversity of worship may properly be established
according to the diverse excellence of things. Thus we readily concede that,
in accordance with uncreated and infinite excellence on the one hand and
created and finite excellence on the other, there can be a twofold worship:
one *religious*, due to God alone, and the other *civil*, applied to creatures.
The latter can in turn be considered either with respect to the *earthly
commonwealth*, in all the duties of reverence, love, and service owed to human
beings even if they are strangers to the faith, or with respect to the
*heavenly commonwealth*, in the honor we render to members of the household
of faith—whether to the living through the mutual performance of duties, or
to the dead through love, praise, remembrance, imitation, and so forth. Yet
we think that religious worship cannot be divided into various degrees
without serious error. We maintain that it is one and belongs to God alone
and is incommunicable to all creatures.

X. Nor can the distinction between *latria and dulia* support this error.
First, it rests on no solid foundation. It is not established by the
*proper meaning of the words*, because among pagan writers the meanings of
λατρεία and δουλεία are interchangeable. Hesychius explains λατρεία by
δουλεία, and a glossary gives δουλεία as λατρεία and λατρεύω as δουλεύω.
Indeed, by the force of the word, δουλεία implies more than λατρεία. The
latter denotes only the service and worship of a hired servant—λάτρις ὁ ἐπὶ
μισθῷ δουλεύων, “a servant who works for pay”—but the former denotes the
subjection of a bondman and slave who owes himself entirely to his master.
Nor is it established by *Scriptural usage*. Although the word λατρεία is
never used in the New Testament except for worship owed to God alone, that
same worship is also frequently expressed by δουλεία, as in Galatians 4:8; 1
Thessalonians 1:9; Matthew 6:24; Romans 12:11 and 14:18; and Ephesians 6:7.
And λατρεία is attributed by the Septuagint to human service in Deuteronomy
28:48. In Leviticus 23:7 servile work is called λατρευτόν, something not to
be performed on the Sabbath. Paul always calls himself a δοῦλος of Christ
and never a λατρευτής. Nor, finally, is it established by the *authority of
the fathers*. Although Augustine distinguished λατρεία and δουλεία, his
intention was not to divide religious worship into two degrees, one
pertaining to God and the other to the saints. His purpose was only to
distinguish the service owed to God alone—which for the sake of instruction
he calls λατρεία, and elsewhere the service of religion—from the service
owed to human beings, which he particularly calls dulia. Thus he says in
book 10 of *The City of God*, chapter 1: “But that service”—namely, as opposed
to latria owed to God—“which is owed to human beings, according to which the
apostle commands slaves to be subject to their masters, is customarily called
by another name, namely dulia.” The adversaries are forced to admit that the
distinction is ἄγραφον, *unwritten*, and that both the Hebrew and Greek terms
are used interchangeably; see Bellarmine, book 1 of *The Beatification of the
Saints*, chapter 12, and Vásquez, Disputation 93 on part 3 of the *Summa*,
chapter 1.

[Marginal heading: The invocation of saints is rejected: 1. From Exodus 20:3.]

XI. The orthodox contend for the worship of God alone against the invocation
of saints and the worship of creatures. First, they argue from God's
*express command*, by which he forbids having other gods before him in Exodus
20:3: “You shall have no other gods before me,” or, as the Septuagint has it,
πλὴν ἐμοῦ, “besides me.” Here the Lord establishes that nothing is to be
religiously worshiped besides himself, the one and supreme God. For whatever
we adore and treat with religious worship is said to *be God to us and to be
regarded as God*, whatever else it may be either in itself or among us,
because we transfer to it the honor that belongs to God alone. This is
confirmed by Christ when he argues against Satan: “You shall worship the Lord
your God, and him alone shall you serve.”

[Marginal citation: Matthew 4:10.]

Although the exclusive particle *alone* does not occur in Deuteronomy 6:13 or
10:20, from which this declaration was taken, it is nevertheless necessarily
included by the nature of the matter. Thus 1 Samuel 7:3 expressly says, “serve
him לבדו, *alone*,” and in the cited passage it is expressed by μόνῳ in the
Septuagint, which

[Printed p. 39 | Internet Archive PDF 67 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the Vulgate follows here. Even if nothing else occurred, the Savior's words
would suffice for us to conclude that religious worship must be rendered to
God alone. Nor can it be answered that the exclusive was added only to the
latter words, where the verb λατρεύω occurs, and not to the former, to
indicate that religious worship is common to God and the saints but that
λατρεία belongs to God alone. For since the same kind of worship is designated
by *adoring and serving*, if the exclusive particle is added to one, it is
understood as added to the other. Moreover, Satan does not ask for λατρεία but
for προσκύνησις: “I will give you all these things,” he says, “ἐὰν πεσὼν
προσκυνήσῃς μοι, if you fall down and worship me.” Since Christ denies him
this, he thereby testifies invincibly that both προσκύνησις and λατρεία are
owed to God alone.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the absence of a precept and example.]

XII. Second, the invocation of saints has no *precept*, *promise*, or
*example* in Scripture on which to rest, and thus is nothing other than a
vicious and damnable ἐθελοθρησκεία, *self-made religion*. The invocation of
God is indeed urged everywhere, but the invocation of creatures is nowhere
mentioned. Indeed, simply “to pray” is more than once used for praying to God,
because there is no other lawful invocation than that of God. Bañez himself
does not deny this in II-II, question 1, article 10: “Neither expressly nor
implicitly,” he says, “do the Scriptures teach that prayers should be made to
the saints.” So also Eck in the *Enchiridion*, chapter 15; Salmerón,
Disputation 7 on 1 Timothy 2; and Cotton, *Institutes*, book 1, chapter 26:
“As for a precept to invoke the saints, the church has never taught that any
exists, except insofar as we are commanded to obey the church.” Bellarmine,
book 1 of *The Beatification of the Saints*, chapter 20, says that “a command
is not required when necessity itself impels us.” But he gratuitously assumes
that there is any necessity for such worship. Indeed, since whatever is done
without faith is sin, worship that is nowhere read to have been instituted by
God must be vicious. As for examples, they admit that the invocation of
saints was not received under the Old Testament because, they think, the
saints had not been received into heaven but were still in limbo. Yet under
the New Testament no prayer is read to have been directed either to the
Blessed Virgin, the apostles, or any saints. This provides a clear argument
for its emptiness.

[Marginal heading: 3. Because they lack the knowledge, power, and will required
in the one who is invoked.]

XIII. Third, invocation presupposes faith in the one who invokes (Rom. 10:14),
and knowledge, power, and will in the one who is invoked: *knowledge*, so that
he may know all our necessities and hear everywhere all prayers, both spoken
and mental, and may truly be a καρδιογνώστης, a knower of hearts; *power*, so
that he can help those in distress; and *will*, so that he wills to help us
and requires this worship to be rendered to him. But these belong to God
alone. He alone is the object of faith; he alone is καρδιογνώστης and
all-knowing; he alone is omnipotent, calls us to himself, and has ears open to
the prayers of supplicants. As for the saints, however, neither their
knowledge nor, still less, their power is established, nothing is established
about their will, and no more is established about their mediatorial and
intercessory office. Rather, they *do not will it to be exhibited to
themselves*, because they *know that it is owed to God alone*, as Augustine
says in book 20 of *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, chapter 21. There he
gives the angels' reason for refusing to be so adored: they *are our fellow
servants* (Rev. 19:10). Scripture attributes omniscience, καρδιογνωσία, the
knowledge of hearts, and omnipotence to God alone in more than one place (1
Kings 8:39; 2 Chron. 6:30; Rev. 2:23; Jer. 17:10), sufficiently proving that
they cannot belong to creatures. More than once it also attributes to the
saints a lack of knowledge about the things done on earth (Isa. 63:16; Eccles.
9:6; 2 Kings 22:20).

XIV. The Roman Catholics cannot supply this deficiency by any of the
following. First is the imaginary *mirror of the Trinity* that they invent,
as though the blessed, in the essence of God who sees all things, saw all the
things done here. Just as this is gratuitously invented without Scripture, it
is rejected as easily as it is proposed, and it has displeased not a few

[Printed p. 40 | Internet Archive PDF 68 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

leading figures within the papacy, including Lombard, Thomas, William of
Auxerre, Bañez, Estius, and many others.

Nor does it follow that because the saints see the face of the God who sees
all things—that is, enjoy the immediate vision of him—they see all things in
God. Otherwise, they would not be ignorant of the day of judgment, which is
nevertheless unknown to them. Moreover, this mirror will be either *natural*
or *voluntary*. If the former, absolutely nothing will be hidden from them,
any more than from God, which it is impious to say. If it is voluntary, they
will see nothing in it except what God has been pleased to represent—but who
can say what those things are? Second, they appeal to a *revelation from God*
by which he reveals the prayers and thoughts of the faithful to the saints.
But besides the fact that such a revelation is vainly alleged while Scripture
is silent, why must so many roundabout paths be sought when God calls us
directly to himself? And to what good would God reveal our necessities and
prayers to the saints so that they might offer them to him again? God did
sometimes will to reveal thoughts of the heart to prophets so that they could
discharge their office, as was done for Elisha in 2 Kings 5:26. But it does
not follow that this is ordinarily done for the saints when Scripture is
silent about it or that they have any office toward human beings for whose
discharge such knowledge is necessary. Third, they appeal to a *gift of
blessedness* by whose power the saints can look down from heaven upon the
things done here, as Coster maintains in chapter 14 of his *Enchiridion*. But
it is falsely assumed both that such vision is given and that it belongs to
the saints' blessedness. Neither the example of Stephen, who by an
extraordinary dispensation was permitted to see Christ in heaven, nor of Paul,
who was caught up to the third heaven and heard ineffable things there, can
establish that saints dwelling in heaven see the thoughts of the heart, which
belongs to God alone.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because it is idolatrous.]

XV. Fourth, every religious worship and invocation directed to someone other
than God is idolatrous. For idolatry is properly said to be that by which a
creature is worshiped παρὰ τὸν κτίσαντα, “besides the Creator,” or with him
*passed by* (Rom. 1:25), when those *who by nature are not gods* are worshiped
(Gal. 4:8). No one denies that this can rightly be said of the saints. Hence
Nazianzen, in Oration 38, calls idolatry μετάθεσις τῆς προσκυνήσεως ἀπὸ τοῦ
πεποιηκότος ἐπὶ τὰ κτίσματα, “the transfer of adoration from the Creator to
creatures,” and Thomas, in II-II, question 94, article 3, defines idolatry as
occurring “when someone bestows divine honor upon a creature.” Nor can the
empty distinctions and varnishes offered by the Roman Catholics wash away so
great a crime. They deny that they render the supreme worship due to God to
the saints, but only subordinate worship, *not as to God but as to God's
friends*. Yet, first, degrees are wrongly invented in religious worship, which
is one and indivisible. Second, this was the defense of the Gentiles, who
distinguished between the supreme God, to whom supreme worship was owed, and
intermediate and inferior gods, to whom inferior worship was also rendered.
Third, the Arians and Nestorians did not adore Christ *as God*: the former
regarded him as a created God and the latter as a man divided from the person
of the λόγος, the Word. Nevertheless, they are accused of idolatry by the
fathers. Fourth, who would tolerate an adulteress excusing herself on the
ground that she gave the use of her body to another, not as to her husband,
but as to her husband's friend? Yet every religious worship belongs as
exclusively to God as marital affection belongs to a husband. For it is
established that our communion with God is frequently represented by marriage
and idolatry by adultery and fornication. Fifth, the worship the Romanists
exhibit to creatures does not differ from divine worship: neither with
respect to the internal worship of trust and hope that they place in them,
nor with respect to the outward worship of adoration and invocation that they
render them, as was seen above and as we have proved elsewhere in Disputation
2, *On the Necessity of Separation*, theses 16–17, and following. Hence,
although a distinction is made in words to deceive simpler people, in actual
practice the worship remains the same.

[Marginal heading: 5. It rests upon a twofold false foundation.]

XVI. Fifth, the invocation of saints rests upon a twofold false foundation.
The first is that they are our *mediators and intercessors* before God who
obtain temporal and spiritual benefits for us not only by their prayers but
also by their merits. Since this is utterly false and most insulting to
Christ, as we will demonstrate in its proper place, whatever is built upon it
must also be false and fictitious. The second is the *canonization* or
*apotheosis of saints*, by which the Roman pontiff, with certain ceremonies
and especially by a golden ladder, declares that the saints to be invoked by
the church have been carried into heaven and are persons who ought to be
worshiped and invoked. But this impious fiction of θεοποιία, *deification*,
by which an earthly little man who is not certain of his own salvation wills
to pronounce upon that of another and sacrilegiously make himself the
distributor of heavenly goods, is a mere imitation of impure paganism and
Jewish superstition. It has no foundation either in Scripture or in pious
antiquity, as learned men have long observed and solidly demonstrated. See
John Prideaux, Lecture 13, and Hospinian, *On the Origin of Feasts*, chapter
6. This is to say nothing of the many fictitious saints who are worshiped,
either persons who never existed or who are anything but blessed.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the testimonies of the ancients.]

XVII. Sixth, the invocation of saints was unknown to the apostolic church and
the first centuries of Christianity. This is evident from the testimonies of
the most ancient fathers. Justin says in *Apology* 2, “Accordingly, we adore
God alone and gladly serve you in other matters.” The church of Smyrna denies
that “we can worship anyone else,” and so forth, in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical
History*, book 4, chapter 15. Irenaeus says in book 5, chapter 22, “The
disciples of Christ ought to serve him alone,” and so forth. Tertullian, in
*To Scapula*, chapter 2, and the *Apology*, chapter 17, says, “That which we
worship is one God,” and so forth. Epiphanius says in *Heresies* 69, “The holy
church of God does not adore a creature.” Athanasius says in Oration 3
*Against the Arians*, “A creature does not adore a creature; it belongs to
the Deity alone to be adored.” Gregory of Nyssa says to Placilla, “He who
adores a creature, although he does it in the name of Christ, is an idolater,
putting the name of Christ upon an idol.” Nazianzen says in Oration 14, *On
Peace*, “μηδ’ ὑπερσέβοντες, μηδ’ ὑποσέβοντες, neither over-worshiping nor
under-worshiping; for the former is impossible and the latter wicked and
impious.”

That the saints were neither worshiped nor invoked by them at that time could
be shown by various arguments. There is no mention of temples, altars,
images, feasts, gifts, and so forth dedicated to the saints, as is everywhere
seen among the adversaries. In the church's public worship they sang hymns to
God alone. In explaining the first precept and the Lord's Prayer, they did not
mention religious prayers to the saints as the Romans do. The canonization of
saints was unheard of and unknown among them. Many of them believed that the
souls of the saints dwelt outside heaven until the resurrection of the flesh.
Before the fourth century, pagans nowhere charged Christians with worshiping
the saints. And when the pagans worshiped God in the highest place and his
ministers in the second, Christians answered so constantly that they
obstinately denied adoring anyone but the one supreme God and nowhere
distinguished the worship of God from the worship of his ministers. These and
similar things clearly prove that such worship was unknown in the first
centuries. The celebrated Dallaeus collected them in book 3 of *On the Object
of Religious Worship*.

Although the error gradually prevailed in the course of time, becoming
evident at last around the year 370, there were still those who vigorously
opposed it. It was condemned by the Council of Laodicea, canon 35, and the
Third Council of Carthage, canon 23. It was attacked by Epiphanius against the
Collyridians who invoked the Virgin, *Heresies* 79, and by Augustine in *On
True Religion*, chapter 55. Augustine maintained that the saints are to be
*honored for imitation*, not *adored for religion*, with the *worship of love
and fellowship* that is owed to

[Printed p. 42 | Internet Archive PDF 70 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

fellow servants and fellow citizens, not the worship *of religion* that
belongs to God, in book 20 of *Against
Faustus the Manichaean*, chapter 21.

[Marginal heading: 7. From the testimonies of the adversaries.]

XVIII. Seventh, not a few of the adversaries support us here. Cassander, in
his *Consultation*, articles 20–21, notes various errors under this head. Vives
on Augustine's *City of God*, book 8, chapter 27, frankly admits that many
“Christians very often sin in a good matter because they venerate male and
female saints no differently from God; nor in the case of many do I see what
distinction there is between their opinion about the saints and what the
Gentiles thought about their gods.” Cardinal Richelieu, in book 3, chapter 4
of his *Method*, acknowledges that the primitive church did not invoke the
saints before the fourth century, and he claims that the Catholic Church does
not absolutely oblige Christians actually to pray to saints, p. 410. In this
way, of course, he attempts to remove the reproach of idolatry from his
church, but in vain, since the practice is confirmed by manifold sanction and
continual observance.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XIX. There is no valid inference from a civil appeal to living saints to the
religious invocation of the dead. Among the living there is a mutual exchange
of duties; between the living and the dead there is none (Isa. 63:16; Eccles.
9:6–7). The former rests upon God's command and promise and the approved
examples of the saints of the Old and New Testaments; the latter lacks these.
The former is merely social and an effect of mutual love; the latter is a
religious and principal part of the worship owed to God alone. The former
rests upon the love of the living alone; the latter also upon their merits.
Nor, if the departed saints are now endowed with greater love, does it follow
that they will to be invoked by us. Indeed, by retorsion, they would permit
this much less than they did in this life (Acts 10 and 14), because they now
know far more perfectly that this honor is due to God alone. And although we
could address them by voice or letter because they lived with us in our
earthly homeland, they must not therefore be invoked in their heavenly
homeland, which is very far removed from us. If invocation of saints rests on
the same principle as addressing the living, why does Paul, who so often
commands some to pray for others, never command anyone to invoke the saints,
and why is he himself never read to have prayed to Stephen, the Blessed
Virgin, or anyone else?

Therefore the Wallenburg brothers, in *Augustinian Method*, chapter 32, and
the bishop of Condom, in *Exposition of the Catholic Faith*, vainly attempt to
persuade us that the Roman Catholics invoke departed saints in no other way
than the Reformed ask living saints to pray for them. Their protestation is
contrary to the deed, and the matter itself cries out that asking for pardon,
protection, salvation, deliverance from evils, and similar things is one
thing, while asking for their intercession is another. Nor can the church's
intention be alleged here, where the words themselves prove the contrary,
unless we wish to say that the church intends to mock us by thinking one
thing and saying another, which no one would maintain.

XX. It is one thing for Jacob to wish that Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh
be called by his name and be two heads of tribes among Jacob's sons when he
says that he asks for *his name to be invoked over his grandsons* (Gen.
48:16). Tostado, Ribera, Cajetan, Cornelius a Lapide, Arias Montanus, and
others acknowledge this, just as Scripture speaks elsewhere in Isaiah 4:1.
It is another thing for him to will that they invoke him after his death,
which we deny.

[Marginal citation: Job 5:1.]

XXI. When Eliphaz advises Job to turn to one of the saints, he does not speak
of departed saints but calls for living supporters. He denies that Job can
produce any holy person who agrees with him and affirms that God afflicts the
innocent and just, as the antithesis and the course of the context teach. When
Job cries out to his friends in chapter 19:21, the term רעי means
*companions*, whose mercy he implores. Angels or heavenly inhabitants are not
meant, since they were not his associates and familiar friends, but those
whom he formerly had as companions and friends, whom he invites to
συμπάθεια, compassion.

Hence in the following verse he complains about the same friends, not about
angels. The *advocating angel, one among a thousand*, treated in Job 33:23,
denotes none other than the Angel of the covenant, Christ. To him alone can
belong the predicates attributed there: showing mercy, praying for
deliverance, declaring uprightness to a person, and interceding with the
presentation of a כפר, a λύτρον, a ransom, עליו, on his behalf, before him,
that is, the Father. For Christ alone can say מצאתי כפר, “I have found a
ransom.” Thus the Ordinary Gloss explains the text.

XXII. *Invocation* is one thing; *commemoration* is another. Moses does not
address Abraham or flee to his protection in Exodus 32:13. Rather, he directs
his prayers to God, asking him to remember the covenant he made with the
fathers and mercifully pardon their calf-worshiping descendants.

XXIII. The councils Bellarmine adds, held four hundred years after the time of
the apostles, could not introduce any new dogma of religion that had not
previously been found in Scripture or proclaimed for so many years in the
primitive church. They show what happened as the darkness of superstition
prevailed, not what ought to be done.

XXIV. It is established that in the first three centuries no father is found
as a patron of this invocation. Under Origen—who was, as Jerome says of him,
seemingly a good interpreter but a bad dogmatic theologian—the opinion about
the saints' intercession began to creep in among the doctors. Still, no one
therefore invoked the saints or taught that they should be invoked, until
gradually Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, and Nazianzen among the Greeks, and Ambrose
among the Latins, opened a wider window to this error. This arose partly from
Platonic philosophy and the remnants of Hellenism and partly from poetic and
oratorical license, hyperbolic praises, and apostrophes. Here the relevant
observation of Sixtus of Siena in *Bibliotheca Sancta*, book 6, annotation
152, is apt: “It ought to be remembered that the words of preachers are not
to be taken with all the rigor with which they first reach the hearers' ears.
For declaimers often proclaim many things by hyperbole, either induced by the
occasion of places, times, or persons, or carried away by the force of their
affections and the flow of speech.” The fathers also more often report that
saints *were invoked* than that they *should be invoked*. Or they use a
hypothetical invocation only hesitantly and with limitation, in such terms as
“as I think,” “unless I am mistaken,” “if the dead have any perception,” and
“if they have any care for our affairs.” When they do not express this
condition, it is understood to be implicit.
