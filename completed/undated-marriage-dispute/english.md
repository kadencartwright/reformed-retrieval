# On a Certain Marriage Dispute

*A fresh translation of the complete French response at CO X/1 238–239. The
collective response survives in a contemporary copy; CO reports that its final
release clause was added in Calvin's own hand.*

[CO X/1, p. 238]

Very dear lords and brothers. Since you have seen fit to have our counsel and
opinion concerning the case conducted before you over the marriage dispute
between Sir John Focard and Margaret Heberarde—namely, whether the promises
made between them ought to take effect—we, having assembled in the name of
God, have resolved by common agreement to declare to you what follows.

Having seen the proceedings and acts extracted from your register, we find
that the said Margaret, her mother, and her sister formally declare that she
was deceived and coerced. Her aunt (the widow of Sir Pierre Focard), the
brother-in-law, and his servant testify that they heard and learned it so,
although only after the act.

There remain three witnesses. One of them, named Alexis Myot, formally
testifies that he was present when the parties made the donation of their
bodies. The second, named Simon Gaillard, says that when it came to this
donation of bodies, the said Margaret said no word that he heard. The third,
Master Pierre Nemauso, a royal notary, says that when it came time to proceed
to the donation of bodies, he perceived from the said Margaret's bearing that
she took no pleasure in it, since she was weeping. This caused him to turn his
back, and he heard none of the words customarily pronounced.

He also testifies that from that day onward, when he greeted the said Margaret
by the name of fiancée, she always answered him that she was not one and that
he knew it well. Thirdly, when questioned in his bed, he testified that he
had not wished to receive the marriage contract which he had drafted, because
he knew well that it was not according to God, since the party did not consent
to it.

[CO X/1, p. 239]

It must therefore be noted that, however much ceremony there was for making a
promise of marriage—indeed, one repeated the next day—there is nevertheless
only one witness who testifies that the young woman spoke in order to agree to
it. The second says that he does not know whether she spoke. The third, to whom
greater credit ought to be given because he is a public notary and was called
to receive the marriage contract, testifies that he perceived an evident sign
of unwillingness.

Since this is so, there is no doubt that the party's testimony is admissible
for determining whether or not she consented. It remains to determine by whom
this can be proved. The notary is certainly a sufficient witness. The aunt,
the brother-in-law, the sister, together with Pierre Havart, ought, as it seems
to us, to count as one witness—especially when the mother confesses to having
forced her daughter. Thus, by both strictness and equity, it will always be
judged that the said young woman was forced and consequently that there was no
foundation for marriage.

In addition, the said Sir John Focard declares and affirms that over so long a
period of time he never had a means of speaking with the said Margaret and
never could learn that she loved him. Thus, as to the present case, we cannot
judge that there was a marriage. Rather, the act, having been performed
contrary to all reason and good order, ought to be held null.

Nevertheless, because this matter is delicate and much collusion between the
parties could occur, in order to forestall every harmful opening, to secure the
parties' peace, and to prevent reproaches and murmuring in the future, our
opinion has been that the said Sir John Focard should have the mother and
daughter summoned before the ordinary court, so that she may clear herself by
a solemn oath and confirm what they have already attested. A definitive
sentence can then be given, by which, once the said promises are declared null,

[Addition in Calvin's hand:] each party shall be set free to marry elsewhere.

## Translator's notes

1. The body addresses “lords and brothers,” says “we” assembled in God's name,
   and calls the decision one of common agreement. It has no date or individual
   signature. CO's notice of Calvin's handwritten final clause does not erase
   the response's collective voice.
2. “Donation of their bodies” renders the repeated legal formula *donnation de
   corps*. The translation retains the formula rather than substituting a
   modern marriage-law term.
3. The French gives no punctuation to disambiguate *La tante delle vefve de
   Sire Pierre Focard le beau frère et son serviteur*. The English construes
   three deponents: the aunt (Pierre Focard's widow), the brother-in-law, and
   his servant. The later list of the aunt, brother-in-law, sister, and Pierre
   Havart supports treating the brother-in-law as a separate person, but the
   manuscript should be checked before using the clause for a prosopographical
   claim.
4. The singular *quelle aie a se purger* follows a reference to both mother and
   daughter. “So that she may clear herself” preserves the singular without
   asserting more precisely which woman the copyist intended.
5. Every account of deception, coercion, tears, refusal, love, and consent is a
   reported declaration, deposition, confession, or finding within this
   historical proceeding. This edition does not independently verify any claim
   about the named people and is not contemporary legal or marital advice.
6. The English was translated afresh from [`french.txt`](french.txt) and
   checked against the facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not
   consulted or used for wording.
