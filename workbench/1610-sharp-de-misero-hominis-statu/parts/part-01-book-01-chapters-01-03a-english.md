BOOK I: ON SIN AND ITS PUNISHMENT

CHAPTER I

On Evil in General

Since we have resolved to discuss the wretched state of man under sin—the
knowledge of which leads us to the knowledge of ourselves, and that knowledge
to seek Christ and, in him, salvation and life—we reduce the following
discussion to two chief topics. The first concerns the evil of guilt, or sin,
and its punishment; the second concerns the powers left in man, wretched
through sin: what and how great they are, how far they can extend, and whether
they can lead man along the way of salvation. On both topics some of the
ancients and more recent writers have thought and written badly, especially
the papists. Following partly the heresies of the Pelagians and partly the
dreams of the Scholastics, they have fallen into the gravest heresies about
this doctrine, as will appear from the discussion that follows. In explaining
the first topic we shall have to treat: 1. evil in general; 2. the evil of
guilt, or sin; and 3. the evil of punishment.

Five foundations concerning evil in general must be maintained.

[1610 printed p. 22 | Google Books PA22]

I. Foundation. Evil is by nature nothing positive. Nor is it a negative
nonbeing, but a privative one. Privations, however, are of two kinds: 1. bare
privations, which merely deprive but do not dispose a thing badly, as rest is
the privation of motion; 2. privations which both deprive and dispose badly,
as disease is the privation of health. Thus sin signifies not only the absence
of good, but also badly affects and disposes the subject in which it exists.
Evil is then taken in two ways. First, adjectivally, as *kakon ti*, “some evil
thing,” as when we call a man evil; and in this sense what is called evil is a
positive being. Second, it is taken substantively, in itself and simply; and in
this sense evil is not a positive being but a pure privation. As for Thomas
Aquinas's statement near the end of question 1, article 1, *On Evil*, that evil
is a being not of reality but of reason and is thus good: this must be
understood not of the nature of evil considered in itself, but of the act of
the intellect. For evil's being understood is good, and the intellect which
understands something about evil understands well, since every act of
understanding as such is good. That evil is therefore not something positive
is proved as follows.

Argument I, from the nature of evil. Evil is nothing other than the absence of
the good required for the perfection of a creature or action. Hence the
Apostle calls it *anomia*, lawlessness, in 1 John 3:4. But the absence of good
posits no more than nakedness does, and so forth. Therefore.

Argument II. If evil were a positive being, it could be desired, since being
is by its nature desirable. But evil by its nature is not desirable; otherwise
it would be good, for good is defined as what all things desire. When some evil
is chosen, moreover, this is done not for its own sake but for the sake of a
true or apparent good attached to it. Therefore.

Argument III. Being and good are convertible; therefore

[1610 printed p. 23 | Google Books PA23]

if evil is positive, it is good. But this is absurd. Therefore.

Argument IV. If evil were a positive being, it would have been made by God
through Christ, John 1:3. But it was not made by God, because all the things
made were good, Genesis 1 and 2; 1 Timothy 4:4. Therefore. Thus Augustine, on
the words “without him nothing was made” in John 1, says that sin is nothing.

Argument V. Augustine, volume 5, *City of God* 2.22, says that every evil of
guilt and punishment is a privation of good. Anselm, *The Fall of the Devil*
20, says that evil simply, or simple evil, is not some entity but the pure
privation or defect of a good that is due. Ambrose, in the book *On the Soul*
which Augustine cites at the end of book 1 *Against Julian*, says, “What is
wickedness but a lack of good?” and so forth. Consequently evil is neither a
substance nor a real accident, and therefore not a positive being.

Objection. Amos 3:6 and Isaiah 45:7 say that evil is made by God; therefore it
is a positive being.

Response. God is said to make the evil not of guilt but of punishment. As
Judge he brings it upon people, not by effecting something, but by withdrawing
blessings and good things. When he sends famine through barrenness, for
example, he withdraws a blessing from the earth, restrains the influence of
the heavens and the sun's heat so that it does not warm, prevents it from
raising vapors, or does not permit the vapors already raised to descend. This
withdrawal of blessing is, furthermore, called good and truly is good. Thomas
Aquinas says in question 1, article 1, *On Evil*, that insofar as the order of
justice requires a sinner to be deprived of the good he desires, punishment
itself is thus good simply, because it is just and conduces to God's glory,
and so forth. A little later in the same place he says: “It is therefore clear
that evil is said to be created, not insofar as it is evil, but insofar

[1610 printed p. 24 | Google Books PA24]

as it is simply good and evil in a certain respect.” I therefore conclude with
Thomas that essential evil is nothing, but that the thing to which evil
happens is something. For the remaining philosophical objections which can be
raised, see Thomas, question 1, article 1, *On Evil*.

II. Foundation. There is no supreme and separate evil that is the first cause
of all evil, as the Manichaeans maintained when they posited those two
principles, one of good and the other of evil. This is proved as follows.

Argument I. If there is an evil of this kind, it either is eternal or began in
time. If eternal, there will be two eternal and therefore also infinite things,
which is absurd. If it began, it derived its origin either from itself, from
another evil, or from good. Not from itself, because nothing gives itself the
beginning of its own being. If from another evil, then it is not the supreme
evil. If from good, then it is from another; but what is from another is not
supreme. These are absurdities. Therefore.

Argument II. If there is a supreme evil, then there is some evil separate from
every good, just as that in which there is no evil is called the supreme good.
But there is no evil which is not in a good, because it is only a privation,
which cannot exist by itself, as was proved above. Therefore.

Argument III. Every evil comes from good, as will be proved afterward—and does
so accidentally and apart from nature's intention. But what occurs
accidentally is not the first and supreme principle, because what occurs
through itself is prior to what occurs accidentally. Therefore.

III. Foundation. Good is the cause of evil; that is, evil arises from a thing
that had been good. This is proved as follows.

Argument I. Everything that is in a subject comes from some internal or
external cause. But evil is in a subject privatively; it therefore comes from
some cause. This cause will be either

[1610 printed p. 25 | Google Books PA25]

a being or a nonbeing. It cannot be the second, because a nonbeing cannot be
the cause of any being; therefore the cause of evil is a being. But every
being is good. Therefore, if evil is from a being, good is the cause of evil:
namely, the free will of an angel or a man is the cause of the evil of guilt,
but God is the cause of the evil of punishment.

Argument II. Augustine, *Enchiridion* 14, says that evil cannot arise except
from good. Likewise Dionysius, chapter 4, *On the Divine Names*, says that good
is the beginning and end of all evils.

Objection. Matthew 7:18: “A good tree cannot bear bad fruit.” But the fruit is
called the effect of the cause. Therefore.

Response. Augustine, in the *Enchiridion*, says that by the tree understand
the will, and by the fruit the outward work. The sense, then, is that a good
work proceeds from a good will, just as an evil work proceeds from an evil
will; and the evil will itself is from a good thing, as a bad tree is from good
soil. Moreover, good is not the cause of evil through itself, but only
accidentally.

Objection II. Every agent produces something like itself. But the likeness of
evil does not preexist in good. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is true of a cause through itself, but good is the
cause of evil only accidentally, as will be proved afterward.

IV. Foundation. Good is not the cause of evil from itself, but only
accidentally. This is proved as follows.

Argument I. One opposite does not by its nature and through itself beget the
other; rather, it destroys it. Light does not of itself beget darkness, and
knowledge does not produce ignorance but expels it. But good and evil are
opposed like possession and privation. Therefore.

Argument II, from Thomas, article 3. Every effect through itself is assimilated
to its cause, because a cause acts insofar as it is in act. But every evil as
such is not assimilated to good insofar as good is an agent; therefore evil
does not have a cause through itself.

Argument III. Every cause through itself has a certain and

[1610 printed p. 26 | Google Books PA26]

determinate ordering toward its effect. But evil occurs by omitting an order,
while what occurs according to order is good. Therefore.

Argument IV. Whenever an efficient cause acts, it sets before itself a real or
apparent good; alternatively, what has a cause through itself is intended by
its cause. For what happens apart from an agent's intention is not an effect
through itself but an accidental one. Digging a grave, for example, is not the
cause through itself of finding treasure, because this happens apart from the
digger's intention. But evil is never intended by the agent. When Adam sinned,
he did not set disobedience before himself, but an apparent good attached to
it: “we shall be like gods,” and so forth. For whatever an agent intends is
desirable; but everything desirable has the character of good, to which evil
as such is opposed. Therefore.

Objection. When someone knowingly and willingly gets drunk, steals, and so
forth, he sets evil itself before himself. Therefore.

Response. I deny the antecedent. What he properly and for its own sake sets
before himself is the pleasure of wine and the usefulness of money, not the
vice of theft or drunkenness except accidentally, insofar as he cannot attain
those apparent goods without this evil. Thus no one does evil unless he
intends what appears good to him. To an adulterer it appears good to enjoy
sensual pleasure, and therefore he commits adultery, and so forth.

V. Foundation. Every evil is in a good, whether in a substance or in an
action. It is in a substance by the privation of that good which it could and
ought to have; it is in an action by the privation of that manner, order, and
measure according to which the action ought to be performed.

It must be noted that “good” is used in three ways. 1. The perfection of a
thing itself is called its good, as keenness of sight is the eye's good and
virtue is man's good, and so forth.

[1610 printed p. 27 | Google Books PA27]

2. The thing itself which possesses its perfection is called good, as a man
endowed with virtue, and so forth. 3. The subject itself is called good insofar
as it is in potency to perfection, as the soul is to virtue and the substance
of the eye is to keenness of sight, and so forth. Since evil is nothing other
than the privation of due rectitude, and privation exists only in a being in
potency; since, moreover, to be in potency is nothing other than to be ordered
to act, and that is said to be deprived which is naturally fitted to have
something but does not have it—it follows that evil is in good insofar as a
being in potency is called good. But good taken in the first way, as
perfection, is removed by evil; therefore evil cannot be in such a good. Good
taken in the second way, finally, as a composite of subject and perfection, is
capable of evil by reason of the subject but not of the perfection. For when
evil arrives the subject remains but the perfection is removed, as blindness
exists in the substance of the eye but removes sight. From these points it
follows that evil can in no way exist in the good that is pure act without any
admixture of potency, such as God is. That every evil is in good is proved as
follows.

Argument I. Evil is not through itself and essentially a being; therefore it
does not subsist by itself. But it is somewhere; and therefore, since there can
be no middle between good and evil, it necessarily exists in good as its
subject. Therefore.

Argument II. Evil is a privation. But every privation is in a subject, as
blindness exists only in an eye, and so forth. Every subject, however, is good
because it is a being. Therefore.

Argument III. Augustine says in *The Morals of the Manichaeans*: “I shall ask
what evil is; perhaps you will answer, corruption. But corruption is not in
itself, but in some substance which it corrupts.” This substance, however, is
good. Therefore.

Argument IV. Augustine, *Enchiridion* 12–14, teaches that nothing is

[1610 printed p. 28 | Google Books PA28]

so evil that it is not also good with respect to the subject in which it
exists; but every subject is a being and is therefore good. In chapter 26 he
says that evils cannot exist entirely without goods or except in goods.
Therefore.

CHAPTER II

The Division of Evil, with a Discussion of the Evil of Guilt

Evil is divided into the evil of guilt and the evil of punishment. Or, as
Tertullian says in book 2 *Against Marcion*, evil is either of transgression or
of punishment. Or, as Basil says, evil is divided into what is evil by its own
nature, such as sin, and what is evil and harmful to our perception. These
divisions come to the same thing.

It is asked first here whether evil is rightly divided into guilt and
punishment.

Response. Augustine, in the book *On Faith, to Peter*, so divides the evil of
the rational creature, which we are discussing here. Although one and the same
thing is sometimes both guilt and punishment, it is so in different respects.
Sin insofar as it is sin is not punishment, but insofar as God the Judge
consequently punishes a preceding sin by it, it is punishment. Guilt and
punishment differ: 1. in their efficient cause, for guilt is from the rational
creature, while punishment is inflicted by God the Judge; 2. guilt is the evil
of a nature or action, while punishment is the evil of the sinner; 3. guilt is
often voluntary, but every punishment as such is involuntary. For punishment
opposes the will in three ways, as Thomas holds in question 1, article 4: 1. it
opposes the actual will when someone knowingly undergoes punishment; 2. the
habitual will when a good is taken from someone unaware who would grieve if he
knew it; 3. it opposes the natural inclination of the will, as when

[1610 printed p. 29 | Google Books PA29]

someone is deprived of the habit of virtue who does not wish to possess
virtue, even though the natural inclination of the will is toward the good of
virtue. 4. Guilt often consists in acting, but punishment in suffering. Thus
Augustine, *On Free Choice*, calls guilt the evil we do, but punishment the
evil we suffer.

It is asked, secondly, whether the evil of guilt is worse and has more of the
character of evil than the evil of punishment.

Response. The evil of guilt is worse than any evil of punishment. This is so:
1. the more alien something is from the highest good, namely God, the more it
has the character of evil. But guilt is more alien from God than punishment,
because he is the author of punishment, not guilt. 2. The evil of guilt as
such cannot in any way be called good, but the evil of punishment as such can
in some way be called good, because as such it is from God and is the
execution of divine justice. Therefore. 3. The evil of guilt is opposed to
moral good, while the evil of punishment is opposed to pleasurable good. But
moral good is better than pleasurable good. Therefore. 4. According to
Augustine, *On the Nature of Good*, evil is a privation of order. But guilt is
farther removed from order than punishment, because guilt is disordered in
itself but is put in order through punishment. Therefore the evil of guilt is
worse than any evil of punishment.

The Hebrew word for the evil of guilt or sin is *ḥāṭāʾ*, that is, a wandering
from a prescribed target. In Greek it is *hamartia*. *Hamartanein* means to
attain or apprehend; with the privative particle prefixed, it means to stray
from what one ought to attain. It is also called *asebeia* insofar as it is
committed against the first table, *adikia* insofar as it is committed against
the second, and *kakia* or *ponēria*. The Latin *peccare* means to stray from
order in the

[1610 printed p. 30 | Google Books PA30]

manner of cattle.

Since sin is a concrete noun for which no abstract exists, and since it is a
privation, as we explained above, it cannot properly be defined. The Apostle,
however, describes it thus in 1 John 3:4: “Sin is lawlessness.” Augustine also
defines it in *The Harmony of the Evangelists* 1.4 and *The Loss of Grace* 1.1:
“Sin is transgression of the law.” Bellarmine, *The Loss of Grace* 1.1, badly
contends that Augustine's definition in *Against Faustus* 22.27 is a
definition of sin in general when he says that sin is something said, done, or
desired contrary to the eternal law. It is only a definition of actual sin,
not of every actual sin, but only of sin of commission.

CHAPTER III

On the Causes of Sin, and First on Its Efficient Cause

Properly speaking, sin—since it is a privation—does not have an efficient
cause, but a deficient one. Augustine therefore rightly says in *City of God*
12.7: “Let no one seek the efficient cause of an evil will; for it is not
efficient but deficient.” For teaching's sake, however, we shall identify the
causes which come closest to the nature of an efficient cause.

The efficient cause of the first and all subsequent sins of the angels is
their own free will. The efficient causes of men's sins, however, are of two
kinds: 1. internal; 2. external.

The external causes are of two kinds: causes common to all sins and causes
particular to certain sins. The common causes are either principal or
instrumental. There are five principal causes: 1. the Devil, Genesis 3:1;
Ephesians 6:11; 1 Peter 5:8; 1 John 3:4. 2. Men, by words or counsel, by deeds
or enticements, and by bad example, Genesis 3:6; Romans 3:16 and 12:2;

[1610 printed p. 31 | Google Books PA31]

1 Timothy 2:14. 3. Objects entering the senses, which stir up either desire or
anger and move the will through the senses. Objects do this accidentally and
because of their misuse. Thus the beauty and sweetness of the object enticed
Eve to sin, Genesis 2:6; 2 Samuel 11:2; 1 John 2:6. 4. God's law, and this
accidentally, because we strive after what is forbidden, and where there is no
law there is no transgression, Romans 3:24; 4:15; 7:7–8. 5. Incitements to
sin, such as idleness, softness, and so forth, 2 Samuel 11:2; Ezekiel 15:49.

Instrumental causes are of two kinds: 1. some are such through themselves,
being ordered toward such effects; 2. some are such accidentally, being
instituted for a use other than that to which we put them.

External causes particular to certain sins also exist. Thus the tree of the
knowledge of good and evil was an efficient cause of Adam's first sin, but
accidentally.

Internal efficient causes are either those which alone were in Adam when he
first sinned, or those which are in both Adam and us after the first sin.

The internal cause which alone was in Adam when he first sinned was his free
will, for unlike us he was not endowed with evil affections, ignorance, and
weakness.

There are seven internal causes which are in both Adam and us after the first
sin. 1. Concupiscence, or the corruption of nature itself, as in Romans 12 and
7:8; James 1:14–15. This precedes the will in us, but did not do so in Adam's
first sin. 2. The consent of the will, by which sin is roused by
concupiscence, conceived within, afterward brought forth into the light, and
completed, Matthew 5:28; James 1:15. The reverse occurred in Adam's first sin,
because 1. he willed to sin, and the corruption of nature followed afterward.
3. Ignorance, by which the will is often deceived,

[1610 printed p. 32 | Google Books PA32]

for we often choose things which we suppose are good but are not; if we knew
they were evil, we would not choose them, as in Proverbs 14:22; 1 Timothy 1:13.
4. Weakness, even in those endowed with knowledge. The Apostle calls these
weaknesses “passions of sins” in Romans 7:5, which the soul cannot resist
because of the weakness of faith; and so Peter denied Christ. 5. In some there
is resolute wickedness, which drives them inwardly to sin. Thus the devils and
Judas sinned, 1 John 3:8. 6. A habit and wicked custom of sinning acquired from
many acts. 7. Actual sins themselves are causes of other sins, and this in five
ways.

I. When through one sin that which restrained us from sinning is removed. Thus
original righteousness and grace were removed by Adam's first sin.

II. When God punishes sin by sins, Romans 1:25–26; 2 Thessalonians 2:11.

III. When one admitted sin disposes and inclines a man to perpetrate other
sins. Such are the sins of drunkards and fornicators, and so forth.

IIII. When one sin cannot be fostered without many sins following it. Hence
avarice is called the root of other sins, 1 Timothy 6:9.

V. When one sin is committed because of another. Thus Pilate condemned Christ
in order to preserve his rank and tyrannical office, while Judas betrayed
Christ because of avarice.

It is asked whether God is the efficient cause of sin.

Florinus, Montanus's disciple, according to Irenaeus as reported by Eusebius,
*Ecclesiastical History* 5.20, was the first to assert that God is the author
of sin. After him the Seleucians did so, according to Augustine, *On Heresies*
59. Finally the

[1610 printed p. 33 | Google Books PA33]

Libertines asserted the same thing. Bellarmine, *The Loss of Grace and the State
of Sin* 2.3 and following chapters, attempts to prove that the doctrine of
Calvin and others entails our asserting that God is the author of sin, and
that it is not men but God who sins. We shall answer his arguments afterward.
We, however, assert that sins fall under God's providence in two ways,
corresponding to providence's two forms: 1. conserving; 2. deserting.
Conserving providence has three degrees. 1. Continuation, by which each and
every thing is continued in the state in which it was created. 2. Completion,
by which God effectually completes in each and every thing what he wills; sin
has no place in these degrees. 3. Ordering, by which he orders and directs all
things, even the most disordered, toward their ends—namely, his glory. In this
sense sin falls under God's providence.

Deserting or permitting providence is that by which God does not restrain the
actions of others, but permits the manner of them to the agent's will. In this
sense also sin falls under God's providence, in which permission God's
operation is no less effectual than in conservation. Those who have dreamed
that there is a bare permission in God therefore err. This desertion is of two
kinds: total desertion, which, if it were granted, would cause things to return
to nothing; and partial desertion, when he deserts only in part. Partial
desertion is in turn either first or derived from the first. The first was
that by which God deserted the angel and man, each perfect in his own manner,
not by withdrawing his hand from their being, but by withdrawing from the
continuation and action of grace, to which he was in no way obligated. What
arises from the first desertion is the daily withdrawal of grace, which God is
obligated neither to give nor continue to any creature. This is not an
efficient but a

[1610 printed p. 34 | Google Books PA34]

deficient cause of sin. For, as Thomas rightly says in question 3, article 1,
on the cause of sin, something is an efficient cause of sin in two ways: 1.
because it sins itself; 2. because it causes another to sin. Neither can apply
to God.

Sin therefore falls under God's providence: 1. because he foresaw from
eternity that it would occur; 2. because he most wisely permitted it to occur,
and for the best reasons, namely that he might be glorified both in justice
and in mercy; 3. because he orders and governs it by his providence: 1.
subjectively; 2. insofar as it serves the execution of justice, namely insofar
as he punishes sins with sins; 3. insofar as he directs sins toward good ends.
It therefore follows that God is not the author of sin, which is also proved
as follows.

1. Argument. Scripture testifies that God is not the author of sin, Exodus
20:3; Deuteronomy 32:4; Psalm 5:5; Ecclesiastes 7:29; Hosea 13:9 and 6:1; John
8:44; Romans 9:14; James 1:13; 1 John 2:16 and 3:8, and so forth. Therefore.

2. Argument. God forbids and hates sin, as Scripture testifies throughout.
For God is the cause only of that which he loves. Therefore.

3. Argument. God is most perfect and is perfect goodness, but the effect of a
cause simply and infinitely good cannot be evil. Therefore.

4. Argument. Scripture teaches that God created all things very good, and that
man and the angel are the cause of sin, Genesis 1 and 3; John 8:44; Romans 1:12
and following; 1 John 8:8; Jude 3. Therefore.

5. Argument. Every sin is committed because one departs from the order that
looks to God as the ultimate end of all things. But God inclines all things
toward

[1610 printed p. 35 | Google Books PA35]

himself as their ultimate end, and does not turn away from himself, because he
is the highest good. Therefore.

6. Argument. Every sin arises because someone in acting does not reach the end
for which he ought to act, and this happens because of a defect in the active
principle. But the active principle cannot be deficient in God, because he is
of infinite power, nor does he fall short of a due end, because he is
infinitely good. Therefore.

7. Argument. Properly speaking, sin has no efficient cause. Therefore. The
antecedent is proved: an efficient cause is only the cause of good things,
because every efficient cause, by effecting something, posits something in
being. But privations, such as sins are, posit nothing in being; rather, they
denote the absence of things that truly are. Sins, moreover, are not good
things. Augustine therefore rightly says in *City of God* 2.7: “Evil has no
efficient cause, but only a deficient one.” Therefore.

8. Argument. What properly has an efficient cause also has a properly so-
called end. But sin does not have a properly so-called end, because an end is
a being, and is therefore good and a thing's perfection. Therefore.

9. Argument. Augustine says in *Eighty-three Questions* that man is not made
worse with God as his author. But through sin man is made worse; therefore God
is not the author of sin.

10. Argument. According to Fulgentius, *To Monimus* 1, God is not the author
of that of which he is the avenger. But God is the avenger of sin; therefore
he is not its author.

11. Argument. Athanasius says in his oration *Against the Idols*: “That, he
says, would at once be absurd and impossible.” And afterward: “Otherwise it
could not be regarded as good, since it would possess a mixed and contrary
nature,” and so forth.
