CHAPTER XVII.

ON SYNODS.

ACTS 15:6.

“Therefore the apostles and presbyters assembled to consider this matter.”

Here we have a distinguished testimony for the authority of councils. In this
question both the Roman Catholics and the Separatists have gone wrong: the
former exalt the authority of councils beyond measure by attributing
sinlessness to them, while the latter depress it below its proper place. We
take the middle way and inquire how much should be attributed to councils,
since the Arminians abolish every necessity for councils. We therefore premise
the following considerations in place of distinctions.

1. The constitutions of synods are understood to

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 693 | PDF p. 742 | split word joined: obligare]

bind either authoritatively, as commands of God, or charitably, as counsels
and exhortations.

2. A friendly exhortation or counsel binds both because of the intrinsic
goodness in its object or subject matter, insofar as the counsel of brethren
is the very Word of God, and because of the authority of the friend who
counsels and exhorts. For everyone who counsels, as such, is a superior; and
all superiors are to be obeyed according to the fifth commandment of the
Decalogue.

3. Consequently, a twofold superiority must be recognized in the fifth
commandment: one of dominion and jurisdiction, and another of reverence and
gifts. The former superiority is an inadequate and narrower subject of the
fifth commandment.

4. All friends, brethren, and equals endowed with greater grace, experience,
and light are to be acknowledged as superiors insofar as they counsel what is
right and good for us from God's Word. Had David rejected Abigail's counsel
and persuasion when she dissuaded him from inordinate vengeance, he would also
have rejected God's counsel and sinned against the fifth commandment, no less
than if the high priest or a prophet of God had commanded by prophetic
authority the very thing Abigail had only privately advised. There is this
difference, however: private advisers are superiors by a superiority of
reverence, not of jurisdiction, and they do not have the power of

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 694 | PDF p. 743 | split word joined: Ecclesiasticarum]

ecclesiastical censures to exercise against those who spurn and despise their
counsels and exhortations. A synod and the church, however, do have that
power.

5. The power of judging, or aptitude, must be distinguished from the
ecclesiastical right to judge. A lower presbytery has the right to judge many
matters concerning which it cannot actually judge for lack of gifts. Such
causes must therefore be committed to provincial or national synods.

6. Although the government of the church through synods is a matter of
positive law, yet, once this foundation is laid—that the Lord and Savior has
committed the power of the keys to every particular church—the government of
churches united in a province or nation is a matter of natural law.

7. Synods are necessary for the well-being of the church, and in the visible
church there are always synods, whether composed of fewer members, as in a
parochial church, or of more, as in a provincial or national church or in a
general and ecumenical council. For greater and lesser do not vary the
species. The same argument that proves it lawful for a parochial synod to
assemble also demonstrates that a general synod of the whole visible catholic
church is lawful.

8. Whoever grants an association of the authorities of particular churches
must

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 695 | PDF p. 744]

acknowledge the authority of both provincial and national synods over
particular churches.

Hence our first assertion: A general council, or general synod, is an assembly
of pastors, teachers, and elders, convened in the name and by the authority of
Jesus Christ from all the visible churches at some one place to treat those
matters that concern the church's faith, morals, and good order, or the right
administration of discipline. On this definition one may consult James of
Almain, *On Ecclesiastical and Lay Power*, chapter 15, and also John Gerson's
treatise *On Ecclesiastical Power*. They give the same definition of a
council, except that they would have a general synod consist only of those who
are in the hierarchical state and be assembled in the name and by the
authority of the Roman Pontiff. Almain nevertheless concedes that a general
council can be assembled without the intervention of the Roman Pontiff's
authority in three cases: 1. When the pope is naturally dead, or civilly
dead—that is, excommunicated for some crime or heresy—because, he says, it is
known that the apostolic see was vacant for two years. 2. When the pope is
hostile and refuses reformation. 3. When the time and place have been appointed
by the preceding general synod, as was done at the Council of Basel.

The Roman Catholics acknowledge that from Matthew 18, “Tell the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 696 | PDF p. 745 | split word joined: Ecclesiæ]

church,” it is rightly proved that a general council is of divine right. 1.
Because a council is a means of saving and edifying everyone, both pastors and apostles.
2. Because although the apostles were God-inspired and infallible when
prophesying and writing canonical Scripture, they could nevertheless err in
other respects. And if Peter had stubbornly adhered to Judaism and had not
obeyed Paul or the church when it corrected him, he would undoubtedly have
been excommunicated. 3. By “the church” in Matthew 18 we cannot understand
only prelates and bishops, says the Parisian School in *On the Power of the
Church*, page 17, because in those words Christ addresses Peter, a prelate.
Peter, however, cannot be accuser, judge, and witness, say Almain and Gerson.
They therefore judge Peter himself bound to obey the church. 4. The power of
the keys was given immediately by Christ to all the rulers of the visible
catholic church; therefore so was the exercise of this power. Although, by a
kind of physical necessity because of wars prevailing in the Christian world
or other impediments, its exercise may be impossible for the Christian church,
it is nevertheless morally lawful. For many things are inconvenient, and thus
inexpedient here and now because of the corruption of human nature, which are
nevertheless morally lawful.

Assertion 2. Every particular pastor, even outside a synod, has the power of
jurisdiction in preaching and determining truth

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 697 | PDF p. 746 | split word joined: secundùm]

according to God's Word. Jeremiah 1:10: “See, I appoint you this day over the
nations and kingdoms, to root out and demolish, to destroy and overthrow, to
build and to plant.” Behold, oversight and power are granted to one definite
pastor apart from the church. 1 Timothy 6:17: “Charge those who are rich in
this age not to be high-minded.” 2 Timothy 4:1: “I therefore charge you in the
sight of God,” and so forth; “preach the Word,” and so forth. Paul, therefore,
as a pastor and before the synodical determination was made in Acts 15, could
determine by preaching that circumcision was not necessary and that it was
necessary to abstain from things strangled, blood, and fornication. Yet an
individual pastor does not have the full power of jurisdiction over his own
acts of preaching.

1. Because the church can deprive him of such power for just causes.

2. Because he cannot employ the censure of excommunication against those who
despise the preached Word unless the jurisdictional power of the whole church
intervenes.

3. Because he alone cannot synodically determine the same truth that he had
previously determined in preaching by pastoral power, since one individual
pastor is not a synod. Accordingly, the apostle James in Acts 15:15 can only
say, “This is my judgment,” and so forth. Therefore, although that judgment of
James was the very

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 698 | PDF p. 747 | split word joined: Verbum]

Word, which James as an ordinary pastor could preach, it was nevertheless not
a synodical dogma or an ecclesiastical constitution except insofar as it was
determined by the church assembled in synod. So Samuel, as a prophet, was
furnished with the immediate power to anoint David as king. Nevertheless,
Samuel, as a member of the kingdom of Israel, did not have power to anoint
David king at Hebron separately and apart from the whole kingdom. For that
anointing and inauguration of David as king could be performed only by all the
estates of the kingdom, not by one particular prophet.

Assertion 3. Synods have authoritative power to bind all the particular
churches in the place where the synod was assembled.

1. Because if Matthias was admitted to the apostleship in Acts 1 by the common
votes of the church assembled in synod, then all particular churches are bound
to regard Matthias as an apostle. Nor can this inference be evaded on the
pretext that this synod was apostolic and extraordinary.

1. Because the adversaries cite this very passage to establish the ordinary
power of the entire multitude, by which the assembly of all Christians has the
right to elect its pastors by free votes until Christ's second coming.

2. James of Almain and other Roman Catholics draw the forceful conclusion from
this passage that Peter, and therefore the pope, is subject to a general
council,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 699 | PDF p. 748 | split word joined: quoniam]

since Peter himself was unwilling to elect Matthias as an apostle without the
consent of the other apostles and the whole church assembled there in synod.

3. If this synod was extraordinary and apostolic, why were the votes of the
whole multitude required? For whatever is concluded by extraordinary and
apostolic power can and should be concluded without the consent and votes of
the multitude.

2. Likewise in Acts 6, if the apostles did not ordain the seven deacons by
ordinary synodical power but by apostolic and extraordinary power, why is the
election of the deacons attributed to the whole multitude? Verse 5: “This
speech pleased the entire assembled multitude, and they chose Stephen,” and so
forth.

3. In Acts 15 the synod of several visible churches established synodical
decrees—which in chapter 16:4 are called “the decrees of the apostles and
presbyters,” and which bind other visible churches—in verse 18. Synods,
therefore, have right and authority over particular churches. Those who say
this synod is not a rule and pattern for other synods are greatly mistaken.
Their reasoning rests on this synod's threefold privilege: 1. That it was an
apostolic synod. 2. That the Holy Spirit was present at the synod. 3. That the
matter synodically determined was canonical Scripture itself.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 700 | PDF p. 749]

But we answer: 1. The synod should no more be denominated apostolic because
apostles were members of it than it should be called presbyterial and
ecclesiastical because its members were not only apostles but also
presbyters, brethren, and—if the adversaries are to be believed—the whole
people.

2. This answer opens the way to evading all the promises made to the pastors
of the Word and the church of Christ until the consummation of the world. For
all these promises were made primarily to the apostles themselves. These
promises—“Behold, I am with you until the consummation of the age”; again, “I
will send you another Comforter, the Holy Spirit, who will lead you into all
truth”; again, “I will give you a mouth and wisdom which the adversaries will
not be able to resist,” John 16:13; Matthew 28:20; Matthew 10:19–20; Luke
21:14–15—were fulfilled in Acts 2, Acts 5:29, and Acts 4:8–10, when the
apostles were filled with the Holy Spirit. But because these promises were
both made to and fulfilled in the apostles, on the adversaries' hypothesis it
follows that they are not made to us at all. For we are neither apostles nor
immediately inspired, nor, when synodically assembled in Christ's name, do we
have the promise of such a presence of the apostolic Spirit as was both
promised and truly poured out upon the apostles. But this is very absurd. For
the same Holy Spirit, although

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 701 | PDF p. 750]

not in the same measure as was promised to the apostles, is promised also to
us and to the whole catholic church, to be assembled synodically until the
consummation of the age.

3. That in this synod the apostles furnished us with the model, type, and
living image of an ecclesiastical and ordinary synod—not an apostolic and
extraordinary one—for our imitation until the end of the world is evident
from various circumstances in the text. For in verse 2 Paul and Barnabas were
sent as delegates of the church to the apostles and elders concerning this
question. But Paul, as an apostle, knew the whole mystery of the gospel
perfectly by supernatural revelation, Galatians 1:12, 16–17; Ephesians 3:3–4,
9. Therefore, he was not sent as an apostle to learn more through a synodical
determination than he already knew perfectly. He was sent to the synod as an
ordinary pastor, therefore, for our perpetual imitation in such cases.

2. Because in verse 6 the apostles assembled to consider this matter. Yet as
apostles they had no need whatsoever of any synodical assistance. Indeed, when
Paul was taught the mystery of the gospel, Galatians 1:16–17, he did not
consult with flesh and blood, and thus with no synod. Therefore, the apostles
assembled as ordinary pastors for our imitation.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 702 | PDF p. 751]

3. Verse 7 says that there was much debate and disputation concerning this
matter. Yet the apostles, as apostles delivering canonical Scripture, had no
need of disputation, debate, or consultation shared with others. For in writing
Holy Scripture they were illumined by the immediate irradiation of the
infallible Spirit.

4. They present their judgments in order at the synod: first Peter in verse 7,
then Paul and Barnabas in verse 12, and last of all James in verse 13. The whole
synod, with the Holy Spirit assisting, acquiesced in verse 22 in James's
judgment as more exact and complete than the others. But if they had presented
their judgments as apostles, there would be no reason why the entire synod
should acquiesce in James's judgment rather than Peter's, Paul's, or Barnabas's,
since equal apostolic illumination and infallibility guided them all alike.

5. Finally, the synod's decree is a judgment in which the apostles,
presbyters, brethren, and also, in its own place, the whole church equally
acquiesced after laborious debate and investigation of the truth, with
counsels exchanged on both sides. But nowhere in Scripture do we read that
the brethren either were immediately God-inspired and infallible or were
collateral causes with the apostles in dictating or writing canonical
Scripture. For the apostles themselves, if they had acted by the apostolic
Spirit, could

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 703 | PDF p. 752]

outside a synod have proposed for belief to absolutely all the churches this
doctrine about abstaining from blood, things strangled, and fornication, no
less than the article concerning Christ's death and resurrection. The
alternative is that their purpose was to leave to all posterity a model of
synods and of what is to be done in the church until the consummation of the
age whenever disputed questions and conflicting judgments arise concerning
articles of faith: namely, according to the practice of the apostles, the
question is to be committed to the synodical determination of pastors,
teachers, and elders.

First, therefore, we argue as follows. If, according to the law of nature, no
one can be judge in his own cause, then an appeal can be made from the
presbytery of one particular congregation to a presbytery of several
congregations assembled in synod. But the former is true; therefore so is the
latter.

For 1. When Paul and Barnabas had a great dispute with the false brethren
about the necessity of circumcision in Acts 15:1–2, they could not be judges
in that cause in whose judgment the opposing party would be willing to
acquiesce, and so they appealed to the Jerusalem synod. Likewise, when the
churches of the Greeks and Hebrews disputed about alms and neither party had
any power of jurisdiction over the other, the matter was referred to a synod
composed of the twelve apostles and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 704 | PDF p. 753 | split word joined: Particularibus]

particular churches, and there the matter was judged and determined.

2. It is foreign to Christ's wisdom and providence that he should have
instituted no remedy for relieving the poorer and afflicted whenever they are
oppressed by particular churches, although this is especially the concern of
Christ, Psalm 72:4. Experience also cries out that Diotrephes sometimes rules
as a lord and the evil servant excommunicates his fellow servants. Jerome
complained against the Luciferians that in the time of Constantius and Valens
the Arians held power. And Athanasius, in his book *On the Solitary Life*,
complained that the church was oppressed by the Arians no differently than it
had once been oppressed by the priests of Baal in Elijah's time. Basil, Letter
17, says: “We can now say that in this time there is neither ruler, nor
prophet, nor prayers, nor offering, nor incense.” Hence the author of the book
entitled *The Burden of the Church*, whether Ockham or someone else, chapter
42, and Francis Pico della Mirandola, in his oration to Leo X, lament the
calamities of the church: that there is neither faith, truth, religion, nor
discipline; that ecclesiastical dignities, benefices, bishoprics, the papal
dignity itself, and everything in the church are for sale. Hence Ockham and
James of Almain loudly insisted that an ecumenical synod was necessary,
because the pope cannot be judge in his own cause. Hence Emperor

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 705 | PDF p. 754 | split word joined: Ludovicus]

Louis the Bavarian, as related in the *German Chronicle*, book 4, page 297,
appealed from Pope John XXI, ill-informed, to a general council and the pope better
informed. For the emperor had been excommunicated because he had assumed the
title of emperor without the pope's confirmation. Sigismund, Duke of Austria,
appealed from Pius II to the next succeeding pope and a general council.
Likewise, Philip the Fair appealed from Boniface VIII to the apostolic see and
a general council, as Platina says in the *Life of Boniface*. The gloss on
canon law, canon 27, question 3, also forbids anyone to judge in his own cause.
The University of Paris appealed from Leo X, who impiously rejected the
Council of Basel, and Luther likewise appealed from the same Leo to a general
council.

From all these cases it is clear that it is a matter of natural law for those
injured and oppressed by the presbyteries of particular churches to appeal to
more general and higher synods.

2. When common enemies, heresiarchs, and false teachers pollute and ravage
many particular churches, all the particular churches ought by nature's own
impulse to come together in one general synod, uniting and joining their
authorities to drive away the common enemy. Thus James of Almain holds that
the charitable and declarative power of convening a general synod belongs to
all private

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 706 | PDF p. 755]

persons, although authoritative power belongs to the pope alone—or, as we
teach, to all the rulers. Hence in his treatise *On the Authority of the
Church*, last chapter, proposition 4, he says: “For if the right hand were
bound, or at the nod of imagination obstinately refused to devote itself to
defending the body, then the entire authority to defend the body would reside
with the left hand. And if one part of a province were unwilling to resist
enemies seeking to destroy it, who doubts that the remaining part, although
smaller in number, would then have authority to defend the whole province?”
Certainly, it is entirely agreeable to nature that if foreign enemies invaded
a kingdom or a province of the kingdom, all the kingdom's inhabitants would
be bound by the law of nature itself to repel the public enemy with united
forces. In the same way, when several associated particular churches form one
visible body and maintain among themselves one external and visible communion
in the Word, in participation in the sacraments, and in mutual admonitions,
exhortations, and so forth—visibly united under the one head, Christ—they ought
with united forces to repel all errors, scandals, and heresiarchs and cast
them out of this visible kingdom of Christ. They must do this not only as
Christians but also as churches, since every part, and all the parts joined
together, must watch over and provide for the salvation and security of the
whole.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 707 | PDF p. 756]

The reason for this connection is that if a communion of gifts ought to
flourish among Christians, then so also should a communion of authorities
among sister churches as such. This argument rests on the necessity of synods.
For the adversaries are forced to acknowledge the necessity of synods in
which sister churches help one another by mutual counsels, admonitions, and
exhortations. Yet they deny the authority of synods and their jurisdictional
power over particular churches. But this is in fact to deny the necessity of
synods. For the Jerusalem synod in Acts 15, which they recognize as a lawful
and necessary ecclesiastical assembly, commanded and imposed its doctrines
and decrees upon particular churches not merely by way of counsel and
brotherly persuasion but by way of authority and jurisdictional power. Verse
28: “For it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to impose no further
burden upon you besides these necessary things.” To impose the burden of a
command, however, is not merely to counsel or persuade. And chapter 16:4: “As
they passed through the cities, they delivered to them for observance the
decrees that had been determined by the apostles and presbyters who were at
Jerusalem.” Nor is the synod an invention of the Roman Catholics but an
institution of Christ. Hence it was enacted at the Council of Basel that a
general council should be assembled within five years, then within seven years
after the council's dissolution, and third,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 708 | PDF p. 757 | split word joined: decennio]

that an ecumenical council should be assembled every decade, and that the
Roman Pontiff had no right to exceed that prescribed time. Therefore,
advocates of purer discipline among the Roman Catholics, such as Gerson in *On
the Church* and Francis Zabarella in his treatise *On Schism*, complained
bitterly that, contrary to what had been enacted at the Councils of Constance
and Basel, there were now no general councils. Bellarmine, *On Councils*, book
1, chapter 6, acknowledges that the Council of Pisa was convoked against
Julius II and that for this reason the same Julius rejected that council at
the Lateran Council. And because many general councils condemned Roman
Pontiffs and stripped them of papal dignity, and also dared to strike the
heresies and errors of the Roman Church with censures, those who vigorously
contended for the papal dignity and the primacy of the Roman See dared to deny
the necessity of general councils. Thus Bellarmine, *On Councils*, book 1,
chapter 10, says: “Just as during those three hundred years after Christ's
birth the church remained safe without general councils, so it could also
have remained so for another three hundred, and again for another five
hundred, and for another thousand.” And Peverius, *Commentary on Exodus 19*,
disputation 2, number 14: “It is vain to do by more what can be done equally
well by fewer.” Pighius, *On the Hierarchy of the Church*, book 6, chapter 10,
calls the general council an invention of Constantine the Great, unsupported
by the authority of God's Word. And

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 709 | PDF p. 758]

Clement VII said that general councils are in some way useful, but dangerous
if the primacy of the Roman Pontiff and his power are called into question.

The Arminians likewise deny the necessity of councils, as do the Socinians.
Thus the Remonstrants teach in *Apologia*, chapter 15, folio 289, that “a
decision made in a synod is not to be regarded lightly, but rightly inclines
our minds to a more careful consideration of the decision made. But reason
does not permit it to prescribe to anyone, or compel a dissenter to assent or
obey.” From Episcopius, disputation 33, thesis 4: “We determine that synods are
useful for establishing divine truth and averting or extirpating heresies and
errors only in the case that they are assembled to deliberate, discuss,
examine, and persuade by reasons and arguments. To put an end to disputes
concerning heads of religion otherwise than by persuasion is to introduce
tyranny into the church and, if not wholly to abolish the liberty of
consciences, at least to constrain and bind it severely.” So also among the
Socinians, Theophilus Nicolaides says in his refutation of the *Treatise on the
Church* that errors or controversies are not removed in this synodical way,
but only violence is inflicted upon our consciences.

Similarly, the Separatists, denying the authority of synods, say that
synodical decrees have only as much weight as the authority and reason they
derive from God's Word, and that those decrees are persuasive, not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 710 | PDF p. 759 | split word joined: mandatoria]

mandatory or commanding, unless violence is to be inflicted upon consciences.
But if this reasoning were valid, it would follow that the preaching of the
Word and all pastors commanding faith and obedience in Christ's name could
only persuade and could not command with authority. For the preached Word, no
less than a synodical decree, has as much weight as the authority and reason it
has from God's Word. Yet it is certain that pastors can command in God's name,
Galatians 6:9–10; 1 Thessalonians 2:13. Furthermore, no authority would belong
to confessions of faith, creeds of orthodox doctrine, or declarations, since
all creeds and all confessions possess only as much authority as they have
from God's Word. We nevertheless deny that the conclusions of synods possess
absolute, unlimited, and peremptory authority; but it does not therefore
follow that all authority of synods should be abolished.
