# CHAPTER XI

## Jackson's opinion concerning fate and necessity is examined

With Scripture we assert that the soldiers divided CHRIST'S garments, that
they did not break CHRIST'S bones, and that the Medes devastated the Chaldeans
most contingently and freely. They did so by a choice in no way pressed by
force, but by an indifferent judgment of reason, since in most deeds of this
kind they sinned gravely. At the same time, these things occurred necessarily,
with modal necessity, κατά τι, *in a certain respect*, and from the supposition
of the divine decree, so that such a division of the garments, such a
non-breaking of the bones, and such a devastation by the Medes could not fail
to occur. God therefore decreed that all these things should occur
contingently.

Jackson objects in the same place: “GOD'S will, then, is not fulfilled if a
person does not do what God wills and commands him to do. They give two
answers to this. 1. Every person's conscience accuses him of by no means doing
GOD'S will on earth as the Angels do in Heaven. 2. GOD fulfills his will by
punishing that person whenever it is not fulfilled by him.” Response. All the
Arminians and

[Marginal summary: Jackson, together with his Arminians, is ignorant of what
the will of the sign is and what the will of good pleasure is.]

[Chapter XI; printed page 119; PDF page 170]

Jesuits are utterly ignorant of the nature of the will of the sign. It is as
impossible to resist GOD'S properly so-called will, or to prevent it from
being done even when it is done through free agents, as it is to resist GOD'S
Omnipotence, or for God to fall from his counsel and purposes, fail in his
promises, or fall short of his oath. Scripture has demonstrated above that all
these things are impossible. Sinners therefore do not resist GOD'S will by not
doing what his approving will prescribes. “For GOD omnipotently does whatever
he wills, even through free agents.” Isaiah 14:26–27; Psalm 115; Isaiah 46;
etc. (2.) GOD'S punishing will is always fulfilled in human beings, but the
question now concerns GOD'S will to be fulfilled by human beings. Our
adversaries blasphemously imagine that here GOD falls short in will, counsel,
decree, intention, purpose, promise, and oath. But since this will of GOD that
is to be fulfilled by human beings is never fulfilled, for us it is not GOD'S
properly so-called will, but only his approval of the creature's duty, whether
or not that duty is actually performed. As for the object of the approving
will precisely as such, God did not decree that it should come to be or exist
in the world of created things, nor did he will from eternity that it should
come to be, as Jackson maintains.

“The Platonists,” says Jackson in chapter 20, “derive most human events or
outcomes from the order and disposition of the heavenly causes. When this
disposition cannot be overcome by secondary causes they call it fate; when it
can be overcome by industry, so that it cannot conquer sloth, they call it
fortune.” Response. The question is not what fate is for Plato, but what it is
for Jackson, the Arminians, and the Jesuits. If future contingents occur before
and apart from every decree or intention of GOD, then with respect to GOD,
though not to human beings, they are fortuitous—and what could be more absurd?
2. With respect to contingently operating causes, everything can be overcome
by human industry with God's aid and concurrence. Do we therefore eat, drink,
walk, trade, speak, sleep, watch, repent, and escape the blows of the angry
deity by fortune? Certainly not. But in relation to GOD'S decree, by which all
things are fixed and determined, nothing can be overcome, because JEHOVAH'S
counsel and his thoughts stand forever: Psalm 33:10–11; Isaiah 13:24 [as
printed]; Isaiah 46:10–11; Hebrews 6:16–17. This remains true even when God
executes those invincible and fixed counsels through human beings—the Medes,
the Persians, and other agents acting with complete freedom.

[Marginal note: What fate is for Plato according to Jackson. Plato rather
mocks fate and seems in the *Gorgias* to ascribe the opinion concerning fate
to women. He says that women are to believe “ὅτι τῶν εἱμαρμένων οὐδ’ ἂν εἷς
ἐκφύγοι”—“that no one could escape the things allotted by fate”—that no
one could escape fate.]

“Most creatures,” he says in chapter 21, “are the subject of contingency; the
human race

[Chapter XI; printed page 120; PDF page 171]

or human society is contingency's proper sphere, outside whose circumference
neither fortune nor fated events wander. Yet not every part of a human being is
subject to fate. This,” he says, “is an immutable fate: those who do good
receive rewards, while those who do evil are punished, according to Paul's
statement in Romans 2, ‘GOD will render to everyone according to his works.’
Human actions and choices themselves are contingent, not fated—otherwise the
very nature of virtue and vice would be removed—but the outcomes or
consequences of the actions are fated, not contingent.”

[Marginal summary: Jackson includes rewards and punishments within fate—that
is, within GOD'S wise decree—but excludes all other beings from GOD'S
providence and decree, as if the governance of all things belonged to blind
fortune.]

Response. If *fate* is taken for GOD'S wise, immutable, and absolute decree,
by which God determined all things with himself and declared in his eternal
mind everything that occurs in time—as we understand it, apart from any
relation to the stars or heavens—then not only the human race, but all
necessary and contingent things, goods and evils, things future, present, and
past, prosperity and adversity, famine, lack of food, the generations and
corruptions of all living things, growths and diminutions, fertility, abundant
produce, honor, disgrace, barrenness, bereavement, numerous and fruitful
offspring, and whatever occurs or never occurs but can occur—such as CHRIST'S
deliverance from death by more than twelve legions of Angels and the breaking
of his bones—either occur by this fate, that is, by the divine decree, or they
all roll along by fortune and chance, whatever atheist people may say to the
contrary. Nor does Scripture restrict fate, that is, GOD'S decrees, to
“punishments and rewards and the outcomes and consequences of contingent
actions” any more than to the actions themselves and everything enumerated
here. Jackson dreams otherwise, professing wisdom above and beyond what is
written. Because Paul subjects rewards and punishments to GOD'S will, does he
exclude all other things from divine fate and immutable providence? By no
means. The sermons of the Prophets are full of this, as are those of CHRIST
and the Apostles. They ascribe winds, rains, storms, fair weather, creeping
things, little sparrows, every bird, fish, brute beasts, and all their motions,
actions, approaches, and withdrawals—indeed, everything without exception—to
this fate, that is, to GOD'S wise counsel and providence.

“Blows or punishments,” he says, “are then properly called fated whenever God
cannot repent or retract his sentence. It is when GOD'S eyes are closed to
human tears and his ears are stopped against prayers.” Response. Jackson quite
clearly implies that God can repent of everything which he decrees or
establishes with himself from eternity, whether good or evil,

[Chapter XI; printed page 121; PDF page 172]

whether they flow from him effectively or permissively, unless they are
punishments deserved by the hardened—those punishments indeed are fated. God
can therefore retract what he established with himself from eternity
concerning his Son's incarnation, the faith and perseverance of the Elect,
the Church's deliverance, the destruction of enemies—if they are not extremely
hardened—the immutability and stability of the new covenant, and the gospel
promises. That is to say, God is mutable. A sentence of GOD or any external
dispensation of GOD is not the fate of which we speak; for God governs
external actions by fate, that is, by his wise decree. Otherwise, the
Arminians here insinuate an internal change and revocation of GOD'S counsels,
which Conrad Vorstius and the Socinians blasphemously fabricate concerning
God. Thus punishments are absolutely decreed, as is the non-breaking of
CHRIST'S bones. Indeed, all things that occur or do not occur are fated, since
all things occur by fate, that is, by the immutable counsel of the Omniscient
and Most Wise GOD. Not even a shadow of change can fall upon God concerning a
sparrow's flight any more than concerning eternal punishments or the rewards
of human beings and Angels.

Jackson: “With respect to certain objects, a human being has true liberty,
choice, or contingency, and is given by his Creator the power of producing
variations in antecedents in thought, word, and deed. But once the antecedents
have been produced by human beings—though not without divine cooperation—God
alone decrees the consequences, without any concurrence or vote from the
human being.” I answer: 1. No one among us denies that a human being has true
liberty and choice. But the question is whether this liberty is absolutely
indifferent and free from every determination, even the gentlest, by which it
is limited to one side of a contradiction—although with respect to the manner
of tending toward that side the choice is completely free. We most certainly
believe that so unrestrained a choice is opposed to GOD'S dominion over the
acts of creatures; to the special promise of the covenant of grace concerning
the production of new obedience in the Elect; to the confidence of prayer by
which God bends even the hearts of the most hostile enemies, and our hearts,
toward himself and toward walking in his ways; to consolation and hope; to his
special care and love for his own; to the mercy and grace of election which
separates the Elect from others left to their native hardness; to the
immutable and absolute decree; to the sole, primary, and invincible cause of
the futurition of every creaturely act; and to the omnipotence of divine
grace. 2. But if a human being freely and absolutely has every variation of
the antecedents—namely, simply to hear

[Chapter XI; printed page 122; PDF page 173]

or never to hear, to believe or not, to pray, to repent or not, to persevere or
fall away—then God decrees nothing among these things. Alas, what an idle,
slumbering, Epicurean providence! 3. Resulting punishments often come through
the free ministry of the Chaldeans punishing the obstinate apostasy of the
Jews, or through Absalom, Shimei, and a rebellious and apostate people, by
which God exacts vengeance for the sins of David and Solomon. Thus the events
and consequences no more occur with God alone as Author than do the
antecedent evil or good actions which rewards and punishments follow.

Jackson: “For every possible good deed or offense of a creature, whether moral
or spiritual, punishments or rewards corresponding to its degree and weight
have been appointed from eternity, though not with absolute necessity, since
the eternal decree left to a person the possibility of not offending in this
or that kind or to this degree, or, if he should offend, of praying for pardon
and mercy.” I answer. You see, dear Reader, that neither good nor evil works,
nor their degrees, are decreed by God as future. Everything is left hanging
and entrusted to the Epicurean possibility of acting or not acting. But why
should punishments or rewards be decreed so peremptorily, and not good and
evil actions and their corresponding degrees as well? All things are written
in his book before they exist. He numbered from eternity the drops of water,
grains of sand, and our thoughts—Isaiah 40; Psalm 139. Did he entrust all these
things to the new Lady Goddess Possibility, or to Contingency, the Goddess and
Queen of Heaven? 2. Woe to us if from Eternity he let loose the reins of choice
to the mere possibility of offending or not offending, more gravely or more
lightly. How much effort these atheists expend in contradicting Scripture:
“He chose us before the foundations of the world were laid, that we should be
holy.” He chose us that we might bear fruit; he predestined us that we should
be to the praise of his glory. Others were προγεγραμμένοι, *foreordained*,
to this judgment. Jackson entrusts the whole business of election and
reprobation, and their means, to the mere possibility of offending or not.
Ah, wicked man, “you have entrusted the sheep to the wolf.” Meanwhile, God has
punishments and rewards ready under such a disjunction, appointed in one
degree or another, in case good or evil people should happen to be the
craftsmen of their own eternal fortune. God has determined to deal with them
in this way. Where, I ask, is the decree and ordination of the means as well as
the ends? 3. God therefore fixedly and immutably decrees nothing concerning a
creature's future good deed or offense. But Scripture says that we are
appointed and predestined to walk holily—Ephesians 1:4—and to good works,
chapter 2:10,

[Marginal summary: Jackson and the Arminians leave everything to the
possibility of fortune and wandering contingency, as if GOD had not determined
all things from eternity by his immutable decree.]

[Marginal references: Ephesians 1; John 15; Ephesians 1:6–8.]

[Marginal summary: Scripture abundantly demonstrates that each and every

decree, and his invincible providence.]

“to obtain salvation through Christ,” apprehended, I believe, freely by faith,
“not to wrath”—1 Thessalonians 5:9; “through sanctification
of the Spirit and belief in the truth”—2 Thessalonians 2:13. These are not
wandering possibilities. We were “chosen and ordained according to the
foreknowledge of God the Father, through sanctification of the Spirit, εἰς
ὑπακοὴν καὶ ῥαντισμὸν αἵματος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ”—“for obedience and
sprinkling with the blood of Jesus Christ.” God therefore does not leave us to
a possible sanctification, an obedience most contingently indifferent and
wandering, absolutely free—or rather fortuitously fickle, slippery, possible,
and changeable. I do not suppose our being “predestined to conformity with the
Son of GOD,” Romans 8:29, to be merely possible. We were “placed or appointed
to suffer afflictions” through the Devil's instruments—1 Thessalonians 3:3.
Indeed, the object of GOD'S decree or special providence is never established
in Scripture as wandering and indifferently possible, but as something
actually future or nonfuture. Such are: sparrows falling to the ground,
Matthew 10:29; the hawk spreading its wings toward the south rather than the
north, and the eagle's flight, Job 39:26–27; “the drawing up of drops of water,
their falling upon human beings, the spreading of thick clouds, and crashes
for the judgment or blessing of a people,” as it seemed good to God, Job
36:27–30; and GOD'S “command given to thunder, snow, showers, and rain, so that
human hands are sealed from work in the fields,” Job 37:3–7, concerning which
Elihu speaks in verse 11. “Finally, God himself by their changes turns over in
his wisdom his counsels concerning their work, whatever he commands them to do
upon the face of the world, upon the earth,” verse 12. “Whether for a scourge,
for his land, or for beneficence, he makes it ready.” Indeed, the determinate
things—not wandering possibilities—of this most wise decree are: the warming
from clothing, chapter 37:16; “the foundations and cornerstone of the earth,”
chapter 38:5–6; “the shutting and bounding of the sea so that the earth is not
overwhelmed by the rushing excursions of its swelling waves,” verses 8–10;
“the dawn's rising to terrify evildoers,” verses 12–13; “the storehouses of
snow and hail prepared for the day of battle,” verses 22–23; “east winds,
underground channels, rain, dew, ice, the influence of the Pleiades, Orion's
bands, the risings and settings of Arcturus and the northern constellations,
the most efficacious actions of the heavens upon these lower things, and the
serenity of sapphire clouds and air,” verses 24–25 and following; “the feeding
of the most ravenous lions and ravens,” Job 39; “the births of wild goats and
deer, and the number of their months of pregnancy,” verses 4–6; “the wild
ass's dwelling, fierceness, and pasture,” verses 8–10; and “the wild ox's
fierceness—it can serve GOD, though not human beings when they command it,”
and so forth. All these things, which are in themselves possible,

[Chapter XI; printed page 124; PDF page 175]

any wise person who has read the book of Job and Holy Scripture can easily
understand to be fixed and determined to one outcome by GOD'S wise decree,
namely, to a certain futurition. Next, the casting of lots and the hereditary
division of the land by lots among Israel's tribes happened in the same way,
Joshua 14–18 and 20–22: according to Jacob's prophecy, which preceded the
casting of lots by many years, it was to occur from GOD'S singular and most
wise decree, Genesis 49, and likewise Deuteronomy 33. Thus Proverbs 16:33. And
who does not see that the division of CHRIST'S garments and casting lots for
them—which was never fulfilled in David—likewise the piercing of his hands and
feet, Psalm 22:17–18; the non-breaking of CHRIST'S bones, Psalm 34:20; and
CHRIST'S nativity in Bethlehem, Micah 5:2, were decreed by God to occur through
Augustus's freely imposed census, Luke 2:1–3, and Joseph and Mary's free
journey from the city of Nazareth into Judea, to David's city called Bethlehem,
in order to be registered? By GOD'S decree determining to one outcome a thing
possible in itself, the lot similarly fell to Matthias, not Joseph Barsabbas.
I pass over infinitely many other things of this kind. Considered in
themselves and with respect to that unrestrained and slippery possibility
arising solely from GOD'S productive power and in no way depending upon his
will and decree, they have no cause or reason why they never exist rather than
exist, unless we take refuge in GOD'S free counsel. But Scripture and all
theologians of sound judgment have hitherto established GOD'S particular
counsel as the primary and invincible cause of all things—life, death, honor,
disgrace, poverty, riches, good and adverse fortune. The Jesuits and Arminians
object, I admit, especially Gabriel Pennotto and Thomas Jackson, who devised a
new fiction, GOD'S disjunctive decree, along with the fabricated middle
knowledge.

We do not deny that the mode of acting—namely, freely and contingently—is
decreed by GOD. For example, God decreed that Peter should believe freely,
that is, with no necessity of nature, but from an internal, living, and more
than spontaneous choice and from the indifference of a judgment that perceives
no connection with the ultimate end so simply necessary that Peter could not,
of himself and in the divided sense apart from GOD'S decree and
predetermination, repudiate what he chose. Nevertheless, this does not prevent
the act of believing in Peter from being determinately future and determined
by GOD'S decree. Jackson therefore speaks most falsely when he says that
“whatever exists is not the object or part of the object of the divine decree,
but only that which”

[Marginal reference: Jackson, *On Providence*, chapter 22, section 2.]

[Chapter XI; printed page 125; PDF page 176]

“is capable of being persuaded toward good or evil and is changeable in its
inclination by fear of punishment or hope of reward. Magistrates take care
that insane people and rabid dogs do no harm; but they do not bind dogs from
biting, or mad people, by laws or statutes. Instead, they command people who
use reason to take care that dogs and the deranged are restrained so that they
do no harm. God's decree concerning the governance of human beings is the rule
and standard of all human decrees and presupposes a human being capable of
reason and more receptive of divine sanction than brute animals. The proper
and formal object of the divine decree is the regulation of this human
contingency and liberty for obtaining or averting the outcomes and
consequences of mercy, justice, and kindness, which are exactly proportioned
to the nature and quality of the choice and counsel.”

[Marginal summary: Jackson maintains that only the possible is the object of
the divine decree.]

Response. Scripture says that everything which exists is decreed by God. In
addition to what we have said, Romans 11:36–37, Revelation 4:11, and Proverbs
16:4 prove the same. Jackson denies providence, and therefore God himself, if
he denies that each and every thing occurs by GOD'S counsel, decree, and
special providence. Scripture proclaims this, the theologians' systems and
books proclaim it, the commonplaces demonstrate it, the harmonies and
confessions establish it, and the catechisms themselves teach it. (2.) The
movements of rabid dogs and the speech, words, utterances, and deeds of insane
people come about by God's decree, Matthew 8:28–30. Are punishments not decreed
for sinners? Jackson concedes this in chapter 21. But dogs' bites are very
often punishments inflicted by the Just Judge: 1 Kings 14:11; 16:4; 21:19;
2 Kings 9:10, 36, 24; 2 Kings 22:38; Jeremiah 15:3. God imposes
a law upon dogs and all creatures so that they do not harm his people, Exodus
11:17. Indeed, he seals the sun and stars so that they do not rise; he forbids
the winds to blow; he addresses fish, the sea, and rivers, and dried things
tremble. Therefore he decreed that all these things should occur, Ephesians
1:11. This atheist restricts providence and GOD'S decrees to a human being
insofar as he acts freely. That is, he decreed that Peter could believe and
could refrain from believing. Behold grace! Behold Jacksonian providence! But
the possible as such does not fall under a decree, for every such possible
thing exists through GOD'S creative power, by necessity of nature,
antecedently to GOD'S free will. And if we suppose the impossible—that God
decrees nothing—Peter can speak or walk, and refrain from speaking or walking.
So far is a freely acting human being, considered precisely as capable of
acting or not acting, from being the formal object of the divine decree. On
that account everything would occur by fortune or by the creatures' own
providence. For what does God

[Chapter XI; printed page 126; PDF page 177]

do by decreeing that a person can act or not act more than fortune or the
creatures do? He created the human being free, of course, but this gift is the
power to act freely. No decree here makes Peter more able to believe than not
believe, or not believe than believe. The same must be said of all contingent
actions. Yet since nothing is more opposed to providence than fortune, Jackson
makes mere fortune and the changeable indifference of things—things which can
exist or not exist when providence is put to sleep—the proper and formal
object of the decree. This is to establish black as the object of white and
darkness as the object of light. 2. It is false that “the divine decree
concerning the governance of a human being is the rule or standard of human
decrees.” For human laws are to be regulated with respect to morality by
GOD'S word and revealed will. But GOD'S decrees, by which God permits all the
crimes that occur, are not for us the Rule of moral actions.
