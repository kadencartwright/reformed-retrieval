1 TIMOTHY 1:15.

“This saying is sure and worthy in every way to be received: Christ Jesus came
into the world to save sinners, of whom I am chief.”

In these words we have three things asserted by Paul concerning faith.

1. Its object is the Word of God insofar as it is true. Hence the Gospel word
is called a sure saying in relation to the intellect, which ought to be
certain of the truth of the Gospel word.

2. Its object is good in relation to the heart, will, and affections. Hence
the Gospel is called a saying “worthy of all acceptance” (ἄξιος πάσης
ἀποδοχῆς), a saying worthy to be received in every way. This is the formal and
specific object of saving faith: namely, Christ not only insofar as he relates
true things, which the intellect apprehends, but insofar as he promises good
things—namely, remission of sins, righteousness, and eternal life.

3. In these

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 542 | PDF p. 593 | split word joined: verbis]

words, “of whom I am chief,” he sets down the inseparable effect of faith.
Hence one who believes truly and savingly applies all the Gospel promises by
name to himself. Hence

It is asked whether faith is not a special trust in mercy, but rather such a
confidence—as the Remonstrants define it in their *Confession*, chapter 11,
section 2—by which I firmly determine that it cannot happen that I escape
eternal death and attain eternal life otherwise than through Jesus Christ and
by a way other than that prescribed by him. We deny this.

1. For in this way the object of faith is not Christ and his mercy toward all
believers, but the history of the Gospel, by which I firmly believe that I
shall escape hell and attain eternal life only through Christ and through his
way prescribed in the Gospel. In this way the Jews placed confidence in Moses
and Joshua on account of the promised land: namely, that they would escape
from Egypt and obtain the land of promise in no other way than that which had
been prescribed by Moses and Joshua. To believe in Christ in this way is only
to believe Christ when he relates that people attain eternal life through
repentance and faith. But this is historical faith, which exists in demons and
many reprobates. For they can believe and firmly determine that it cannot
happen that they escape eternal death and attain eternal salvation otherwise
than through Jesus Christ and by a way other than that prescribed by him. The
object of this

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 543 | PDF p. 594]

faith is not Christ, who died for sins and was made by God wisdom,
righteousness, sanctification, and redemption for us, but the bare way
prescribed by Christ for arriving at eternal life—that is, the mere letter of
the Gospel and a historical account.

2. By “the way prescribed by Christ through which we may escape eternal death
and attain eternal life,” they understand—as they themselves explain in the
same place, *Confession*, chapter 11, section 4—the way of true obedience, or
of good works. For in the same place they define their “obedient confidence”
in this way. Hence this is the faith of the Arminians and Socinians: “I
trustingly rest and rely upon my good works prescribed by Christ in the
Gospel, by which I confidently hold that through them, and by no other way, I
shall arrive at eternal salvation and escape eternal death.” Hence they do not
have even a syllable about Christ dead and satisfying for our sins in the
object of their faith. Our theologians, however, validly conclude that Christ
is true God, consubstantial with the Father, because Christ is the object of
faith and our Mediator and Surety, who by his death and blood made the fullest
satisfaction to God the Father for our sins. But they want Christ to be the
object of faith insofar as he historically relates and declares to us the true
way and path for arriving at eternal salvation. On that account the apostles,
who in their canonical writings also relate to us the true

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 544 | PDF p. 595 | split word joined: rationem]

way and path for arriving at eternal salvation, are objects of faith. Hence it
sufficiently clear that they covertly deny Christ to be God consubstantial
with the Father, and then define saving confidence and faith for us as mere
confidence in our good works prescribed by Christ. Paul, on the contrary,
contends in Romans 4 that Abraham's justification through faith excludes all
confidence and boasting in good works.

It is certain that the Gospel of Jesus Christ, which commands good works as
lower causes of eternal life, is only the object *by which* faith acts, while
Christ the Mediator, who died for sins, is the object *which* faith
apprehends. Hence they define faith admirably indeed as “trust and confidence
in our good works prescribed by Christ so that we may attain eternal life.”
For who does not know how empty a hope, how insubstantial a consolation, how
unstable a joy, and how deceptive, futile, and shadowy a peace of conscience
trusting reliance upon our good works brings in the day of temptation and at
the final point of life? The wavering consciences of Papists who repent among
us bear witness: in the agony of death they cast away and loathe confidence in
their works and merits, and flee to the satisfaction accomplished by Christ
alone through his precious death and blood.

3. In the Scriptures true faith is not merely

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 545 | PDF p. 596 | split word joined: firmus]

a firm assent to the way of worshiping God prescribed by Christ, which is the
historical and dogmatic faith of the Romanists. Beyond the assent of the mind,
it is defined as the heart's trust in God through the Mediator and a trusting
reliance by which the sinner rolls himself into Jehovah's bosom. Hence אֶל
(“to”) and שָׁעַן עַל (“to lean upon”), πιστεύειν ἐπὶ or εἰς Θεόν (“to believe
upon” or “into God”). Proverbs 3:5: “Trust in the Lord with your whole heart”
(בְּטַח אֶל יְהוָה). Isaiah 50:10; Psalms 71:6 and 125:1. So also Isaiah 48:2:
“They leaned upon the God of Israel.” נִסְמְכוּ derives from סָמַךְ, “he leaned,
relied.” Psalm 112:8: “His heart is supported.” And the unusual verb שָׁעַן,
in the Niphal נִשְׁעַן, means “to lean, to rest upon”; it occurs in Isaiah 31:1
and 2 Samuel 1:6: “Saul was leaning upon his spear.” It is also employed in
Isaiah 10:20: “Those who escaped from the house of Jacob will lean upon
Jehovah, the Holy One of Israel.” From it also comes מִשְׁעָן, “staff, rod,” in
2 Samuel 22:19 and Isaiah 3:1, “the staff of bread.” Thus we rely upon God in
Christ as a weary traveler relies upon a rod or staff. The Hebrews also
express trusting by a verb that signifies rolling oneself upon God. Psalm
22:8: גֹּל אֶל־יְהוָה, “he will roll himself upon”—properly, “roll toward”—
Jehovah. So Jeremiah 34:4: “The host of heaven will be rolled up like a
scroll.” Genesis 29:10: “Jacob rolled the stone from the mouth of the well.”
Psalm 37:5: “Roll your ways upon Jehovah.” Hence the person who believes rolls
and turns himself and his burdens upon God, which is far more than assenting
to God as one who relates true things. So ἔρχεσθαι εἰς τὸν Χριστὸν, “to come
to Christ,”

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 546 | PDF p. 597]

in John 6:37 and Matthew 11:28–29, is to believe, John 6:46. So πιστεύειν εἰς
or ἐπὶ Θεὸν, “to believe into” or “upon God,” Romans 10:11 and John 14:1. So
τὸν Χριστὸν λαμβάνειν, “to receive Christ,” John 1:12.

4. Faith and reliance upon God are opposed to confidence in horses, chariots,
flesh, and the arm of men, Isaiah 31:1 and Jeremiah 17:5. But to trust and rely
upon a horse is not to believe a horse when it speaks true things, but to rely
upon and rest in the horse's strength and speed with affection and trust.
Faith, therefore, is essentially trust in the heart.

5. Fear is opposed to faith. Matthew 8:26: “Why are you fearful, O you of
little faith?” Fear, however, is not in the mind but in the heart and
affections. Since contraries are in the same subject, it follows that faith
also is not bare assent in the mind, but trust in the heart.

6. The object of justifying faith is the mercy of God in Christ, by which he
justifies the ungodly, Romans 4:5. But the object of the Remonstrants'
confidence—by which we determine that we shall be saved in no other way than
that prescribed by Christ in the Gospel—is no more mercy toward the elect and
believers than it is God's omnipotence, truthfulness, and justice when we
believe that eight souls were saved in Noah's ark or that God can kill a
thousand Philistines with the jawbone of a donkey.

7. This faith of the Remonstrants is pure Socinianism. For Smalcius the
Socinian, in *On the Divinity of Christ*, chapter 14, defines faith as not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 547 | PDF p. 598 | split word joined: tantùm]

only assent but also confidence, “by which we place hope and confidence in
Christ; we depend entirely upon him, with a firm hope of attaining those
things which he promised to those who obey him—that is, if we reform our life
according to his prescription,” and so forth.
