# ON THE CHURCH

Thus far we have dealt with Christ the Mediator, his benefits, and the means
by which he bestows these benefits and applies them to his own. We must now
speak of the subject to which these benefits are applied, namely, the Church.

In Hebrew the Church is called קהל in Deuteronomy 5:22 and עדה in Numbers
16:2, that is, a congregation. In Greek it is ἐκκλησία, from ἐκκαλεῖν, “to
call out” or “summon.” Among the Athenians it signified an assembly summoned
from the rest of the populace by the herald’s voice to hear the senate’s
decision—an assembly that the Latins call a *concio*. By preeminence, the word
is transferred to sacred matters, which surpass human matters in every way:
it signifies the holy assembly called by the heralds of the Word to hear the
divine oracles, or called from the world to heaven, from Satan’s kingdom to
God’s kingdom in Christ. No one joins himself to the Church of his own accord;
each and every person is anticipated by God’s call. The translators rendered
those Hebrew words by συναγωγήν. The apostles, however, used the word
ἐκκλησίας rather than συναγωγῆς, because συναγωγὴ

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 5 | printed col. 10 | bsb10175568]

signifies any sort of gathering, even of irrational and inanimate things,
whereas “Church” signifies only a gathering of rational beings.

Furthermore, the word “Church” includes the idea of calling, from which anyone
who wishes actually to be a member of the Church must take his beginning.
Socrates, however, calls it σύναξις in book 6 of his *Ecclesiastical History*.

In Scripture the word “Church” signifies various things.

1. It signifies an assembly consisting only of the ungodly, summoned or
dispersed by civil governors, as in Psalm 26:5 and Acts 19:32.

2. An assembly of God’s people summoned by ecclesiastical governors, as in
Acts 2:39 and chapter 11:26; 1 Corinthians 11:18 and 14:4; and afterward.

3. By a metonymy of the adjunct, an assembly of those professing one faith,
even if they have not been summoned into one place, as in Acts 8:1, 3 and
9:31.

4. An assembly consisting only of the saints triumphant, as in Ephesians
5:27.

5. An assembly consisting only of the elect militant, Acts 8:28.

6. A visible assembly mixed of believers and reprobates, Acts 5:11.

7. A particular church considered without its pastors, 1 Timothy 3:5;
Revelation 2:1.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 6 | printed col. 11 | bsb10175568]

8. The pastors and people of a particular church together, Acts 1:20.

9. By synecdoche—putting the whole for a part—it also signifies the senate of
presbyters, as in Matthew 18:17.

10. One household that professes Christ, as in Romans 16:5.

11. By a metonymy of the adjunct it signifies a sacred building among Latin
writers; see Augustine, book 3, *Questions on Leviticus*, and elsewhere.

12. It is used for the universal assembly of elect men, both triumphant and
militant, as in Matthew 16:18; Ephesians 1:22 and 5:23; Colossians 1:18. In
this sense it is called the Catholic Church in the Apostles’ Creed, where we
say, “I believe the Church,” not “I believe in the Church.” (The Nicene Council
does add the preposition *in*, as do the Papists, contrary to Augustine,
Cyprian, and others.) For trust does not rest in the Church as it does in God;
rather, “I believe the Church” is said:

First, because whatever Satan may contrive against it, we believe that it will
always exist.

Second, because we believe not only that it exists in general, but that we are
its members.

Third, so that we may profess that we join ourselves to the true Church and
wish to live and die as its true members.

The Papists therefore ineptly attribute the epithet “Catholic Church” to the
Roman Church, since in former times the Roman Church was only a particular
church.

What it is today, however, can be called a church only in the first sense.

This Catholic Church is the city of the living God, or the assembly of holy
people who by God’s gracious election have been called into union with Christ
the bridegroom (νυμφίῳ), unto eternal life.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 6 | printed col. 12 | bsb10175568]

The principal efficient cause of the Catholic Church is God—Father, Son, and
Spirit. (No one has the Church as mother unless he has God as Father.) He is
the same with respect to creation, gathering, and administration, but the mode
of operation differs. The Father is the invisible efficient cause with
respect to election and visible with respect to creation. The Son is
invisible with respect to the efficacy by which redemption is applied, but
visible by reason of the sacrifice offered on the cross and its promulgation,
the Gospel. The Holy Spirit is invisible with respect to regeneration and the
internal call, but visible with respect to the external call; see Ephesians
1:7–8 and 1 Corinthians 5:18.

The proximate efficient cause of the Church’s gathering and administration is
Christ, the head of the Church.

The cause moving God from eternity to bring the Church into being is his own
good pleasure (εὐδοκία), Romans 9:10; 1 Timothy 2:4.

The cause serving this purpose in time is the merit and intercession of
Christ, John 17.

Taken generally, the instrumental cause is twofold. 1. The external cause is
the Word of God, communicated inwardly, outwardly, or in both ways; hence it is
called the seed of the Church, Hebrews 4:11, and its foundation, Ephesians 2:20.
2. The internal cause is faith.

Taken specifically with respect to the members, the instrumental cause is
twofold. 1. With respect to adults, it is effectual calling through the
preaching of the Word, Romans 10. 2. With respect to infants and others to whom
that means is not given, it is internal illumination, as in Romans 8:9 and
Luke 1:44.

The administering causes with respect to adults are the heralds of the Word,
as in 1 Corinthians 3:10 and Ephesians 4:11–12.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 7 | printed col. 13 | bsb10175568]

The adjuncts of this bringing into being are that some become members of the
Church sooner and others later, John 10:16; Matthew 20:5 and verse 2; and that
this formation, begun at the origin of the world and continued without
interruption, is to continue to the end of the world.

The material cause of the Church is twofold. 1. Its remote and common material
cause is the human race, from which the elect are called out to eternal life.
By that calling the rest are rendered inexcusable, John 10:16; Romans 1:16.
Angels, therefore, are not members of the Church, as is proved by these
arguments.

Argument 1. Only those for whom Christ gave himself to sanctify them are
members of the Church, Ephesians 5:16. But he did not give himself for angels
in order to sanctify them, because the good angels remained in their holiness
and therefore had no need of sanctification, while the evil angels have not
been delivered. Therefore.

Argument 2. The whole Church and all its members are said to have been
redeemed by Christ’s blood, Acts 20:28. But all the redeemed were formerly
captives under sin and were thus justified in Christ, things which do not
apply to the good angels. Therefore.

Argument 3. In Scripture Christ is called the mediator of God and men, but
nowhere of angels. Therefore.

## OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. God is said to have gathered all things in heaven and on earth in
Christ. Therefore he gathered angels too, and consequently they are members
of the Church.

Response. As often elsewhere, so also here the universal particle must be
restricted to the subject under discussion. Here, however, he speaks

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 7 | printed col. 14 | bsb10175568]

of the Mediator’s office, and Christ is called the mediator of God and men.
Moreover, by those who are in heaven he means the Church triumphant, and by
those who are on earth, the Church militant.

Objection 2. Christ is called the head of men and angels. Therefore angels are
members of the body, namely, of the Church.

Response. This is the fallacy of taking a qualified statement absolutely, for
Christ is called head in two respects.

First, properly, with respect to the Church.

Second, with respect to all natures subject to him insofar as he is mediator.
He is head of the angels in the latter, not the former, sense, just as he is of
all the other creatures, which nevertheless are not members of the Church.
Moreover, he is called head of the angels because they are ministers of the
Church, not because they are members properly so called.

Second, the proximate and proper material cause is the elect, not the
reprobate. This material is considered in two ways.

1. With respect to God, in which regard it is absolutely perfect, because all
things are present to him.

2. With respect to us, in which regard it is not wholly prepared and perfect,
but is gradually perfected through calling and justification; see 2
Corinthians 5:18–19; Galatians 4:6.

The formal cause of the Catholic Church is considered in two ways: with
respect to the whole or with respect to the parts. Considered with respect to
the whole, it is communion with Christ the head through faith, and the
communion of the members with one another through love. Moreover, what faith
effects in adults, a hidden knowledge of Christ effects in infants. It follows
from this that all who

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 8 | printed col. 15 | bsb10175568]

are members of the Catholic Church are elect, but not conversely. Not all the
elect are actual members of the Church, since before calling and justification
they are not actual members of it. It also follows that all who are to be
saved must be incorporated into the Church through faith and the knowledge of
Christ, because outside the Church there is no salvation, and no one has God
as Father who does not have the Church as mother.

Considered with respect to the parts, the formal cause varies according to
their diversity. In the part triumphant in heaven it is perfect—in the highest
degree in Enoch, Elijah, and the saints who rose with Christ; less perfectly in
others, whose soul alone is in heaven. In the Church militant it is still less
perfect, because here, successively and in varying degrees and beginnings, we
have communion with Christ through faith as our faith receives its growth.
From these things it follows that the Catholic Church, considered in itself,
is invisible, because we believe this Church, as the Creed says, and faith
concerns invisible things, Hebrews 11:1.

Two things are to be considered in this form of the Church: 1. The joining or
union of Christ with the Church. 2. Communion.

The joining is Christ’s and the faithful’s mutual obligation to love one
another; it is also that love itself. Its efficient cause on Christ’s part is
the Holy Spirit, as in 1 Corinthians 6:17; Romans 8:9; 1 John 3:24. Hence it is
called the fellowship of the Holy Spirit, 2 Corinthians 13:13.

The efficient cause on our part is twofold. First, the internal cause is
faith, Ephesians 3:17. Second, the external cause is the Word and the
sacraments.

Two things are to be considered in this union. First, obligation. Second,
love.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 8 | printed col. 16 | bsb10175568]

The obligation between Christ and the Church is explained in Scripture by the
analogy of betrothal, as throughout the Song of Songs; Hosea 2:19; 2
Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:29; Revelation 19:7 and 21:2, 9–10. This
betrothal is effected through effectual calling, through the Holy Spirit’s
internal operation and the external preaching of the Word. From these arises
faith, by which we apprehend Christ’s gracious love and are stirred to love
Christ in return. It is sealed, as if by a betrothal ring, through baptism and
the Holy Supper.

The mutual love between Christ and the Church is illustrated by the analogies
of the love of bridegroom and bride, the adherence of branches to the vine,
John 15:5, the relation of members to the head, Ephesians 2:22 and 5:23, and
mutual indwelling, John 14:23 and 15:5; Ephesians 3:17; Revelation 21:2–3.

Christ’s love toward the Church is considered in two ways:

1. In itself.

2. With respect to its effects.

In itself it is considered in two ways. 1. As an effect of the divine will,
namely, gracious election, John 13:18. 2. As an effect of Christ’s human will,
John 13:1.

The effects by which Christ has demonstrated and does demonstrate his love
are found both in words and deeds. In words, he did so in his own person,
either before the incarnation in various appearances or most fully after the
incarnation. He also does so in the person of ministers, through whom he both
promulgated and promulgates promises and prescribed duties.

He demonstrated it in deeds when he died for the Church, and he still
demonstrates it because he intercedes for the Church and preserves each of the
Church’s members,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 9 | printed col. 17 | bsb10175568]

protects them in faith against enemies, and renews them, and so forth. He will
demonstrate it fully in complete glorification.

The Church also demonstrates the effects of love toward her bridegroom in
words and deeds. In words:

1. In confessing his name.

2. In proclaiming his benefits.

3. In correcting offenders.

4. In instructing the ignorant.

In deeds:

1. In invocation.

2. In zeal for good works.

3. In enduring persecutions on account of his name.

4. In desiring to dwell with him and celebrate that marriage.

From this union of Christ and the Church follows a threefold communion:

1. Of all spiritual and bodily goods, insofar as it benefits the Church.

2. Of sufferings, Acts 9:4.

3. Of the joy of glory, Romans 8:19; Philippians 3:21; 2 Timothy 2:11; 1 Peter
4:13; 1 John 1:3 and 3:2.

From these things it follows:

1. That the joining of Christ and the Church is spiritual, not bodily—not
only because it occurs through the Holy Spirit and faith, but also because of
their mutual love and affection, 1 Corinthians 6:17. Such also is the union of
the members of the Church with one another, as in Acts 4 and 1 Corinthians
12:13.

2. That the marriage between Christ and the Church is not carnal but mystical.
It is therefore called a great mystery in Ephesians 5: a mystery because of
that hidden love known only to Christ and the elect, and great because of the
excellence of the thing, since the Church’s happiness depends on this
marriage, and also because of its sublimity, which in

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 9 | printed col. 18 | bsb10175568]

this life we perceive only in part.

3. Although the joining with Christ in this life is only spiritual, it will
in its time become bodily, when the marriage of the Lamb has been celebrated
in heaven. Then they will forever enjoy to the full that most delightful
fellowship with Christ.

The end of the Church is threefold. 1. The principal end with respect to God
is his glory, Ephesians 1:3. 2. The subordinate end with respect to the elect
is their salvation. 3. With respect to Scripture, the Church is to be its
guardian, witness, interpreter, herald, and vindicator. In these respects the
Church is called the pillar and foundation of the truth.

The chief attributes of the Catholic Church, to which all the others can be
referred, are four.

1. Catholic, by reason of its breadth, because it is circumscribed by neither
time, place, nor persons; hence it is called the mother of us all.

2. One, because there was, is, and will be one body of Christ; one faith and
Spirit, by which the members are connected with Christ the head; one God, whom
all the members are called to worship and glorify forever; one love, by which
the individual members are bound to one another; and finally one salvation of
all. Hence it is called the city, the spiritual house, and the Jerusalem of
God; with respect to the bond of love, it is called Christ’s friend, beloved,
bride, and body.

3. Holy, because it has a most holy and sanctifying head, in whom the sins of
the Church are not imputed, while its indwelling corruption is gradually
removed through the Holy Spirit; also because it has been called out of the
profane world and aspires in all its parts toward that most perfect holiness
which it will possess when Christ presents the whole Church before the Father
without wrinkle or blemish, Ephesians 1:27.

4. It is called prophetic and apostolic by reason of its generation and

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 10 | printed col. 19 | bsb10175568]

foundation, Ephesians 2:20.

From these things it is evident how ineptly the Papists call their particular
apostolic church catholic and apostolic.

# ON THE CHURCH TRIUMPHANT

The Catholic Church has two parts, one triumphant in heaven and the other
militant on earth.

The Church triumphant differs from the Church militant:

1. The latter needs internal and external ministry, and therefore faith and
hope; the former does not.

2. In the latter the good are mixed with the wicked; in the former there are
only the good.

3. In the latter there are internal and external temptations, spiritual
struggle against internal and external enemies, and finally persecutions. In
the former, all enemies having been conquered, all triumph, and every tear is
wiped from their eyes.

4. In the latter, while away from the Lord, we see the promised goods in a
mirror and as in a riddle. In the former, as in our homeland, we shall see
with open face and be admitted into possession of the promises—that is, the
joy and glory which are believed here by faith and awaited in hope.

The Church triumphant, therefore, is that assembly which triumphs after its
enemies have been defeated and which, with Christ its head now raised, already
enjoys glory and joy in part—namely, in its souls. After the resurrection it
will enjoy full glory in body and soul forever.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 10 | printed col. 20 | bsb10175568]

We discussed the state of this Church where we treated glorification.

Between us and the Papists there are three controversies concerning the
Church triumphant:

1. On the invocation of the saints.

2. On the worship of images.

3. On relics, the dedication of temples, feast days, and pilgrimages, insofar
as all these things are performed in honor of the saints.

## CONTROVERSY I

### On the invocation of the saints

The following questions pertain to this controversy:

1. Whether the souls of the saints already enjoy the vision of God.

2. On the canonization of the saints.

3. On worship.

4. On invocation.

5. On intercession for us.

### QUESTION I

#### Whether the souls of the saints already enjoy the vision of God

Bellarmine says in book 1, chapter 1, *On the Beatitude of the Saints*, that
the state of the question is whether the spirits of the godly have been
admitted to enjoy the blessedness that consists in the clear vision of God.
There he falsely says that Calvin and our writers deny this. Tertullian, to be
sure, says in book 4 *Against Marcion* that until the glorious resurrection
spirits are in Abraham’s bosom, which he wishes to be a region that, if not
heavenly, is at least higher than the lower regions, where refreshment is
provided for the souls of the righteous. Vigilantius wishes Abraham’s bosom to
be a certain honorable and free custody, as Jerome testifies in his book
*Against Vigilantius*. Pope John XXII taught the same around the year 1330 and
commanded the Parisians not to teach otherwise in the school or elsewhere,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 11 | printed col. 21 | bsb10175568]

and required all those promoted to degrees to swear to this position, as
William of Ockham testifies in his *Work of Ninety-Three Days* and Gerson in
his sermon on Easter. The Greeks also held this opinion, as is evident from a
session of the Council of Florence.

We, on the contrary, prove by the following arguments that spirits enjoy
heavenly blessedness and the clear vision of God.

Argument 1. The souls of the saints are said to be before the throne of God,
clothed in white robes and serving him night and day, Revelation 2:15.
Therefore.

Argument 2. God receives the spirits of the saints, and they are said to
return to him, Acts 7:59; Ecclesiastes 12:9. Therefore.

Argument 3. Christ promised the thief a heavenly paradise, not an earthly
one, Luke 23:43, which Paul calls the third heaven, 2 Corinthians 12:2, 4. Nor
can souls be delighted by enjoyment of a bodily paradise. Therefore.

Argument 4. After death the saints are with Christ, Philippians 1:23. But
Christ is in heavenly blessedness and enjoys the vision of God. Therefore.

5. The spirits of the righteous are said to be in the heavenly Jerusalem,
where the angels are, Hebrews 12:22–23. But that blessedness and the vision of
God are there. Therefore.

Argument 6. The apostle teaches that after the body is destroyed by death, we
migrate in soul to heaven, 2 Corinthians 5:2. Therefore.

Argument 7. If we could not see God until after the resurrection, Paul would
not desire in 2 Corinthians 5 to be away from the body so that he might not be
away from God. There he describes the threefold state of believers:

1. When they are in the body and away from the Lord in this life.

2. When they are away from the body and are with God in soul after death.

3. When they are away neither from the body nor from

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 11 | printed col. 22 | bsb10175568]

the Lord after the resurrection, 2 Corinthians 5:8 and following. But the
first alternative is true. Therefore.

Argument 8. God is no more inclined to punish than to reward. But the ungodly
are already in torments, Luke 16:23, 29. Therefore the godly are in glory.

Argument 9. While Christ’s body lay in the tomb, his soul was in heaven, Luke
23:43. Therefore.

Argument 10. In this life the saints have in Christ a merit of eternal life;
when this life has ended, therefore, they obtain their reward fully. The
inference is proved as follows: if among men it is forbidden to withhold wages
until morning, Leviticus 19, much less does God do so by withholding the
reward until that morning of the resurrection.

Argument 11. If souls do not possess glory, they do not possess the thing
hoped for. But hope deferred afflicts the soul, Proverbs 13:12, whereas
affliction does not befall the souls of the blessed after this life, even by
the testimony of our adversaries.

## OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. In Matthew 20:8 the head of the household gives everyone his
wages at evening. By evening he means the resurrection, just as by the
denarius he means eternal life. Therefore.

Response. Chrysostom warns in his treatment of this passage that a parable
need not correspond in every respect; only its aim must be considered. Its aim
is that all the elect are to be given eternal life together, or that the
fathers of the New Testament labor for less time than the fathers of the Old
Testament, or that those called no less late arrive at life just as quickly.
Moreover, grant that he speaks of the last, public, and universal judgment.
That does not remove the particular judgment by which each person receives a
portion of his reward at death.

Objection 2. According to Hebrews 11:39–40, the fathers did not receive the
promise, but

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 12 | printed col. 23 | bsb10175568]

will receive it in their own time, lest they be perfected without us.
Therefore.

Response. Primasius and others understand the incarnation by “the promise,”
according to the saying, “Many kings and prophets desired to see the day of
Christ.” 2. Augustine, in tractate 49 on John, and other Fathers commenting on
this passage say that “the promise” means the perfect blessedness of body and
soul. That perfection does not remove lesser rewards, such as the blessedness
of souls until the day of judgment.

Objection 3. According to 1 John 3:2, we shall not see God as he is or be like
him until he appears at the last judgment. Therefore, before that day the
saints are not granted the vision of God.

Response 1. John does not speak of Christ’s appearing, but ours, for he says,
“what we shall be has not appeared,” and afterward, “we know when it appears,”
that is, what we shall be. He therefore speaks not about what the faithful
are, but what they appear to be before the world. But this does not follow:
“the world does not see our glory before the day of judgment; therefore the
souls of the saints do not see God.”

2. He speaks of the perfect conformation of all the faithful together. This
does not remove the partial conformation of the soul for a time.

Objection 4. According to Revelation 6:9, the souls of the faithful are said
to be under the altar. This signifies a receptacle where souls dwell quietly,
but in darkness, and so forth. Therefore.

Response. I deny the premise, because the altar does not signify a place
outside heaven. Revelation 7:15 calls the temple in which that altar stands
heaven. Moreover, the passage treats the delay of vengeance against the
ungodly, not the glory of the godly, for afterward they are called “clothed in
white robes,” that is, glorified.

Objection 5, from reason. The examination and judgment of the good and evil

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 12 | printed col. 24 | bsb10175568]

will occur on the last day. Therefore rewards and punishments are deferred
until that day.

Response. The universal and public judgment does not remove the particular
and private judgment. Therefore, if the conclusion speaks without
qualification of every reward, the inference is denied.

Objection 6. After death Christ’s soul did not ascend into heaven before the
resurrection, but first descended to hell and ascended into heaven after the
resurrection. Therefore.

Response. I deny the premise, because Christ himself said the contrary in Luke
23:43. How the article “he descended to hell” is to be understood may be seen
in its proper place. Moreover, they concede that after death Christ’s soul was
blessed and glorious. The souls of the faithful are so too. Further, if we
were to grant that Christ must be imitated in every example, as they wish, it
would follow that all believers descend to hell, rise afterward, and ascend
into heaven after forty days, which is absurd.

Objection 7. The demons are not yet punished but are kept to be punished after
the day of judgment, as in Matthew 8:29 and Luke 8:26. Therefore, neither are
men. But if ungodly men are not punished, neither are the godly glorified.

Response. I distinguish the premise. It is true that they are not yet fully
punished, because they have not been wholly and finally thrust into the abyss;
many dwell in this world to try human beings, Ephesians 2:2. It is evident,
however, that they are punished in part, both by the punishment of loss and
the punishment of sense. Bede therefore rightly says on James 3, “Wherever
they may fly, they carry their own hell everywhere.”
