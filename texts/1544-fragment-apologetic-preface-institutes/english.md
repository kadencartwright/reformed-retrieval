# Fragment of an Apologetic Preface for the *Institutes*

*A fresh translation from the Latin text at CO IX 841–846. Bracketed boundary
and page notices are editorial, not Calvin's words.*

[The surviving authorial fragment begins.]

[CO IX, p. 841]

Certainly, if I hear that this labor of mine has not been unwelcome to good
people, I shall think not only that I have applied my effort excellently, but
also that I have attained the highest object of my wish. For what ought we to
strive for more throughout life than to commend all our services to Christ's
members, and so to Christ himself in his members? And so, when it was recently
reported to me that some who deserve to be regarded in that rank had taken
some offense from the latest edition of my *Institutes*, because in my
treatment of the Eucharist I seemed both to stir up again disputes which arose
in our time over the presence of the Lord's body and blood in the Supper,
though now largely put to rest, and obliquely to assail faithful servants of
the Lord as if they asserted there a local presence unworthy of Christ, news
of that sort affected me not slightly. It grieved me all the more, indeed,
that they were offended on these counts. For while the evil of offense is
weighty in itself, truly there is nothing from which I wish more to be seen as
foreign than every appetite for quarreling and fomenting turmoil. And
certainly there is no kind of dispute I dread more than precisely the one they
think I revived, since I know it has been agitated far too long, to such
detriment to godliness. Yet I do not doubt that I have the means to appease
their minds, if only they are willing to accept a fair satisfaction—which I
hope they will do. First, I shall beg them not to believe that I wished to
disrupt the concord begun among the German churches, which I have resolved to
defend steadfastly to the end no less than I embraced it gladly. I seem to
myself to have declared that by a sufficiently lucid testimony in that work.
For the things that pertained to asserting the true communication of the
Lord's body and blood which believers receive in the Supper were explained
there briefly indeed, yet not obscurely. For I confess

[CO IX, p. 842]

what has both always been received in the church and is taught today by all
who think rightly: that the sacred mystery of the Supper consists of two
things—bodily signs which, set before our eyes, represent invisible realities
to us according to the measure of our weakness, and spiritual truth which is
both signified and exhibited through the symbols themselves. When I wish to
demonstrate in a familiar way what that truth is, I customarily set forth
three things: the signification, the matter which depends upon it, and the
power or effect which follows from both. Signification resides in the
promises; by matter I mean Christ with his death and resurrection; and by
effect I understand redemption, righteousness, sanctification, eternal life,
and whatever other benefits Christ brings us. Moreover, although all these
things have regard to faith, I nevertheless leave no room for the cavils of
those who, when they say that Christ is received by faith, want him to be
conceived merely by understanding and imagination. For the promises offer
him, not so that we may remain fixed upon mere sight and bare knowledge, but
so that we may enjoy true communication of him. And indeed I do not see how
anyone can trust that he possesses redemption and righteousness in Christ's
cross and life in his death, unless he rests first of all upon true communion
of Christ himself. For those benefits would not reach us unless Christ first
made himself ours. I say, therefore, that in the mystery of the Supper Christ
is truly exhibited to us through the symbols of bread and wine, and thus his
body and blood, in which he fulfilled all obedience to obtain righteousness
for us: namely, so that first we may grow together with him into one body;
then, made participants in his substance, we may also feel the power in the
communication of all his benefits. If this is the chief article of concord,
as surely it is—that in the Supper Christ's body and

[CO IX, p. 843]

blood are not merely figured but are truly exhibited through the church's
ministry and represented as if before us—I not only wished the concord to
remain intact but also endeavored, insofar as it lay in me, to strengthen it.
Nor indeed should anyone take badly the expression I used there, when I said
that the Lord's Supper is bread sanctified in his body and wine in his blood.
Since those words were taken from Chrysostom, who is acknowledged to yield to
none of the ancients in proclaiming the dignity of the Supper, I did not fear
that by speaking thus I should seem in any way to diminish or obscure the
communion of the body and blood which believers receive under bread and wine.
And indeed those practiced in reading the ancients are not ignorant how much
weight that mystical *eulogia* which occurs so often among them bears. If I
had said nothing about the Supper besides that one little word, I confess, all
good people would rightfully call me to account. But so many splendid
commendations, with which I continuously pursued the fruit and excellence of
this sacrament, release me more than enough from every suspicion.

Now as to their thinking that I cast aspersions on some of those with whom we
have been reconciled, here I would have them consider at what time I first
wrote those things: I sharpened my pen against those who seek almost nothing
in the sacrament but a gross presence of Christ that may be perceived by
bodily sense and almost handled with the hands. By this stupid superstition we
see not merely the mass of people but even the leading men themselves held in
the papist churches. For, being little concerned for true faith, by which
alone we both enter into fellowship with Christ and cohere with him, provided
they have his carnal presence, which they have dreamed up beyond the Word,
they think they have him sufficiently present. Against them, I say, the whole
discussion is directed: those who seek Christ in the symbols with the body,
not the mind, and with the eyes rather than with faith. For one can see from
the whole sequence of the discourse that I strive for nothing other than that
we should conceive of such a presence of Christ's body in the Supper as
neither fastens him to the element of bread, nor encloses him in the bread,
nor circumscribes him in any fashion belonging to this age—all of which
plainly detract from his heavenly glory. Then, one that neither takes from him
his proper measure, nor disperses him over many places at once, nor attaches
to him an immense magnitude diffused throughout heaven and earth, things
which not obscurely contradict the truth of his nature. If it cannot be
denied that under the tyranny of that Roman Antichrist people's minds
everywhere were steeped in errors of this kind, why, pray, are things that
properly apply to enemies transferred to our brothers and friends, as though
they had been deliberately aimed at them? And indeed, since these things had
been published some years before, in the

[CO IX, p. 844]

revision of the work it did not seem good to strike them out, lest I seem
somehow to consent to the errors in repudiating which I still continue
steadfastly. For the power of Christ is not diminished when such absurdities
are removed, so as to prevent him from exhibiting himself most present to us
and actually fulfilling what he promised about giving his flesh as food. If
someone asks me about the manner, I shall not be ashamed to confess that the
mystery is too sublime either to be comprehended by my understanding or to be
explained in words. And, to confess the truth, I experience it more than I
understand it. Therefore here, without dispute, I embrace the truth of God, in
which I can safely rest. He declares that his flesh is food for my soul and
his blood is drink. I offer my soul to him to be fed by such nourishment. In
his sacred Supper he commands me under the symbols of bread and wine to take
and eat his body and blood. I have no doubt that he both truly offers them and
I receive them, and that I enjoy a true and substantial eating. I reject only
absurdities—whatever appears unworthy of Christ's heavenly majesty or alien
to the truth of his human nature, since it must also conflict with the Word of
God; for that Word teaches that Christ was received into the glory of the
heavenly kingdom in such a way as to raise him above every condition of this
world, and no less carefully commends in his human nature the things that
belong to true humanity.

If I had now passed over in complete silence these things, which I had truly
written previously, I did not doubt that many would suspect that the defense
of truth against the papists was thus being abandoned and betrayed by me, and
that their delusions were being as it were approved by my connivance. Added to
this was that I saw some becoming more averse to the concord established to
the great public good among the German churches, because they suppose that a
substantial eating of Christ's body in the Supper, of the sort Luther
affirmed, cannot stand unless it is entangled with a local presence and a kind
of circumscription. I thought it useful that they should recognize how empty
and frivolous this suspicion is, both so that they may cease doing wrong to an
eminent apostle of Christ, through whose ministry the light of the gospel
shone upon them in this age, and so that, freed from a false fear of danger,
they may embrace concord with us from the heart. Since it is possible to
attest by many arguments that this was my mind, first I should like to ask all
godly people not to think that I have in mind renewing that controversy which
troubled the churches for far too long. I wholly dread it as ill-omened; I
rejoice intensely, as is right, that it has at last been restrained and
settled; and I profess that I will apply my effort to the best of my ability
so that it may at length be entirely extinguished. Then, I ask them not to
suspect that I

[CO IX, p. 845]

think otherwise than that a true and substantial communication of the Lord's
body and blood is exhibited to believers under the sacred symbols of the
Supper—not so that they perceive it merely by imagination or mental
understanding, but so that they enjoy it in reality as nourishment for eternal
life. Finally, I ask them not to think that I wished either to assail those
who in the German churches fight together with us under Christ the Lord
against the Roman Antichrist, or to tear away anything from their doctrine.
For I do not persuade myself that those upon whom the Lord through his gospel
has

[CO IX, p. 846]

shone rave so much that the superstitions I censure there fall upon them.
Rather, let them settle this in their minds: nothing is a greater wish for me,
or will be a greater care, than to cultivate by every means the fullest
agreement with all the German churches which have given their name to Christ
and his holy gospel. Since we have more than enough struggles with the devil
and all the ungodly who are in his pay, we ought not to take so much leisure
for ourselves as, by fighting among ourselves, in some measure to tear apart
Christ himself, whose members we wish to be accounted.

[The surviving authorial fragment ends.]

## Translator's notes

1. Calvin repeatedly uses *communicatio* for the sacramental “communication”
   of Christ's body and blood, and *communio* for “communion.” The translation
   retains that distinction. Likewise, *exhibere* is rendered “exhibit” and
   *manducatio* “eating” because these are substantive claims in Calvin's
   argument, not decorative synonyms.
2. *Materia* at p. 842 is “matter” in Calvin's threefold analysis, not
   manuscript material; he immediately defines it as “Christ with his death
   and resurrection.” *Substantia* later on the page is correspondingly kept
   as “substance.”
3. The Greek *εὐλογία* (*eulogia*) at p. 843 literally means “blessing.” Here
   Calvin invokes its recurrent patristic, sacramental use. The following
   *unicum illud verbulum* (“that one little word”) may point specifically to
   this term or more broadly to the single quoted expression; the translation
   does not force a choice.
4. The printed Latin at p. 843 reads *non sum veritus ne ... viderer, quam sub
   pane et vino percipiunt fideles ... communionem*. The relative *quam* has
   no expressed antecedent in the transmitted sentence. The translation
   supplies the readily recoverable relation—“the communion ... which
   believers receive”—without silently changing the Latin.
5. *Ac si destinato in eos dicta forent* (p. 843) uses the adverbial or
   substantivized *destinato* compactly; “as though they had been deliberately
   aimed at them” conveys the contextual force without emending it to agree
   with *dicta*.
6. In *Tantum absurda reiicio, quae ... esse apparet* (p. 844), the relative
   and the impersonal-looking singular *apparet* sit awkwardly beside plural
   *absurda*. The English recasts the clause as “whatever appears” but the
   normalized Latin preserves the print.
7. The “eminent apostle of Christ” at p. 844 is Luther, named in the preceding
   sentence. The expression is Calvin's own unusually honorific language in
   this effort to protect inter-church concord.
