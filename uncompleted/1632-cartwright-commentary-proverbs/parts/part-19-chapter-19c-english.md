Vers. 9. A witness of falsehoods will not be innocent, etc.

It is precisely the same as the fifth, except that יאבד (*will perish*) is put in place of לא ינקה (*will not escape*). If it is asked why he repeated it at so small an interval, the reason for the repetition seems to be sought from the circumstances of the preceding Proverbs. For it commonly happens that, when affairs flow according to desire, everyone promises and professes faithful and perpetual service to those who abound in wealth. Yet afterward they treacherously disappoint that promise when riches have flown away as though upon wings they had seized. Solomon confirms by this Proverb that God will avenge this perfidy, and he applies the preceding thesis to the hypothesis. He also teaches that they will not escape God’s vengeance, however well God may provide for the man struck by calamity and he may obtain good from God: it did not stand through the treacherous that the man stripped of his fortune did not perish in that calamity of his.

V. 10. Delight does not befit a fool; much less a servant, that he should obtain rule over Princes.

In תענוג (*delight*) there is a metonymy of the effect for everything that brings delight. In עבד (*a servant*) and משל (*rule*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily servitude and dominion to the servitude and dominion of the mind.

The purpose is that the ungodly are to be beaten down by punishments, while the pious are to be animated and stirred up by rewards to pursue piety.

Men are generally moved very greatly by decorum, so that they often ascribe more to decorum than to justice whenever that which has the appearance and likeness of decorum seems to conflict with what is just. Therefore, in order to render the elevation and pleasures of the ungodly hateful to us, he teaches that it is a thing which has an ugly and deformed appearance. But because men judge variously concerning decorum, so that what seems becoming to some appears deformed to others, and contrariwise, it must be understood that Solomon appoints the eye of God, Angels, and pious men as the judge of this decorum. They look upon each thing acutely and clearly as it is, whereas the dull and bleary eyes of ungodly men do not.

The sense, therefore, is that in the eyes of God, Angels, and pious men, for the ungodly to fare well, to be fed with pleasures, to be splendidly clothed, to lie softly, and to descend to every relaxation of mind is an unbecoming thing. By contrast, in the judgment of God, Angels, and the pious, it is a conspicuous and becoming thing for them to live sparingly and harshly, to pass their life on the bread and water of affliction, to use cheaper and coarser clothing, to lie upon a harder bed, and to be kept from those amusements which befit the freeborn. We saw this previously at 10. 13, where he taught that the whip befits the back of a fool, or ungodly man; and at 26. 3 he teaches how greatly the rod, or correction, befits a fool. For he affirms that it is just as becoming as a whip or spur is suitable and adapted to a horse, or a halter to an ass. Thus, just as it is unbecoming

[1632 witness | PDF p. 405 | printed columns 784-785]

to follow a laggard and neither spur him nor furnish him with a rod, to feed an ass with mixed fodder when a thistle is most suitable for it, or to adorn with a gilded bridle one for whom a halter is fitting, so it is unbecoming to soothe an ungodly man with pleasures. By contrast, delight is becoming to the pious.

If the cause of this is asked, Isa. 26. 10 answers it. The ungodly man abuses these pleasures to harden his mind in crimes; he flatters himself and brings it into his mind that, since he is treated so softly and delicately, he is not unacceptable to God. (It sometimes happens that the pious are corrupted by the prosperity of this world, to say nothing of the ungodly.) By contrast, however, it often happens that, with the rod clinging to his back, he advances toward repentance, as may be seen in Manasseh and in the Israelites led away into captivity. But that delight befits the pious is clear from this: it is right that those occupied to the point of weariness in the duties of piety should be refreshed and recreated with pleasures.

From this it is perceived how perversely affairs are administered here in the world—in households as well as in the Commonwealth and Church—where, in promoting and elevating mortals, piety is not regarded, but our advantages, or anything else rather than piety, which ought above all to be regarded.

But if slight pleasure in food, drink, clothing, and lying down does not befit a fool, much less does princely rule; if punishment and disgrace are just and due to him, it is far from the case that he ought to be heaped with honour. For the man who is called a *fool* in the first part of the Proverb is called a *servant* in the latter part. And indeed, the wise of this world call slaves not only those who have bound themselves to servitude by law or condition, but also those who, being stupid and dull, were born for servitude and undertaking commands, not for rule. Hence they have even obtained the names of bodies, as those who are fit only for burdens. But those whom Scripture calls slaves here and in other places are called slaves and bondmen by the best right, since they have been enslaved to the servitude of sin and to the devil himself. Thus what Scripture predicted concerning Canaan may be said most truly concerning them: that he would be a slave of slaves. How deformed and shameful to behold this is, moreover, is taught by what is written at Eccles. 10. 3, where Solomon professes that he saw a most unworthy thing: namely, slaves carried on horses while Princes went on foot before them.

But here a difficulty arises, because God loads the ungodly with the goods and advantages of this life; likewise, he appointed Saul, Jeroboam, and Jehu as Kings. Why, therefore, should this not sometimes be permitted to men, especially since God does it so often? I answer: it rests upon us always to keep God’s commands before our eyes so that we may render obedience to them. We are not, however, to propose all God’s deeds to ourselves for *imiationem* [printed thus; imitation], but only those deeds for whose imitation we are fortified and invited by a command. For God often grants all things to the ungodly according to the desire of their mind so that afterward he may afflict them with a graver and more atrocious punishment, which is not permitted to men. God sets hypocrites over a people in order to avenge the people’s sins and ingratitude; this has not been granted to men.

Vers. 11. A man’s understanding prolongs his wrath, and his glory is to pass over a defection.

In עבור (*to pass over*) there is a metaphor drawn from travellers who pass by someone known to them when he meets them on the road as though he were unknown.

The purpose is to animate the pious toward meekness and patience, and to drive away the things which are opposed to this virtue.

Solomon assigns a man’s understanding—whether a male’s or a female’s—as the cause of long-suffering.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 406 | printed columns 786-787]

Hence it is that James, in chapter 1, when exhorting to endurance, immediately adds that if anyone lacks wisdom, he should ask it from God. Therefore it must be attributed to wisdom—and indeed wisdom sent and descended from heaven—if anyone does not requite evil with evil. Everyone grasps and hunts after the little glory of wisdom and understanding; yet very few are those who enter this road which leads to wisdom, Matt. 7. 15. They would nevertheless do so if, when injured in any matter, they considered that they themselves had sinned more gravely against God, and that this very thing was God’s chastening, Psal. 39. 10. In the consideration of these things this understanding is discerned.

Moreover, by this judgment Solomon meets those who generally ascribe it to stupidity, and sometimes to timidity and cowardice, if anyone is composed in mind to receive injuries calmly. To blunt these temptations, may the most true and incorruptible judgment of the Holy Spirit come to our aid, for he refers patience to no other fountain than wisdom. Hence it is that David referred Abigail’s counsel in 1 Sam. 25—by which he was recalled from the rage he had conceived in his mind—to God as its only author and fountain, and celebrated it exceedingly. Meanwhile, let those tremble greatly who refer God’s work to the Devil.

But if it is a man’s understanding and wisdom that prolongs wrath, it follows that it is a man’s foolishness and ignorance that render him irascible and agitated. Men generally refer this to fortitude and spiritedness of mind. Hence it is even boasted among us, he hath metall in him. And indeed it is metal, but metal dug out of the lowest mines of hell—namely, reprobate and vile metal which has never admitted the image and character of the Holy Spirit engraved upon it. Those who do not wish to seem to approve this irascibility in every respect refer it to a certain affection peculiar to their nature, and by this they suppose themselves to be defended and excused to some degree. But rather let them say what the matter is: that this belongs to their foolish and accursed nature, so that, suffused with shame over so vile a thing, they may repent.

In the latter hemistich he declares the effect of meekness and forbearance: to pass over another’s defection. For there are some who say that they have not been moved with wrath against those by whom they were offended, and nevertheless do not meanwhile wish to spare them, but to retaliate for the injury received. Solomon, however, teaches that the genuine fruit of meekness and long-suffering is to pass over a defection.

Here the word *defection* must also be noted, for it signifies not just any fault, but a grave offence. Many will profess that they would present proofs of their meekness if they had been offended only in a small sum of money, but that, when afflicted with so great a loss, they cannot contain themselves from rushing against the offender. Others say that they would have borne it calmly if they had been injured in their goods, but that, having been injured in reputation and the esteem of their name, they cannot refrain from taking vengeance. But Solomon says that a defection is to be passed over. To this is added what was said above, that charity covers a multitude of transgressions, 1 Pet. 4. 8. Likewise there is what appears at Matt. 18: that a brother is to be forgiven not seven times, but seventy times seven.

For this matter he brings another argument from the adjunct of decorum. In it he anticipates and forestalls what men object to this meekness of spirit: namely, that it is a dishonourable thing if anyone should wink at an injury inflicted and especially at a grave offence presented to him. The Holy Spirit, by contrast, confirms that it is for honour and decorum if anyone passes over a defection in silence, as though he were not aware of it. For just as it is ascribed to the noble breeding of a horse that it passes the barking of dogs quietly and calmly, so also it belongs to magnanimity not to be moved by the insults of others. See 1 Sam. 10. 27. Yet these things are to be understood in this way, insofar as

[1632 witness | PDF p. 407 | printed columns 788-789]

the injuries inflicted adhere only to our own persons and do not spread to God or the Church.

V. 12. The roar, as of a young lion, is the indignation of a King; and his goodwill is as dew upon the grass.

In מלך (*of a king*) there is a synecdoche for everyone who possesses the highest control of affairs.

The purpose is to instil in everyone fear of Royal power.

The roar of any lion is formidable, but especially that of a young lion, because, being himself in ἀκμῇ, at the prime and vigour of his strength, he thunders more powerfully. Thus we read at Isa. 15 that the lowing of a three-year-old heifer is most powerful and exceedingly resonant. Since, therefore, the indignation of a King is represented by the roar of a young lion, it must be a formidable thing. For who is so stout-hearted that he would not tremble at the roar of the fiercest lion? Hence also Scripture, in order to set forth Christ as terrible to all the enemies of the Church, calls him the lion from the tribe of Judah. The lion has this characteristic: he does not utter his roar before he is certain of taking the prey and the prey has been brought so close that it cannot escape his claws. Hence Amos 3. 4 writes thus: “Will the lion roar in the forest without attaining his prey? Or especially will the young lion utter his voice without taking the prey?” He excels in roaring to strike terror, by which the course of beasts is slowed—indeed, of those which surpass the lion in their own speed and swiftness.

Foolish men, in order to shake off this honour and fear due to a King and commanded by the Holy Spirit, 1 Pet. 2. 17; Rom. 13. 4, are accustomed to reason thus with themselves: Who is he? Is he not, like the rest, a son of Adam? Is not his spirit in his nostrils? Is he not—as sometimes happens—the son of an ignoble man? (Hence the seditious defected from David, 2 Sam. 20. 1.) Yet let them remember that he is a King, and that by divine ordination Royal dignity joins to him such power and majesty that it rightly places him in as great an eminence above his subjects as the lion has above the other beasts of the field; for the lion is also called the King of all wild beasts.

Therefore that counsel of Solomon which he proposes elsewhere, at Eccles. 8. 2, 3, 4, is to be followed. But if at some time you are oppressed by the King’s indignation, you will roll yourself as a suppliant to his knees so that you may obtain favour and appease his wrath. For just as writers say that a lion, however fierce and greedy for prey, will nevertheless pass untouched the man who falls down before it as though dead, so no way is more suitable or shorter for averting a King’s indignation than for someone to strive to return to favour with him through the most submissive abasement of himself.

But if one must tremble at a man’s roar, what must we do when God roars from the heavens? (See Amos 1.) Above all, what must we do when he roars with that formidable voice: “Go, accursed,” etc.; “having been bound hand and foot,” etc.? Matt. 25.

But if it is asked whether Kings ought not to put a bridle upon their wrath and indignation just as much as common people, I answer: they ought to apply themselves to this part so much more than their subjects, because by their indignation they cause infinitely more harm than their subjects. But in this place it is not so much asked what Kings ought to do as the danger which commonly arises from their just or unjust wrath is placed *ab oculos* [printed thus; before the eyes]. Just as consciousness of duty ought above all to keep us in our duty, so that protection by which mortals are accustomed to take precautions for themselves, with wrath and indignation taken into account, is not to be neglected, Rom. 13.

Moreover, these things are not said so that through fear of a King we should depart even a nail’s breadth from the duty commanded by God,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 408 | printed columns 790-791]

and for that reason we heard above at Eccles. 8 that our obedience toward Princes is circumscribed by the barriers of our covenant struck with God, and the counsel is given that no one should persist in an evil cause.

The first hemistich is confirmed from the opposite in the latter, because by a King’s goodwill a person is not only preserved among the living, but moreover is gladdened and flourishes, just as grass, by dew sent down from heaven, both lives, is green, and thrives. See Prov. 16. 15. And in a Prince’s benevolent regard it is somehow granted to behold God’s outstretched brow and pleasant countenance. He confirms this by the reason applied to the fifth precept: namely, that in obedience to parents the reward both of a long life and of a pleasant one has been prepared. But if a King’s goodwill revives and refreshes a sad countenance, what will God’s calm and serene countenance effect?
