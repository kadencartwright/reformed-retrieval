QUESTION XIV.

On the Hour of Christ’s Passion.

The evangelists seem to relate the time of Christ’s crucifixion in different ways. At John 19:14, Pilate sat down on the judgment seat about the sixth hour. At Mark 15:25, Christ is said to have been crucified at the third hour, while at verse 33 darkness came over the whole land from the sixth hour until the ninth. At Luke 23:44 darkness came about the sixth hour. At Matthew 27:45 darkness lasted from the sixth hour until the ninth. Maldonatus, Suarez, and others raise various questions here, but the principal one, and the only one we intend to address, is this: How was Christ crucified at the third hour and yet not condemned to the cross before the sixth? Some brush this question lightly aside as easy, but others, both ancient and more recent, acknowledge that it is quite difficult. De Dieu calls it most difficult. I find various opinions on reconciling these passages; as is my custom, I embrace the most common of all.

Augustine leads the train of those who have offered opinions here. In the lengthy chapter 13 of book 3 of *The Harmony of the Evangelists*, he labors greatly over the solution of this difficulty. He offers two solutions, the former as his own view and the latter as another’s, although he himself approves it well enough. Yet he does so modestly, professing himself ready to accept a third solution from anyone who can offer one better than either of his. He also proposes both in tractate 117 on John, but in his exposition of Psalm 63—our Psalm 64—at verse 3 he gives only the first, just as this alone is also offered in question 15 of the book of *Sixty-Five Questions* and in question 65 of the book of *Questions on the Old and New Testaments*. These two books are certainly not Augustine’s, for they contain many things contrary not only to express Scripture but also to Augustine himself, even though Suarez and others cite them as genuine writings of Augustine.

The first solution is this: Mark speaks of Christ’s crucifixion by the tongues of the Jews, while John and the other evangelists speak of Christ’s crucifixion by the hands of the Romans. The former occurred at the third hour, three hours after sunrise and three before noon, when the Jews cried, “Crucify him, crucify him!” The latter occurred at noon, when Pilate handed Christ over to the soldiers to be crucified.

The other solution is that John may be understood to mean the sixth hour not of the day but of the παρασκευῆς, the preparation; and that the παρασκευὴν began at the ninth hour of the night, that is, three hours before sunrise, when in Caiaphas’s house Christ was condemned by the priests as guilty of blasphemy worthy of death. From this ninth hour of the night until the third hour of the day there were six hours, so that John’s sixth hour of the παρασκευῆς was the same as Mark’s third hour of the day. In the third part of the *Summa*, question 46, second reply to the second objection, Thomas Aquinas adduces both these solutions of Augustine; he does the same in the *Catena* on Matthew 27:25, without any indication of his own dissent. Maldonatus and others, both papists and Protestants, therefore had all the less reason to reject them with disgust. I indeed think both should be rejected, but neither indignantly nor irritably, since each is more plausible than various inventions by writers both earlier and later.

These are the reasons why the former does not please me. First, the crucifixion of which Mark speaks at 15:25 is a true and real crucifixion by the hands of Roman soldiers, because this alone is the subject of Mark’s preceding verses; he is by no means speaking of a metaphorical crucifixion by the tongue. Second, the counsels, vehement desires, and furious conspiracies of the Jews concerning Christ’s death long preceded Mark’s third hour. Third, the Jews’ demand that Christ be crucified cannot be called Christ’s crucifixion before Pilate had passed sentence that he be crucified, especially since Pilate was still resisting his crucifixion.

The latter solution displeases me for these reasons. First, the παρασκευὴ of Passover in the strict sense began at the ninth hour of the day, three hours before sunset and before the evening in which Passover was eaten; taken broadly, however, it began at the first hour of that day, when the sun rose. Yet neither Scripture nor reason begins the παρασκευὴν at the ninth hour of the night. Second, no one assigns more than twelve hours to the παρασκευῇ, however it is understood, whereas this solution gives it fifteen: three of the night and twelve of the day. Third, there is no reason why we should assign the preparation for Christ’s death to the ninth hour of that night—an hour of which Scripture says nothing—rather than to earlier hours, in which Scripture expressly places Christ’s arrest, betrayal, Last Supper, and other acts preparatory to his death. Fourth, this solution supposes that Christ hung on the cross for six hours, from the third until the ninth, although John and the other evangelists allow only three hours on the cross, as we shall see below.

The second opinion holds that copyists corrupted Mark’s text: the evangelist had supposedly written “the sixth hour” with the character ϛ, which they call the ἐπίσημον, but copyists, deceived by its resemblance, changed it into γ, the mark for the number three. This is Jerome’s opinion at the beginning of Psalm 77; Cajetan follows him on Mark 15:25, as does Sixtus of Siena in book 6 of his *Bibliotheca*, annotation 131. The Ethiopic Bibles—

—follow this reading, for in them Mark has “the sixth hour.” In the passages cited above Aquinas gives this opinion without any indication of displeasure. Theophylact takes the same path at John 19:14, except that he transfers the error from Mark’s text to John’s: in John he would have the genuine reading be “the third hour,” not “the sixth.” In his commentary on Mark, Theodore Beza calls this solution violent. Nevertheless, he says that it is found both in Camerarius’s manuscript and in his own very ancient one, and that the poet Nonnus follows it. Indeed, at John 19:14 Beza himself affirms that this reading is correct. In chapter 11 of his *Harmony of the Passion*, Gerard adduces Peter of Alexandria, who affirms that John’s Gospel, written out by John’s own hand and preserved in his day in the church at Ephesus, read “the third hour,” not “the sixth.”

This opinion is rejected, I believe, by all other papists and Protestants alike, because it opens far too wide a door for rash minds to correct Holy Scripture. According to the judgment of Pius V, Jerome’s commentaries on the Psalms were relegated by Mariano Vittori—as he himself relates in his preface—to the final volume as doubtful, if not spurious. Bellarmine admits that forgers corrupted them in very many places. Jerome certainly has nothing similar in his commentaries on Matthew and Mark. Augustine, who handles this question so often and at such length, surely would not have passed Jerome over if he had read any such thing in his writings. What is cited under Peter of Alexandria’s name seems to be a mere fable; who can believe that John’s autograph still existed in his day?

As for the matter itself, if the view attributed to Jerome were true—namely that Mark should read “the sixth hour”—no difficulty would remain, and by this reading every inconsistency among the evangelists would be removed. But the testimony of every printed and manuscript copy of Mark contradicts this alteration of the sacred text. Nor can the Ethiopic translator, only recently published, sustain so great a matter on his authority alone, and the authority of Theophylact and his followers against all other witnesses is no greater. Indeed, even if we granted their correction of John’s text, the difficulty would by no means be solved but rather increased, since John would be joined with Mark against Matthew and Luke: the latter two affirm that the Lord was crucified at the sixth hour, the former two at the third.

I pass over the fact that this correction recounted by Theophylact contradicts Theophylact himself. At Matthew 27:45 he holds that Christ was fastened to the cross at the sixth hour, at noon—I grant, for a weak and false reason: namely, that at the same hour and on the same day those chosen in Christ were to be redeemed by his cross as Adam had sinned in Paradise. Adam, however, sinned in Paradise on the sixth day of the week, the very day on which he was created, and at noon of that day, because noon, the sixth hour of the day, is the usual time for eating. These twigs are extremely loose, and the arguments too trifling to deserve refutation, although Cornelius a Lapide and other papists embrace them as beautiful mysteries.

The third opinion is that of John Piscator, who would have John speak according to Roman custom and Mark according to Jewish custom. The Jews, he says, counted the hours of the artificial day from sunrise, while the Romans counted from midnight; and the sixth hour from midnight coincided with the third from sunrise, which in Judaea at that time occurred at four in the morning. This opinion of Piscator does not please me, even though many very learned men approve it with one variation or another: namely, De Dieu on Mark 15:25, the Belgian annotators on John 19:14, the latest English annotators on Luke 23:44, Erasmus on Matthew 27:45, and others. I find the following faults in it. First, Piscator supposes that at Passover the sun rose in Judaea at four in the morning. This is manifestly false, for at the equinox, which in Judaea coincided with Passover, the sun never rose before six in the morning.

Second, suppose that the sun rose at four, that Christ was accused before Pilate so early in the morning, and that after proceedings lasting two hours he was condemned and crucified at the sixth hour—that is, the third after sunrise, as Mark has it. It would follow that Christ hung on the cross not merely six hours, but a full nine. This contradicts not only all other Christians but Piscator himself, who affirms on Mark 15:25 that Christ was fastened to the cross for only six hours.

Third, those who correct Piscator on John 19 and hold that Christ suffered at the third hour, or nine in the morning, so that he hung on the cross for six hours, do not solve the present difficulty: how John’s Roman sixth hour, as they would have it, coincides with Mark’s Jewish third. For the latter was the Roman ninth hour, not the sixth, unless one follows Piscator’s explanation above, which must necessarily be rejected.

Fourth, those who amend Piscator’s opinion make John’s sixth hour precede Mark’s third by three full hours, which is as great a difficulty as if it followed Mark’s third by the same number of hours.

Fifth, everyone holding this opinion would have Christ hang on the cross for six hours, from the third until the ninth. Although some ancients affirm this, as Suarez cites them in his commentary on the third part of Thomas, question 50, article 6, almost all the Fathers and more recent writers, Protestant as well as papist, assign Christ only three hours on the cross, from the sixth hour until the ninth, as the passage under discussion, John 19:14, seems to compel. For at about the sixth hour Pilate had not yet passed sentence, and so it is certain that Christ was not crucified before that hour. Furthermore, the darkness that did not arise before—

—the sixth hour, at noon, came immediately after Christ was fastened to the cross. The common explanation for it is that by a divine miracle the sun withdrew its rays from beholding the greatest crime ever committed on earth. This explanation would not hold if for three full hours, from the third to the sixth, the sun had looked upon Christ hanging on the cross and had so long supplied miserable mortals with light by which to behold this horrible spectacle.

Sixth, the foundation of this whole opinion is not firm: namely, that John 20:19 speaks of days and hours according to Roman custom because it refers even the late evening to the preceding day, not the following, contrary to the Jewish custom by which evening preceded the day rather than followed it. Reply: even if John once or twice followed Roman custom in days and hours, it does not prove that he did so always, usually, or in the disputed passage before us, where Matthew and Luke, who are not alleged to follow Roman custom, clearly begin Christ’s passion at the sixth hour along with John. Moreover, it was sometimes the custom of both Jews and Romans to refer nights to the preceding day rather than the following one. Certainly, on the most solemn day for sacrificing Passover between the two evenings of the fourteenth of Nisan, both evenings were referred not to the fifteenth day, which they began, but to the fourteenth, which they closed.

Seventh, the very learned William Estius explains this opinion differently on Matthew 27:5 and Mark 15:25. He holds, and often repeats, that the sun rises three hours later in Italy than in Judaea. Thus when it was the third hour in Italy, where Mark wrote his Gospel, it was the sixth hour—that is, noon—in Judaea, where Christ suffered. This is surely great carelessness in a learned man. Grant that the sun rises three hours later in Italy than in Judaea—which is nevertheless not true unless a full quarter of the semicircle from east to west is assigned to the interval between Judaea and Italy. Indeed, the degrees of longitude from the east as far as Jerusalem will be four times those counted from Jerusalem to Rome. But leaving this aside, the conclusion wholly contradicts the argument. For if the sun rises three hours later in Italy than in Judaea, then the sixth hour, or noon, in Italy will not be the third hour after noon in Judaea, as Estius says, but the third before noon. I would indeed have thought this was Estius’s meaning had he not written otherwise repeatedly. In any case, the difficulty would not be removed even then. Grant that John’s sixth hour at Jerusalem was the third hour after noon at Rome; why would Mark, when relating what occurred at Jerusalem, give Roman hours adjusted to the horizon not of Judaea but of Italy? Besides many other absurdities, it would certainly follow that according to Mark Christ did not expire before the twelfth hour of the Jewish day and the sixth hour of the Roman night—that is, not before sunset. Indeed, if he hung on the cross for six hours and was fastened to it at three in the afternoon, he was not taken down until three hours after sunset. Even if he remained on the cross for only three hours, he still would not have been taken down before sunset and night, or indeed until after sunset; and so he was buried within the bounds of the Sabbath and rose not on the third day but the second. All these conclusions are contrary to the Christian faith.

The fourth opinion is that of those who interpret the third hour not of the crucifixion but of the division of the garments noted in the preceding verse, as though it occurred at the third hour after the crucifixion. De Dieu rashly intimates that this is the Arabic translator’s opinion, for neither the Arabic nor the Syriac departs here from the Greek, translating καὶ with ו. I grant that the Persian adds an ill-fitting appendage to its correct translation—“and when they had put him on the cross at the third hour”—namely, “after they had hung him upon the cross.” This is partly a tautology and partly a corrupt paraphrase. In any case, De Dieu expressly rejects this opinion. Heinsius, however, embraces it tenaciously on Mark 15:25 and attempts to prove it at length and with bitterness, rejecting the common and true opinion as sophistry. In the passage cited, Suarez opposes this opinion as found in Marcellus, a most learned Roman monk, from whom Heinsius could have taken it, although he conceals the fact. I do not approve Baldwin or Walaeus here: in solving this difficulty he sometimes gives the common and true opinion in Grotius’s words, but elsewhere adduces words from De Dieu and Heinsius which contradict both the truth and each other, without any indication of displeasure.

I argue against Heinsius’s opinion as follows. First, this is the only verse in which Mark notes the time of the crucifixion. Therefore, if the time here refers not to the crucifixion but to the division of the garments, Mark entirely passes over the time when Christ was crucified, even though he notes the time of an action much less noteworthy.

Second, Matthew places the division of the garments immediately after the crucifixion at 27:35. Luke does the same at 23:34, John at 19:23, and Mark himself at 15:24. Mark therefore does not contradict himself and all the others by deferring the division of the garments for three full hours after the crucifixion.

Third, according to the accounts of Matthew, Mark, and Luke, the garments were divided before the darkness arose. But if the darkness—much less the actions before the darkness—did not arise until a full three hours after the crucifixion, then Christ would have hung on the cross for more than six hours.

Fourth, Heinsius’s sole foundation is a Hebraism in the conjunction καὶ, which he would have signify μετὰ, “after,” just as, he says, the Hebrew conjunction ו is frequently rendered in the Old Testament. Heinsius does not prove this Hebraism with even a single example in which the Seventy translators—

—ever translated the conjunction ו with μετὰ. I grant that things connected by a conjunction occur frequently enough after the things to which they are joined, but this does not happen through a Hebraism or any force in the particle ו. The passage Heinsius presses especially, Joshua 7:25, “after they had stoned him,” is irrelevant. First, the Greek translators do not render the Hebrew word וישרפו as μετὰ τὸ λιθοβολῆσαι, which Heinsius needed to prove, but omit those words altogether as identical in sense with what precedes. The Latin translator and the Syriac do the same. It is true that the Chaldean paraphrast there renders ו with בתר, “after,” but under no necessity, for the Arabic translator, the old English version, and the new Belgian version retain the conjunction “and,” although the new English and Italian versions render it “after.” But grant that Joshua must necessarily be translated in this way here: when ו is nevertheless rendered with “and” twenty thousand times and far more often, what does this single passage have to do with the text before us? No translator in any language, ancient or modern, renders it with “after,” except the Ethiopic alone; Heinsius does not seem to have seen it, since otherwise he would doubtless have cited it. In any case, no text can be produced where, in a single verse enclosed by its own punctuation, a determinate time is applied to a particular action by a copulative conjunction, and yet that same time must be detached from that action and referred to another. Least of all is that true in the words before us, for Heinsius openly admits here that the division of the garments is bound to no certain time. We therefore conclude that it is not bound to the third hour after the crucifixion. Thus by his own admission Heinsius’s entire argument is empty and has vanished.

There remains the fifth and last opinion, that of Calvin, Beza, Gualter, Pareus, Rollock, Gerard, Diodati, and other Protestants; and of Jansenius, Maldonatus, Ferus, Baronius, de Valentia, Suarez, and almost all the papists. It holds that the passages must be reconciled by a twofold distinction. The first is that the Jews and Romans counted the hours of days and nights differently. The Romans began the day at midnight and counted twenty-four hours from there until the next midnight; or, for easier use, they divided the natural day into two periods of twelve hours, as we do today, counting the first first hour from midnight and the second from noon. The Jews, however, began their day at sunset and counted twenty-four hours from there until the next sunset. Like the Romans, they also divided their whole natural day, or νυχθήμερον, for easier use into two periods of twelve hours. They counted the first first hour from sunset and placed the first twelfth hour at sunrise, just as they counted the second first hour from sunrise and placed the second twelfth hour at sunset. Everyone concedes these points, and they are easily proved from Matthew 20, where the latter part of the νυχθημέρου is expressly divided into twelve daylight hours. From this it is clear that our and the Roman sixth hour in the morning corresponded to the Jews’ first daylight hour; the Jews’ third daylight hour to our and the Romans’ ninth in the morning; the Jews’ sixth daylight hour to our twelfth; their ninth to our third in the afternoon; and their twelfth to our sixth in the evening. Strictly speaking, there were no equinoctial days in Judaea during the year except two, for the sphere is oblique for them as for us. Yet because the elevation of the pole was much lower for them than for us and they came much nearer than we do to a right sphere, while their summer days were shorter than ours and their winter days longer, it followed that throughout the year their artificial day could more easily than ours be reckoned into twelve hours—not always equal, but somewhat longer in summer and shorter in winter.

The greater—indeed the entire—difficulty lies in the second distinction. The Jews divided their days not only as above, but also subdivided the twelve hours of both night and day into four quarters, each containing three hours. They called these quarters of the night watches. That the night was divided into four watches is clear from various passages of Scripture. At Luke 12:38 we read of the second and third watch, at Matthew 14:25 of the fourth watch, and at Psalm 63:6 of the watches of the night in general. The quarters of the day do not appear so distinctly in Scripture, but they may rightly be inferred from the correspondence of day to night. Indeed, almost all the quarters of the day are expressly named in different passages of Scripture. The third, sixth, and ninth hours are often mentioned as times appointed for prayers and sacrifices, and from that public use they were commonly employed in other private affairs, as may be seen in Matthew 20 and elsewhere. Days were divided into four quarters not only among the Jews but even among the Romans, as the Magdeburg Centuriators prove in century 1, book 1, and Baronius after them from Censorinus. Whether these quarters were named from their first or last hour is not, we think, worth disputing: whether the first quarter is named from its first hour, as most hold, or from the third, as Baronius and a few others hold, matters little, provided that hour, whether first or last, embraces the whole quarter.

With these points established, the solution of the question becomes sufficiently clear and easy. Mark says that Christ was crucified at the third hour because he was crucified within the span of the quarter named from the third hour. John and the others then say that he was crucified at the sixth hour, or about the sixth, because the sixth hour was the other endpoint of that second quarter. Thus actions performed between the third and sixth hours could be said to occur either at the third or the sixth, being named from either endpoint at the speaker’s discretion.

There are two principal objections to this opinion. First, the division of the day into quarters is asserted but not proved. Reply: the proof of this assertion is better than any offered for the preceding opinions. The inference from the division of the night into quarters to the division of the day into the same is sound, especially since almost all the beginnings of the day’s four quarters are mentioned in Holy Scripture: namely, the third, sixth, and ninth hours. From these three the fourth follows of itself, whether you call it the first or the twelfth.

Second, it is objected that this opinion supposes Christ to have been crucified about the sixth hour and the darkness also to have arisen about that same hour; yet Scripture relates so many things between Christ’s crucifixion—or at least between Pilate’s sentence that Christ be crucified—and the darkness’s onset that they do not seem capable of being accomplished in so little time. Reply: this is a hard and weighty objection. Nevertheless, we answer that Scripture and the best authors often should not be understood with mathematical rigor, but more broadly according to civil and political usage, especially where the qualifying particle “about” is expressly joined to the time. Everything done between the endpoints—that is, the beginning and end—of the sixth hour may therefore be said to have been done at the sixth hour; and everything accomplished within half an hour before or after six may be said to have occurred at about the sixth hour. Now many actions could have been performed in an hour and a half, especially by men full of fury and malice who hastened and drove Christ’s destruction onward with all their strength and every device. Those lengthy disputes among the Jews, Pilate, and Christ; the scourging, crowning, sending to Herod, washing of Pilate’s hands, and many other things recorded in the history of the Passion occurred before Pilate finally sat upon the judgment seat at about the sixth hour. The journey to Golgotha, everything that happened on the way, the crucifixion itself and the division of the garments, and several other things could easily have occurred in an hour and a half. The rest occurred either during the three hours of darkness or during the period after the darkness in which Christ remained alive.

Furthermore, this darkness does not seem to have been as dense and palpable as the Egyptian darkness, in which nothing at all could move. Rather, it was such that the Lord could speak to the thieves, his mother, and John, while the bystanders too could speak to him and mock him, whether we accept Origen’s, Jerome’s, or the Areopagite’s opinion about that darkness. For whether dense clouds, with Origen, wrapped the sun in gloom; or the sun itself, with Jerome, withdrew its rays; or the moon, with Dionysius, rushed up and caused a total eclipse of the sun, the darkness still need not have been so great that all the things mentioned could not occur in it, nor do I know anyone who affirms that it was. Nevertheless, Dionysius’s account of the darkness seems highly fabulous. Why multiply miracles without necessity: that almost in an instant the full moon flew from the east to the meridian; that in another instant it placed itself in the ecliptic directly beneath the sun so as to obscure its whole face; that it then remained in that state for three hours; and fourth, that in another instant it returned to its former station in the east? Each of these would have been as great a miracle as the darkness itself. Although Scripture is utterly silent about them, Dionysius writes that he saw them all with his own eyes. It is well that Origen, Jerome, and other learned men of the earlier centuries had heard nothing whatever of these spurious fables. Our Ward certainly ought not, in his questions on Matthew 27, to have approved these unrestrained ditties of the Pseudo-Areopagite, of which the papists themselves are ashamed.
