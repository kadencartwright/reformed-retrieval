SECTION FOUR NOTION II

PERKINS

God is not ruled, much less dependent upon secondary causes; rather, he justly orders them even when they act unjustly.

ARMINIUS

1. This is so. For he is the first cause and the cause of causes. Yet a person does not immediately depend upon secondary causes if he takes from the foreseen free act of rational creatures the occasion for making some decree and establishing some order among things—a decree he would not have made and an order he would not have established if the free secondary causes had been going to act otherwise and not in this way.

2. The Apostle says that the whole creation was subjected to vanity on account of him who subjected it. It would not have been liable to this vanity if the one for whose sake it was created by God had continued in his original integrity.

3. The decree to send Christ into the world rests upon foresight of the fall. For he is the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world. He was made a little lower than the angels because of his suffering death; he became a partaker of flesh and blood so that through death he might destroy the one who had the dominion of death, that is, the devil. He was appointed High Priest for men and taken from among men so that he might offer gifts and sacrifices for sins.

4. And in this same way stand all God’s decrees by which he resolved to punish his creatures, according to the words in Genesis 18: “Far be it from you to destroy the righteous with the wicked. Will not the Judge of the whole earth do justice?” Exodus 32: “Whoever has sinned against me, him will I blot out of my book.” First Samuel 2: “I did indeed say that your house and your father’s house would walk before me forever. But now Jehovah says: Far be it from me, for those who honor me I will honor, but those who despise me shall be despised.” Yet the infliction of punishments is so far from depending on secondary causes for this reason that, if those causes could escape them by any means or power, there is nothing they would not attempt.

5. I would also wish the verb *to ordain* to be used in its proper sense. Those who interpret it as “to decree that something should occur” seem to me to depart from that sense. For it means to establish order in things that have been done, not to ordain that things to be done should occur, although the Fathers sometimes also use it in this latter sense. But then those same Fathers deny that God is the ordainer of evils. Thus Augustine says that God knew how to ordain not the crime but the punishments of crimes.

RESPONSE 1

Here Arminius begins to lay aside the mask of candor and return to his own disposition—namely, one that is crafty and sophistical, though with no more successful a result. “A person,” he says, “does not immediately depend upon secondary causes if he takes from the foreseen free act of creatures the occasion for making some decree.” First, what will result if God takes from the foreseen free acts of rational creatures not merely the occasion but also the cause for making a decree? If this should happen, must he be said to depend upon secondary causes? If he must be said to depend upon them, then according to Arminius’s opinion God himself—that is, the first cause—will depend upon secondary causes. For it is altogether certain that, according to Arminius, God takes from men’s foreseen sins not only the occasion but also the cause for making a decree that consigns them to eternal destruction. Arminius establishes final unbelief and impenitence not only as the cause of damnation but also as the cause of the decree of reprobation.

Moreover, according to Arminius, God takes from the creature’s foreseen free act not only the occasion but also the cause, not only of reprobation but also of election. For according to him the cause of the decree is the same as the cause of its execution. But who doubts that piety and obedience on both sides are not merely the occasion but also the cause of the salvation of both angels and men: in angels, the legal obedience by which they guarded themselves from sin; and in men, the evangelical obedience by which they rise again from sin and persevere to the end in faith and repentance? But if God does not depend at all upon secondary causes, although he is moved by creatures’ acts—whether as occasions or as causes—to elect or to reprobate, why did Arminius not profess this openly and plainly? Why did he prefer to mention the *occasion* of the decree rather than its *cause*? Who does not see that he did this so that the odium of this assertion, which would readily arise in a godly reader when its cause was mentioned, might either be broken altogether or at least diminished by mentioning the occasion alone?

2. But however matters may stand, these claims will make little progress, since they contain a gross error—whether God is thought to take from creatures an occasion or a cause for making his decrees. For whether the creature’s act is called a cause or an occasion, according to Arminius it is of such a kind that God is moved by it to enter upon his decrees, just as Arminius himself philosophizes about serious grief over sins in his *Response to Article 31*, article 16. But as to a cause of this kind for divine predestination on the side of the one willing—that is, a cause of the act of predestination itself, or of the decree—Aquinas says that no one was ever so insane as to assert it, as we cited him in his proper place and treated the whole matter more fully.

3. What does this mean, “God takes from the creature’s foreseen act the occasion for making a decree”? Does Arminius not know that this act of the creature is also from God? Why, then, does he not say that when God institutes decrees of this kind, he takes the occasion for making them from his own foreseen act just as much as from the creature’s foreseen act, since the very act elicited by the creature is also efficiently produced by God, as Arminius himself acknowledges? Consequently, the order just mentioned exists not only between God’s decree and the creature’s act foreseen as the occasion of the decree, but also between two decrees of God.

For example, take the decree of election as an instance: let the creature’s act foreseen as the occasion of this decree be the act of faith. If, then, I say, the act of faith has been foreseen before this decree, God’s decree to give faith will also be prior to the decree of election to salvation—that is, to the decree to confer salvation. Consequently, the decree concerning the means will be prior to the decree concerning the end, which is foreign to all sober reason. Take the decree of reprobation: let its occasion be sin and the foresight of sin prior to reprobation. Then, I say, the decree to permit sin must be prior to the decree to punish sin. Now let these things be compared with one another, and let it be soberly asked which decree concerns the end and which the means. It is evident that God can in no way be said to have decreed to punish sin to this end, that sin might be permitted; for only on this rationale would the decree to permit sin be established as prior to the decree to punish sin. Conversely, it will be neither absurd nor paradoxical if someone says that God decreed to permit sin to this end, that he might punish it and thereby manifest his justice. On this rationale the divine decree to manifest his justice will beyond all controversy be prior to the decree to permit sin, and consequently sin will not have been foreseen before this decree at all, either as its cause or as its occasion.

4. Furthermore, if faith is prior to the decree to confer salvation, it will also be intended by God before salvation, since faith is God’s gift. Therefore faith will be prior to salvation in God’s intention; therefore it will also be posterior in execution, and God will crown us with salvation before he endows us with faith. That is no less paradoxical and utterly abhorrent to common reason and experience alike. But anyone who says that salvation, just as it is posterior in execution, is prior to faith itself in the divine intention will seem to affirm nothing that is not supremely agreeable to reason. The matter will come to the same thing in the decree of reprobation, provided the proper proportion is applied. For if sin has been foreseen before this decree

[Praefatio; printed p. 23; BSB canvas 31]

as its cause or occasion, the permission of this sin must be intended by God before damnation. But if the permission of sin is prior to damnation in God’s intention, it will follow that it is posterior in execution. From this it would be concluded that men are first damned by God on account of sins and afterward permitted to sin. This is like a monstrosity against which all reason and the usage of things revolt. Conversely, if someone says that damnation, just as it occurs later in execution, is prior in intention, then although the affections protest, reason does not object. Moreover, for restraining the affections there come to our aid from the Word of God both Solomon’s statement about the wicked made for the day of evil and Paul’s about vessels fitted together for destruction.

Yet this is not how I prefer to arrange the matter, although Piscator thought it should be so arranged. The permission of sin and punishment on account of sin do not seem to me to be separated in the intention as though one were subordinate to the other. Rather, they should be joined into one whole and adequate means to the end intended by God, which is the manifestation of the glory of divine corrective justice. For neither does the punishment of anyone but a sinner serve to illustrate this glory, nor does the permission of sin without punishment. But the two joined together contribute to this end. From this it is not necessary that either be prior to the other in the divine intention; instead, it is necessary that they be intended together by God. I do not say that they are intended by God so that they may exist at the same time, but that they are intended together by God so that they may exist—not at the same time, but in the order in which they are naturally disposed to exist: first the permission of sin occurs, and then condemnation on account of sin. Hence we do not need to assert that God permits sin in men to this end, that he may punish them on account of sin. Rather, he both permits sin and punishes men on account of sin to this end, that he may disclose and illustrate the glory of his justice.

Finally, when Arminius says that “God would not have made this decree if the secondary causes had not been going to act in this way,” we profess this to be true, but with an interpretation far different from Arminius’s. For inference is valid both from effect to cause and from cause to effect. But we deny that the futurity of the creature’s act is prior to God’s decree; indeed, we say that God’s decree is prior to the futurity of the creature’s act. For example, from eternity Peter was going to believe, and by God’s decree he was going to be saved from eternity. We also say with Arminius that if Peter had not been going to act in this way—that is, to believe—God would not have made the decree to save Peter. But if it is asked which of these two was prior by nature, we say that the decree to save Peter was prior. We prove this as follows. If Peter had not been going to believe, the cause would have been that God had decreed from eternity to deny Peter efficacious grace for believing. And the reason why God had decreed to deny Peter efficacious grace for believing would in turn have been that he had decreed not to admit Peter into the kingdom of heaven. For because he wills to save, he therefore wills to give faith, according to the words, “And as many as were ordained to eternal life believed.” Therefore also, because he wills not to save, he wills not to give faith, according to the words, “If our gospel is hidden, it is hidden among those who perish.” But we have treated these matters at greater length below.

2. Let us proceed to the second member. First, here he initially turns away from the question. For the question is not whether the created order would have been free from subjection to vanity if Adam had not sinned, but whether God would not have decreed to make the creation liable to vanity if Adam had not sinned. In the execution of the decree, one thing is the cause of another, just as in the execution of predestination to life grace is the cause of glory. But it does not follow from this that something in us is the cause of the decree. Likewise, there is a different order in the execution of the decree than in the decree itself, or in the divine intention. For example, grace is prior to glory in execution, but it by no means follows from this that grace is prior to glory in the divine intention. By precisely the same rationale, if in the execution of the other decree Adam’s sin is prior to the creation’s vanity, it will not follow from this that this sin was prior in the divine intention—I mean with respect to its permission.

2. Again, we do not deny this consequence: if Adam had not been going to sin, God would not have decreed to subject the creation to vanity on account of Adam’s sin. But meanwhile the question remains entire: which of these two would have been prior in God’s intention? We establish it as follows. If Adam had not been going to sin, the cause would have been that God had decreed to grant him efficacious grace for avoiding sin, and that for this end: that no punishment would be inflicted upon him or the creation on account of the demerit of his sin. Thus God would first by reason have resolved not to manifest his glory by way of punitive justice, and for that reason would not have willed to permit Adam to sin.

3. Likewise, we deny that the decree to send Christ into the world rests upon foresight of the fall. For if it rested upon foresight of the fall, it would also rest upon and presuppose God’s decree to permit the fall. That this is false is proved as follows. First, there is no priority and posteriority in the divine mind except a priority of reason. Such priority exists when the rationale of one thing is sought from the rationale of another, just as the rationale of the means is derived from the rationale of the end. Therefore, if the decree to permit sin were prior to the decree to send Christ, the sin to be permitted would stand as the end and Christ to be sent as the means to this end, which cannot be said with any congruity. For Christ was not sent, nor was he to be sent, to this end: that sin might be permitted. It is far more congruous to affirm that sin was to be permitted, or was permitted, by God so that Christ might be sent, and thereby the way might be prepared for manifesting God’s mercy tempered with justice.

Second, if the decree to permit sin had been prior to the decree to send Christ, it would follow that the permission of sin was prior to Christ’s mission in the divine intention and consequently posterior in execution. Thus Christ would be sent before sin was permitted. Who does not see how foreign that would be to all reason? It is much more probable that the decree to send Christ was prior to the decree to permit sin, since, just as Christ’s mission was posterior in execution, so it seems right that in the intention it was prior to the permission of sin. Yet this way of philosophizing does not please me either. It is evident that the permission of sin, just as it was in part prior to Christ’s mission, was also in part posterior to that same mission. The permission of sin was prior in the generations that preceded Christ, and posterior in those that followed him. It seems to me that the permission of sin and the mission of Christ should not be described as things subordinated to each other in God’s intention, but as things coordinated with each other and forming one means fitted to manifest God’s mercy, though mercy tempered with justice. But there will be a proper place to explain these matters more fully and to establish correctly the sequence of the divine decrees.

Third, as for the arguments by which Arminius maintains that the decree to send Christ rests upon foresight of the fall and consequently rests upon God’s decree to permit the fall, no one could use arguments more corrupt. First, they suppose that the order of things in the intention is the same as the order found in execution. This is notably false. Indeed, its opposite is so celebrated a truth that nothing more commonplace occurs in the byways of the academies. Arminius argues in sum as follows: Christ’s mission presupposes sin, “for he is the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world”; therefore, the intention or decree to send Christ presupposes sin in God’s foreknowledge and consequently also the decree to permit sin. Or thus: Christ’s mission is posterior in execution to permitted sin; therefore, in the divine intention it is also posterior to permitted sin and consequently to the decree to permit sin.

Second, consider that Christ is the Lamb who takes away the sins not only of the world preceding Christ but also of the world following Christ, and the permission of the latter sins followed Christ’s mission in execution. Fourth and finally, Christ is the Lamb who takes away sins committed even in crucifying him, and innumerable other sins of blasphemy and impiety against Christ himself. Will it therefore follow that foresight of these sins preceded the decree to send Christ? Arminius himself knows this to be impossible.

4. “And in this same way,” he says, “stand all God’s decrees by which he resolved to punish his creatures”—that is, they rest upon foresight of sin. But tell me, if you please: do they rest upon foresight of sin merely as an occasion? For so at the beginning you preferred to express your opinion more mildly. Or do they also rest upon it as a cause? According to Arminius, it will certainly be necessary that sin be established in God’s foresight as a cause of the decree as well. But what then? Should an effect not deservedly be said to depend upon its cause? And consequently, whenever secondary causes are established as the causes of the divine decrees, God himself, however much he is the first cause, will depend upon secondary causes in making his decrees.

2. But let us see what weight the arguments have. “Far be it from you to destroy the righteous with the wicked.” That is: God destroys none but the wicked. We acknowledge this; we also grant that he did not even decree to destroy anyone except a wicked person—that is, anyone except one who was going to be wicked before his destruction, but not anyone except one who was or was going to be wicked before the decree. For the wicked person exists as wicked before his destruction; but he does not exist as wicked before the decree. Nor does it follow from this that he was foreseen as wicked before the decree. For this argument is not valid: God destroys none but one who is wicked before his destruction; therefore, he did not decree to destroy anyone except one who was foreseen as wicked before the decree. Only establish that inference by some evident rationale, and we will yield and immediately pass over to your opinion.

3. But we are prepared to demonstrate that this inference is both invalid and inconsequent. First, God does not save any adult except one devoted to good works. Does it therefore follow that the decree to save rests upon foresight of good works? If it did, why should election not be said to be from works? The Apostle expressly denies that. Second, if the decree to destroy rests upon foresight of sin, the foresight of sin will certainly be prior to the decree to destroy; therefore, the decree to permit sin will also be prior to the decree to destroy on account of sin. I demonstrate as follows that this is notoriously false. First, in matters to be done, whatever is prior in the intention has the character of an end; whatever is posterior has the character of a means to the end. Therefore, if God’s decree to permit sin is prior to his decree of destruction on account of sin, the permission of sin must have the character of an end and destruction the character of a means to the end. Thus God would decree a man’s destruction to this end, that sin might be permitted—than which scarcely anything more absurd can be imagined.

Conversely, it does not seem foreign to reason, especially Christian reason, that God should permit men to sin to this end, that he might punish them on account of sins and thereby illustrate the glory of his justice, since Scripture testifies that Eli’s sons “did not listen to their father’s voice, because Jehovah willed to put them to death.” Arminius also admits that God permitted Ahab to go up to Ramoth-gilead contrary to the word of the Lord, and permitted this *as sin*, because God willed to punish him.

Second, if the permission of sin is prior in God’s intention or decree, while destruction is posterior, it will follow that in execution the permission of sin must be posterior and destruction on account of sin antecedent to permission, which no sane person would affirm. Conversely, since in the execution of the divine decrees the permission of sin precedes and destruction on account of sin follows, it seems much more fitting that in God’s intention destruction should obtain the prior place and the permission of sin the posterior place. I would not, however, readily affirm this. It seems more probable to me that God by no means permits sins in order that he may punish, and that these things are not subordinated to one another in the divine intention as means and end, but are rather coordinated and compose one integral means whose end is the disclosure of God’s glorious justice. I therefore judge that we should say neither that God permits sin in order to punish nor that he punishes in order to permit—although one of these can be said with some probability, while the other can be said with none at all—but that God both permits sin and punishes on account of sin in order to demonstrate his glorious justice.

Arminius adds that it does not follow from these things that the infliction of punishments depends upon secondary causes—that is, upon those who have deserved the punishment—and gives this singular rationale: they would wish to escape these punishments if they could. Behold the distinguished disputant! First, then, it will be false that “your destruction is from yourself, Israel” (for what else is this but that Israel’s destruction, or the infliction of the penalty that destroys him, depends upon Israel himself?), since Israel would desire above all to escape his destruction if he could. Second, the infliction of punishments certainly depends upon God as its physically efficient cause, but who doubts that it also depends upon the creatures’ sins as meritorious causes—that is, as morally efficient causes? Otherwise, our salvation could not be said to depend upon Christ insofar as he is its meritorious cause. Third, causes of this kind are the principal ones, since they are ordinarily used to answer the question why these people are punished by this or that kind of death. The magistrate, dominion, or execution is not ordinarily presented here as the cause, but the crime of those who perish. Fourth, we do not fault Arminius or the Arminians for making the infliction of punishments depend upon secondary causes; we fault them for teaching that the divine will to inflict punishments, or to punish—which is eternal—rests upon the actions of secondary causes. But it is customary for Arminius to corrupt the state of the question in this way and turn away from what is at issue to what is not disputed—that is, from the decree to punish to the punishment itself. For you seem to have readily persuaded yourself that this deception would lie hidden among the unskilled and would by no means be detected.

5. What Arminius says about the proper and improper meanings of the word *to ordain* and their application to Perkins seems to me more imperiously stated than either justly or appositely. First, he acknowledges that the Fathers employed this word in the sense which he himself wishes to reject as improper, while he confirms by no authority the meaning which alone he calls proper. Moreover, if *to ordain* properly means to establish order in things that have been done, it is impossible for God properly to be said to have ordained anything from eternity, since from eternity nothing had yet been done. The Holy Spirit also uses this verb in Greek with this very meaning which Arminius censures as improper: “And as many as were ordained to eternal life believed.”

In what follows, on p. 134, Arminius himself not only acknowledges that this word is used in two ways—namely, for establishing order in things that have been done and disposing and determining those things to a fitting end, and for resolving and decreeing that something should occur—but also admits both meanings without distinguishing propriety from impropriety. Piscator does the same in *On the Grace of God*, p. 75: “What is it to ordain here? Is it to decree, or rather to govern?” Hemingius also, in *On Universal Grace*, as cited by Piscator in *On the Grace of God*, p. 37, employs this word in both senses without marking either one with any ἀκυρολογίας, or misuse of language: “The rule must be maintained: God ordains all our works to his own glory—good works, indeed, so that they may be done; but evil works when they have been done.”

Finally, Arminius speaks of ordination in this very sense on p. 44: “Christ, I say, ordained by God as Priest and Mediator through the blood of his cross.” Likewise, on p. 27 he says that the cause of the permission of sin is God’s ordination, “so that the permitted sin may in actual fact be a means of illustrating his glory.” Here ordination cannot be understood as establishing order in sin after it has been committed, for it precedes the sin itself and its permission as their cause. Perhaps if the matter is examined, Arminius himself will be found to speak of ordination in this sense more often than in the other. I therefore do not know to what end he pronounces, on I know not what authority, that “he would wish the verb *to ordain* to be *used* in its proper sense.” And why did he not meanwhile take care that the verb *to use* was not employed in an improper sense? For if he meant that the verb *to ordain* should *be used*, why did he say “in its proper sense”; or if he preferred that it be “employed in its proper sense,” why did he say “used”?

2. But however just this censure may have been, it was nevertheless not apposite. What then? Does Perkins employ this word in its proper sense? According to Arminius’s interpretation, therefore, *to ordain* in this passage of Perkins means “to decree that something should come to be.” But see how little this exposition fits. For Perkins says that God ordains creatures even when they act unjustly. What would this mean? On Arminius’s interpretation, it would mean that God ordains creatures so that they may come to be. Absurdly so, for they are already

in being when they act, and no ordination is needed so that they may come to be. Rather, *to ordain* in this passage seems to mean nothing else to Perkins than *to govern*: namely, that God justly governs creatures even when they act unjustly.
