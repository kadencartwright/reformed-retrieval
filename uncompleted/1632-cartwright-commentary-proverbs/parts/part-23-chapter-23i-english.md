Verse 17. Do not envy sinners in your heart, but remain, or persevere, in the fear of the Lord all day.

Verse 18. For there is an end, and your expectation will not be cut off.

In חטאים, “sinners,” there is a synecdoche of the genus standing for notorious and hardened sinners. In היום, “day,” there is a synecdoche of the part standing for time. In אחרית, “end,” there is a metonymy of an adjunct standing for the reward granted after a work has been completed. In תקותך, “your expectation,” there is a metonymy of an adjunct standing for the thing hoped for.

The purpose is to draw the godly away from envying sinners by setting a reward before them.

Someone is said to envy the wicked when he grievously torments himself at the sight of their prosperity. This happens especially when, making a comparison with their condition, he notices that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 520 | printed columns 1024-1025]

his own is utterly different because of his scant means and lack of possessions. For in Psalm 73:3 the prophet confesses that he fell into this offense while contemplating too intently the prosperity and happiness of the wicked. Not that it is impermissible to examine and thoroughly investigate both the prosperity of the wicked and our own misery, so that we may know God's most wise government of the world and the church. Rather, the fault lies in viewing these things with such bleary eyes that we exalt the happiness of the wicked beyond measure and depreciate our own condition more than is just. For we could not be such unfair appraisers of things if the eye of our judgment were sound and did not suffer from such dimness that it represented small things to us under the appearance of great ones, and one thing under the appearance of two, and sometimes attributed an appearance to what does not exist. This happens because in this matter we consult the flesh, not faith, and fix our eyes only on things perceived by the senses. For if we considered the matter as it is, we would judge the little that suffices for one righteous man to be better than the great abundance of many wicked men, Psalm 37:16. And in Proverbs 8 the riches of the righteous are called lasting possessions, or substance. Who, then, is so foolish that he would not prefer fruits that can be stored for a year or two to the wicked's fruits that last, as it were, only an hour, enduring for merely two days?

When he says that we must not envy in our heart, he teaches that we ought to be so disposed that we do not even desire the condition of the wicked, much less betray this fault of mind in speech, and least of all strive to be like them.

In the latter half-verse he exhorts us to the opposite good: “But persevere in the fear of the Lord.” From this it follows that envy of the wicked and the fear of God cannot coexist, but that the more someone envies the wicked, the less he fears God. For thus the prophet confesses in Psalm 73 that by envying the wicked he was brought almost to the point of abandoning godliness and judging all the godly most unhappy. Therefore, with Job, we must abhor the condition of the wicked, not desire it, Job 21:16. Nor should we grow angry because everything succeeds according to their wishes, since the indignation arising from this often not only draws us away from the fear of God but also plunges us into dreadful murmuring against him.

In the latter verse he gives the reason for the preceding exhortation from an adjunct, the reward or outcome of the godly. Since this reward is certain and unquestionable, resting on God's most firm promise, it ought to keep us in the pursuit and love of godliness. Hence, in Psalm 73:24, God is said, in the person of David, to be about to guide all his people here by his counsel and afterward receive them into glory. And in Psalm 37, verse 37, he says, “Observe the blameless man and consider the righteous, for his end, or reward, is prosperity.” Likewise, James 5:11: “You have heard of Job's patience, and have seen the end which the Lord brought about.” Therefore, so that we may have strength to stand firm in this severe temptation, our eyes must be withdrawn from the present state of things and turned toward the future. Hence also the apostle teaches in Romans 8:24 that we are saved by hope, but that hope concerns not present things but those which are unseen. Moses furnished us an excellent example in this matter, Hebrews 11:23–26. Travelers undertake arduous journeys eagerly if they understand that decent and generous lodging is being prepared for them when the journey is finished. Soldiers, spurred by the hope of spoils, expose themselves to the heaviest labors and certain dangers. Those who are going to run in the stadium abstain from all pleasures in order to win the prize set before them. How ardently, then, ought we to strive to acquire and preserve godliness, when the prize set before us is not uncertain and perishable, as theirs is, but

[1632 witness | PDF p. 521 | printed columns 1026-1027]

as absolutely certain as it is everlasting!
